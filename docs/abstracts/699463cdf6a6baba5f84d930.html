Introduction

Epilepsy is one of the most common neurological disorders worldwide, affecting millions of people.1 Antiseizure medications (ASMs) remain the primary treatment for seizure control and may be used as monotherapy or polytherapy.2 Commonly prescribed ASMs include sodium valproate, carbamazepine, phenytoin, topiramate, vigabatrin, and levetiracetam. Although these drugs act through different mechanisms, such as modulation of ion channels, enhancement of inhibitory neurotransmission, or regulation of synaptic release, they all reduce neuronal excitability and help achieve seizure control in approximately 70% of patients.

As up to 50% of epilepsy cases are diagnosed before the age of 18 and is typically chronic, extended use of ASMs is common. Emerging evidence suggests that long-term use of ASMs may be associated with structural changes in the brain.3 This systematic review aims to investigate these potential effects by synthesizing findings from studies that use magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to assess ASMs-related brain changes.

Methods

We conducted a systematic review in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines.4 We performed a comprehensive search across four databases: PubMed, Medline (EBSCO), Scopus, and Cochrane. We adapted the search equations to the syntax of each database while applying the same search terms across all platforms. These terms combined keywords related to epilepsy, antiseizure medications, neuropharmacology, MRI and brain structure. After searching the databases with the selected keywords, we managed the screening using Covidence. In addition, we manually identified and added three studies to Covidence, where they underwent the same screening criteria.

After removing duplicates, two independent reviewers (N.P. and A.K.L) screened the titles and abstracts of all identified articles. To get selected for the full text screening process and be included in the review, the studies had to meet the following criteria: (1) include people with epilepsy, (2) involve people with epilepsy receiving ASMs, and when applicable, include people with epilepsy prior to ASM administration, and (3) use MRI to report brain structural changes (in the gray or white matter).

We included only experimental and quasi-experimental studies (controlled trials, non-randomized controlled trials, before and after studies, and interrupted time-series studies), prospective and retrospective cohort studies, case-control studies, analytical cross-sectional studies, and systematic reviews. We excluded all other publication types. 

Since this review focused on the long-term effects of ASMs, we excluded studies involving people who had undergone epilepsy surgery or received other, non-pharmacological treatments.

Results

From the 4,843 articles identified through database searches and manual inclusion, Covidence automatically removed 2,195 duplicates. We then screened the titles and abstracts of the remaining 2,648 articles. Of these, 92 articles met the inclusion criteria and progressed to full-text retrieval. After full-text screening, 23 articles met all inclusion criteria and were included in the systematic review.

Across the included studies, the reported effects of chronic ASM use on the brain were largely drug-dependent. Some studies reported that long-term use of specific ASMs, such as valproic acid and phenytoin, was associated with morphological brain changes, including cortical thinning and volume loss of the cortical motor regions, visual cortex and cerebellum. In contrast, some studies reported that ASMs such as levetiracetam were associated with increased volumes of basal ganglia and a restoration of normal activation patterns. Findings across studies were also heterogeneous. Some found that the use of vigabatrin was associated with brain abnormalities, such as hippocampal changes in patients with infantile spasms, whereas studies conducted in adults did not. Finally, other studies examining the effects of topiramate and carbamazepine found no structural changes but did report functional changes in brain activation.

Conclusion

This systematic review demonstrates that the long-term effects of ASMs on brain structure, as assessed by MRI, are heterogeneous and drug dependent. While some ASMs, such as valproic acid, phenytoin, and vigabatrin may be associated with structural brain changes, others, including levetiracetam, appear to have neutral or potentially beneficial effects. Additionally, other ASMs such as topiramate and carbamazepine appear to affect brain function and activation rather than brain structure. These findings highlight the need for longitudinal, well-controlled imaging studies to better disentangle medication effects from disease-related changes and to inform treatment decisions, particularly in patients requiring lifelong therapy.
