Introduction

Sleep spindles are brief bursts of thalamo-cortical activity (11-16 Hz, 0.3-2 s) known to play a key role in motor memory consolidation. They have been widely studied in adults, and recent work shows that they follow a very specific rhythmic pattern. Sleep spindles can occur as isolated events, or they can cluster into “trains.” These trains are spaced apart by an infraslow rhythm (~0.02 Hz), and within each train the spindles repeat at a characteristic mesoscale frequency (~0.2-0.3 Hz). A higher occurrence of spindles in trains has been shown to have a positive impact on memory consolidation.1 However, sleep spindles in infants have been less extensively investigated. Existing work has primarily focused on describing their developmental features, such as age of emergence, changes in frequency, duration, and cortical localisation. To our knowledge, no study has specifically examined their potential rhythmic pattern. In addition, although some studies suggest that atypical development can affect spindle characteristics, the impact of prematurity remains understudied. As sleep spindles may provide an important marker of early brain development and sensorimotor maturation, the aim of this study was to characterize infant sleep spindles and determine whether specific rhythmic patterns are already present early in development. We also examined potential differences between extremely preterm and full-term infants.

Methods

We collected high-density EEG data (Electrical Geodesics, Inc system, 128 channels) from 50 infants, born either very preterm (i.e., before 32 weeks of gestational age (GA)) or full-term (between 37-42w GA) around 2 months-old of corrected age (2M). The study protocol was approved by the Comité de Protection des Personnes Ile de France 3 (protocol DEVine, CEA 100 054) and was conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki and local data protection regulations. Data were recorded during daytime sleep for 10 min at a 1000Hz sampling rate, and were scored by a pediatric neurophysiologist for sleep states.2 After an initial 0.2–45 Hz bandpass filter, data were preprocessed with the APICE pipeline.3 Spindles were detected during non-REM using a wavelet-based algorithm1 adapted for infants. After detecting the spindles, we classified them as either grouped (trains of spindles separated by less than 15 seconds) or isolated. We then measured several characteristics for each infant, including the interval between spindle trains, the interval between spindles within a train, the amplitude, the frequency, the ratio of trains to isolated spindles, their duration and density, and compared them between preterm and full-term groups. Infants with fewer than 3 spindles and less than 50 seconds of continuous non-REM (artefact-free) were excluded from subsequent analyses. To analyse the temporal organisation of spindle occurrence within a train, we applied a procedure similar to4 using a two-step Morlet wavelet transform: first on the spindle frequency range (11–16 Hz), to obtain the temporal envelope of spindle-band power, then applying a second transform to this envelope over a slower frequency range (0.01-0.6Hz) to estimate its mesoscale modulation spectrum at 2M for both full-term and preterm infants. Time–frequency decomposition of spindle-band power was then performed in Brainstorm. Morlet wavelets (1–30 Hz) were applied on epochs from –12 to +15s relative to spindle onset. Student’s t-tests against a resting baseline (-2 to -0.5 s pre-onset) were performed on central electrodes (C3, C4) to identify significant spindle-band activity and visualise a potential mesoscale rhythm. The same procedure was extended to the whole scalp to generate topographical t-maps of significant spindle-related activity. These time-frequency analyses were subsequently compared between preterm and full-term infants using permutation testing (n=1000). 

Results

Data from 18 preterm infants without major brain abnormalities (mean GA at birth: 27,35w +/- 1,93w ; post-menstrual age (PMA) at EEG: 50.51w +/- 0,74w) and 9 full-term infants (mean GA at birth: 40,00 w +/- 1,22w ; PMA at EEG: 50.10w +/- 0,98) showed sufficient non-REM duration (mean : 302,75s +/- 160,51s) and were considered for analyses. No significant differences (p > 0.34) in spindle features were found between preterm and full-term infants, for none of the central electrodes. A specific rhythmic pattern was observed in both groups. Mesoscale spectra revealed a common rhythm of spindle occurrence of about 0.08 Hz (=12.5 s) (Figure 1A), which is roughly twice as slow as what is typicaly seen in adults (0.2-0.3 Hz).1 Student’s t-tests against baseline performed on central electrodes (15 preterm and 8 full-term infants ; p < 0.05, False Discovery Rate-corrected) supported the presence of this mesoscale rhythm (Figure 1B). T-maps against baseline across the whole scalp (Figure 1B) highlighted spindle activity localized in the frontal and central regions for both groups, consistent with previous findings in infants and children.5 Moreover, separating the early (0–1.5 s) and late (1.5–3 s) spindle phases revealed a clear propagation pattern from central toward frontal regions, and from the generation site to the contralateral site. On these same maps, fast-spindle activity (13–16 Hz) appeared stronger than slow-spindle activity (11–13 Hz) in preterm infants, whereas full-term infants displayed a more balanced distribution. Finally, although permutation tests comparing the time–frequency maps between groups yielded only modest statistics (-1.5 < t < 1, p < 0.05) (Figure 1C), they still indicate significant differences particularly over occipital regions. This aligns with the t-maps against baseline computed across the scalp which reveal more diffuse spindle-band activity in preterm infants in comparison to the more precisely localised activation clusters observed in full-term infants (Figure 1B). 

Conclusion

This study examined sleep spindle characteristics in 2M-old infants, with a particular focus on their temporal dynamics. Our results revealed a mesoscale organisation, with spindles recurring within spindle trains at an interval of approximately 12.5 s. Although twice slower than the rhythm reported in adults, the presence of such structured spindle dynamics at this early developmental stage suggests the emergence of procedural memory-consolidation mechanisms supported by developing thalamo-cortical connectivity. Since slow-spindle density normaly increases with age (fast spindles reflecting an immature precursor and slow spindles marking more advanced maturation,5 especially in frontal and prefrontal regions), the predominance of fast spindles in preterm infants may indicate a less mature cortical stage. In addition, the more diffuse spindle activity seen in preterm may indicate reduced specialisation of brain regions and connections involved in motor memory consolidation, suggesting a potential delay in thalamo-cortical maturation compared with full-term infants. Although replication on larger groups is needed, this study demonstrates the potential of defining sleep spindle features as key markers of early sensorimotor development following premature birth. Further analyses of slow-wave-spindle coupling, reflecting consolidation efficiency, would be valuable but are limited by non-REM duration. It would also be relevant to assess whether spindle maturation at 2M relates to the structural maturation of neural connections from term age—an aspect our dataset could address, given that we have both EEG and MRI data acquired at term and at 2M. Overall, this framework may support early detection of neurodevelopmental risks and guide timely interventions.
