Introduction

Amyloid positron emission tomography (amyloid PET), widely used to determine whether an individual is positive for amyloid-β (Aβ)—a key pathological hallmark and putative initiating factor of Alzheimer’s disease (AD)—is regarded as the most robust and reproducible modality1. However, its high cost and invasiveness make it an undeniably resource-intensive examination. Recently, blood-based measurements of Aβ have attracted increasing attention because of their practicality and accessibility. Plasma Aβ assessment has also been reported to detect Aβ abnormalities approximately six years earlier than amyloid PET, suggesting a distinct role as a convenient biomarker for early screening2.

The recent study has further suggested that dysfunction of the glymphatic system, a major pathway for Aβ clearance, precedes conversion to Aβ positivity3. Because glymphatic function depends on cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) influx, exchange, and efflux, we hypothesized that the choroid plexus—an essential structure involved in CSF production and homeostasis—may exhibit detectable features that precede Aβ positivity identifiable by plasma Aβ measurements. In particular, choroid plexus volume (CPV) has been shown to increase across the clinical spectrum from cognitively normal (CN) to mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and AD dementia, and has been interpreted as an imaging marker reflecting blood–CSF barrier alterations, CSF dynamics, and inflammatory or vascular changes along the AD continuum4. Accordingly, the present study focused on CPV.

Specifically, among cognitively normal individuals, we examined whether baseline CPV and longitudinal change in CPV over a two-year interval differed between those who converted to plasma Aβ positivity during follow-up (“plasma amyloid converters”) and those who did not (“non-converters”), to test whether CPV alterations precede plasma Aβ conversion.

Methods

Participants and cohort construction

Starting from 502,128 UK Biobank registrants (as of 2025-09-01), we constructed a “plasma amyloid converter/non-converter imaging cohort” using the following steps. We first identified participants with baseline multimodal MRI available (3D T1-weighted, 3D T2-FLAIR, diffusion MRI, and resting-state fMRI; n = 64,987). We excluded individuals with all-cause dementia (UK Biobank Data-Field 42018) and those meeting mild MCI criteria based on ICD-10 codes (G31.8, R41, F06.7), yielding a cognitively normal (CN) imaging cohort (n = 64,355). We then retained participants with plasma Aβ42/40 measured at both baseline and follow-up (Instance 2 and Instance 3; n = 408). To focus on incident amyloid conversion, we restricted the sample to baseline plasma amyloid–negative participants (Aβ42/40 > 0.05615; n = 375). The follow-up interval was defined as 1.5 ≤ interval < 2.5 years. Plasma amyloid converters were defined as participants whose follow-up Aβ42/40 decreased to ≤ 0.0561 (n = 31), whereas non-converters remained > 0.0561 (n = 344). After excluding participants lacking CPV data, 30 converters and 340 non-converters remained eligible for matching. Optimal 1:1 matching without replacement was performed using Mahalanobis distance with the Hungarian algorithm, targeting close alignment on age, sex, APOE ε4 allele number (0/1/2), intracranial volume (ICV), and follow-up interval. This procedure yielded 30 matched pairs (total matched n = 60).

MRI acquisition and imaging-derived phenotypes

Structural 3D T1-weighted images were acquired on a 3T Siemens MAGNETOM Skyra scanner using a 32-channel head coil and a 3D MPRAGE sequence (TR = 2000 ms, TE = 2.01 ms, TI = 880 ms, flip angle = 8°, 1.0-mm isotropic resolution, GRAPPA/iPAT = 2; acquisition time ≈ 4 min 54 s). CPV (mm³) and ICV (mm³) were obtained from UK Biobank imaging-derived phenotypes generated using FreeSurfer version 6.0 (official site: https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/) within the UK Biobank automated brain imaging pipeline (Figure 1). FreeSurfer processing quality control was performed using a two-step procedure: automated checks using the Qoala-T approach, followed by manual review for cases near the automated decision threshold (per UK Biobank brain MRI documentation: https://biobank.ctsu.ox.ac.uk/crystal/crystal/docs/brain_mri.pdf).

Statistical analysis

Baseline balance between matched converters (n = 30) and matched non-converters (n = 30) was evaluated using Welch’s t-tests for continuous variables (age, ICV, follow-up interval) and chi-square tests for categorical variables (sex, APOE ε4 allele number). Primary analyses were conducted at the matched-pair level for (i) baseline CPV and (ii) within-participant change in CPV over follow-up, defined as ΔCPV = CPVfollow−up − CPVbaseline. For each outcome, normality of within-pair differences was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. When within-pair differences were non-normally distributed, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used, and results were summarized using medians [IQR], Hodges–Lehmann (HL) paired location shifts with 95% confidence intervals, and effect size r derived from the standardized test statistic. When within-pair differences were approximately normal, paired t-tests were used, and results were summarized using means ± SD, mean paired differences with 95% confidence intervals, and Cohen’s d as an effect size. All p-values were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*). Effect sizes were interpreted as follows: for r, |r| < 0.20 very weak, 0.20–0.39 weak, 0.40–0.59 moderate, 0.60–0.79 strong, and 0.80–1.00 very strong; for Cohen’s d, |d| < 0.20 negligible, 0.20–0.49 small, 0.50–0.79 medium, and ≥ 0.80 large. Longitudinal (ΔCPV) analyses were restricted to participants with CPV available at both time points; the resulting sample size was reported alongside the results. All statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.12.

Results

Participant characteristics

Balance checks in the matched sample (30 plasma amyloid converters and 30 matched non-converters) showed no statistically significant intergroup differences across the matching variables (Figure 2A), indicating successful matching.

Baseline CPV: converters vs non-converters

Baseline CPV analyses included all 30 matched pairs. The distribution of within-pair baseline CPV differences was non-normal; therefore, we used the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Baseline CPV was smaller in plasma amyloid converters than in matched non-converters (median [IQR] 1071.00 [445.45] vs 1388.90 [817.65] mm³). The Hodges–Lehmann paired location shift was −252.45 mm³ (95% CI −378.55 to −135.40; p < 0.001; Figure 2B), corresponding to a moderate effect size (r = −0.59).

Longitudinal ΔCPV: converters vs non-converters

Longitudinal ΔCPV analyses included 26 complete matched pairs, because follow-up CPV data were missing for 4 pairs. Within-pair differences in ΔCPV were approximately normally distributed, supporting the use of paired t-testing. Over the 2-year interval, plasma amyloid converters showed a numerically greater increase in CPV than matched non-converters (mean ± SD 83.74 ± 147.83 vs 22.91 ± 135.62 mm³). The mean paired difference was +60.82 mm³ (95% CI −8.12 to 129.76; p = 0.081; Figure 2B), with a small-to-medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.36).

Conclusion

In a rigorously matched longitudinal sample of cognitively normal UK Biobank participants, plasma amyloid conversion (Aβ42/40 transition from >0.0561 to ≤0.0561 over 2 years) was preceded by a significantly smaller baseline choroid plexus volume (CPV), despite balancing age, sex, APOE ε4 allele number, intracranial volume, and follow-up interval. In interpreting this pattern, we consider two alternative scenarios. In the AD literature, the choroid plexus is generally thought to enlarge in proportion to disease progression. Therefore, one possibility is that (A) individuals with smaller CPV have weaker choroid plexus function, which in turn makes them more likely to transition to amyloid positivity. If this interpretation is correct, CPV could be expected to reflect AD-related abnormality even earlier than plasma Aβ and might serve as a candidate ultra-early biomarker. Alternatively, (B) at an extremely early stage of AD—such as the stage captured in the present study—the choroid plexus may instead shrink, followed by subsequent enlargement that eventually overtakes the trajectory of cognitively normal comparators. If this latter scenario is correct, then treating a single time-point CPV as an ultra-early diagnostic biomarker would not be feasible. This uncertainty is the most important limitation of the present work. Accordingly, future studies should extend the observation window both before and after conversion and directly characterize CPV trajectories in plasma amyloid converters to determine which temporal pattern is supported.

Additional limitations should also be noted. First, choroid plexus segmentation is technically challenging and may be prone to relatively large measurement error. In this study, we adopted the UK Biobank quality control as provided; however, future work aiming for more rigorous quantification may need to incorporate additional procedures, potentially including systematic manual correction. Second, the ΔCPV analyses were affected by reduced sample size due to follow-up missingness, which not only lowers statistical power but may also introduce systematic bias (e.g., if follow-up is missing for health-related reasons). Replication in larger longitudinal cohorts with improved follow-up completeness is therefore warranted.

In conclusion, we found that CPV was significantly smaller in converters than in matched non-converters at a stage preceding blood-based amyloid positivity, suggesting that CPV assessment may have potential to capture an ultra-early AD-related phenotype that could complement plasma screening. However, clarifying whether this reflects a stable predisposing trait (scenario A) or an early transient reduction followed by later enlargement (scenario B), and establishing the robustness and biological specificity of the measurement, will require further longitudinal investigation.
