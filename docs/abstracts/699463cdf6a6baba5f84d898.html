Introduction

Characterising normative developmental trajectories of electrophysiological signals across the human lifespan has long been a crucial research question in neuroscience. Previous works have largely focused on modelling mean trajectories1 thereby overlooking population heterogeneity and limiting the ability to estimate individual-level deviations. To address this limitation, we derive a normative atlas of resting-state magnetoencephalography (rs-MEG). Here, we define a normative atlas as a collection of regionally resolved, lifespan-informed normative models that characterise normative ranges of imaging-derived phenotypes (IDPs) from rs-MEG across spatially detailed cortical and subcortical regions. It encodes age- and sex-specific centiles of variation for a diverse set of IDPs, encompassing both oscillatory and non-oscillatory rs-MEG measures. Analogous to normative reference ranges used in clinical medicine, such an atlas defines expected ranges of brain activity against which empirical measurements can be evaluated to produce individual-level interpretation, enabling downstream applications in precision neuropsychiatry.2

 To construct the normative atlas, we analyse large-scale (1,385 individuals), multi-site (3 datasets) rs-MEG data in source space, enabling regionally detailed characterisation of functional brain activity across both cortical and subcortical structures, defined using Destrieux 2009 and the automatic subcortical segmentation atlas. Leveraging a diverse set of periodic and aperiodic IDPs, the atlas moves beyond prior lifespan MEG studies that primarily reported mean age-related effects by explicitly modelling the full normative range of variation, yielding age- and sex-specific centiles. Normative models are estimated using the MEGaNorm pipeline3 with hierarchical Bayesian regression (HBR) to accommodate inter-site variability and nonlinear lifespan trajectories.4

Methods

To derive a representative normative atlas of resting-state MEG, we used data from 1,385 healthy participants aged 6–88 years (719 female; mean age = 43.1 years, SD = 23.11). Data were pooled from three datasets: the Cambridge Centre for Ageing and Neuroscience (Cam-CAN), Boys Town National Research Hospital (BTNRH), and the Open MEG Archive (OMEGA). Cam-CAN and BTNRH data were collected using a 306-channel Elekta MEGIN system, whereas OMEGA data were acquired using a 275-channel CTF axial-gradiometer system.

MEG recordings were preprocessed using the MEGaNorm pipeline.3 Preprocessed sensor-level data were projected to volumetric source space using a linearly constrained minimum variance (LCMV) beamformer. Source estimates were then parcellated using the Destrieux 2009 atlas for cortical regions and the automatic subcortical segmentation atlas for subcortical regions, resulting in a total of 194 brain regions. Regional time series were segmented into 10-second segments. Power spectral density (PSD) for each segment was computed using Welch’s method and averaged across all segments to yield a single PSD estimate per region.

As IDPs, we computed both relative and absolute spectral power for the theta (3–8 Hz), alpha (8–13 Hz), and beta (13–30 Hz) frequency bands. Relative power was defined as the ratio of band-limited power to total periodic power across 3–40 Hz, and absolute power as the logarithm of band-limited power; we refer to these as the original relative and absolute powers. Because PSDs are influenced by 1/f-like aperiodic structure, we decomposed each PSD into periodic and aperiodic components using the specparam algorithm.5 From the resulting periodic (aperiodic-adjusted) spectra, we extracted relative and absolute power for the same frequency bands, which we refer to as adjusted relative and absolute powers. We also computed original and adjusted absolute power for the broadband range (3–40 Hz) using the non-adjusted and aperiodic-adjusted PSDs, respectively, to quantify total spectral energy. Finally, we extracted the aperiodic exponent and offset to characterise scale-free neural activity.

For normative modelling, we used hierarchical Bayesian regression (HBR)4 with a Sinh–Arcsinh (SHASH) likelihood and B-spline basis functions, implemented in the Predictive Clinical Neuroscience Toolkit (PCNtoolkit; version 0.35), to model non-linear and heteroscedastic trajectories of brain IDPs. HBR enables the explicit modelling of site-related batch effects and supports federated learning, allowing decentralised model updates without sharing private data. Age was included as a covariate, while sex and site were modelled as random intercepts to account for systematic differences in offset power levels between batches. The outputs of normative modeling include a z-score (deviation score) for each regional IDP and each subject. This deviation score reflects an individual’s position relative to the reference population, with positive and negative deviation scores indicating higher and lower IDP values than the population average, respectively.

Normative models were trained on 50% of the data and evaluated on the remaining 50%. Model performance was assessed using the standardised mean squared error (SMSE) to evaluate central tendency, and the Shapiro–Wilk test statistic (W), skewness, and excess kurtosis (kurtosis−3) to quantify the distributional validity of the estimated centiles. Lower SMSE values (with 1 as a baseline) indicate a more accurate estimation of the true mean. Skewness and excess kurtosis values approaching zero, together with W values approaching 1, indicate that the resulting deviation (z-score) distributions closely approximate normality.

Results

Figure 1a presents an evaluation of the quality of the fitted normative models across IDPs and brain regions using quantitative out-of-sample diagnostics. The low SMSE values (baseline is 1) indicate that the models accurately capture the central tendency (50th per- centile) of the data. Furthermore, the excess kurtosis, skewness, and W statistics show that the derived deviation scores approximate Gaussianity, highlighting the calibration of the es- timated centiles. Mild leptokurtosis remains in the deviation-score distributions, especially for the adjusted absolute power of the alpha frequency band, due to the high sensitivity of kurtosis to outlying samples. Overall, the out-of-sample density plots demonstrate that the normative atlas accurately captures the shape of the underlying distributions of IDPs and produces normal-like distributions of IDPs across most brain regions. 

We used the normative atlas to derive growth charts of IDPs for each region. The charts were generated by sampling from the posterior predictive distributions across ages 6–80 for both sexes. Figure 1b shows examples of these charts for males in four representative regions. The charts illustrate substantial inter-individual variability across the lifespan, along with complex, non-linear, and heteroscedastic age-related trajectories, in line with our previous findings.3

Spatial comparison of IDP growth charts highlights additional insights. Figure 1b shows that the pattern of normative ranges is largely similar across homologous brain regions; for example, the growth charts of original relative powers in the left superior temporal sulcus and left Precuneus Gyrus closely match those in the right hemisphere. However, non-homologous regions show markedly different developmental trajectories. For example, while the original relative power of alpha increases in the right superior temporal sulcus, it decreases in the right precuneus gyrus.

Conclusion

In this study, we present a first step toward deriving a normative atlas of resting-state magnetoencephalography, providing spatially resolved normative models of both aperiodic and periodic imaging-derived phenotypes. To this end, we leveraged a large-scale multisite dataset, covering a wide age range (6 to 88 years) and employed non-linear, heteroscedas- tic HBR models with a SHASH likelihood to account for site-related variability and more accurately model both central tendency and dispersion of IDPs across the lifespan.3

The normative atlas allows characterization of population-level heterogeneity and both quantification and localization of individual-level deviations in rs-MEG signals. By modeling the full distribution of age-related variation of spatially detailed rs-MEG IDPs across the lifespan, the atlas enables comparison of developmental trajectories across different regions and rs-MEG IDPs for the first time. This contrasts with prior electrophysiological studies, which focused on mean trajectories and assumed homogeneity within the healthy population; an assumption contradicted by evidence of substantial inter-individual variability in MEG.3 By deriving normative ranges, the atlas enables computation of individualized deviation scores, highlighting functionally deviant brain regions that may indicate abnormalities. Im- portantly, the atlas is trained exclusively on healthy participants and deviations are detected relative to population norms without requiring patient data, supporting a transdiagnostic approach in precision neuropsychiatry. 

The derived normative atlas is extensible and supports calibration on new, local, or private datasets, as well as federated learning. The HBR framework enables normative atlases to be extended as new data become available, without requiring access to the original training datasets, by using the posterior distribution of the pretrained models as prior. This feature allows continuous refinement of models without centralized data sharing. This is particularly important for extending the atlas to populations underrepresented in this study. Additionally, these models can be recalibrated on local samples where the sample size is small or restricted in terms of age range. Our models can address this issue by partial pooling, while accounting for sensitivity to site-specific variability. To facilitate such applications, the data processing pipeline and derived models will be openly shared via the MEGaNorm package.3

We also acknowledge several limitations. First, our current analysis excluded the delta and gamma bands due to limitations of the specparam algorithm, resulting in biologically implausible negative periodic power values for these frequency bands. The application of alternative PSD parameterisation methods may help extend this modelling to the delta and gamma bands. Second, our dataset is primarily derived from Western populations and therefore lacks broader demographic diversity. 

The normative atlase represents a step toward individualized, transdiagnostic analysis of rs-MEG signals. Although further development and validation are needed, the atlas can serve as a scalable reference for studying atypical brain development, ageing, and neuropsy- chiatric disorders through the open-access models and pipeline, combined with continuous community-driven refinement.
