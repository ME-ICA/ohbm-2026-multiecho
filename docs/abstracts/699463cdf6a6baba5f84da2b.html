Introduction

Others’ bodily displays are powerful social cues that convey information about intentions and internal states. Actions directed toward an observer constitute a special class of social events, as they imply potential involvement in interaction. Such engagement can be inferred from bodily cues, including movement kinematics, action orientation, and gaze, whose monitoring is known to reliably engages two neural systems: the Action Observation Network (AON)1, associated with processing sensorimotor features of observed actions, and the Mentalizing Network (MENTA), including the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (dmPFC) and temporo-parietal junction (TPJ), commonly linked to inferences about others’ mental states2.

Although these systems are often described as supporting distinct levels of social information (e.g., action kinematics versus intention encoding), growing evidence challenges this dichotomy, showing that both networks are sensitive to features that signal engagement, such as action directedness and eye contact, and exhibit increased functional coupling in interactive contexts3. An alternative view therefore suggests that AON and MENTA jointly encode social affordances, sharing complementary and multimodal representations of engagement-relevant bodily cues4.

Using fMRI and Representational Similarity Analysis (RSA)5, we tested the representational geometry of these systems while participants passively observed grasping actions varying in engagement level. We hypothesized that engagement is encoded by shared representational formats across AON and MENTA, expressed as covariance in multivoxel pattern modulation across regions, and tested explicit perception of engagement and bodily-cue selectivity to determine whether engagement arises from specific action features or from an integrated engagement representation.

Methods

PARTICIPANTS AND PROCEDURE

26 right-handed healthy volunteers (13 females; mean age = 24.3 ± 3.4 years) with normal or corrected-to-normal vision participated after providing written informed consent. All procedures were approved by the local ethics committee and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants underwent four fMRI runs of an event-related action observation task. They passively viewed 2-s video clips depicting grasping actions varying along three engagement-relevant features in a 2 × 2 × 2 factorial design (cf. Figure 1A, top): GOAL (PASS vs. PLACE), PERSPECTIVE (second- vs. third-person), and GAZE VISIBILITY (transparent vs. sun glasses). These manipulations generated a graded engagement continuum, from minimally engaging (PLACE–third-person–no gaze) to maximally engaging (PASS–second-person–visible gaze). Each of the eight conditions was presented four times per run. Catch trials (10%) required explicit goal judgments to ensure attention. Following scanning, participants rated each stimulus on perceived communicativeness, involvement, and feeling observed, providing ratings to compute a multivariate ‘Engagement Index’ used to measure explicit perception of engagement.

IMAGES ACQUISITION AND PROCESSING 

Functional and structural images were acquired on a 3 T scanner. Functional images were collected using a T2*-weighted EPI sequence (TR = 2000 ms, TE = 30 ms, 2 mm isotropic voxels, whole-brain coverage). Preprocessing was performed in FSL and included distortion correction, motion correction, slice-timing correction, coregistration, normalization to MNI space, spatial smoothing (4 mm FWHM), and high-pass temporal filtering.

Condition-specific responses were estimated using a voxel-wise GLM with separate regressors for each of the eight experimental conditions. Events were modelled as 2-s boxcars convolved with a canonical HRF. Motion parameters were included as nuisance regressors, and temporal autocorrelation was addressed via pre-whitening. Condition-specific beta maps were combined across runs using fixed-effects models and served as input for representational analyses.

RSA ANALYSES

RSA was performed on regions of interest (ROIs) belonging to the AON and the MENTA, defined using meta-analytic functional masks parcellated with the AAL3 atlas for a total of 9 pairs of homologue ROIs (left-right hemisphere; cf. Figure 1A, bottom). For each participant and ROI, representational dissimilarity matrices (RDMs; 8 × 8) were computed from multivoxel beta patterns using correlation distance (cf. Figure 1B, top).

A ‘Neural RSA’ (Figure 1B, bottom) was used to assess representational connectivity, indexing covariance of pattern modulation during the task across regions. Subject-specific ROI–ROI correlations were Fisher z-transformed and tested at the group level with FDR correction. To characterise large-scale representational organisation, hierarchical clustering and dimensionality reduction were applied to the average neural RDMs, allowing identification of similarity clusters and visualisation of network topology.

A ’Model RSA’ (Figure 1B, bottom) was used to assess the selectivity of neural representations for engagement-relevant features. Neural RDMs were compared to four model RDMs: three conceptual models capturing GOAL, PERSPECTIVE, and GAZE, and one behavioural model derived from post-scan engagement ratings (Mahalanobis distance). Because conceptual models were non-orthogonal, neural–model correspondences were estimated using partial correlations to isolate each feature’s unique contribution. Correlation coefficients were Fisher z-transformed prior to group-level inference.

Statistical inference combined one-tailed t-tests with FDR correction and Bayesian t-tests to quantify evidence for both effects and null results. Noise ceilings were estimated to assess the reliability of neural RDMs and contextualize model fits.

Results

NOISE CEILING AND ENGAGEMENT RATINGS:

Noise ceiling estimates indicated reliable representational geometry across participants. Upper bounds of ROI neural RDMs ranged from r = 0.64 (left TPJ) to r = 0.77 (right dmPFC), supporting the interpretability of subsequent RSA results.

Participants’ engagement ratings were highly consistent, as indexed by Mahalanobis distances well below the χ² cutoff across stimuli (mean ≈ 1.5) and the few and unsystematic outliers. The engagement-rating RDM showed a categorical separation between PASS–second-person actions and all other conditions (cf. Figure 1A, middle, pink square). 

NEURAL RSA (Figure 2A);

The Neural RSA revealed three key features of neural similarity across AON and MENTA regions: 

The cluster analysis revealed six similarity clusters. Two were left-lateralized, one linking the left IPL with the Superior Temporal ROI (yellow), and another combining the left TPJ with BA45 (green). A broad, inter-hemispheric parieto-frontal cluster (cyan) joined the right dfAtt and IPS with the left IPS, BA44, and premotor area. Two predominantly right-lateralized clusters emerged, with one cluster grouping the ST, IPL, and BA45 (indigo), and another bringing together the TPJ, premotor area, and BA44 with the left dfAtt (pink). Finally, the dmPFC formed an isolated cluster (red). 

MODEL RSA (Figure 2B):

The ‘Perspective’ model yielded the strongest and most consistent correlations, with robust Bayesian evidence across both MENTA and AON regions. In particular, the right TPJ, left premotor area, and left dorsofrontal Attention ROI showed the most reliable correspondence with this model.

The ’Goal’ model showed the strongest correlations and trends with left-lateralized, posterior ROIs, that is the left IPL, TPJ, and the Superior Temporal area. Bayesian analysis also highlighted evidence for a significant correlation between the ‘Goal’ model and the right IPL. 

The ‘Gaze’ model yielded no significant results, indicating that gaze visibility was not a major factor affecting neural patterns during the task. Weak correlations in the left dmPFC and left Superior Temporal area were accompanied by strong Bayesian evidence for absence, suggesting that gaze visibility did not shape representational patterns in these ROIs.

Finally, the ‘Engagement Rating’ model also had no significant results. However, differently from the ‘Gaze’ model, it yielded statistical trends in a right-lateralized cluster of areas which included the right TPJ, right IPL, right Premotor ROI, and the right dfAtt. Interestingly, these areas also display proximity in terms of similarity patterns as highlighted by the clustering analysis (see Figure 2B, pink heatbar). Nonetheless, Bayesian evidence for the Engagement model was anecdotal in these ROIs (≈1). 

Conclusion

While extensive research has examined how the AON and the MENTA encode specific features of others’ behavior, less attention has been devoted to a critical dimension of social stimuli: their potential to engage the observer. Building on previous work showing that potential interaction modulates social processing, we investigated how engagement-relevant cues - Goal, Perspective, and Gaze - shape neural representations during passive action observation.

Using RSA, we addressed three questions: whether AON and MENTA regions show representational connectivity; whether regions exhibit feature-specific representational profiles; and whether engagement itself constitutes a distinct representational dimension. Our results converge on three main findings.

First, Neural RSA revealed a marked dissociation within MENTA. The TPJ, but not the dmPFC, showed robust representational similarity with frontoparietal AON regions. In particular, the right TPJ exhibited strong covariance with premotor and parietal cortices, whereas the dmPFC was representationally isolated, clustering only with its contralateral homologue. This dissociation suggests that TPJ and dmPFC play fundamentally different roles in processing engagement-related action information. While the TPJ appears integrated within sensorimotor representational circuits, dmPFC recruitment may depend on richer interactive demands, such as reciprocal exchange, sustained joint attention, or strategic monitoring - conditions not present in our passive task.

Second, clustering and model-based RSA revealed systematic feature selectivity across regions. Left-lateralized temporo-parietal regions, including the IPL, ST, and left TPJ, aligned with the Goal model, consistent with their established role in action goal encoding. By contrast, the most robust representational effects were driven by action Perspective. The Perspective model reliably explained representational geometry in the left premotor cortex, right TPJ, and left dorsofrontal attention region, indicating that action direction toward the observer constitutes a dominant social affordance. 

Premotor perspective tuning aligns with evidence from recent mirror neuron research, supporting the view that observed actions are mapped as potential motor affordances4. Perspective-based coding in the right TPJ supports accounts linking this region to self–other perspective processing. Importantly, TPJ-premotor representational coupling emerged even during passive observation, extending prior work that emphasized interactive or task-driven contexts. This suggests that coupling between mentalizing and sensorimotor systems can arise from directional cues alone, without explicit interaction.

Third, engagement did not emerge as a distinct representational dimension. The Engagement Ratings model showed only weak, right-lateralized trends and anecdotal Bayesian evidence. Similarly, the Gaze model yielded evidence for absence. These null results likely reflect task constraints: passive viewing, repetitive stimuli, minimal gaze manipulation, and instructions emphasizing action goals may have reduced sensitivity to subjective engagement or to gaze monitoring. More broadly, our findings suggest that engagement is not encoded as an abstract feature but instead emerges from the integration of goal and directional cues within a distributed sensorimotor network.

Overall, our results support a distributed, affordance-based account of engagement encoding. They identify the TPJ - particularly the right TPJ - as a key interface linking mentalizing and sensorimotor systems, while clarifying that dmPFC involvement may depend on richer, reciprocal interaction. Future studies employing interactive paradigms and dynamic social exchanges will be crucial to further delineate these representational roles.
