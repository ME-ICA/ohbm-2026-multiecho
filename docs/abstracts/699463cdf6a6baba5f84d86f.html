Introduction

Misophonia is an impairing and understudied condition characterized by decreased tolerance to specific sounds or related sensory cues. Often emerging in childhood or adolescence and affecting an estimated 5% of the population,1 misophonia is associated with reduced quality of life, heightened anxiety, and increased risk of self-harm. Despite its clinical impact, it is not yet included in major diagnostic systems, and its underlying mechanisms and diagnostic boundaries continue to be refined.

Although commonly conceptualized as a disorder of sound intolerance, growing evidence indicates that visual cues, such as observing someone chew or seeing repetitive movements, can also elicit distress and, in some cases, heighten responses to auditory triggers.2 These findings raise important questions about whether auditory and visual triggers engage shared neural mechanisms or operate through modality-specific pathways, a distinction that has significant theoretical and clinical implications.

Auditory mental imagery provides a means to probe how internally generated sound representations contribute to misophonic reactions, testing whether responses depend solely on external input or also on endogenous auditory processes. Prior work shows that mental imagery of misophonic triggers elicits heightened autonomic and facial-muscle reactivity, indicating that internal representations can drive misophonic physiology similarly to external stimuli2. Yet it remains unknown whether mental imagery engages the same neural representations and perceptual mechanisms as actual sound perception.

Here, we investigate how visual components of trigger stimuli and auditory mental imagery contribute to affective and neural responses in individuals with misophonia, with the goal of clarifying the multisensory and internally generated processes that shape misophonic experiences.

Methods

Forty participants with misophonia and twenty-six age-, biological sex–, and handedness-matched controls participated in the study in accordance with the Institutional Review Board at the University of South Carolina. Participants with clinical or subclinical misophonia were recruited based on elevated scores on the Duke-Vanderbilt Misophonia Screening Questionnaire (DVMSQ).3 Misophonia participants were excluded if their triggers were tied to a specific individual (e.g., only a sibling chewing), if their triggers did not include a clear visual component (e.g., whispering from an adjacent room), or if they were unable to tolerate at least five seconds of trigger stimulation. All participants completed the Selective Sound Sensitivity Syndrome Scale (S-Five).4 Individualized trigger stimuli were generated for each misophonia participant,5 and these same stimuli were presented to their matched control counterparts in identical order. Data for the three stimulus modalities were collected in separate scanning sessions, with session order counterbalanced across participants. Participants either listened to sounds, watched silent videos, or generated auditory mental imagery of trigger, aversive, or non-aversive stimuli. A block design was used. Each block consisted of either three sounds, three videos, or two mental imagery prompts presented for a total of 15 seconds, followed by a 4-second rating period (Figure 1). Participants in the misophonia group rated their level of distress, whereas control participants rated how antisocial the stimulus was, using a scale from 1 (not at all) to 4 (extremely).

Functional images were acquired using a T2*-weighted multiband EPI sequence (TR = 1.5 s, TE = 30 ms, flip angle = 68°, matrix size = 70 × 70, field of view = 210 mm, voxel size = 3 mm³) with 50 interleaved axial slices and a multiband acceleration factor of 2. Anatomical images were de-identified using PyDeface by removing facial structures. Functional MRI data were preprocessed using fMRIPrep v25.1.4’s standard pipeline, with slice-timing correction omitted and TOPUP fieldmap correction applied. The resulting images were spatially smoothed with a 6 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel in SPM12. First-level analyses included nuisance regressors for 12 head-motion parameters, mean CSF, mean white matter, framewise displacement, and derivative of RMS variance over voxels (DVARS). Trials designed to elicit misophonia that received a participant distress rating of “not at all” were excluded from the model. Whole-brain voxel-wise general linear model (GLM) was conducted to model the neural activation in response to trigger, aversive, and non-aversive stimuli for audio, video, and mental imagery. We examined the contrast Trigger vs. Aversive separately for audio, video, and imagery to localize the brain regions differentiating trigger stimuli from general aversive stimuli for each sensory modality. We evaluated group-level activation patterns within the misophonia group and group differences between misophonia and control participants across the whole brain. Additionally, to investigate whether activation differences were associated with misophonia severity, we conducted a second-level GLM to examine the brain regions whose neural activation increases as the S-Five total severity scores increase across misophonia and control participants, controlling for age and sex. Both first-level and second-level analyses were performed using SPM12. All results were thresholded at voxel level p<.001, and cluster-size based correction was applied.

Results

For misophonia participants only, we found significantly higher activation in bilateral insula, anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), supramarginal gyrus (SMG), and middle frontal gyrus (MFG), as well as significantly lower activation in bilateral precuneus (PreCun) and parahippocampal gyrus (PHG) for trigger compared to aversive stimuli, for all three modalities. Moreover, we observed significantly lower activation in bilateral superior temporal gyrus (STG) for audio triggers and significant lower activation in bilateral occipital cortices for video triggers compared to aversive stimuli.

For the group difference, we found significantly higher activation in bilateral insula and SMA, as well as right SMG for misophonia participants compared to control participants, when processing auditory triggers compared to aversive stimuli. Across the entire sample, higher S-Five total score was associated with heightened activation in bilateral insula, SMA, and thalamus, as well as right SMG, for audio triggers compared to aversive stimuli. Similar trends in those regions of group differences and the association with severity scores were observed for the visual and imagery modality. However, no clusters survived the multiple comparison corrections for these two modalities.

Conclusion

This study provides new evidence that misophonia involves heightened neural and affective responses not only to auditory triggers but also to corresponding visual cues and internally generated auditory representations. Across all three modalities, sound, silent video, and auditory mental imagery, individuals with misophonia showed a consistent pattern of increased activation in regions central to salience detection and affective processing, including the bilateral insula, ACC, SMG, and MFG, alongside reduced activation in posterior midline structures such as the PreCun and PHG. These findings support models positioning misophonia as a condition of aberrant salience attribution and self-referential or contextual regulation rather than a purely auditory sensory intolerance.

Modality-specific effects further refine this interpretation. Reduced STG activation during auditory triggers and reduced occipital activation during visual triggers suggest that individuals with misophonia may show decreased engagement of primary sensory cortices when confronted with personally salient trigger stimuli. This pattern may reflect a shift from modality-specific perceptual processing toward heightened affective and integrative network engagement. Importantly, auditory imagery of trigger sounds elicited neural responses similar in direction to those generated by real sensory input, indicating that misophonia-related distress can arise from internally generated representations, not solely from external stimuli. This finding has meaningful implications for understanding intrusive trigger-related thoughts and for developing imagery-based therapeutic strategies.

Group comparisons revealed that, relative to matched controls, individuals with misophonia exhibit exaggerated activation in the insula, SMA, and right SMG during auditory trigger processing, highlighting dysregulation in circuits involved in interoception, action preparation, and sensorimotor integration. Moreover, symptom severity was positively associated with activation in these same regions and in the thalamus, underscoring a dimensional relationship between neural responsivity and clinical impairment. Although similar patterns emerged for visual and imagery conditions, these effects did not survive correction, suggesting that stimuli and design modification can be warranted.

Taken together, these findings advance current understanding of misophonia by demonstrating that the condition engages a distributed network supporting salience detection, affective reactivity, and multimodal integration across auditory, visual, and mental imagery domains. They underscore the importance of considering misophonia as a multisensory and representational phenomenon, with implications for classification, assessment, and intervention. Future research should continue to refine the boundaries between sensory-driven and imagery-driven responses, examine the temporal dynamics of network engagement, and test whether targeted modulation of salience or interoceptive systems can reduce distress in individuals with misophonia.
