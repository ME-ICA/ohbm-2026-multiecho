Introduction

Psychological flexibility refers to the ability to adapt to changing contexts, balance competing desires, and remain open to experiences while staying committed to one's values. It supports adaptive emotion regulation, resilience, and well-being, whereas reduced flexibility is linked to anxiety, depression, experiential avoidance, and rigid cognitive-affective patterns.1 The most widely used measure of psychological flexibility, the Acceptance and Action Questionnaire-2 (AAQ-2), captures experiential avoidance and difficulties engaging with aversive internal states.2 Despite its clinical importance, surprisingly little is known about the neural mechanisms underlying psychological flexibility, and MRI studies directly linking AAQ-II scores to brain function remain scarce. Existing neuroimaging work indicates that regulating emotional memories and future-oriented imagery engages prefrontal control regions and the posterior cingulate cortex (PCC), a default mode hub implicated in self-referential processing.3 To examine these mechanisms, paradigms that contrast imagination and suppression of aversive content—such as the Imagine/No-Imagine (INI) task—allow for the investigation of how individuals regulate personally relevant future fears. The present study addresses the lack of MRI evidence on psychological flexibility by examining the neural correlates and network dynamics of psychological flexibility and investigating whether AAQ-II scores are related to neural responses during the imagination of feared future events.

Methods

54 participants (33 females, 21 males; mean age = 24.19 years, SD = 2.29, range = 21–31) were included in the study, approved by the local Ethics Committee. MRI data were collected using a 3T Siemens Tim Trio scanner with a 32-channel head coil. Functional imaging utilized T2*-weighted echo-planar imaging with multiband sequences for shorter TRs. Participants performed the INI task in a pseudo-randomized block design during fMRI scanning. Preprocessing with fmriprep4 included motion correction, CompCor noise components, and 8 mm FWHM smoothing. Single-subject contrasts for imagine > suppress and suppress > imagine were calculated using a General Linear Model. Group analyses employed z-standardized contrasts in a one-sample t-test with clusters (>15 voxels) identified via the DIFUMO atlas (threshold: p < 0.001). Psychological flexibility was assessed using the AAQ-2, and linear regression was employed to examine the relationship between brain activity and AAQ-2 scores. Dynamic Causal Modelling (DCM)5 analyzed interregional connectivity during suppression and imagination, correlating connectivity strengths with AAQ-2 scores.

Results

During imagination (imagine > suppress), clusters were found in the bilateral PCC and ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC). Linear regression revealed a significant negative correlation between PCC activity and AAQ-2 scores, indicating higher PCC activity in individuals with greater psychological flexibility (right PCC: slope: -4.97; intercept: 32.13; r: -0.364; p = 0.007; left PCC: slope: -5.94; intercept: 34.91; r: -0.337; p = 0.013). For suppression (suppress > imagine), clusters emerged in the inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), middle frontal gyrus (MFG), superior parietal lobule (SPL), and superior occipital sulcus (SOS). DCM revealed condition-specific connectivity patterns: positive MFG connections to other regions during suppression and strong PCC-vmPFC coupling during imagination, both with negative self-regulation. However, connectivity parameters were not significantly correlated with AAQ-2 scores.

Conclusion

This study identifies distinct neural mechanisms underlying psychological flexibility during suppression and imagination of future threats. PCC and vmPFC activity during imagination reflects self-referential and emotional processing, with PCC activity correlating with greater psychological flexibility. Although PCC activation is often linked to rumination, it may also reflect adaptive meaning-making processes that support acceptance-based regulation. Suppression engaged executive control regions (IFG, MFG, SPL), highlighting cognitive inhibition processes. DCM results revealed condition-specific interactions between self-referential and executive control networks. By identifying PCC engagement as a neural correlate of flexibility, this study suggests a mechanism through which flexibility supports adaptive emotion regulation and resilience. More broadly, these results highlight psychological flexibility as a key factor in affective disorders: flexible individuals may engage neural systems that buffer against rigid, avoidance-based processing, whereas inflexibility may foster maladaptive pathways that increase vulnerability to persistent anxiety and depression.
