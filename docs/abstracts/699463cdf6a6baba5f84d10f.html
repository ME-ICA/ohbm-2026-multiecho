Introduction

Resting-state fMRI networks (RSNs) can be employed to increase our understanding of the functional organization of the healthy brain and to investigate how this organization is disrupted across distinct brain disorders. In contrast to task-based fMRI, resting-state acquisitions lack an experimental design and therefore rely on data-driven methods. Among these, independent component analysis (ICA) combined with dual regression (DR) has been demonstrated to be an effective model-free approach for assessing group differences in RSNs.1 Despite growing evidence of RSN alterations in schizophrenia, challenges remain regarding the reproducibility and consistency of findings across studies. The aim of this study was to design a pipeline to identify significant group differences and to evaluate the reproducibility of these findings by comparing them with results reported in the literature, thereby contributing to the establishment of potential biomarkers for schizophrenia.

Methods

The dataset analyzed originates from the UCLA Consortium for Neuropsychiatric Phenomics LA5c (open-access, OpenNeuro repository). It comprises 121 healthy controls (HC) and 50 patients with schizophrenia (SZ), both with age-range of 21-50 years (M=32.9, SD=9.0) and with education level-matched. fMRI data were acquired in a 3T Siemens Trio scanner with an EPI protocol (slice thickness=4mm, 34 slices, TR=2s, TE=30ms, flip angle=90°, matrix 64×64, FOV=192mm, oblique slice orientation). During the rs-fMRI scan, the participants were instructed to relax and keep their eyes open. Additionally, a T1-weighted MPRAGE image was collected (TR=1.9s, TE=2.26ms, FOV=250mm, matrix=256×256, sagittal plane, slice thickness=1mm, 176 slices) and defaced for subject anonymity.2 Data analysis was performed using FMRIB Software Library FSL. The first two volumes of each individual functional scan were discarded to ensure magnetization equilibrium. Standard preprocessing included: slice timing, motion correction, removal of non-brain tissues, normalization to MNI152 template, high-pass filtering (0.01Hz), spatial smoothing (5mm FWHM), resampling to 2x2x2m3 voxel size, ICA decomposition with automatic dimensionality and signal denoising using FMRIB's ICA-based Xnoiseifier (FIX). Then, cleaned rs-fMRI data from all subjects were temporally concatenated and submitted to MELODIC ICA with limited dimensionality (20 ICs). The resulting group-level components were correlated with a reference study3 and the significant RSNs (Pearson’s r > 0.25) were used as predictors to derive individual-level component timeseries and associated spatial maps (DR). Between-group differences were determined using nonparametric permutation testing (5000 permutations) and threshold-free cluster enhancement (TFCE) approach to control multiple comparisons (p<0.05). Brain areas were identifying in FSLeyes with Harvard-Oxford cortical and subcortical atlases (Figure 1).

Results

Following the group ICA, seven RSNs were identified through correlation with the reference study: visual (VN), default mode (DMN), sensorimotor (SMN), cerebellum (CN), auditory (AN), executive control (ECN) and frontoparietal (FPN) networks. Six of these networks showed statistically significant differences between HC and SZ following stage 3 of the DR analysis. In comparison with the HC group, the SZ group exhibited increased functional connectivity within the posterior DMN, particularly in the precuneus, as well as hyperconnectivity of this network with left-lateralized sensory-processing areas (Figure 2a). On the other hand, patients exhibited reduced activation within the VN (lateral portion and occipital pole), the SMN, and the ECN, including the bilateral frontal pole, frontal medial cortex, and the left paracingulate gyrus. Patients also revealed hypoconnectivity between the right putamen and the SMN, as well as between the left putamen and the AN (Figure 2b). These findings area consisted with recent studies indicating that hyperactivation in the posterior default mode network (DMN) may be associated with the misattribution of thoughts and internal experiences characteristic of psychosis, including hallucinations.4 Simultaneously, reduced functional connectivity in prefrontal regions and hypoconnectivity between the putamen and sensorimotor areas may reflect negative symptoms and cognitive impairments commonly associated with schizophrenia.5

Conclusion

The present findings indicate that RSNs are differentially affected in schizophrenia, with both hyper- and hypoconnectivity patterns emerging across sensory, motor, and higher-order cognitive systems. Alterations in the posterior default mode network, sensorimotor circuits, and prefrontal regions converge with previous evidence, reinforcing the notion that disruptions in large-scale network organization underlie core symptoms of the disorder. Importantly, although previous studies have reported diverse and sometimes conflicting results, the reproducibility of these findings across independent investigations highlights the potential of aberrant connectivity in higher-level networks as reliable biomarkers.
