Introduction

Autism is characterised by marked neurobiological and clinical heterogeneity, which often limits the sensitivity of traditional group comparisons. It is increasingly evident from studies in both neuroimaging and genetics that behavioral definitions only partially describe autism with considerable variation present in biological mechanisms. This suggests that autism reflects a combination of interacting mechanisms rather than a single defining feature.1  

Capturing the diversity of developmental trajectories in autism remains a challenge for neuroimaging research. Normative modelling offers a framework for addressing this challenge by quantifying individual brain measure deviations from typical developmental patterns.2 Recent methodological advances have made normative modelling a robust tool for studying individual differences in large, heterogeneous datasets. Longitudinal normative modelling offers a way to capture heterogeneity and to describe an individual’s brain–behaviour relationships beyond diagnostic labels. 

The study of white matter has helped illustrate how abnormal brain connections may be related to various behavioural and cognitive symptoms exhibited in individuals with autism. Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), specifically fractional anisotropy (FA), serves as a measure of microstructural organisation.3  

The goal of the present study is to characterise individual differences in white-matter development in autism using longitudinal diffusion MRI data from LEAP. Specifically, we aim to (i) quantify individual deviations in white-matter microstructure using diffusion-based normative models of FA within the LEAP cohort; (ii) examine if these deviations differ between the autism and no-autism groups or relate to clinical features within the autism group; and to (iii) assess longitudinal changes in white-matter across LEAP’s multiple timepoints.

Methods

The data were obtained from the EU-AIMS Longitudinal European Autism Project (LEAP4), a multicentre programme designed for identifying biological markers of autism across development. Data were collected at five European centres and included 544 unique individuals (344 at Wave 1, 297 at Wave 2, and 226 at Wave 3). The mean intervals between waves were approximately 1.5 years (Wave 1–2) and 6 years (Wave 1–3). 

Single-shell diffusion-weighted data (b = 0/1500 s/mm², 60 directions, 2 mm isotropic voxels) were acquired using echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequences. Preprocessing included noise reduction, motion and eddy-current correction, and susceptibility distortion correction using FSL’s EDDY. Diffusion tensors were fitted with DTIfit to generate FA maps, which were processed using Tract-Based Spatial Statistics (TBSS). White matter tracts were segmented using the Johns Hopkins University (JHU) white matter atlas, yielding 48 tracts for which mean FA values were extracted.

We used normative modelling to quantify individual deviations in white-matter organisation relative to population expectations. For each of the 48 tracts in the JHU atlas, pre-trained warped Bayesian Linear Regression models were applied to predict expected FA values conditional on age, sex, and site. To model non-linear age effects, this approach involved applying a third-order polynomial B-spline basis expansion over age, with five evenly spaced knots, combined with a SinhArcsinh warping function. To harmonise model predictions across sites, small wave-specific adaptation sets of control participants were used to recalibrate normative model intercepts and variance estimates

Clinical and behavioural assessments followed the EU-AIMS LEAP protocol. For this study, we selected measures consistently available across all three waves: These included the Full-Scale Intelligence Quotient II (FSIQ2), the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule, Second Edition (ADOS-2) Social Affect calibrated severity score (SA-CSS), the ADOS-2 Restricted and Repetitive Behaviours score (RRB-CSS), and the ADOS-2 Total Calibrated Severity Score (CSS Total); the Social Responsiveness Scale (SRS); the Repetitive Behaviours Scale–Revised (RBS-R); the Short Sensory Profile (SSP); and the Vineland Adaptive Behaviour Scales, Second Edition (VABS-II), including the Adaptive Behaviour Composite (ABC), Communication, Daily Living Skills, and Socialisation domains. To handle incomplete data, we developed a hierarchical, multi-step imputation approach that accounted for structured missingness (design-driven absences) and random missingness (incidental loss or incomplete assessments) based on the Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) software.

Analyses were performed separately for cross-sectional (Z) and pair-wise longitudinal (Z-diff5) scores across the three LEAP waves. Group mean differences were tested for using two-tailed t-tests, while the proportion of extreme deviations (|Z| > 2.6; p ≈ 0.01) per group were compared using Mann–Whitney U-tests for the proportion of extreme cases, with Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction across tracts (n=48).

To examine multivariate brain-behaviour associations, we used penalised canonical correlation analysis (CCA) linking clinical measures and FA deviations. Model performance was evaluated out-of-sample through ten train–test (70/30) splits, and significance was determined via 1,000 permutations of the clinical data. Feature stability was quantified as the proportion of splits in which each variable showed non-zero weight. For longitudinal analyses, the difference between visit pairs were used along with Z-diff scores.  We also conducted univariate correlations between clinical and Z scores.

Results

The normative models were successfully adapted to the LEAP data and fitted across all 48 white matter tracts (JHU) (45), modelling FA trajectories across age (Figure 2A - selected centile plots). The model fits were similar across tracts, with mean explained variance of ~0.44–0.50 for Waves 1–2 and 0.12 for Wave 3. The z-score distributions were close to symmetrical (mean skew ≈ 0) but showed moderate positive kurtosis (≈ 0.8–3.8).

In the group comparison analyses, a few tracts (including the cerebral peduncles, callosal regions, and external capsule) showed nominal group differences across cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses. However, no effects remained significant after FDR correction, indicating no reliable case–control FA differences. 

Cross-sectional CCA results reveal significant out-of-sample correlations between white matter FA deviations and the clinical measures for Waves 1 and 2 but not Wave 3 within the autism group. In Wave 1 the analysis shows r = 0.26 ± 0.11 with p = 0.001, consistently selecting FA loadings in the cingulum L, anterior limb of the internal capsule R, external capsule L, and genu of the corpus callosum for the first component. Wave 2 showed a similar pattern (C1: r = 0.32 ± 0.10, p = 0.001), consistently selecting the bilateral cingulum, superior longitudinal fasciculus, splenium of the corpus callosum, and cerebral peduncles for the first component. Although no significant components were found in Wave 3 (all p ≥ 0.05), the stability analysis still highlighted several of the same FA tracts as in the earlier waves. Clinical variables did now show significant stability. Across the longitudinal analyses, only the Wave 1-3 comparison had significant canonical correlations. Component 1 showed a moderate association (r = 0.36 ± 0.17, p = 0.001) and included stable FA Z-diff loadings in several projection and association tracts, such as the genu of the corpus callosum, left inferior cerebellar peduncle, bilateral anterior corona radiata, right sagittal stratum, bilateral external capsule, left uncinate fasciculus, and right tapetum. This was also the only analysis in which clinical variables were stably selected, specifically ΔCSS total and ΔSA-CSS. 

To identify the univariate associations with symptoms within the autism group. Pearson correlations were computed and pooled across ten imputations using Rubin’s rules, with FDR correction applied within the clinical family. The cross-sectional analyses showed significant associations between Z-scores and multiple clinical dimensions across waves. In Wave 1, sensory processing scores (SSP) and restricted behaviours (ADOS-RRB) showed widespread negative associations, while adaptive communication (VABS) exhibited positive correlations. Wave 2 showed widespread correlations between VABS and many of the same tracts from in Wave 1 but also parts of the internal capsule, thalamic radiation and sagittal stratum. By Wave 3, associations persisted but were weaker and less robust after correction. Longitudinally, we observed less pronounced effects, with only a few nominal associations emerging between changes in clinical scores and Z-diff scores. Wave 2-3 comparisons showed nominal correlations in ADOS scores across cingulate and projection pathways, with one tract (left superior fronto-occipital fasciculus) surviving FDR correction. Moderate ADOS correlation patterns were also seen in Wave 1-3 for fronto-limbic and thalamic pathways. The pontine crossing tract emerged as a key region for longitudinal change.

Conclusion

In this study we demonstrated extensive associations between individual variation in white-matter organisation and autism clinical features, in the absence of group-level differences between autistic and non-autistic individuals. This suggests that white matter alterations in autism follow a dimensional and developmentally stable pattern. Using normative modelling and multivariate analysis, we found that individual deviations within shared fronto-limbic, callosal, and cerebellar-thalamo-cortical circuits provide the strongest link to adaptive, sensory, and behavioural traits. Rather than reflecting categorical group differences, these findings point to consistent yet individually patterned white-matter architecture across development. While larger and more complete longitudinal datasets will be essential to deepen this understanding, our results offer a clear framework for studying heterogeneity in autism.

Our first conclusion is that white matter alterations in autism are of a dimensional nature and are not entirely associated with diagnosis. Using normative modelling we were able to reveal significant correlations between individual deviations in white matter integrity and clinical traits in autism. This finding is supported by the CCA results showing stable component loadings in the cingulum, corpus callosum, and cerebellar tracts across Waves 1 and 2, by the pooled correlations revealing direction-specific associations between FA deviations and sensory (SSP), adaptive (VABS), and behavioural (ADOS) traits; and by the absence of significant group-mean FA differences despite clear within-group dimensional effects. Therefore, we demonstrate that autism is characterised by stable yet individually patterned white matter organisation, consistent with the broader view of neurodevelopmental diversity as a continuum rather than a dichotomy.

Second, we show that the white matter pathways associated with clinical traits in autism are stable across cross-sectional analysis. We see consistent patterns across waves resulting from our analyses: Vineland Adaptive Scores (specifically communication and total subscale scores) positively correlate with tracts such as the cingulum, superior longitudinal fasciculus, corpus callosum, internal capsule, and cerebellar peduncles; however, ADOS scores display both mixed and recurrent associations, positive for restricted behaviours and negative for social domains, in the fronto-limbic and cerebellar regions. We observed these patterns across all developmental stages and waves, even though the statistical significance varies due to variations in sample size. These findings suggest that while each person's brain profile is unique, certain neurostructural signatures for sensory versus adaptive functioning do replicate consistently. 

Third, autism-related white matter circuits show longitudinal stability, linking brain and behavioural change across development. Using white matter microstructural differences as markers for clinical trait changes and comparing these across waves, we demonstrated that the same fronto-limbic, callosal, and cerebellar-thalamo-cortical systems were also significant longitudinally. By using this difference-vs-differences approach, the Wave 1-3 pair shows that tracts including the inferior cerebellar peduncle, genu of the corpus callosum, external capsule, and anterior corona radiata still showed associations with behaviour change, with ADOS and repetitive behaviours scores showing negative widespread associations particularly in the pntine crossing trac. These findings providing evidence that the involved circuits do not represent transient findings, but an ongoing relationship between evolving brain structure and autistic traits over time.
