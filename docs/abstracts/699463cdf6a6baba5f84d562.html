Introduction

Cognitive training aims to improve cognitive skills, but training-related gains vary in amount and scope across individual participants. Hence, it has become imperative to identify individual characteristics at baseline that are associated with individual differences in training gains. One such potential factor is the degree of modular organization of functional brain networks, defined as the extent to which brain regions are more strongly connected to regions within the same functional network than to regions outside of the network. In adults, more modular organization of functional brain networks at baseline has been found to be associated with greater benefits of cognitive training.1 As network integration and segregation, two key aspects of modularity, continue to change during middle childhood, we asked whether network modularity would also be associated with training-related performance gains in children undergoing task-switching training.

Methods

Children aged 8 to 11 years completed nine weeks of either high-intensity task-switching training (17% single tasking blocks, 83% task-switching blocks per training session; N = 42; 21 girls, 21 boys; mean age = 9.98, SD = 0.69) or high-intensity single-task training (83% single tasking blocks, 17% task-switching blocks per training session; N = 42; 22 girls, 20 boys; mean age = 9.88, SD = 0.72). We acquired functional MRI data during the performance of a task-switching paradigm at four timepoints: before the start of training, after approximately three weeks, after approximately six weeks, and at the end of training. We regressed out the main effects of the task on the BOLD timeseries using finite impulse response general linear models before constructing the functional connectomes. Functional brain networks were based on a network parcellation of children that assigned 400 brain parcels to one of seven major functional networks (visual, somatomotor, dorsal attention, ventral attention, limbic, frontoparietal, default mode).2 We calculated unweighted modularity of these networks at five thresholds (2%, 4%, 6%, 8%, and 10%) of the connectivity matrix. We measured task-switching performance using linear-integrated speed accuracy score (LISAS)3 to account for potential speed-accuracy tradeoffs. We then used Bayesian linear mixed models to test for training-related changes in LISAS and in network modularity, and to relate pre-training modularity and change in modularity to training-related changes in LISAS. Finally, we tested whether children showed the expected pattern of less modular networks compared to adults who did not undergo any training (N = 53; mean age = 24.7, SD = 2.6; 28 women, 25 men).

Results

As expected based on prior research, children (mean [across thresholds] = 0.33, SD = 0.094) showed overall less modular networks at the pre-training session (mean [across thresholds] = 0.33, SD = 0.094) than adults (mean [across thresholds] = 0.42, SD = 0.071; pFDR < .001 for each threshold, FDR-corrected for multiple comparisons). With training, LISAS decreased indicating that task-switching performance improved (linear effect of session: est. = –0.13; 95%-CI –0.19, –0.06; quadratic effect of session: est. = 0.03; 95%-CI 0.01, 0.06), especially in the high-intensity task-switching group (linear effect of session x group x condition [single vs. repeat]: est. = 0.12; 95%-CI 0.00, 0.24). The modularity of network organization increased with training across both training groups (2%-threshold: est. = 0.02; 95%-CI 0.01, 0.03; 10%-threshold: est. = 0.02; 95%-CI 0.01, 0.04), albeit not consistently across all thresholds used for network definition (i.e., 95%-CIs included zero for the 4%-, 6%-, and 8%-thresholds). More modular organization of functional brain networks (i.e., higher modularity indices) before training was associated with faster improvements in task-switching performance (interaction of pre-training modularity x linear effect of session: est. = –1.19; 95%-CI: –2.03, –0.36). However, the effect of pre-training modularity became less prominent towards the end of training (interaction of pre-training modularity x quadratic effect of session: est. = 0.36; 95%-CI: 0.07, 0.64).

Conclusion

The present findings demonstrate that the modular organization of brain networks is still developing in middle childhood. Moreover, the results suggest that higher modularity of functional brain networks in children is associated with faster training-related improvements, potentially reflecting faster adaptation to training demands. Modular organization is thought to be particularly efficient especially in large-scale networks4 such as the brain, and to make networks more adaptable when environments change frequently,5 such as during task switching. Additionally, modular networks are thought to support learning by preventing the forgetting of previously learned skills and tasks when learning new ones.4 Our results further demonstrate that examining the organization of large-scale brain networks can advance our understanding of individual differences in learning.
