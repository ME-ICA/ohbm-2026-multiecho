Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic autoimmune disorder that affects the central nervous system, leading to inflammation, demyelination, and neurodegeneration. These pathological changes, including neuronal and synaptic loss, may disrupt the brain's excitation/inhibition (E/I) balance, which is essential for maintaining healthy neural function [1]. Although EEG, MEG, and magnetic resonance spectroscopy have been used to estimate E/I balance, functional MRI (fMRI), which is commonly applied in MS research, has not yet been used for this purpose. This study applies a novel multimodal method to estimate E/I balance in individuals with MS using MRI data. It investigates E/I balance both at a global level (over the whole head) and at the local level of specific resting-state networks affected by MS. We focused on two functional networks: the somatomotor network, associated with fatigue in MS, and the default mode network (DMN), associated with cognitive impairment in MS.

Methods

We used the resting-state structural connectivity (rsSC) framework [2], which combines resting-state fMRI and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) to derive a hybrid connectivity matrix. This approach embeds brain structure into function and estimates connectivity that best explains observed functional interactions, even when direct structural links are absent. The rsSC matrix contains positive and negative values, interpreted as excitatory and inhibitory interactions at the macroscale. We quantified E/I balance as the ratio of positive to negative rsSC connections. This metric serves as an indirect estimate of E/I balance, linking microscale synaptic activity to global brain network organization.

We applied this method to data from 12 healthy female controls (HC; age 42 ± 12) and 24 females with multiple sclerosis (MS; age 48 ± 11), matched for sex, age, and years of education. All MS participants met the revised McDonald diagnostic criteria [3]. The dataset was collected at the UZ Brussel hospital collected the dataset using a 3T scanner. 

For global analyses, we computed the E/I metric across all regions of interest. For network-level analyses, we calculated intra-network E/I (within-network connections), inter-network E/I (connections between the network and other regions), and combined E/I (all connections involving the network).

We used the Schaefer atlas for functional parcellation and validated the results using two anatomical atlases: Desikan-Killiany and Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL).

To assess group differences, we used the Mann-Whitney U test and applied false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple comparisons. We treated the choice of atlas as an independent variable and corrected for 9 comparisons within each atlas.

Results

We first assessed the global E/I ratio across the entire brain for each atlas. No significant differences in global E/I were observed between HC and individuals with MS (pwMS), regardless of atlas selection. Effect sizes were small or negligible across conditions.

Within the DMN, we found a significant group difference in intra-network E/I when using the Schaefer atlas, with pwMS showing lower E/I ratios than controls. No significant differences in inter-network or combined E/I were found within the DMN across any atlas. The effect sizes for intra-network E/I were medium for the Schaefer atlas.

For the somatomotor network, we observed similar findings: only intra-network E/I was significantly lower in pwMS compared to HC when using the Schaefer atlas, with a large effect size.

Conclusion

Our findings highlight network-specific alterations in the E/I balance in people with MS, particularly within the DMN and somatomotor network, which were not apparent at the global level.

Previous studies using magnetic resonance spectroscopy have reported decreased levels of GABA (a primary inhibitory neurotransmitter) in DMN regions of MS patients [4]. In contrast, the current study suggests lower intra-network E/I in people with MS, which could reflect either reduced excitatory drive or increased inhibitory tone. We argue that this difference may reflect changes within the network itself, as no significant differences were found in outgoing connections. Moreover, the E/I measure used here reflects large-scale connectivity changes that result from a complex interplay of microscopic phenomena, which may account for the observed differences.

In the somatomotor network, the reduced E/I in people with MS contrasts with findings from animal models, which report a pro-excitatory shift [5]. Similar to the DMN, the changes we observe in the somatomotor network reflect macroscopic alterations in E/I balance that are confined to intra-network connections.

Importantly, our results were robust only when using the Schaefer atlas. Functionally defined parcellations may better align with resting-state network-level phenomena and offer greater sensitivity to network-specific changes in disorders like MS.

This study is limited by a relatively small sample size of the dataset. Future research should validate these findings in larger cohorts and explore E/I dynamics during task-related brain activity. Despite these limitations, rsSC offers a promising, MRI-based proxy for studying E/I balance, which could complement EEG and MRS approaches. Given the widespread availability of fMRI and DWI, rsSC may prove useful in large-scale or clinical studies of MS and other neurological disorders.
