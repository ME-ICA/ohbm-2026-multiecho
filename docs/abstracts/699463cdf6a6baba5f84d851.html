Introduction

Brain activity is shaped and constrained by the underlying anatomy and organization, forming the foundation for human cognition and behavior. However, the precise nature of structure-function relationships across multiple spatial and temporal scales remains incompletely understood. While previous studies have employed MRI to link structural wiring to functional configurations, recent advances in large-scale data consortia now enable leveraging of cellular and molecular metadata for refined predictive models1. Here, we present a nuanced approach to investigate the extent to which structural and neurochemical features can explain regional resting-state activity at a high temporal resolution. Specifically, we aimed to identify the features that are most predictive and uncover biological correlates of age-related changes in brain oscillations.

Methods

We analyzed data from 350 individuals (18–88 years) from the Cambridge Center for Ageing and Neuroscience with a balanced age distribution, measured using magnetoencephalography (MEG) during resting-state eyes-closed. Individual power spectra (1–60 Hz, 1-Hz bins) computed for 200 cortical parcels using the Schaefer-200 parcellation. We additionally focused on temporal autocorrelation of brain signals (1–40 time lags), given the high informativeness on neurophysiological aging2. Five minutes of group-averaged MEG data were used after regressing out the effects of age, sex, and intracranial volume. We applied partial least squares regression (PLSR) and 10-fold cross-validation to predict the averaged power spectrum and autocorrelation, respectively, in 200 brain areas from 55 biological maps (neuromaps3; enigma toolbox4). Power spectra were log-transformed and normalized by subtracting global mean power per parcel to isolate regional deviations from the mean spectrum. Variable importance in projection (VIP) scores identified top predictors. Incremental PLSR tested minimal feature sets by sequentially adding maps based on predictive contribution. We used spin-tests (1,000 permutations) to assess the statistical significance of each model. For age-related effects, individual PLSR models were constructed for each subject using individual deviations from the mean spectra and autocorrelation function, respectively, and beta weights were correlated with chronological age using Pearson correlation.

Results

Overall Prediction Accuracy: The multivariate model predicted regional power spectrum differences with high accuracy (R² = 0.83, pspin < 0.05) and achieved comparable performance in predicting autocorrelation structure  (R² = 0.88, pspin < 0.05). Cytoarchitectural properties (mean neuronal density), gene expression (first principal component), and specific neurotransmitter receptor distributions (5-HT4, opioid, histamine) were among the strongest predictors. Critically, predictor maps exhibited frequency and time-lag specific, opposing relationships. Incremental regressions revealed that approximately 18 maps (power) and 12 maps (autocorrelation), respectively, achieved R²~0.8, matching the full model performance. The key predictors remained similar to the full model, suggesting biological information is distributed but can be retrieved from a limited set of maps.

Age-Related Neurophysiological Changes: Subject-level analyses identified anatomical and molecular correlates of age-related MEG deviations (range individual R² power: 0.66-0.97; range individual R² autocorrelation: 0.93-0.99). The strongest associations between power-related model weights and age were found for cyclooxygenase (COX-1), myelin content, serotonin 1A receptors, evolutionary expansion, and histamine H3 receptors (with an average r > 0.3 across the frequency spectrum, see Figure 1). Age-related autocorrelation changes were primarily predicted by evolutionary expansion, opioid receptors, cerebral blood flow, COX-1, and acetylcholine transporter density, and generally showed a 50% overlap with the top 10 predictors for power. 

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that regional variation in resting-state MEG power spectra and temporal autocorrelation are substantially governed by specific microstructural and neurochemical distributions. The multivariate, holistic approach, taking the full spectrum and autocorrelation function into account, reveals complex relationships between brain structure, neurochemistry, and brain activity. Importantly, we identified markers underpinning neurophysiological aging, implicated, for example, in inflammatory processes and neurodegeneration (COX-1) or basic organizational and structural features (cortical expansion, myelin) known to undergo specific lifespan trajectories.
