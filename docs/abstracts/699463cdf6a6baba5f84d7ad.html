Introduction

As people age, they usually develop some degree of brain frailty, and age remains the leading risk factor associated with stroke. In the UK, approximately 100,000 strokes occur each year, averaging one every five minutes.1 Timely diagnosis and identifying the stroke type are also crucial to the outcome and recovery post-stroke.

MRI is widely used for in vivo diagnosis of brain conditions, providing good tissue contrast for both structural and functional measurements. However, high infrastructure and operational costs limit its accessibility and its suitability for repeated use in research. Field-Cycling Imaging (FCI) is an emerging technology that allows the magnetic field strength to be varied during data acquisition.4 Unlike conventional MRI, images are acquired at multiple magnetic field strengths in a single scan. The behaviour of water protons in the body is dependent on magnetic field strength, and the aim is to observe how these dependencies change between different field strengths.

In this work, we build an SVM ensemble models with an SVM meta learner to automate stroke diagnosis using Field-Cycling Imaging by combining predictions across different field strengths. By making multiple predictions at each magnetic field strength, this approach preserves the structure and consistency of the features observed at each field strength. In this study, the ensemble was evaluated on two tasks: detecting the presence of stroke in brain images using FCI data and distinguishing between haemorrhagic and ischaemic stroke types within the cohort. We examine the effectiveness of this method and its performance compared to individual classifiers across different field strengths

Methods

Dataset

The dataset used in this study is part of the PUFFINS 2 project and comprises thirty participants.2 Thirteen were clinically diagnosed as stroke patients, and seventeen were healthy volunteers. In the stroke group, there were five haemorrhagic and eight ischaemic strokes. All scans were performed using the FCI scanner with images acquired at 0.2 mT, 2.0 mT, 20 mT, and 200 mT. Each scan session uses five distinct evolution times, ranging logarithmically from 5ms to 550ms, to assess the T1 relaxation properties of brain tissues. For each participant, twenty images were acquired per scan. The slice thickness was 10mm, and the in-plane resolution was 3.1 mm x 3.1 mm.  All participants were scanned twice. The first scan was taken about 2-10 days following stroke onset to capture acute-phase features, and a second scan was taken approximately thirty days later. All images were axial views, providing a uniform mid-slice view across subjects. See Senn et al. for more information about the image acquisition details.2 Sample images from the dataset are shown in Figure 1.

Model and training

Preprocessing of the imaging data involved multiple steps, including Motion correction,  denoising to remove scan artefacts using a denoising Convolutional Neural Network (dCNN) in MATLAB 2024, a 90-degree rotation to conform to radiological standards, resizing images to 224 by 224 to fit the feature extractor model, and normalising pixel intensities to between 0 and 1. No further preprocessing was done.

The model was constructed in three steps. First, features were extracted from a pretrained Vision Transformer (ViT) model.3 The ViT 16B 224 by 224 from PyTorch was used for this purpose. Each image was encoded into A 768-dimensional feature vector, representing the learned class tokens or embeddings. The second step was a classification phase, in which a set of SVM models was trained on the generated embeddings. For each field strength, a separate SVM was trained.  In the final step, a separate SVM (meta-learner) was trained on the previous SVM's predictions.

Models were created for two separate tasks. The initial task was to distinguish between participants with and without stroke, and the second task was to differentiate between ischaemic and haemorrhagic stroke types. The dataset was divided into training and test sets by scan visit, with the first visit used for training and the second for testing. During training, the testing set served as a holdout for validation. The classifier's performance was evaluated using accuracy and the Precision-Recall Area Under the Curve (PR-AUC), which is less sensitive to imbalances in category numbers. All Hyperparameters for the models were optimised using the hyperopt library from Python.

Results

Figure 2 summarises the results of applying the models developed on the second visit data. Figure 2 (a) shows the PR- AUC performance on the stroke versus no stroke task for individual field strengths and for the ensemble model that combines all predictions. Overall, the ensemble model shows higher PR- AUC compared to the others with PR-AUC generally increasing as field strength decreases. Regarding accuracy, 80% was achieved with 0.2 mT images, 82% with 2.0 mT images, 74% with 20 mT images, and 77% with 200 mT images, all of which were surpassed by the ensemble, which reached 89% accuracy. These results demonstrate the superiority of using an ensemble in this setup.

Figure 2 (b) compares the PR-AUC performance of the models for differentiating between haemorrhagic and ischaemic stroke. There is no improvement in performance when differentiating between haemorrhagic and ischaemic strokes across all modalities. The data from the lowest magnetic field, 0.2 mT, model achieved a similar PR-AUC as the ensemble model, showing that low magnetic fields are most sensitive to the presence of stroke.  

Conclusion

In this study, we proposed using an ensemble and data generated at multiple low magnetic fields by FCI to detect the presence of stroke in the acute phase. Results showed that while models based on individual field strengths are weak predictors of stroke, combining these individual predictions into an ensemble improves performance for stroke detection and enables discrimination between ischaemic and haemorrhagic stroke types. 

Again, each field strength produced different performance levels, highlighting the unique features associated with each field strength and underscoring the importance of tailoring them to specific brain conditions. Our analysis revealed that all models trained at 200 mT and 20 mT consistently underperform across all metrics compared to those trained at other field strengths. This is particularly evident in distinguishing stroke from non-stroke images. The best results among the field strengths for this task were obtained with 2.0 mT images, which achieved a PR-AUC of 0.87 and an accuracy of 0.82 in differentiating a healthy brain from stroke. Conversely, for the ischaemic versus haemorrhagic task, 0.2 mT images proved superior, with a PR-AUC of 0.96 and an accuracy of 80%. To summarise, no single field strength consistently dominates the results across the two tasks; therefore, combining the different fields proved to be a more effective approach for prediction. Future research will focus on collecting additional data and employing multi-task learning to improve the model's performance.
