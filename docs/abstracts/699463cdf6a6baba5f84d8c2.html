Introduction

Affective disorders (i.e., mood, anxiety) may fundamentally involve disruptions in adaptive emotion processes that arise from aberrant neural network functioning. In particular, prior neuroimaging studies have highlighted a fronto-limbic network, whereby anticipatory activity in the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (dmPFC) both varies with anxiety levels and modulates amygdala responses to threat.1,2 However, clinical translation of this and other prior emotion regulation studies may have been limited by traditional, correlative neuroimaging approaches.3 Further, fMRI-recorded BOLD hemodynamic signals constitute a relatively slow and indirect measure of neural activity.4 Alternatively, stereoelectroencephalography (sEEG) both increases temporal specificity and offers neuromodulation (i.e., disruptive electrical stimulation) in deep brain regions. Thus, replicating and extending prior fMRI work using sEEG offers direct measures of neural activity and enables a novel, causal approach for network mapping of emotion regulation processes.3,4 This study aimed to investigate how disruptive dmPFC stimulation during the anticipation of threat and the response to threat may modulate amygdala reactivity. 

Methods

Eleven patients with medically refractory epilepsy undergoing video-sEEG monitoring in the UAB Epilepsy Monitoring Unit (EMU) completed the experimental protocol between October 2023 and July 2025. A Pavlovian fear conditioning paradigm was presented audio-visually via a computer interface (Figure 1). A pure tone (700 Hz) served as a warning cue, or conditioned stimulus (CS+), and a 100-dB white noise served as a threat, or unconditioned stimulus (UCS). The threat was either presented alone (0.5 s; unpredictable) or co-terminated with the warning cue (8 s; predictable sham), with 10 trials of each type. However, on 5 trials with the warning cue, disruptive stimulation of 20 Hz pulse trains at 2 milliamps was delivered to the closest electrode contact to a predetermined dmPFC target (MNI: -4, 40, 25) two seconds prior to the offset of the CS+ and UCS (predictable stim).5 Preoperative MRIs were co-registered with post-operative CTs to determine precise electrode contact locations in the brain using Reproducible Analysis and Visualization of intracranial EEG (RAVE) and Freesurfer (v 8.1.0). All sEEG data processing was conducted in RAVE. For each trial and frequency within the analysis window, decibel power data (log-transform of power) were transformed into percent signal change from baseline, where baseline was set to 0.5 s before stimulation onset (5.5-6 s). To avoid the inclusion of possible stimulation artifact, analysis was set to 0.5 s after stimulation offset and threat onset (8.5-9 s). Decibel power change in the high-frequency activity (HFA) band (40-150 Hz) and theta band (4-8 Hz) was analyzed. Electrodes in the amygdala were selected for analysis. Linear mixed-effects models were used to assess the main effects of trial type (unpredictable, predictable sham, predictable stim). 

Results

There was a significant effect of trial type on amygdala HFA mean dB power change, χ²(2) = 6.73, p = 0.03. Follow-up contrasts found greater amygdala HFA responses to unpredictable relative to predictable sham threats (0.27 + 0.14, t = 1.96, p < 0.05) and greater amygdala HFA responses to predictable threats with stimulation than to predictable sham threats (0.40 + 0.16, t = 2.52, p = 0.01) (Figure 2A). Linear models did not yield significant theta-band effects. However, a significant positive correlation was found between HFA and theta amygdala activity across all three trial types, r(169) = 0.57, p < 0.001 (Figure 2B).

Conclusion

The present study provides direct causal evidence for a top-down fronto-limbic mechanism underlying human regulation of responses to threat. Prior correlative neuroimaging work has suggested that anticipatory dmPFC activity modulates subsequent amygdala responses to threat.1,2,5 We demonstrate that disruptive stimulation to the dmPFC enhances amygdala threat reactivity, supporting a causal role for the dmPFC in regulating downstream limbic responses. Greater responses to unpredictable relative to predictable sham threats are indicative of effective emotion regulation.1,2,5 This expected pattern supports prior models for regulation of threat responses by replicating effects with a novel technique, while also providing a baseline against which stimulation effects can be meaningfully interpreted. Greater responses to predictable threats with stimulation relative to predictable sham threats indicate that disrupting dmPFC activity during anticipation of the threat enhances amygdala threat reactivity. Further, there was no difference in amygdala activity between predictable threats with stimulation and unpredictable threats, suggesting that disrupting dmPFC activity inhibits the ability to regulate emotional responses to threat. A positive correlation between amygdala HFA and theta across all trial types may reflect consistent coupling between local neuronal activation in the amygdala and theta-mediated network engagement that persists independent of threat anticipation. This pattern may relate to the transfer of information during the threat response, rather than the regulatory processes that support emotion regulation. Overall, our findings demonstrate the utility of sEEG for directly probing brain-behavior relationships during emotion regulation. Given that prior work investigating neurobehavioral responses to threat demonstrates this ability varies with anxiety severity, future work using our validated technique will examine how anxiety symptom expression relates to neuromodulatory influences on the fronto-limbic circuit.2 Our study lays critical groundwork for guiding the design of neuromodulation interventions and randomized clinical trials that can alter deep subcortical and cortical circuit mechanisms implicated in affective dysfunction.
