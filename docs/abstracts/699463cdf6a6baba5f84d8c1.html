Introduction

The dynamic interplay between emerging schizophrenia-spectrum symptoms and cortical maturation during late adolescence — a critical neurodevelopmental window— remains poorly understood. Establishing the causal direction (whether symptoms drive brain changes or vice versa) is a fundamental challenge due to confounding and reverse causality in observational studies. This study introduces an innovative causal inference framework to disentangle this relationship, with a focus on testing for adaptive neural responses and sex-specific pathways during this vulnerable transition to adulthood.

Methods

We leveraged longitudinal data from the IMAGEN cohort^1, assessing participants at ages 19 (baseline) and 23 (follow-up). Subclinical psychotic, negative, and depressive symptoms were measured using the Community Assessment of Psychic Experiences (CAPE) scale^2. High-resolution T1-weighted MRI scans were processed with FreeSurfer to extract cortical thickness and surface area for 68 Desikan-Killiany atlas regions. Our multi-stage analytic approach comprised: (1) cross-sectional partial correlations (covariates: sex, site, handedness, BMI, intracranial volume); (2) sex-stratified cross-lagged panel models to examine longitudinal predictions; and (3) a novel, optimized two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis. This MR method constructed validated polygenic risk scores (valid-PRS) by rigorously excluding SNPs associated with both exposure and outcome to minimize horizontal pleiotropy^3. Causal estimates were derived from publicly available GWAS summary statistics for schizophrenia (PGC)^4 and brain structure (ENIGMA)^5, with the IMAGEN sample excluded to avoid bias. Significance was assessed via a powerful multi-timepoint, multi-threshold permutation framework. Finally, spatial gene enrichment analysis linked significant brain-symptom associations to molecular pathways using data from the Allen Human Brain Atlas.

Results

We identified a complex, bidirectional relationship. Cross-lagged models showed that more severe negative/depressive symptoms at age 19 predicted greater cortical thickness in the right insula and rostral anterior cingulate (rACC) at age 23 (Figure 1), suggesting a potential compensatory response. Striking sex differences emerged: males exhibited more widespread brain-symptom correlations, particularly in fronto-temporal-insular circuits. Our optimized MR provided robust genetic evidence for causality: schizophrenia genetic liability significantly caused accelerated thinning in the left insula and left rACC (Figure 2). This pattern supports a novel “pathologic thinning-compensatory thickening” dual-process model, where disease-related vulnerability and adaptive plasticity coexist in different brain regions. Reverse MR indicated that thicker cortex in specific areas (e.g., isthmus cingulate) could causally influence higher subsequent symptom scores. Gene enrichment revealed distinct molecular signatures for each symptom dimension: positive symptoms with synaptic transmission/immunity; negative symptoms with cell adhesion/synapse assembly; and depressive symptoms with cell migration/proliferation pathways.

Conclusion

This study provides the first integrative causal evidence for a dynamic, symptom-driven model of cortical restructuring in youth at risk for schizophrenia. We demonstrate that early symptoms are not merely consequences of static brain deficits but can actively shape later brain structure, potentially through adaptive thickening. The proposed dual-process model, with its regional and hemispheric specificity, offers a more nuanced framework than simple atrophy models. Furthermore, the symptom-specific molecular pathways underscore the biological heterogeneity within the schizophrenia spectrum. These findings highlight the critical importance of developmental timing, sex differences, and neural plasticity, offering novel targets for staging and personalized early intervention strategies aimed at redirecting maladaptive neurodevelopmental trajectories.
