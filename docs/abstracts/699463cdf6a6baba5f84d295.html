Introduction

Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) is a non-invasive brain stimulation technique delivering a low electrical current through electrodes to modulate cortical excitability. When applied over the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) while participants face an acute stress situation, tDCS can prevent stress-induced cortisol release1 as well as stress-related modulation of decision making and cognitive functioning, suggesting a relationship between cortico-subcortical loops and stress reactivity.

At the central level, the same tDCS stimulation of the left DLPFC has been suggested to induce an extracellular dopamine (DA) release in the ventral striatum, as evidenced by Positron Emission Tomography (PET) imaging combined with [11C]-Raclopride2,3. This suggests that, in resting conditions, tDCS may trigger dopamine release through fronto-striatal circuit modulation, potentially contributing to its cognitive and motivational improvement effects. However, little is known about the effect of tDCS on dopamine transmission, brain connectivity and cognitive functioning in acute stress situations, even though stress is known to increase dopamine release in limbic regions and modulate cognitive processes.

The current study therefore examines how DLPFC tDCS administered during an acute stress paradigm modulates striatal dopaminergic dynamics using [¹¹C]-Raclopride PET and the b-ntPET approach4 as well as brain connectivity using resting state fMRI, HPA axis reactivity (Cortisol, ACTH) and decision-making capacities. We hypothesized that, in an acute stress situation, tDCS might not simply amplify or reduce this limbic response but rather redistribute dopaminergic activity toward striatal subregions that are usually less engaged during stress. Such a shift would be expected to modulate connectivity patterns in these regions and modulate cognitive processes associated with stress regulation.

Methods

Study design - In a randomized, sham-controled, double blind two arms parallel study, 29 healthy participants were recruited to receive a single session of either active (2mA, 30 min, n = 16, 23.9 ± 3.5 years, 11 female) or sham (n = 13, 22.4 ± 2.6 years, 10 female) bifrontal tDCS session while undergoing an acute stress experimental situation. Five minutes after the start of tDCS, participants underwent 15 minutes stress protocol using Maastricht Acute Stress Task involving alternating, hand immersion in a cold sleeve and mental arithmetic. Stress reactivity was assessed regarding cortisol and ACTH with seven blood samples collected at 15-min intervals during the rest phase and 5-min intervals during the stress protocol. Finally, decision making capacities were evaluated with the Delay Discounting Task.

Brain activity and DA dynamics was concomitantly recorded using a combined PET-MRI acquisition. Acquisition protocol consisted in a preliminar anatomical MRI (T1) followed by a 110 minutes PET acquisition (bolus-plus-continuous-infusion of [11C]-Raclopride) with 35 minutes of resting-state fMRI acquisition, followed by 30 minutes of tDCS with 15 minutes of concurrent stress induction, and a final 30 minutes resting-state fMRI session (see Figure 1).

Analyses - Hormonal effect was assessed by measuring cortisol and ACTH in blood samples taken before the stress task (T10, T25, T40), during the stress task (T55, T60, T65), and after the task ended (T80). Baseline was calculated as the mean of pre-stress measurements. DA dynamics was evaluated by transient changes in [11C]-Raclopride binding induced by endogenous dopamine release using b-ntPET model4, as well as the Simplified Reference Tissue Model (SRTM). The b-ntPET method estimates within a Bayesian framework the conventional kinetic parameters (R1, k2, k2a) of the radiotracer binding to D2 receptor, as well as k2a variation over time induced by a potential endogenous dopamine release expressed as a “discharge” function h(t) modeled by an onset time, peak time, magnitude, and shape. Conversely, SRTM estimates the binding potential under the assumption of time-invariant kinetics and absence of neurotransmitter-induced displacement. Modeling was performed on regional kinetics extracted from striatal regions with the Oxford-GSK-Imanova connectivity striatal5 (left and right sensorimotor, executive and limbic striatum). Model adequacy (b-ntPET vs. SRTM) was assessed using root mean square error (RMSE) to evaluate relative goodness-of-fit. With the b-ntPET model, the area under the curve (AUC) of the estimated discharge function h(t), expressed as a percentage of the total PET AUC (pAUC), was computed as quantitative index of the transient neurotransmitter effect. Temporal variation of the non-displaceable binding potential (BPND) of the radiotracer to its receptor were computed for various time intervals: baseline (40–50 min, T40-50), stress onset (50–60 min, T50-60), during stress (60–70 min, T60-70), post-stress 1 (70–80 min, T70-80) and post-stress 2 (80–90 min, T80-90).

Results

Hormonal - Hormonal levels were normalized by the baseline level and submit to Repeated measure ANOVA with group and timing as factors. No significant differences between group nor timing were found for cortisol levels. ACTH levels varied with timing (p = 0.002), with a tendency regarding the interaction between group and timing (p = 0.098). Post-hoc test showed significant increases of ACTH level between baseline and 10 and 25 minutes (T65 and T80) after stress task start for the sham group, but not with the active group, showing a greater stress effect in the sham group. In addition, we find a tendency toward greater ACTH values in the sham compared to active group at T60 and T65 (respectively p = 0.091 and p = 0.099).

Model comparison – RMSE showed a better accuracy of the b-ntPET model in all striatal regions for the active as for the sham group. The b-ntPET model was used for the rest of the analysis.

Discharge evaluation - A mixed ANOVA (group and regions) performed by hemisphere on pAUC showed a significative difference between regions (left hemisphere p = 0.0007; right hemisphere p = 0.0002), and between groups for the right hemisphere for active group, which showed a lower pAUC (p = 0.039). Post-hoc test corrected for multiple comparison raised a greater pAUC in the limbic region compared to executive (left p = 0.001; right p = 0.003) and sensorimotor (left p = 0.019; right p = 0.028). This suggests that limbic striatal regions exhibited a larger dopaminergic discharge than other striatal regions, in both groups.

Levene test showed a lower pAUC variance in right limbic region of the stim active group compared to sham (p = 0.041), which evidence that active tDCS reduced intersubject variability of the discharge in this region.

BP variation in time - The level of BPND decreased significantly compared to the baseline in most region and at T50-60, T60-70 and T70-80 time interval, both for the active and the sham groups. Moreover, a tendency toward significative group difference was observed in BPND at T70-80 in the left sensorimotor region (p = 0.1), with the active group showing lower BPND (see Figure 2). 

Conclusion

This multimodal PET-MRI study shows that the combination of bifrontal tDCS and an acute stress paradigm modulates both endocrine and striatal dopaminergic responses. Hormonal data revealed a tendency toward lower ACTH response in the active tDCS group, suggesting that stimulation may attenuate stress reactivity. Using [¹¹C]-Raclopride, we found that b-ntPET modelling provided a better fit than SRTM, confirming presence of transient dopamine fluctuations. Furthermore, the limbic striatum showed the strongest dopamine-related discharge of all regions. Dynamic binding potential analyses further indicated a specific decrease in the left sensorimotor striatum after stress in the active group, indicating time and region-specific dopaminergic modulation by tDCS after stress. Together, these results demonstrate that prefrontal tDCS may influence stress-induced dopaminergic activity in striatal subregions.
