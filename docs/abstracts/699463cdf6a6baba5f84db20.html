Introduction

In multisite neuroimaging studies, addressing site effects (i.e., data harmonization) is important for valid and generalizable results.1,2 Widely used data harmonization methods such as neuroComBat often assume consistent covariate effects across sites.1 In this work, we used synthetic and plasmode simulations with the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) study to compare the performance of various site effect controlling strategies, including linear mixed-effects models and several ComBat variants, across scenarios involving homoskedasticity, heteroskedasticity, and fixed and random covariate effects. Our work provides critical guidance for enhanced reliability of multisite neuroimaging analyses. 

Methods

Using ABCD baseline data, we conducted plasmode simulations to evaluate the following six analytic strategies for data harmonization: ordinary least squares (OLS) with and without robust standard errors, ComBat with and without intracranial volume (ICV) adjustment, and the mixed effects model with and without random slopes for target covariates (i.e., age, fluid intelligence score, or child behavioral checklist score). In each bootstrap, we randomly selected 5 study sites and randomly sampled participants with replacement stratified by the selected sites. For each simulation, we regressed each regional grey matter volume (GMV) on age, sex, ICV, and one target covariate, and evaluated the estimation accuracy for the target covariate effect. We performed a bootstrap simulation for scenarios under the null and under the alternative. In the null scenario, any genuine association with the target variable was eliminated by random shuffling, whereas in the non-null scenario, the original values were retained to preserve the true underlying relationships between the target variable and the ABCD data. For each scenario, we conducted 5,000 simulations at various sample sizes ranging from 100 to 1,000, and the performance of each analytic strategy was evaluated across the replications relative to the true target effect (bias, standard error (SE), confidence interval coverage, and type I error rate or power). The corresponding target effects estimated using the full sample were considered true effects in the plasmode simulations. 

Our synthetic simulations encompassed four scenarios: (A) fixed vs. (B) random age effects across sites, and (C) consistent vs. (D) varying outcome variance across sites (Fig 1). In all simulations, we simulated random intercepts from five sites. For each scenario, we conducted 1,000 simulations at various sample sizes ranging from 100 to 500. For scenarios that included random effects for age, we manipulated the tau2 variable for standard deviation of age. For scenarios controlling for heteroskedasticity, we utilized inverse gamma distribution to model site-specific variances across sites. Similarly to the plasmode simulations, we conducted simulations under the null and under the alternative for all scenarios and evaluated the performance (bias, SE, confidence interval coverage, and type I error or power) for each of the same six analytic strategies. 

Results

Our null plasmode studies indicated similar performances between traditional (OLS, mixed effects) and ComBat approaches across multiple metrics: suggesting that these traditional strategies can yield comparable results under certain assumptions. All methods demonstrated consistent standard error estimation (Fig 2X), bias, and coverage (not shown). 

Under the non-null plasmode simulations, all methods were consistent estimators. However, all methods underestimated standard error (Fig 2Y) and had low confidence interval coverage (< 95%). When evaluating the model of a mixed effects model with a random slope intercepts for covariates using plasmode simulations, the bootstraps yielded similar results to the synthetic simulations under the null: the mixed effects model with random intercepts had the highest coverage (~0.95) and standard error ratios closest to the expected value of 1, along with expected bias (~0) and coverage (~0.95). However, all methods, including the mixed effects model with random slopes continued to fail under the alternative. 

To understand these results, we used synthetic simulations to understand what features of the data contributed to biased standard error estimates. In these simulations, we controlled random slope variance and heteroskedasticity across sites under the null of zero mean covariate effect. Under Scenario 1 (fixed age effect and homoskedasticity across sites) all strategies accurate standard error estimates and coverage (not shown). In Scenario 2 (random age effects and homoskedasticity), strategies that did not account for heterogeneity in the age slopes underestimated the standard errors and continued to underestimate at lower ratios with increasing sample size except in the mixed effects model which slightly underestimated the SE (Figure 2C). As a result of the underestimated SEs, all methods had low coverage (~0.26-0.90; Figure 2D) that continued to underestimate at lower values with increasing sample size, except for the mixed effects model with random slope. All strategies had similar performance in Scenario 3, providing consistent standard error estimates and nominal coverage despite site effects and site heteroskedasticity– except for ComBat which included ICV in the ComBat adjustment model (Figure 2E,F). In Scenario 4, which included random slopes and heteroskedasticity across sites, results were consistent with Scenario 2 with slightly under estimated SEs (not shown). 

Conclusion

Together, our simulations show that heterogeneity in the covariate effect across sites is a driving factor in the failure to accurately estimate associations in the presence of site effects. ComBat does not have the existing structure to address such differences. We found that introducing a site-specific scaling effect for the target covariate using a random slope term improved data harmonization and provided nominal coverage. Other strategies, particularly ComBat-based approaches, failed to account for age as a random effect. Instead, they focused on controlling site variance, which was less important than accurately modeling age-related variability in our results. It is important to note that the way we generate the covariate independent of site under the null hides some complexity where site-related heteroskedasticity could affect inference for the covariate if there is unbalance in the covariate across sites.
