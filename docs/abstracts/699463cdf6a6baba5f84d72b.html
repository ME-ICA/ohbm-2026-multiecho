Introduction

Stress represents a key transdiagnostic contributor to internalizing disorders, and given the substantial global burden of internalizing disorders—now the leading cause of disability worldwide^1—and that approximately one-third of individuals do not respond adequately to existing first-line treatments^2, developing accessible brain-based interventions that directly target neural mechanisms underlying stress regulation represents a critical priority.

Real-time neurofeedback training allows individuals to gain voluntary control over neural substrates underpinning emotional reactivity, with marked success by fMRI neurofeedback studies in producing reductions in clinical symptoms and maintenance effects^3. However, fMRI limitations including motion sensitivity, operational costs and confined scanning environments constrain implementation across multiple sessions under real-world settings.

Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) addresses these limitations through non-invasiveness, portability, and cost-effectiveness. The lateral prefrontal cortex (PFC) represents an ideal neurofeedback target given its dual role in cognitive control and emotion regulation,^4 with well-validated cognitive reappraisal strategies offering a theoretically-grounded approach for strengthening prefrontal regulation of stress reactivity^5, nevertheless, this potential remains unexplored in fNIRS neurofeedback training and maintenance of training effects under real-world stress conditions remains uncharacterised. 

The present study examines the efficacy and translational utility of real-time fNIRS neurofeedback with explicit regulation using cognitive reappraisal strategies enhances regulatory control of brain activity at individualised lateral PFC target channels (primary outcome) and whether training effects translate into enhanced and domain-specific stress regulation under ecologically valid settings using the socially-evaluated cold pressor test (SECPT) (secondary outcome).

Methods

Using a pre-registered (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT06866028), randomized double-blind, sham-controlled parallel-group trial, 60 healthy young adults (ages 18-27 years; 24 males, 36 females) were recruited from the University of Hong Kong student population and randomly allocated to either real-time neurofeedback (experimental group, n=30) or yoked feedback (sham group, n=30). 

The single-session intervention (see Fig 1 for experiment flow) began with introduction to cognitive reappraisal and guided practice applying reappraisal sub-strategies toward exemplar negative images depicting interpersonal violence, catastrophes, and threatening animals. Participants then received standardized information about neurofeedback principles and training protocol instructions: during VIEW trials, participants looked at images naturally without controlling emotions; during REAPPRAISE trials, participants applied reappraisal strategies to reduce emotional reactivity toward images.

The formal training protocol consisted of: (1) initial practice run without feedback for task familiarization, serving simultaneously as a functional localizer; (2) four consecutive neurofeedback training runs providing continuous feedback during REAPPRAISE trials; (3) final transfer run assessing regulatory capacity without feedback. The neurofeedback training employed a looming size paradigm wherein presented images shrunk in size to signal achieved control as feedback values increased. The experimental group received feedback from real-time activity at individualized target channels; the sham group received yoked feedback from recordings of previous experimental group participants completing identical runs.

A NIRSport2 device (NIRx, Berlin, Germany) acquired fNIRS raw voltage data at 5.09Hz using wavelengths of 760nm and 850nm at 50 long-separation channels positioned over covering the prefrontal cortex following standard 10-20 placements, with 8 short-separation channels at lateral source locations. An a priori region-of-interest (ROI) channel mask was set at the left lateral PFC to guide individualized channel selection during the functional localizer. Real-time processing and neurofeedback delivery was facilitated through Turbo-Satori software (Brain Innovation, Maastricht, Netherlands), where target channels with consistently increased oxygenated hemoglobin activity during REAPPRAISE relative to VIEW conditions were selected for neurofeedback.

Following the neurofeedback training, all participants completed the SECPT, involving immersion of the non-dominant hand in cold water (~1°C) for a maximum of 3 minutes. Visual analogue scale (VAS) ratings were recorded before and after the SECPT for pain levels (0=no pain, 100=extremely severe pain) and stress levels (0=no stress, 100=extremely severe stress). Additional measures collected before and after training included anxiety levels (VAS; 0=not at all anxious, 100=extremely anxious), valence and arousal (Affect Grid), and ratings of perceived control over brain activity and success in applying reappraisal strategies. At the end, participants indicated whether they believed they received real neurofeedback and rated their confidence.

NIRS Brain AnalyzIR toolbox was utilized to preprocess data and compute subject-level general linear model analyses using the iteratively reweighted least-squares method. Resulting parameter estimates were extracted for group-level statistical analyses performed using RStudio. 

Primary outcome analyses compared activations at individualized target channels during REAPPRAISE trials using mixed ANOVAs across training runs (1-4) and practice versus transfer runs, and exploratory channel-wise analyses examined each lateral PFC mask channel; significant effects received post-hoc Bonferroni-Holm corrections. Secondary outcomes employed mixed ANOVAs to verify successful stress/pain induction with the SECPT, followed by one-tailed t-tests of baseline and post-SECPT stress/pain ratings across groups.

Results

Primary outcome analyses examining neural-level training success revealed significant between-group differences in lateral PFC activation at individualized target channels across the four training runs, F(1,58)=9.20, p=.004, ηp²=.14, with the experimental group demonstrating consistently higher activations compared to the sham group during runs 1 (p=.029), 2 (p=.011), 3 (p=.004), and marginally during run 4 (p=.072). Comparing practice and transfer runs revealed a significant group effect, F(1,57)=6.46, p=.014, ηp²=.10, with no differences during the practice run, t(57)=1.33, p=.190, but significantly higher activations for the experimental group during the transfer run, t(57)=2.64, p=.011, Cohen's d=.70.

Channel-wise analyses within the lateral PFC ROI mask identified channel S4-D6, corresponding to the left inferior frontal gyrus, as showing particularly pronounced training effects. Across the training a significant interaction effect emerged, F(2.19,122.87)=4.51, p=.048, ηp²=.05, with a significant between-group differences during run 3, t(56)=2.83, p=.006, Cohen's d=.76. For practice versus transfer comparison, a significant group effect was present, F(1,55)=5.47, p=.023, ηp²=.09, with marginally significant differences between groups at the transfer run, t(55)=1.93, p=.059, Cohen's d=.52.

Secondary outcomes analyses revealed the SECPT successfully induced stress across all participants, with a significant main effect of timepoint, F(1,58)=65.65, p<.001, ηp²=.53, reflecting substantially higher stress reported post-induction (M=56.12, SE=3.64) compared to pre-induction (M=23.33, SE=2.65). Between groups, baseline stress levels were comparable, t(58)=-0.24, p=.407 (experimental: M=22.70, SE=4.13; sham: M=23.97, SE=3.39), however post-SECPT stress levels were significantly lower in the experimental group (M=50.33, SE=5.14) compared to sham (M=61.90, SE=4.49), t(58)=-1.70, p=.048 (one-tailed), Cohen's d=-.45, equivalent to 27.2% reduction in post-induction stress levels. Analysis of pain ratings during the SECPT revealed a significant main effect of timepoint, F(1,58)=664.36, p<.001, ηp²=.92; however pain levels did not differ between experimental and sham groups at baseline (p=.357) or following the SECPT (p=.216), suggesting a domain-specific transfer of neurofeedback training effects to stress regulation without generalizing to pain perception.

Behavioral outcomes revealed that anxiety levels measured before and after the neurofeedback training showed a marginally significant interaction effect, F(1,58)=3.02, p=.088, ηp²=.05. Anxiety levels were similar between groups pre-training, t(58)=0.319, p=.376 (one-tailed; experimental: M=29.80, SE=4.19; sham: M=28.00, SE=3.79), but notably diverged post-training, t(58)=-1.309, p=.098 (one-tailed), Cohen's d=.34, with anxiety levels diminishing in the experimental group (M=26.03, SE=3.62) while elevating in the sham group (M=32.73, SE=3.62). Exploratory analyses between anxiety changes and activation changes at target channels revealed a marginally significant negative correlation emerged in the experimental group, Pearson's r(25)=-.34, p=.080, while no such relationship was observed in the sham group, Pearson's r(24)=.05, p=.812.

Valence ratings analyzed before and after training showed a marginally significant effect of timepoint, F(1,58)=3.37, p=.071, ηp²=.05, reflecting a trend toward decreased valence across both groups, but no significant group effect, F(1,58)=0.36, p=.550, or interaction effect, F(1,58)=1.13, p=.292. Arousal ratings revealed no significant effects for group, F(1,58)=0.08, p=.776, timepoint, F(1,58)=0.05, p=.828, or interaction, F(1,58)=0.82, p=.369. Negativity ratings for REAPPRAISE trials significantly decreased from the practice run to the transfer run in the experimental group (p=.002) but not in the sham group (p=.192); however, decreases in negativity ratings within the training were comparable between both groups.

Conclusion

The present study provides converging evidence through a methodologically rigorous, pre-registered, double-blind, randomized sham-controlled protocol that single-session fNIRS neurofeedback targeting lateral prefrontal cortex during cognitive reappraisal enables participants to gain voluntary regulation of prefrontal activity with successful and domain-specific transfer to novel, ecologically-valid stress contexts.

Examination of our primary pre-registered outcome revealed that fNIRS-based neurofeedback successfully enabled volitional control over targeted prefrontal regions. The experimental group demonstrated consistently higher activations at individualized target channels compared to the sham group throughout training. Critically, this control persisted during the transfer run without feedback, demonstrating sustained regulatory capacity rather than transient effects. This validates the precision and specificity of targeting lateral PFC regions involved in cognitive emotion regulation, reflecting successful volitional control over lateral PFC activity rather than non-specific mechanisms such as general engagement or placebo effects.

Channel-wise analyses identified S4-D6, corresponding to left inferior frontal gyrus, as showing pronounced upregulation preferentially in the experimental group. Together, the significant upregulation within target channels and channel-specific effects within the lateral PFC mask demonstrate that fNIRS-guided neurofeedback enables precise, targeted neural modulation of regions critically involved in cognitive emotion regulation.

Our secondary outcome revealed that neurofeedback training effects transferred functionally to stress regulation under real-world conditions. While both groups began with identical baseline stress levels and experienced comparable pain (evidenced by similar hand immersion durations and pain ratings), the experimental group reported significantly lower stress following the SECPT — a 27.2% reduction compared to sham group. This domain-specific effect indicates that neurofeedback specifically enhanced stress regulation capacity without altering pain perception. Enhanced regulatory capacity was further reflected in divergent anxiety trajectories, with anxiety levels decreasing in the experimental group while increasing in the sham group following neurofeedback training. These findings demonstrate that neurofeedback promotes adaptive learning of regulatory abilities that functionally transfer to stress regulation within ecologically-valid contexts.

Altogether, these findings establish critical prerequisites for translating laboratory-based neurofeedback into real-world clinical and preventive applications: maintenance of regulatory control in the absence of continuous feedback and successful transfer of acquired regulatory skills to novel stressful contexts that replicate naturalistic conditions. Importantly, the successful transfer of regulatory control into novel contexts provides critical evidence that trained regulation extends to real-world stressors. 

This precision-guided approach combining individualized neural targeting of established regulatory circuitry with evidence-based cognitive strategies positions fNIRS neurofeedback as a promising intervention for stress-vulnerable populations. Applications span therapeutic frameworks as adjunctive treatment alongside psychological interventions for individuals with stress-related disorders, and preventive frameworks as training for at-risk populations facing chronic stress exposure. fNIRS’s practical advantages including non-invasiveness, portability, and cost-effectiveness enable scalable real-world integration across intervention settings that traditional fMRI approaches cannot reach. Together, these advantages position fNIRS neurofeedback as a widely applicable, scalable pathway for stress regulation within preventive and therapeutic frameworks.
