Introduction

A fundamental goal in neuroscience is understanding abstract mental processes, such as cognition, consciousness, and perception, by investigating each as a neurophysiological process. A pertinent example of this is the study of the philosophical “binding problem,” which questions how a unified synchronous sense of perception arises from independent asynchronous sensory channels.1 Recent investigation has focused on frameworks classifying characteristics of functional brain activity on a continuum from integrative, or synchronous, to segregative, or asynchronous,2,3 and how these characteristics arise from the structural physiology of the brain.4 In this work, we explore how the harmonics arising from the brain’s SC graph Laplacian provide such a continuum, participate in MEG functional activity, and assess whether harmonic participation profiles in MEG functional recordings differ between three recently identified harmonic regimes that exist on this integration-segregation continuum, namely integrative, segregative, and degenerate regimes.4 These harmonic regimes, based on the Laplacian “gap-spectrum,” were previously found to exhibit specific properties related to brain function, such as participation in functional networks and resting-state functional recordings from functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI).4 However, it has not previously been reported whether there is a consistent pattern of participation of structural harmonics in frequency rich content contained in MEG recordings not found in fMRI. In this study, we demonstrate that integrative, segregative, and degenerate harmonic regimes exhibit unique profiles of participation in MEG, linking the structurally derived harmonic integration-segregation continuum to the brain’s functional activity. In addition, we show novel structure-function relationships between the frequency of structural Laplacian harmonics of a complex SC graph Laplacian formulation and MEG-derived functional recordings.5

Methods

We used a publicly available dataset of 36 healthy subjects (23 males, mean age 21.75 years) which includes a resting state MEG recording, an SC matrix, and a distance matrix computed from MRI tractography for each subject parceled into the Desikan-Killiany brain atlas. For each subject, we compute the normalized Laplacian matrix ℒ as well as the frequency dependent complex Laplacian matrices ℒ*(ω), as described in Xie et al, at frequencies f between 1 and 40 Hz as follows5:

ℒ = I - D-1/2CD-1/2      (1)

ℒ*(ω) = I - αDC*(ω)     (2)

C*(ω) = Ce-jωτ, ω=2πf     (3)

Where ℒ and ℒ* are the Laplacian and complex Laplacian matrices, respectively, I is the identity matrix, C and C* are the SC and complex SC matrices, D is the diagonal degree matrix of C, τ is the time delay obtained from distance adjacency matrix and a transmission speed parameter, and α is a global coupling parameter controlling weights given to long range white matter connections. We then compute the eigen-decomposition of each Laplacian matrix to obtain an orthonormal matrix of harmonics and associated eigenvalues for each of the previously computed Laplacian matrices as follows.

ℒ or ℒ*(ω) = UΛUT     (4)

Where U is the orthonormal matrix of harmonics, and Λ is the diagonal matrix of harmonic eigenvalues. The subject-specific “Gap Spectrum” is then computed from the harmonics, which, combined with the harmonic eigenvalues, allows the harmonics to be clustered into integrative, segregative, and degenerate regimes.4

Gap(λi) = minj≠i| λi − λj |     (5)

Where λi is the eigenvalue corresponding to the ith eigenvector of U. The MEG recording for each subject is used to compute regional power spectral density (PSD) and cross coherence functional connectivity (FC) for each subject. The ordering of subject-specific structural harmonics Usub are then rearranged to match a reference set of harmonics derived from the average SC matrix across subjects using equations 1-4. A harmonic match is obtained using the Hungarian matching algorithm to best align harmonic eigenvectors with the reference eigenvectors. We then compute harmonic participation for each of the subject-specific harmonics in subject-specific empirical PSD and FC by computing how well these functional metrics align with each harmonic’s power as defined by equations 6 and 7.

Fproj = UTFU, Pproj = UTP     (6)

EFC = || Fproj ||2, EPSD = || Pproj ||2

Where U is the matrix of subject specific harmonic eigenvectors after reference matching, F is an empirical MEG FC matrix, P is an empirical MEG PSD, EPSD and EFC are the harmonic participation energy for PSD and FC, respectively, and || · ||2 denotes an L2-norm. Finally, we compare mean harmonic participation energy across all subjects in different harmonic regimes (integrative, degenerate, and segregative), at different complex Laplacian frequencies f (ranging between 1-40 Hz), and at different frequency bands of PSD and FC.

Results

The structural harmonics are successfully categorized into integrative, degenerate, and segregative regimes based on their gap spectrum and absolute value of eigenvalue using the complex formulation of the SC graph Laplacian matrix, as has previously been done for harmonics of the non-complex Laplacian formulation.4 The integrative and segregative nature of harmonics is visually observed in glass brains of the harmonics, with lower index (integrative) harmonics (U1 − U3) showing involvement across the entire brain, and higher index (segregative) harmonics (U76, U 84, U86) exhibiting regional specificity. The non-complex Laplacian harmonic participation energy profiles in MEG functional recordings show differences between integrative, segregative, and degenerate harmonic regimes. This corroborates the profiles previously computed on functional time-series data derived from fMRI.4 In these profiles, the integrative modes display the largest participation energy, and the degenerate and segregative modes show relatively lower participation energy. A notable local minimum is found for U2, the second harmonic in profiles for both PSD and FC, with a much more distinct minima observed in the harmonic participation for PSD. This can be explained by the fact that U2 has a negative relationship between hemispheres, and functional brain activity generally displays relatively low hemispheric specificity in resting state, meaning it is expected that this harmonic has relatively low participation in functional recordings. A similar dependency between the harmonic regime and the harmonic participation energy profile is uncovered when using complex Laplacian-derived harmonics to examine harmonic participation in functional MEG recordings.5 The integrative harmonics exhibit greater mean harmonic participation energy and larger values than exhibited by the harmonics in the degenerate regimes.

However, as opposed to the harmonics derived from the non-complex Laplacian formulation, the segregative harmonics of the complex Laplacian formulation exhibit greater harmonic participation energy values than degenerate harmonics. In general the integrative and segregative harmonics display a wider range of harmonic energy than degenerate harmonics, with notably much larger maximum participation energy values across subjects and complex Laplacian frequency. Our analysis also reveals novel frequency dependencies on harmonic participation energy profiles. The harmonic participation energy of complex Laplacian harmonics show a strong dependency on the frequency at which the complex Laplacian is computed in both MEG derived PSD and FC. At relatively low and high frequencies in the range of 1-40 Hz (6.28-251.33 rad/s), the harmonic participation energy is very high in integrative and segregative harmonic regimes (low and high index harmonics, respectively). However, at intermediate Laplacian frequencies the harmonic participation energy profile is relatively flat, and does not change significantly between harmonic regimes. These analyses were run again on different PSD and FC frequency bands (alpha, beta, delta, theta), but the results did not differ significantly from those presented.

Conclusion

We found that harmonic participation energy exhibits a notable relationship with integrative, segregative, and degenerate harmonic regimes in MEG functional recordings using both the non-complex and complex Laplacian formulations. However, this relationship differs between Laplacian formulations, with the complex Laplacian segregative harmonics exhibiting much greater participation energy in MEG derived functional brain recordings, and non-complex Laplacian harmonics showing a relatively flat harmonic participation energy among both degenerate and segregative harmonics. These results both point to a relationship between the integration-segregation continuum that the brain’s structural harmonics exist on, and the brain’s function as observed by MEG. In MEG, the relationship is additionally shown to depend on the frequency of the complex graph Laplacian, where low frequencies and high frequencies correlate with the harmonic gap-spectrum better than intermediate frequencies. Overall, this emphasizes the potential differential role of these harmonic regimes in brain function and suggests that not all structural harmonics have equal importance in brain function; integrative and segregative harmonics have more pronounced presence in function relative to degenerate harmonics. The group specific harmonic participation energy profiles that integrative, degenerate, and segregative harmonics exhibit have shown to carry across functional imaging modalities, namely fMRI and MEG, emphasizing the role these regimes may play in brain function, and providing a potential link between an integration-segregation continuum and the physiological structure of the brain. In addition, the frequency dependency these profiles exhibit in functional MEG recordings suggests that complex harmonic frequency modulates this link.
