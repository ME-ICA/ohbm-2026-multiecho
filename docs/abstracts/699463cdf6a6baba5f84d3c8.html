Introduction

High-penetrance copy number variations (CNVs) disrupt tightly regulated developmental programs, conferring significant transdiagnostic psychiatric risk. However, elucidating the mechanisms linking genomic variation to brain structure faces a critical bottleneck: traditional prospective imaging is underpowered or absent for most pathogenic CNVs due to low prevalence and logistical challenges associated with recruitment and testing. Furthermore, population-based cohorts like the ABCD study and UKBB have healthy volunteer biases that limit the ability to identify brain-gene associations.1 To overcome these limitations, clinically-acquired imaging and genetic data, collected as part of clinical care, present a powerful, yet underutilized resource. We integrated electronic health records (EHR), genomics, and neuroimaging from a large-scale pediatric biobank to systematically map the impact of cumulative CNV burden—defined by gene constraint—on the clinical phenome and brain morphometry.

Methods

We analyzed genotype arrays from 17,750 pediatric patients in the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia (CHOP) biobank. CNVs were identified using PennCNV with all CNVs > 50 kb retained.2 The CNV risk score was defined using the loss-of-function observed/expected upper-bound fraction (LOEUF) score from the gnomAD database, with the score equal to the cumulative inverse LOEUF metric across all genes contained within CNVs, separately for deletions and duplications. The clinical phenome was assessed using hierarchically clustered phecodes derived from ICD-10 codes.3 A PheWAS was performed using logistic regression, modeling CNV risk scores adjusted for number of unique encounters, sex, earliest encounter age, latest encounter age, and ten genetic ancestry principal components.

For neuroanatomical analysis, we retrospectively curated MRI scans collected as part of routine clinical care with associated genetic data (N = 1,048). Scans were extracted from the CHOP Picture Archive and Communication System (PACS), de-identified, and processed using a clinical segmentation pipeline in FreeSurfer (recon-all-clinical).4 Scans were excluded from analysis based on indications of severe gross pathology in radiology notes, surface reconstruction errors (Euler numbers <-60) and low SynthSeg+ segmentation quality scores (<0.65 in any region). To account for non-linear developmental effects, we generated normative deviation scores using GAMLSS brain growth charts derived from clinical controls, as described previously (Schabdach et al., 2023).5

Results

We detected 79,247 CNVs >50 kb across the cohort (mean 4.5 CNVs/patient) and 12,961 patients had a non-zero CNV risk score (Figure 1A). Phenome-wide analysis demonstrated the pathogenicity of the risk score (Figure 1B): CNV burden was significantly associated with 98 diagnostic phecodes (out of 863 phecodes with >100 cases). Within brain-related phecodes, deletion risk scores were specifically associated with nervous system congenital anomalies (OR = 1.01 per unit increase in risk score, p < 10-16), lack of coordination (OR = 1.01, p < 10-10), and convulsions (OR = 1.008, p < 10-5); duplication risk scores were associated with sleep apnea (OR = 1.003, p < 10-5); and both deletion and duplication risk scores predicted intellectual disability risks (del OR = 1.02, dup OR = 1.005; p < 10-5).

To investigate the neuroanatomical correlates of this clinical burden, we mapped associations between CNV risk scores and brain structural deviations. In the subset of patients with processed clinical imaging (N = 1,048), both deletion and duplication burden were associated with increased ventricle volume (Deletion: robust effect size index (RESI) = 0.16, pfdr < 10-5; Duplication: RESI = 0.09, pfdr = 0.01). However, deletion burden specifically drove global reductions in cortical (RESI = -0.09, pfdr = 0.01; Figure 1C) and subcortical (RESI = -0.08, pfdr = 0.03) gray matter. These deletion-specific reductions were spatially clustered in the temporal and parietal cortices (peak RESI = -0.14, pfdr = 0.001), an effect largely absent in duplication carriers (Figure 1D).

Conclusion

These findings reveal that copy number variation exerts differential effects on brain structure depending on copy number state. While ventricular expansion is associated with both CNV deletions and duplications, cortical atrophy is specific to deletion burden. This dichotomy is consistent with overall lower pathogenicity of duplications relative to deletions. Deletion-specific signatures, focused primarily in the temporal and parietal cortices, may relate to distinct neurological phenotypes identified in the PheWAS (e.g., motor deficits). As the neuroanatomical effects of specific CNV loci are often dosage dependent, future studies will construct clinically-ascertained cohorts of carriers with neuropsychiatric-risk CNVs identified through genome-wide association to parse brain-gene relationships. Such an approach promises to validate and discover pathogenic CNVs while linking their pathogenicity to brain structure.
