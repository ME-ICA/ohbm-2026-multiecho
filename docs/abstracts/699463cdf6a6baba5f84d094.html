Introduction

In recent years, there has been a significant focus on longitudinal studies of the brain due to advancements in network neuroscience. Analysis of longitudinal studies is of significant interest as it aims to explore the gradual changes in brain connections over time. Longitudinal data, acquired through repeated observations of the same subjects, is valuable for studying brain disorders and neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s.1 However, data acquisition is a significant challenge due to high-cost and time-consuming pipelines. This leads to an increased dropout rate among the participants involved in the studies, resulting in a common issue of missing data points within the longitudinal datasets. Moreover, from a clinical side, each subject is scanned only once, resulting in typically unpaired data, i.e., data collected at different time points from different subjects.2,3 As a result, it remains unclear whether the observed differences are due to developmental progress or inter-subject variations. To address this limitation, we propose a novel Generative Cross-Domain Translator (GCT-Net). It is a graph-based cycle-consistent generative model for longitudinal brain connectivity prediction under unpaired conditions.4 GCT-Net translates brain graphs from one time point to the next time point across different subjects, without requiring direct temporal correspondence. The model incorporates edge convolution to better capture local structural relationships in brain graphs, thus enabling robust translation across varying clinical conditions.  It also leverages cycle-consistency and adversarial learning to ensure stable domain translation, while its graph-based architecture preserves topological properties. Experimental results on multiple datasets demonstrate that GCT-Net outperforms existing generative and graph-based baselines. This makes GCT-Net a flexible framework applicable to diverse brain imaging cohorts.

Methods

Consider a brain graph denoted as G(V,E) where V represents the nodes and E represents the edges. The brain connectivity at two time points, t1 and t2 represented as G(t1)(V(t1), E(t1)) and G(t2)(V(t2), E(t2)), respectively. Here, t1 and t2 correspond to distinct, unpaired graph structures from different subjects acquired at different time points.

The overall model follows the principle of the cycle-gan framework as shown in Fig 1. First, the input sample is passed through the first generator to produce a target connectivity. Then, this generated connectivity is passed to a second generator, which reconstructs the original input sample. Following this reconstruction process, we employ two generators, Gent1->t2 and Gent2->t1 . Each generator consists of an edge-convolution layer followed by batch normalization. 

The edge-convolution layer works by dynamically aggregating information from a node’s neighbors to update its feature representation. Specifically, for each node, the layer considers both the features of the node itself and those of its connected neighbors. These differences capture the local connectivity patterns, allowing the model to understand how a node’s role in the network relates to its surrounding regions. The aggregated information is then passed through a learnable function, typically a small neural network, which transforms the node features while preserving important structural relationships. This allows the model to capture local topological changes for accurate longitudinal brain graph translation. The input graph is first encoded through edge convolution, after which the resulting features are concatenated with edge features encoded by a fully connected layer. The combined representation is then passed through another fully connected layer to predict the output graph. To distinguish between generated and real graphs, we introduce a discriminator. The discriminator is composed of two graph convolutional network (GCN) layers. The discriminator is trained in an adversarial manner against the generator. Thus, allowing it to correctly classify real and generated graphs. The discriminator feedback helps the generator to produce more realistic brain graphs.

The framework jointly optimizes adversarial and cycle-consistency loss functions to guide the training process. The adversarial loss encourages the generators to produce realistic brain graphs that closely match the distribution of the target domain. In parallel, the cycle-consistency loss ensures that the translated graphs can be accurately mapped back to the original domains. This helps in preserving essential structural information. Both loss functions are a combination of L1 loss and Frobenius distance, where the L1 loss helps preserve edge details and the Frobenius distance enforces overall structural similarity in the reconstructed graphs.

Results

For experiments, we used the OASIS (150 samples) and ADNI (43 samples) datasets due to their longitudinal design, clinical relevance, and widespread use in neuroimaging research. Although these datasets originally provide paired longitudinal scans at t1(baseline) and t2(follow-up) from the same subject, we simulated real-world scenarios where longitudinal data are typically unpaired. To achieve this, we created an unpaired dataset by randomly discarding five samples from both t1 and t2 sets, ensuring no subject overlap between the two time points.

For the experiment, we trained our model on an Intel(R) Xeon(R) CPU with a 2.20GHz processor on Ubuntu 22.04.3 distribution with 12GB RAM. We trained our cycle-GAN model with 70 epochs. We evaluated our model with three metrics, i.e., mean-absolute-error (MAE), Frobenius distance, and Pearson correlation, respectively. We optimized the algorithm using the ADAM optimizer.

To evaluate the performance of our method, we compared it against the Cycle-Consistent Graph-to-Sequence Generative Adversarial Network (CGTS-GAN) as a baseline.5 Across both datasets, the proposed GCT framework consistently outperforms the CGTS baseline, demonstrating substantial efficiency and accuracy gains. For instance, on the OASIS dataset, GCT reduces the mean absolute error (MAE) by 0.9154, decreasing it from 0.9568 (CGTS) to 0.0414. Similarly, the Frobenius Distance is reduced by 299.22, dropping from 300.98 (CGTS) to 1.759, indicating a significantly improved preservation of global graph structure. In addition, GCT achieves a notable improvement in Pearson correlation, increasing it by 0.853 from 0.127 (CGTS) to 0.98. These consistent and substantial improvements clearly demonstrate the superior accuracy, stability, and biological consistency of the proposed GCT framework compared to the baseline method.

Moreover, across both OASIS and ADNI datasets, the ablation study shows that incorporating both L1 and Frobenius losses shows the best overall performance, achieving the lowest MAE and Frobenius Distance alongside consistently high Pearson correlations in both reconstruction directions. Models without edge attributes or discriminator loss exhibit noticeably degraded accuracy, thus showing their importance in stable longitudinal brain graph generation. Conclusion

This paper presents GCT-Net, a cycle-GAN based graph translation framework designed for unpaired longitudinal brain connectivity prediction. Experimental evaluation on the OASIS dataset demonstrates that GCT-Net can generate accurate and consistent connectivity translations in the absence of paired data. The use of graph-edge convolution ensures permutation invariance, an essential property for graph-structured data. The results validate the effectiveness of the proposed approach in capturing temporal changes in brain networks. In future work, we plan to extend this framework to accommodate multiple time points, further enhancing its utility in longitudinal neuroimaging analysis.
