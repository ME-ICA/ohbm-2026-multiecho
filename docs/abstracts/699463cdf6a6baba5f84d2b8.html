Introduction

Hyperglycemia contributes to vascular damage and neuroinflammation, increasing the risk of stroke, vascular dementia, and other neurological complications1,2. However, the mechanisms by which elevated glucose perturbs brain physiology remain understudied. One emerging contributor is glymphatic dysfunction, as impaired clearance of metabolic waste may amplify oxidative stress and inflammatory burden3,4. Although chronic hyperglycemia has been linked to altered glymphatic flow5,6, the acute effects of glucose elevation and their hormonal drivers remain unknown7. Specifically, oral glucose ingestion elevates plasma glucose but also triggers increases in insulin and incretin hormones8,9, each of which may independently influence glymphatic dynamics. Here we used ultrafast fMRI to examine how oral glucose ingestion and intravenously (IV) induced hyperglycemia differentially modulate human glymphatic function.

Methods

Two complementary experiments were developed.

Experiment 1 (Oral glucose ingestion): Two healthy adults underwent the ultrafast fMRI scan (TR=0.8 s, TE=34.2 ms, multiband factor=6; Siemens 3T Prisma). Each session began with the acquisition of high-resolution anatomical images, including MPRAGE and T2-weighted scans10,11. This was be followed by ultrafast fMRI, each lasting approximately 5 minutes per session. After the baseline acquisitions, the participant exited the scanner to consume the glucose drink (75 g Glucola, 296 mL) and then return to the scanner, as illustrated in Fig. 1a. Ultrafast functional MRI acquisitions were repeated every ten minutes for a total of seven post ingestion sessions. Each participant completed a second visit in which the experimental procedures were identical except that the glucose drink was replaced with water. This control condition enabled us to isolate the effects of glucose ingestion from fluid consumption.

Experiment 2 (hyperglycemic clamp): Another two healthy adults underwent the ultrafast fMRI scan as described in Experiment 1. To separate the effects of hyperglycemia from those of gut hormone release, we used a glucose hyperglycemic clamp procedure. This method allows precise control of blood glucose levels without oral intake, thereby eliminating the confounding of gut hormone secretion. One intravenous catheter was placed in a distal arm for serial blood sampling, and a second intravenous catheter was placed in the opposite arm for infusion. The intravenous dextrose infusion is used to maintain the plasma glucose at target level (180 mg/dL). Serial blood samples were obtained at 5 minute intervals beginning at baseline and continuing through the final scan for measurement of plasma glucose.

Results

In our pilot study, we investigated glymphatic system responses to elevated glucose levels using ultrafast fMRI. The imaging slab was positioned with its inferior boundary cut across the fourth ventricle (see Fig. 1b). CSF inflow effect was assessed by computing the signal power (in decibels) at fourth ventricle. As shown in the left panel Fig. 1c, CSF signal power increased during the first 40 minutes after ingestion and subsequently declined, indicating that the glymphatic system was acutely influenced by glucose digestion. In contrast, the water consumption did not produce comparable changes, as shown in the right panel of Fig. 1c. Fig. 1d presents the raw resting-state signals from one subject, demonstrating a pronounced increase in signal fluctuation amplitude from time point b (baseline) to time point 4 (peak glucose) and then decline from time point 5 to 7.

To assess the coupling between vascular and CSF dynamics, we examined the cross-correlation between the negative derivative of the gray matter BOLD signal and the CSF signal, a reported marker of the relationship between blood volume fluctuations and CSF inflow12. Time courses from the 4th ventricle were extracted and used as seed regressors with varying temporal lags in FSL FEAT13. The resulting statistical maps are shown in Fig. 1e. Following dextrose ingestion, blood volume fluctuations exhibited strong correlations with CSF inflow, whereas this coupling was largely reduced at baseline. The cross-correlation function (see  Fig. 1f) further demonstrated that the coupling peaked at a lag of –0.8 s, indicating that changes in blood volume preceded and likely drove subsequent changes in CSF inflow.

To separate the effects of hyperglycemia from those of gut hormone release, we used a hyperglycemic clamp procedure. This method allows precise control of blood glucose levels without oral intake, thereby eliminating the confounding of gut hormone secretion. As shown in Fig. 2, glymphatic activity decreased as blood glucose levels increased during the hyperglycemic clamp, which is opposite to the pattern observed after oral glucose ingestion. These preliminary findings suggest that glymphatic responses to glucose ingestion may not be driven by hyperglycemia but rather by other factors such as gut hormone release.

Conclusion

This study provides initial evidence that the human glymphatic system is acutely modulated by both hyperglycemia and hyperglycemia-induced incretin release. Oral glucose ingestion elevates plasma glucose, insulin, and incretin hormones, and it was associated with increased CSF inflow. In contrast, intravenously induced hyperglycemia without a gut hormone response resulted in decreased CSF inflow. These findings suggest (1) that glymphatic system responds to short term metabolic changes, and (2) that plasma glucose and gut hormones may exert opposing effects on glymphatic dynamics. A key limitation of this pilot work is that plasma glucose levels were not matched across the oral and intravenous conditions. To rigorously isolate the effects of incretins, future studies will incorporate a hyperglycemic clamp procedure in both experimental paradigms. Together, these observations offer important mechanistic insight into how acute glycemic fluctuations may influence fluid transport pathways relevant to diabetes, neurodegeneration, and inflammatory brain disorders.
