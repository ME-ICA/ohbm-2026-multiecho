Introduction

Thalamocortical connections are relevant for integrating sensory, motor, and cognitive information across cortical and subcortical brain regions1. Disruption of these circuits has been consistently associated with cognitive deficits observed in focal neurological conditions, such as thalamic stroke2. Lesions involving the mediodorsal (MD), pulvinar (Pul), or anterior thalamic nuclei have been associated with domain-specific deficits in memory, attention, and processing speed1. However, clinical outcomes show inter-individual variability, likely reflecting individual differences in thalamic structure and function3, besides the differences in etiology, e.g., lesion localization. The current study investigates whether distinct thalamocortical structural neuroanatomical profiles differentiate individuals and whether these neuroanatomical patterns are associated with cognitive impairment following thalamic stroke. If individual thalamocortical differences, potentially predating lesions, are associated with cognition, then the outcome of thalamic stroke will depend on pre-existing anatomical patterns, and not only on lesion localization. We applied structural MRI–based thalamic and cortical volumetry combined with hierarchical and k-means clustering to identify data-driven thalamocortical profiles in a large healthy cohort and replicated and validated these profiles across independent samples using a random forest classifier. The validated profiles were then projected onto stroke patients, whose lesion-specific thalamic nuclei were analyzed using a two-stage zero-inflated regression model (TSM) to assess how cluster membership and focal thalamic lesions are associated with cognitive performance.

Methods

A total of 667 healthy individuals and 22 stroke patients were recruited across two research centers, i.e., University of Bari and Inserm, ToNIC, Toulouse, and divided into four cohorts. The first cohort, Uniba1 (n = 363 healthy subjects; mean age = 26.1 ± 6.9 years; 68% female), was used as the training set, and the second cohort, Uniba2 (n = 260 healthy subjects; mean age = 26.7 ± 7.3 years; 57% female), as the testing set. The third cohort, Toulouse1 (n = 44 healthy subjects; mean age = 48.8 ± 14.6 years; 36% female), was used as an independent validation, matched with the age distribution of the stroke patient group, which included patients with left thalamic stroke (Toulouse2: n =22, mean age = 49.4 ± 15.4 years; 41% female). High-resolution T1-weighted MRI scans were acquired for all participants. Cortical volumes were extracted using Freesurfer with the Desikan-Killiany Atlas4, while thalamic segmentation into 12 nuclei was conducted using HIPS-THOMAS5. Prior to analysis, volumetric data underwent preprocessing, including logarithm transformation, regression of nuisance covariates (age, age², gender, and intracranial volume), and within-subject normalization. To identify distinct thalamocortical profiles, we applied hierarchical and k-means clustering on Uniba1 and replicated them in Uniba2. We, furthermore, validated our findings by predicting Uniba2 cluster labels using a Uniba1 cluster-informed random forest model. Validated clusters were, then, projected on Toulouse1 and on Toulouse2. In stroke patients, we considered only lesioned nuclei, namely the mediodorsal parafascicular region of interest (MD-Pf), the ventral lateral anterior (Vla), the ventral lateral posterior (VLp), and the ventral anterior (VA) nuclei. We examined the association between these lesioned thalamic regions, cluster membership, and their interaction with cognitive performance across four domains: attention, memory, language, and processing speed. We implemented a two-stage model to account for zero-inflated data in case of the absence of a lesion in specific thalamic nuclei. The first part of the model predicted the probability of a non-zero outcome, while the second part modeled the outcome magnitude given that it was non-zero. Average marginal effects (AMEs) of the lesioned voxels of thalamic nuclei were estimated both globally and within each cluster, with interactions between clusters calculated as differences in AMEs. Statistical significance was assessed using the delta-method and bootstrap standard errors.

Results

Hierarchical analysis in Uniba1 identified two robust thalamocortical profiles, cluster 1 and cluster 2, replicated across independent samples with a strong cross-sample classification accuracy (AUC up to 0.78). Cluster 1 showed reduced gray matter in fronto-temporo-parietal areas and anterior-ventral thalamic nuclei, whereas cluster 2 displayed reductions in limbic-occipital areas and geniculate nuclei. These neuroanatomical clusters replicated across independent samples, with a strong cross-sample classification accuracy (AUC up to 0.78). The identified clusters were then projected on the stroke patient cohort (AUC = 74%) to investigate their associations with cognitive outcomes. 

For the TSM, only the thalamic nuclei lesioned in at least one-third of the stroke patients (≥33%) were considered, specifically the MD-Pf (91%), VLP (73%), VA (68%), and VLa (36%) nuclei. Other thalamic nuclei were either healthy or lesioned in only a minority of cases and were thus excluded from further analyses. Larger MD-Pf lesions were associated with poorer attention (z = -2.63, pFDR = 0.036) and working memory (z = -3.3, pFDR = 0.008). These effects were stronger in cluster 1, where MD-Pf damage was significantly associated with reduced attention (z = -4.05, p < 0.001) and working memory (z = -4.87, pFDR < 0.001). VLp lesions were associated with reduced semantic fluency (z = -3.3, pFDR = 0.008). Cluster-specific effects were observed, with attention negatively affected in cluster 1 (z = -2.83, pFDR = 0.02), reduced phonological fluency in cluster 2 (z = -3.17, p = 0.012), and semantic fluency reduced across both clusters (cluster 1: z = -3.57, pFDR < 0.001; cluster 2: z = -2.98, pFDR = 0.012). VLa lesions were associated with reduced phonological fluency (z = –2.66, pFDR = 0.032), especially in cluster 2 (z = -3.43, pFDR = 0.004). They were also associated with reduced semantic fluency (z = -3.57, pFDR < 0.001) across both clusters (cluster 1: z = -3.44, pFDR = 0.008; cluster 2: z = -3.72, pFDR < 0.001). VA lesions were associated with reduced semantic fluency performance (z = -3.52, pFDR < 0.001) across clusters (cluster 1: z = -3.75, pFDR < 0.001; cluster 2: z = -3.25, pFDR = 0.008). Cluster-dependent effects included reduced attention (z = -2.32, pFDR = 0.053) and phonological fluency (z = -2.93, pFDR = 0.012) in cluster 2.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence that inter-individual differences in thalamocortical structural organization affect cognitive vulnerability following focal thalamic stroke. Across a large healthy cohort, we identified two reproducible neuroanatomical profiles defined by distinct patterns of gray matter covariation in cortical and thalamic regions. The replication of these profiles across independent samples, together with robust cross-dataset classification accuracy, suggests that these patterns reflect stable and biologically relevant thalamocortical organization.

In patients, the neuroanatomical clusters differentially affected the cognitive impact of focal thalamic damage. Although lesion burden in MD-Pf, VLp, VLa, and VA nuclei independently predicted deficits across attention, working memory, and language, the direction of these effects varied with cluster membership. Individuals characterized by reduced fronto-temporo-parietal and anterior-ventral thalamic volumes (cluster 1) showed greater attention and working memory impairment in association with MD-Pf and VLp lesions. The MD-Pf supports prefrontal functions, including executive control, attentional regulation, and working memory1. Consequently, when MD-Pf lesions occur in individuals in the cluster with lower fronto–temporo–parietal gray matter, deficits secondary to thalamic lesions are greater in attention and working memory. Conversely, patients in cluster 2, with comparatively lower limbic-occipital and geniculate volumes, exhibited stronger susceptibility to VLa- and VA-related language impairments. A possible interpretation considers that VA and, to a lesser extent, Vla, communicate with dorsolateral prefrontal, premotor, and basal ganglia circuits involved in selective attention and semantic retrieval. These findings suggest that pre-existing thalamocortical architecture is associated with the cognitive outcomes of focal thalamic injury.

Several limitations should be considered. Although cluster profiles were robust across datasets, the stroke cohort was small and only included patients with left-sided lesions. Another limitation is the over-representation of MD-Pf lesions, highlighting the need for a more balanced distribution of lesions across all thalamic nuclei to fully capture their impact on cognition. Furthermore, volumetric gray matter covariation reflects structural associations rather than direct connectivity, and no diffusion-based measures were available to validate anatomical connectivity. Finally, the cross-sectional design of the study impedes within-subject replicability and stability analyses.

Our results advance the understanding of thalamocortical connections to cognition by demonstrating (i) the presence of inter-individual neuroanatomical profiles, and (ii) their relevance for predicting cognitive impairment after thalamic stroke. Using such structural profiles in prognostic models may improve outcome prediction and inform personalized rehabilitation strategies.
