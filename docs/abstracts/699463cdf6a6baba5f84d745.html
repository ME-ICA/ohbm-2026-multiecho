Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) and bipolar disorder (BD) are distinct disorders frequently presenting with overlapping clinical features during depressive episodes. This overlap poses a diagnostic challenge. One longitudinal retrospective cohort study reported that, among adults with a first episode of MDD who transitioned to BD, 17.5% converted within three years of MDD onset;1 in contrast, another study found that eight-year conversion rates ranged from 7.6% to 12.1%.2 Misdiagnosis often leads to inappropriate pharmacological interventions, particularly antidepressant monotherapy, which increases the risk of manic switching, rapid cycling, or treatment resistance.3 Consequently, identifying objective neurophysiological biomarkers to predict conversion risk prior to hypomanic or manic symptom onset is imperative.

While electroencephalography (EEG) offers high temporal resolution, prior research has predominantly relied on linear approaches, such as power spectral density or static functional connectivity. These methods often fail to capture the inherent non-linear dynamics of the brain and its fluctuating states of integration. Theoretical frameworks, such as the complexity hypothesis, posit that pathological states are associated with a loss of physiological complexity, which manifests as excessive randomness or rigid synchronization.4 Elucidating mechanisms distinguishing stable unipolar depression from latent bipolarity requires non-linear methods quantifying spatiotemporal dynamics.

This prospective study aimed to determine whether spatiotemporal dissimilarity between brain regions serves as a predictive biomarker for diagnostic conversion. To this end, the study employed spatiotemporal information-based similarity (STIBS) analysis to assess both the non-randomness of symbolic sequences derived from global and regional field power and the dissimilarity of these sequences across topographical regions.5

Methods

In this prospective longitudinal study with an approximately three-year follow-up period, 97 drug-naïve patients initially diagnosed with MDD were enrolled. Additionally, 120 healthy controls (HC) matched for age, sex, and years of education were recruited to provide baseline comparisons. Exclusion criteria for all participants included a history of neurological disorders, traumatic brain injury, and substance use disorders. At baseline, five-minute resting-state EEG data were acquired using a 62-channel system (sampled at 1000 Hz). Following the observation period, patients were categorized based on longitudinal diagnostic outcomes. The "stable MDD" group (n = 75) comprised patients who maintained their unipolar diagnosis without hypomanic episodes, while the "converted BD" group (n = 22) consisted of those who transitioned to bipolar II disorder marked by the emergence of hypomanic episodes. EEG data were preprocessed as follows: signals were downsampled to 250 Hz, band-pass filtered (1–45 Hz), and cleaned using wavelet-based procedures. Ocular artifacts were removed using second-order blind identification (SOBI), and myogenic artifacts were eliminated via independent component analysis (ICA). Subsequently, the data were re-referenced using the reference electrode standardization technique (REST).

The STIBS framework was applied to characterize non-linear spatiotemporal dynamics. Global field power (GFP) was computed for the whole scalp, while regional GFP (rGFP) was calculated for nine topographical regions of interest (ROIs). These ROIs were constructed by spatially grouping non-overlapping electrodes to generate field power trajectories for bilateral frontal, temporal, and parietal regions, as well as fronto-central, centro-parietal, and parieto-occipital areas. These continuous time series were transformed into symbolic sequences based on the sign of temporal differences (increase vs. non-increase), effectively encoding the trajectory of neural synchronization strength. To construct the pattern spaces, these sequences were segmented into overlapping words of length m, with m ranging from 4 to 8. Three distinct indices were derived to evaluate spatiotemporal dynamics. First, the spatiotemporal non-randomness index (STNRI), computed globally, quantified the degree to which the GFP-based symbolic sequence deviated from randomly shuffled surrogates, as determined via Monte Carlo simulations with 1000 iterations. Second, the brain-region-aggregated STIBS (braSTIBS) measured the entropy-weighted, information-theoretic dissimilarity in temporal ordering between pairs of ROIs. Finally, the brain-region-aggregated STNRI (braSTNRI) assessed regional contributions to spatiotemporal non-randomness. Group differences were assessed using Welch’s analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Games-Howell post hoc tests with false discovery rate (FDR) adjustment. Associations with clinical symptoms were examined using bootstrapped Pearson correlation coefficients.

To further evaluate the predictive utility of these features, a machine learning classification pipeline was implemented. Within each fold of a leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) framework, feature selection using Fisher scores identified the spatiotemporal features that most effectively differentiated between the stable MDD and converted BD groups. Subsequently, a support vector machine (SVM) classifier with a radial basis function (RBF) kernel was trained on the selected features. Balanced class weights were integrated into the cost function to address the class imbalance (n = 75 vs. n = 22). To prevent data leakage, Z-score normalization parameters were computed exclusively using the training set within each fold and applied to the test set. Model performance was assessed via accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and Cohen’s kappa coefficient.

Results

Demographic analyses revealed no statistically significant differences in age, sex distribution, or years of education among the stable MDD, converted BD, and HC groups. Regarding clinical symptoms, both the stable MDD and converted BD groups exhibited significantly elevated scores on the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) and the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) compared to the HC group (all p < 0.001).

The analysis of non-linear spatiotemporal dynamics, quantified via braSTIBS, revealed statistically significant group differences (Fig. 1). The stable MDD group demonstrated significantly lower braSTIBS values than both the HC and converted BD groups. Specifically, pairwise comparisons localized these differences to pairs involving the left temporal region. Relative to the HC group, the stable MDD group displayed significantly lower braSTIBS values for the left temporal–fronto-central (p = 0.0184) and the left temporal–centro-parietal (p = 0.0061) pairs. Similarly, relative to the converted BD group, the stable MDD group exhibited lower values for the left temporal–fronto-central (p = 0.0025) and the left temporal–centro-parietal (p = 0.0009) pairs. No statistically significant differences were observed between the converted BD and HC groups in these spatiotemporal metrics.

To examine associations between EEG indices and psychological measures, Pearson correlation analyses were performed separately within each group. In the stable MDD group, a significant positive correlation was observed between the braSTIBS values of the left temporal–centro-parietal pair and trait anxiety scores (r = 0.2551, p = 0.0271) (Fig. 2).

Finally, configured with an RBF kernel and a word length parameter of m = 4, the SVM classifier distinguished between the stable MDD group and the converted BD group. The feature selection process identified the braSTIBS values for the left temporal–fronto-central and left temporal–centro-parietal pairs as the most robust predictors. Within the LOOCV framework, the model achieved a classification accuracy of 76.29%, sensitivity of 63.64%, specificity of 80.00%, and a Cohen’s kappa coefficient of 0.39.

Conclusion

STIBS analysis revealed that drug-naïve patients in the stable MDD group exhibited a spatiotemporal dynamical profile that was distinct from that of both the HC and converted BD groups. The stable MDD group was characterized by significantly reduced braSTIBS values in inter-regional interactions involving the left temporal region, specifically couplings with fronto-central and centro-parietal areas, whereas the converted BD group did not differ from the HC group in these indices. With respect to clinical associations within the stable MDD group, higher trait anxiety scores were associated with higher braSTIBS values in the left temporal–centro-parietal interaction, indicating that anxiety is linked to the degree of spatiotemporal pattern differentiation along this axis. Finally, to evaluate the predictive utility of the identified spatiotemporal features, an SVM classifier was configured using these left temporal–fronto-central and left temporal–centro-parietal braSTIBS metrics, achieving moderate accuracy in differentiating the stable MDD group from the converted BD group under LOOCV.

The reduction of braSTIBS values observed in the stable MDD group implies that spatiotemporal dynamics in functional systems involving the left temporal region, anterior control regions, and posterior self-referential hubs exhibit abnormal uniformity. This pattern suggests weakened functional boundaries between networks supporting semantic–affective processing, conflict monitoring, and self-referential processing, aligning with theoretical accounts relating persistent depressive states to excessive internal self-focused mentation. In contrast to the converted BD group, which exhibited a profile similar to that of the HC group, the stable MDD group displayed pronounced homogeneity centered on the left temporal region. This distinction indicates that persistent unipolar depression may be associated with a rigid, temporally over-coupled dynamical configuration that is not simply a milder variant of latent bipolarity. Furthermore, the positive association between trait anxiety and left temporal–centro-parietal braSTIBS within the stable MDD group suggests that anxiety relates to network dynamics along a spectrum ranging from strongly homogeneous to relatively more differentiated states. This observation raises the possibility that anxiety-related variations in posterior default mode coupling may influence trajectories along the depressive–bipolar spectrum.

This study has several limitations. First, the findings are based on resting-state EEG in a single, relatively homogeneous cohort of Korean adults, limiting the generalizability of the results to other age ranges, cultural backgrounds, and medicated populations. Second, the evidence linking network homogeneity to rumination, impaired cognitive control, and self-referential processing is indirect, as these psychological constructs were not assessed via dedicated behavioral or task-based paradigms. Third, the classification model, although encouraging, was evaluated solely with internal cross-validation and relied on a limited set of STIBS-derived features, excluding additional dynamical indices such as regional braSTNRI or frequency-specific measures. Therefore, future work should replicate these results in larger and independent samples, integrate task-based probes of self-referential processing and executive function, and combine STIBS metrics with complementary neurophysiological markers to improve the individualized prediction of bipolar conversion risk.

The findings indicate that abnormal spatiotemporal homogeneity centered on the left temporal region represents a candidate systems-level signature of persistent unipolar depression. Consequently, STIBS-derived non-linear EEG measures may serve as clinically meaningful biomarkers to distinguish stable major depressive disorder from latent bipolarity in early illness stages.
