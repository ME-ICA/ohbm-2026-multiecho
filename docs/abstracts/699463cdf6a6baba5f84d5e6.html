Introduction

The brain is a complex, adaptive system that dynamically transitions between different network configurations to support cognition and generate behaviour. Brain signal complexity, which indexes the brain’s ability to process information and switch between networks, has primarily been linked to differences in cognitive functioning across groups (e.g. age, disease states), with higher complexity supporting better performance on cognitive tasks.1 In young adults, several studies have linked complexity to stable domains of cognitive capacity, however results are mixed and limited to single-domain measures.2,3

Brain signal complexity can be quantified using Multiscale Entropy (MSE), with higher values reflecting greater temporal unpredictability and neural flexibility.1,4 Whereas event-related potentials measure state-dependent, task-evoked brain activity, MSE provides a trait-level measure of neural complexity, providing complementary insight to central tendency-based measures.4,5 In this context, trait-level characteristics refer to more consistent, enduring neural properties, whereas state-level characteristics reflect more transient, context-dependent neural functioning, often in response to task demands. 

In the present study, we examined in young adults how brain signal complexity relates to performance on a perceptual task, as well as across a battery of cognitive assessments capturing both state- and trait-level characteristics. Our state-level measures included perceptual task performance and accuracy on an offline continuous performance test, which captures moment-to-moment fluctuations in attention across an extended period. Our trait measures consisted of offline cognitive assessments of the domains of executive control, processing speed, and working memory. Given that MSE indexes trait-level neural dynamics, we hypothesized that higher overall complexity would correlate to better performance on trait-based, but not state-based, measures of cognitive capacity.

Methods

Twenty-six neurotypical adults aged 18-35 underwent electroencephalography (EEG) recording during completion of 600 trials of the Texture Discrimination Task (TDT), as part of a larger study investigating the brain and behavioural correlates of task learning. In each TDT trial, participants saw a briefly presented peripheral target composed of three diagonal bars arranged in either a vertical or horizontal orientation among an array of horizontal bars. Participants then indicated whether the peripheral target was oriented horizontally or vertically. The peripheral target angle (PTA) of the diagonal bars was continually adjusted to maintain performance at 80% accuracy throughout the task session to ensure comparable perceptual thresholds across participants. The PTA value corresponding to each participant’s 80% accuracy point was used as a state-based performance metric in subsequent analyses.

Outside of the EEG session, participants completed a cognitive battery assessing the following domains: executive control (Trails B), processing speed (Trails A and Symbol Digit Modalities Test [SDMT]), working memory (Digit Span [DS] and Letter-Number Sequencing [LNS]), and sustained attention (Sustained Attention to Response Task [SART]). Measures assessing trait-based characteristics included Trails A and B, SDMT, Digit Span, and Letter-Number Sequencing, whereas state-based performance measures included accuracy on the SART, as well as PTA values from the TDT.

EEG preprocessing included bandpass filtering (0.5-55 Hz), decomposition using independent component analysis to remove artifacts, and trial segmentation –200 ms and 1000 ms around the stimulus. MSE was used to estimate brain signal complexity at each electrode. In brief, MSE calculation involved two steps. First, a coarse-graining procedure was applied wherein data were resampled and averaged across increasing numbers of datapoints within non-overlapping windows to create discrete timescales, ranging from fine (scale 1; 2-ms windows) to coarse (scale 12; 24-ms windows). Second, sample entropy was calculated at each timescale to estimate the predictability of the underlying signal.  

Behaviour Partial Least Squares (bPLS) analysis was used to examine brain-behaviour relationships. bPLS uses singular value decomposition to generate latent variables (LVs) that show patterns of covariation between brain and behaviour variables. Our ‘brain’ measure was MSE at each electrode and timescale. Our ‘behaviour’ variables included PTA, SART accuracy, and normalized cognitive test performance on Trails A, Trails B, Digit Span, Letter-Number Sequencing, and SDMT. Significance of patterns identified by LVs was determined using permutation testing (500 permutations), with an LV considered significant if its singular value exceeded the permuted singular value in >95% of the permutations (i.e., p < .05). Reliability of the identified patterns was evaluated using bootstrap resampling (500 resamples) and a designated minimum bootstrap ratio threshold of 2.0, corresponding to a 95% confidence interval or p < .05.

Results

The bPLS analysis identified one significant LV. As illustrated in Figure. 1, LV1 (p = .004) showed that across most electrodes and timescales, higher MSE during TDT task completion was linked to better performance on offline cognitive tests including Trails A, Trails B, Digit Span, Letter-Number Sequencing, and SDMT. The overall pattern of this LV is illustrated in Figure 1b. Generally, the identified brain-behaviour relationship was widespread, with the most reliable effects identified across central electrodes at fine timescales (2-10 ms) and fronto-central electrodes at middle timescales (10-16 ms).

Conclusion

Results of the current study showed that higher widespread brain signal complexity was related to better performance on trait-level cognitive assessments across a variety of domains, including processing speed, working memory, and executive control. In contrast, state-level measures of performance on perceptual and sustained attention tasks were not implicated in the identified brain-behaviour relationship. These findings are in line with previous work suggesting that MSE primarily captures trait-like differences in complexity rather than transient, state-dependent fluctuations.5 Notably, the strongest associations between brain signal complexity and trait-based cognitive performance were concentrated to fine and middle MSE timescales in central and fronto-central sites. These finer timescales have been linked to flexibility in local, within-region, processing (in contrast to complexity at coarser timescales which are linked to flexibility in long-range processing, where information is shared over distributed regions).1 Therefore, our results suggest that among young individuals, greater localized flexibility in brain regions that support higher-level cognitive control may facilitate optimal cognitive functioning. Overall, our results support the conclusion that increased neural flexibility represents trait-level, adaptive brain dynamics that underlie cognitive capacity in younger adults.
