Introduction

Introduction

The human brain undergoes continuous structural changes across the lifespan. These changes are shaped by biological factors—such as genetic architecture and sex hormones—that contribute to distinct neurodevelopmental patterns in males and females. Although sex differences in brain structure are well established, their developmental trajectories across the entire lifespan remain poorly understood. Characterizing the sex-specific lifespan patterns is fundamental not only for understanding the neurobiological basis of cognitive and behavioral differences, but also for establishing precise normative benchmarks of normative development and aging. 

Previous studies that focused on sex differences in brain development were often limited by small sample sizes and restricted age ranges. In large-scale brain charting studies that covering the full lifespan, sex is generally modeled as a fixed categorical covariate within mixed-sex frameworks.1-3 This approach assumes identical developmental patterns in males and females. Such simplification may obscure true sex-specific trajectories, particularly regarding the timing of developmental milestones and the rates of age-related changes, thereby limiting a comprehensive characterization of sex-specific brain maturation.

To address these gaps, we leveraged large-scale neuroimaging data spanning the human lifespan (ages 266 post-conceptional days to 100 years) to construct sex-specific brain growth charts for males and females. We quantified sex differences in the timing of developmental peaks, identified critical age windows during when significant sex differences emerged, and characterized how these differences changed across the lifespan.

Methods

Methods

Structural MRI data processing

The data included 77,491 healthy participants (37,807 females; 37,104 males) from 192 sites (Fig. 1a), undergoing a rigorous and unified four-stage quality control procedure.4 Data were processed using age-appropriate processing pipelines: the FreeSurfer pipeline (v6.0.0) for participants aged two years and older; the dHCP pipeline (v1.0) and the iBEAT pipeline (v2.0) and were employed for neonates and infants (0-2 years), respectively. Infant surfaces were subsequently aligned to the adult fsaverage standard using a three-stage registration technique.4 Finally, 296 global and regional brain phenotypes were extracted for each participant.

Lifespan growth curves modelling

For each phenotype, we applied generalized additive models for location, scale and shape (GAMLSS) to construct the sex-specific normative growth charts. The generalized gamma distribution was used to characterize the data.1 We optimized the model by varying the complexity of the polynomial terms (degrees 1–3) for the  and  parameters and evaluating the necessity of random site effects. The configuration with the lowest Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) was select as the optimal model.

Sex difference of peak age and lifespan trajectories

For each phenotype, we calculated the differences in peak age and trajectories. To quantify these differences, we performed a stratified permutation test (1,000 times). Males and females were pooled, and sex labels were randomly shuffled within 27 predefined age bins to preserve the underlying age structure. For each permutation, normative models were refitted for the permuted male and female groups, and the difference between the two groups was recalculated to construct a phenotype-specific null distribution. Empirical P-values were derived based on the position of the observed difference relative to this null distribution. Significance was determined after correcting for multiple comparisons using the False Discovery Rate (FDR < 0.05) across all phenotypes.

Results

Results

Sex-specific normative growth of global-level brain phenotypes

Consistent with prior studies on lifespan brain development,1-3 most global-level volumetric measures—excluding ventricular volume—exhibited inverted-U shaped trajectories, with developmental peaks occurring from childhood to early adulthood. However, males consistently reached peak values later than female. Specifically, total cerebral volume, total cortical gray matter (GM) volume (Fig. 1b), total subcortical GM volume (Fig. 1c), and total cortical white matter (WM) volume (Fig. 1d) peaked approximately 1–2 years later in males than in females (all PFDR < 0.05). Cerebellar measures—including total cerebellar volume, cerebellar GM volume (Fig. 1e), and cerebellar WM volume—showed larger temporal divergence, with males reaching peak values 3–4 years later than females (all PFDR < 0.05, Fig. 1f). For other morphological metrics, total cortical thickness (CT) and surface area (SA) peaked approximately 0.4 and 2 years later in males (all PFDR < 0.05), whereas total folding index (FI) showed no difference.

Additionally, sex differences in magnitude of trajectories were significant across broad age ranges (PFDR < 0.005). For both cerebral and cerebellar measures, these differences first appeared in early childhood, temporarily declined, then reached their peak during late adolescence to early adulthood, followed by a gradual reduction (Fig. 1b-f). Total CT showed no sex differences across the lifespan. Conversely, sex differences in total SA and the FI emerged in early childhood and increased with age; however, the FI difference maximized in early childhood whereas SA differences maximized in adulthood.

Sex-specific normative growth of subcortical regional phenotypes

Across all subcortical measures, males consistently reach peak volumes significantly later than females by approximately 1–2 years (all PFDR < 0.05; Fig. 2c). Significant sex differences emerged as early as infancy, expended throughout development, and peaked in early adulthood (PFDR < 0.005; Fig. 2d). Subsequently, the magnitude of sex differences gradually narrowed and persisted into late life.

Sex-specific normative growth of cortical regional phenotypes

At the regional level, most cortical phenotypes reached their peaks during childhood or adolescence, yet males consistently exhibited later peak timing compared to females. The magnitude of peak age difference varied across metric: CT showed the smallest difference (0.5–1.5 years), followed by cortical GM (0.5–2.5 years), while the FI and SA exhibited the most pronounced lags, extending up to 7 years in specific regions (Fig. 2e). Next, we examined the onset of significant sex differences in cortical morphology. Sex differences are minimal for CT, with significant differences emerging after 4 years of age only in a small number of regions, primarily located in the lateral temporal and medial occipital cortices. In contrast, sex differences in cortical GM emerged mainly after age 2 and were spatially widespread across the cortex, whereas differences in FI and SA arose predominantly during early childhood (Fig. 2f). Finally, we characterized when sex differences reached their maximal magnitude. These were limited and late for CT, occurred mainly during childhood and adolescence for cortical GM, peaked earliest for the FI, and peaked in young to mid-adulthood for SA (Fig. 2g).

Conclusion

Conclusion

Our models delineate lifespan neurodevelopmental trajectories across 296 structural brain phenotypes separately for males and females, revealing distinct temporal dynamics. Sex differences are characterized by systematic temporal shifts in developmental and aging trajectories, with males generally exhibiting later peak timing and faster age-related decline relative to females. Furthermore, the temporal windows of sex differences are highly phenotype-specific: differences in cortical FI and SA emerge predominantly in early childhood, whereas cortical GM differences expand substantially after early childhood to peak during childhood and adolescence, while CT shows minimal and comparatively late regional effects.

These findings highlight the necessity of sex-specific normative modeling. By explicitly modeling male and female trajectories separately, sex-specific brain charts provide developmentally reference models that avoid biases inherent to mixed-sex frameworks. Such models provide an important methodological foundation for accurately identifying atypical developmental deviations, improving sensitivity to sex-biased disease vulnerability, and enabling more precise interpretation of individual-level neuroimaging phenotypes across the lifespan.
