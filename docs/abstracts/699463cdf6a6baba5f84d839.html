Introduction

So far, studies examining how the brain processes  linguistic information as well as studies on how hearing loss affects language processing used highly controlled isolated words or single sentences. This approach may in many cases be useful for characterizing some core brain areas for language processing. However, it fails to account for complex interactions language elements can invoke, sometimes over multiple sentences, and context dependent processing to resolve semantic ambiguities. Here we focus on processing of content and function words in continuous language. These two word classes are thought inform two different linguistic speech processing problems. Function words inform mainly syntactic processing. They comprise, among others, determiners, pronouns, conjunctions, and propositions. These parts of speech have little or no meaning in isolation but they specify and reference elements, support cohesion within sentence, coherence in narrations, etc. Conversely, content words inform semantic processing. They have meaning and comprise parts of speech like nouns, adjectives, and verbs. 

In our fMRI experiment participants listened to a continuous movie soundtrack (Forrest Gump). Our results indicate that functions and content words invoke overlapping brain activations in early speech processing areas (putative pre-lexical level) and separate brain networks considered to be relevant for syntactic and semantic processing at sentence and narration level. These results highlight the importance of investigating language processing with realistic, continuous stimuli.

Methods

Participants (n=30) provided informed consent and listened to the audio of the Forrest Gump movie while whole head BOLD EPI scans were taken (TR=850ms). The images were preprocessed using fMRIprep. A GLM with function and content word onsets regressors taken from the annotation provided by Häusler and Hanke (2021), a word length regressor, a regressor for words that did not fall into the two  classes, and regressors for nuisance covariates were fitted using SPM12. Importantly, the function and content word regressors were orthogonalized to the word length regressor to remove word length effects. The results of this first level analysis were subjected to a group level t-test and results are thresholded at p < 0.05 FWEc (family-wise error cluster level corrected) with an initial cluster forming threshold at p < 0.001 uncorrected.

Results

Figure 1 shows the statistically significant activations found in the group level analysis  (red: content word, green function word, yellow: overlap). Function and content words both activate early speech processing areas in bilateral middle STS and STG. Beyond this early processing level the significant activations elicited by the two word classes do not seem to overlap in our study. Function words bilaterally activate an extended set of areas including areas implied in syntactic processing at sentence level (anterior temporal cortex and IFG) and core areas of the default mode network (medial dorsal prefrontal cortex, posterior ACC, and angular gyrus) thought to be involved in episodic memory and mentalizing. Content words activate pSTS and pMTG, thought to be involved in lexical-semantic retrieval, as well as more lateral parts of dorsal prefrontal cortex and precuneus which are involved in semantic working memory and attentional control.

Conclusion

We used a natural continuous stimulus with narration and conversation components. With this stimulus we could demonstrate that function and content words, which reflect the basic linguistic distinction between syntactic and semantic processing, share initial processing resources but the brain areas recruited at later processing stages diverge between word classes. The divergence begins in brain areas that support the sentence level syntactic analysis and lexico-semantic retrieval. This may reflect the different types of linguistic processing involved in these word types, as well as the different linguistic information function and content words provide. The differential activation of domain general semantic memory/cognitive processing control and episodic memory  networks  may reflect the differential contribution of content and function words to the construction of a situation model (Kintsch 1998) and to maintaining narration coherence (Zacks and Ferstl,  2016). Together our results demonstrate that, function words elicit robust and wide spread activations when presented in realistic continuous speech that provides sufficiently rich context on which they can “function”. We suggest that linguistic constructs should be investigated in realistic connected speech to integrate the rich context dependence of natural speech processing.
