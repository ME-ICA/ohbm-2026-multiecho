Introduction

Brain aging is associated with progressive structural changes, notably a reduction of gray matter (GM) and functional decline across several cognitive domains. However, distinguishing changes due to the onset of neurodegenerative diseases from those related to normal aging remains challenging. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) enables the quantification of these changes. Cortical thickness is the reference biomarker for measuring structural decline because it reflects the integrity of cortical layers. However, it shows limited sensitivity to early alterations, as age-related changes in MRI image quality and tissue contrast may reduce the reliability of gray–white matter boundary segmentation.

The opening of cortical sulci has emerged as a complementary marker of cortical atrophy. Compared to cortical thickness, the measurement of sulcal opening relies on the high contrast between gray matter and cerebrospinal fluid and reflects changes in adjacent gyri. Wenqi Shu-Quartier et al., 2025 [1] demonstrated in more than 20,000 individuals from the UK Biobank that sulcal opening increases with age and exhibits an inflection point several years before the acceleration observed in cortical thickness, indicating that sulcal opening appears earlier. 

To better characterize this sulcal opening phenomenon, our study aims to model its evolution using a longitudinal cohort named SENIOR. It offers a unique opportunity to observe individual trajectories of brain aging and to identify the age associated with the inflection point. We present here our first results, which suggest the existence of a longitudinal acceleration of sulcal opening, even though the phenomenon does not appear uniformly across all participants.

Methods

Materials

To conduct this analysis, we used the SENIOR longitudinal cohort of 142 adults aged 50 to 79 years who were examined annually throughout the study. From 2012 to 2015 imaging data were acquired with a 3T Siemens Trio imaging scanner and since 2016 with a 3T Siemens Prisma —due to a technical upgrade — at Neurospin center (Gif-sur-Yvette, France).

All T1 anatomical images were acquired with the same protocol (T1 at 3 T: TR = 2,300 ms; TE = 2.98 ms; voxel size = 1 mm iso; matrix acquisition = [176 x 256]) to ensure longitudinal comparability. This protocol included a second T1 image when motion was present. 

 When multiple images were required within the same session, a combined quality-control procedure was used: MRIQC v22. [2] was first applied to quantify image quality based on gray matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid metrics, as well as signal-to-noise and contrast-to-noise ratios. Results were then visually reviewed to confirm automated ranking and exclude corrupted scans.

Sulcal opening was extracted across cortical regions for each retained image using BrainVISA Morphologist 2023. Sulcal recognition was performed on the first acquired image and then propagated across all time points. Processing was performed on a Linux cluster (Ubuntu 22.04) using Python 3.11. Morphologist outputs were visually inspected. Individual trajectories of sulcal measurements were plotted as a function of age to assess consistency and identify potential trends.

Methods

We used a method described in WenQi Shu-Quartier et al., 2025 [1] which consists of a statistical modeling using piecewise linear regression with bootstrap resampling on UK Biobank data, in which age-related sulcal trajectories are modeled as two linear phases joined at a single breakpoint representing a transition from slow to accelerated opening. Models were implemented using the segmented algorithm in the statsmodels Python package, with constraints enforcing positive slopes to reflect sulcal widening with age and restricting breakpoint ages to a biologically plausible interval.

Breakpoints were estimated via nonlinear optimization using a least-squares cost function. Bootstrap resampling (N = 100 iterations) was applied to compute confidence intervals for breakpoint age and slope differences and to reduce overfitting bias. Breakpoints were considered reliable when both linear segments were statistically significant (p < 0.05) and when bootstrap variability of the breakpoint age was limited, indicating a stable and well-defined transition point.

To enable comparison with the UK Biobank, since the SENIOR cohort includes only 142 subjects and thus provides limited statistical power at each age, each acquisition was treated as an independent data point in order to increase the number of observations per age.

All code, parameters, constraints, and bootstrap procedures were identical across the two datasets. Age distribution, model stability, and bootstrap confidence intervals were evaluated to assess reliability.

Although the SENIOR sample is smaller, its dense temporal sampling offers a unique opportunity to track individual changes and examine potential accelerations through individual-level analyses in which each subject’s longitudinal sulcal opening was examined across all available images and visualized to identify potential slope breaks (Fig. 1).

Results

Visual inspection of individual opening trajectories confirmed progressive opening of several sulci over a 10-year period, however, statistical modeling at the individual level did not reveal significant breakpoints, which was expected given the limited number of participants with complete longitudinal follow-up and the natural variability of aging trajectories.

The combined analysis of all participants revealed clear age-related trends. Several sulci showed a monotonic increase in opening with age. Piecewise linear models consistently identified a slope change, indicating an acceleration of opening. In the SENIOR cohort, the breakpoint is found occurring at 64.40 years (95% bootstrap CI = [63.7–65.2]) for the left superior temporal sulcus (STS). Before this age, STS opening progressed very slowly, with a mean slope of 0.0023 mm/year, whereas after the breakpoint, the slope increased sharply to 0.0455 mm/year—an acceleration of 19.6-fold. The normalized change metric reached 2.94, with a bootstrap standard deviation of 3.15. Several sulci showed accelerations of comparable magnitude, such as left and right anterior lateral fissure (F.C.L.a_left/right), right posterior segment of the lateral fissure, (F.C.L.p_right) and left marginal branch of the cingulate sulcus (S.F. marginal_left).

A similar pattern was observed in the UK Biobank sample, though the breakpoint occurred earlier for STS sulcus. In this cohort, the transition point was detected at 57.31 years (95% CI: [56.0–58.6]). Before this age, opening increased moderately by 0.0075 mm/year, while after the breakpoint the slope reached 0.0421 mm/year—an acceleration of 5.6-fold. The normalized change index reached 2.20, with a bootstrap standard deviation of 1.57 (Fig. 2).

The acceleration effect was strongest in the upper portion of the distribution, suggesting that excessively widened sulci may reflect early structural vulnerability. These results confirm the main phenomenon observed in UK Biobank and demonstrate that sulcal opening reflects accelerated aging trajectories several years before cortical thickness [1]. Despite persistent statistical uncertainty due to the limited cohort size, trajectory patterns were highly consistent across these two datasets. This suggests that sulcal opening is not merely a cross-sectional artifact, but instead reflects longitudinal structural change.

Conclusion

Across two independent datasets, we observed that sulcal opening follows a nonlinear trajectory with a clear change in slope at a specific age. The existence of a breakpoint implies that cortical structural decline does not unfold gradually throughout adulthood, but instead accelerates abruptly later in life. In the UK Biobank cohort, this transition occurred around age 57, whereas in the SENIOR cohort it appeared later, around age 64. Although the precise timing differs between cohorts, the underlying phenomenon remained highly consistent: sulcal opening accelerated markedly after the breakpoint.

The magnitude of this acceleration was particularly striking. In the SENIOR cohort, sulcal opening increased by nearly a twenty-fold factor after the breakpoint, whereas in the UK Biobank sample the acceleration was more moderate, with an increase of approximately six-fold. These findings suggest that beyond a certain age, structural integrity declines much more rapidly. Indeed, white matter fiber degeneration reduces mechanical support, which promotes thinning and separation of gyri, resulting in a disproportionate sulcal opening. Such mechanisms could explain why changes in sulcal geometry precede measurable cortical thinning during aging [3-4].

The earlier breakpoint observed in UK Biobank may stem from the fact that the sample corresponds to the 10% of subjects with the widest sulci, and therefore the 10% most likely to develop neurodegenerative conditions. In contrast, the stronger acceleration observed in the SENIOR cohort likely reflects late-life processes. Importantly, the replication of this pattern across two distinct cohorts suggests that sulcal opening is a robust biomarker of nonlinear brain aging. Identifying the age at which trajectories shift could be crucial for designing interventions, as preventive strategies may need to be implemented before this breakpoint to maximize effectiveness.

For future research, it would be interesting to assess the evolution of white matter during aging to determine whether structural reorganization coincides with the onset of sulcal opening.
