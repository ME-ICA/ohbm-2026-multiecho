Introduction

Individuals with Alzheimer's disease (AD) exhibit substantial heterogeneity in the severity of amyloid and tau proteinopathies, vascular changes, and neurodegeneration, making the identification of biologically meaningful subtypes across the AD spectrum critical for improving prognosis and tailoring interventions^1. However, biomarker profiles distinguishing subtypes in the preclinical phase of AD remain unexplored. Therefore, we aimed to determine whether data-driven subtyping of preclinical and prodromal AD, based on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and blood-based biomarkers, can identify distinct trajectories of disease progression and clinical outcomes.

Methods

We analyzed baseline biomarkers and longitudinal clinical and cognitive assessments from the PREVENT-AD cohort^2, including up to 12 yearly visits from 298 adults with a family history of AD. All participants were cognitively normal at baseline visit, and 66 participants converted to mild cognitive impairment (MCI) during follow-up assessments (see Figure 1A for demographic and clinical characteristics of the cohort). Plasma biomarkers indexed global tau (p-tau231) and amyloid burden (Aβ42/40 ratio), while lobar White Matter Hyperintensity (WMH) burden was extracted from T1-weighted and FLAIR MRI scans using BISON, and lobar cortical atrophy was derived from T1-weighted MRI scans through voxel-based morphometry using the PELICAN pipeline^3. We applied the SuStaIn (Subtype and Stage Inference) algorithm^4 to cross-sectional baseline data to subtype and stage individuals based on their biomarker profiles. To characterize the overall progression pattern in this at-risk cohort, we first examined biomarker abnormality progression in the entire sample treated as a single subtype. To identify potential heterogeneous subtypes, we then incorporated clustering and cross-validation and selected the optimal number of subtypes using the Cross-Validation Information Criterion. The resulting data-driven subtypes were compared in terms of cognitive decline trajectories using linear mixed-effects models, controlling for age at baseline, education, sex, and baseline cognition as covariates. We included global cognition, visuospatial, language, attention, and immediate/delayed recall scores from the Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status (RBANS, version C).

Results

When examining the average biomarker trajectory of the entire sample as a single subtype, SuStaIn identified a pattern consistent with the model proposed by Jack and colleagues^5, with amyloid and tau abnormality appearing first, followed by posterior WMHs, and finally frontal and temporal lobe atrophy (Figure 2A). In the subtype analysis, SuStaIn identified three distinct subtypes with differing biomarker trajectories: (1) an amyloid/tau-first subtype, (2) a frontotemporal atrophy-first subtype, and (3) a WMH-first subtype, corresponding to proteinopathy-predominant, neurodegeneration-predominant, and cerebrovascular-predominant patterns, respectively  (Figure 2B). At baseline, the subtypes did not differ in terms of global cognition, as measured by the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (Figure 1E), but the AD-pathology-first subtype was significantly older than both the healthy subtype and the atrophy-first group (Figure 1B). Furthermore, the atrophy-first subtype, on average, had higher education levels than the healthy subtype (Figure 1F), while the AD-pathology-first group included relatively more females compared to the atrophy-first subtype (Figure 1E). The rate of conversion to MCI was higher in the AD-pathology-first group than healthy and atrophy-first subtypes (Figure 1C). In follow-up analyses, we found a steeper decline in visuospatial/constructional skills in the WMH-first compared to the atrophy-first subtype (t = -3.15, p < 0.001), as well as faster decline in attention in the atrophy-first compared to the WMH-first subtype (t = -2.56, p = 0.05) (Figure 2C).

Conclusion

These findings in a unique cohort of at-risk individuals support plasma- and imaging-based subtyping as a scalable and clinically translatable approach for capturing heterogeneity in preclinical Alzheimer’s disease pathophysiology and associated cognitive trajectories. By integrating peripheral biomarkers with neuroimaging signatures, this framework enables the identification of biologically distinct disease subtypes prior to overt clinical impairment. This has implications for therapeutic and pharmacological disease management, as interventions in the future could be tailored to subtype-specific mechanisms.
