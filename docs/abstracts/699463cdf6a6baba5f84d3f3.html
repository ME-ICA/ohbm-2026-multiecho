Introduction

Motion sickness (MS) is a reaction to contradicting sensory inputs of the visual, vestibular and proprioceptive system within the sense of balance. This occurs during passive motion (motion induced MS) or in virtual reality conditions (visually induced MS). Such conflicts have an influence on distributed cortical–subcortical networks involving visual, vestibular, and motion related brain regions. Key structures, e.g. the posterior insula, and temporo-parietal junction interact with prefrontal regions that exert top-down modulation of vestibular inputs1. When sensory inputs of systems involved in motion processing diverge, prediction-error signals propagate through thalamo-cortical vestibular pathways and can lead to uneasiness, tiredness, headaches and increases to vertigo or even nausea and vomiting. 

While motion sickness has gained renewed relevance with the emergence of autonomous vehicles, little is known about the cortical response to MS conflicts. EEG research has shown that MS is accompanied by changes in oscillatory dynamics related to sensory reweighting, and attentional allocation. Modulations in alpha activity in particular have been linked to vestibular information processing and its cortical reactivity2. However, most evidence stems from visually induced MS paradigms, where the visual–vestibular conflict is dominant. Although intra‑vestibular conflicts in form of contradicting signals from semicircular canals and otolith organs are highly potent in provoking motion induced MS, the neural mechanisms behind it is still not well understood.

Here we examined how specific EEG rhythms changed as participants gradually began to feel motion sick in a rotating-chair paradigm provoking motion induced MS. By mapping oscillatory changes across different symptom levels, the present work aims to gain further insights in how the cortex encodes vestibular conflict without visual input and to identify spectral markers associated with stages of MS.

Methods

This experiment was funded by the Deutsche Forschungsgemeinschaft (DFG) under Grant No. 416914220. Ethical improvement was done by ethical committee of Charité -Universitätsmedizin Berlin (EA2/184/22).  43 Participants (age = 30.9 ± 11.9, 19 males, 24 females) were included in this study. To ensure motion sickness could be provoked they underwent audiometry and video head impulse testing to validate intact inner ear function. During the experiment participants underwent the Staircase Velocity Motion Test3 to cause motion sickness by inducing an intersensory conflict between the semicircular canals and the otolith organs. The experiment was conducted in complete darkness and with eyes open. During this test participants were rotated around their vertical axis starting with a speed of 6°/s. The speed increased every minute and participants were instructed to move their head according to a metronome at a frequency of 0.25 Hz for 48s and after that a phase of 12 s without head motion to report symptoms. Subjective symptom intensity was assessed every minute by the Motion Illness Symptom Classification, MISC. This scale ranges from 0 to 10. A score of 0 or 1 indicates no specific symptoms. Scores from 2 to 5 correspond to vague to severe dizziness. scores from 6 to 9 indicate some to retching nausea and a score of 10 denotes vomiting. The rotator speed was increased every minute until participants reported to experience a MISC rating of 8, decided they don’t want to continue or an experimental duration of 25 minutes was reached. The time until the experiment was terminated can be interpreted as a measure of a person’s susceptibility to motion sickness. Participants performed the rotating chair experiment on average for 13.1 minutes (SD = 5.5 minutes). Additionally, to the experimental conditions a baseline condition was measured with participants only moving their head according to the metronome without any rotation of the chair. Spectral analysis focused on power changes across standard frequency bands assessed by 32-channel EEG and standard preprocessing using the MNE toolbox for resampling, referencing to average and filtering. Noisy or flat channels were interpolated and eye artifacts were removed using Independent Component Analysis (ICA). Data were segmented into epochs with length of 1s. Epochs containing further artefacts were rejected using autoreject. Afterwards, for lasting epochs power analysis for five typical frequency bands (delta 1-3.9 Hz, theta 4-7.9 Hz, alpha 8-12 Hz, beta 13-30 Hz, gamma 30.1-40) was performed using Fast Fourier Transform and mean areas under the curves were calculated for each stage of motion sickness. Mean power of each frequency band was then aggerated over the time of 45s for each speed in the experiment and electrode were aggregate to four areas of interest( frontal: Fz, F3, F4, F7, F8, Fp1, Fp2, central: FC5, FC6, FC1, FC2, Cz, C3, C4, temporal: TP10, TP9, P8, P7, T7, T8, CP5, CP6 and parieto-occipital: O1, O2, POz, Pz, Oz, P3, P4, CP2, CP1). Resulting mean frequency band power is log-transformed and baseline corrected using mean power of the baseline condition for further analysis (see figure 1). A linear mixed effect model was conducted to examine the influence of the severity of motion sickness measured by the MISC-rating on frequency power in interaction with region and frequency band. The MISC rating was defined as a categorical variable with the possible values low (MISC 0-1), medium (MISC 2-5) or high (MISC 6-8). As covariates age, sex and the susceptibility to motion sickness were included. When conducting post-hoc pairwise comparison of the estimated marginal means p-values were corrected for multiple comparison using False discovery rate and the effect size Cohen´s d and it´s 95% Confidence interval (CI) are reported. 

Results

The linear mixed effect model revealed a significant interaction effect of motion sickness symptom severity with brain region and frequency band (F(24, 10638) = 1.90, p = 0.005). All lower order interactions as well as all three main effects portrayed significant effects as well (all p < 0.05). The covariates age (F(1, 41)=7.14, p=0.011) influenced the frequency power significantly while sex and the susceptibility to motion sickness did not. Random intercepts and slopes indicated variability across participants (SD=0.086), with more variability for high MISC ratings (SD = 0.222), reflecting individual differences in EEG power response to task difficulty. Post hoc pairwise comparison of the three-way interaction (corrected for False discovery rate) of EEG power between different intensities of motion sickness symptoms within different frequency bands and regions showed that alpha power increased first during the transition from low to medium symptom severity in frontal areas (plow-medium = 0.045, d=-0.12, 95% CI [-0.3, 0.1]). Additionally, with an increase of symptoms from medium to high ratings a global significant rise in alpha power can be observed (frontal: pmedium-high =0.004, d = -0.47, 95% CI [-0.79, -0.16], central: pmedium-high =0.002, d = -0.51, 95% CI [-0.82, -0.20], parieto-occipital: pmedium-high =0.006, d = -0.46, 95% CI [-0.77, -0.15], temporal: pmedium-high = 0.003, d = -0.49, 95% CI [-0.80, -0.17]). Concerning the beta frequency band, a significant increase in power can be observed in the parieto-occipital area (plow-medium=0.034, d=-0.24, CI [-0.46, -0.02], pmedium-high= 0.006, d = -0.46, 95% CI [-0.77, -0.15]). For gamma frequency band the shift from low to medium symptom severity showed a significant increase in power in the parieto-occipital (plow-medium=0.023, d=-0.28, 95% CI [-0.50, -0.05]) and temporal (plow-medium=0.024, d=-0.30, 95% CI [-0.52, -0.08]) regions. Whereas, with further increase of symptoms significant reduction of frontal gamma power can be observed (pmedium-high= 0.030, d= 0.41, 95% CI [0.10, 0.72]) accompanied by a further increase in power in parietal areas (pmedium-high=0.040, d= -0.32, 95% CI [-0.64, -0.02]). For delta power initial increase in central, frontal and temporal areas can be observed (frontal: plow-medium =0.024, d = -0.26, 95% CI [-0.48, -0.03], central: plow-medium =0.024, d = -0.24, 95% CI [-0.47, -0.02], temporal: plow-medium = 0.002, d = -0.36, 95% CI [-0.59, -0.14]). followed by a global significant increase of power when symptoms reached high severity (frontal: pmedium-high = 0.004, d = -0.27, 95% CI [-0.79, -0.16], central: pmedium-high = 0.030, d = -0.37, 95% CI [-0.68, -0.06], parieto-occipital: pmedium-high = 0.012, d = -0.42, 95% CI [-0.73, -0.11], temporal: pmedium-high = 0.002, d = -0.48, 95% CI [-0.80, -0.17]). For theta power only, a significant temporal increase appears when comparing data related to medium and high symptom severity (pmedium-high=0.036, d=-0.36, 95% CI [-0.67, -0.05]). All other pairwise comparisons did not show significant changes. Estimated marginal mean of power is displayed for each region and frequency band in Figure 2.

Conclusion

Discussion

This study identifies frequency-specific changes in cortical activation with increasing vestibular conflict during motion-induced MS using a rotating chair experiment. We found a reduction in frontal gamma power when subjects MS symptoms change from medium to high intensity. Frontal gamma activity is linked to multisensory integration, and cognitive control via the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex. Its decline in the context of MS severity may indicate a breakdown of compensatory mechanisms once vestibular conflict exceeds the capacity for cortical stabilization. This aligns with models proposing a progressive loss of top-down regulation during the transition from discomfort to pronounced nausea1.

In contrast, alpha power increases in frontal regions already at the transition from low to medium symptom levels and expanded towards the other areas with further symptom severity. Increased alpha power is typically associated with inhibitory regulation and might have an influence on sensory systems by a re-allocation of resources4. This progression suggests that, with increasing vestibular conflict, the cortex tends to limit incoming sensory information in an attempt to stabilize perception. This strategy may help initially but can become counterproductive once sensory reliability deteriorates. Additionally, the increase in parietal beta power across both symptom transitions may indicate a stronger reliance on internal bodily estimates when sensory motion cues lose consistency. Taken together, the rises in alpha and beta activity point toward a cortical shift toward stabilization and compensation during escalating vestibular conflict.

The global increase of delta power with higher symptom intensity suggests a generalized cortical slowing with a pronounced engagement of autonomic and interoceptive processes, mechanisms known to intensify during nausea states. The increase in temporal theta activity from medium to high symptom intensity may reflect enhanced sensorimotor integration demands during unfolded of intra-vestibular conflict. However, the spatial localization of theta effects has been inconsistent in literature and appears to depend on the investigated stimulation modality5. Our findings therefore suggest that intra-vestibular conflict may engage theta-generating circuits differently from visually induced MS.

Limitations

First, symptom ratings were collected once per minute, which restricts the temporal precision with which EEG changes can be aligned to subjective experience. Symptom-based categorization, although ecologically valid and widely used in MS research, introduces interindividual variability in onset thresholds and temporal dynamics. Second, the spatial resolution of EEG limits precise attribution of oscillatory changes to deep structures such as cerebellum and brainstem vestibular nuclei that are known contributors to MS. Third, Due to explorative nature of our analysis correction for multiple comparison was only applied within each region and frequency band. Furthermore, the rotating-chair paradigm primarily probes intra-vestibular conflict. Extrapolation to visually induced MS or complex multisensory environments should be made with caution.

Conclusion

The results highlight a structured sequence of cortical changes accompanying the development of motion induced MS. Increasing symptoms were associated with rising alpha and beta power across fronto-parietal regions, temporal beta power, global delta power enhancement, and a late-stage reduction of frontal Gamma power. This pattern suggests an early shift toward inhibitory and compensatory mechanisms, followed by a breakdown of frontal integrative processes and increased low-frequency synchronization as nausea intensifies. These oscillatory changes outline how cortical networks reorganize when vestibular conflict grows and may help identify markers relevant for susceptibility assessment or the development of countermeasures.
