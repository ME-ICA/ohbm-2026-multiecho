Introduction

Hemispheric specialization is a fundamental feature of human brain organization, supporting language, cognitive control, and socio-emotional functions1. Although decades of research have documented structural and functional asymmetries across cortical regions, such region-wise descriptions provide limited insight into the large-scale network principles that give rise to hemispheric differentiation. Addressing this gap offers an opportunity to uncover fundamental organizational mechanisms governing hemispheric specialization in the human cortex. 

       Recent studies have begun to examine hemispheric differences from a more global, systems-level perspective2, including the emerging concept of global brain asymmetry, which characterizes large-scale imbalances in functional and structural organization across the two hemispheres3. However, these approaches usually are pure data-driven, and have not directly addressed whether homologous cortical locations are embedded within the same large-scale functional networks, nor how divergence in network affiliation contributes to hemispheric specialization. 

       Here we introduce Interhemispheric Network Divergence (IND), a network-level metric that quantifies whether homotopic cortical locations in the two hemispheres are assigned to the same or different functional networks. Using this approach, we systematically examined how interhemispheric divergence in network relates to evolutionary expansion, cortical microstructure, molecular architecture, and functional organization, relationships that have not been jointly characterized to date. 

Methods

Interhemispheric Network Divergence (IND) was computed on the symmetric fsLR surface by assigning each homotopic vertex pair to large-scale functional networks using 10 templates (Fig. 1). To characterize the functional relevance of IND, we used Neurosynth decoding to identify cognitive processes associated with its spatial pattern. We then performed comprehensive spatial correspondence analyses, comparing IND with multiple functional maps including the principal connectivity gradient, inter-individual variability, intrinsic oscillatory activity (e.g., theta-band power), and other task-free functional annotations.

       In addition, to assess structural organization, IND was correlated with cortical fiber-length profiles capturing short-, intermediate-, and long-range white-matter projections, as well as with microstructural maps such as myelin content and cortical thickness. Evolutionary and developmental influences were examined through cortical expansion maps and neurotransmitter-related expression maps. Transcriptomic associations were quantified, followed by GO enrichment analyses (biological process, cellular component, molecular function). Finally, the relevance of IND to psychiatric vulnerability was explored by correlating IND with cortical thickness and surface-area alteration maps across eight major disorders. All spatial associations were evaluated using 5000 spin-test permutations.

Results

Interhemispheric Network Divergence exhibited a strikingly heterogeneous distribution across the cortex. Rather than following a uniform homotopic organization, IND was strongly concentrated in transmodal association regions of the dorsolateral and orbitofrontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, and temporoparietal territories, whereas unimodal somatosensory, auditory, and visual territories showed minimal divergence (Fig. 1). This spatial pattern closely tracked the principal functional gradient, revealing that hemispheric divergence increases systematically along the sensory-to-transmodal cortical hierarchy (Fig. 2, pspin < 0.001). IND also aligned with major axes of inter-individual variability (Fig. 2, pspin < 0.001), indicating that the regions where hemispheres diverge most at the network level are also those where human brains differ most across individuals.

       From a structural perspective, IND showed robust positive correspondence with cortical thickness and negative correspondence with cortical myelin content (pspin < 0.001). These results suggest that hemispheric divergence disproportionately emerges in microstructurally less myelinated, more plastic association zones, consistent with theories proposing that transmodal cortex supports flexible and context-dependent computations. IND also exhibited significant spatial coupling with fiber-length profiles that index the prevalence of short-, intermediate-, and long-range white-matter projections (Fig. 2, all pspin < 0.001). In particular, areas with higher IND tended to be enriched in intermediate- and long-range projection patterns, underscoring the role of large-scale connectivity architecture in shaping topological hemispheric specialization.

       Evolutionary and developmental analyses further contextualized these findings. IND displayed robust correlations with maps of cortical expansion estimated from comparative primate neuroanatomy and from normative patterns of human developmental expansion (all pspin < 0.001). These associations indicate that IND preferentially localizes to cortical regions that expanded most across human evolution and late postnatal development. Such a relationship suggests that IND captures an organizational property emerging over recent evolutionary timescales, particularly in regions subserving high-order socio-cognitive and symbolic functions.

       Transcriptomic analyses further supported these findings. IND showed spatial correspondence with neurotransmitter-related gene-expression maps, and GO enrichment highlighted biological processes involving signaling, chemical synaptic transmission, and cell communication (all pspin < 0.001). Significant enrichment in cellular components such as synapses, neuronal compartments, plasma membrane structures, and neuron projections further emphasized a molecular basis for hemispheric divergence, while no molecular-function terms reached significance. These results indicate that hemispheric divergence is embedded not only in large-scale connectivity patterns but also in the molecular architecture of the cortex.

       Finally, we assessed the relevance of IND for psychiatric vulnerability. IND demonstrated modest but consistent spatial correspondence with cortical thickness alteration maps across four major psychiatric disorders, including schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, depression, and OCD (all pspin < 0.001). This suggests that regions exhibiting strong hemispheric divergence may represent structurally vulnerable nodes across diagnostic categories. 

Conclusion

Together, these findings establish IND as a previously unrecognized, multiscale organizational dimension underlying human cortical lateralization. IND integrates functional hierarchy, cortical microstructure, network-scale organization, evolutionary expansion, and molecular patterning into a coherent framework that accounts for why hemispheric specialization is most pronounced within high-order association cortex. By characterizing hemispheric differences at the level of functional network affiliations, rather than regional properties or connection strength, IND provides novel insight into the biological bases of hemispheric specialization. This framework further delineates cortical regions in which interhemispheric network differentiation may both support human-unique cognitive capacities and confer vulnerability to psychiatric disorders.
