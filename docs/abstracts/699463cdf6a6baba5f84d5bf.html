Introduction

Visual mental imagery is a core cognitive process that supports comprehension during naturalistic tasks such as reading, yet its neural implementation varies markedly across individuals.1 While imagery is often treated as a subjective faculty, emerging evidence suggests that it can be systematically modulated and mapped at the neural level.2 In this study, we investigate the neural correlates of visual mental imagery elicited during a naturalistic reading task, with particular emphasis on how imagery strength varies parametrically across the imagery spectrum. By combining trial-wise imagery ratings with individual differences in imagery vividness, we aim to characterize brain regions and networks that support imagery generation, as well as alternate mechanisms engaged by individuals with varied imagery ability.

Methods

Functional MRI data were acquired from 40 participants (28 females, age range = 21 - 29 years), with six additional participants excluded due to excessive motion artifacts. Participants completed a reading task comprising 16 passages, including 12 semantically coherent passages and 4 jumbled control passages. Following each passage, participants rated the extent of their visual mental imagery on a four-point scale (1 = none, 2 = low, 3 = mid, 4 = high) (Fig. 1A). Individual imagery ability was assessed using the Vividness of Visual Imagery Questionnaire-2 (VVIQ2).3

At the first level, events were modeled using a canonical hemodynamic response function, with four conditions corresponding to jumbled passages (c0) and low (c1), mid (c2), and high (c3) imagery ratings.4 At the second level, group general linear models were constructed with group mean as the intercept and VVIQ2 scores entered as a continuous regressor to identify imagery-related modulation. Cluster-level thresholding was applied at voxelwise p < 0.001 with a minimum cluster extent of 10 voxels to define regions of interest (ROIs). Mean contrast values were extracted from unthresholded first-level maps within each ROI. ROI-level statistics included one-sample t-tests, permutation-based p-values, Cohen’s d, bootstrap-derived 95% confidence intervals, and false discovery rate correction. To characterize the differences in low imagery ability and high imagery ability, 10 participants with the lowest scores and 10 participants with the highest scores were grouped, and second level analysis was repeated on these two groups.

Results

Behavioral analyses showed that mean imagery ratings during the reading task varied systematically across participants and as measured by VVIQ2, scaled with individual differences in trait imagery vividness (Fig. 1B). This supports the use of imagery ratings and VVIQ2 as parametric predictors in the fMRI analyses that followed.

Group-level analyses reveal distributed cortical activation across the three visual imagery conditions, low (c1), mid (c2), and high (c3), relative to the jumbled reading controls (c0). While the overall spatial pattern of activation was broadly similar across imagery levels, differences in the extent and intensity of activation were evident across conditions indicating that naturalistic reading accompanied by visual imagery engages a distributed set of brain regions at the group level. These regions include a distributed network among the frontal, insular, cingulate, precuneus, precentral, temporal, and lingual regions, and are consistent with the visual and associative cortices engaged during imagery.

Region-of-interest (ROI) analyses showed inter-individual variability as expected. Across all participants (n = 40), mean contrast estimates extracted ROIs defined by the group showed variability within and across participants (Fig. 2D). In the sub groups, the high imagery (c3 - c0) condition elicited distinct responses. The low imagery subgroup (lowest VVIQ scores, n = 10), exhibited a reduced and more variable ROI response across the motor, parietal, and associative regions (Fig 2E). The high imagery subgroup (highest VVIQ scores, n = 10), showed stronger, more consistent responses across temporal, medial and frontal ROIs (Fig 2F). These findings suggest systematic modulation of brain activation as a function of both task-evoked imagery strength and subjective imagery ability.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that visual mental imagery during naturalistic reading can be parametrically modulated and reliably mapped at the group level and further characterized at the individual level using fMRI. Individual differences in imagery vividness shape the recruitment of cortical regions. However, we also observe common trends at the group level when the comparisons are drawn against stimulus type. Paragraphs with “high” imagery and “low” imagery content engaged visual, temporal and medial regions at the group level. ROI analyses revealed substantial inter-subject variability, and participants at the opposite ends of the imagery spectrum show distinct response profiles. These findings support the view that imagery-related brain networks are flexible and may reflect compensatory or alternative strategies in individuals with varied imagery capacity to undertake daily tasks. Naturalistic reading paradigms thus provide a valuable framework for brain mapping of internally generated visual experiences.
