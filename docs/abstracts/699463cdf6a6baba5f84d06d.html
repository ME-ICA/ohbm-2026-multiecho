Introduction

Growing evidence demonstrates that the modern food environment negatively impacts brain health. Yet, few studies investigate the effects of variability in food access, also known as food insecurity (FI), on these aspects of health. According to the UN’s Food and Agriculture Organization, FI affects over 2.3 billion people around the world1, increasing risk of obesity, cardiovascular disease, and all-cause mortality. This risk is calculated after controlling for income, race, ethnicity, education, age, and sex2.

Causal mechanisms behind how FI influences health risk remain an open question. Current hypotheses involve alterations to food-related neurocognition (e.g., food reward, impulsivity, risk-taking), potentiating overconsumption and subsequent gain of adipose tissue3. This is supported by animal studies demonstrating that FI exposure is associated with impaired cognitive flexibility and alterations to neural structures critical to food reward learning (e.g., nucleus accumbens, midbrain, and striatum)4. Analogous research in humans is scarce and often non-naturalistic due to the difficulty of ensuring participant wellbeing during FI. However, experimental methods could still ethically be used to probe causal mechanisms in humans because most of the increase in all-cause mortality occurs with even mild FI2; mild FI is characterized by uncertainty about sufficient capacity to acquire food, contrasting with severe FI, which is characterized by insufficient food quantity and/or quality.

Here, we conducted a pilot study where we experimentally induced mild FI in humans to translate the animal work to the human brain. We hypothesized that even mild FI will produce changes to neurocognition and their neural substrates. In keeping with findings in animal models, we predicted that striatal activity after tasting a high-fat high-sugar drink will decrease. This will be accompanied by increased impulsivity, risk tolerance, and ad libitum food intake.

Methods

To inform a planned single-blinded, randomized, controlled trial, we conducted an ongoing, pre-registered pilot study (https://aspredicted.org/pvmh-52hh.pdf ) using a novel method to induce mild FI in healthy human adults. We aimed to (1) design and validate a paradigm to induce mild FI in participants not currently experiencing FI and (2) test whether even mild FI can result in neurocognitive changes associated with opportunistic overconsumption.

Participants: 16 healthy participants (8 female) aged 18-25 (mean = 22.8 years) were enrolled in the pilot study. Participants were excluded if they reported recent exposure to FI, use of psychotropic medication, or major medical diagnoses. 

Design: The experiment took place across 8 sessions that spanned a two-week period (Figure 1). Sessions 1 and 8 served as a pre- and post-test of outcome measures. Sessions 2-7 induced mild FI by providing meals of variable portion size. We used fMRI to assess brain activity, neuropsychological tests to assess cognition, and a food preference task followed by an ad libitum buffet to assess ingestive behavior.

Food insecurity manipulation: We operationalized mild FI as the uncertainty of food availability2. Uncertainty was induced by varying food provision during Sessions 2-7 between 10% and 40% of estimated daily total energy expenditure (TEE). The meal consisted of sandwiches, apples, and cookies. Following the meal, participants were asked to remain in the lab for an additional 60 minutes to prevent meal supplementation, strengthening the FI manipulation. Participants were told that they were participating in a study testing how hunger influences cognition so that participants remained unaware of the FI manipulation, thus allowing for an ecologically valid induction of uncertainty about the availability of food. Participants were debriefed at study conclusion. Uncertainty was verified by self-report in response to the question “Before the session today, how certain were you that you would receive enough food to eat?” using a visual analogue scale (1 = maximal uncertainty and 10 = maximal certainty).

Neurocognitive assessments: Impulsivity was assessed using three measures: a temporal discounting constant from a monetary choice questionnaire, motor compulsivity via a go/no-go task, and trait questionnaires. Risk tolerance was assessed using the Balloon Analogue Risk Task5.

Behavioural measures: Willingness to pay was tested using a modified Becker-DeGroot-Marschak auction where participants bid on 36 equicaloric, equally liked food pictures. Food intake and eating rate were assessed via ad libitum buffet of the same lunch meal as in the FI manipulation.

fMRI taste stimulation: To measure striatal response to food reward, participants were given high-fat high-sugar chocolate and strawberry milkshakes inside the MRI scanner with an artificial saliva as control (KCl, NaHCO3). Participants rated subjective liking, wanting, and familiarity of each milkshake pre-scan. The milkshakes were delivered in an event-related design with visual cues preceding taste delivery and a 1-4 second jitter between trials.

Data acquisition: fMRI data were collected at Montréal General Hospital (3T Siemens Prisma, 64-channel coil; TE=34ms, TR=1500ms, 2mm isotropic, flip angle=70°, FOV=192mm, multiband=4, 72 interleaved slices). MPRAGE provided anatomical reference (TE=2.52ms, TR=1900ms, 1mm isotropic, flip angle=9°, FOV=250mm, 176 slices).

Data preprocessing and analysis: Preprocessing included slice timing correction, realignment, distortion correction (topup with opposite phase encoding), spatial normalization (MNI152), and smoothing (6mm³). GLM analyses used SPM12 with age, sex, and BMI as covariates. Given our small pilot sample, a less stringent cluster-defining threshold (0.005) tested a priori hypotheses with small volume correction (striatal mask, WFU_PickAtlas). Post hoc whole-brain analyses used standard thresholds (0.001) with family-wise error correction.

Results

Low certainty of food availability on the post-test day: The primary index for the validity of the paradigm was whether uncertainty about food availability was induced. All participants reported feeling uncertain that they would be provided enough food to eat for lunch on the post-test day (mean=2.43, SD=1.54).

Increased ad libitum consumption: Participants consumed significantly more kcal at the post-test when compared to the pre-test (mean increase=43.6% kcal, d=1.12, p<0.001). No differences in food-general or macronutrient-specific valuation were observed.

Impulsivity was unaffected by experimental manipulation: As expected, impulsivity traits as measured by the UPPS-P did not differ before and after development of uncertainty. However, contrary to our hypotheses, no significant differences were observed in either temporal discounting or motor compulsivity. However, there was a trending increase in risk tolerance (mean increase=18%, d=0.53, p=0.06).

Neuroimaging results: Participants exhibited a trending decrease in BOLD response in the bilateral VS in response to the high-fat high-sugar milkshakes at the post-test compared to the pre-test (pSVC = 0.09; Figure 2A). BOLD response in the bilateral dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) was positively correlated with self-reported uncertainty (pFWE= 0.03; whole-brain corrected; Figure 2B). 

Conclusion

We developed and piloted a novel experimental paradigm to induce mild FI in healthy human adults by varying meal provision over 8 days in a two-week period. Our results provide preliminary evidence that even short-term exposure to uncertainty about food availability may alter neural responses, ingestive behavior, and neurocognition in a manner consistent with hypothesized mechanisms behind the health risk uniquely generated by food insecurity. Neural responses to palatable food may be attenuated, ad libitum food consumption increased, and risk tolerance may be enhanced. These results are consistent with both the previous animal work and influential theories of obesity contending that reduced responses (often localized to the striatum and other dopaminergic source and target areas) to predicted food rewards contribute to overeating. In the context of FI, neuroplasticity that promotes overconsumption would clearly be an adaptive mechanism, allowing one to store energy reserves and insure against times of scarcity. However, when food security is restored, overeating becomes maladaptive, as it may lead to weight gain and concomitant increases in risk for obesity and cardiovascular disease. 

The positive correlation between BOLD response in the dlPFC and self-reported uncertainty about food availability is also consistent with the hypothesis that exposure to FI may increase impulsivity and risk-taking behavior. Increased dlPFC activity may reflect compensatory cognitive control in participants who experienced greater uncertainty of food availability.

A key strength of this paradigm is its ecological validity. Unlike previous experimental approaches that rely on explicit instructions or imagined scenarios, our method induced genuine uncertainty through experience of variable food provision. The cover story that we were studying how hunger affects cognition allowed participants to experience food insecurity naturalistically without demand characteristics biasing their responses. The success of this approach is evidenced by low certainty ratings at post-test, suggesting that participants genuinely did not know whether they would receive adequate food.

However, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the brief exposure period may not fully capture the chronic stress and metabolic adaptations associated with real-world food insecurity. Second, participants’ dietary patterns outside of experiment days were not collected. Third, participants may have become familiar with study meals and procedures, potentially confounding post-test measurements. Without a control group that receives meals of consistent portion sizes, we cannot attribute observed changes to uncertainty rather than repeated testing or other time-dependent factors.

A planned, single-blinded, randomized, controlled trial will address these limitations by including a control group receiving meals of consistent portion size matched for total caloric provision. Additionally, given established sex differences in both food insecurity prevalence and metabolic responses to nutritional stress, we will analyze outcomes stratified by sex and explore potential effects of gender identity on the psychological experience of food uncertainty.

In conclusion, these pilot data support the feasibility of experimentally modeling mild FI in humans and provide preliminary evidence for mechanisms - increased consumption and altered reward processing - that may contribute to the elevated health risk observed in food-insecure populations.
