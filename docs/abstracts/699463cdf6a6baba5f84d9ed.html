Introduction

Cytoarchitecture describes the distribution, density, shape, and size of cell bodies and has been a fundamental criterion for defining individual brain areas. Distinct cytoarchitectonic patterns are associated with specific connectivity1,2 and support specific functions.3 Thus, cytoarchitecture is far more than an anatomical curiosity, it is intrinsically tied to how the brain develops, operates, and adapts. A thorough understanding of cytoarchitecture is therefore essential for elucidating the structural and functional organization of the brain and its pathology. Modern image analysis techniques enable sophisticated, observer-independent assessments of cytoarchitecture. For example, texture analysis has been applied to histological sections to reveal structural differences between cortical subregions4. This approach yields quantitative Haralick features that characterize image properties based on the distribution of gray-level pairs. Although texture analysis is a powerful tool for detecting cytoarchitectonic differences in the brain, the resulting measures are not directly interpretable in terms of underlying cellular organization. To address this limitation, we combined image texture analysis with cell segmentation algorithm to elucidate how texture features reflect specific cytoarchitectonic properties.

Methods

We analyzed 98 cortical patches from 12 areas of the BigBrain dataset (areas: 44, Fp1, 4a, 7P, FG1, FG3, FG4, hOc1, hOc2, hOc3d, hOc4d, hOc5). Texture analysis was performed using the Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix, and 21 Haralick features invariant to the number of grey levels were extracted. Cell segmentation was conducted using the Contour Proposal Network.5 Both texture analysis and cell segmentation were applied separately to the six cortical layers, resulting in texture and cellular data for 588 images. From the segmentation output, we derived a range of cellular metrics, including mean cell size, percentage of cell covered pixels in the image, ratio of large to small cells, kernel density estimates, number of neighboring cells within a 100 µm radius and mean distance to them, mean distance to 10 nearest neighbors and spatial autocorrelation (Morans’ I). Additionally, graph-based clustering using the Louvain algorithm was employed to identify cell clusters and characterize their properties including modularity, cluster size, number of cells per cluster, intra-cluster distances and number of clusters relative to cell count per layer. These cellular features were correlated with the 21 texture features to identify which texture measures are sensitive to specific aspects of cellular arrangement.

Results

Correlation analysis revealed that the 21 texture features could be grouped into three main clusters based on their correlation patterns. The first group exhibited correlation profiles indicative of sparse distribution of cells and small cell clusters. This pattern was observed primarily in images of the cortical layer 1. The second group reflected denser cell distributions while still displaying clustered organization of the cells. Such pattern was associated with images of layers 3-5. The third group of texture features indicated a relatively homogenous distribution of densely packed cells and was mostly associated with the cortical layer 2. Within these groups, individual texture features could be further differentiated according to their positive, negative or absent correlations with the mean cell size.  

Conclusion

We demonstrate that image texture features are systematically associated with distinct cytoarchitectonic properties corresponding to specific cortical layers. This work establishes an important link between abstract image-derived features and biologically interpretable aspects of brain microstructure. This enables the informed application of advanced image analysis techniques to the study of cellular organization. Our approach represents an important step towards a detailed and quantitative description of cytoarchitecture. Extending this approach to additional cortical and subcortical regions, as well as to other species, opens the possibility to link cytoarchitectonic organization with functional, behavioral and pathological processes.
