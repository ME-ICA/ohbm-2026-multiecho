Introduction

Visual-induced kinesthetic illusion (V-KI) evokes subjective sensations of movement without physical action, accompanied by increased excitability in primary motor cortex (M1), particularly when the observed movement matches the observer's body part (Kaneko et al., Neuroscience, 2007). However, previous fMRI studies reported significant activation in the bilateral supplementary motor areas, contralateral dorsal and ventral premotor areas, and superior and inferior parietal lobules, while activation in M1 was not statistically significant (Kaneko et al., PLoS One, 2015). Despite the high clinical applicability of V-KI, the neurophysiological basis for the subjective intensity of the illusion remains unclear. This study aimed to clarify how the strength of the subjective kinesthetic illusion induced by visual input is explained by cortical activation patterns and to elucidate the neural correlates of the kinesthetic sensation as a psychological phenomenon. This will help verify whether brain activity can predict the strength of an illusion in the absence of overt movement or proprioceptive stimuli. This will contribute to a more refined understanding of how motor-related cortical networks engage with visually generated motor experiences.

Methods

Fifteen healthy adults participated in this fMRI study using a 3T GE SIGNA Premier scanner equipped with a head-mounted display (HMD). Participants observed videos of wrist flexion-extension movements of self-body and other-body perspectives, as well as a baseline fixation video. Each video type was presented for 12 seconds in a randomized order across 13 blocks, repeated in two experimental sets. This study was conducted with the approval of the Ethics Committee of Tokyo Metropolitan University.

Functional imaging data were acquired using echo-planar imaging (EPI) with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 1000 ms, echo time (TE) = 30 ms, flip angle = 70°, matrix size = 128×128, voxel size = 1.875×1.875×2 mm³, slice thickness = 2 mm, interslice gap = 2 mm, number of slices = 73, and number of volumes = 585.

Two sessions were conducted on separate days. On Day 1, participants underwent visual-only stimulation. On Day 2, synchronized tactile stimulation was applied manually to the fingers (index to little finger) using a soft brush, aligned with a pre-recorded tactile video, and presented concurrently with the visual stimuli inside the MRI scanner. After each session, participants rated body ownership and illusion intensity using a visual analog scale ranging from -10 cm (strongly disagree) to +10 cm (strongly agree).

Preprocessing of fMRI data included realignment, co-registration, normalization to MNI space, and spatial smoothing (8 mm FWHM), performed using SPM12. Beta values for 20 predefined ROIs (Kaneko et al., PLoS One, 2015) were extracted using the MarsBaR toolbox. A generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) was fitted using beta values as predictors and illusion intensity as the dependent variable. Model selection was based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and subject-specific random intercepts were included to account for repeated measures.

Results

Tactile stimulation significantly enhanced both body ownership and kinesthetic illusion intensity for the virtual self-body. Stepwise GLMM identified the left primary motor cortex (M1) and left superior parietal lobule (SPL) as explanatory variables for illusion intensity. Among them, the left M1 was a significant predictor (p = 0.0041; 95% CI: [1.2885, 6.1427]). The model demonstrated robust goodness-of-fit, with a significant reduction in AIC compared to null models. Left M1 β-values exhibited a strong positive association with illusion ratings, indicating that individuals with higher M1 activation experienced stronger illusions.

The left SPL also contributed modestly as an explanatory variable, with a trend-level effect (p = 0.081; 95% CI: [–0.269, 3.694]), suggesting a possible interaction or modulatory role in supporting embodied perception. No other premotor or parietal regions showed significant predictive value when tested independently. The confidence interval for M1 effect size suggests a medium-to-large effect, supporting its role as a core generator of embodied illusion.

Conclusion

Our results reveal a direct relationship between cortical activity in the primary motor cortex and the subjective strength of visually-induced kinesthetic illusion. This study newly identifies M1 as a significant neural correlate of the psychological intensity of movement illusion. These findings reinforce the idea that the subjective quality of kinesthetic illusion is not merely a byproduct of multisensory integration but is tightly linked to the engagement of motor-executive regions.

Notably, previous studies using vibration or tendon stimulation have already established the involvement of the primary motor cortex (M1) in proprioceptive-driven kinesthetic perception (Naito et al, Neuron, 2002). However, these studies relied on afferent input that inherently activates the sensorimotor system. In contrast, the present study uniquely demonstrates that M1 can also be engaged during visually induced kinesthetic illusions (V-KI) in the absence of any proprioceptive stimulation. By isolating visual input as the sole driver of the illusory motion experience, our findings provide compelling evidence that motor areas are capable of internal simulation even without somatosensory feedback.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that the primary motor cortex is directly involved in generating visual-driven motor-sensory illusions even in the absence of peripheral sensory input. This extends our understanding of sensorimotor integration beyond overt actions and proprioceptive input.
