Introduction

The human brain’s higher-order cognition depends on the dynamic integration of large-scale networks, with white matter (WM) tracts providing the indispensable structural substrate for long-range information transmission. While research regarding functional networks—predominantly focused on gray matter connectivity—is currently quite mature, the specific structural networks underlying this functional activity remain largely unexplored.1

Traditionally, neuroimaging has viewed WM primarily as a structural cabling system. However, recent studies have confirmed the existence of physiologically meaningful blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signals within WM.These signals reflect neural activity associated with specific behavioral tasks, providing a novel perspective for understanding cerebral transmission mechanisms. Therefore, we aim to examine this relationship using WM fiber activity.

To reconstruct brain architecture, diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) estimates the anisotropic diffusion of water in fiber bundles, yielding voxel-wise tensors whose principal orientations support tractography and quantify WM integrity. In parallel, the functional correlation tensor (FCT) models the local, direction-dependent synchrony of BOLD signals among neighboring WM voxels. This captures anisotropic patterns of functional coupling that conventional structural imaging cannot reveal.

By bridging these structural and functional domains, this study investigates the dynamics of structure-function coupling (SFC) under motor and working memory states. We achieve this by quantifying the geometric similarity between structural tensors and functional tensors within specific WM tracts. Simultaneously, by examining differences in information transmission mechanisms across distinct cognitive loads within working memory (i.e., 0-back vs. 2-back), we aim to elucidate the task-specificity of WM functional architecture.

Methods

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) data were obtained from the Human Connectome Project (HCP) Young Adult database, comprising 95 subjects aged 26–30 years (42 females, 53 males). The dataset included Diffusion-Weighted Imaging (DWI), T1-weighted structural imaging, and task-based functional MRI (fMRI) for motor and working memory (0-back and 2-back conditions) protocols. All datasets underwent standardized preprocessing via the HCP minimal preprocessing pipelines.

For structural analysis, Diffusion Tensor (DT) models were reconstructed from DWI data to generate Fractional Anisotropy (FA) maps. Analysis was strictly restricted to WM regions defined by an FA threshold of > 0.2 to minimize partial volume effects and signal contamination from non-neural tissues. For functional analysis, to ensure the FCT accurately reflected underlying neural activity, fMRI data underwent spatial smoothing (6 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel) to enhance the signal-to-noise ratio, followed by temporal filtering to isolate low-frequency BOLD fluctuations. Nuisance regression was applied to remove signals from cerebrospinal fluid and gray matter, mitigating physiological noise and cortical signal contamination.

To characterize regional heterogeneity, the JHU ICBM-DTI White-Matter Labels atlas was registered to each subject's individual native space. Regional segmentation was performed based on the corresponding locations within the FA maps and strictly masked by the FA thresholds to ensure anatomical accuracy. To quantify structure-function similarity, a modified tensor similarity algorithm based on the Euclidean distance approach was employed.2 This metric integrates FA weighting: the inverse of Euclidean distance characterizes geometric proximity, while an FA product term serves as a weighting factor to ensure that the similarity metric possesses substantive physiological meaning only when significant anisotropic characteristics are present in both structural and functional tensors.

Finally, this integrated framework was applied to quantify the degree of SFC coupling within WM regions. Furthermore, to investigate task-specific functional activation patterns, we independently analyzed differences in the FA of the FCT across tasks. By employing paired sample t-tests to compare the motor task with both the low-load (0-back) and high-load (2-back) cognitive tasks, we aimed to elucidate the specific activation characteristics and connectivity differences of functional networks under varying cognitive loads.

Results

Geometric similarity analysis between DT and FCT revealed that structure-function coupling (SFC) indices within cognitive and attentional networks were significantly higher during working memory (WM) conditions compared to motor conditions, as shown in Figure 1a, b.

In terms of regional distribution, regions showing significantly enhanced coupling across both working memory load conditions (0-back and 2-back) compared to the motor task included the Left Corticospinal Tract (CST L), Left Anterior Limb of Internal Capsule (ALIC L), and Right Tapetum (Tapetum R). Regarding task specificity, the 0-back condition exhibited significant coupling enhancement in the Genu of the Corpus Callosum (Genu CC) and the Right Superior Corona Radiata (SCR R). Conversely, the 2-back condition involved a broader network, with significant differences observed in the Left Anterior Corona Radiata (ACR L), Right Superior Corona Radiata (SCR R), and Left Superior Longitudinal Fasciculus (SLF L).

The finding that working memory tasks exhibit significantly higher structure-function similarity than motor tasks suggests that cognitive processing requires stronger geometric alignment between functional transmission and structural anatomy. Among the overlapping significant regions, the elevated coupling in the CST L implies the CST is more than a passive output conduit. Research indicates the CST exercises preventive inhibitions to withhold premature actions and attenuate noise. Unlike the diffuse activation typical of motor tasks, this inhibitory signaling during working memory is strictly confined to the anatomical pathway. Consequently, it yields 'cleaner' directional coherence, highlighting the CST’s role in integrating cognitive information with motor output.3 Similarly, high similarity in the ALIC L and Tapetum R indicates reliance on these pathways for interference filtering and sensory integration during cognitive tasks. 

Regarding task-specific mechanisms, the 0-back task requires high bilateral prefrontal synchronization. Studies link inter-hemispheric efficiency for vigilance to the Genu CC.4 In contrast, during high-load (2-back) tasks, the SLF acts as the Fronto-Parietal Network backbone. Our findings suggest that under high demands, functional connectivity becomes constrained to these specific long-range pathways to ensure rapid and accurate information exchange between frontal and parietal regions.

Furthermore, paired sample t-tests of FCT FA explore task-specific activation, as shown in Figure 1a, b. Five regions primarily involving visual processing and attention, showed higher FA during the 0-back task relative to motor conditions: the Left External Capsule (EC L), Left Sagittal Stratum (SS L), Left Anterior Corona Radiata (ACR L), Retrolenticular Part of Internal Capsule, and Left Corticospinal Tract (CST L). Notably, despite being traditionally viewed as a motor output pathway, the CST showed elevated functional FA during the 0-back task. This suggests active recruitment during stimulus discrimination and response inhibition, reflecting its role in motor preparation and cognitive control. Conversely, only the Left Cerebral Peduncle showed higher activation during motor conditions. As this region transmits motor commands to the spinal cord, the sustained, rhythmic right-hand movements likely produced stronger functional anisotropy.

Two regions exhibited significantly higher FA during working memory: the SLF L and Left Inferior Fronto-Occipital Fasciculus (IFOF L). Both pathways are integral to semantic retrieval and phonological loop integration. Previous research shows that the brain automatically converts nameable visual stimuli into abstract semantic concepts for encoding.5 Our results demonstrate that high-load WM tasks significantly activate the left-hemisphere language-semantic network, confirming that WM functional metrics align closely with the phonological and semantic components of classical working memory models.

Conclusion

By quantifying the geometric similarity between DT and FCT within WM regions, alongside analyzing functional anisotropy, this study systematically investigates the dynamic modulation of SFC under varying cognitive loads. Our findings reveal that working memory tasks exhibit significantly higher structure-function similarity in cognition- and attention-related WM regions compared to simple motor tasks.

Regarding information transmission mechanisms, the results suggest that high-load cognitive tasks engage WM pathways with higher directional specificity (e.g., for signal integration and inhibition), whereas simple motor execution may involve more diffuse or saturated functional activation that yields lower geometric alignment with the structural core. Specifically, significant enhanced SFC and FA were observed in critical pathways such as the CST and ALIC, underscoring their involvement in cognitively controlled motor decision-making and response inhibition.

Furthermore, this study delineates distinct WM functional profiles across different cognitive loads. Under low-load conditions, coupling enhancement predominantly occurs in structures facilitating sustained vigilance and inter-hemispheric communication, such as the Genu CC. Under high-load conditions, the brain recruits semantic retrieval mechanisms that convert visual imagery into semantic concepts for nameable stimuli. These high-demand tasks also rely on long-range fibers within the Fronto-Parietal Network to enable rapid information update and exchange.

Through tensor analysis techniques, this study reveals how the brain achieves task-specific dynamic integration within a finite structural substrate, providing critical empirical evidence for WM's dynamic role and functional plasticity in cognitive control.
