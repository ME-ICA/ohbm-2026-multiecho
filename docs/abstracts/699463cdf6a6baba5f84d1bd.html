Introduction

Decades of experimental tracer studies in mammalian species, including mouse, cat and non-human primates, have generated detailed wiring diagrams of cortical areas.1 In humans, non-invasive estimates of connectivity derived from diffusion MRI tractography and functional neuroimaging have combined to reveal, at a coarser scale, the spatial layout of the brain’s network of connections.2

The thalamus is a densely connected subcortical structure that serves as a hub for sensory processing, motor control and integration of activity across the cortex. Diffusion MRI tractography has been used to delineate individual thalamic nuclei and their connectivity profiles. However, human in vivo tractography lacks histological ground truth, and is known to suffer from both Type I and Type II errors. When examining in vivo estimates of regional brain connectivity, it is vital to compare results to the existing, ‘gold standard’ anatomical and experimental literature. 

To aid in this, we have performed a series of systematic literature surveys, manually screening more than >2800 studies to date to collate extant information on the organisation of cortical connections from 29 thalamic nuclei in the primate brain. Using a tiered rating system to classify strength of evidence, we provide the seed for a ‘living network’ to be updated over time in line with new evidence. With a dedicated website and open access data repository, MANUAL provides a portal to an evidence-based and expert-verified thalamocortical connectome, versioned to track new additions and amendments and with scope to extend to other brain systems and networks.

Methods

To define thalamic nuclei, we used the hierarchical schema defined by Ding et al.3 Twenty-nine nuclei within six nuclear groups (anterior; medial; lateral; posterior; intralaminar; midline) were defined based by the atlas (Fig. 1). 

To allow integration with existing and future neuroimaging studies, we defined cortical areas using the Desikan-Killiany-Tourville (DKT) atlas (Fig. 1). The DKT atlas defines 31 cortical regions-of-interest based on sulcal landmarks, we additionally included the amygdala and hippocampus as key thalamic targets. Where possible, corresponding Brodmann Area (BA) labels were identified for each cortical area using a digitised surface atlas.4

For each thalamic nucleus, systematic literature surveys were performed using a pre-defined lexical search strategy with Boolean operators. Search terms included phrases to identify functional, structural, experimental and lesion studies while excluding non-primate studies, reviews, preprints and conference papers. Search results were screened based on title and abstract for inclusion into the study using set criteria.

Each article included in the study was read to identify evidence of connections to or from discrete cortical areas. Evidence was graded into three tiers: ‘0’ No evidence found; ‘1’ Indirect evidence (e.g.: tractography); ‘2’ Direct evidence (e.g.: histology, tracer studies), and ‘3’ Multiple lines of evidence. For each connection, the highest level of evidence was recorded along with citation (including figure and/or text location) and, optionally, experimental details (Fig. 1). Once completed, evidence for each nucleus’ cortical connections were independently verified using the text citations by another investigator.

All searches, screening and evidence ratings were performed manually without use of Large Language Models or other automated tools. 

For comparison, we performed diffusion tractography using multishell diffusion-weighted imaging acquired in 100 participants of the Human Connectome Project. Streamlines were seeded in the nucleus reuniens and average (normalised) connectivity strength displayed on the surface.

Results

To date, we have screened 2825 studies, identifying evidence for 53 unique thalamocortical connections from four nuclei to 23 cortical areas. 

Of 53 connections, the most common source of evidence was non-human primate tracer studies (49/53; 92%), followed by diffusion tractography (12/53; 23%). In total, 79 connections had no supporting evidence, four connections were supported by indirect evidence (grade = 1), 37 by direct evidence (grade = 2) and 12 by multiple lines of evidence (grade = 3). 

Example connectivity maps are shown in Figure 2 for the posterior nucleus of the thalamus (Po) and the nucleus reuniens (medioventral nucleus; Re). Performing comparative diffusion tractography, we found that while streamlines from the nucleus reuniens faithfully captured connections to the frontal cortex, they failed to accurately trace connections to medial parietal and superior temporal cortex (Figure 2).

Conclusion

With MANUAL, we aim to provide a community-led and expert-verified gold standard reference for tractography studies of the human brain. The MANUAL project surveys current available evidence to support the existence of physical connections between specific thalamic nuclei and cortical areas in the primate brain. 

Future developments will include versioned releases and invitations for contributions from neuroanatomical experts around the world. Moving forward, MANUAL can also serve as a valuable ground truth resource for validating future  LLM-based studies aimed at summarising scientific literature.
