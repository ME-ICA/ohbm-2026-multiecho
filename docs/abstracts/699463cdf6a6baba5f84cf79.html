Introduction

The hippocampus plays a central role in forming cognitive maps, yet how its system-level activity evolves during the earliest stages of map-learning remains unclear. Classic rodent research suggests that cells in the hippocampus increase their theta-phase precession and firing rates to preferred place fields in learned maps particularly during active relative to passive navigation. More recent rodent studies specify a progression of non-selective hippocampal neural firing during early learning to more sparse, selective, and orthogonal spatial coding at late-learning.1,2 Further, spatial navigation studies in humans report reduction in hippocampal activity with goal proximity and more stable responses with greater attention to locations. These together suggest that active self-directed navigation is associated with lower hippocampal system-level metabolic demand with the formation of more efficient map representations. However, previous human fMRI studies to measure whole-hippocampal map-learning activity have been limited by insufficient spatial resolution, incomplete brain coverage, and distortions that complicate subfield-level interpretations. Here, we report human functional imaging data based on a novel immersive virtual-maze paradigm with 3T MRI and also ultra-high-field 7T MRI to obtain whole brain coverage that more precisely measures hippocampal along with whole-brain mesoscopic-scale responses during the initial phase of map learning. We hypothesized that, consistent with our prior research based on regions-of-interest (ROIs) in low-resolution 3T scans,3 hippocampal activity at 7T would show a clear voxel-wise reduction during active exploration that complements robust activation in task-relevant cortical networks.

Methods

We briefly incorporate data from our previous study using a 32-channel head coil 3T Siemens Skyra system in which 21 participants engaged in an immersive virtual maze containing realistic city scenes and landmark stores connected by traversal paths during fMRI scanning (Fig. 1). During map-learning, participants either freely navigated the maze using a button box to move (Free) or were shown video tours (Tour). Participants were then tested during map-retrieval, in which they navigated to different targets from various start positions. 3T map-learning functional data were collected over 4 echo-planar-imaging (EPI) runs each with 100 volumes (240 s) consisting of 38 axial slices with in-plane resolution 2.8 mm x 2.8 mm (78 x 78 matrix), 3 mm thickness, and repetition time (TR) 2.4 s. Coplanar T2-weighted images with higher in-plane resolution (1 mm x 1 mm) were acquired for anatomical registration to a T1-weighted magnetization prepared rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE) image at 1 mm isotropic resolution with 192 sagittal acquisitions, and subsequent spatial normalization. Analysis focused on map-learning response estimates at Junction, Landmark, and Traversal events between Free and Tour conditions in whole-brain voxel-wise general linear models (GLM) and anterior-to-posterior hippocampal ROIs based on the Automated Anatomical Label (AAL) atlas. 

Critically, we further acquired proof-of-concept data using a Siemens Terra 7T scanner with an 8-channel transmit/32-channel receive head coil. Three healthy adult participants freely navigated a similar virtual maze during this second fMRI scanning protocol (Fig. 1). Here, participants actively moved for 270 s followed by 40 s of rest cyclically for up to 1 hour (3 runs). Functional data were collected with a custom k-t–accelerated 3D echo planar imaging (EPI) sequence with skipped controlled-aliasing-in-parallel-imaging (skipped-CAIPI; 3×2, kz-shift = 1, 4 shots per k-plane with a EPI factor of 25) segmentation4 with an additional k-t undersampling factor of 6, enabling 600 micron isotropic resolution and whole-brain coverage within a volume TR of 3.5 s. Identical acquisition parameters were used for both functional and anatomical imaging (TE = 18.3 ms, 298×298 matrix, 152 slices, excitation flip angle = 18°, no partial Fourier). For anatomical reference, we acquired magnetization-transfer (MT)–weighted 3D-EPI using the same sequence framework5, which provides high tissue contrast suitable for subfield segmentation and high resolution fMRI analysis directly in native EPI space. All k-space data were reconstructed in a low-rank subspace with rank = 32 and regularization strength of 0.001, chosen to maximize temporal SNR while minimizing spatial blurring. Hippocampal subfields were segmented from the MT-weighted EPI images. Functional responses during early map-learning were estimated in these subfields and in whole-brain voxel-wise examinations using a GLM that contrasted block conditions of active exploration versus rest.

Results

At 3T, anterior hippocampal responses to Junctions, Landmarks, and Traversal events were lower during Free compared to Tour map-learning conditions, with responses to Junctions notably even lower than for Traversal events. Lower hippocampal responses to Junctions in contrast to Traversals during map-learning were associated with more successful and faster retrieval navigation to target locations. Thus, reduction in hippocampal activity suggested an underlying sparse but selective neuronal engagement at the sub-voxel level, albeit at the voxel resolution of 3T imaging. Regions including the orbitofrontal cortex, caudate, supplementary motor area, and middle temporal gyrus, implicated in decision-making, showed higher responses during Free than Tour map-learning, which correlated with hippocampal sparse response. Crucially, at 7T, whole-brain voxel-wise analyses similarly revealed robust and distributed task-evoked activity during active spatial map learning (Fig. 2). Relative to rest, active navigation elicited widespread positive responses (red overlay in Fig. 2AB) across the left motor cortex, visual cortex, cerebellum, and dorsal-attention, frontoparietal, and salience networks, consistent with the multimodal demands of visually guided, goal-directed exploration, and consistent with the 3T findings. Concurrently, negative responses emerged in the right motor cortex and default-mode regions (blue overlay in Fig. 2A), reflecting contralateral motor suppression due to right-hand button-box use as well as increased attentional engagement and externally oriented cognitive processing during navigation. Focusing within the hippocampus, the entire hippocampal formation showed a strikingly uniform reduction in activity during active exploration compared with rest. This reduction was spatially diffuse across the full anatomical extent of the hippocampus (Fig. 2C) and aligns with the prior 3T findings. Subfield-resolved analyses (Fig. 2D) further demonstrated consistently negative responses across CA1–3, dentate gyrus, hippocampal tail, subiculum, and entorhinal cortex. In contrast, surrounding cortical regions—specifically perirhinal areas 35/36 and parahippocampal cortex—exhibited positive responses, with the parahippocampal cortex showing the strongest activation. This opposing pattern suggests a functional dissociation during the early phase of spatial learning. That is, while the hippocampus reduces system-level activity as map representations begin to form, adjacent cortical regions provide strong visuospatial and contextual support necessary for constructing route knowledge.

Conclusion

A rich collection of studies have elucidated spiking activities in local fields of approximately thousands of neurons as well as individual neurons in the hippocampus involved in encoding cognitive maps of spatial environments and the animal’s location within them. Firing patterns of hippocampal neurons in cases of better map-learning essentially show greater selectivity and orthogonality in responding to different spatial locations.1 Nevertheless, important knowledge gaps remain about the system-level neurocomputational mechanism underlying the formation of these cognitive maps in the hippocampus, the translation of animal-based findings to human hippocampal map-learning and retrieval, whole-brain communication and control contributing to and dependent on hippocampal map computations, and the role of metabolic constraints on hippocampal computational strategies. Our findings in this present work that combines human functional brain images acquired across 3T and 7T systems progressively addresses the above knowledge gaps. At 3T, we showed that when using approximately 3 mm isotropic hippocampal voxels, which consists of hundreds of thousands of neurons, mean blood oxygen level dependent (BOLD) signal in anterior hippocampal regions are lower when human participants navigate spaces with agency compared to passive touring.3 Reduced hippocampal BOLD responses were associated with greater motor-decision-related processing responses in frontal, striatal, and temporal regions, and better route retrieval performance later on. Critically, our 7T data validated our observations at 3T in that we found, at the mesoscale, clearly functionally delineated and reduced hippocampal BOLD during active map-learning relative to other adjacent brain regions. This replicates our findings at the 3T level now also at 600 micron resolution, which consists of tens of thousands of neurons. Integrating across hippocampal neural selectivity and coding orthogonality found in animal studies, and our human 3T and 7T data, we suggest that more efficient cognitive map representations of the spatial environment in the hippocampus are associated with lower metabolic requirements as measured using BOLD. Such lowered BOLD responses during more effective spatial map encoding likely reflects sparse encoding of spatial locations in the entire hippocampal system that involves more selective firing of neurons reserved for preferred place fields in a non-redundant manner system-wide. Our findings diverge from the notion that better spatial map processing involves greater hippocampal activity in general. We propose instead that human hippocampal function should be considered within a framework of reflecting energetically conserved operations that encode the state-space of its afferential inputs.
