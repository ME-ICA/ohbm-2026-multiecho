Introduction

Dementia, a progressive neurodegenerative condition, is devastating for individuals and carers bringing loss of independence, decreased quality of life and suffering with huge societal costs. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) is important to assess the regional vulnerability to neurodegeneration and inform about dementia subtypes, alongside exclusion of confounding intracranial pathology.1 However, technological, social and economic barriers limit the accessibility to MRI, motivating the design and implementation of cost-effective and accessible MRI, such as ultra-low field (ULF) MRI (below 0.2T).2 These scanners are safer for people with MRI contraindications, and are portable. This study specifically investigates the correspondence of brain scans acquired from 64mT ULF MRI (Hyperfine Swoop) and matched conventional high field (HF) (3T/1.5T) MRI scans, from a diverse and representative sample recruited from South London and Maudsley NHS Trust memory services. The purpose of this work is to evaluate structural volumetric changes comparing HF and ULF scans and to investigate the ability of ULF MRI to detect such changes, with the overall aim to understand the potential of including ULF into standard clinical memory care.

Methods

37 participants were recruited from South London and Maudsley memory services to acquire matched HF and ULF scans. The HF scans were acquired using the 3T GE SIGNA Premier scanner (except 1 instance of 1.5T GE SIGNA Artist scanner), while the ULF scans were collected using 64mT Hyperfine Swoop scanner in the Centre for Neuroimaging Sciences at King’s College London. The matching sequences acquired from the scanners were the T1w and T2w, with a resolution of 1x1x1 mm3 for HF and 1.3x1.3x5 mm3 for ULF. ULF scans were preprocessed using multi-resolution registration (MRR), to combine three orthogonal T2w scans into one higher-resolution image (of effective resolution 1.5x1.5x1.5 mm3) per participant.3 Then both HF and ULF scans were preprocessed using the recon-all-clinical analysis tool available through FreeSurfer. This includes the use of SynthSeg, for which we used as input T1w MPRAGE scans for HF, and MRR T2w for ULF. SynthSeg then produced segmentations of brain areas, through a generative probabilistic brain atlas.4 This pipeline was chosen as it is specifically indicated for low-field scans. The high and ultra-low volumes obtained through the recon-all-clinical tool were used to compare the linear correlation and the exact agreement of volumetric measures, through Pearson’s r and Lin’s Concordance Correlation Coefficient (CCC) respectively. This latter coefficient can indicate the existence of systematic bias in volume estimates between the scanners, as it reflects both precision and accuracy. The values were calculated using segmented brain areas from both scanners, at both regional and tissue level, aggregating the regions into cortical grey matter (GMC), subcortical grey matter (GMS), white matter (WM) and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). To additionally evaluate the extent of under/over-estimation at a tissue level of ULF, the percentage difference (Δ%), in relation to HF scans was calculated as Δ%=100*(ULF-HF)/HF.3 Lastly, we were interested in understanding whether regional correlations were impacted by region size, therefore we used HF scans to compute the median size of each region across participants and we used Spearman’s rho to investigate the relationship of regional size to regional ULF-HF correspondence.

Results

Our sample consisted of 62% female and 38% male, with mean age 72 years (std=7, range=60-87). Of these a high proportion were from minority ethnicities (60% were of White ethnicity, 30% of African/Caribbean/Other Black background, 5% Hispanic, 4% Arab and 3% Indian). Diagnosis following the scans were 43% mild cognitive impairment (MCI), 11% dementia unspecified, 17% Alzheimer’s disease (early and late onset), and isolated instances of MCI with vascular cognitive impairment, suspected Parkinson’s Disease/Lewy body dementia, mild cognitive impairment with recurrent depressive disorder, mixed anxiety and depression, age-related changes, and 16% were not yet diagnosed. 

To analyse the MRI scans dataset, firstly the tissue level was taken into account, and total volumetric correlations between HF and ULF of each group were evaluated.3 The results of the analysis, reported in Figure 1a, show very high r values (between 0.93-1.00), highlighting that GMC and WM appear to have equally high correlations, but imperfect agreement as reflected in the lower CCC scores (r=0.98, [CCC GMC=0.71, WM=0.76]), while GMS appears to have lower correlation and agreement (r=0.93 [CCC=0.57]), and CSF appears perfectly correlated (r=1.00), obtaining additionally a high agreement between scanners (CCC=0.99). Regarding our investigation of the percentage difference (Δ%) between scanners at the tissue level, the results showed that in GMC, GMS, WM and CSF, ULF exhibited bias to underestimate regional size (Median Δ%[IQR]=-8.70%[1.78%], -10.05%[3.74%], -7.66%[2.02%], -1.53% [7.08%]). A separate analysis only of the hippocampal percentage difference (left and right combined) resulted in Median Δ%[IQR]=-15.44%[5.61%], highlighting the same ULF underestimation bias.

Next, the same correlations investigated for the tissue-level analysis were considered for regional level. Regional volumes from the left and right hemispheres were combined into a single symmetric value. The overall range of the regional correlations was between moderate and high values (r=0.74-1.00, Median[IQ1,IQ3]=0.86[0.79-0.92]), while exact agreement between scanners reported was more variable (CCC=0.19-0.99, Median[IQ1,IQ3]=0.67[0.45-0.82])(Figure 1b). Figure 1c shows Pearson’s r and CCC of left and right regions considered separately.

As our study focuses primarily on participants from memory services, hereafter we are highlighting the high correlations obtained in a subset of medial, lateral and basal temporal and medial parietal cortex structures, left and right combined, and others whose atrophy has been indicated as a signature for Alzheimer’s Disease in MRI1- consisting of the hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, parahippocampal gyrus, amygdala, precuneus, posterior cingular cortex, inferior parietal lobule, middle and inferior temporal gyrus, fusiform gyrus, lateral ventricles, third ventricle and superior frontal gyrus. For these the overall correlation range resulted in r=0.77-1.00 (Median[IQ1,IQ3]=0.93[0.90-0.96]), although showing more variance in exact scanner agreement (CCC=0.23-0.99, Median[IQ1,IQ3]=0.76[0.54-0.88]). Lastly, the impact of median regional size on the ULF-HF correlations resulted in rho=0.06 (p=0.65) across regions, indicating that regional size has a negligible effect on the correlations obtained.

Conclusion

This work highlights the potential of incorporating ULF-MRI in standard clinical care as a valid alternative to HF scans when these are not feasible, with the goal to increase scanning accessibility. The study analysis focused on the correlation between high and ultra-low field MRI scans, acquired in patients from real world, diverse memory services in London referred for MRI scan for diagnostic purposes. Our findings were in line with some of the promising results in correlating ULF and HF found in a separate study done by Váša et al. (2025)3 with the same methodological pipeline in healthy subjects, as well as a study by Sorby-Adams et al. (2024)5 in people with dementia, in which hippocampal volume correlations were comparable to our promising results. From our study, on a tissue level the Pearson’s r correlations were high, ranging between 0.93 to 1.00, also showing very good agreement (CCC) between the scanners. Percentage difference showed a slight tendency of ULF scans to underestimate tissue volumes in all tissues. On a regional level, correlations ranged between moderate to high Pearson’s r values, and showed high correlations in key dementia areas. A key strength of this study is the representativeness of a wide range of patients recruited from memory services, including a broad range of clinical features, comorbidities, ethnicities, risk factors and diagnoses highlighting the generalisability of our findings.
