Introduction

Adaptive behavior depends on the brain’s ability to flexibly transition between distinct configurations of neural activity that support diverse cognitive and affective processes. Understanding how such transitions are orchestrated has become a central goal of Network Control Theory (NCT), which views the brain as a time-varying system that continuously reconfigures its functional architecture to meet environmental demands1. While prior work has focused on how specific networks support domain‐specific functions, far less is known about which regions universally enable transitions across domains2. Here, we conceptualize this capacity as universal control: the extent to which a brain region is able to facilitate transitions across a broad range of mental states. We hypothesize that regions exhibiting high universal control are characterized by peripheral positions within the structural network, a facilitatory neuromodulatory profile3, and are located in unimodal systems on the cortical hierarchy4 – ultimately enabling low-cost access to energetically distant states. We further argue that inter-individual differences in universal control might be reflected by underlying aberrations in the brain’s energetic landscape: a flattened landscape may render transitions unstable, whereas an excessively steep landscape may trap the system in rigid configurations, both leading to impaired cognitive functioning, a transdiagnostic marker of psychopathology5. To test this mechanistic account, we assessed how pharmacological modulation of neuromodulatory systems influences control energy in an intervention study with healthy participants. Given the known receptor-binding profiles of these drugs, we predicted specific increases or decreases in whole-brain universal control energy. Results from this pharmacological approach might offer mechanistic insights into psychiatric disorders that are partly characterized by dysregulation of the very receptor systems targeted by these drugs.

Methods

We analyzed minimally preprocessed data from 751 participants of the Human Connectome Project (HCP) who completed both diffusion MRI and task fMRI sessions and the NIH Toolbox cognitive assessments. First-level task fMRI analyses were performed using SPM12. For each participant, t-contrast maps were derived for two conditions from four tasks mapping onto the RDoC domains: working memory, social processing, reward, and emotion regulation, yielding eight task-derived brain states. Diffusion-weighted images were reconstructed using q-space diffeomorphic reconstruction in DSI-Studio. Deterministic tractography (100,000 streamlines/subject) was used to estimate weighted structural connectivity matrices, where edge weights reflected streamline counts between regions. For each subject, optimal control energy was computed for all possible state-to-state transitions using previously described methods. Then, for each brain region, mixed-effects models were used to estimate average control energy across all subjects and their transitions. The resulting t-values yielded a statistical brain map of universal control. To validate the functional relevance of this intermediate brain phenotype, we used two complementary approaches. First, we performed reverse-inference decoding with the Neurosynth database to identify associated cognitive terms with our brain map. Second, we extracted a latent factor of general cognitive performance using principal component analysis of all NIH Toolbox measures and correlated this factor with each participant’s similarity to the universal control map, controlling for age and sex, and motion. Anatomical specificity was confirmed using spatial permutation testing with 1,000 null maps. To examine how our brain phenotype relates to topological and neuromodulatory markers, we computed region-wise modal and average controllability, degree, and participation coefficient from the group-averaged structural network. We then performed a partial least squares (PLS) analysis to relate these topological markers to universal control. Separately, we also obtained receptor-density maps for the major neuromodulatory systems (dopaminergic, cholinergic, noradrenergic, and serotonergic receptor densities) from the Allen Human Brain Atlas and conducted a second PLS analysis to quantify how receptor distributions spatially correspond to universal control. Lastly, we also derived functional gradients using the group-averaged resting-state connectivity matrix and spatially correlated these gradient maps with universal control. As we were further interested in whether universal control follows a biologically plausible hierarchical signal propagation, we assessed time-resolved correlations between universal control and functional gradients, topological markers, and receptor maps to test whether their spatial associations systematically evolve over the simulated transition window. Finally, to causally test whether and how neuromodulatory systems influence control energy, we conducted a double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover study in which healthy participants received agonistic or antagonistic drugs that modulate neuromodulatory tone, alongside placebo. Whole-brain universal control energy was computed for each condition and analyzed using mixed-effects models controlling for motion and session order. Observed drug-induced increases or decreases in universal whole-brain control energy relative to placebo were then compared against theoretically derived expected effects based on each compound’s known receptor affinities, allowing us to test whether neuromodulatory receptor profiles predictably increase or decrease whole-brain control energy.

Results

Across all state-to-state transitions, top universal control regions were primarily located within dorsal attentional, visual, and frontoparietal networks. Meta-analytic decoding using Neurosynth revealed that the universal control map was most strongly associated with terms related to attentional and executive cognitive functions. At the individual level, similarity to the identified universal control phenotype was positively related to cognitive performance (r = 0.19, p < .01). Spatial permutation testing confirmed the anatomical specificity of this association (p < .01). Partial least squares (PLS) analyses revealed that universal control was positively associated with modal controllability (r = .09, p < .05) and negatively associated with average controllability  (r = -.10, p < .05), degree  (r = -.25, p < .01), and participation coefficient  (r =- .32, p < .01). Additional PLS analyses showed positive associations between universal control and excitatory neuromodulatory receptor systems (r = .29, p < 0.01), and negative associations with inhibitory receptor systems (r = -.32, p < 0.01). Universal control was significantly aligned with the principal sensory-to-transmodal functional gradient (r = .33, p < .01). Time-resolved analyses of control inputs showed systematic changes in the spatial distribution of control influence over the course of state transitions. Early time points were characterized by stronger contributions from unimodal cortical regions and higher modal controllability, whereas later time points showed increased involvement of heteromodal cortical regions, limbic areas, and subcortical systems. Receptor-specific analyses indicated that excitatory neuromodulatory receptor systems contributed more strongly at earlier time points, while inhibitory receptor systems showed increasing contributions at later time points (β =1.38, p < .001). In the pharmacological intervention study, changes in whole-brain universal control energy differed by drug condition: Antagonistic modulation of facilitatory neuromodulatory systems was associated with increased control energy, whereas agonistic modulation of facilitatory systems or antagonistic modulation of inhibitory systems was associated with reduced control energy in line with a priori predicted effects (r = .80, p < 0.05).

Conclusion

In this study, we identified an intermediate universal control phenotype that captures the extent to which individual brain regions support transitions between diverse neural configurations. By relating universal control to structural network topology as well as functional connectivity and neuromodulatory receptor distributions, we provide a mechanistic framework describing how these factors jointly shape brain dynamics. Higher universal control was preferentially located in network nodes with high modal controllability, indicating that regions facilitating domain-general state transitions tend to occupy peripheral positions in the anatomical network, optimizing them to give the system access to hard-to-reach network states rather than serving as integrative hubs. Its association with excitatory neuromodulatory receptor systems and inverse association with inhibitory systems indicates that universal control is preferentially linked to neuromodulatory mechanisms that enhance neural excitation. Spatial alignment of universal control with the sensory-to-transmodal functional gradient further indicates that this control phenotype is related to the unimodal end of the cortical hierarchy, providing evidence that universal control is strongest in stimulus-driven regions, rather than in transmodal hubs. Extending this observation, time-resolved analyses demonstrated that universal control is not static but propagates hierarchically during state transitions: influence emerges early in unimodal cortical systems and subsequently engages heteromodal, limbic, and subcortical regions. This progression is paralleled by a shift from excitatory to inhibitory receptor dominance, suggesting a transition from fast, facilitatory control to later-stage stabilization of brain dynamics. Individual differences in universal control recruitment predicted cognitive performance, identifying universal control as an intermediate phenotype linking large-scale brain dynamics to behavior. Pharmacological manipulations targeting excitatory and inhibitory neuromodulatory systems altered control energy in predicted directions, supporting a causal role of neuromodulatory tone in shaping the energetic demands of brain state transitions. Together, these findings suggest that cognitive functioning emerges from a hierarchically organized control system constrained by structural and functional network topology and neuromodulatory tone. This framework has implications for understanding cognitive dysfunction in patients in the mood–psychosis spectrum, where impairments in cognitive functioning represent a core clinical symptom.
