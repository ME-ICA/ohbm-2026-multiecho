Introduction

Limbic Encephalitis (LE) is a severe autoimmune disease causing subacute short-term memory loss and various psychiatric abnormalities, often accompanied by temporal lobe epilepsy. The disease mechanism centers on autoantibodies that target neuronal surface (e.g. LGI1) or intracellular (e.g. GAD) antigens.1 Autoantibody identification is critical for the clinical diagnosis and effective treatment of LE patients. Despite heterogeneous symptoms, these disorders share a distinctive pathological focus on the medial temporal lobe, particularly the hippocampus, driving the characteristic memory and psychiatric impairment.1 While clinical criteria form a firm foundation, ongoing research aims to identify non-invasive imaging features that might correlate with specific antibody profiles to aid early diagnosis, predict treatment responses, and monitor subtle disease activity. Previous studies using conventional neuroimaging tools have often relied on gross hippocampal volume or subfield volumes in voxel-space, with limited success in distinguishing distinct LE subtypes.2 However, volumetric averages fail to capture the sub-regional morphological changes that could define distinct immunopathologies and the analysis within voxel space may not adequately capture hippocampal geometric and anatomical complexities. To address this, we utilize the Hippocampal Shape and Thickness Analysis (HIPSTA) tool3, which computes thickness and curvature measures by unfolding the hippocampus into a two-dimensional surface space. Since precise structural correlates corresponding to specific antibody pathologies or other clinical phenotypes remain poorly characterized, we hypothesized that either: Hippocampal morphometry shows distinct, reproducible patterns specific to antibody subtypes, potentially reflecting underlying disease stratification, or variability in hippocampal structure is shaped mainly by broader clinical or pathological factors rather than strict antibody subtype-specific effects.

Methods

Cohort & Data Acquisition: T1-weighted MRI data were retrospectively collected from 55 patients with autoimmune limbic encephalitis (LE) and 199 healthy controls. The patient group included LGI1 (n=7, 71% female, median age 64 years [IQR 62-70]), NMDAR (n=5, 100% female, median age 31 years [IQR 24.5–57.25]), CASPR2 (n=9, 11% female, median age 69 years [IQR 52-75]), and GAD (n=34, 67% female, median age 28 years [IQR 23.5-43]) antibody-positive individuals. Healthy controls were 51% female with a median age of 30 years (IQR 24-47.5). Scans were acquired on 3T scanners at the University Hospital Bonn or the Human MRI Core Facility using high-resolution 3D T1-weighted sequences (≤1 mm isotropic). For each patient, two MRI time points were included: the earliest available scan after disease onset and the most recent, to differentiate acute versus post-acute stages. Patients were classified as acute or post-acute using a threshold of ≥24 months since disease onset.

Image Analysis: T1w images were processed using FreeSurfer (v7.4.1) for skull stripping, bias-field correction, segmentation, and cortical surface modelling. Hippocampal subfields were delineated with FreeSurfer’s dedicated hippocampal segmentation pipeline. These subfield segmentations were further analysed with HIPSTA, yielding vertex-wise hippocampal thickness measures across the unfolded surface.

Statistical Analysis: Analyses were conducted in Python 3.12 using scikit-learn (v1.7.2), SciPy (v1.16.3), pingouin (v.0.5.5) and MNE-Python (v1.10.2). HIPSTA matrices were obtained separately for left and right hemispheres for both timepoints for each patient, these were treated as independent samples. Feature matrices were smoothed with a Gaussian kernel (FWHM = 3 grid units) and normalized in two stages: intra-subject z-scoring to reduce age- and sex-related variability, followed by inter-subject z-scoring relative to controls, preserving hemisphere-specific spatial patterns. Exploratory dimensionality structure and clustering: Normalized vertex-wise thickness matrices (41x21) were flattened into 861-element vectors per hemisphere. PCA was applied to characterize intrinsic structure, retaining the first components explaining cumulatively ≥80% of variance. Next, k-means clustering was performed in PCA space, with k equal to the number of antibody groups compared. Associations between cluster membership and categorical variables were assessed using two-tailed Fisher’s Exact Tests (p < 0.05 considered significant). Cluster-based permutation statistics and effect size maps: Vertex-wise group differences were evaluated using MNE-Python’s cluster-based permutation framework to correct for multiple comparisons. Paired-sample t-statistics (two-tailed, p<0.05) were computed and formed clusters on the 41×21 HIPSTA grid, with significance assessed via 5,000 permutations of group labels. Cohen’s d effect size maps were computed vertex-wise using the compute_effsize function from the Pingouin library and were subsequently masked to display the effect size only within the identified significant clusters. Classifier training: PCA components cumulatively explaining ≥80% of variance were used as features for a Random Forest classifier. Model performance was evaluated with stratified 5-fold cross-validation to ensure balanced antibody group representation.

Results

While the initial PCA on hippocampal thickness demonstrated that LGI1- and NMDAR-associated LE cases were more discernible than the overlapping CASPR2 and GAD cohorts (Fig. 1A), unsupervised k-means clustering reinforced the overall heterogeneity across the dataset. Importantly, this clustering revealed that the LGI1 group showed substantial internal homogeneity, with most subjects mapping to a single cluster (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, the NMDAR-positive patients were also found in a distinguishable cluster relative to the highly overlapping CASPR2 and GAD cohorts, though their distribution was notably more scattered than the tightly clustered LGI1 group (Fig. 1A). These findings indicate that hippocampal structure captures meaningful autoantibody-specific variation, but with differing degrees of separability between groups. Therefore, we focused on NMDAR and LGI1 in the following analysis. To further investigate NMDAR and LGI1 positive patient groups we performed another PCA followed by unsupervised k-means clustering, which lead to a significant separation of NMDAR and LGI1 positive patient groups (Fig. 1C, Fig. 1D). Statistical analysis indicated a robust association between cluster membership and antibody status (Fisher's Exact Test, p = 3.79x10-7, OR = 0.0, Cramer's V = 0.838), confirming that the clustering successfully and robustly distinguished LGI1 from NMDAR patients (Fig. 1F). Investigation of the principal component loadings indicated that variation along the medial-to-lateral axis accounted for the strongest differentiation among antibody groups. Specifically, investigation of the first two principal components (PC1/2), which captured 38.85% and 13.75% of the total variance, showed a distinctive medial-to-lateral pattern of change crucial for group separation (Fig. 1H). 

To further investigate the medial to lateral pattern difference, we utilized permutation paired-sample t-statistics (p < 0.05) combined with Cohen’s d effect size measurements. We identified significantly different pattern areas between LGI1 and NMDAR, demonstrating that the medial-to-lateral thickness in these areas successfully discriminated the two conditions. LGI1 patients exhibited thicker subregions in the medial hippocampus relative to NMDAR patients. Conversely, the medial-to-lateral pattern highlighted that NMDAR patients demonstrated relative thinning in the lateral subfields (Subiculum and CA1), confirming the discriminatory power of between LGI1 and NMDAR (Fig. 1E).

Classification analysis confirmed that hippocampal thickness features are highly effective in distinguishing between LGI1 and NMDAR patients. The Random Forest classifier, utilizing PCA components (≥80% variance explained), achieved excellent discrimination between the two groups. Stratified 5-fold cross-validation yielded high metrics, including accuracy of 97.1% and an F1 score of 0.970. The robustness of the prediction was confirmed by the confusion matrix, which showed only one misclassified patient. (Fig. 1G).

Conclusion

The present results demonstrate that advanced, surface-based hippocampal morphometry successfully identifies significant, antibody-specific morphological fingerprints in LE. Our analysis achieved successful unsupervised clustering and distinction of the LGI1 and NMDAR LE subtypes, showing that distinct immunopathologies map onto unique structural substrates. As the spatial patterns concentrated along the medial to lateral axis, this provides novel insights into understanding the specific pathogenesis of these disorders. These findings establish a strong proof of concept for non-invasive hippocampal MRI biomarkers to differentiate LE subtypes, offering a substantial advantage over standard volumetric analysis. Future work will therefore be multi-pronged, focusing on methodological refinement and investigating the heterogeneity of patterns. 

To better address the morphological heterogeneity observed in GAD and CASPR2 cohorts, we will optimize feature selection by investigating the performance of other metrics. The lack of clear separation between highly heterogeneous groups, such as GAD and CASPR2, suggests two possibilities: either the current imaging metrics are insufficient to capture an antibody-specific phenotype, or no distinct, antibody-specific phenotype exists in the hippocampus for these conditions. Instead, the observed variations in hippocampal morphology may be driven by other confounding clinical or pathological factors (e.g., neuropsychology test performance or seizure frequency) that create distinct subgroups within the broader diagnosis. While the search for a singular antibody-specific phenotype continues, acknowledging these other influencing factors opens a crucial avenue for clinical validation: features that distinguish these non-antibody-specific subgroups could still be highly valid for clinically stratifying patients and guiding treatment, regardless of the underlying autoantibody.

To strengthen the machine learning approach, future classification analyses must address the significant sample size imbalance across the cohorts. Our initial attempt to include the large, heterogeneous GAD group demonstrated this risk, as it caused the Random Forest classifier to overfit and misclassify most samples as GAD. This failure strongly suggests that models trained on imbalanced data reflect sample size disparities rather than true biological separability. Consequently, subsequent classification focusing even on just the LGI1 and NMDAR groups requires methodological refinement. Implementing techniques such as oversampling (e.g., SMOTE) or utilizing class-weighted cost functions could rebalance the training data. This vital improvement would ensure the model is penalized equally for misclassifying a rare sample (e.g., LGI1 or NMDAR), ultimately yielding a more robust and clinically relevant assessment of the hippocampal features' discriminatory power between the groups.
