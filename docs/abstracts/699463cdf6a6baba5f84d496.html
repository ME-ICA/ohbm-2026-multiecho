Introduction

Cortical blindness is the complete or partial loss of vision following a lesion in the visual pathway.1,2 Some people with cortical blindness report that light can induce degraded conscious vision or non-visual sensations, also known as “blindsense.”3 The neural mechanisms underlying blindsense are unknown and significant for understanding how the brain can adapt after injury. In a previous study, it was discovered that a cortically blind person (P4) with a lesion in his left primary visual cortex indicated a possible experience of blindsense in his blind visual field (right homonymous superior quadrantanopia).4 He described a non-visual conscious “sensation” or “feeling” for the presence of images, despite being unable to consciously “see.” He described the sensations as a “disturbance in the force” and like his “brain is notifying [me] that something is going on,” emphasizing that these sensations are distinct from normal vision. When instructed to press a button upon experiencing these sensations, P4’s responses always corresponded with the presentation of images (i.e., no false positives). In the first study, compared to the sighted field, the responses to stimuli presented in the blind field followed similar eye metric patterns (e.g., blink rate, pupil size, and microsaccades) but were decreased and delayed. Building from this study, we endeavored to further our understanding of this patient’s visual conscious experiences by investigating the physiological correlates of blindsense in cortical blindness through a customized paradigm. Notably, this case study may offer an innovative, multimodal neuroimaging study of blindsense, laying the foundation to better understand residual neural processing and visual conscious awareness in cortical blindness.

Methods

A customized visual task was designed to reliably induce P4’s blind field sensations (Figure 1). Visual stimuli (a black and white square image) appeared for 1 second, alternating its contrast 4 times during its presentation. Stimuli appeared in one of three on-screen locations: upper left quadrant (sighted field), bottom right quadrant (sighted field), and upper right quadrant (blind field). In a subset of trials, no stimulus appeared. Following stimulus presentation, two self-paced questions were shown. The first question instructed P4 to report what he experienced during the previous trial along a 10-point scale: “0” if he did not see anything during the stimulus presentation; “5” if he noticed a “sensation” or “feeling”; and “10” if he clearly saw an image. The second question instructed the patient to report which image appeared. The visual task was administered with simultaneous MEG (1200Hz sampling rate; CTF 275 MEG system) and fMRI (GE MR-750 3T; TR = 1000ms). For both the MEG and fMRI studies, eye tracking was recorded using the EyeLink 1000 Plus System (SR Research Inc.).

Results

Behavioral results show that P4 indicated the stimuli in the sighted field were clearly seen, while the blind field stimuli were felt as a sensation. Additionally, slower reaction times were observed in the blind versus sighted field. In the sighted field, images presented induced a biphasic pupillary response, increased blink rate, and decreased microsaccade rate (Figure 2a). Image-evoked MEG field potentials were also observed, particularly beginning in the posterior scalp regions around 100 ms post-stimulus (Figure 2b). In the blind field, similar patterns of change were recorded in the eye metrics data, but the responses were delayed (~100 ms -1000 ms) and exhibited a reduced magnitude of change (Figure 2a). The blind field MEG responses were also delayed relative to the sighted field (~200 ms), and the pattern of change on the scalp was distinct, including frontal scalp potentials that were absent in the sighted field (Figure 2b). fMRI data collection is underway to explore if distinct brain networks are activated for blindsense versus normal conscious vision.

Conclusion

In this study, we have evaluated stimulus-evoked pupil size, microsaccade, and blink rates in tandem with fMRI and MEG in a cortically blind patient who reports non-visual “sensations” for images presented in his blind field. Results show delayed and decreased eye metric responses and posterior activations to stimuli in the blind field, which correlate directly with stimulus onset. We plan on conducting further MEG and fMRI analysis, including generating spatio-temporal maps, to determine which regions are correlated with the posterior activations and blind field-evoked sensations. Our preliminary results show that the physiological mechanisms of blindsense are correlated with delayed and decreased neural responses, which may correlate with subtle, conscious perceptual experiences of blindsense. This case study may lay the foundation to better understand the different roles of lesion location and subjective experience that contribute to the phenomenon of blindsense and inform us of the mechanisms behind the visual conscious experience of cortically blind patients.
