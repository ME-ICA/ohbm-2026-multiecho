Introduction

The discovery of ketamine’s rapid-acting antidepressant and pro-hedonic effects marked a turning point in the treatment of major depressive disorder (MDD). Although an ever-growing body of research demonstrates ketamine’s efficacy in quickly ameliorating depressive symptoms, its precise mechanism of action remains elusive. Recently, our team employed multivariate information-theoretic measures to characterize how ketamine modulates neural information dynamics in healthy individuals to facilitate its prohedonic effect1. Our results showed that ketamine induces a pronounced shift in resting-state information dynamics towards redundancy, indicating that nodes more dominantly represent the same information across time. Based on these findings, we hypothesize that ketamine’s marked reconfiguration toward redundant information integration may underlie its beneficial effects more generally, and that these therapeutic effects may wane as the neuromodulatory changes dissipate. To test this hypothesis in the depressed brain, we are pooling data from single-dose ketamine trials in MDD patients that include resting-state scans across the full therapeutic window. Here, we present preliminary analyses from one such dataset.

Methods

The presented analysis is based on an openly available dataset2 examining ketamine’s effects on resting-state dynamics in treatment-resistant MDD patients (n = 33). In this randomized, cross-over trial, participants received both placebo (saline) and a single ketamine infusion (0.5 mg/kg). Resting-state scans were acquired at baseline and approximately 2 and 10 days after each administration, and Montgomery–Asberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) scores were collected at all corresponding time points. Bold data was preprocessed using fMRIprep and parcellated using the Schaefer-Tian 232 atlas. To assess how ketamine administration alters neural information dynamics, we employed Whole-Minus-Sum (WMS) integrated information3. Briefly, WMS is based on mutual information and quantifies whether information processed between two nodes is predominantly transferred between them and synergistic, i.e., depends on their joint activity, or redundant, i.e., the same information is represented in both nodes across time. Since WMS only captures the balance between these two dynamics, we further applied integrated information decomposition to isolate redundancy-specific dynamics4. For each run, we computed local WMS for every pair of brain regions and averaged them across time to obtain run-wise information-integration matrices. Based on our hypothesis that ketamine’s therapeutic effect (reductions in MADRS scores) covaries with its impact on neural information integration dynamics and should thus diminish as these neural effects wane, we submitted the information-integration matrices to the Network-Based Statistic (NBS) algorithm5. The NBS model explicitly tested whether clusters of edges shift as a function of drug condition, time post-administration, and MADRS scores. To probe putative neurochemical underpinnings of ketamine’s neuromodulatory effects, significant NBS-derived clusters were additionally related to receptor density maps, controlling for spatial autocorrelation and Family-wise error rate.

Results

The preliminary analysis identified a significant NBS cluster associated with changes in MADRS scores from baseline to 2 days post-ketamine administration. This indicates that alterations in neural information flow covary with the severity of depressive symptoms and are mediated by the neuromodulatory impact of ketamine on resting-state information dynamics. No significant associations emerged between the identified NBS cluster and the receptor density maps, suggesting that, post-acute, ketamine’s effect on neural information dynamics is unlikely to be driven by a single receptor system and instead may reflect cascading downstream interactions across multiple systems.

Conclusion

Given that these findings are preliminary and derived from a subset of data, definitive conclusions are deferred until the full dataset has been analyzed. However, these tentative findings highlight the promise of the used study design in combination with information-theoretic measures in identifying ketamine’s ability to modulate neural information dynamics as an important factor for its therapeutic effects. Yet, while redundancy-dominated information dynamics emerge as a potential candidate mechanism, their predominant role in facilitating ketamine’s therapeutic benefits remains to be demonstrated using the full dataset.
