Introduction

Motor Neuron Disease (MND) causes progressive degeneration across the brain, predominantly characterised by the loss of both upper and lower motor neurons however, the disease is increasingly recognised as a multi-system disorder with involvement of extra-motor areas1. This neurodegeneration leads to progressive muscle weakness and wasting and is ultimately fatal.

Historical fMRI research shows that people living with MND (plwMND) exhibit decreased contralateral activation of motor areas during movement execution compared to non-neurodegenerative controls (NCs)1 as well as inhibitory dysfunction2. While these findings provide insight into overall activity changes, very little work has examined how individual body parts are represented in the motor cortex. As motor neurons degenerate and dysfunction spreads across body regions, the cortical representations of affected body parts may be altered. It is unclear whether affected regions disappear, shift, or undergo plastic changes as the disease progresses.

Beyond its clinical relevance, MND provides a valuable model for studying how cortical organisation changes as motor dysfunction emerges across multiple body regions. Characterising changes to cortical representations as neurodegeneration progresses could provide a basis for mapping the plasticity of the brain’s motor regions. Accordingly, our study aims to characterise motor body maps in plwMND using fMRI, establishing a framework to investigate how motor cortical representations evolve with neurodegeneration.

Given the heterogeneity in the laterality and pattern of limb involvement in MND, we also examined multiple body regions (such as the hands and feet) together for a more comprehensive and specific assessment of motor activation within affected and unaffected limb regions across plwMND.

Methods

We used ultra-high-field (7T) fMRI (Siemens Magnetom, 2D Echo-Planar Imaging (EPI), TE: 22 ms, TR: 900 ms, TA: 9mins 18s, FA: 45°, Resolution: 1.5mm3) to map body representations in 25 plwMND (mean age 55.5 ± 9.2 ) and 37 NCs (mean age 55.1 ± 7.98). Participants were instructed to perform a body localiser task within the scanner, moving their lips, left and right hands, arms, and feet independently when instructed on screen. People with MND with limited mobility were asked to attempt movements.

Data were preprocessed using fMRIPrep and normalised to MNI space. Two plwMND were excluded due to their inability to complete an MRI scan, two plwMND and one NC were excluded due to missing data, and two plwMND were excluded for excessive motion, resulting in the inclusion of 19 plwMND and 36 NCs in further analysis.

Voxel-wise activations were estimated using a general linear model implemented in Nilearn, including six motion regressors, motion-censoring regressors (frames censored when framewise displacement exceeded 0.5 mm), a third-order polynomial drift model, and three principal components from CSF and white-matter masks. To generate individual-specific body maps, each body part was contrasted against all other conditions. In addition, to examine the activation of multiple regions together, we calculated contrasts of left- and right-hand conditions and left- and right-foot conditions against all other conditions each.  

Group-level body maps for plwMND and NCs were estimated using second-level intercept models fit to the groups independently, with Z-scores corrected for multiple comparisons using FDR.

Results

We successfully generated body maps in plwMND. Despite evident motor dysfunction in, for example, the lower limbs (as defined by a clinically significant reduction in motor ability), body maps were still visible and generated (see Figure 1, where a participant was fully wheelchair-bound and unable to move legs).

Visually, the locations of these maps were largely consistent with those of NCs, suggesting that core motor organisation may be preserved despite motor dysfunction. 

In addition, visual inspection of activation during hand or foot movements indicates that plwMND may show less widespread significant activation during movement (see Figure 2).

Conclusion

These preliminary results suggest that 1) it is possible to reliably extract body maps in participants with extensive motor dysfunction, and 2) people living with MND show reduced significant activation in limb regions of the motor cortex during movement execution, consistent with previous reports of altered overall motor activity in MND. This reduction may reflect impaired motor performance, neurodegeneration-related cortical changes, or alterations in excitation–inhibition balance that render cortical representations less distinct.

Future work will disentangle these factors by relating cortical activation to clinical measures of motor impairment, examining structural correlates, and assessing the similarity of activation patterns across body parts (using representational similarity analysis). Longitudinal analyses will further characterise how motor body representations change as the disease and dysfunction progress.

The successful identification of body maps in plwMND provides a foundation for more detailed analysis of motor cortical organisation under neurodegeneration, enabling future studies to examine somatotopic or motor fine-grained organisation, longitudinal changes as the disease progresses, or metabolic imbalances in affected regions.
