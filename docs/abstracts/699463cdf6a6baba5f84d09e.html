Introduction

More than 60,000 infants are born very preterm (VPT) (born at or less than 32 weeks gestational age) annually in the United States, a population with a high risk of substantial medical complications, neurodevelopmental deficits, and increased lifelong healthcare costs. 1 One of the greatest concerns is cognitive deficits, which affect 25–50% of children born preterm and consistently result in lower cognitive performance compared to their full-term peers. Unfortunately, these deficits cannot be accurately diagnosed until ages 3–5 and tend to worsen as children enter school and face increasing cognitive demands. With limited and unvalidated intervention strategies currently available, understanding the neurological mechanisms underlying these early cognitive developmental issues is critical for advancing biologically informed interventions. In this study, we aim to elucidate the associations between early (at birth) brain functional patterns and the longitudinal cognitive development using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) from a large prospective VPT cohort. We construct individual functional connectivity (FC) networks at the region-of-interest level using a learning framework specifically validated for neonates and derive network-level connectivity (e.g., within the default mode network) using a Bayesian hierarchical model. 2 We employed a linear mixed-effects model to estimate the effect of network connectivity on longitudinal cognitive outcomes, examining interactions with age to disentangle developmental heterogeneity while controlling for relevant clinical and maternal factors. We hypothesize that altered brain functional patterns antecede the manifestation of cognitive deficits and exert a significant longitudinal impact on cognitive development.

Methods

This cohort study was nested within the Cincinnati Infant Neurodevelopment Early Prediction Study (CINEPS), 3 a collaborative study across five level III/IV neonatal intensive care units (NICU) of the Greater Cincinnati area, including Cincinnati Children’s Hospital Medical Center (CCHMC), University of Cincinnati Medical Center, Good Samaritan Hospital, St. Elizabeth's Healthcare, and Kettering Memorial Hospital. The Institutional Review Board of the CCHMC approved this study, and we obtained written parental informed consent for every child prior to enrollment. A total of 395 VPT infants were recruited between September 2016 and November 2019, and we excluded infants with cyanotic heart disease, chromosomal or congenital anomalies, and mechanically ventilated on more than 50% supplemental oxygen.

All the MR images were acquired during unsedated sleep on a 3.0 Tesla Philips Ingenia scanner with a 32-channel receiver head coil at CCHMC between 39- and 44-week postmenstrual age. The acquisition parameters for axial T2-weighted MRI: echo time = 166 ms, repetition time = 8,300 ms, flip angle = 90°, spatial resolution 1×1×1 mm3. For DTI: echo time = 88 ms, repetition time = 7,000 ms, flip angle = 90°, spatial resolution = 2×2×2 mm3, 36 directions of diffusion gradients with a b-value of 800 s/mm2. For multi-band rs-fMRI: echo time = 45 ms, repetition time = 1,195 ms, flip angle = 55°, spatial resolution 2.5×2.5×2.5 mm3, number of frames = 400. The rs-fMRI images were preprocessed following the developing Human Connectome Project (dHCP) processing pipeline and parcellated into 90 regions of interest (ROIs) according to a neonatal automated anatomical labeling (AAL) atlas.4 All images were manually screened to ensure their quality. We estimated the ROI level FC using a structural connectome guided  learning framework2, resulting in an asymmetric adjacency matrix with a size of 90 × 90 for each infant. We mapped the brain regions to six predefined networks, including default mode network (DMN), somato-motor network (SMN), dorsal attention network (DAN), frontoparietal control network (FPC), visual network (VIS), and cingulo-opercular network (CO). Longitudinal cognitive assessments were completed during two follow-up visits at CCHMC. At 22 to 26 months corrected age, cognitive development was measured using the Bayley-III (Third Edition) cognitive subtest. At 36 month corrected age, the Differential Ability Scales Second Edition (DAS-II) was used to obtain the General Conceptual Ability score (general cognitive ability measurements). A total of 257 subjects with both 2 and 3 year cognitive outcomes and high-quality processed MR images were included in the analysis (Table 1).

We fitted a Bayesian hierarchical model with a linear mixed-effect model to estimate the longitudinal effects of the network-level connectivity at birth and the cognitive outcomes at 2 and 3 years. We controlled for several potential confounders, including gestational age (GA), postmenstrual age (PMA), sex, the severity of bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), and breastmilk feeding in all models. Specifically, the network connections were derived by parameterizing ROI connectivity, accounting for the variance.5 A linear mixed-effect model was employed to estimate the effects of network connectivity on outcomes, where random intercept is incorporated to account for within-subject variability. To assess the heterogeneity effects of the FC, the interaction term between FC and age was included in the model. Model parameters were estimated with the No-U-Turn Hamiltonian Monte Carlo sampling in Stan with 2000 iterations, 1000 burn-in iterations, where convergence was checked by the Brooks-Gelman-Rubin method.

Results

Based on the results (Table 2), increased intra-network connectivity of the DMN was associated with higher longitudinal cognitive scores (1.60, 95% Credible Interval (CrI): [0.010, 3.40]). In contrast, lower longitudinal cognitive scores were associated with reduced FPC intra-network connectivity (-2.96, 95% CrI: [-3.60, -2.32]), as well as reduced connectivity between FPC and SMN (-1.26, 95% CrI: [-2.32, -0.26]), FPC and VIS (-1.74, 95% CrI: [-2.70, -0.56]), FPC and CO (-3.93, 95% CrI: [-4.72, -3.08]), and CO intra-network connectivity (-0.39, 95% CrI: [-0.65, -0.11]). Furthermore, our analysis revealed significant negative interactions between age and both FPC intra-network (FPC-FPC) and FPC-CO inter-network connectivity. This indicates that the adverse associations with cognitive outcomes become stronger over time. Specifically, we observed a more pronounced decline in cognitive scores at age 3 compared to age 2 for both FPC intra-network connectivity (-1.52, 95% CrI: [-2.18, -0.85]) and FPC-CO inter-network connectivity (-1.28, 95% CrI: [-2.03, -0.53]). These findings suggest that the adverse effects of altered FPC-FPC and FPC-CO connectivity are not static but progressively intensify during early development. Among the potential confounding factors, higher gestational age (GA) (1.35, 95% CrI: [0.59, 2.10]) and breastmilk feeding (1.98, 95% CrI: [0.22, 3.66]) showed significant positive associations with cognitive performance. These findings highlighted both the developmental vulnerability associated with premature birth and the protective neurocognitive benefits of breastfeeding.

Conclusion

This study suggests the enduring impact of altered functional network connectivity at birth on longitudinal outcomes at 2 and 3 years in VPT infants. We identified significant FC differences involving the DMN, FPC, SMN, VIS, and CO, all of which are associated with early cognitive development. Especially, we observed a positive association between intra-DMN connectivity and longitudinal cognitive outcomes, which is consistent with prior literature and enriches our understanding of early cognitive development in VPT populations. We also identified hub-like functional patterns within the FPC and their negative associations with longitudinal cognitive outcomes. These findings suggest that the FPC is particularly vulnerable to disruptions associated with preterm birth. Furthermore, decreased cognitive performance at ages 2 and 3 was linked to increased connectivity in FPC-FPC, FPC-SMN, FPC-VIS, and FPC-CO connections. Notably, the adverse effects of FPC-FPC and FPC-CO connectivity strengthened over time, indicating a progressively worsening impact on cognitive development. These findings provide new insights into the specific functional network alterations associated with prematurity and underscore the potential need for early, targeted interventions to support cognitive development in this high-risk population.
