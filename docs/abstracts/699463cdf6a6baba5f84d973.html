Introduction

Neuroimaging research on memory and imagination has revealed that these processes rely on many of the same neural structures, particularly those of the default mode network (DMN). However, relatively less is known about the neural oscillatory mechanisms that enable the brain to remember the past and imagine the future. The aim of the current research is to leverage the high temporal precision of magnetoencephalography (MEG) to address this gap. Of particular interest is spectral power in the theta and alpha bands, which has previously been implicated in scene construction and episodic memory,1,2 as well as in the delta band, which is related to connectivity within the DMN.3 Here we compare neural oscillatory activity during the “construction” phase of memory and imagination (the process of searching for a core schematic event scaffold) with the “elaboration” phase (the process of expanding on such a scaffold to produce an event narrative). This distinction is important for two reasons: first, it allows this research to impose temporal structure on the relatively temporally unconstrained and idiosyncratic processes of memory and imagination. Second, differences in neural activity between memory and imagination are seen primarily during the construction phase in fMRI research,4 leading us to expect analogous results for neural oscillations.


Methods

MEG was collected using a 275-magnetometer CTF system at 2000 Hz from 34 healthy young adults (12 males; age range 18–35 years, mean age 25.1 years). On a series of trials (Fig. 1), participants were presented with a concrete noun as a prompt and asked to either recall a past autobiographical event involving the cue word (recall task), imagine a future autobiographical event involving the cue word (imagine task), or complete a non-autobiographical control task involving object imagery and semantic processing (picturing and defining words related to the cue). Participants completed 48 trials for each task. On each 12-second trial, participants made a button press response to indicate when they had an event in mind for the recall and imagine tasks, and when they had thought of two words related to the cue in the control task. This response time allowed us to operationalize the processes of construction (pre-button press) and elaboration (post-button press). After each trial, participants rated the level of detail of their memory or imagination on a 4-point scale. Analyses of behavioural data used mixed effects ordinal regression for detail ratings and mixed effects linear regression for reaction times, as implemented in the R packages “ordinal” and “afex” respectively. MEG data preprocessing used the MNE-Python library. Raw data were bandpass filtered between 1 Hz and 100 Hz and epoched from 2s prior to trial onset to 2s after, in order to avoid edge artifacts in time-frequency decompositions. Independent component analysis (ICA) was used to manually identify and remove artifacts from blinks, saccades, and heartbeats. Covariance matrices from ICA-cleaned data, in combination with structural MRI scans coregistered to MEG sensor locations, were used to compute linearly constrained minimum variance (LCMV) beamformer weights across a 12mm volumetric grid of sources. Morlet wavelet time-frequency decomposition of source estimates was used to estimate source-level power in delta (1–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), and alpha (8–12 Hz) bands, corrected for pre-trial baseline power. Time-frequency data were further epoched from button presses to 1.5 seconds prior (construction period) and from button presses to 1.5s after (elaboration period), and average power in these periods was computed within frequency bands and epochs. Average spectral power was computed across trials, resulting in participant-level source power maps for each task, frequency band, and period (construction vs elaboration). Contrasts between tasks were evaluated using random field theory-based family-wise error p value corrections as implemented in SPM12. For certain analysis, the recall and imagine tasks were collapsed into a single “autobiographical” task for comparison with the control task.


Results

Behaviourally, shorter response times were associated with higher detail ratings (p < 0.001). Participants also exhibited shorter construction times for imagined future events versus recalled past events (p < 0.001) but did not ascribe systematically different levels of detail to each event type (p = 0.12). Both autobiographical tasks were characterized by higher delta and theta power in medial parietal and medial prefrontal regions (core nodes of the DMN; Fig. 2A) and lower alpha power over a broad (both medial and lateral) parietal region during construction and elaboration compared to pre-trial baseline (all cluster-level p < 0.001). Medial parietal delta power during construction and elaboration was also greater for the autobiographical compared to the control task, and broadly-distributed parietal alpha power during elaboration was lower in the autobiographical tasks compared to the control task (all cluster-level p < 0.05). However, no significant differences in theta power emerged between the autobiographical and control tasks. A single voxel in the medial prefrontal cortex showed higher theta power for the recall versus the imagine task in the construction period (peak-level p < 0.05; Fig. 2B). All other contrasts between the recall and imagine tasks, across frequency bands and periods (construction versus elaboration), were non-significant. Finally, no voxel showed any significant association with detail ratings for any frequency band.

Conclusion

The current results mirror and expand on the fMRI literature on memory and imagination in several ways. First, in keeping with the results of previous fMRI studies, we found that the autobiographical tasks were both associated with activity in core nodes of the DMN, specifically in the medial prefrontal and medial parietal cortices. Moreover, we found that this DMN activity specifically took the form of enhanced low-frequency (delta and theta) power during both construction and elaboration relative to pre-trial baseline. Similarly, as in the fMRI literature, what minor differences were detectable between the recall and imagine tasks were restricted to the construction phase. We also found a decrease in alpha power over a broad (both medial and lateral) parietal region during both construction and elaboration in all tasks, an effect previously associated with externally-directed as opposed to internally-directed attention.5 Interestingly, at elaboration the alpha decrease was smaller for the autobiographical tasks than the control task, likely indicative of a shift of attention inwards to mentally elaborate upon recalled or imagined events. More broadly, this study represents a step toward the use of high-temporal resolution methods such as MEG to examine the neural oscillatory mechanisms underlying temporally unconstrained and idiosyncratic cognitive processes such as autobiographical memory and imagination.
