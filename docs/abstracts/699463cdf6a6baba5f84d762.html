Introduction

Aging is a process marked by fundamental changes across multiple domains. Among the core executive functions, inhibitory control is the first to exhibit an accelerated decline1 and experiences the most pronounced deterioration.2 Functional neuroimaging findings vary across inhibition tasks, reflecting their engagement of different subcomponents – interference inhibition, action withholding, and action cancellation.3 The key regions that have been identified irrespective of the task are: the inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), pre-supplementary motor area (preSMA), right middle frontal gyrus (MFG), and basal ganglia.4 

Age-related effects differ across inhibition tasks. A metanalysis identified age-related deficits in the Stop-Signal and Go/No-Go task whereas no comparable impairments were found in other tasks, including Stroop or flanker tasks, suggesting task-specific vulnerability.5 Other work3 indicates that aging slows response speed across tasks, yet only Stop-Signal performance showed increases in omission errors. Importantly, aging was associated with reduced activity in core inhibition regions in the most demanding Stop-Signal task, whereas the Go/No-Go and Simon task showed compensatory increases with age, in parietal regions and left prefrontal regions respectively, consistent with theories such as Compensation-Related Utilization of Neural Circuits Hypothesis (CRUNCH) and The Scaffolding Theory of Aging and Cognition (STAC), which posit that older adults recruit additional neural resources to meet task demands. 

Together, these findings suggest that age effects depend strongly on task demands, but lifespan characterizations of specific inhibition components remain scarce. To address this gap, we investigated age-related changes in brain activation and behavioral performance during a Go/No-Go task in a large sample (n = 697) spanning ages 36 to 89. 

Methods

Data for this study were obtained from the National Institute of Mental Health Data Archive (NDA) as part of the Human Connectome Project – Aging. We downloaded the data of all healthy adults from the Lifespan 2.0 release who completed the task of interest in the scanner. Sixteen participants were excluded due to incomplete data, and an additional twelve were removed because their age had been anonymized as 100, yielding a final sample of 697 individuals (Mage = 59.74 years, SD = 14.89, 386 women). Most participants were right-handed (n = 623) and all completed the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA; M = 26.36, SD = 2.51). 

Participants performed an adapted version of the Conditioned Approach Response Inhibition Task (CARIT), a Go/No-Go task that measures action withholding, a core component of inhibitory control. The stimuli consisted of geometrical shapes: six Go and two No-Go. Each appeared for 600 ms, followed by a fixation cross of variable duration between 1 and 4.52 seconds (inter-trial interval). The total run time was of 4 minutes and included 92 trials (24 no-go, 68 go). Participants were instructed to press a key whenever they saw a Go stimulus and refrain from pressing for the No-Go stimuli.

Behavioral performance was quantified using two metrics: median Go response times (RT), reflecting response speed, and false alarm rates, reflecting failures of inhibitory control. Separate linear regressions were conducted to model the effect of age on RT and false alarm rates. All statistical analyses related to behavioral and demographic data were performed using MATLAB version 23.2.

Preprocessed task-fMRI data were downloaded from the CARIT Preprocessed Legacy Volume package. We applied an additional spatial smoothing step (8-mm FWHM Gaussian kernel) to improve the signal-to-noise ratio. Smoothing and all subsequent first- and second-level analyses were performed in SPM12 (January 2020 release).

For each participant, we modelled three event types: correct Go trials, correct No-Go trials, and errors (misses and false alarms). Onsets were modelled as stick functions and convolved with the canonical HRF. The twelve motion parameters obtained in preprocessing were introduced as nuisance regressors. The high-pass filter was set to 128 seconds and an autoregressive model was used for estimating serial correlations. To isolate neural responses associated with successful response inhibition we computed the contrast No-Go > Go for each participant. These contrast images were carried over to the second-level analyses, where we performed two one-sample t-tests. The first tested age-related effects, including z-scored age and age2 as covariates; the second tested behavioral effects by including false alarm rate. For each model we tested for positive associations, negative associations, and task-related activation independent of covariates. Multiple comparison correction was applied using a family-wise error (FWE) rate of p < 0.05 and an extent threshold of k = 10 voxels.

Results

Performance on the Go/No-Go task was modelled as a function of age. Both response times on Go trials and false alarm rates significantly increased with age: for response times, β = 0.001, SE = 0.0001, t = 9.06, p < .001 (Figure 1a); and for false alarm rates, β < 0.001, SE = 0.0002, t = 5.68, p < .001 (Figure 1b). Moreover, we found a significant negative correlation between the response times and false alarm rate, r = -0.22, p < .001.

We observed inhibition-related activity (No Go > Go) predominantly in the right hemisphere, including right superior frontal gyrus, middle frontal gyrus (MFG), inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), inferior temporal gyrus, cingulate gyrus, precuneus, and parietal regions, such as the angular gyrus and supramarginal gyrus. Bilateral activations were seen in the insular and orbitofrontal cortex, middle and superior temporal gyrus, caudate, and cerebellum.

Older age was associated with stronger inhibition-related activation in the left primary motor cortex, the left putamen, caudate, and nucleus accumbens. No significant negative correlations with age were identified. Higher false alarm rates – our measure of inhibitory control – were linked to stronger inhibition-related activation in the putamen/basal forebrain and caudate – bilaterally. Again, no significant negative correlations were identified (see Figure 1c). 

Conclusion

This study characterized age-related changes in inhibitory control across almost the entire adult lifespan using one of the largest fMRI samples to date. Behaviorally, age was associated with slower responses and higher false alarm rates. The age-related slowing of response is consistent with previous findings and Salthouse’s processing speed theory, which proposes that age-related deficits stem from a general reduction in processing speed.3 In addition, the longer response times may reflect a strategic adjustment to minimize errors, as suggested by the negative association between response times and false-alarm rates. Neurally, our results corroborate prior work by replicating the canonical response-inhibition network, including the MFG, IFG, preSMA, and basal ganglia, with predominant right-hemisphere engagement.3 Contrary to other neuroimaging studies, we observed age-related increases in activation in motor-striatal regions, rather than fronto-parietal control regions. Furthermore, lower-performing participants exhibited greater neural engagement in putamen/basal forebrain and caudate bilaterally. Taken together, these findings argue against effective compensatory recruitment and instead support an account of neural inefficiency in aging, potentially driven by suboptimal cognitive strategies, impaired interhemispheric communication, or age-related neural dedifferentiation. This has important implications for the behavioral and neural developmental trajectory of inhibitory control across the lifespan and future work should investigate if these results replicate across other response-inhibition tasks.
