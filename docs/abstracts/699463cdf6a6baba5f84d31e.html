Introduction

Diet is a modifiable lifestyle factor for many health risks and preservation of cognition. Recent research on Asian cohorts suggested positive impact of multi-nutrient diet styles such as the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) on cognitive impairment and physical frailty in city-dwelling older adults ^1. Nevertheless, several research gaps remain. First, the influence of DASH on domain-specific cognitive performance and brain aging of older adults in Asians, whose diet preference and culture differs from Western populations, remains to be fully elucidated. Second, it is not clear what aspects of brain aging may mediate the diet-cognition association, which could provide clues to potential mechanistic pathways. Third, few studies have examined the longer-term impact of diet on brain and cognition in Asian cohorts. Establishing such associations (> 10 years) with brain aging processes as potential explanations would underscore the importance of adapting healthy diet patterns early in life to ensure health and wellbeing at late life ^1. To this end, we investigated the association of DASH adherence in a group of Singaporean residents at their middle age (44-60 years), early elderhood (61-73 years), and late life (68-82 years) with brain morphometry, cerebrovascular disease markers, and cognitive performance measured at late life. We hypothesized that better midlife and early elderhood diet quality would be associated with better cognitive performance, preserved brain morphometry, and less severe cerebrovascular diseases at late life; furthermore, some of the these preserved brain attributes would mediate the diet-cognition associations, explaining the potential benefits of diet on cognition through reduction of cerebrovascular risks and/or neurodegeneration.

Methods

1037 consented Singaporean older adults without dementia (aged 68-82 years, 562 Female), who participated in the on-going population-based Singaporean Chinese Health Study (SCHS) ^1 in 1993-1998 (midlife) and/or 2014-2016 (early elderhood), returned as part of the ‘SG70:Towards Healthy Longevity’ cohort between 2021 and 2025 (late life) and completed a multimodal 3T MRI session and neuropsychological assessments. 

DASH scores were calculated at each of the 3 phases based on the food frequency questionnaire and a modified dietary screener. ^1 Informed by past studies, brain metrics corresponding to morphometry and cerebrovascular diseases were derived for subsequent analyses. Specifically, brain morphometric measures were extracted from MPRAGE T1 images (1 mm isotropic) using Freesurfer 5.3 (vertex-wise cortical thickness, surface area, and volume) and CAT12 (voxel-wise grey matter volume GMV and white matter volume WMV). White matter hyperintensity (WMH) was segmented from FLAIR images (1 mm isotropic) using WMH-DualTasker ^2 with manual QC, followed by the derivation of 7 regional WMH patterns using the Bullseye representation and factor analysis (anterior periventricular PV, frontoparietal FP, deep basal ganglia BG, left and right horn, temporal, and parietal. ^3 Vertex-wise gray matter freewater (GM FW), a marker of subtle cerebrovascular insult, was derived from multi-shell diffusion MRI images (1.8mm isotropic, 2 shells, 124 directions, NODDI) using an in-house pipeline. ^4 Finally, scores from 9 neuropsychological tests were standardized (mean 50, SD 10) and averaged into 7 domain scores (attention, executive function, processing speed, verbal memory, visual memory, visuospatial ability, and language). 

Brain and cognitive associations with DASH scores at each phase were examined using general linear regression models (GLM) in R (lm() for cognitive domain scores, FDR-adjusted for 7 domains; the VertexWiseR package for vertex-wise morphometry; lm() for regional WMH, FDR-adjusted for 7 spatial patterns), CAT12 (voxel-wise GMV/WMV), and a published in-house module based on Freesurfer and FSL (vertex-wise GM FW ^4). Cluster level inferences were used for both vertex- and voxel-wise brain analyses (threshold p = .01, cluster p = .05 FWE). The baseline GLM model adjusted for intracranial volume (brain analyses), education, age (current age at late life, and respective age at a given phase), sex, housing, and father’s dialect. Subsequent GLM models further adjusted for self-reported medical history (hypertension, diabetes, stroke, heart conditions, and cancer), late life BMI (4 groups), smoking (3 groups) and drinking history (3 groups), physical activity at early elderhood (3 groups), and late life average sleep duration (5 groups), following our previous work as closely as possible. ^1 Finally, mediation analyses between DASH scores and cognition were conducted on brain metrics and cognitive domains showing statistically significant association with DASH using the R lavaan package (5000 bootstrapping for mediation effect statistical inference based on 95% confidence intervals). All variables entering the mediation analyses were first residualized with respect to the covariates. Further sensitivity analyses included repeating with DASH score quantiles ^1 and restricting to a ‘healthy’ subsample (participants who failed fewer than 4 cognitive domains based on Singaporean norms minus 1.5SD, geriatric depression score <= 5, and Quick Dementia Rating System-converted Clinical Dementia Rating QDRS-CDR = 0). 

Results

Cognition. Higher DASH scores at midlife, early elderhood, and late life were all consistently associated with better verbal memory at late life (beta coefficients = .21, .18, and .24, respectively; FDR ps < .01; Fig. 1A). Multiple regression including all DASH scores in the same model suggested additive influence of midlife (beta coefficient = .14, p  = .035) and late life (beta coefficient = .16, p = .029; Fig. 1B) DASH on late life verbal memory.

Brain morphometry. Better midlife DASH was associated with larger WMV in the corpus callosum, inferior frontal, and parietotemporal areas (cluster pFWE < .05; Fig. 2A and B). With reference to the HCP-YA1065 tractography atlas, the lateral WM regions overlap with the bilateral arcuate fasciculus (AF; 16.6% and 22.5%) and superior longitudinal fasciculus III (SLF3; 14.2% and 14.5%), which are involved in language processing. While better early elderhood DASH and late life DASH was associated with larger volume in posterior motor and inferior frontal cortices, respectively, these GM morphometry was not statistically associated with late life verbal memory.

Regional WMH. Better early elderhood DASH was associated with less anterior PV and deep BG WMH (beta coefficients = -.035 and -.018, FDR ps = .045 and .013), but regional WMH was not statistically related to late life verbal memory. 

GM FW. Better early elderhood DASH was associated with less GM FW in left frontal and right occipital areas (cluster pFWE < .05; Fig. 2D and E); similar association was observed bilaterally, with the addition of cingulate cortices, with late life DASH (cluster pFWE < .05; Fig. 2E). 

Mediation. The relationship between better midlife DASH and better late life verbal memory was partially mediated by larger WMV (beta coefficient = .027, 95% CI = [.008, .052]; Fig. 2C). The relationship between better early elderhood DASH and better late life verbal memory was partially mediated by less GM FW (beta coefficient = .023, 95% CI = [.007, .044]; Fig. 2F), similarly with late life DASH (beta coefficient = .029, 95% CI = [.008, .057]). 

Conclusion

Our results suggested that diet quality at midlife and early elderhood continues to impact cognition and brain health at late life. More reliable association with verbal memory than non-memory domains is consistent with past studies, and might be related to the nutrient balances (e.g., more vegetables and moderate protein) recommended in DASH. Importantly, midlife diet still exerted independent effect on late life verbal memory controlling for more recent diet, suggesting that distal effects of diet to our body cannot be fully ‘undone’ by more recent dietary choices. This is supported by our diet-brain associations that appeared to also be life course dependent. Specifically, better diet quality at midlife may enable white matter neural reserve to build up (larger volume). That such reserve was enriched for tracts involved in language processes and verbal memory warrants further investigation.  Continued better diet quality at early elderhood may slow down the formation or accumulation of vascular and microstructural damages (WMH and GM FW). On the other hand, the diet association with atrophy was relatively limited, adding to the mixed results discussed in recent narrative reviews. ^5 Finally, mediations on the diet-cognition association were partial, suggesting that diet quality in earlier life course confer further protective effects to cognition that should be investigated in the future.
