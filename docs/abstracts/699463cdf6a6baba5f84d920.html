Introduction

Placebo analgesia, the reduction of pain through expectation, has long been attributed to top–down cortical appraisal and descending control through well-characterized brainstem–spinal pathways, including the rACC, anterior midcingulate cortex (aMCC), ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC), insula, periaqueductal gray (PAG), rostroventromedial medulla (RVM), and the spinal dorsal horn 1. Although cerebellar activations and deactivations are frequently observed in human pain and placebo imaging, they have often been dismissed as motor-related epiphenomena. However, recent animal studies have identified cerebellar activity that parallels cortico–brainstem signaling during expectation-based pain modulation. Whether and how cerebellar involvement in human placebo analgesia is organized at the network level remains unclear.

Methods

We analyzed group-level statistical maps from a prior individual-participant data meta-analysis of 20 published studies (N = 603) 2. The outcome definitions, effect-size standardization, and voxelwise statistical inference follow the original meta-analysis. We evaluated three voxelwise outcomes from the prior meta-analysis: (i) pain stimulation effect, (ii) placebo treatment effect, and (iii) brain–behaviour correlations in placebo analgesia. All voxelwise statistical maps were aggregated across studies using a random-effects model, which accounts for between-study heterogeneity.

All statistical maps were projected onto the Spatially Unbiased Infratentorial Template (SUIT) cerebellar flatmap 3. Spatial correspondence between cerebellar maps and functional parcellations was quantified using vertex-wise overlap analyses with the Buckner 17-network 4 and Nettekoven 32-network atlases 5, restricted to cerebellar vertices. Overlap was measured using the Dice similarity coefficient.

Results

Painful stimulation was associated with robust cerebellar activity increases, primarily involving regions linked to higher-order action control and socio-linguistic processing. These effects were centered in Crus I, with activity extending into Crus II and lobule V. When mapped onto canonical resting-state network parcellations, the pain-evoked cerebellar pattern intersected predominantly with the Control B/C, TempPar, Social-linguistic-spatial S1/2/3, and Demand D1 networks.

Placebo treatment was associated with reduced pain-related responses in partially overlapping cerebellar regions, mainly in the left hemisphere, including lobules VI, V, and Crus I, with anterior extension into lobules I–IV. Brain–behavior correlations in placebo analgesia were distributed bilaterally across the cerebellum, encompassing Crus I/II, lobules I–IV, V, VI, and the vermis. Placebo-related decreases and brain–behavior correlations showed substantial spatial convergence across cerebellar territories. Regions exhibiting reduced pain responses under placebo were largely contained within broader cerebellar zones associated with behavioural analgesia, spanning the Somatomotor A/B, Salience/Ventral-Attention A/B, Motor M2/3, Action A2, and Demand D3/4 networks.

Conclusion

These findings indicate that cerebellar responses observed in human pain and placebo analgesia are systematically organized at the network level rather than reflecting nonspecific motor-related epiphenomena. The observed spatial correspondence with action-, demand-, and salience-related cerebellar networks is consistent with a role for the cerebellum in expectation-related modulation of pain and highlights the value of cerebellar network frameworks for interpreting human pain and placebo neuroimaging results.
