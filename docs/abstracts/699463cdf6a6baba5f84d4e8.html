Introduction

Mounting evidence shows that multi-organ relationships across brain, heart, liver, and kidney represent an integrated physiological network in which cardiovascular function, metabolic regulation, and renal clearance jointly shape brain health, vulnerability, and disease progression1. To that end, establishing brain mappings that underline latent cross-organ pathways is essential for understanding the effect of early multi-organ alterations on neural integrity and thus enabling more accurate, mechanistically informed prevention strategies for neurodegenerative and vascular-metabolic disorders. By capitalizing on the unprecedented amount of multi-organ MRI data in the UK Biobank (UKB), we employed a principal network regression model2 to identify all possible functional sub-networks that exhibit significant statistical association with image-derived phenotypes (IDPs) of cardiac, liver, and kidney. Specifically, we have detected 47, 4, and 2 functional sub-networks that are strongly associated with 220 cardiac IDPs, 4 liver IDPs, and 4 kidney IDPs, respectively. By doing so, we established a collection of brain mappings, each linked to characteristic structural or functional features of other organs along the brain–heart–liver–kidney axis. Furthermore, we characterized the community structure of pairwise organ-to-organ relationships by analyzing the functional sub-networks jointly associated with each multi-organ pair, allowing us to uncover clusters of shared neural pathways that reflect coordinated multi-organ influences on brain function.

Methods

Data description. We included 1,481 UKB participants with both T1-weighted and resting-state fMRI scans (age:64.67±7.64, M/F: 46.3%:53.7%). Functional networks (116x116, AAL atlas3) were generated using our in-house preprocessing pipeline, including skull stripping, tissue segmentation, registration, motion correction, and band-pass filtering. Among these participants, 73,321 had available cardiac MRI traits (457 IDPs, UKB data field #102), 69,622 had liver MRI traits (14 IDPs, UKB field #126), and 44,310 had kidney MRI traits (17 IDPs, UKB field #156). Specifically, heart traits include ~200 traits capturing chamber structure, function, strain, aortic morphology, and pulse-wave hemodynamics. Liver traits include hepatic iron concentration (R2*), corrected T1 (cT1) as a fibrosis/inflammation marker, and proton-density fat fraction (PDFF) for liver fat. Kidney traits include segmented parenchymal volumes (left, right, and combined).

Detect functional sub-networks using the principal network regression model. Suppose ∑i denotes the functional connectivity (FC) of  ith subject, which is a covariance matrix of region-to-region co-activations arising from spontaneous, self-synchronized functional fluctuations. Given an organ IPD xi, the principal network regression model seeks to estimate a set of projection vector γ across individuals that the log-variance log⁡(γT∑iγ) is well explained by the linear model: log⁡(γT∑iγ)=xiTβ+εi, where β is the regression slope w.r.t. the underlying organ trait xi and εi is a subject-specific random variable. Since the projection vector γ has a spatial correspondence with brain regions, the resulting estimate of γ is referred to as an organ-specific functional subnetwork. In the reminiscence of PCA (principal component analysis), the network-based regression model yields a set of orthogonal projection directions, each accompanied by a confidence interval quantifying its association with the trait xi. 

Investigate multi-organ relationships through shared neural pathways. We applied the following analyses to elucidate the latent complex multi-organ relationships. 

  1. Organ-brain relationships. We hypothesize that each organ trait is characterized by a unique functional fingerprint, whereby individual differences in cardiac, liver, or kidney measures map onto specific, spatially distributed patterns of brain connectivity. Approach. We analyzed a total of 228 organ traits (220 heart, 4 liver, and 4 kidney). Each imaging trait was evaluated individually as the primary predictor, with age, sex, and imaging site included as covariates. At a significance level p<0.05, 47 functional sub-networks have been identified that exhibit significant association with body organs. Each organ-specific functional sub-network allows us to understand the contribution of brain regions along the brain-heart-liver-kidney axis, along with the effect size β of the underlying organ trait on brain function. 
  2. Multi-organ relationship analysis. We hypothesize that cardiac, hepatic, and renal status are jointly coupled through large-scale functional brain networks, such that coordinated alterations in heart, liver, and kidney imaging traits might be mediated by shared and organ-specific functional sub-networks. Approach. Considering the projection vector γ as the statistically significant functional fingerprint for the underlying organ trait, we construct an adjacency matrix, where each element denotes the inner product of projection vectors between two organ traits. Then, we applied affinity propagation4 (a non-parametric clustering method) to detect latent community structure across multiple organs. 

Results

Functional sub-networks associated with the heart, liver, and kidney. Given the IDPs from the heart, liver, and kidney, the identified significant projection vector  enables us to understand the neural circuits associated that might share common biological pathways. As shown in Fig. 1 top, functional sub-networks showing strong associations with global left ventricular myocardial wall thickness, liver cT1 for fibro-inflammatory activity, and left kidney parenchymal volume were identified, with effect directions indicated by positive and negative associations. Across these three body organ traits, convergent associations were observed within the default-mode network (DMN), frontoparietal network (FPN), and sensorimotor network (SMN). In addition, complementary analyses of pulse wave analysis–derived cardiac output, liver cT1, and right kidney parenchymal volume further revealed consistent involvement of the DMN, FPN, and visual network (VN), as shown in Fig. 1 bottom. Together, these results demonstrate that brain–organ relationships converge on large-scale cortical systems supporting integrative processing and physiological homeostasis, highlighting a shared neural substrate underlying multi-organ regulation.

Multi-organ relationship through brain function. First, we define the projection vector γ for each organ imaging trait as its functional fingerprint. In cases where a trait corresponds to multiple projection vectors, we choose the vector with the smallest p-value to represent it. We next constructed an adjacency matrix capturing organ-to-organ relationships across the heart, liver, and kidney (Fig. 2a), where each node represents a specific organ IDP. We then characterized the distribution of multi-organ affinity degrees (Fig. 2b), which exhibits a near bell-shaped pattern indicating that most nodes share a similar number of connections. A zoomed-in analysis of the top 15% strongest multi-organ relationships further reveals a sparse connectivity profile in which most organs maintain only a few cross-organ links, whereas a small subset of organ IDPs emerge as highly connected hubs. Taken together, these findings suggest that organ-to-organ relationships exhibit a “small-world” topology similar to that observed in functional brain networks.

Our clustering results further corroborate the small-world organization, identifying several community structures spanning heart, liver, and kidney (shown in Fig. 2c). Membership within a community suggests that these organs are linked through a shared functional sub-network. Typical examples are shown in Fig. 1, where IDPs within the same community are linked with the same functional sub-networks. In addition, we detected two non-overlapping intersection regions (highlighted in blue boxes in Fig. 2a), indicating that the involved organs are associated with clearly distinct functional sub-networks. 

Mechanistic role of brain function in multi-organ relationships. Since organ IDPs are connected through shared functional sub-networks, we summarize the frequency with which individual brain regions are involved in these brain–body associations (i.e., brain mapping of projection vectors shown in Fig. 1). As shown in Fig. 2d, the rows correspond to body organs (in the same order as Fig. 2a), and the columns represent brain regions organized by major functional networks: frontoparietal, visual, default-mode, ventral-attention, sensorimotor, and cerebellar. In average, each brain region is associated with 1.64 body organs. Notably, two DMN regions (Angular_R, Frontal_Mid_L) and four visual network regions (Occipital_Mid_L, Cuneus_R, Occipital_Sup_L/R) show the strongest brain–body associations. This observation is consistent with prior work showing that the default-mode network is a primary neural substrate for representing whole-body physiological states, and that the visual network is particularly sensitive to vascular and metabolic signals associated with heart, liver, and kidney function.

Conclusion

This work provides converging evidence that peripheral organs, such as the heart, liver, and kidney, are strongly coupled with large-scale brain functional organization. By leveraging a principal network regression framework, we identified specific functional sub-networks that are significantly associated with organ-specific imaging traits, highlighting shared neural substrates underlying brain–body interactions. Moreover, the observed multi-organ relationships exhibit a “small-world” topology, characterized by dense coupling among a limited subset of organs and neural circuits, alongside more specialized interactions involving just one or two organ systems. These findings suggest that brain–organ communication is structured rather than random or fully-connected topology, with selective hubs mediating cross-organ system integration. Together, our results provide a multi-level perspective on cross-organ influences on brain function and open new avenues for investigating brain–body interactions in aging, disease progression, and population-level health.
