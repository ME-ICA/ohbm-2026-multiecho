Introduction

Extracurricular activities constitute a critical environmental context shaping children's development. With rapid technological advances, engagement has diversified from traditional physical activities to digital platforms, and from individually based to socially interactive formats.1 Childhood is also a period of heightened neuroplasticity and psychiatric vulnerability, during which alterations in brain maturation may contribute to mental health.2 Identifying the neural mechanisms linking activity engagement to mental health is therefore essential. Prior neuroimaging research highlights gray matter volume as a robust marker of experience- and activity-dependent plasticity.3 However, it remains unclear how different activity modalities (digital vs. physical) and social contexts (social vs. non-social) jointly influence mental health and brain development, and whether these relationships are bidirectional. The present study investigated how the interaction between modality and social context relates to psychiatric symptoms and gray matter volume, and whether brain structural differences mediate prospective activity–mental health associations.

Methods

We used data from 8,230 children in the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development Study (ABCD) Study at baseline (Wave 1) and one-year follow-up (Wave 2). Activity engagement was assessed through self-reported weekly screen time for six digital activities (three social, three non-social) and caregiver-reported participation in 28 extracurricular activities (11 social, 17 non-social).4 Mental health was measured using 11 Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL) syndrome scales and lifetime psychiatric diagnoses from the Kiddie Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia (K-SADS). Gray matter volume (GMV) was derived from T1-weighted MRI using the ABCD processing pipeline. All 34 activities were classified along two dimensions—modality (physical vs. digital) and social context (social vs. non-social)—yielding four engagement categories (Fig. 1A).

We implemented a stepwise, data-driven analytical framework (Fig. 1B). First, we examined cross-sectional associations between activity engagement and both regional GMV (68 cortical regions) and CBCL scores at baseline using linear mixed-effects models (LMMs; R lme4 v4.3.3). Each model was fitted separately per activity category and outcome. Fixed-effect covariates included age, sex, race/ethnicity, parental education, household income, and pubertal status; study site was modeled as a random intercept. 

For mental health dimensions showing significant baseline associations with activity categories (FDR-corrected P < .05), we tested longitudinal directionality using two-wave cross-lagged panel models (CLPMs) implemented in lavaan (v0.6.19). The models estimated: (i) prospective effects of baseline activity on Wave-2 mental health (path b); (ii) reverse associations (path c); (iii) temporal stability of each construct (paths a and d); and (iv) baseline cross-sectional associations (paths e and f). CLPMs included time-invariant covariates (sex, ethnicity, parental education, study site) and time-varying covariates (age, income, BMI, pubertal status). Standardized coefficients and 95% CIs were obtained using full information maximum likelihood. Model fit was evaluated using CFI, TLI, and SRMR criteria

For activity categories showing significant longitudinal effects (path b), we tested whether regional GMV mediated the relationship between baseline activity and Wave-2 mental health. Mediators were defined as summed volumes of cortical regions that showed significant activity-specific associations in the baseline GMV analyses. Mediation models were estimated in lavaan with 5,000 bootstrap resamples, adjusting for baseline covariates (sex, age, ethnicity, parental education, income, pubertal status, BMI, study site). We report standardized total, direct, and indirect effects with 95% bootstrap CIs.

Results

Sample characteristics

At baseline, 8230 children (mean age = 9.9 years; 48% female) contributed valid behavioral and activity data; 6893 (83.7%) completed 1-year follow-up. Activity engagement was highly skewed toward digital non-social use (median = 17.5 h/week), whereas participation in physical social, physical non-social, and digital social activities was substantially lower. Demographic characteristics and activity distributions were stable across waves.

Associations between activity categories and mental health

Linear mixed-effects models revealed distinct activity-specific patterns (Fig 2A). Physical social activities showed broad protective associations, predicting lower severity in 7 of 11 CBCL scales, with strongest effects for withdrawn/depressed (β = −0.052 [95% CI, −0.075 to −0.030]), attention problems (β = −0.049 [−0.072 to −0.027]), and social problems (β = −0.048 [−0.070 to −0.026]; all FDR-corrected P < .001). Physical non-social activities were linked to greater internalizing symptoms. Digital activities showed context-dependent risks. Digital non-social engagement was consistently associated with poorer mental health across 9 of 11 domains (β ≈ 0.054–0.074; FDR-corrected P < .001). Digital social activities exhibited mixed associations: reduced withdrawn/depressed symptoms but increased rule-breaking behaviors.

Activity-related gray matter correlates

Morphometric analyses showed a hierarchical pattern across activity categories (Fig 2A). Digital non-social engagement was associated with widespread cortical volume reductions, particularly in bilateral temporal cortices (β ≈ −0.05; FDR-corrected P < .001). Digital social activities showed more localized reductions in bilateral superior parietal regions. Physical activities showed opposite patterns: physical social participation predicted cortical volume increases across frontoparietal and temporal areas, including right rostral anterior cingulate and middle temporal regions (β = 0.032–0.042; FDR-corrected P < .05). Physical non-social activities showed small, nonsignificant positive trends.

Bidirectional longitudinal associations

CLPMs indicated directional pathways over one year (Fig 2B). Physical social engagement predicted improvements in withdrawn/depressed symptoms (β = −0.029 [−0.049 to −0.010]; FDR P = .008) and social problems (β = −0.025 [−0.044 to −0.007]; FDR P = .020). Digital non-social activity predicted worsening rule-breaking (β = 0.035 [0.017–0.054]; FDR P = .001), attention problems (β = 0.042 [0.025–0.058]; FDR P < .001), thought problems (β = 0.034 [0.015–0.052]; FDR P = .001), and total problems (β = 0.027 [0.011–0.043]; FDR P = .002). Reverse pathways from baseline symptoms to later activity were weaker and inconsistent, suggesting activities function more as antecedents than consequences of symptom change.

Mediation through gray matter volume

Gray matter alterations partially mediated longitudinal associations (Fig 2C). For physical social activity, cortical volume increases mediated reductions in social problems (indirect effect: −0.002 [−0.004 to −0.000]; P = .004; 3.8% mediated). Volume decreases linked to digital social activity mediated increases in rule-breaking behaviors (0.003 [0.001–0.005]; P = .004; 3.7% mediated). Digital non-social–related volume reductions mediated multiple outcomes: rule-breaking (0.004 [0.002–0.006]; P < .001; 4.7%), social problems (0.003 [0.000–0.005]; P = .006; 5.0%), attention problems (0.004 [0.002–0.006]; P < .001; 4.7%), and total problems (0.003 [0.001–0.005]; P < .001; 4.2%).

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides an integrative framework demonstrating how the modality (physical vs. digital) and social context (social vs. non-social) of children’s extracurricular engagement jointly shape mental health and brain development. Across analytic levels, a consistent pattern emerged: physical social activities were linked to healthier psychological profiles and increased cortical volume in frontoparietal–temporal regions, whereas digital non-social engagement showed broad associations with elevated psychiatric symptoms and widespread temporal–parietal volume reductions. Longitudinal analyses confirmed that these activity patterns prospectively predicted changes in mental health rather than merely reflecting pre-existing symptoms. Mediation results further indicated that activity-related gray matter differences constitute one mechanistic pathway through which experiences influence later psychological functioning. Together, these findings suggest a hierarchical developmental cascade in which activity modality exerts primary influence on cortical structure, while the social context modulates both neural responses and behavioral outcomes. By characterizing how everyday activities map onto neurodevelopmental trajectories, our work highlights the importance of distinguishing not only between physical and digital modalities but also between social and solitary engagement. This multidimensional framework provides an empirical foundation for developing more nuanced guidelines and intervention strategies that account for both the experiential content and social environment of children’s activities.
