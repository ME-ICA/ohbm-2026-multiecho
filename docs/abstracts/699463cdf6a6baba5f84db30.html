Introduction

Mental disorders are a leading global cause of disability, yet their clinical diagnosis remains largely subjective, relying on behavioral symptoms with significant overlap among conditions like schizophrenia (SZ), bipolar disorder (BD), major depressive disorder (MDD), and obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD). This underscores the need for objective diagnostic tools. While structural MRI (sMRI) and machine/deep learning models show promise in research for classifying single disorders versus healthy controls (HC), critical translational gaps persist. Most models fail to address the complex challenge of differential diagnosis among multiple disorders and lack validation in heterogeneous real-world clinical settings, limiting their clinical applicability.

To bridge this gap, we developed the Patch-based Hierarchical Network (PHN), a deep learning framework for classifying SZ, BD, MDD, OCD, and HC from sMRI data. Trained on a large, well-controlled dataset (n=2,490), the PHN was designed to capture subtle and distributed neuroanatomical alterations. A key contribution of this work is the implementation and deployment of a closed-loop, neuroimaging-based diagnostic support system that integrates the PHN into an active clinical workflow. This study provides a tangible step toward translating computational research into practical tools, leveraging artificial intelligence to offer objective support for psychiatric diagnosis.

Methods

SMHC Dataset: The SMHC training dataset comprised 2,490 participants, including healthy controls (HC) and patients diagnosed with bipolar disorder (BD), major depressive disorder (MDD), schizophrenia (SZ), and obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD). Data were acquired using a 3T Siemens Verio scanner with consistent parameters. Diagnoses followed DSM or ICD criteria. Images underwent quality control (visual inspection and CAT12 grading) and preprocessing, including N4 bias field correction and skull stripping.1 The final sample included 300 HC, 468 BD, 169 MDD, 1,285 SZ, and 268 OCD individuals.

Model architecture of the patch-based hierarchical neural network: We designed a Patch-based Hierarchical Network (PHN, Figure 1) with a two-stage architecture: a pre-attention stage (PHN-stage1) to identify potential disease-related brain regions, and a feature extraction/classification stage (PHN-stage2). Its core is a Patch-based Feature Extraction Network (P-FEN) that employs stacked U-structures for efficient multi-scale feature extraction from input image patches, outputting a normalized disease probability via a perceptron and softmax layer.

Technical details of PHN: The Patch-based Hierarchical Network (PHN) is a two-stage deep learning framework. PHN-stage1 identifies diagnosis-sensitive regions by randomly extracting 300 overlapping image patches (size 48×48×48 voxels) from the input brain volume. Each patch is processed by P-FEN to generate a disease probability. An individual-specific attention (confidence) map is created by aggregating these patch-level probabilities across the brain.

PHN-stage2 selects the top 10 image patches centered on peak-confidence voxels from the attention map. Features are extracted from these patches via the P-FEN, concatenated into a sequence, and integrated using a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network to model spatial dependencies. The LSTM's output is combined with normalized age and sex information, and a final perceptron layer yields the diagnostic probability. Key hyperparameters (patch size, number of patches) were optimized via ablation studies.

Model training and cross-validation: All models were trained on the SMHC dataset using a 5-fold cross-validation scheme. We developed binary classifiers for pairwise comparisons and for "one-versus-others" tasks. The P-FEN was trained for 300 epochs per fold using balanced sampling. 

Quantification And Statistical Analysis: The PHN's performance was compared against five traditional machine learning models (Logistic Regression, SVM, Random Forest, XGBoost, GBDT) and two 3D CNN benchmarks (VGG, ResNet). Features for traditional models were derived from FreeSurfer (cortical/subcortical morphometrics) and PyRadiomics (radiomics),2,3 reduced via PCA to a 16-dimensional set.

Results

Experimental setup and cross-validation performance: Evaluated via 5-fold cross-validation on the SMHC dataset, the PHN achieved robust performance. In pairwise classification, it attained AUCs ≥0.90 in 7 of 10 tasks (e.g., BD-HC, SZ-HC), with the lowest AUC (0.68) for BD-SZ. In multi-class classification (HC, BD, MDD, SZ, OCD), AUCs were 0.90 (HC), 0.73 (BD), 0.91 (MDD), 0.84 (SZ), and 0.93 (OCD). The PHN outperformed alternative algorithms (SVM, Random Forest, a baseline CNN) across all 15 classification tasks (Figure 2A & 2B).

Clinical Translation: Building upon the PHN classifiers, we implemented a real-world, automated neuroimage report system at the Shanghai Mental Health Center (Figure 2C). This system integrates physicians' workstations, an MRI workstation, and an image processing server, automating the workflow from image acquisition to report generation. The system's output includes visualizations of the PHN's inferences, such as radar plots that display one-vs-other predictions, accommodating potential comorbidities. Additionally, bar charts provide pairwise comparisons to aid in distinguishing between specific diagnoses (Figure 2D).

Importantly, the system operates within a closed-loop framework, linking psychiatrist requests, MRI acquisition, automated analyses, results delivery, and feedback collection. By continuously incorporating new brain images and diagnostic feedback from psychiatrists, the system facilitates iterative learning and improvement of the model's performance over time. Specifically, cases where the consensus clinical diagnoses (established by psychiatrists) diverge from the PHN's predictions are identified and collected. This feedback dataset, comprising these discordant cases along with their confirmed clinical diagnoses, is then utilized to fine-tune the existing PHN model parameters. The fine-tuning process involves retraining the relevant model components for a fixed number of epochs (currently set to 100). Following this fine-tuning, the updated model parameters are deployed back into the clinical support system. Based on current data accumulation rates at our institution, we anticipate updating the deployed model at approximately three-month intervals. This real-world implementation demonstrates the feasibility of integrating PHN-based classifiers into clinical workflows to support psychiatric diagnosis and highlights the potential for ongoing refinement and optimization through continuous data integration and feedback.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the significant potential of integrating deep learning with brain structural imaging to improve the objectivity and accuracy of psychiatric diagnoses. The primary contribution of this work is the development and validation of the PHN, a deep learning model capable of differentiating among multiple psychiatric disorders and identifying potential comorbidities. The PHN was successfully implemented in a real-world clinical setting, demonstrating its initial feasibility and laying the foundation for the development of practical neuroimaging-aided diagnostic tools. While further optimization and validation are needed, this work represents a significant step towards bridging the gap between research and practice, with the ultimate goal of leveraging neuroimaging and AI to improve the lives of individuals affected by mental illness.
