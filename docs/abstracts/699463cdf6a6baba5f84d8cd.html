Introduction

Cognitive impairment in Alzheimer’s disease is typically quantified using single-task scores or linear composites that summarize overall performance but discard information about the structure of cognition across domains. However, neurodegeneration, particularly within the entorhinal–hippocampal system, disrupts coordinated cognitive organization rather than isolated abilities, posing a central challenge for human brain mapping¹,³.

The entorhinal cortex plays a critical role in memory integration and cognitive mapping and is among the earliest regions affected in Alzheimer’s disease¹–³. Structural degeneration of the entorhinal cortex has been consistently linked to cognitive decline, yet existing cognitive metrics are limited in their ability to reflect how cross-domain cognitive relationships change alongside regional brain pathology²,⁴. As a result, associations between brain structure, cognition, and disease progression are often examined using univariate or weakly multivariate approaches.

In this study, we introduce the Entorhinal Topology Index (ETI), a multivariate cognitive metric designed to quantify deviations in cross-domain cognitive organization relative to a cognitively normal reference structure. ETI is constructed using pairwise relationships among cognitive tasks and encodes cognitive disorganization as a distance from normative cognitive topology. Using data from the National Alzheimer’s Coordinating Center, we examine whether ETI relates to entorhinal cortex structure, genetic risk, and clinical progression.

By integrating cognitive topology with structural neuroimaging, this work aims to provide a complementary framework for linking brain degeneration to cognitive disorganization and disease progression beyond traditional summary scores.

Methods

Data Source and Study Population

Data were obtained from the National Alzheimer’s Coordinating Center (NACC) Uniform Data Set (UDS), comprising longitudinal clinical, cognitive, genetic, and neuroimaging data collected across Alzheimer’s Disease Research Centers in the United States. Analyses used investigator-level CSV extracts from UDS versions 6–7.

Participants were included if they had valid diagnostic information (NACCUDSD), demographic covariates (age, sex, education), and at least one visit with complete cognitive data required for computation of the Entorhinal Topology Index (ETI). Diagnostic categories were coded as cognitively normal (CN), mild cognitive impairment (MCI), or dementia using standard NACC definitions.

Cognitive Task Selection and Availability Audit

To maximize sample size without imputation, an empirical task-availability audit was conducted. Pairwise availability matrices were computed across candidate cognitive measures, and maximal cliques of jointly available tasks were identified. An eight-task clique was selected a priori based on joint availability and theoretical relevance to memory, executive control, and cognitive integration: Craft Story Immediate Recall (CRAFTVRS), Craft Story Delayed Recall (CRAFTDVR), Category Fluency (Animals), MMSE total score (MINTTOTS), Benton Judgment of Line Orientation (UDSBENTC, UDSBENTD), Digit Span Forward (DIGFORCT), and Digit Span Backward (DIGBACCT).

Visit Timing and Baseline Definition

Visit order was determined using the NACC visit sequence variable (NACCAVST). For each participant, the scoring visit was defined as the earliest visit with complete cognitive data, irrespective of diagnosis. A normative reference visit was defined as the earliest cognitively normal visit (NACCUDSD = 1) with complete data. Visit dates were reconstructed using VISITYR, VISITMO, and VISITDAY.

Construction of the Entorhinal Topology Index

Cognitive task scores were z-standardized relative to the cognitively normal reference sample, using means and standard deviations derived from the earliest complete CN visit per participant.

For each participant, absolute pairwise differences between standardized task scores were computed, yielding a vector of cross-domain cognitive dissimilarities. A covariance matrix of these dissimilarities was estimated from the CN reference sample and ridge-regularized (λ = 0.001) prior to inversion. ETI was defined as the square root of the Mahalanobis distance between each participant’s dissimilarity vector and the CN reference centroid.

Neuroimaging Measures and Alignment

Entorhinal cortex (EC) measures were derived from structural MRI processed using standardized surface-based morphometry pipelines. Bilateral gray matter volume, surface area, and mean cortical thickness were extracted and averaged across hemispheres. MRI scans were aligned to ETI scoring visits by selecting the scan closest in time within ±365 days. 

APOE Genotyping

APOE genotype was derived from NACCAPOE and coded as ε2/ε2, ε2/ε3, ε3/ε3, ε3/ε4, or ε4/ε4. Derived variables included APOE ε4 carrier status and ε4 allele dose (0–2). Genotype was anchored to the earliest available visit.

Statistical Analyses

Cross-sectional associations between ETI and EC measures were examined using linear regression adjusted for age, sex, education, and diagnosis, with HC3 robust standard errors. APOE main effects were evaluated with and without diagnostic adjustment. Moderation analyses tested EC × APOE ε4 dose interactions.

Longitudinal conversion from CN to MCI/dementia and from MCI to dementia was assessed using Cox proportional hazards models, with baseline defined as the earliest ETI-scored visit and censoring at last follow-up. Models adjusted for age, sex, and education. Kaplan–Meier curves stratified by ETI tertiles were generated for visualization.

Software

All analyses were conducted in Python using pandas, NumPy, statsmodels, and lifelines.

Results

Sample Characteristics and ETI Computation

The Entorhinal Topology Index (ETI_v3) was computed for 24,242 participants from the NACC UDS with complete cognitive data at at least one visit. Normative scaling was based on cognitively normal (CN; NACCUDSD = 1) participants using the earliest complete visit per subject (n = 12,602). ETI scores were assigned to all participants using their earliest complete-case visit, irrespective of diagnosis.

Of these, 2,399 participants had at least one entorhinal MRI scan available. Applying a ±365-day alignment window between ETI scoring and MRI acquisition yielded a final MRI-aligned sample of 903 participants. Age, education, and diagnostic distributions were comparable between included and excluded participants, though mean ETI values were modestly higher in the MRI-aligned subset (Table 1).

Associations Between ETI and Entorhinal Cortex Structure

In the MRI-aligned sample (n = 903), higher ETI was associated with reduced entorhinal cortex volume. In linear regression models adjusted for age, sex, education, and diagnosis, mean entorhinal volume was inversely related to ETI (β = −0.00177, 95% CI −0.00293 to −0.00061, p = 0.0028; Table 2). Diagnosis was a strong independent predictor, while age, sex, and education were not significant.

Mean entorhinal cortical thickness showed a similar association with ETI (β = −2.24, 95% CI −3.45 to −1.04, p = 2.6×10⁻⁴). Entorhinal surface area was not associated with ETI and was not examined further.

APOE Genotype and ETI

In the full ETI-scored cohort with available APOE data (n = 90,609), neither APOE ε4 carrier status (β = 0.17, 95% CI −0.04 to 0.38, p = 0.12) nor ε4 allele dose (β = −0.02, 95% CI −0.13 to 0.10, p = 0.79) was associated with ETI after adjustment for age, sex, education, and diagnosis (Table 3). APOE ε2 carrier status also showed no robust association after diagnostic adjustment.

APOE Moderation of ETI–Entorhinal Structure Associations

Moderation analyses were conducted in MRI-aligned participants with APOE data (n = 350). For entorhinal volume, the main effect on ETI remained significant (β = −0.00153, 95% CI −0.00264 to −0.00042, p = 0.0068), while APOE ε4 dose and the volume × ε4 interaction were not significant (interaction β = −0.00169, 95% CI −0.00593 to 0.00255, p = 0.435; Table 4).

Similarly, entorhinal thickness was inversely associated with ETI (β = −2.69, 95% CI −4.15 to −1.24, p = 2.8×10⁻⁴), with no evidence of moderation by APOE ε4 dose (interaction β = −0.57, 95% CI −4.10 to 2.96, p = 0.753). Results were unchanged when analyses were restricted to CN and MCI participants.

ETI and Risk of Clinical Conversion

In Cox proportional hazards models, ETI did not predict conversion from CN to MCI or dementia (n = 8,586; HR = 1.01, 95% CI 0.995–1.022, p = 0.22; Table 5). Older age increased conversion risk, while higher education and female sex were protective.

Among participants with MCI at baseline (n = 3,534), higher ETI was strongly associated with increased risk of conversion to dementia (HR = 1.03, 95% CI 1.02–1.03, p = 8.4×10⁻¹²). Kaplan–Meier analyses stratified by ETI tertiles showed progressively reduced dementia-free survival with increasing ETI (Figure 1).

Conclusion

This study introduces the Entorhinal Topology Index (ETI) as a robust, novel metric for quantifying cross-domain cognitive disorganization and its relationship to structural changes in the brain, specifically the entorhinal cortex (EC). Using a large sample from the NACC UDS dataset, we demonstrated that ETI scores were significantly associated with reductions in entorhinal cortex gray matter volume and cortical thickness, independent of diagnostic category. These findings are consistent with prior evidence identifying the entorhinal cortex as a critical locus of early Alzheimer’s disease–related pathology and cognitive disruption¹–³.

A key contribution of this work is the observation that ETI, unlike traditional cognitive scores, integrates multiple cognitive domains into a single, coherent index of cognitive dysfunction. This multidimensional approach aligns with evidence that Alzheimer’s disease affects distributed memory networks rather than isolated cognitive functions¹, and complements detailed structural mapping studies demonstrating selective vulnerability of medial temporal lobe subregions, including the entorhinal cortex²,³.

Although APOE genotype is a well-established genetic risk factor for Alzheimer’s disease, ETI showed no independent association with APOE genotype and did not moderate the relationship between entorhinal degeneration and cognitive disorganization. This dissociation is consistent with longitudinal work indicating that cognitive and neurodegenerative progression cannot be fully explained by genetic risk alone once disease stage is considered⁴.

Importantly, ETI predicted clinical progression from mild cognitive impairment (MCI) to dementia. Higher ETI scores were strongly associated with increased risk of conversion to dementia among individuals with MCI, supporting prior evidence that multivariate cognitive and structural markers are informative for disease progression. ⁴,⁵

In summary, ETI represents a meaningful advance in linking cognitive disorganization to entorhinal cortical degeneration and clinical progression in Alzheimer’s disease. By integrating multiple cognitive domains into a topology-based metric, ETI complements existing neuroimaging and neuropsychological approaches and provides a framework for studying cognitive–brain relationships beyond traditional summary scores. Future work will focus on longitudinal refinement, multimodal extension, and validation across independent cohorts.
