Introduction

Cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) is characterized by the deposition of amyloid-β (Aβ) in cerebrovascular walls, leading to intracerebral hemorrhage and cognitive impairment. While most cases are sporadic and age-related, rare forms exist, including familial CAA caused by genetic mutations (e.g., APP) and iatrogenic CAA (iCAA) associated with neurosurgical procedures. Notably, patients who received cadaveric dura mater grafts (Lyodura) in childhood have been reported to develop iCAA after a latency period of several decades.1 This phenomenon strongly suggests that Aβ seeds contained in the graft may have propagated and aggregated within the brain via a prion-like mechanism, making iCAA an extremely important model for understanding the transmission of pathological proteins in the human brain. However, the role of tau protein—another major component of Alzheimer's pathology—in the pathogenesis of iCAA remains largely unclear. Although recent post-mortem studies have reported the coexistence of tau pathology in iCAA patients,2 there are no reports visualizing its spatial distribution in the living brain or elucidating its localization relationship with Aβ. While tau protein is also posited to possess prion-like transmissibility, it remains unknown whether it exhibits a widespread distribution similar to Aβ or a distinct behavior in iCAA. The aim of this study was to elucidate the distinct propagation mechanisms of these two proteins by capturing the accumulation patterns of amyloid and tau pathology relative to the dural graft site using in vivo imaging and revealing their "spatial dissociation." To this end, we performed multi-tracer PET imaging targeting amyloid and tau in a rare case of iCAA who underwent dural grafting in childhood and developed intracerebral hemorrhage at a young age, and analyzed the spatial distribution of both proteins in detail.

Methods

Case and Clinical Evaluation: The patient was a 39-year-old male. He had a history of partial cyst wall resection and dural grafting using Lyodura for a left temporal arachnoid cyst at the age of 1. In September 2022, 38 years after the transplant, he was presented with transient numbness of the right leg, dysarthria, and aphasia. Head CT revealed a subcortical hemorrhage (approx. 2 cm) in the left parietal lobe. Long-term video EEG monitoring confirmed epileptiform discharges with phase reversal in the left temporoparietal region, leading to a diagnosis of symptomatic epilepsy focal to the hemorrhage. Furthermore, 3T MRI Susceptibility Weighted Imaging (SWI) revealed multiple cortical/subcortical microbleeds and cortical superficial siderosis predominantly in the left hemisphere, fulfilling the Modified Boston criteria v2.0 for probable CAA. Screening for secondary causes such as systemic amyloidosis and collagen diseases was negative. Genetic analysis (Sanger sequencing) confirmed the APOE ε3/ε3 genotype and identified no pathogenic mutations associated with familial Alzheimer's disease or hereditary CAA (e.g., APP, PSEN1, PSEN2), ruling out a genetic predisposition.

Image Acquisition: Structural images (CT and 3T MRI: T1, T2*, SWI, FLAIR) were acquired to evaluate hemorrhagic lesions and anatomy. PET imaging was performed in December 2022 and January 2023, approximately 3–4 months after the onset of hemorrhage. Amyloid imaging was performed using [11C]PiB (~500 MBq, 50–70 min post-injection), and tau imaging was performed using Florzolotau (18F-PM-PBB3) (~185 MBq, 90–110 min post-injection). PET/CT images were acquired using a Discovery ST Elite PET/CT system (GE Healthcare). After low-dose CT for attenuation correction, emission data were collected in 3D mode and reconstructed using the OSEM (Ordered Subset Expectation Maximization) algorithm.

Image Analysis: Image analysis was performed using PMOD software (Version 4.4, PMOD Technologies LLC, Zurich, Switzerland). As preprocessing, PET images for each tracer were coregistered to the individual's high-resolution 3D T1-weighted MRI using a rigid matching algorithm. For quantification, Standardized Uptake Value Ratio (SUVR) images were generated using the cerebellar cortex as the reference region. Region of Interest (ROI) analysis was performed using the AAL (Automated Anatomical Labeling) atlas to calculate mean SUVR values in each gyrus of the ipsilateral (left) and contralateral (right) hemispheres. These values were used to quantitatively evaluate the difference in accumulation between the left and right sides and the peak locations to analyze the spatial distribution characteristics of amyloid and tau pathology.

Results

Spatial Contrast between MRI and PET: SWI revealed widespread microbleeds and cortical superficial siderosis in the cerebral hemispheres, in addition to the subcortical hemorrhage scar in the left parietal lobe (Fig. 1A). While these hemorrhagic lesions were bilateral, they were distributed slightly more predominantly on the grafted (left) side.

Amyloid PET Findings ([11C]PiB): [11C]PiB PET images showed that amyloid-β accumulation was localized to the "right" parietal and temporal cortices, contralateral to the dural graft site (left) (Fig. 1B). While sporadic CAA and Alzheimer's disease typically present with bilateral diffuse accumulation,3 this case exhibited a highly specific distribution pattern with markedly dominant accumulation in the contralateral hemisphere, most distant from the graft site. Quantitative evaluation using VOI analysis also showed maximum accumulation in the right parietal lobe, consistent with visual assessment.

Tau PET Findings (Florzolotau): In contrast, Florzolotau PET images revealed extremely intense tracer accumulation in the "left" temporal and parietal cortices, extending continuously from the dural graft site (Fig. 1C). This region of tau accumulation was spatially strongly correlated with the vicinity of the Lyodura graft and the predilection sites for microbleeds on MRI. Quantitatively, the peak accumulation was observed in the left temporal lobe, whereas tau accumulation in the right hemisphere, where amyloid accumulation was prominent, was minimal. Thus, the distributions of the two proteins were completely reversed.

Spatial Dissociation of Pathological Proteins: Multi-tracer PET analysis revealed a clear "spatial dissociation" between amyloid-β and tau protein within the same case. Specifically, amyloid was localized to the contralateral side distant from the graft, while tau was localized to the ipsilateral side near the graft. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis showed a marked decrease in the Aβ42/40 ratio (0.047), while phosphorylated tau (p-tau) and total tau (t-tau) levels were within normal ranges. This supports the finding that the tau pathology captured by PET remained a localized accumulation.

Conclusion

This study is the first report to visualize the spatial distribution of amyloid-β and tau pathology in the living brain using multi-tracer PET in a case of iCAA related to childhood Lyodura grafting. The remarkable "spatial dissociation"—contralateral amyloid accumulation versus ipsilateral tau accumulation—strongly suggests that the two proteins possess fundamentally different propagation mechanisms in the human brain.

The phenomenon of amyloid-β accumulating predominantly in the contralateral hemisphere distant from the graft site indicates that graft-derived Aβ seeds were widely disseminated via the bulk flow of blood, brain interstitial fluid (ISF), or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF).4 This supports the hypothesis that Aβ can diffuse throughout the brain via intravascular or perivascular fluid-mediated mechanisms.

In contrast, the fact that tau protein accumulated at high concentrations limited to the gyri adjacent to the grafted dura implies that tau seeds were not diffused by fluids but were "directly seeded" from the graft into the adjacent brain parenchyma.5 This distribution is clearly distinct from the "Braak staging" dependent on neural connectivity seen in typical Alzheimer's disease, and provides powerful evidence that tau aggregates propagated directly between neurons in a prion-like manner based on physical proximity.

This case serves as a crucial in vivo model supporting the protein propagation hypothesis (prion-like hypothesis) in the human brain. Clinically, it demonstrates that iCAA is not merely an amyloid angiopathy but a complex pathology that can be accompanied by tauopathy originating from the graft site. Therefore, in the long-term monitoring of patients with a history of neurosurgery, multimodal evaluation using both tau PET and amyloid PET is essential. In the future, these findings may contribute to the development of pathology-specific disease-modifying therapies targeting these distinct propagation pathways (fluid diffusion vs. direct propagation).
