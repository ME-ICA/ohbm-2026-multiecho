Introduction

Pain and touch have classically been identified  as independent somatosensory modalities. They are mediated by different types of receptors with different densities and receptive field sizes and characterised by distinct ascending pathways which project in part to different cortical regions.1 This anatomical segregation is reflected by marked perceptual differences, such as the opposite gradients of spatial acuity observed for tactile and nociceptive stimuli.2 However, recent evidence has suggested greater degree of cortical-level integration between the sensory-discriminative aspects of touch and pain.3 This possibility raises the question of whether the mechanisms of spatial attention engaged during the processing of tactile and nociceptive stimuli are mediated by distinct or shared neural circuits. Top-down cognitive functions such as spatial attention are known to play a key role in shaping the neural and behavioural responses to painful and tactile stimuli. However, so far these processes have largely been examined independently  in separate studies. 

Therefore, the aim of the present fMRI study was to directly compare the effects of spatial attention orientation across the two sensory modalities, in order to identify the mechanisms and to map the neural substrates that support anticipatory attention to tactile and nociceptive stimuli  delivered to different spatial locations (right or left hand).

Methods

Thirteen right-handed, healthy, female participants (mean age 25 yrs ± 1.7 S.D) took part in the study. During the fMRI acquisition, participants performed a cuing-like task, in which a cue presented at the beginning of each trial indicated the sensory modality (capital letter P or T to indicate pain or touch), and the location (two identical arrows - either < or > - presented to both sides of the letter) of the task-relevant target for that trial (Figure 1). The cue was shown on the screen for 200 ms. After 800 ms, the actual stimulation was applied for 300 ms on the dorsum of one hand. Nociceptive and tactile stimulations were delivered through a MRI-compatible, pneumatic stimulator developed in our laboratory, which uses medical air to activate cylinders made of MR-compatible brass. Six cylinders for each hand were employed, three of them delivered tactile stimulations and the other three delivered painful stimulations. Participants were instructed to covertly attend to the task-relevant sensory modality (pain or touch) and location (left or right hand) to judge whether the target delivered on that trial matched the features indicated by the cue. The stimulation could be fully congruent with the cue, fully incongruent with the cue, congruent with the cue in terms of sensory modality but not spatial location, or congruent with the cue in terms of spatial location but not sensory modality. This design resulted in a total of 16 experimental conditions (sensory modality × spatial location × cue congruency). Each condition was presented across 20 trials yielding 320 trials overall, divided in 4 fMRI runs. On a minority of trials participants were asked to make the congruency judgment between the cue and the actual stimulation explicit by providing a vocal response upon presentation of the word “Respond” on the screen. Participants verbally indicated whether the latest stimulation received was fully congruent to the previous cue (“Yes” or “No”) with responses recorded via an MRI compatible microphone. 

We used an event related fMRI protocol divided in four runs and functional volumes were acquired on a 3T GE Signa Architect system. Each run comprised 342 volumes of 46 consecutive 3-mm-thick slices (TR= 1500ms, TE= 30ms, voxel size= 2x2x3 mm, flip angle= 70). The SPM25 software was used for preprocessing and data analysis. The general linear model approach was applied at the single-subject level and the group-level random effects analyses were performed by entering the individual contrast images corresponding to the effects of interest into separate one-sample t-tests.  A double statistical threshold (single-voxel statistics and spatial extent) was used to achieve a combined (i.e., corrected for multiple comparisons) significance level of α < 0.05, as computed by the 3dClustSim AFNI routine with the “-acf ” option (AFNI version 24.2.06, https://afni.nimh.nih.gov/pub/dist/doc/program_help/3dClustSim.html). 

Results

The contrast left hand stimulation (fully congruent to the cue vs. fully incongruent) compared to right hand stimulation (fully congruent to the cue vs. fully incongruent) show increased BOLD signal in the left cerebellum (peak coordinates in MNI space X= -10; Y= -64; Z= -22, k= 138, α < 0.05 k < 111, Figure 2). Activations below the threshold level were detected in the right Superior Parietal Lobule (k= 76, p < 0.001 k > 96.3) in the contrast right hand stimulation fully incongruent to the cue vs. fully congruent. 

Conclusion

Our findings are consistent  with current evidence indicating that the cerebellum plays a key role in attentional orienting through predictive coding mechanisms and modulation of frontoparietal networks. Previous studies showed that the left cerebellum contributes to early stages of attentional processing, modulates cortical activation and inhibition in response to anticipated stimuli,4 and integrates spatial and temporal cues.5 Consistently, in our somatosensory cuing-like task we observed cerebellar involvement in facilitating attention toward the left hand when stimulation was preceded by an informative cue. These results support the hypothesis that the left cerebellum contributes to perceptual preparation and enhances the processing of anticipated somatosensory input, reinforcing its role in multimodal attentional orienting. Moreover, our preliminary results  suggest that the processing of tactile and painful stimuli share neural substrates mediating the anticipatory spatial attention effects. A larger sample and further analyses will enable us to verify the present results and to further investigate the lateralization of the attentional effects observed below threshold.
