Introduction

Current models of language neuroimaging largely converge on a dual-stream framework that dissociates a dorsal stream dedicated to mapping sound to articulation (phonological processing) from a ventral stream responsible for mapping sound to meaning (semantic processing)1. These functional networks are underpinned by specific white matter pathways. The dorsal stream would involve the Arcuate Fasciculus (AF)/ Superior Longitudinal Fasciculus (SLF), and the frontal Aslant tract while the semantic ventral stream would rely on the Inferior Fronto-Occipital Fascicle (IFOF), the Inferior and Middle Longitudinal Fascicules (ILF & MLF), and the Uncinate Fascicule (UF)2. Crucially, this current knowledge of white matter function relies predominantly on studies evaluating the impact of tract disruption on language abilities in patients3, with a focus on cortico-cortical association fibers, leaving the precise role of commissural and subcortico-cortical projection fibers less explored. The present study aims to further explore the contribution of the entire white matter connectome—including association, commissural, and projection fibers—to phonological and semantic processes in healthy participants. We employ the "Functionnectome" approach4, an innovative method designed to project the functional activity (BOLD signal) recorded during specific language tasks directly onto the white matter pathways connecting the involved brain regions.

Methods

Healthy participants (N=295; 147 female; mean age = 25.59 ± 6.27 years) underwent a slow event-related functional run (364s total) designed to isolate specific language processes5. Participants completed two tasks: Semantic (SEM), deciding if two visually presented words were man-made objects, and Phonological (PHONO), deciding if two visually presented pseudowords ended with the same sound. Stimuli were presented for 3s (15 pairs/task) alternating with 9s inter-trial fixation.

Functional volumes acquired on a 3T scanner (T2*-weighted EPI, TR=2s) were preprocessed (slice timing, motion correction, anatomical registration, MNI normalization) using SPM12. In addition to a classical BOLD analysis (Fig 1, first row), we then applied the Functionnectome analysis, computing three additional maps by projecting the BOLD signal onto anatomical priors for association, commissural, and projection fibers (Fig. 1, rows 2-4).

For both the BOLD and Functionnectome maps, individual first-level contrasts (SEM>PHONO and PHONO>SEM) were computed and subsequently entered into second-level group analyses to compute activation maps for both contrasts, applying a Family-Wise Error (FWE) corrected significance threshold of p=0.05.  A custom combination of the Rojkova6 and XTRACT7 probabilistic atlases were used to locate tract activations.

Results

Joint BOLD and Functionnectome analyses successfully uncovered the specific brain regions and associated-white matter tracts related to phonological and semantic judgments on visual verbal stimuli (Fig. 1). 

PHONO>SEM activations were observed in the left Supplementary Motor Area and bilateral regions including the occipito-temporal junctions, intraparietal sulci, and superior frontal sulci extending into the precentral gyri. The dorsal phonological stream demonstrated important reliance on the left SLF III and the Right SLF I and SLF II, alongside striato-parietal and thalamo-precentral projection fibers, supported by commissural fibers linking the precentral gyri activations. 

In contrast, SEM > PHONO activations engaged a widespread bilateral network, comprising brain regions classically associated to semantics, such as the angular/posterior middle temporal gyri, the anterior superior temporal sulcus, and the anterior inferior front gyrus. Ventral semantic stream showed extensive involvement of the entire language connectome, encompassing almost all major ventral association tracts (including the bilateral ILF, MLF and IFOF) but also tracts that have been described as belonging to the dorsal pathway such as AF/SLF. A great involvement in the anterior and posterior commissural fibers was observed with a widespread recruitment of projection fibers. These results indicate that semantic processing relies on a highly integrated and expansive subcortical-cortical network

Crucially, specific sub-segments of white matter tracts were differentially recruited by both tasks. For instance, a more lateral portion of the left SLF III was more involved in PHONO (see Fig. 1, A2) while a more medial aspect of the same tract projecting to the orbital inferior frontal gyrus was more activated during SEM (Fig. 1. B2). 

Conclusion

All in all, this study demonstrates that applying a functional approach to examine white matter tract contribution to language provides valuable refinement, deepening our understanding of the precise neural underpinnings and organization of the language connectome. The results suggest that the dorsal phonological pathway relies on a more restricted set of white matter tracts than what is observed for the ventral pathway. Interestingly, the tracts that were activated by semantics also included fibers (AF/SLF) thought to belong to the dorsal pathway. This last result lends support to the proposal that the long segment of the AF (from the temporal pole to the inferior frontal gyrus) plays an important role in lexical-semantic retrieval8.
