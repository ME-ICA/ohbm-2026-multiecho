Introduction

Iron plays a fundamental role in brain physiology, cellular metabolism and myelination. However, excessive iron can facilitate oxidative damage and is associated with neurological diseases^1. Across the adult lifespan, cortical iron is known to accumulate slowly, with estimated time constants of 11–20 years^2. Iron is also distributed non-uniformly across the cortex, and displays a characteristic laminar pattern. The superficial white matter (SWM), the thin myelinated layer beneath the cortical sheet, shows higher iron concentrations than both the deep white matter (DWM) and the cortex^3. SWM contains short cortico-cortical fibers, and its elevated iron levels may reflect high local fiber density or late-myelinating pathways with greater metabolic demand. However, it remains unknown whether age-related iron accumulation within the cortex varies by depth, and whether higher iron content is related to faster accumulation over time.

Furthermore, sex-related differences in cortical iron content and iron accumulation are largely unexplored^1. Women show lower peripheral iron levels during reproductive years, largely due to menstrual blood loss and iron transfer during pregnancy, and are more vulnerable to iron deficiency. After menopause, iron stores are expected to rise, yet evidence for parallel changes in brain iron is sparse. It is unknown whether females exhibit distinct cortical iron accumulation trajectories, including differences in accumulation rates or in late-life plateau levels.

Paramagnetic susceptibility (χpara) provides a non-invasive quantitative MRI (qMRI) marker of iron content in brain tissue^4. Unlike conventional qMRI measures, it is less affected by myelin, providing a more iron-specific marker. Here, we leverage this measurement to characterize laminar- and sex-specific aging trajectories of iron. We estimate time constants of age-related increases in χpara for DWM, SWM, and cortex, and assess sex-related differences in these accumulation patterns.

Methods

Participants:
We analyzed data from 140 healthy adults (90 female) aged 20–80 years. All procedures were approved by the Colorado State University Institutional Review Board. Exclusion criteria were designed to generate a neurologically, psychiatrically, and cognitively healthy sample^5.

MRI acquisition and processing:
MRI data were acquired on a 3T Siemens MAGNETOM Skyra system with a 64-channel RF head coil. Multi-echo 3D GRE data (1 mm isotropic resolution, TR = 34 ms, 6 echo times spanning 4–29.55 ms, flip angle = 14°) were collected for estimating R2* and quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM). QSM reconstruction was performed using QSMnet, while R2PRIMEnet was used to derive R2′ from R2*. To decomposes the total susceptibility signal into its paramagnetic (χpara) and diamagnetic (χdia) components, we employed chi-sepnet^4. The default relaxometric constants were used. Importantly, χpara isolates the positive, iron-related susceptibility contribution from the mixed signal obtained with standard QSM, offering a more iron-specific measurement compared to conventional approaches^4 (Fig. 1AB).

Structural T1-weighted scans were acquired using an MPRAGE sequence (0.9 mm3 resolution). These scans were processed with FreeSurfer for cortical parcellation and generation of white matter and pial surfaces, which were then rigidly registered to the GRE space. Cortical depth sampling was performed using FreeSurfer’s mri_surf2vol function, which projects surface coordinates into volumetric space. Depth profiles were computed across the cortical ribbon as well as within adjacent white matter (Fig. 1C).

Modeling iron accumulation:
Age-related trajectories of χpara were modeled using an exponential growth function:

                                                            χpara = a ∗ (1 - eage/τ)

This model captures rapid early increases in iron-related susceptibility that gradually plateaus with age, consistent with known biological dynamics^2. The parameter a represents the asymptotic χpara level at advanced age, whereas the parameter τ denotes the characteristic time constant describing how rapidly the curve approaches this plateau. Larger values of the time constant τ correspond to slower accumulation rates. Notably, here we assume zero χpara values as birth. Although this is a simplification, it was necessary to stabilize the fit given that our sample only includes individuals aged 20 years and older.

Model fitting was performed using a Bayesian nonlinear regression framework, estimating the posterior distributions of the model parameters. Priors were defined for each parameter based on physiological constraints: τ was modeled with a truncated normal distribution (10 ± 25 years, constrained to 0–100), and a was assigned a uniform prior between 0 and 0.5. Markov Chain Monte Carlo sampling (PyMC) was used to obtain full posterior distributions and predictive intervals. 

We fitted the model separately across male and female participants for 34 left-hemisphere cortical regions. For each ROI and each sex, model fitting was carried out independently at four depth levels: deep white matter (DWM; 140% of cortical depth into WM), superficial white matter (SWM; 40% of cortical depth into WM), the cortical boundary between gray and white matter (0%), and the mid-cortical layer (40% of cortical depth).

Results

χpara increased from early adulthood to older age, reflecting progressive iron accumulation. Older participants exhibited higher mid-cortical χpara values than younger ones (Fig. 1B). These age-related differences were region-depandent, showing the strongest elevations in frontal and parietal association cortices. In contrast, early sensory regions showed comparatively smaller changes. Depth-wise sampling of χpara values along the white-matter–to–pial axis, revealed a characteristic iron laminar profile, with a prominent peak in the superficial white matter (Fig. 1C), in line with previous reports^3. Age effects were evident across the cortex, indicating strong age-related iron accumulation.

Fitting the exponential growth model showed that it captured the age-related trajectories well (Fig. 2A, median RMSE=11%). The model’s two parameters reflect the asymptotic χpara level at advanced age (a) and the time constant governing how rapidly this level is approached (τ). Figure 2B shows whole-brain averaged posterior distributions across the laminar axis, from DWM to the middle of the cortex. The plateau parameter (a) was higher in the superficial white matter and at the cortical boundary, exceeding mid-cortex and DWM values by 4.1 ppb on average (94.7% of posterior samples, across all ROIs). In contrast, the time constant (τ) was longest in the mid-cortex, indicating slower accumulation. Relative to mid-cortex, τ was shorter by 4.7 year at the cortical boundary (for 81% of posterior samples mid-cortex>cortical boundary, across all ROIs and depths tested), 6.3 years in SWM (84%), and 6.9 years in DWM (87%). Thus, while SWM regions reach higher asymptotic χpara levels, cortical mid-layers show a more prolonged accumulation trajectory.

We also observed sex-specific differences in the iron accumulation trajectory (Fig. 2B). Across all sampled depths, females exhibited lower plateau values than males, indicating reduced asymptotic χpara levels at advanced age. These differences were strongest in the white matter, with higher male plateau values in the DWM (0.9 ppb difference, 89% of posterior a samples males > females, across all ROIs tested) SWM (1.4 ppb, 93%), and cortical boundary (1.1 ppb, 89%), and were present but weaker in the mid-cortex (0.6 ppb, 71%). The accumulation time constant also differed by sex. It was systematically longer in males across regions and depths, corresponding to a slower approach to the plateau. On average, time constants were 4.3 years longer in males than in females (for 82% of posterior τ samples males > females, across all ROIs and depths tested). To test the robustness of these sex differences, we ran a permutation test shuffling sex labels. Under this null distribution, no systematic differences emerged: 50.5% of posterior τ and 53.8% of posterior plateau samples were higher in one permuted group, consistent with chance. Together, these results indicate that females show lower plateau iron levels and a faster accumulation trajectory.

Figure 2C presents the spatial distribution of the model parameters across the white-matter–to-cortex axis. The plateau value (a) showed a strong regional heterogeneity. Within white matter, the largest plateau values were concentrated in association cortices, whereas in the cortex, the highest values appeared in sensorimotor areas. The time constant (τ) also showed large regional variability. In both the deep and superficial white matter, time constants were low and relatively homogeneous. The strongest spatial variation was observed in the mid-cortex, where frontal regions exhibited the highest time constants, reflecting a slower increase in iron-related susceptibility. Overall, figure 2 illustrates that both the magnitude and the rate of age-related increases in χpara are strongly dependent on cortical depth and anatomical region.

Conclusion

Here, we delineate how iron-related susceptibility changes with age across depth, region, and sex, providing a detailed in-vivo description of iron accumulation during adulthood and aging.

First, we replicated the characteristic depth-dependent profile of iron observed in postmortem work^3, in vivo using χpara as a quantitative iron marker. Previous cortical profiling works demonstrated that QSM and R2* showed biases in reporting cortical iron due to the presence of myelin. Our findings confirm that χpara peaks at the SWM, in line with prior evidence of high SWM iron levels. Importantly, we extend these observations by showing that  χpara laminar profiles are strongly age-dependent. Age effects were most pronounced in the mid-cortical layer, indicating that iron accumulates differently along the laminar axis.

We further show that age-related increases in χpara are spatially heterogeneous across cortical regions. These spatial variations in iron accumulation are well described by an exponential growth model, previously proposed for postmortem iron content^2. Our estimated time constants closely align with those earlier reports, with values in the range of 11–20 years in the cortex, and shorter time constants in white matter compared to the cortex.

A key contribution of the present study is the characterization of iron accumulation dynamics in vivo, across a large cohort, multiple cortical regions, and distinct laminar depths. This was made possible by utilizing χpara, a novel susceptibility-based metric that offers greater iron specificity than conventional quantitative MRI contrasts^4. As a result, we could build upon ex-vivo literature and reveal depth-dependent patterns of cortical iron. First, we found that the elevated iron concentration in SWM corresponds to higher plateau values compared to both the mid-cortex and deep white matter. Despite these higher asymptotic levels, SWM iron appears to accumulate more rapidly, whereas the iron content in the mid-cortex plateaus at lower values but shows a more gradual trajectory toward them. We also identify pronounced sex-specific differences in iron accumulation trajectories. Females exhibited lower plateau values, particularly in white matter, and shorter time constants, indicating a faster approach toward a lower asymptotic value. While sex differences in peripheral iron regulation—particularly related to reproductive physiology—have been observed before, evidence for comparable effects in the brain has been limited^1. Our findings suggest potential differences in brain iron homeostasis between males and females, though the biological basis and implications require further investigation. 

Finally, we observe distinct spatial patterns in the model parameters, reflecting region- and depth-dependent iron accumulation processes. In SWM, time constants were short and relatively homogeneous, whereas plateau values varied considerably across regions. In the cortex, frontal areas exhibited slower accumulation, while primary areas showed higher plateau values. Interestingly, sensorimotor regions exhibiting high early-life iron showed correspondingly high plateau values, but slower time constant.

In conclusion, here we exploited the novel susceptibility source separation parameter, χpara, to demonstrate that iron accumulation dynamics are strongly dependent on cortical depth, region, and sex. These divergent dynamics may reflect distinct functional roles of iron and may imply different vulnerabilities to iron-related tissue alterations. These results lay the groundwork for future studies linking iron dynamics to cognitive function, neurodegenerative vulnerability, and lifespan brain health.
