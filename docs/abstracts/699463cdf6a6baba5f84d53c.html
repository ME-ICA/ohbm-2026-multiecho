Introduction

The hierarchical organization of the frontal lobe supports cognitive control, decision-making, and action planning, with the cingulate cortex (CC) contributing to its functional modulation. In particular, the critical anatomical and functional position of the CC as an interface between the limbic, executive, and sensory systems enables it to regulate the emotional and motivational aspects of these functions.

Neuroanatomical studies reveal a clear structural and neurochemical distinction along the rostro-caudal extent of the cingulate cortex1, and functional studies highlight that these diverse parts of CC are differently involved in major resting-state networks: the posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) is a node of the default mode network (DMN) while the anterior cingulate(ACC) and anterior mid-cingulate (aMCC) cortex are part of the salience network (SN)2. 

Despite the well-established functional specialization of the frontal lobe, the molecular principles through which cingulate-driven affective signals shape the organization of large-scale frontal networks remains largely unresolved. Specifically, it is unknown whether the receptor architecture of the cingulate cortex merely reflects its functional roles, or whether it independently contributes to the segregation of the frontal lobe. 

We quantitatively integrate high-resolution receptor density mapping with intrinsic functional connectivity (FC) analyses of the macaque CC to test our hypothesis that the distinctive neurochemical architecture of the CC underpins the organizational principles of the cingulo-frontal system.

Methods

We characterized the receptor architecture of the cingulate cortex in the three macaque brains we had previously used for the analysis of the prefrontal (PFC) and motor cortices3,4. Brains were frozen, sectioned in the coronal plane (20µm) and processed to visualize cell bodies and 14 receptor types for glutamate, GABA, acetylcholine, noradrenalin, serotonin and dopamine. As for the PFC and motor areas, cingulate areas were identified in the histologically processed sections, and color-coded autoradiographs, with their borders validated by a statistically testable method. Mean receptor densities were extracted from the 28 identified cingulate areas, together with the 33 PFC and 16 motor areas, receptor-wise standardized and displayed as a heatmap (Fig. 1A). Similarities between cingulate, premotor and frontal receptor fingerprints were assessed using hierarchical and k-means clustering.

Each of the identified areas was then drawn on the MEBRAINS template to create a parcellated 3D volume enabling a seed-based analysis with publicly available resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) datasets. Briefly, data were processed with the HCP-style NHP pipeline, and connectivity was computed by correlating (Pearson coefficient) the residual activity of each of the 28 cingulate seed areas and the frontal and premotor areas. Corelation coefficients were then transformed to z-scores using Fisher-z-transformation5, which corresponds to FC. 

Results

Hierarchical and k-means clustering revealed five neurochemically distinct clusters within the cingulo-frontal system, which are visualized on the 3D-volume based atlas (Fig. 1A). The first branching separated somatomotor-related areas – cluster (C) 5 and C4 –, from higher-order associative areas – C3-C1 (in brown, green, red, purple and blue, respectively; Fig. 1A). Three clusters displayed particularly distinctive receptor fingerprints, each containing areas from a single cortical region: PFC (C1), anterior cingulate (C3) and primary motor (C5) areas. Indeed, areas of C1 presented relatively higher GABAA and M3, but lower AMPA receptor densities than those in the remaining clusters. Areas of C3 expressed overall the highest receptor densities, whereas the opposite was true for areas of C5. 

FC analyses (Fig. 1B) revealed a clear differentiation within C3: 1) ventral ACC areas (e.g. areas 25p) presented a more localized connectivity with surrounding areas; 2) more dorsally located ACC areas (e.g. area 24b) showed a broader FC pattern across all clusters, but strongest correlation was with mediodorsal and lateral PFC areas; and 3) aMCC areas (e.g. area a24b’) most strongly correlated with medial and dorsal (pre)motor areas, as well as to medial PFC areas 9m and 8Bm. 

The two remaining clusters, C4 and C2, combined both cingulate and frontal areas. C4 included areas with relatively low receptor densities, mostly premotor areas, but also areas 24dd and 23cd, which correspond to the caudal cingulate motor area, and area p24a’. Interestingly, whereas 24dd and 23cd clustered with the ventral subdivisions of premotor area F4, their correlation strength was stronger with areas of the dorsal than of the ventral premotor cortex (correlation strengths higher than 0.5; Fig. 1B). C2 contains all areas of the PCC, which, together with ventro-lateral PFC areas, showed intermediate to low receptor densities. Despite a generally consistent FC pattern across most PCC areas, the subdivisions of d23 and v23 display a more specialized profile, characterized by their strongest correlations with the dorso-lateral PFC and dorsal premotor cortices (Fig. 1B).

Conclusion

We demonstrated that, based on differences in their receptor fingerprints, areas of the cingulo-frontal system can be divided into five clusters, each encompassing core elements of established functional networks (e.g. default mode and salience). Thus, receptor fingerprints define the local neurochemical identity and computational capacity of functional network hubs. However, the observed dissociations between neurochemical and FC patterns (e.g. for PCC areas) indicate that FC can extend beyond local neurochemical constraints during broader, more global integration, thereby highlighting a mechanism for functional flexibility. 

Our receptor-based parcellation provides a high-resolution blueprint for understanding how local neurochemistry contributes to, but can be modulated within, the large-scale functional organization principle of the brain. Importantly, by linking receptor architecture to FC in the cingulo-frontal system, this study provides a framework to understand how affective and motivational signals from the CC influence cognitive and motor functions.
