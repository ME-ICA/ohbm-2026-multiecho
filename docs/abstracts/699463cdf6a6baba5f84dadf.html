Introduction

Obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD) is increasingly conceptualized as a disorder of brain network dynamics1, wherein the coordination and flow of information across brain regions may be fundamentally altered. Entropy, an information theoretic construct, can be used to capture information in fMRI time series data, and is shown to be modulated by task predictability2 . However, no studies have attempted to capture the dynamics of information entropy, that is, how information embedded within fMRI time series dynamically changes over a task.

Here, we introduce a novel analytical framework to capture task-evoked information dynamics by computing time-resolved entropy measures across the brain during a motor control task. We hypothesized that youth with OCD would exhibit altered coherence in cross-cerebral information dynamics compared to healthy controls, and that these dynamics would show diminished responsiveness to task structure. By examining how information complexity evolves temporally within and across brain regions, this approach provides unique insights into disruption of brain networks in OCD.

Methods

We acquired fMRI data (Siemens 3T Verio) from youth with OCD (n=37) and age-matched healthy controls (n=47, HC) during a modified Go/No-Go motor task. Participants either responded to all presented visual stimuli (red or green circles) or selectively responded only to green targets. The task alternated between active response blocks and rest epochs. We computed the information dynamics within time series data from each of 246 cerebral parcels3. Each window (width=96s) spanned two active task epochs and two rest epochs, advancing to create 97 consecutive, overlapping windows across the full task duration. Within each window, we calculated Sample Entropy (SampEn), which quantifies information complexity with lower values indicating more regular, predictable patterns while higher values reflecting greater irregularity and information content4. This yielded a time series of Sample Entropy (tSE) for each region, capturing how information dynamics evolved across the task. We computed the average tSE in each group (HC, OCD) for each of the 246 nodes. 

To assess intergroup differences in the characteristics of information dynamics, we calculated the Sample Extropy and Coefficient of Variance (CV) of average tSE.Sample entropy of information dynamics quantifies the regularity in fluctuations of information dynamics across task demands: lower values indicate more periodic (predictable) changes in information, while higher values reflect more stochastic (irregular) fluctuations in information. Coefficient of variance indicates the range of information dynamics, with higher CV suggesting greater fluctuation or more adaptability in information over time. 

To assess the coherence of information dynamics across the brain, we computed pairwise correlations between all regional tSE time series within each group (HC, OCD), generating 246×246 similarity matrices (30,135 unique region pairs). Higher correlation values indicate greater synchrony in how information dynamics evolved across regions.

We applied agglomerative hierarchical clustering (AHC) using Ward's method to identify clusters of regions exhibiting similar information dynamics within each group. We used AHC (Ward method) because it is robust in generating solutions independent of cluster size, it is effective in clustering high-dimensional data, and  it uses a simple similarity metric (coefficients) as a basis for clustering. In each clustering solution, we determined the optimal number of clusters through the convergence of a) the Within-Cluster Sum of Squares (elbow) plot and  the threshold versus number of clusters plot (ensuring stable cluster sizes). Clusters were numbered in descending order of their size (HCC1 – HCCn; OCDC1 – OCDCm).   

For each cluster, we computed an average cluster time series (tClusterSE) representing the mean information dynamics across all regions in that cluster. We calculated two summary statistics:  Sample Entropy of tClusterSE (regularity of cluster dynamics), and  Coefficient of Variance (CV) of tClusterSE (magnitude of fluctuation).

Results

Inter-group Differences of Information Dynamic Sample Entropy and Coefficient of Variation (CV) 

On average, OCD exhibited significantly higher SampEn compared to HC. This increase indicates that the average tSE in OCD participants was less predictable and more irregular than in HC. Conversely, the CV of the average tSE in OCD was significantly lower than in HC. This effect suggests that information dynamics in OCD are less predictable but also more constrained (in terms of their variability), suggesting that the predictability of SampEn might be inversely related to fluctuations in amplitude. Across all 246 nodes in both groups, we observed a highly significant negative correlation (r = -0.842, p < 0.0001) between SampEn of the average tSE and the CV of the average tSE.

Reduced Cross-Cerebral Coherence of Information Dynamic in OCD 

Analysis of cross-cerebral similarity revealed that OCD exhibited significantly reduced similarity in information dynamics between brain regions compared to HC. Statistical comparisons of the similarity matrices demonstrated poorer coherence of information dynamics in OCD across the cerebrum. Mass univariate inter-group comparisons revealed widespread differences in cross-cerebral coherence (thresholded at pFDR<5x10⁻⁵). This widespread loss in coherence of information dynamics suggests fundamental disruption in coordinated information processing across the brain in OCD.

Distinct Subnetwork Organizations of Information Dynamics in OCD and HC Agglomerative hierarchical clustering identified distinct subnetwork organizations between groups. HC revealed 7 clusters of regions with homogeneous information dynamics, whereas OCD revealed only 6 clusters. The cluster solutions differed not only in number but also in their internal coherence. Intra-cluster correlations (quantified by within-cluster similarity) were stronger in HC than OCD, indicating that even within identified subnetworks, OCD exhibited reduced coherence. The hierarchical relationships between clusters, as reflected in dendrogram structures, demonstrated more distinct functional differentiation in HC compared to OCD.

Subnetwork Level Information Dynamic

At the subnetwork level, analysis of tClusterSE revealed a significant negative correlation (r = -0.751, p = 0.0031) between coefficient of variance and sample entropy across HC and OCD clusters. This relationship was observed across both groups, indicating that subnetworks with higher magnitude fluctuations showed more regular, predictable patterns of information dynamics.

Conclusion

These analyses allowed us to demonstrate altered dynamics of information flow in OCD, a clinically significant and novel demonstration. Several salient results were evident from this novel approach to capturing task-evoked information dynamics from fMRI data.

First, at the regional level, OCD exhibited significantly higher Sample Entropy but significantly lower Coefficient of Variation compared to HC. This paradoxical pattern suggests that information dynamics in OCD are less predictable (higher entropy) but also more constrained (lower CV). The highly significant negative correlation between SampEn and CV indicates that the predictability of information dynamics might be inversely related to fluctuations in amplitude. This suggests a fundamental alteration in how information is processed in OCD with regions showing more irregular, unpredictable dynamics but with a reduced capacity for adaptive modulation of the magnitude of these fluctuations. This constrained irregularity may reflect a rigid, inflexible pattern of information processing that cannot appropriately adjust to task demands.

Second, OCD exhibited significantly reduced similarity in information dynamics between brain regions, suggesting poorer coherence of information dynamics across the cerebrum. The widespread loss in the coherence of information dynamics demonstrates that OCD involves a fundamental disruption in how information processing is coordinated across the brain. This reduced coherence may underlie the cognitive inflexibility and difficulty integrating information that characterizes OCD.

Third, hierarchical clustering revealed distinct subnetwork organizations in HC and OCD. In HC, information dynamics are organized into seven functionally distinct subnetworks. While in OCD, this organization is less distinct both in number (only 6 clusters), suggesting that functional differentiation of information processing subnetworks is reduced.

Fourth, the negative correlation between coefficient of variance and sample entropy at the subnetwork level demonstrates coordinated information dynamics wherein the amplitude and regularity of information processing are coupled. This relationship, observed across both HC and OCD clusters, reveals a fundamental principle of information dynamics organization: subnetworks with larger fluctuations in information content show more regular, predictable temporal patterns. These complex analyses allowed us to demonstrate altered dynamics of information flow in OCD, a clinically significant and novel demonstration.
