Introduction

With increasing age there are reliable declines in many aspects of cognition, including learning and memory ability. An interest in preventing or mitigating decline has focused attention on the benefits of activity participation for cognitive health in older age. Results from these studies suggest that participation in daily life activities is associated with the successful preservation of cognitive function and resilience to Alzheimer’s disease. In parallel, recent work has refined our conceptualization of activity participation by highlighting the importance of considering daily life novelty and diversity; these metrics characterize an individual’s engagement across many different types of activities (i.e., activity diversity) and locations/contexts (i.e., experiential diversity) in daily life. Medial temporal lobe structures have shown associations with both activity and experiential diversity in younger and middle-aged adults. Nevertheless, there have been no studies to date that have examined these constructs within-person, or with neuroimaging indices in older adults, to rigorously investigate the specific aspects of daily life activity participation that support cognitive function in older adults.

Methods

Participants (N=32) ages 61-82 (mean = 70.82 ± 6.07) completed tasks indexing both learning and memory ability, a three-week daily life sampling protocol measuring both activity and experiential diversity, and a simultaneous PET-MRI scan that facilitated the measurement of gray matter volume, cortical thickness, and tau burden. In the laboratory, participants completed assessments that included the Face-Name Associative Memory Task1 and the California Verbal Learning Test2. To facilitate comparisons across multiple tasks and scales measuring learning and memory ability, we created composite scores indexing each construct. To measure activity diversity, the breadth and evenness of different daily life activities, participants completed short surveys delivered to their mobile phone four times per day over twenty-one days. Consistent with prior work, activity diversity was calculated using Shannon’s entropy3. Experiential diversity, the variability in an individual’s location over the course of a day, was measured using continuously sampled geolocation data from an app downloaded on participants’ phones over the course of twenty-one days. For each participant, experiential diversity was quantified as the roaming entropy of their physical locations for each day of sampling4. Average levels of both activity and experiential diversity were used in subsequent analyses. Scanning was performed on a Siemens 3T Biograph mMR, capable of simultaneous PET-MRI acquisition. A T1-weighed MPRAGE scan was acquired for each participant (TR = 2530 ms, TE =1.69 ms, Flip angle = 7 degrees, 192 slices, slice thickness = 1.0 mm, FOV = 256 × 256 mm). All MRI data used in these analyses wee preprocessed using fMRIPrep 22.0.2. Both the entorhinal cortex and hippocampus were used as regions of interest defined using the Desikan–Killiany atlas included as the default parcellation within FreeSurfer (entorhinal cortex region; Figure 1D). Gray matter volumes were adjusted for total intracranial volume (ICV) using a covariance approach5 and averaged across hemispheres as no a priori effects of hemisphere were expected. Cortical thickness was also averaged across hemisphere. For the [18F]MK-6240 tau PET scan, participants received approximately 5 mCi of tracer (5.28 ± 0.37) and were scanned 80–100 min post-injection (4 ´ 5-minute frames) during the same scanning session in which the MRI data were acquired. Standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) images for MK-6240 were created by normalizing mean tracer uptake to the inferior cerebellar gray matter reference region. Geometric transfer matrix partial volume correction (PVC) on FreeSurfer-derived regions of interest was applied to account for partial volume effects. Linear models were used to test for the contribution of activity and experiential diversity, gray matter volume, cortical thickness, and tau burden on learning and memory ability. All models included age, sex, and years of education as additional covariates. The effects are reported as the estimate from the model with 95% confidence intervals and the corresponding p-value. Johnson-Neyman intervals were used to interpret interaction effects.

Results

Neither activity nor experiential diversity were directly associated with memory ability (BActivity = -0.04 [-0.19, 0.10], p = 0.547; BExperiential = 0.03 [-0.11, 0.17], p = 0.681) or learning ability (BActivity = 0.02 [-0.12, 0.16], p = 0.787; BExperiential = 0.06 [-0.07, 0.20], p= 0.367) in our sample of older adults. Interestingly, cortical thickness in the entorhinal cortex moderated the association between activity diversity towards both memory ability (BActivity = 0.33 [0.06, 0.59], p = 0.017; Figure 1A) and learning ability (BActivity = 0.31 [0.04, 0.58], p = 0.023; Figure 1B); no such effects were observed for experiential diversity, ps > 0.208. In other words, participants who had high levels of both daily life activity diversity and entorhinal cortex thickness tended to have better memory and learning ability. The same pattern was not observed when using indices of gray matter volume in the entorhinal cortex or hippocampus, ps > 0.052. In contrast, we did observe an interaction between entorhinal tau burden and experiential diversity towards learning ability (BExperiential= -0.51 [-1.03, -0.01], p = 0.049; Figure 1C), but not between entorhinal tau burden and activity diversity (BActivity = -0.10 [-0.58, 0.38], p = 0.675). These data suggest that individuals with both low levels of entorhinal tau burden and high levels of daily life experiential diversity tended to have higher levels of learning ability. There were no significant associations observed in the domain of memory, ps > 0.493, or with estimates of hippocampal tau burden, ps > 0.101.

Conclusion

Taken together, these findings suggest that it is the combination of both daily life diversity and brain health that helps to support cognition in older adults. Specifically, our results revealed that the entorhinal cortex was a critical locus of both memory and learning ability when viewed in combination with measures of daily life activities and experiences. Importantly, we also found that there was a double dissociation across activity and experiential diversity; whereas activity diversity and entorhinal cortex thickness showed linkages to both memory and learning ability, it was experiential diversity that was by moderated by levels of entorhinal tau that predicted learning ability. Despite its limitations, these results offer the intriguing possibility that modifiable facets of daily life function, such as engaging in a variety of different activities or experiencing a greater number of novel locations, may be important buffers to help maintain cognitive function as people age.
