Introduction

Type 2 diabetes (T2D) affects over 500 million adults worldwide and is increasingly recognized as a risk factor for impaired brain health. T2D has been linked to reduced gray matter volume, cortical thickness, and surface area, yet prior studies are limited by small sample sizes, low clinical diversity, and inadequate control for obesity. This project seeks to identify brain structural correlates across the adult T2D continuum (normoglycemia → prediabetes → T2D) beyond obesity-related effects and to determine whether age moderates these associations.

Methods

This was a cross-sectional secondary analysis of 10,327 adults (age [Mean (SD) = 54.2 (15.7)] years, 48.1% female, 21.5% with prediabetes, 13.0% with T2D, Figure 1) from 6 international cohorts. Prediabetes was defined by HbA1c 5.7–6.4%, fasting glucose 5.6–6.9 mmol/L, 2-hour glucose 7.8–10.9 mmol/L, or self-reported prediabetes/borderline diabetes. T2D was defined by self-report or physician diagnosis, HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, fasting glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, or random/2-hour glucose ≥ 11.0 mmol/L. Normoglycemia was defined by self-report and verified by A1C in cohorts with available data. Mean cortical thickness (CT), cortical surface area (CSA), cortical and subcortical gray matter volume (GMV), and intracranial volume (ICV) were derived using FreeSurfer. 

All statistical analyses were conducted in R. Site effects were harmonized with ComBat, and to account for outliers, the top and bottom 1% of data were winsorized. Linear regression models assessed associations with (A) glycemic status and (B) the glycemic status × age-group interaction (young <40, middle-aged 40–64, older ≥65). Analysis A adjusted for age (as a continuous covariate), sex or gender, BMI, and hypertension; analysis B\ included age-group by glycemic status interactions, main effects of age-group and glycemic status and adjusted for sex or gender, BMI, and hypertension. All volumetric models are additionally adjusted for ICV. Least-square means were calculated and post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using the emmeans package, to follow up on models with significant glycemic status or glycemic status by age interactions. Ordered group differences (normoglycemia ≥ prediabetes ≥ T2D) were tested using the restriktor package, which implements likelihood-based tests under linear inequality constraints. FDR correction was applied.

Results

Order-restricted tests showed stepwise reductions across groups (normoglycemia > prediabetes > T2D) in ICV, total CSA, and mean CT. Adjusted means declined from normoglycemia to T2D for ICV (1,490 → 1,470 → 1,450 cm³; t=4.17, FDR-p<0.001), CSA (24.72 → 24.59 → 24.14 cm²; t=2.52, FDR-p=0.015), and CT (2.461 → 2.456 → 2.434 mm; t=2.04, FDR-p=0.034).

Significant group-by-age interactions emerged for cortical GMV (F=4.27, FDR-p=0.005) and subcortical GMV (F=5.56, FDR-p<0.001). 

Post-hoc analyses of group differences within each age group showed modest effects of glycemic status in young adults: T2D was associated with lower subcortical GMV vs. normoglycemia (β = –0.188, P = 0.012), with marginal cortical differences (β = –0.126, P = 0.050). Prediabetes showed marginally lower subcortical GMV vs. normoglycemia (β = –0.092, P = 0.069) with no cortical differences, and no significant T2D–prediabetes differences emerged. In middle-aged adults, both T2D and prediabetes were associated with reduced GMV relative to normoglycemia (T2D—subcortical: β = –0.239, cortical: β = –0.237; both P < 0.001; prediabetes—subcortical: β = –0.114, P < 0.001; cortical: β = –0.083, P = 0.002). T2D also differed significantly from prediabetes (subcortical: β = –0.186; cortical: β = –0.161; both P < 0.001). In older adults, T2D was associated with lower subcortical and cortical GMV compared with both normoglycemia (subcortical GMV: β=-0.115, P<0.001; cortical GMV: β=-0.139, P<0.001) and prediabetes(subcortical GMV: β=-0.175, P<0.001; cortical GMV: β=-0.146, P<0.001). Prediabetes showed slightly higher subcortical GMV than normoglycemia (β = 0.060, P = 0.012) in older adults, with no cortical differences.

Post-hoc age–group comparisons showed that glycemic-status effects were stronger in middle-aged than older adults (Figure 2). Middle-aged adults exhibited larger T2D- and prediabetes-related reductions in subcortical GMV (T2D: β = –0.125; prediabetes: β = –0.114; both FDR-p < 0.001) and cortical GMV (T2D: β = –0.098, FDR-p = 0.002; prediabetes: β = –0.083, FDR-p = 0.002).

Conclusion

T2D was associated with the greatest structural reductions, with prediabetes showing intermediate deficits between T2D and normoglycemia. The impact of (pre) diabetes on brain volume was strongest in middle-aged adults, suggesting that middle age may represent a vulnerable time window during which dysglycemia has a disproportionately stronger impact on brain structure.
