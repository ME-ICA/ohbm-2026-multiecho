Introduction

Obesity is associated with both structural and functional abnormalities in the central nervous system, affecting spatially discrete locations that span multiple canonical networks (e.g. reward).1 Evidence suggests that certain neuropathologic changes may be ameliorated by weight loss,2 but the anatomical scope of network recovery remains ill-defined. Bariatric surgery is a highly effective intervention, capable of achieving sustained reductions in body mass for those who struggle with conservative weight-loss methods. Imaging studies have begun to probe and report neural correlates of bariatric surgery, but generalizability of individual findings is often limited due to sample size and methodological heterogeneity.3 To remedy these limitations, coordinate-based meta-analytics (CBMA) such as alteration likelihood estimation (ALE) have been developed as a powerful approach for accessing the most consistent regional and connectomic effects reported in imaging literature (by quantifying spatial convergence in a common coordinate space) and establishing standardized targets that can be used across clinical and research centers.4 But, as of yet, no such meta-analysis nor meta-connectomic modeling has been carried out on the relevant corpus. 

It remains unclear which cortical/subcortical areas reliably exhibit post-op changes in bariatric surgery patients. Identifying this network of regions is essential for 1) understanding how neural architectures may recover and 2) developing biomarkers capable of quantifying postoperative normalization of brain function (e.g. as a clinical trial endpoint). We address this gap by 1) synthesizing results from all imaging studies (to date) of brain structure/function pre vs. post bariatric surgery, to obtain data-driven stereotactic targets, and 2) constructing a meta-analytic connectivity model (MACM), to confirm the functional relationship between these regions and elucidate the specific network substrate altered by bariatric surgery.

Methods

A comprehensive literature search was performed to identify neuroimaging studies that compared patients pre/post bariatric surgery and reported standardized coordinate locations of structural or functional change. Of the 1121 records screened for redundancy/relevance, 51 studies were assessed for compliance with standards of CBMA and 17 articles were selected; these reports included results (377 foci) from 49 imaging experiments/contrasts (23 task-based; 12 resting-state; 14 voxel-based morphometry), collectively representing 503 subjects. 

To synthesize coordinate data across studies, foci were modeled (per-experiment) as spatial probability distributions proportional to sample size and aggregated via activation likelihood estimation (ALE; p=0.01; cFWE=0.05; 1000 permutations) in GingerALE v3.0.2, computing regions of spatial convergence (Figure 1A). Talairach coordinates of peak significance were selected as nodes (Figure 1B). Then, for each node: 1) a region of interest (ROI; radius=4mm) was defined and used to seed the BrainMap Database (Task-Activation Sector, Normal Mappings); 2) experiments reporting ≥1 coordinate within a given ROI were analyzed for spatial convergence, computing node-to-whole-brain connectivity; 3) significant co-activations within non-overlapping ROIs were defined as edges in the MACM (Figure 2A).

The initial ALE meta-analysis was then repeated with less stringent experiment-filtering criteria (e.g. pre- vs. post-op imaging correlations with external variables) to identify additional regional effects related to bariatric surgery, and the MACM was recomputed (Figure 2B). Sub-group analyses were also performed per-modality but were underpowered.

Results

Significant neurologic changes following bariatric surgery were identified in the right hemisphere, including the putamen, caudate body, inferior/middle frontal gyri (BA9), precentral gyrus (BA6), and frontal sub-lobar white matter; see Figure 1A for visualization of these results and Figure 1B for tabulated statistics. Meta-analytic connectivity modeling (MACM) confirmed that these regions (with the exception of the one white matter focus) comprise a functional network (Figure 2A). Additional effects were localized to the left putamen and medial dorsal nucleus (MDN) of the thalamus, as well as the right anterior nucleus (AN) of the thalamus, insula (BA13), and inferior frontal gyrus (BA44), all of which were confirmed to share functional connections with multiple regions in the network affected by weight loss after bariatric surgery (Figure 2B).

Conclusion

This study identified a robust large-scale neural network consistently altered following bariatric surgery. Our model spans a similar yet non-identical spatial distribution as the neuropathologic changes exhibited in non-surgical obese patients, suggesting the potential for partial functional recovery following weight loss; however, this claim requires further investigation. To that end, the MACM provides an expandable framework for probing how peripheral metabolic interventions precipitate changes in cerebral function. The network structure may inform a future biomarker of surgical response or serve as a data-driven prior for artificial intelligence-based approaches to monitoring non-surgical interventions in obesity.5
