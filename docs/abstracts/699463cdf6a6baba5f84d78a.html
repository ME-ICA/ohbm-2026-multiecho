Introduction

Chronic pain conditions with a predominant nociplastic component, such as juvenile fibromyalgia (JFM), are characterized by widespread pain and sensory hypersensitivity in the absence of clear associated peripheral damage1. Epidemiological evidence indicates that JFM has an estimated prevalence ranging from 1% to 6%, predominantly affecting adolescent girls2. 

Evidence points toward a broader dysfunction that encompasses not only pain processing but also motor performance and sensorimotor integration. Behavioral research shows consistent reductions in maximum muscle strength, indicating possible deficits in the integration of motor output and effort regulation3,4. Neuroimaging studies further show altered activation and connectivity across key neural networks, such as the sensorimotor, salience, and default mode networks5. These impairments suggest alterations in the central mechanisms that disrupt both pain processing and motor control, potentially contributing to persistent pain and the amplification of associated functional symptoms. 

Among various functional assessments used in fibromyalgia research, handgrip strength tasks have consistently demonstrated strong clinical relevance, offering a simple yet sensitive measure linked to multiple symptom domains, including fatigue, disability, and cognitive dysfunction3. Yet, despite consistent behavioral evidence, no functional neuroimaging studies have explored the neural basis of motor control in JFM. Understanding how adolescents with JFM generate and sustain force may offer critical insights into how nociplastic pain disrupts motor performance.

In this context, our first objective was to characterize maximal voluntary contraction (MVC) in comparison to a group of healthy participants. A further objective was to examine brain activity underlying motor control during force production and maintenance at 50% MVC. We also aimed to assess fatigue and pain by integrating subjective ratings with force metrics.

Methods

A total of 38 adolescent girls aged 13–17 years with a diagnosis of JFM and 31 healthy controls (HC) were included in this study. JFM participants were recruited at Cincinnati Children’s Hospital Medical Center (CCHMC) and met the 2010 American College of Rheumatology criteria. They had to report ≥3/10 average pain over the past week, have ≥5 tender points, score ≥7 on the Functional Disability Index, and be on a stable medication regimen for at least 3 weeks (excluding opioids). HC were required to have no current or past chronic pain condition, with no psychiatric or neurological disorders. At enrollment, they had to report a pain Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) score of 0 and an FDI <7. Exclusion criteria included typical MRI contraindications, pregnancy, neurological disorders, developmental delay or current use of opioids. 

Participants were familiarized with the experimental procedures and trained in the tasks and rating scales to ensure full comprehension. The experimental protocol (see Figure 1) consisted of two main parts: (1) an initial assessment of maximum voluntary contraction (MVC) followed by (2) an fMRI strength/fatigue task performed at 50% of each participant’s individual MVC. During the MVC assessment, participants performed three 5-second maximal squeezes, each separated by a 60-second rest period using an MRI-compatible hand dynamometer. This assessment was used later to individualize task difficulty at 50% of each participant’s MVC, ensuring a force demand scaled to their physical capacity. 

Inside the scanner, participants performed an isometric hand-grip task in which each trial followed a repeated sequence of Preparation, Press, and Rest periods. During the press phase, participants attempted to match their grip force to the visual target corresponding to 50% MVC. Intermittent visual analogue scale (VAS) ratings were included to assess perceived effort and physical tiredness.

MRI data were collected using a Philips Ingenia 3.0T system with a 32-channel head coil. Functional images consisted of T2*-weighted echo-planar imaging sensitive to BOLD contrast, and structural images were acquired using a high-resolution T1-weighted sequence.

Preprocessing followed the standard pipeline implemented in the CONN Toolbox v20.b running on MATLAB, including realignment and unwarping, centering, outlier detection (ART), direct normalization of functional images to MNI space, and spatial smoothing with the specified FWHM kernel. Task-related activity was modeled using a general linear approach that distinguished preparation, force-production, and rating periods. Motion parameters and outlier volumes were included as nuisance regressors. Second-level analyses were performed using two-sample t-tests to compare task-related activation between the JFM and HC groups, controlling for mean force output during the task as a covariate.

Force-production performance (mean force, maximum force, mean absolute error and proportion of time within target) was analyzed using linear mixed-effects models including group and condition as fixed factors and subject as a random effect. 

Results

The JFM group showed marked impairments across behavioral measures during the assessment of the MVC. Compared with controls, they exhibited significantly lower MVC. The adjusted estimated marginal means indicated that controls reached 16.9 kg, whereas patients reached 14.3 kg, yielding an adjusted group difference of 2.66 kg (t(65) = 2.75, p = 0.007). They also showed a lower maximum force (F(1,65) = 7.56, p = 0.007) and mean force (F(1,65) = 7.75, p = 0.007). These differences remained significant after adjusting for age and BMI.

During the 50% MVC task inside the scanner, patients consistently produced lower force. The overall mean force was 6.98 ± 1.66 kg (range 2.96–11.6 kg) and the overall maximum force was 9.53 ± 2.28 kg (range 4.95–17.0 kg). Linear mixed models including group, trial, age, and BMI showed significant group differences in force production. For mean force, there was a main effect of group (F(1,65) = 5.12, p = 0.027) and trial (F(8,536) = 3.07, p = 0.002), as well as a significant group × trial interaction (F(8,536) = 4.26, p < 0.001). For maximum force, there was likewise a significant main effect of group (F(1,65) = 5.14, p = 0.027) and trial (F(8,536) = 10.77, p < 0.001), without a significant group × trial interaction (F(8,536) = 1.63, p = 0.115). This difference in force production is related to the reduced MVC observed during the initial assessment.

Both groups were able to reach the target force, but in the last part of the task, the JFM group had difficulty maintaining it.  Across trials, they spent significantly less time within the prescribed force band, demonstrated greater variability, and showed a progressive decline in performance. Subjectively, patients reported higher baseline pain, fatigue, stiffness, and cramping, and only the JFM group exhibited substantial post-task increases across all VAS measures.

Neuroimaging analyses revealed reduced activation in the JFM group across the full press phase, with significant clusters emerging after FDR correction. These included a cluster in the left visuomotor cortex (pFDR-corr = 0.001), another in the right visuomotor cortex (pFDR-corr = 0.057), and a cluster in the left medial temporal gyrus (pFDR-corr = 0.035). However, when analyzing force production and maintenance separately, no reliable group differences emerged during the initial force production phase at the 50% MVC. In contrast, during the sustained force maintenance phase (see Figure 2), the JFM group showed hypoactivation in a significant cluster located in the left visuomotor cortex. This cluster (k = 492 voxels, pFDR-corr = 0.001) encompassed posterior parietal regions, which are associated with visuomotor integration. Notably, during the final trials of the maintenance phase, multiple clusters showed significant group differences. A contralateral M1–premotor cluster (k = 203, p = 0.001) and a large bilateral sensorimotor cluster spanning M1, SMA, S1, parietal, and insular regions (k = 1089, p = 0.001) showed markedly reduced activation in the JFM group. Additional significant clusters were observed in the parietal cortex (e.g., k = 154, p = 0.004; k = 185, p = 0.001) and in the prefrontal cortex (e.g., k = 203, p = 0.002; k = 226, p = 0.001), all showing stronger activation in controls. These findings indicate that the most extensive brain alterations arise during the late stage of sustained force maintenance.

All results are consistent with the behavioral evidence of reduced performance in the JFM group. These effects remain significant after adjusting for the individual force output.

Conclusion

This study provides an integrated behavioral and neuroimaging characterization of motor control and performance in adolescents with JFM compared with healthy controls. The convergence of behavioral and brain evidence suggests that the JFM group showed baseline motor impairments, beginning with a reduced MVC, indicating a diminished maximal strength capacity. When performing the sustained 50% MVC task, both groups were initially able to reach the required target force. However, the JFM group exhibited higher fatigue across trials. In the later trials of the task, they produced lower force outputs, spending less time in the target band. fMRI data revealed that behavioral results were paralleled by reduced activation in motor and visuomotor integration regions, especially during sustained effort. This was especially evident during the maintenance phase of force production.

Together, these findings suggest that JFM involves a disruption of central mechanisms supporting sustained motor control and sensorimotor integration. This dysfunction may contribute to both reduced physical performance and the amplification of fatigue and pain symptoms characteristic of nociplastic pain conditions.
