Introduction

Successful sentence comprehension in aging is often maintained through the recruitment of additional neural resources, a phenomenon broadly described as neural compensation.^1 While established models of aging typically emphasize the recruitment of homologous right-hemisphere frontal regions to support language,^2 the potential contribution of broader, domain-general networks remains less characterized. Specifically, it is unclear how the aging brain reorganizes its resources to manage the increased demands of processing syntactically complex or noncanonical sentences. The present study aimed to investigate the neural substrates of sentence processing in healthy older adults compared to healthy young adults, exploring whether the older group would exhibit distinct recruitment of extra-linguistic regions to handle increased syntactic complexity.

Methods

The study included 53 healthy right-handed, Czech-speeking volunteers, divided into two groups: 19 Healthy Adults (HA; mean age = 68 years) and 34 Healthy Young adults (HY; mean age = 24.6 years). Participants performed a sentence comprehension task inside the scanner, reading either canonical or noncanonical sentences with varying syntactic complexity and deciding whether the sentences matched a presented picture. Functional imaging data were acquired and analyzed using SPM. A general linear model was used to estimate task-related activation. Random-effects group analyses assessed age-related differences. We examined the group difference (HA > HY) for two specific complexity contrasts: Canonical vs. Noncanonical, to identify regions where older adults show greater activation increases for noncanonical syntax relative to canonical syntax; and Canonical Complex vs. Noncanonical Complex, to identify regions recruited by older adults specifically when processing high-complexity noncanonical sentences compared to their canonical complex counterparts. Statistical maps were thresholded at a cluster-corrected significance level of p < .05.

Results

The fMRI results revealed robust age-related differences in neural recruitment (HA > HY) for both contrasts. For the Canonical vs. Noncanonical contrast, older adults showed significantly greater activation in the right insula (peak x = 33, y = 2, z = 7; k = 53, p-corr = .047) and the left cerebellum (anterior lobe/culmen; peak x = -27, y = -52, z = -29; k = 53, p-corr = .047). For the Canonical Complex vs. Noncanonical Complex contrast, older adults exhibited a significant cluster of over-activation in the left superior occipital gyrus (peak x = -24, y = -79, z = 25; k = 99, p-corr = .002).

Conclusion

These findings provide evidence supporting a multi-domain view of neural compensation in aging. To maintain performance on complex or linguistic tasks, the aging brain appears to recruit a scaffold of non-linguistic resources.^3 The concurrent activation of the right insula and left cerebellum in the older group supports the view that aging necessitates the recruitment of domain-general systems to process complex syntax, reflecting increased executive effort, while the specific recruitment of the left occipital cortex during high-complexity contrasts suggests a potential compensatory reliance on visual attention or visual imagery strategies. This pattern aligns with previous findings,^4 suggesting that older adults compensate for reduced neural efficiency by engaging a broader, distributed network of cognitive control. These results highlight the involvement of extra-linguistic systems in preserving language comprehension.
