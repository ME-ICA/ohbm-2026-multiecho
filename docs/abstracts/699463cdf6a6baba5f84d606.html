Introduction

Organisms form associations between environmental stimuli and outcomes, a process critical for adaptive behaviour and survival. The conditioning-extinction paradigm, rooted in Pavlovian learning principles, provides a widely used framework to investigate these associative processes across species. The conditioning-extinction paradigm has established a distributed network supporting aversive learning and extinction, prominently involving the amygdala, hippocampus, insula, anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), and prefrontal cortex (Fulllana et al., 2018).  Within this circuit, the amygdala and hippocampus are central to the neural circuits governing aversive learning and extinction. Direct evidence for oscillatory dynamics between the amygdala and the hippocampus in such learning paradigms in humans is limited. Here, we leverage iEEG recordings from the human amygdala and hippocampus during a novel conditioning-extinction paradigm. We employ negative and neutral images from the International Affective Picture System (IAPS) as unconditioned stimuli (US), ensuring ecological validity and enhancing the generalizability of our findings. By examining both conditioning and extinction phases, we aim to delineate the task induced responses and oscillatory dynamics interactions between the amygdala and the hippocampus. We anticipated task induced condition differences in the low frequency range (2-12 Hz) especially with rapid onset latency for emotion valence paradigms, based on our previous amygdala neural recordings (Sonkusare et al., 2023). 

Methods

This study was undertaken in a cohort of 30 patients with refractory epilepsy who underwent stereo-electroencephalography neurosurgical procedure for clinical evaluation. 

Electrode localisation (Figure 1A)

Intracranial electrodes were localised on a 3-d MNI brain template using a fusion of pre-operative magnetic resonance (MR) and post-operative computed tomography (CT) scans using Brainstorm. Overall, there were 18 patients with a total of 53 contacts in the amygdala and 18 patients with 57 contacts in the hippocampus. Of these 14 subjects had concomitant electrodes in amygdala and the hippocampus. The electrodes contacts localised in the amygdala and the hippocampus are shown in Fig. 1A. The conditioning-extinction paradigms is shown in Fig. 1B. The conditioning and extinction phase of the experiments were conducted on successive days.

Analyses

After preprocessing the data (see Methods), we systematically evaluated the dynamics and condition differences of the amygdala and hippocampal activity by employing event-related spectral perturbation maps and using permutation testing to identify frequency-specific condition differences.  Ultimately, comprehensive connectivity analytical approaches utilised to probe the connectivity between amygdala and hippocampus: functional connectivity (weighted phase lag index), directed connectivity (transfer entropy), effective connectivity (dynamic causal modelling).

Results

1) Amygdala ERSPs:In the time-frequency analysis of the aversive learning phase, the amygdala showed greater theta and gamma (~ 35Hz) activity to negative compared to neutral CS+ (Fig. 2A top). During the extinction phase, the same frequencies of theta and gamma showed reduced activity to negative compared to neutral CS+

2) Amygdala’s theta activity predicts emotional valence of negative and neutral USs: participants who rated negative images as more aversive (i.e., had larger neutral–negative valence differences) showed greater theta power to CS+ negative stimuli compared to CS+ neutral ones (i.e., more negative y-values) (R2 = .48, **p=.01) (Fig. 2A right).

3) Hippocampal ERSPs: The hippocampus showed greater alpha and gamma activity to negative compared to neutral CS+  during conditioning. During extinction increased activity to negative compared to neutral CS+ in the alpha and gamma range was observed.

Connectivity

4) Weighted phase lag index (wPLI): This measure evaluates the consistency of non-zero phase lag relationships between two signals, providing a robust estimate of true interaction. wPLI results showed a double dissociation of connectivity: in the negative condition, we observed less low theta (3-5 Hz) and greater high theta-alpha (6-8 Hz) during conditioning with the opposite pattern observed during extinction  (Fig. 5B left): low theta 3-5Hz (CS+ negative: mean = 0.32, sd 0.06), extinction: 0.34, sd = 0.07,  T89 = -2.34, PFDR = 0.0001, pFDR significance at .025; high theta 6-8 Hz (CS+ negative: mean = 0.32,sd =  0.06, extinction: mean = 0.34, sd = 0.07),  T89 = -2.34, PFDR = 0.0001, pFDR significance at .025.  For the neutral condition, wPLI results also showed a double dissociation of connectivity and opposite to that for negative condition: we observed greater low theta (3-5 Hz) and reduced high theta (6-8 Hz) during conditioning with the opposite pattern observed during extinction  (Fig. 2B ): low theta 3-5Hz - CS+ negative: mean = 0.32, sd 0.06, extinction: mean = 0.34, sd = 0.07  T89 = -2.34, PFDR = 0.0001, pFDR significance at .025; high theta 6-8 Hz - CS+ negative: mean = 0.34, sd = 0.06, extinction: mean = 0.32, sd 0.05,  T89 = 2.66, PFDR = 0.009, pFDR significance at .025.

5) Transfer Entropy (TE): TE is a powerful measure of directed connectivity. Based on our wPLI results, we focused on low (3-5 Hz) and high theta (6-8 Hz) range to assess the directionality of information flow. We computed TE in both directions—amygdala-to-hippocampus and hippocampus-to-amygdala. To reduce multiple comparisons, we computed directional TE differences (conditioning minus extinction) for each frequency band and valence.  The results revealed a frequency-dependent directional pattern. For both the negative and neutral conditions, amygdala-to-hippocampus TE was significantly greater in the low theta range (3–5 Hz) i.e amygdala drove hippocampal activity during conditioning and hippocampus drove amygdala activity during extinction (negative stimuli conditioning: mean = 0.012, SD = 0.52; extinction: mean = –0.21, SD = 0.47; T₈₉ = 2.67, PFDR = 0.009 ; neutral stimuli conditioning: mean = -0.04, sd = 0.26, extinction: mean = 0.049, sd 0.28, T89 = -2.82, PFDR = 0.005).

Dynamic causal modelling (DCM): DCM is a hypothesis-driven computational approach that estimates the direction of causal connectivity between brain regions, accounting for modulatory effects of experimental conditions.  Model spaces were informed by prior connectivity results of wPLI and TE, and we tested connectivity models in the 3–5 Hz and 6–8 Hz frequency bands. This task input modulates the strength of a directional connection in each model.  For the 3–5 Hz band, the winning model robustly indicated amygdala-to-hippocampus connectivity with a probability of 92.3%, whereas in the 6–8 Hz band, the hippocampus-to-amygdala model was favoured with a probability of 94.9%. (Fig 5C).

Conclusion

Using intracranial recordings with high spatiotemporal resolution, our study provides direct evidence for frequency-specific oscillatory dynamics within the amygdala–hippocampal circuit during emotional learning and extinction. Complex high and low theta dynamics dominated amygdala-hippocampal connectivity with asymmetric dependencies from the amygdala to the hippocampus and vice-versa for aversive learning and extinction. These findings advance our mechanistic understanding of how distinct frequency bands coordinate to support adaptive emotional behaviour from rich high-fidelity signals of which human neuronal data have been notably scarce.
