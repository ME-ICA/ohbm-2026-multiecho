Introduction

Breast cancer is the most prevalent malignancy among women. Notably, up to one-third of patients exhibit cancer-related cognitive impairment (CRCI) prior to the initiation of systemic therapy, indicating the presence of disease-intrinsic neural alterations1. Although chemotherapy-associated cognitive deficits have been extensively studied, the neural mechanisms underlying pre-treatment cognitive changes remain poorly understood. The hippocampus, a key structure for memory and spatial cognition, displays marked functional heterogeneity along its longitudinal axis2. However, it is unclear whether alterations in hippocampal subregional connectivity progress linearly with cognitive decline or reflect more complex, non-linear reorganization in relation to cognitive status. In this study, we examined resting-state functional connectivity (rs-FC) of hippocampal subregions in pre-chemotherapy breast cancer patients to delineate status-specific neural signatures associated with cognitive impairment.

Methods

Data acquisition: A total of 146 breast cancer patients were recruited prior to systemic treatment. Cognitive status was assessed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), and patients were categorized into a mild cognitive impairment group (BC-MCI; n=43, MoCA<26) and a cognitively non-impaired group (BC-nonMCI; n=51, MoCA≥26). In addition, a demographically matched healthy control group (NC; n=24) was included for comparison.

fMRI preprocessing: Resting-state fMRI data were acquired and preprocessed using DPABI (version 6.0). Preprocessing steps included slice-timing correction, head motion correction (participants with motion exceeding 3 mm or 3° were excluded), spatial normalization to MNI space, and nuisance signal regression.

Functional Connectivity Analysis: Seed regions were defined for the left and right hippocampus, including the entire hippocampus as well as its rostral and caudal subregions. To capture fine-grained network topology, target regions of interest were defined using the Brainnetome Atlas(BNA), which parcellates the brain into 246 subregions based on structural and functional connectivity. This atlas was selected to improve spatial specificity beyond conventional anatomical templates. Mean time series were extracted from all regions of interest, and pairwise functional connectivity was calculated.

Statistical analysis: Group differences in z-transformed functional connectivity maps were assessed using one-way analysis of variance in SPM12, with age and education included as covariates. Significant effects were identified at a voxel-level threshold of p < 0.001 and a cluster-level false discovery rate–corrected threshold of p < 0.05. Post hoc analyses were conducted to determine whether connectivity changes followed a graded pattern across groups (NC > BC-nonMCI > BC-MCI).

Results

The three groups were well matched in age and education (all p > 0.05). Analysis of functional connectivity revealed significant group differences that were predominantly driven by the right hippocampus. Post-hoc analyses demonstrated status-dependent, non-linear patterns of connectivity alteration rather than a simple monotonic decline with increasing cognitive impairment (Fig. 1). Specifically, connectivity between the right entire and caudal hippocampus and the left inferior parietal lobule—a key hub of the default mode network—was significantly increased in the BC-MCI group relative to both the BC-nonMCI and NC groups. This paradoxical hyperconnectivity in cognitively impaired patients suggests pronounced network dysregulation. In contrast, the BC-nonMCI group exhibited distinct connectivity patterns involving the right insula, a core region of the salience network, and the middle frontal gyrus(MFG), which is implicated in executive control. These alterations differentiated the BC-nonMCI group from both healthy controls and cognitively impaired patients, further supporting the presence of non-linear, status-specific network changes. Collectively, these findings indicate that pre-treatment CRCI is characterized by complex, cognitive status–dependent dysregulation of functional networks, with preferential involvement of right-lateralized circuits supporting spatial cognition and executive function.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates aberrant right hippocampal functional connectivity in breast cancer patients prior to chemotherapy. The preferential involvement of the right hippocampus is consistent with the cognitive domains assessed by the MoCA, particularly visuospatial and executive functions, which rely on right-lateralized hippocampal–cortical networks³. The observed hyperconnectivity in the BC-MCI group likely reflects neural inefficiency or a compensatory failure, often described as “hyperconnectivity in the failing brain,” whereby excessive network recruitment or reduced inhibitory control emerges in response to accumulating pathology4. Importantly, these findings challenge the assumption that cognitive decline follows a uniform, linear trajectory of network disconnection. Instead, distinct, status-specific deviations—such as paradoxical hyperconnectivity—may represent sensitive biomarkers of early cognitive vulnerability. Moreover, the unique connectivity profiles observed in the BC-nonMCI group suggest that neural reorganization begins before overt cognitive deficits become clinically detectable. Together, these results highlight the potential utility of rs-FC as an early stratification tool for identifying patients at risk for CRCI shortly after breast cancer diagnosis5.
