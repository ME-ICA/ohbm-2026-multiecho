Introduction

Human voice perception depends on coordinated interactions between temporal auditory cortex and frontal auditory regions. While functional neuroimaging studies suggest prolonged maturation of frontal–temporal communication during development1,2, they lack the temporal resolution and directionality required to determine how feedforward and feedback signaling evolve across childhood and adolescence. In particular, it remains unclear whether developmental changes primarily affect bottom-up sensory-driven pathways, top-down feedback pathways, or both. Intracranial electrophysiology provides a unique opportunity to directly quantify directed functional interactions at the level of neural population dynamics. Here, we tested how feedforward and feedback interactions within the auditory ventral stream change across development using intracranial electroencephalography (iEEG) recordings in cohort of pediatric and young adult patients.

Methods

We analyzed intracranial sEEG recordings from 31 children, adolescents, and young adults (ages 8–24) undergoing clinical monitoring for epilepsy. Participants listened to a 165-sound naturalistic stimulus set including speech, music, animal vocalizations, environmental, and mechanical sounds (1.5 s duration; RMS-normalized), presented during an auditory one-back task to maintain attention. Neural responses were analyzed using broadband high-gamma activity (70–150 Hz). 

Recording sites were anatomically localized and assigned to auditory ventral stream regions using established parcellations. Temporal auditory regions included primary auditory cortex (A1), Belt, Parabelt, and superior temporal gyrus (STG). Frontal auditory regions included frontal operculum (FOP), ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (vlPFC), and orbitofrontal cortex (OFC). Analyses were performed at the channel level and aggregated across regions where appropriate.

Directed functional interactions were quantified using population communication models (PCM)3. For each source–target region pair, multivariate linear models were trained to predict held-out neural responses in a target channel from population activity in a source region. Separate feedforward (temporal→frontal) and feedback (frontal→temporal) models were fit using identical modeling assumptions. Source activity was expanded into a lagged design matrix using past temporal bins only (~200 ms history; no future bins). Data were split into train (65%), validation (20%), and test (15%) sets at the trial level. Ridge regression regularization was optimized on the validation set, and an optional static nonlinear output transform was fit on validation data. Model performance was quantified as the Pearson correlation between predicted and observed responses on held-out test data.

To assess the temporal alignment of directed interactions, lag-sweep analyses were performed by systematically shifting source activity relative to the target (±150 ms in ~25 ms bins) and recomputing predictive performance. For each target channel, the lag producing maximal prediction was identified and refined using quadratic interpolation to estimate peak lag in milliseconds.

Linear mixed-effects models tested the effects of age and hemisphere (left coded as 1, right as 0) on prediction strength and peak lag, with random intercepts for channel. Statistical significance was assessed using two-sided tests (α = 0.05).

Results

Population communication models revealed robust directional asymmetries across the auditory ventral stream. Feedforward temporal→frontal prediction was reliably observed across frontal targets but did not vary with age. In contrast, feedback frontal→temporal prediction strength increased significantly with age, indicating progressive strengthening of top-down interactions from frontal auditory regions to temporal cortex across development.

Feedforward prediction exhibited a strong hemispheric asymmetry, with significantly weaker temporal→frontal prediction in left-hemisphere channels compared to right-hemisphere channels. This hemispheric effect was consistent across temporal source subregions and frontal targets. In contrast, feedback frontal→temporal prediction did not show a significant overall hemispheric bias, suggesting more symmetric maturation of top-down pathways.

Lag-sweep analyses revealed additional developmental structure in the temporal alignment of directed interactions. Several frontal→temporal pathways exhibited significant age-related shifts toward earlier optimal lags, consistent with faster feedback signaling in older participants. Notably, FOP→STG interactions showed a significant negative age effect on peak lag (β = −3.58 ms/year, p = 0.024), indicating that frontal activity increasingly preceded temporal responses with age.

In contrast, select feedforward temporal→frontal pathways showed age-related shifts toward later optimal lags. Belt→FOP (β = 8.71 ms/year, p = 0.036) and A1→FOP (β = 7.62 ms/year, p = 0.043) interactions exhibited progressively later temporal→frontal peak lags with age, suggesting prolonged refinement of feedforward timing from early and mid-level auditory cortex to frontal regions.

Hemispheric effects on peak lag were region- and direction-specific. OFC→Parabelt and FOP→Parabelt feedback interactions showed significantly more positive lags in the left hemisphere, indicating stronger frontal-leading dynamics relative to the right hemisphere. Conversely, in several temporal→frontal pathways (e.g., Belt→OFC), the right hemisphere exhibited relatively stronger feedforward timing. The A1–FOP pair demonstrated a robust bidirectional hemispheric asymmetry, with one hemisphere consistently reaching peak coupling approximately 50 ms earlier depending on interaction direction.

Together, these results indicate that development of the auditory ventral stream involves coordinated changes in both the strength and timing of directed interactions, with feedback pathways strengthening and accelerating with age, and feedforward pathways showing later-emerging temporal alignment and pronounced hemispheric asymmetries.

Conclusion

These findings provide direct electrophysiological evidence that maturation of the human auditory ventral stream is characterized by asymmetric developmental trajectories in feedforward and feedback signaling. While feedforward temporal→frontal predictive interactions are already robust by late childhood and show minimal age-related change in overall strength, feedback frontal→temporal interactions continue to strengthen across development, consistent with prolonged maturation of top-down cortical control mechanisms.

Beyond changes in interaction strength, lag-sweep analyses reveal systematic developmental refinement of interaction timing. Age-related shifts toward earlier optimal lags in frontal→temporal pathways suggest increasing efficiency and temporal precision of feedback signaling with maturation. In contrast, later optimal lags in select temporal→frontal pathways indicate that feedforward timing from early and mid-level auditory cortex to frontal regions continues to be refined into adolescence and young adulthood. Together, these patterns support a developmental model in which bottom-up pathways provide early-established sensory drive, while top-down predictive and modulatory influences are progressively sharpened over development.

The observed hemispheric asymmetries further suggest that maturation of directed auditory interactions is not bilaterally uniform. Region- and direction-specific differences in optimal lag between hemispheres point to divergent developmental specializations in left and right auditory–frontal circuits, potentially reflecting hemispheric biases in temporal integration, predictive processing, or functional lateralization of auditory cognition. Notably, robust bidirectional asymmetries in early auditory–frontal pairs (e.g., A1-FOP) indicate that hemispheric differences emerge at early stages of the ventral stream and persist across interaction directions.

Taken together, these results align with hierarchical and predictive processing frameworks of cortical development, in which sensory-driven representations are established early, whereas feedback-mediated integration and temporal coordination mature over extended developmental timescales1,4,5. By directly quantifying the strength and timing of directed neural interactions at the population level, this work provides a mechanistic account of how auditory cortical communication is refined across development, with implications for understanding the emergence of mature auditory perception and cognition.
