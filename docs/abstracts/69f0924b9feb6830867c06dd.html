Introduction

Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) is a widely used technique to study brain activity. In group fMRI studies, the data is traditionally modelled using a multi-level GLM. While a multi-level can be rewritten as a joint model directly linking the group-level parameters to the fMRI time series, this is computationally intractable due to the high dimensionality of fMRI data.ˆ1 

In practice, the typical group fMRI analysis adopts a summary statistic approach. FSL uses contrasts of first-level parameter estimates and their variances for group analysis, while SPM uses only the first-level contrasts.ˆ2ˆ3 These are, however, not equivalent to traditional hierarchical statistical models which estimate first and second-level parameters jointly.

We propose a factorisation of the joint model that enables modelling first-level parameter estimates and their contrasts within a computationally tractable Bayesian hierarchical framework. Our approach simultaneously estimates the within- and between-subject variances, accounts for the spatial structure of the brain and is fitted via a scalable expectation-maximisation algorithm. Here we present preliminary findings from simulation studies and an application to real resting-state fMRI data from the UK Biobank study, which demonstrate that the proposed model yields more accurate parameter estimates, similar power and better controlled false positive rates than existing methods.ˆ4

Methods

Figure 1 presents our Bayesian hierarchical model. Instead of jointly fitting all subjects' time series data (Eq. 1), we show a factorisation (Eq. 2) that enables inference to be carried out on first-level parameter estimates rather than the full fMRI time series, while still allowing simultaneous estimation of within- and between-subject variances.

The factorised data is then fit with a Bayesian hierarchical model (Eq. 3). Continuous spike-and-slab priors are placed on the second-level parameters to induce shrinkage of small effects towards zero, and the pattern of the shrinkage itself is spatially regularised.

The proposed model is evaluated and benchmarked in a fully synthetic simulation study. We compare it with two existing methods, ordinary least squares (OLS) and an estimate+variance summary statistics approach using generalised least squares (SS-GLS) similar to FSL. We generate fMRI time series with 200 time points for 20 subjects over 100 voxels arranged on a 10 x 10 grid, according to Equation 1. At the first level, the design matrix consists of an intercept and a single covariate sampled from a standard normal distribution. At the second level, the design matrix contains only an intercept. The first second-level parameter is the population mean of the intercept which is set to zero across all voxels and is used to assess false positive rates; the second parameter is the population mean of the covariate coefficient which is set to a nonzero constant within a circular region of radius 2 centred on the grid and to zero elsewhere, inducing a spatially structured signal used to assess power. This procedure is repeated for 1000 simulation runs. 

False positive rates are further assessed using a task analysis of the resting-state fMRI data from the UK Biobank; we extracted the middle (MNI z=0) slice, 1,845 in-mask voxels, 490 timepoints, TR 0.735, drawing subsets of 50, 100, 250 and 453 subjects. At the first level, the design matrix consists of an intercept and a regressor corresponding to a 20-seconds on, 20-seconds off block design. At the second level, the design matrix again consists of an intercept only. 

For the proposed model, inference on non-zero signal is obtained with the posterior probability of inclusion of the second-level parameters. This approach has been shown to yield reliable inference in comparable neuroimaging settings.ˆ5

Results

Figure 2 summarises our preliminary results across the simulation study and the resting-state data analysis. In the fully synthetic simulations, the proposed spatial model yields lower MSE and smaller bias for both null and non-null parameters compared to ordinary least squares and the summary-statistics approach, with the largest gains observed at smaller sample sizes. The summary-statistics approach exhibits inflated false positive rates for smaller numbers of subjects, whereas the proposed model maintains low false positive rates across all sample sizes. All methods achieve near perfect power in the considered settings. 

Similar behaviour is observed in the resting-state analysis, where the proposed model shows well-controlled false positive rates for all sample sizes considered, while the two other methods show higher false positive rates in some instances.

Conclusion

We introduce a computationally tractable Bayesian hierarchical model for group fMRI through a factorisation of the single-level representation of the multi-level GLM. This reparametrisation enables scalable, simultaneous estimation of the within- and between-subject variability, and the model's sparsity structure explicitly accounts for the spatial structure of the brain. We are working on real data evaluations to demonstrate the increased power of the model. Across simulations and real resting-state fMRI data, the proposed model achieves improved estimation accuracy and more reliable control of false positive rates compared to commonly used approaches, thus providing a practical alternative for large-scale group fMRI studies.
