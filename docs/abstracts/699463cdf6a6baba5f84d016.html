Introduction

Very preterm birth (VPT; <32 weeks gestational age) or very low birth weight (VLBW; <1500 g) disrupts typical brain maturation and leads to long-lasting structural alterations that persist into adulthood1,2. Although reductions in cortical and subcortical gray matter volume have been consistently reported at the group level, recent work3 has revealed substantial inter-individual heterogeneity in cortical development across the lifespan after preterm birth, indicating that the consequences of early dysmaturation vary markedly between individuals. Whether comparable heterogeneity also characterizes subcortical gray matter volume (sGMV) remains unclear.

Here, we address this gap by applying a large-scale, normative modeling framework4 to longitudinal MRI data from adults born VPT/VLBW. This approach allows us to quantify individual patterns of sGMV deviation relative to typical developmental trajectories and to determine whether subcortical structures exhibit similarly diverse outcomes as those observed in the cortex. Our findings aim to clarify the extent and nature of individual variability in the subcortex following preterm birth and to advance a more individualized understanding of long-term neurodevelopmental consequences.

Methods

We analyzed longitudinal T1-weighted MRI data from the Bavarian Longitudinal Study (BLS), a population-based cohort followed from birth to adulthood. MRI was acquired at the ages of 26 and 38 years (BLS-26 and a subset of the ongoing investigation of BLS-38, respectively). Participants born VPT/VLBW (n = 96 at age 26, n = 89 at age 38) and full-term (FT) controls (> 37 weeks of gestation, n = 107 at age 26, n = 104 at age 38) were included. Subcortical volumes were extracted using FreeSurfer’s (v. 7.3.2) automated processing pipeline (recon-all) for the thalamus, caudate, putamen, pallidum, hippocampus, amygdala, and nucleus accumbens of both hemispheres. A normative reference framework pre-fitted on about 170,000 MRI scans was employed, providing lifespan reference trajectories corrected for age and sex4. Random effects of study were calculated separately for BLS-26 and BLS-38 datasets based on FT subjects. Next, individual deviation centile scores were obtained and classified into infranormal (< 5th percentile) or supranormal (> 95th percentile). Heterogeneity was quantified as (i) the proportion of extranormal (< 5th / > 95th percentile) deviations per region and (ii) cross-subject spatial correlation of deviation profiles. Temporal stability was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) between ages 26 and 38.

Results

On a group level, VPT/VLBW adults showed significantly lower volumes in all investigated sGMV areas (pFDR < 0.05, except for the left putamen in 26-year-olds) compared with FT controls (Fig. 1a-b). The extent of individual heterogeneity in subcortical deviations was substantially less compared to previously identified cortical deviations3: up to 48% of preterm participants aged 26 years and 58% aged 38 shared an infranormal deviation in the same regions, compared to only about 27% of preterm individuals that shared a deviation in cortical areas.3 In contrast, FT controls showed the expected ~5% rate of infranormal deviations per region, reflecting the definition of deviation thresholds based on normative models trained on large reference samples. Regional overlap was highest in the thalamus of both hemispheres. Within-subject deviation profiles were widely conserved between the ages of 26 and 38 years, with ICCs ranging from 0.47 to 0.95 (p < 0.001). Nine of 14 subcortical structures showed excellent temporal stability (ICC ≥ 0.80), including the bilateral caudate, putamen, and hippocampus, as well as the left amygdala and accumbens area. In contrast, the thalamus and pallidum showed weaker stability (ICCs ≤ 0.64), with the right pallidum (ICC = 0.47) showing the least stable trajectory across adulthood (Fig. 1c). Gestational age and birth weight significantly correlated with the number of extranormal deviations (ρ = -0.46, p < 0.001; ρ = -0.39, p < 0.001), indicating a dose-dependent link between prematurity and deviation extent.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that subcortical gray matter development after preterm birth seems to be less individually variable compared to cortical aberrations. Similar to cortical deviations though, subcortical deviations remain temporally stable along the adult lifespan. Individual deviation profiles revealed regionally conserved but person-specific subcortical patterns, with up to 58% of preterm adults showing an infranormal deviation in the same region. This convergence is substantially higher than what has been reported for cortical deviations, where only about 27% of individuals shared extranormal patterns.3 The stronger regional overlap of deviations in subcortical structures might suggest that these regions represent primary sites of early vulnerability, consistent with their rapid maturation and metabolic sensitivity during the late gestational period.

Overall, our findings underscore the value of individualized deviation profiling for understanding long-term neurodevelopment after preterm birth. Group averages can obscure both the consistent subcortical vulnerabilities shared across many individuals and the wide variability of cortical trajectories. Single-subject characterization therefore offers a clearer picture of long-term brain outcomes and helps identify those who may benefit most from targeted support. Using normative modeling on longitudinal BLS data, we show that preterm birth leads to distinct yet temporally stable alterations in subcortical gray matter volume.
