Introduction

Population-level neuroimaging studies rely on registration to group-average brain templates to analyse population trends. These templates and registration tools can be divided into surface or volume based. The features represented by group-averages are registration method dependent due to the nature of the data used by each method. Surface registration aligns cortical meshes through inflation and spherical mapping, establishing vertex-wise correspondence across subjects and producing group-average surfaces that respect cortical topology but lack a natural volumetric embedding. Volumetric registration aligns images into Euclidean 3D space, requiring voxel-wise averaging that cannot guarantee realistic cortical geometry or vertex-wise correspondence. Here we introduce and demonstrate a technique for creating a group average surface embedded in volumetric space leveraging vertex-wise correspondence from surface-based registration to estimate mean cortical geometry directly in the volumetric frame.

Methods

We analysed 1,064 Human Connectome Project subjects1 with FreeSurfer white-matter cortical surfaces2 in fsLR-32k space, ensuring shared mesh topology and vertex correspondence. Surfaces were nonlinearly mapped into the OMM-1 volumetric template3 using MMORF4, and all vertex coordinates were analysed in template space unless otherwise stated.

For each vertex, coordinates from all subjects were used to define a cross-subject 3D point cloud. Taking the vertex-wise simple (Euclidean) mean of this cloud smooths and attenuates cortical folding, since the mean of points on a nonlinear manifold such as the curved surface of the brain generally does not lie on the manifold itself, producing a biased group surface that does not follow tissue boundaries (Fig. 1A), as previously observed for vertex-wise surface averaging2. To overcome this smoothing bias, we modelled cross-subject variability at each vertex using a subject-by-subject k-nearest-neighbour (kNN) graph in OMM-1 space, constructed from Euclidean distances between vertex coordinates. Distances were converted to Gaussian edge weights using an adaptive kernel width derived from the mean kNN distance and scaled by a global σ-scale parameter, controlling the locality of the weighting. Eigenvector centrality was computed on the resulting sparse adjacency matrix, upweighting subjects whose vertices were consistently considered to be a neighbour and therefore representative of the dominant local geometry. A vertex-wise centrality percentile threshold p was then applied, retaining only subjects above the p-th percentile, and final average vertex coordinates were computed as a centrality-weighted mean over this subset. We performed a structured parameter search over neighbourhood size (k = 10 – 200), Gaussian kernel scale (σ-scale = 0.5  – 2.0), and centrality percentile threshold (p = 50 – 100) to assess stability and quality of the resulting average surface. The unweighted Euclidean mean across all subjects served as a baseline comparator.

During testing of the centrality-weighted averaging procedure, localised spike artefacts were observed in regions where the set of selected subjects for averaging varied inconsistently across neighbouring vertices. We quantified this effect using a neighbour-agreement score, defined as the overlap of selected subject sets within each vertex’s 1-ring neighbourhood (Fig. 1B), consistent with prior observations of local surface instabilities in group averaging. Vertices with neighbour agreement below a global threshold τ were refined by replacing their centrality values with the mean centrality of their neighbours, followed by recomputation of the percentile selection and centrality-weighted average. In this work, τ = 1 was found to be optimal, enforcing full neighbourhood agreement and suppressing isolated inconsistencies without global smoothing.

Surface quality was evaluated using a Boundary-Based Registration (BBR) inspired WM/GM boundary sharpness metric5. For each surface, T1-weighted image intensities were sampled at multiple points along the surface normal within a ±0.5 mm window centred on the WM/GM boundary. A linear model was fit to each vertex-wise intensity profile as a function of distance along the normal, yielding one slope per vertex that quantified the sharpness and consistency of the local boundary transition. These slopes were then averaged across all valid vertices within each hemisphere to produce a single summary measure of WM/GM boundary alignment quality for each surface.

Results

A structured parameter search over Gaussian kernel width (σ), neighbourhood size (k), and centrality percentile threshold (p) showed multiple regions of high boundary sharpness for the eigenvector-centrality method (Fig. 2A). Larger σ values produced broader areas of good performance across k and p, indicating greater robustness to parameter choice. Based on this behaviour, k = 100, p = 90, and σ = 2.0 were selected as a representative operating point for subsequent analyses.

Volumetric slice overlays demonstrated visually that, under the selected parameters, the eigenvector-centrality–weighted surface (cyan) aligned more closely with the WM/GM boundary than the simple Euclidean mean (magenta) (Fig. 2B). The Euclidean mean showed systematic flattening relative to individual subject anatomy, whereas the centrality-weighted surface tightly followed the cortical ribbon.

Surface renderings provided a complementary view of these geometric differences (Fig. 2C). The eigenvector-centrality average preserved sulcal and gyral folding in both lateral and medial views, while the simple Euclidean average appeared oversmoothed, with attenuated curvature and reduced anatomical detail. Overlayed views highlight the shallower sulci and shorter gyri of the Euclidean mean relative to the centrality-weighted surface.

Boundary sharpness was evaluated in subject space by mapping each group-average surface back to individual subjects and calculating the BBR metric (Fig. 2D). Native subject surfaces showed the highest boundary sharpness and served as the reference. The eigenvector-centrality–weighted average was closest to this reference, while the Euclidean mean showed reduced sharpness and a narrower cross-subject distribution, consistent with geometric smoothing from vertex-wise averaging.

Conclusion

We present a centrality-weighted framework for constructing group-average cortical surfaces directly embedded in volumetric template space. By modelling cross-subject variability at each vertex as a graph and preferentially weighting well-connected subjects, the method reduces the smoothing bias inherent to Euclidean averaging. The resulting surfaces preserve cortical folding patterns, suppress local artefacts, and more closely match subject-native WM/GM boundaries. This method provides a practical approach for building surface-informed volumetric templates with consistent surface and volume geometries and will form the basis of template creation using MMORF in the future. The final aim will be to use such a template to improve cortical alignment following volumetric registration.
