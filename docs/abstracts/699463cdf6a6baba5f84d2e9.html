Introduction

Nearly half of individuals with major depressive disorder (MDD), even after recovery, experience a recurrence of depressive symptoms in their lifetime. Given high rates of recurrence, better understanding trait risk factors for MDD (present even in remission) can inform the development of more precise and effective interventions that contribute to sustained recovery. One well-established trait risk factor for MDD involves emotion regulation (ER), with individuals with MDD continuing to exhibit difficulties with ER strategy use even in remission. Broadly, ER is a motivated behavior that involves effortfully engaging ER strategies to modulate one's emotional state. Recent work has suggested that, depending on the context, engaging in greater ER effort can be either helpful or harmful.1,2 For example, it has been shown that it may be adaptive to engage in more effortful ER only when the context demands it, such as when there is a notable discrepancy between one's experienced emotion and one's desired emotion.2,3 Individuals with depression, in particular, tend to engage in effortful ER even when their experienced emotion and desired emotion are comparable.2,3 Given that the adaptiveness of effortful ER appears to be contextually-sensitive, this suggests that it may be important to consider the flexibility with which individuals adapt ER effort across contexts in clarifying mechanisms underlying successful regulation. In the present study, we thus adopt a flexibility lens in examining ER effort to help clarify how flexibility in exerting ER effort may facilitate successful ER, as well as neurocognitive correlates of this specific type of flexibility.

Methods

Using ecological momentary assessment data from 106 young adults who were healthy volunteers or individuals with remitted depression, we developed a novel, theoretically-informed, and ecologically-valid measure of ER effort flexibility. Participants were asked to complete 3 pairs of smartphone-based surveys each day for 7 days. In each "pre" survey, we assessed negative affect prior to ER strategy use. In each paired "post" survey, we assessed ER effort and perceived ER success since the "pre" survey completed approximately 30 minutes ago. We measured ER effort specifically regarding use of putatively adaptive ER strategies, such as acceptance, decentering, distraction, and reappraisal. We operationalized ER effort flexibility as the within-person slope representing total putatively adaptive ER effort that occurred in response to fluctuations in negative affect in everyday life, capturing responsiveness of ER to affective contextual demands. We examined the relationship between ER effort flexibility and perceived ER success in everyday life, specifically examining whether reduced ER effort flexibility explained less perceived ER success among individuals with remitted MDD relative to healthy volunteers. 

To clarify possible neurocognitive mechanisms underlying ER effort flexibility, we also explored neurocognitive correlates of ER effort flexibility in a subset of the sample (42 healthy volunteers and 50 individuals with remitted depression) who completed task-based fMRI. Functional neuroimaging data were collected using a Siemens 3 Tesla MAGNETON Prisma MRI Scanner, equipped with a 32-channel head coil. Specifically, whole-brain BOLD fMRI data was acquired using a T2*-weighted EPI sequence that was optimized to reduce artifacts, with the following parameters: gradient echo, single-shot sensitive; 642 acquisition matrix; echo time 22ms; and repetition time 800ms. Participants completed a Parametric Go/No-Go inhibitory control task in the scanner. The Parametric Go/No-Go Task was projected by a high-resolution LCD projector onto a screen, that was viewable by participants using an angled double mirror system, mounted on the head coil. Stimuli were presented using the IFIS system, which consists of an integrated stimulus display and experimental control package that records button presses during fMRI tasks. Participants were given a five-button response box, but only used one button to respond during the Parametric Go/No-Go Task. During this task, participants were asked to respond as quickly and accurately as possible to the letters "x", "y", and "z", while inhibiting prepotent responses to repeating letters (e.g., "x", "x") and other letters (e.g., "a", "b", ... "w"). We tested ER effort flexibility as a predictor of functional brain activation during successful inhibition trials. We also tested ER effort flexibility as a predictor of functional brain activation during successful target trials, as a comparison. We analyzed fMRI data using SPM12 using multiple regression, covarying demographic, motion, and site variables as well as depressive symptom severity and total ER effort with a voxel-level threshold of p<.001 (uncorrected) and cluster-level threshold of p<.05 (FWE-corrected).

Results

Individuals with remitted MDD demonstrated reduced ER effort flexibility in everyday life compared to healthy volunteers, such that they typically exerted less effort in putatively adaptive ER strategy use when experiencing greater negative affect than usual (B=-0.80, 95% CI = [-1.45, -0.21]). Greater ER effort flexibility related to greater perceived ER success in everyday life (B=0.79, 95% CI=[0.15, 1.88]), even after covarying depression history (B=-0.85, 95% CI = [-1.72, 0.47]) and total ER effort (B=-0.00, 95% CI = [-0.12, 0.10]). Reduced ER effort flexibility fully explained the relationship between having a history of depression and less perceived success with emotion regulation in everyday life (indirect effect: B=-0.60, 95% CI = [-1.82, -0.05]). 

During a Parametric Go/No-Go inhibitory control task, individuals with higher ER effort flexibility in everyday life showed greater activation in the right medial frontal gyrus, specifically in the right supplementary motor area (SMA), during successful inhibition trials (Figure 1). Individuals with remitted MDD showed lower activation in this right SMA region during successful inhibition relative to healthy volunteers (d=0.44, t(89.73)=2.15, p<.04). Greater activation in this right SMA region significantly related to higher ER effort flexibility in everyday life (R=0.52, p<.001), but not inhibition task performance. In contrast, during successful target trials, individuals with higher ER effort flexibility did not show differential functional activation in any brain regions.

Conclusion

We demonstrate the right SMA as a neurocognitive correlate of ER effort flexibility relevant to successful inhibition rather than attentional processes more broadly. The SMA has been suggested to play an important role in anticipating the need to exert effort, and increased SMA activation has been found as individuals prepare to exert effort for more difficult tasks.4 Additionally, transcranial magnetic stimulation of the SMA has been found to lead to physical effort appearing less effortful.5 Given that we found that individuals who exhibit greater ER effort flexibility in everyday life show greater activation in the right SMA when engaging in successful inhibition during an inhibitory control task, this suggests that the SMA may be implicated in individuals' ability to attend to contextual demands (e.g., recognizing increased negative affect in everyday life; recognizing higher cognitive demands of No-Go trials); select the most appropriate, contextually-sensitive response (e.g., increasing ER effort in response to increased negative affect; increasing cognitive effort in response to change from Go to No-Go trials); and monitor whether changes to effort are needed (e.g., reducing ER effort based on reduced negative affect; reducing cognitive effort in response to change from No-Go to Go trial). Future work can explore whether interventions to improve ER effort flexibility, such as by targeting the right SMA with neuromodulation, can increase successful ER in individuals with remitted MDD and thus reduce risk for recurrence of MDD.
