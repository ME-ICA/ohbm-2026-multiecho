Introduction

As average lifetime increases and the population ages, understanding the aging process is becoming increasingly essential, highlighting the necessity for the development of biomarkers of biological age. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) enables the estimation of “brain age”, a marker that can better reflect disease and mortality risk than chronological age. However, brain age models often show reduced performance when applied to datasets that differ in site or demographic characteristics from the training data, limiting their generalisability as biomarkers.

In this work, we evaluated four brain age models trained on the same datasets (IXI, AIBL, DLBS, GSP, NKIRSE, OASIS-1, SALD): three based on Gaussian process regression -Kalc (Kalc et al., 2024), brainageR (Hobday et al., 2022) and More (More et al., 2023)- and a simple fully convolutional network, SFCN (Peng et al., 2021). To assess their performance on unseen data, we evaluated all models on white participants from the UK Biobank (UKB).

We then investigated two strategies to improve cross-dataset generalisability, i.e. achieving high accuracy on a new site with different population or scanning characteristics as well as limited training data: i) Model stacking, which integrates predictions from multiple models, and ii) fine-tuning, which adapts a pre-trained deep neural network to a new target distribution. We developed these strategies using white participants from the UKB and evaluated their generalisation on several underrepresented UKB populations (‘Mixed’, ‘Asian or Asian British’, ‘Black or Black British’, ‘Other ethnic group’) as well as on the Latin American dataset ‘BrainLat’. The key question we explored is how well these two strategies enhance cross-dataset generalisation. As both strategies require additional data from the application site, we also investigated how much data is needed to achieve this generalisation.

Methods

We first trained all four models (Kalc, brainageR, More, and SFCN) on the same seven datasets (IXI, AIBL, DLBS, GSP, NKIRSE, OASIS-1, SALD) to predict chronological age from brain scans of healthy individuals. The trained models were evaluated using the mean absolute error (MAE) on UK Biobank White participants (N = 29,841) and the Latin American BrainLat cohort (N = 146, from Peru, Argentina, Mexico, Colombia, and Chile). 

We then compared two strategies to enhance model generalisation: stacking and fine-tuning. The stacking model learns to make the best use of the predictions of the available base models. Fine-tuning is usually applied to deep neural networks such that the weights of the last few layers are adapted. Both approaches require additional data beyond the original training set.

A two-level stacking model was applied to combine the predictions of three GPR-based brain age models (Kalc, brainageR and More). Each model employs a different preprocessing pipeline and feature space: Kalc applies CAT12-based preprocessing to extract non-linearly registered and modulated gray matter (GM) and white matter (WM) features, representing voxelwise tissue-densities after nonlinear registration which then undergo principal component analysis (PCA) and ensemble strategies. brainageR uses SPM12-based preprocessing including segmentation and normalization, deriving features from GM, WM, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) after masking, followed by PCA retaining 80% variance, the features represent tissue-class probability distributions across all three compartments (GM, WM, CSF). More focuses only on GM volume extracted with CAT12 which is smoothed using a 4mm Gaussian kernel, resampled to 4mm isotropic before undergoing PCA retaining 100% variance. To stack them, a second-level model was trained on the predictions of the base models to optimally combine their outputs. We compared random forests and ridge regression as second-level models.

For fine-tuning we used a deep learning (DL) model, the simple fully convolutional network (SFCN) (Peng et al., 2021). As an end-to-end architecture, SFCN requires minimal preprocessing: after skull stripping and bias correction, all MRIs were linearly registered to the MNI152 template. The SFCN is a lightweight 3D CNN architecture consisting of a straightforward sequence of convolutional blocks. Each block includes a 3D convolution, batch normalization, and a rectified linear unit (ReLU), with max-pooling between blocks to progressively reduce spatial resolution. The model treats age prediction as a soft classification problem, outputting a probability distribution over discretized age bins and optimized with Kullback-Leibler divergence against Gaussian-smoothed age labels, which improves stability and reduces regression bias. In order to fine-tune the deep learning architecture, all the layers were frozen except for the last one which can capture new information when trained on additional data.

To evaluate the two generalisation strategies, we split the UK Biobank white samples into a training set (N=23,879) and a held-out test set (N=5,962), stratified by age and sex. By progressively increasing the training set size we applied each strategy accordingly: ridge regression and random forest models were trained as stacking models, while the last layer of the SFCN model was fine-tuned. Performance was evaluated on the test set, repeating training 100 times to estimate variance. This setup allowed us to identify the most effective stacking approach and the number of samples required for both stacking and fine-tuning to effectively adapt to a new data distribution. Based on these findings, we then performed cross-dataset generalization experiments for the underrepresented populations from both UK Biobank and BrainLat,applying each strategy to a stratified subgroup (N=100) and evaluating performance on the remaining data (N=40–390 depending on subgroup).

Results

The four ‘out-of the box’ models were applied to new datasets (UKB white samples, UKB non white samples and BrainLat, Figure 1). Even though all four models were trained on the same data, we observed very different and inconsistent results (brainageR: MAE=15.44, Kalc: MAE=16.99, More: MAE=4.44, SFCN: MAE=3.70 for the UKB white samples, see Figure 1a). In general, More and SFCN models were more accurate. The high MAE for brainageR and Kalc could be partly due to the way these models were trained and utilised, for instance we used brainageR together with Octave instead of MATLAB in the original model. 

We evaluated two different stacking models, random forest and ridge regression, trained using the predictions of three first level models (brainageR, Kalc and More). The learning curves obtained by progressively increasing sizes of training sets provided three observations: 1) Both methods performed better than the best first level model (More model), 2) ridge regression outperformed random forest in this framework (with 100 training samples, the MAE is 3.46 for ridge regression and 3.71 for random forest), and 3) the performance plateaued approximately after 100 samples. 

Similar results were observed with fine tuning of the SFCN model which also required 100 samples to reach a plateau (MAE=3.57 after 100 samples), the MAE still decreased albeit slowly (reaching MAE=3.51 at 500 samples) but it never performed better than the ridge regression stacking model (MAE=3.46 at 100 samples).

Regarding the cross-dataset generalisation results (Figure 1d), we evaluated how well a model trained on one underrepresented UKB subgroup transfers to the other UKB subgroups that were not used during training. This setup reflects a realistic scenario in which only a small amount of data from a specific target group is available, while the model may later be applied to individuals with different demographic backgrounds.

Across all UKB subgroups, stacking showed strong cross-group generalisation. When trained on any underrepresented subgroup and tested on the remaining UKB subgroups, the stacking model consistently outperformed each individual first-level model on those unseen groups. For example, when evaluating performance on the UKB Mixed group, the More model alone achieved a MAE of 4.40, whereas stacking models trained on other UKB subgroups consistently achieved lower MAEs.

In contrast, models trained on BrainLat did not transfer well to UKB. Although they performed well on their own cohort (MAE = 4.92, compared with 6.72 for More), they showed substantially worse performance when evaluated on UKB subgroups (MAE 6.33–7.15), suggesting that site effects may dominate over demographic differences in this setting.

Overall, stacking outperformed fine-tuning both in accuracy and stability. The overall MAE was lower for stacking (4.24 vs. 4.87), and performance varied less across subgroups. For instance, when trained on the UKB Mixed subgroup and tested on the Black subgroup, stacking achieved a MAE of 2.70, compared with 3.62 for fine-tuning.

Conclusion

Overall, our results show that models trained on the exact same datasets demonstrate different performance on new samples. This is likely due to the compounded effect of data processing, training, scanner, site, and demographic differences. This confirms that out-of-the-box brain age models might not directly transfer reliably in cross-cohort settings. 

To overcome this we evaluated adaptation strategies, stacking and fine-tuning. Both provided substantial improvement in accuracy. In particular, stacking with a ridge regression systematically outperformed all first-level models and reached a stable plateau with approximately 100 samples, making it a lightweight and realistic solution. Fine-tuning the SFCN DL model led to similar gains, showing that retraining the last layer using 100 samples can help the network adjust to a new distribution. It appears that the deep learning model has less room to incorporate new information. Future work could involve retraining the last two layers to see if this gives the network more room to incorporate more distribution knowledge.

Applying these strategies to underrepresented groups from the UKB and BrainLat, we observed clear improvements compared to the non-adapted versions. This indicates that demographic and scanner differences can be compensated for, at least partially, with a small amount of representative data rather than requiring full training from scratch. Taken together, these results suggest that while a single universal brain age model is still unrealistic, simple adaptation steps (either stacking or fine-tuning) offer an effective and practical way to increase generalisation and bring brain age closer to being a robust biomarker across diverse populations and sites.
