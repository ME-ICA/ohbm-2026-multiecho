Introduction

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) is widely used in clinical and neuroscientific research, yet long acquisition times remain a limitation. Wideband MRI accelerates acquisition via simultaneous multi-slice excitation, while Single-Excitation Wideband MRI (SE-Wideband) reduces slice-dependent distortions by shifting the separation gradient to the phase-encoding direction; however, the resulting inclined acquisition leads to insufficient high-frequency k-space coverage and characteristic image blurring.^1

Although non-Cartesian zig-zag trajectories have been proposed to mitigate this blurring by improving k-space coverage, such designs rely on rapid gradient polarity switching and are difficult to integrate with standard clinical sequences and Cartesian parallel imaging frameworks. Consequently, their adoption in routine 3D anatomical MRI and other modern fast clinical sequences remains limited.

Unlike conventional undersampling artifacts, SE-Wideband blurring arises from slice-dependent phase dispersion and cannot be realistically simulated using standard MRI acceleration datasets, rendering existing public resources unsuitable for systematic evaluation of learning-based restoration methods.

To address this gap, we construct the first human brain SE-Wideband MRI dataset and perform a systematic comparison between a traditional filter-based denoising method and multiple deep learning architectures. Quantitative evaluation using the structural similarity index (SSIM) and normalized root mean square error (NRMSE), along with visual assessment, was conducted to demonstrate differences in image restoration performance. This establishes a reproducible benchmark for future development of learning-based reconstruction techniques for Wideband MRI and facilitates reproducible evaluation in this previously underexplored imaging regime.

Methods

Sequence Design and Data Acquisition

A 3D Cartesian implementation of Single-Excitation Wideband MRI (SE-Wideband) was integrated into a standard clinical 3D T1-weighted imaging sequence (Magnetization-Prepared Rapid Gradient Echo, MPRAGE) to ensure compatibility with routine clinical workflows and Cartesian GRAPPA reconstruction. In contrast to earlier SE-Wideband implementations based on non-Cartesian zig-zag trajectories and rapid gradient polarity switching, a constant separation gradient was applied throughout the readout, resulting in an inclined acquisition geometry. This design preserves native twofold acceleration while introducing characteristic Wideband blurring, which served as the primary target for image restoration in this study.

The SE-Wideband dataset was acquired as part of a government-funded brain mapping project in Taiwan. Seventy-six healthy adult participants were scanned on a Siemens 3T PRISMA system. For each subject, paired standard MPRAGE and SE-Wideband images were acquired. Standard MPRAGE images were used as anatomical references for quantitative evaluation and model training.

All procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Fu Jen Catholic University Hospital, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Preprocessing and Dataset Splitting

All images underwent preprocessing and quality control to ensure stable anatomical alignment and signal consistency. Slices with severe motion artifacts or insufficient coverage were excluded. Image reconstruction was performed using vendor-provided pipelines for standard MPRAGE and an in-house pixel-shifting algorithm for SE-Wideband to correct geometric distortions. Peripheral background regions were excluded during training and evaluation to avoid spurious intensity fluctuations.

To prevent information leakage, a strict subject-wise split was applied, with 60 subjects used for training, 8 for validation, and 8 for testing. The final split was selected to ensure balanced intensity distributions across subsets.

Deep Learning Models and Training

Because the dataset was acquired using a 3D anatomical sequence, all restoration experiments were performed in the 2D image domain. Approximately 6,000–8,000 axial slices were available per training run.

Seven commonly used image-domain architectures were selected as baseline models, including U-Net, Residual U-Net, Residual Attention U-Net, Deep Residual U-Net (DRU-Net), Transformer-based U-Net, Iterative Residual CNN (IRCNN), and Residual Denoising CNN (ResDnCNN),^2 covering a broad range of convolutional, residual, attention-based, and iterative denoising designs. In addition, lightweight versions of two advanced restoration models, NAFNet^3 and Uformer,^4 were evaluated to explore their applicability.

All models were implemented in PyTorch and trained on 256 × 256 image patches using the AdamW optimizer with early stopping. Loss configurations were selected using a ratio-sweep strategy to identify stable and representative settings without exhaustive loss-weight optimization.

Evaluation

Model performance was assessed by comparing restored images with standard MPRAGE references, raw SE-Wideband inputs, and images denoised using Block-Matching and 4D Filtering (BM4D)^5, using NRMSE and SSIM. Quantitative results were complemented by direct visual comparison across methods to evaluate artifact suppression and structural fidelity.

Results

This study evaluated SE-Wideband image restoration using a dedicated human brain dataset acquired from 76 healthy subjects with a customized Cartesian SE-Wideband MPRAGE sequence. After preprocessing and strict subject-wise splitting (60/8/8), approximately 8,000 paired axial slices were obtained for evaluation. Each pair consisted of a standard MPRAGE reference image and the corresponding SE-Wideband image affected by characteristic Wideband blurring and ringing artifacts.

Figure 1 presents a representative visual and quantitative comparison between a conventional denoising baseline and multiple deep learning–based restoration methods. The raw SE-Wideband input exhibited pronounced blurring and oscillatory artifacts along high-contrast gray–white matter boundaries. BM4D, included as a widely used traditional MRI denoising method, achieved limited improvement, reducing noise but leaving residual ringing and visibly oversmoothing anatomical structures.

In contrast, all deep learning–based methods produced substantial improvements in both visual quality and quantitative metrics. Compared with the raw SE-Wideband input (NRMSE 7.29% ± 1.14%, SSIM 0.619 ± 0.059) and BM4D (NRMSE 7.10% ± 1.13%, SSIM 0.653 ± 0.056), learning-based restorations consistently reduced NRMSE to approximately 4.9–5.1% and increased SSIM to 0.79–0.82 when averaged over the full test set. These gains reflect improved suppression of Wideband-induced artifacts while preserving anatomical contrast and structural fidelity.

Visual inspection of the cortical region of interest further illustrates these trends. While BM4D smoothing attenuated noise at the expense of edge sharpness, deep learning models effectively suppressed ringing artifacts and restored clearer gray–white matter boundaries. Differences among learning-based architectures were comparatively modest: several CNN-based models (e.g., ResDnCNN, DRUNet, and IRCNN) yielded particularly clean reconstructions with natural textures, whereas more complex attention- or transformer-based models achieved similar quantitative performance but did not consistently provide additional visual benefits.

Overall, Figure 1 demonstrates that deep learning–based restoration markedly outperforms traditional filtering for SE-Wideband MRI across both quantitative metrics and visual assessment. These results highlight the value of the proposed SE-Wideband dataset as a benchmark for systematic comparison and confirm that learning-based methods can effectively mitigate Wideband-specific artifacts that are not well addressed by conventional denoising approaches.

Conclusion

This work introduces the first dedicated human brain SE-Wideband MRI dataset and establishes a reproducible framework for benchmarking deep learning–based restoration methods. Using this dataset, we demonstrate that learning-based approaches consistently outperform both raw SE-Wideband images and a conventional denoising baseline (BM4D), achieving substantial improvements in artifact suppression and structural fidelity as reflected by lower NRMSE, higher SSIM, and visual assessment.

Across diverse architectures, performance differences were relatively modest, indicating that loss design and data characteristics play a larger role than model complexity in the SE-Wideband setting. Notably, lightweight CNN-based models already achieved competitive performance, suggesting that effective restoration does not necessarily require highly complex architectures.

Beyond quantitative improvements, these results demonstrate the practical feasibility of deep learning as a post-processing strategy for SE-Wideband MRI, allowing artifact reduction to be addressed without further modification of acquisition sequences. This preserves compatibility with standard Cartesian imaging and provides a flexible pathway toward integration with existing reconstruction pipelines.

At the same time, the present benchmark highlights open challenges when extending learning-based restoration to more complex acceleration scenarios, motivating future efforts that jointly consider Wideband blurring and parallel imaging effects. Expanding the dataset across scanners, protocols, and acceleration settings, along with physics-aware or hybrid-domain constraints, represents a promising direction for further improving robustness and generalization.

Overall, this study establishes a publicly valuable dataset and benchmark that defines current performance levels for SE-Wideband restoration and provides a foundation for continued methodological development toward clinically robust Wideband MRI reconstruction
