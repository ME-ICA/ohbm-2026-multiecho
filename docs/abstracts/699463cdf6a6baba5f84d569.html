Introduction

Habits are integral to decision-making, enabling efficient behavior with minimal deliberation, but they can become maladaptive when they override goal-directed control. Traditional accounts have linked habit formation to stimulus–response associations arising from model-free reinforcement learning (RL), in which action values are shaped by reward history. However, accumulating evidence suggests that habitual behavior may also emerge independently of value-based learning. In particular, previous work has proposed and shown that past choice frequency can influence current choice even when controlling for reward history [1][2]. In this view, choice repetition, captured by a reward-independent choice kernel (CK), can bias decisions and constitutes a frequency-based mechanism for habit formation that operates alongside and separably from RL. However, how such mechanisms are implemented neurally remains unclear.

To close this gap, we conducted a pre-registered fMRI study [3] using a modified version of the Reward Pairs task, in which reward magnitude and choice frequency were manipulated orthogonally. First, we sought to replicate and extend prior behavioral findings on frequency-based habits across different decision situations. Second, we aimed to investigate the neural mechanisms underlying frequency-driven habitual behavior.

We hypothesized that (1) behavior would be best explained by a computational model integrating RL and CK mechanisms; (2) prior choice frequency would influence current choice, including in a test phase without feedback; and (3) RL and CK signals would be associated with distinct neural circuits, with RL values reflected in ventral striatum and ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC), and CK values in dorsolateral striatum and parietal cortex.

Methods

Participants and Task Design 

Seventy-one participants completed an fMRI-adapted version of the Reward Pairs task, a decision-making paradigm designed to isolate the effects of prior choice frequency [2]. The task consisted of two phases:

Learning (2 blocks of 96 trials each): Participants made repeated, time-pressured choices between pairs of stimuli. Choice frequencies were manipulated independently of reward values (Fig. 1). For example, of two stimuli associated with a reward of 7 points, one stimulus was more often paired with a stimulus providing 9 points. This stimulus would therefore be chosen less frequently than the other 7-point stimulus, which was more often presented together with a stimulus that provided 5 points. Participants received full-information feedback about outcomes of both chosen and unchosen stimuli to control for value uncertainty.

Test (136 trials): Without feedback, participants chose between the originally trained stimulus combinations, and for the first time also between pairs of stimuli with equal reward value but different choice frequencies during learning. Compared to the original Reward Pairs task, stimuli, presentation timing, and intertrial intervals were optimized for fMRI.

Exclusion criteria

Participants were excluded from analyses if behavioral performance fell below a pre-specified accuracy threshold or if excessive head motion was observed. Excessive motion was defined as more than 20% of volumes flagged for framewise displacement exceeding 0.5 mm. This resulted in a final sample of N=60.

Behavioral Analyses 

To assess whether choice history influences behavior independently of reward learning, and to derive trial-wise latent variables for fMRI analyses, we fitted a set of computational models to participants’ choice data.

We compared five candidate models:

  1. random choice
  2. RL with stimulus value updates only based on reward history
  3. CK capturing only reward-independent effects of past choice frequency
  4. combined RL+CK model (weighted sum of 2. and 3.)
  5. variant of 4. with an additional constraint on weights

The RL model is based on the following Q-value update rule, based on rewards:

Qkt+1=Qkt+αq(rt-Qkt)

While the CK model tracks choice history via the following update rule, based on previous actions:

Hkt+1=Hkt+αH(at-Hkt)

Where:

  • k: stimulus index
  • t: trial index
  • αq/h: learning rates for Q-values and H-values
  • rt: reward received at trial t
  • at: action taken at trial t

Choice probabilities were computed using a softmax function over Q-values, H-values, or a sum of both. 

Model comparison was performed using Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). In addition, generalized linear mixed-effects models (GLME) were used to test the influence of prior choice frequency on choice behavior at the descriptive level.

Imaging

Functional data were acquired using a Philips Achieva 3T scanner. Preprocessing was performed with fMRIPrep [4] and included slice-time correction, spatial normalization, and the estimation of nuisance regressors. Volumes with framewise displacement exceeding 0.5 mm were flagged and modeled as separate nuisance regressors in first-level GLMs.

Trial-wise RL and CK values derived from the computational models were used as parametric modulators in GLMs. Parametric modulators were time-locked to the onset of the first and/or second stimulus presentations, depending on the model.

At the second level, statistical maps were thresholded using a voxel-wise cluster-forming threshold of p<0.001 (uncorrected). Statistical inference was performed using small-volume correction (SVC) with family-wise error (FWE) correction at p<0.05 within regions of interest (ROIs).

Regions of Interest

ROIs were defined a priori in accordance with our preregistration (see [3] for more details and references). 

Value-related ROIs

  • Ventral striatum

  • Ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC)

Habit/Frequency-related ROIs

  • Dorsolateral striatum

  • Motor cortex

  • Premotor cortex

  • Parietal cortex

Results

Behavioral Data 

Comparison of computational models of choice behavior favored the RL+CK model (BIC = 481.87), outperforming RL-only (BIC = 497.48) and CK-only (BIC = 718.07) models. This suggests that for most participants choices were driven by both reward learning and choice frequency. GLME analysis confirmed a significant main effect of prior choice frequency (z = 2.875, p = 0.004), replicating prior findings [2].

Imaging Results

GLM1: Pre-registered Model

In the pre-registered fMRI model (GLM1), trial-wise RL values and CK values were included as parametric modulators at the onset of both the first and second stimulus presentations across all three runs. Hypothesis-driven tests were conducted using SVC within the predefined ROIs.

For reward-based signals, RL-values were associated with activity in the ventral striatum ROI at the onset of the first stimulus (peak t=4.19, p<0.05 FWE, SVC). No significant RL-value–related effects were observed at the onset of the second stimulus or within vmPFC.

For frequency-based signals, no significant effects of CK-values were observed in the preregistered dorsolateral striatum ROI at the onset of the first stimulus. By contrast, at the onset of the second stimulus, CK-values showed significant effects within the dorsolateral striatum ROI (t=4.23, p<0.05 FWE, SVC).

GLM2: Chosen-Value Model

In a secondary fMRI model (GLM2), analyses focused on the chosen option by including only the chosen RL- and chosen CK-values as parametric modulators at the onset of the second stimulus. This model was motivated by the hypothesis that value- and frequency-based signals may be particularly relevant for the selected action.

Within this model, chosen CK-values were associated with activity in the preregistered dorsolateral striatum ROI (t=4.92, p<0.05 FWE, SVC) as well as in motor and premotor ROIs (t=4.80, p<0.05 FWE, SVC). Chosen RL-values were associated with striatal activity (t=4.99, p<0.05 FWE, SVC) but did not yield significant effects within vmPFC.

Conclusion

This pre-registered fMRI study investigated the behavioral and neural mechanisms underlying frequency-based habitual behavior using a task that orthogonally manipulated reward value and choice frequency. At the behavioral level, we replicated and extended prior findings showing that choice history influences decisions independently of reward, with computational model comparison favoring an integrated RL+CK account. These results strengthen the case for frequency-based mechanisms as a robust contributor to habitual behavior alongside reinforcement learning.

At the neural level, reward-related Q-values were associated with activity in striatal regions implicated in reinforcement learning, while frequency-related H-values were observed in dorsolateral striatum and motor-related regions, in line with preregistered hypotheses. However, not all preregistered effects were detected.

This pattern suggests that reinforcement learning and choice-frequency mechanisms may rely on partially distinct neural circuits that jointly contribute to decision making, particularly at the level of action selection. Rather than reflecting a single, unified habit system, frequency-based habits may arise from interactions between striatal and motor-related circuits that bias behavior through repeated action tendencies. In summary, by combining preregistered computational modeling with hypothesis-driven fMRI analyses, this study provides converging evidence that habitual behavior reflects the interplay of reward learning and choice repetition. These findings refine neurocomputational accounts of habit formation and motivate future work using multivariate, connectivity-based, or alternative model-based approaches to further elucidate how value- and frequency-based signals are integrated in the human brain.
