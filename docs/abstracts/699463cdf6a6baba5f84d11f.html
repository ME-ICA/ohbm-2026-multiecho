Introduction

Dystonia is a motor impairment affecting 20% of children post basal ganglia arterial ischemic stroke (CAIS).1 It is thought to arise from disrupted inhibitory processing in the sensorimotor cortex, which is reflected by changes in beta event-related desynchronization (ERD) during movement.2 In a prior MEG study during a cognitive-motor task, beta ERD was greater in both hemispheres of CAIS with no dystonia, and the non-lesioned hemisphere of CAIS with dystonia, compared to healthy controls. This finding suggested bilateral compensatory activity in children who had experienced CAIS without developing dystonia.3 Consequently, the objective of the current study is to determine if similar patterns of beta ERD occur during passive movement and explore associations with corticospinal tract (CST) microstructure. 

Methods

Diffusion MRI and MEG data were collected in a subset of CAIS and healthy control participants from a prior study.3 For the present analysis, MEG data were collected during a passive movement task to assess beta reactivity without the confounding effects of impaired motor execution. Passive finger movements were produced using a pneumatically driven device, alternating between left and right hands, with a randomised inter-movement interval to reduce the participant’s ability to anticipate the movement. MEG data were processed using the BrainWave MATLAB toolbox (https://github.com/cheynelab).4

Data were segmented into epochs time-locked to finger-lift, and co-registered to individual MRIs. Source images of beta power were then computed using a beamformer algorithm. Diffusion data were pre-processed using MRtrix3.5 Multi-shell multi-tissue constrained spherical deconvolution (MSMT-CSD) was used to generate masks for 10 subsegments of the CST, which were subsequently applied to extract DTI-derived metrics (Fig. 1). A Laterality Index (LI) was calculated as (FAL – FAU ) / (FAL + FAU ) x 100 to compare the lesioned and non-lesioned hemispheres. Positive LI values indicate higher DTI-derived metrics in the non-lesioned hemisphere. 

Results

Twenty-six participants were included: Eight CAIS with dystonia (Mean = 14.0; SD = 2.54), 8 CAIS without dystonia (Mean = 14.2; SD =3.79) and 10 age and sex-matched healthy controls (Mean = 13.6; SD =2.65). Compared to healthy controls, beta ERD amplitude during passive movement was greater in the affected hand of CAIS with dystonia (p = 0.036) and the unaffected hand of CAIS without dystonia (p = 0.030), with similar trends in other hands (Fig 2.A). Significant hemispheric asymmetry was observed for mean diffusivity across all three groups from subsegment 4 to subsegment 10 (p = 0.018 to p < 0.001) corresponding to the mid to upper portion of the CST (Fig 2.C). For fractional anisotropy, asymmetry was less pronounced, reaching significance only at subsegment 7 (p = 0.001) (Fig 2.D). 

Conclusion

Group differences in CST microstructure were consistent with motor outcomes but did not mirror functional measures of beta reactivity during passive movement. Our finding of greater beta ERD during passive movements of both hands in all CAIS groups compared to controls was unexpected and may reflect increased beta reactivity to proprioceptive input in CAIS independent of the presence of dystonia. These studies suggest that the basic function of the CST is relatively preserved in dystonia. Future studies are needed to establish the additional role of cognitive processes associated with active motor preparation in the maladaptive response of dystonia.
