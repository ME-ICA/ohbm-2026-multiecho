Introduction

Paranoia, here referring to ideas of persecution, is the belief that somebody else is going to harm you, despite the actual intent1. It is common in the general population2 and also in clinical conditions such as schizophrenia (SCZ), depression, or anxiety3, as it is considered a transdiagnostic construct. The development of paranoia could be described as a cycle, where increased attention leads to heightened awareness of external/internal stimuli, resulting in an extensive search for meaning as an attempt to interpret and understand those stimuli. For those stimuli, unusual explanations are assigned due to aberrant attribution resulting in feeling of threats. While substantial research has examined paranoia at the behavioral level, less is known about its neural correlates. 

Looking at the neural level, the triple network model (TPN) of aberrant salience is important in SCZ-research4. It postulates that increased attention to internal/external stimuli leads to a more intense search for meaning, resulting in feelings of threat. It integrates three key networks: the salience network (SN), anchored in the anterior insula (AI) and dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC); the default mode network (DMN), centered around the posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) and precuneus; and the central executive network (CEN), primarily involving the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC)4. 

Prior work has investigated TPN-connectivity in SCZ4, but to the best of our knowledge no study has investigated how TPN-node connectivity relates to paranoia across diagnoses. Therefore, the aim of this study was to examine paranoia as a transdiagnostic phenomenon, to apply the TPN model derived from SCZ-research, and to analyze the associations between paranoia and brain connectivity.

Methods

Data from three multicenter studies (IMAGEN, STRATIFY, IntegraMooDS) were combined, yielding a total sample of N = 2094. The sample included 1536 healthy individuals and participants with mental health diagnoses, including major depression (n = 151), alcohol use disorder (n = 136), eating disorders (n = 106), bipolar disorder (n = 57), schizophrenia (n = 51), and psychosis (n = 11). Paranoia was assessed using the Symptom-Checklist-90 and the Brief Symptom Inventory. First, to obtain a standardized paranoia score, data was harmonized following Neidhart et al.5, using healthy controls (N ≥ 1625) as reference cohort for z-standardization. Second, resting state functional connectivity (rsFC) data of N = 2094 was preprocessed and analyzed using ENIGMA HALFpipe and FSL 6.0 (FMRIB Software Library, Oxford, United Kingdom). Seed-based correlation analysis employed TPN nodes (AI, dACC, PCC, precuneus, dlPFC) as seeds of interests. Group-level analysis was performed investigating TPN connectivity across the brain with paranoia as the variable of interest, controlling for age, sex, and site (model a); additionally, a second model controlled for group status (healthy, MD, AUD, ED, BD, SCZ, psychosis; model b). Fisher’s z-transformation and Benjamini-Hochberg correction were applied.

Moreover, random forest (RF) regression was applied to investigate feature importance of “connectivity associations” related to paranoia (model a). The sample was split (Train: 80, Test: 20) with 200 trees, a maximal depth of 10, a minimal sample split of 5, and a random state of 42. For hyperparameter optimization GridSearchCV was applied with 5-fold cross validation. Root mean absolute error (RSME) and R2 was applied for model evaluation. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis was conducted for visualization of the best 10 features. Pearson correlation analysis between SHAP and permutation values was applied as further validation test. Additionally, the best 10 features (“connectivity associations”) were evaluated in an external dataset from the Treatment-Resistant (TRS) Biobank of N = 127 within the SCZ-spectrum, while using the “persecution” item from the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANNS) to assess paranoia. Spearman correlation analysis and linear regression was conducted to test the 10 best features from the original dataset N = 2094 in this external validation sample (N = 127).

Results

Validation checks of the behavioral data harmonization showed no change in pre- and post-scores (p > .05), while they were highly correlated (p < .01), indicating successful harmonization. Overall, paranoia was common and had an exponential distribution, whereby most of the individuals experienced low paranoia scores and few experienced high paranoia scores, consistent with findings from the general population2. Paranoia was associated both with hyper- and hypoconnectivity within and between TPN-nodes (SN, DMN, CEN) as well as other networks such as the visual network (VN), or sensorimotor network (SMN) (pFDR < 0.01, k > 10). Key regions involved the hippocampus (SN), frontal medial cortex (DMN), frontal pole (CEN), temporal fusiform cortex (VN), and precentral gyrus (SMN). Patterns of hyper- and hypoconnectivity are illustrated in Figure 1A-E. Similar associations with paranoia remained when controlling for group status (healthy, MD, AUD, ED, BD, SCZ, psychosis). 

The final RF regression model had a RSME of 1.21 with nearly 5% explained variance (R2 = .045). SHAP values and permutation values were highly correlated with each other (p < .01). SHAP analysis revealed that the 10 most important features (“connectivity associations”) related to paranoia were: PFC connectivity with the lateral occipital gyrus (LOC), inferior temporal gyrus (ITG), and Crus I; and connectivity with VIIIa and VIIIb (part of the cerebellum); PCC connectivity with the parahippocampal gyrus (PHG) and the medial frontal cortex (MFC); precuneus connectivity with the subcallosal cortex (SC) and ITG; and dACC connectivity with the middle temporal gyrus (MTG) (see Figure 1F). 

Using these 10 features as transdiagnostic biomarkers and testing them in an external dataset of N = 127 individuals within the SCZ-spectrum, we found significant associations between paranoia and dlPFC connectivity with LOC (p = -.266, pFDR < .01) and VIIIa (pFDR = .325, p < .01)). Other associations did not survive multiple correction. Regression models further validated that both brain connectivity associations (dlPFC – LOC; dlPFC – VIIIa) significantly predicted paranoia (p < .05), while age and sex were not significant.

Conclusion

Paranoia appears to be a transdiagnostic phenomenon at both behavioral and neural levels, present in the general population and across mental disorders. It was previously shown that individuals with paranoia are generally more anxious or depressed and experience more bizarre thoughts and perceptual abnormalities2. This transdiagnostic approach highlights the importance of feelings of threat across mental health conditions, as attribution styles and meaning-making shape emotional experiences and social interactions. Our findings support the TPN as a framework for investigating the neural basis of paranoia, highlighting the roles of the SN, DMN, and CEN. 

Looking at the 10 most important features (“connectivity associations”), we found that dlPFC, precuneus, PCC, and dACC may be key hubs of the TPN-nodes and show connectivity related to paranoia with: PHG, LOC, SC, Crus I, FMC, ITG, MTG, VIIIa and VIIIb (see figure 1F). These areas seem to be associated with the development of paranoia, as they are important for functions such as visual perception and processing, executive functions (e.g. memory), emotion regulation, behavior and error monitoring, or semantic processes and social cognition (see figure 1G). Consequently, how we perceive our environment has an impact on how we think and react to it. Furthermore, the external validation replicated our results and revealed that the connectivity between dlPFC and LOC or VIIIa may represent transdiagnostic biomarkers of paranoia and should be further investigated, as these regions are involved in executive functions (dlPFC), visual perception (LOC), and arousal processing (VIIIa) (see figure 1G).

Due to this cross-sectional design, studying the progression of paranoia was not possible. Further analyses should therefore incorporate longitudinal data investigating the time course of paranoia. Additionally, machine learning models can be biased. In this case, we found only a small R2. This may be because paranoia is a very specific symptom and this study adopts a transdiagnostic approach, in which paranoia is not usually incorporated into the classic diagnostic spectrum of most of the diagnoses included here.
