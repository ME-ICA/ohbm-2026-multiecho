Introduction

The glymphatic system (GS) is responsible for waste drainage that has been shown to occur via movement of fluids along the perivascular spaces. Traumatic brain injury (TBI) has been reported to disrupt GS function. A recent review of post-TBI glymphatic function (Miettinen et al., Frontiers in Neuroscience 2025) indicates that TBI has negative effects on glymphatic drainage that results in accumulation of metabolic waste products in the brain. However, findings from the human post-TBI studies of glymphatic function are inconsistent with some showing increases and some decreases in glymphatic drainage. GS disfunction has been associated with the development of cognitive impairment and increased risk of developing neurodegenerative disorders.  

Functional seizures (FS) are events that phenotypically resemble epileptic seizures but are not associated with any EEG epileptiform correlates. While associated FS psychopathology is well established, the neuropathophysiological mechanisms of FS initiation and maintenance continue to be investigated. The two-hit hypothesis indicates that childhood trauma that is associated with neuroinflammation (first hit) may be the predisposing factor, while a later stressful event e.g., TBI may be the second hit that initiates the cascade of FS development (Sharma et al., EBR 2021). TBI is frequently identified as a precipitating factor in FS.  Further, FS patients frequently experience cognitive deficits that are comparable to those present in patients with epilepsy (Van Patten et al., Lancet Psychiatry 2025).

Several non-invasive MRI-based approaches have been developed to evaluate glymphatic activity. One of them is the diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) analysis along the perivascular space (ALPS) that provides indirect evidence of glymphatic transport by assessing diffusivity along perivascular pathways. In the present study, we examine the relationship between ALPS index and cognitive performance in a large sample of patients with FS.

Methods

Participants: The analysis includes 168 participants with all data collected prospectively under IRB protocols approved by the University of Alabama at Birmingham and the Brown University/Providence, Rhode Island VA. There were 81 patients with FS after TBI (TBI-FS), 64 patients with TBI who did not develop FS (TBI-only), and 23 healthy controls (HC). Imaging: High-resolution MRI data were acquired using a Siemens MAGETOM 3T PRISMA scanner with a 64-channel head coil with the following parameters for diffusion MRI acquisition: TR=3230ms, TE=89.2ms, b-values = 1500 and 3000 s/mm2 applied respectively in 46 (b=1500) and 47 (b=3000) directions, number of b0s=6, phase-encoding direction=anterior-posterior, flip angle=78°, FOV=210x210mm,1.5mm3 voxel resolution; and for T1-weighted 3D Magnetization Prepared Rapid Acquisition with Gradient Echo (MPRAGE) acquisition: TR=2400 ms, TE=2.22ms, TI=1000 ms, flip angle=8°, FOV=240x256mm, 0.8mm3 voxel resolution. Measures: ALPS index was calculated bilaterally (left and right hemispheres) in native space, with the mean as the average of bilateral values. The Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) was available for 60 subjects (30 TBI-FS, 30 TBI-only). Scores ranged from 0-30, with higher scores indicating better cognitive performance. The Quality of Life in Epilepsy-31 (QOLIE-31) questionnaire data were available for 120 participants (62 FS, 58 TBI-only), with T-scores calculated for the Cognitive subscale and Overall quality of life. Higher T-scores indicated better perceived cognitive function and quality of life. Statistical Analysis: Demographic characteristics were compared across groups (TBI-FS vs. TBI-only vs. HC) using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with Bonferroni-corrected post-hoc pairwise comparisons. Pearson correlations were computed to examine associations between demographic variables (age, education) and mean ALPS index, both overall and within each group. To test whether correlations differed significantly between groups, Fisher's r-to-z transformation was applied. Linear mixed models (LMM) were used to examine main effects and interactions between group and demographic covariates on ALPS values, with hemisphere (right, left) as a within-subject factor and subject as a random effect. Continuous predictors were mean-centered for interpretability. To assess group differences in ALPS while controlling for age, an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) approach was implemented using LMM with age as a nuisance covariate (ALPS ~ Group + Age + Hemisphere). Estimated marginal means (EMMs) were computed for each group, representing age-adjusted ALPS values at the sample mean age. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons between groups were Bonferroni-corrected for multiple comparisons. Partial eta-squared (η²p) was calculated as an effect size measure for the group effect. Finally, group differences in cognitive measures were assessed using independent samples t-tests with Cohen's d effect sizes. Pearson correlations examined associations between ALPS index and cognitive measures, computed separately for each group and overall (combined TBI-FS and TBI-only). Fisher's z-test compared correlation coefficients between groups. All analyses were conducted in MATLAB using the Statistics and Machine Learning Toolbox, with α = 0.05.

Results

168 participants contributed valid ALPS index measurements yielding 336 observations across hemispheres. Mean ALPS values were comparable across groups, with HC participants showing slightly higher right-hemisphere values (M = 1.037, SD = 0.037) compared to left (M = 1.015, SD = 0.033). Similar patterns were observed in TBI-FS (right: M = 1.029, SD = 0.050; left: M = 1.003, SD = 0.040) and TBI-only (right: M = 1.025, SD = 0.041; left: M = 1.003, SD = 0.033) groups.

LLM was fitted to the ALPS index, with group, hemisphere, and their interaction as fixed effects and participant ID nested within hemisphere as a random effect. The model revealed no significant main effect of group, F(2, 330) = 1.12, p = 0.328, indicating no substantial between-group differences. However, a significant main effect of hemisphere emerged, F(1, 330) = 33.99, p < 0.001, suggesting an overall laterality bias favoring higher ALPS values in the right hemisphere. The group × hemisphere interaction was not significant, F(2, 330) = 0.21, p = 0.814, implying that hemispheric differences in the ALPS were not moderated by diagnostic group. The intercept term was highly significant, F(1, 330) = 114,955.00, p < 0.001, reflecting a robust overall elevation in ALPS values across the sample.

Partial eta-squared (η²p) effect sizes were computed from the Type III sums of squares. The group main effect yielded a negligible effect size (η²p = 0.007), whereas the hemisphere main effect was moderate (η²p = 0.093), highlighting its contribution to variance in ALPS values.

Bonferroni-corrected post-hoc tests for the group main effect (collapsed across hemispheres) confirmed no significant differences between HC and TBI-FS (M_diff = 0.010, 95% CI [-0.007, 0.027], p = 0.478), HC and TBI-only (M_diff = 0.012, 95% CI [-0.005, 0.030], p = 0.295), or TBI-FS and TBI-only (M_diff = 0.002, 95% CI [-0.010, 0.014], p = 1.000). For the hemisphere main effect (collapsed across groups), right-hemisphere ALPS was significantly higher than left-hemisphere (M_diff = 0.024, 95% CI [0.015, 0.033], p < 0.001).

To further examine hemispheric differences, asymmetry was quantified as the difference between right- and left-hemisphere ALPS (right minus left) for each of the 168 participants. A LLM on these asymmetry scores, with group as a fixed effect and participant ID as a random intercept, revealed no significant main effect of group, F(2, 165) = 0.21, p = 0.814. Bonferroni-corrected post-hoc tests confirmed no group differences in asymmetry: HC versus TBI-FS (M_diff = -0.004, 95% CI [-0.030, 0.022], p = 1.000), HC versus TBI-only (M_diff = 0.000, 95% CI [-0.026, 0.027], p = 1.000), and TBI-FS versus TBI-only (M_diff = 0.005, 95% CI [-0.014, 0.023], p = 1.000). The intercept for asymmetry was significant, F(1, 165) = 33.99, p < 0.001, consistent with the overall right-hemisphere bias observed in the primary model.

Table 1 presents correlations between ALPS index and cognitive measures. In the combined sample, QOLIE-31 Cognitive showed a significant negative correlation with ALPS (r = -.203, p = .026), suggesting that higher perceived cognitive function was associated with lower ALPS values. When examined by group, significant negative correlations emerged exclusively in the TBI-only group: QOLIE-31 Cognitive (r = -.362, p = .005) and QOLIE-31 Overall (r = -.404, p = .002) were both associated with ALPS. No significant correlations were observed in the TBI-FS group. MoCA scores did not correlate significantly with ALPS in either group.

Table 2 presents Fisher's z-test results comparing the ALPS-cognitive correlations between TBI-FS and TBI-only groups. The correlation between ALPS and QOLIE-31 Overall differed significantly between groups (z = 3.25, p = .001), with TBI-FS showing a non-significant positive trend (r = .178) and TBI-only showing a significant negative correlation (r = -.404). The QOLIE-31 Cognitive correlation approached significance (z = 1.94, p = .053).

Conclusion

The key findings from this analysis are:

1. Group Differences in Cognitive Function: TBI-only patients showed significantly higher cognitive scores than TBI-FS patients across all measures, with large effect sizes (d = -0.88 to -1.60). This indicates that TBI-FS patients report substantially lower perceived cognitive function and quality of life compared to TBI-only patients.

2. ALPS-Cognitive Associations in TBI: In TBI-only patients, significant negative correlations were observed between ALPS and both QOLIE-31 Cognitive (r = -.362) and QOLIE-31 Overall (r = -.404). This suggests that TBI-only patients with lower ALPS index values (potentially indicating altered perivascular space function) report better perceived cognitive function and quality of life.

3. Absence of ALPS-Cognitive Associations in TBI-FS: No significant correlations between ALPS and any cognitive measure were observed in the TBI-FS group. The direction of the QOLIE-31 Overall correlation was opposite to TBI-only (positive trend vs. negative), which was confirmed by the significant Fisher's z-test (p = .001).

4. MoCA was not associated with ALPS: The MoCA, an objective measure of general cognitive functioning, showed no significant association with ALPS in either group or in the combined group of TBI-FS and TBI-only. This is in contrast to the significant associations observed with self-reported cognitive function (QOLIE-31).

5. Differential ALPS-Cognition Relationships: The significant difference in correlation patterns between groups (Fisher's z = 3.25, p = .001 for QOLIE-31 Overall) suggests that the relationship between perivascular space function and cognitive outcomes may differ between TBI-FS and TBI-only.

 

Interpretation: The negative correlation between ALPS and self-reported cognitive function in TBI-only patients is noteworthy. Lower ALPS values, which may reflect altered diffusivity along perivascular spaces, were associated with better perceived cognitive outcomes. This relationship was absent in TBI-FS patients, suggesting distinct underlying mechanisms. The lack of association with objective cognitive testing (MoCA) while showing associations with subjective measures (QOLIE-31) warrants further investigation into the specific aspects of cognitive experience that relate to perivascular space integrity in TBI.
