Introduction

Compared to purely cortical processes, subcortical-cortical interactions remain relatively understudied. Recent work (Nazzi et al., 2025) showed that infraslow functional connectivity changes are synchronized in the cortex and the subcortex, with two ‘clusters’ of subcortical regions (comprising, respectively, hippocampus/amygdala and thalamus/basal ganglia) displaying synchronized changes in their connectivity pattern with cortical regions. We hypothesized that this coordinated cortical-subdoctoral dynamics might be related to fluctuations in arousal

Arousal, driven by ascending neuromodulatory projections that broadly innervate both the cortex and subcortical structures, exhibits periodic fluctuations on the order of seconds that are mirrored in pupil diameter and EEG vigilance indices. Simultaneous fMRI-pupillometry and fMRI-EEG studies have demonstrated that these arousal shifts significantly modulate the BOLD signal (Wong et al., 2013), driving global, low-dimensional fluctuations in brain dynamics. However, their impact on subcortical regions and cortico-cortical interaction remains relatively unexplored. Specifically, it is unclear whether the recently identified dichotomy in subcortical connectivity reflects a differential sensitivity, or different responses to arousal.

To address this gap, we leveraged high-resolution 7T fMRI and simultaneous eye-tracking data from the Human Connectome Project to test these hypotheses. 

Methods

HCP dataset. The HCP’s 7T dataset included 184 human participants, a subset of the HCP S1200 release. Resting-state functional MRI (rfMRI) data were collected over four separate 7T imaging sessions, lasting approximately 15 minutes. For our analysis, we employed REST2 recordings, with repetition time (TR) of 1s and 1.6 mm isotropic voxels, of a subsample of 88 subjects (41 females) who also had concomitant pupillometry and eyetracking data, recorded with an EyeLink device with a sampling rate of 1000 Hz.

fMRI parcellation and preprocessing. Cortical voxel-wise signals were parcellated with the Schaefer 100-7 networks atlas (Schaefer, 2018), while the Tian S4 was used for subcortical regions (Tian et al. 2020). We performed three basic additional preprocessing steps on top of the HCP’s minimal preprocessing pipeline: signals were de-trended, standardized and band-passed in the frequency band [0.0001, 0.1 Hz] with a first-order Butterworth filter. We also included linear regression of the mean global signal in the functional connectivity analyses (FC).

Pupil size preprocessing. Raw pupil area measurements were converted to pupil diameter, downsampled to 50 Hz and temporally filtered in the same frequency band of BOLD signals. Artifacts, such as blinks or false detections, were corrected using nearest-neighbor interpolation. The resulting pupil time series were synchronized with the fMRI recording by removing periods containing more than 50% missing data and standardized.

Phase-synchronization FC. To characterize dynamic functional connectivity, we computed the instantaneous phase synchronization between all pairs of brain regions (Fig. 1a). First, the preprocessed BOLD time series for each region were transformed into analytic signals using the Hilbert transform, allowing for the separation of the signal's instantaneous amplitude and instantaneous phase, which was extracted by computing the angle of the analytic signal. Instantaneous functional connectivity was then quantified as the cosine of the phase difference between region pairs at each time point. The first principal eigenvector v of the instantaneous FC matrix serves as a parsimonious representation of the phase FC structure. The temporal evolution of v across time thus captures dynamic changes in FC. We applied Principal Component Analysis to v extracting the dominant mode of cortical-subcortical connectivity evolution. 

Pupil-fMRI coupling. To investigate the temporal coupling between arousal and brain activity, we performed a lagged correlation analysis. The BOLD signal was shifted by six seconds to account for hemodynamic lag. Pearson correlation coefficients were computed between the pupil time series and the mean BOLD signal of each ROI across a range of temporal lags (from -10 to +10 seconds). The temporal relationship between arousal and brain activity was then characterized by identifying the lag at which the correlation was maximal. To improve precision beyond the sampling rate, parabolic interpolation was applied to the three points surrounding the peak of the average cross-correlation function for each ROI.

Results

Arousal and whole-brain activity. Consistently with Yang et al. (2024), we observed a robust temporal coupling between spontaneous fluctuations in pupil diameter and brain-wide activity. Pupil diameter undergoes dilation and constriction cycles with a characteristic period of ~20s, on top of slower, non-periodic variations. In Fig. 1c, these events (highlighted by red arrows corresponding cyclic minima) are aligned with cortical and subcortical BOLD signals. Pupil dilation is manifestly associated with brain-wide BOLD variations. We further explored this relationship by computing lagged cross-correlation between pupil diameter and brain networks, including both common cortical networks and two groups of subcortical regions (“SC1” comprising the thalamus and basal ganglia and “SC2” comprising the hippocampus and amygdala; Fig. 1b). Our analysis revealed that the pupil-BOLD coupling is not uniform but follows a temporal hierarchy across functional networks. We found a broad distribution of peak response latencies ranging from approximately -6 to +1 seconds. Sensory and attentional networks (e.g., Visual, Somatomotor, Dorsal Attention) generally exhibited earlier peak correlations compared to higher-order systems, while the Default Mode Network (DMN) displayed peak couplings at distinct, later temporal lags. Notably, the two subcortical clusters were found at the opposing ends of this hierarchy, as SC2 displayed the earliest peak, and SC1 the latest. 

Arousal and cortical-subcortical interactions. The dominant mode of subcortical-cortical connectivity represents a principal axis of dynamic connectivity variation: high values of the mode are associated with strong SC1-SMN and SC2-DMN coupling, while weak values of the mode are associated with SC2-SMN coupling. The mode time series closely tracked the ongoing pupil diameter throughout the recording session (Fig. 1e). As a result, large values of pupil size were consistently associated with large DMN-SC2 coupling, low values with larger SMN-SC2 coupling. This FC-pupil correlation did not just reflect the characteristic cycles, but also tracked slower variations in pupil diameter.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest a strong relationship between the functional architecture of the subcortex, brain wide (subcortical-cortical) dynamic connectivity, and arousal fluctuations. 

We confirmed previous observations of a stable association between arousal cycles (15-20 s) and sequential activity propagation through brain networks. By including subcortical regions in the analysis, we could grossly characterize this process as a ‘traveling wave’ starting in SC2 (hippocampus/amygdala), traversing the cortical hierarchy from sensory networks to the default mode network, and ending in SC1 (thalamus/basal ganglia). 

While the functional role of these events is not known, we speculate that it might be associated with shifts between a more outward-related (increasing pupil size) and a more inward-related (decreasing pupil size) mode of cognition, underlying processes of memory formation and consolidation. Consistently with this hypothesis, the FC between subcortex and cortex undergoes arousal-related fluctuations. During increasing pupil transients, the hippocampus is more strongly coupled to sensorimotor regions, while during decreasing pupil transients, it is more coupled to default mode network regions.
