Introduction

Topological maps are one of the core features of brain organization. While sensory maps, like retinotopy in visual cortex and tonotopy in auditory cortex, have been characterized in detail using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) in neurotypical adults, they have rarely - or in the case of tonotopy not at all - been studied in children. This is surprising given that the auditory system is one of the first to mature, making it particularly vulnerable to disruption early in life with potential long-term consequences for language and social development. For instance, in a valproic acid (VPA) mouse model of autism, tonotopic maps were found to be distorted with an overrepresentation of high frequency tones1. Understanding the neural mechanisms underlying atypical sound processing in neurodevelopmental disorders is crucial given it affects up to 90% of children with autism spectrum disorders (ASD) and ~50% of children with attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). Here we present the first results of tonotopic mapping using a short and engaging fMRI protocol in 6-11-year-old children with ASD and related neurodevelopmental disorders compared to typically developing (TD) children.

Methods

97 children between 6 and 11 years old participated in an ongoing fMRI study focused on better understanding the underlying mechanisms and consequences of auditory hypersensitivity. The research protocol was approved by the institutional review board of San Diego State University and parental consent and child assent were obtained prior to participation. Autism diagnoses were confirmed by a licensed clinical psychologist, supported by the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (Second Edition) and Autism Diagnostic Interview-Revised. Caregiver and teacher forms (including the Child Behavior Checklist, Conners-3, and Screen for Child Anxiety Related Disorders) were also administered. All children were reported to have normal peripheral hearing. 

For tonotopic mapping, 2x5 minutes of fMRI data were acquired on a 3T Siemens Prisma scanner at the SDSU MRI Center using a multiband EPI sequence (acceleration factor=4, TR=1125ms, TE=33ms, flip angle 65°, 2.5mm3 voxels, 56 slices, 264 volumes, including 8 dummy scans discarded during pre-processing). Upward  and downward sweeping sequences of pure tones (low-frequency extreme: 130-175 Hz; high frequency extreme: 3000-4000 Hz) were created with pure tones randomly selected from 1/2 octave wide intervals at a rate of 1 octave per 8.72s. Six upward or downward sweep sequences (48s long each) with no gap between successive sweeps were presented per fMRI run via active noise-cancelling headphones (OptoActive II, noise reduction by >30dB). Two silent child-friendly animated movies that do not feature any verbal interactions between characters were played simultaneously to increase engagement and reduce motion. MATLAB and the Psychtoolbox were used to synchronize EPI, auditory stimuli and movie onset. High-resolution T1-weighted scans (1mm3 voxel size) were also collected for each child.

MRI data were preprocessed with SPM12 and the CONN toolbox (v21), including rigid-body realignment, normalization to the Montreal Neurological Institute template, high-pass filtering (at 0.008 Hz) and nuisance regression to remove physiological and motion confounds. Images were spatially smoothed with a 6mm full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel and voxel time series converted to percent signal change. 

Following methods described by Langers et al.2, linear regression models were used to fit a cosine and sine function modelling the sinusoidal response to the sweeps. The sign of the sine function was flipped for the runs with downward sweeps. Regression coefficients for the two runs were averaged and converted to polar coordinates, reflecting the response amplitude (=√(beta2cos + beta2sin)) and frequency tuning (phase=arctan(betasin/betacos). The median amplitude and frequency tuning were then calculated for voxels in regions A1, R and RT (i.e. primary auditory cortex, as defined by Sereno et al.3), separately for the left and right hemisphere. General linear models tested for differences in frequency tuning by group (ASD, TD), age, biological sex assigned at birth (male, female) and group-by-sex interactions, separately for left and right primary auditory cortex, with mean motion during the scan added as a covariate. 

Results

Data from n=14 children (n=3 TD, n=5 ASD, n=4 other neurodevelopmental disorders) were excluded due to excessive motion during the scan (n=2) or not completing the MRI scan (n=12; two of which had counterindications for MRI). Consequently, high-quality tonotopic mapping fMRI data were available for 36 TD children (n=18 female), 27 children with ASD (n=11 female), and 20 children (n=10 female) with diagnoses of other neurodevelopmental disorders (n=14 ADHD, n=2 ODD, n=1 intellectual disability) or a family history of ASD (n=3). The three groups did not significantly differ in age, sex, gestational age at birth, or motion during the fMRI scan. Response amplitude also did not significantly differ between groups for left or right primary auditory cortex. However, there was a significant main effect of group for frequency tuning in both left (F(2,75)=4.6, p=.01) and right (F(2,75)=3.4, p=.04) primary auditory cortex (Figure 1A). Post-hoc tests showed that this was driven by higher frequency tuning in the ASD compared to the TD group (see Figure 1B). There was no significant effect of sex or sex-by-diagnosis interaction, neither were there any significant correlations with age or motion.

Conclusion

These results illustrate that tonotopic mapping is feasible in children, including those with neurodevelopmental disorders, using a short and engaging fMRI paradigm that only requires passive listening. While tonotopy has not previously been characterized in young children, the absence of an age effect was expected given critical periods for auditory cortex development established from animal and postmortem studies and reduced plasticity of the central auditory system after preschool-age (e.g. in children fitted with cochlear implants after age 74). In TD children, tonotopic maps in primary auditory cortex resembled the typical high-low-high gradient reported in adults in previous studies. The overrepresentation of high frequencies observed in children with ASD is consistent with tonotopic maps described in a VPA mouse model of autism1. In rodents, valproic acid exposure also results in atypical vocalizations5. Additional neuroimaging studies in longitudinal cohorts will be needed to understand the genetic and environmental causes of altered auditory cortex topology and its long-term consequences on higher-level auditory, language and social communication development. Importantly, while our results suggest that an overrepresentation of high frequencies in auditory cortex is significantly more common in children with ASD than in TD children, this does not imply that it is specific to ASD, particularly given the sample size of children with other neurodevelopment disorders (predominately ADHD) was limited for these analyses. Larger cohorts and/or dimensional analyses will help determine which clinical or behavioral factors are most strongly associated with altered tonotopic map development.
