Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias (ADRD) result from overlapping neurodegenerative and vascular disease processes, including the abnormal accumulation of beta-amyloid, tau, and other co-occurring proteinopathies that complicate diagnosis and treatment. While positron emission tomography (PET) and cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers can detect specific pathologies in vivo, their cost, invasiveness, and limited accessibility restrict large-scale or routine clinical use. Deep learning has demonstrated strong performance for classifying brain disorders and predicting clinically relevant outcomes from 3D T1-weighted (T1w) structural MRI. Transfer learning and related pre-training strategies have emerged as a powerful way to leverage large neuroimaging cohorts to learn generic MRI representations that can be adapted to downstream tasks with limited labeled data, such as neuropathology classification. This study addresses this gap by evaluating multiple transfer learning models for predicting autopsy-confirmed neuropathologies from T1w MRIs.

Methods

The primary dataset for our experiments consisted of a subset of the National Alzheimer’s Coordinating Center (NACC [1]) database (N=950; age: 76.01 ± 11.86, 390F/560M), with rich multimodal data including both in vivo MRI imaging and post mortem neuropathology in the same subjects.  For the task of predicting neuropathology from T1w MRI, we designed and trained a 3D convolutional neural network (CNN) with four 3×3×3 convolutional layers, 3D average pooling, instance normalization, ReLU activations, and dropout (0.5). A 3D DenseNet-121 was included as a comparison model. Both architectures were first trained from scratch for separate binary neuropathology tasks to predict amyloid-β (Aβ), tau, cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA), transactive response DNA-binding protein (TDP-43), Lewy bodies (LB), and hippocampal sclerosis (HPSC) using Adam (learning rate 1×10−4 with decay), early stopping, batch size 8, and up to 100 epochs, with performance summarized by balanced accuracy and F1 score. The same architectures were then pre-trained via supervised sex classification on UK Biobank T1w MRIs (N=49001; age: 64.69 ± 7.74, 25292F/23709M) [2] and, separately, on 3D synthetic T1w MRIs generated by a Latent Diffusion Model trained on UK Biobank [3]. Pre-trained models were subsequently fine-tuned end-to-end for each neuropathology, enabling direct comparison of training from scratch versus transfer learning, and of pre-training on real versus synthetic MRI. Fine-tuning used the same optimization scheme and early stopping criteria as the from-scratch experiments, with classification heads adapted as needed for the binary neuropathology labels, with downstream performance again evaluated using balanced accuracy and F1 score for each neuropathology.

Results

UK Biobank pretraining consistently improved T1w-based detection of multiple neuropathologies relative to training from scratch, whereas pretraining on 100k latent diffusion–generated images produced smaller or inconsistent gains. DenseNet-121 with UK Biobank initialization achieved the highest F1 scores for Lewy pathology and Amyloid, while similarly pretrained 3D CNNs yielded the best balanced accuracies for CAA and HPCS0, showing that both architecture and pretraining domain shape downstream performance. Across all models, TDP-43 and Tau remained difficult to detect, with low F1 scores despite reasonable balanced accuracies, indicating subtle T1w signatures that may require multimodal data or more pathology-specific pretraining to improve sensitivity.

Conclusion

In our neuropathology prediction task, transfer learning from a large real-world MRI cohort consistently outperformed training from scratch, while synthetic pretraining yielded only modest gains. UK Biobank–initialized DenseNet and 3D CNN models improved balanced accuracy and F1 scores for several neuropathologies, though Tau and TDP-43 remained challenging to detect, likely due to weak T1w signatures. Overall, these findings underscore both the promise and current limits of transfer learning for neuropathology detection from T1w MRI, motivating future work using multimodal imaging and pathology-specific pretraining.
