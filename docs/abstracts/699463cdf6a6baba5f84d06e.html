Introduction

Cross-species imaging frameworks are increasingly essential for linking nonhuman primate research with human brain organization, yet cerebellar comparisons remain challenging due to differences in imaging resolution, contrast, and the delineation of micro- and macrostructural features. The cerebellum plays a critical role in motor coordination, cognitive processing, and affective regulation, making it a key structure for translational neuroscience. However, systematic comparative studies are limited by methodological inconsistencies and the lack of harmonized datasets across species.

To address these challenges, we combine high-resolution multimodal 7T MRI—including mean apparent propagator (MAP)-MRI, magnetization transfer ratio (MTR), and T2-weighted imaging1,2 with matched histology from the same marmoset and macaque brains3,4 to generate detailed 3D maps of cerebellar lobules, deep nuclei, and microstructural organization. These nonhuman primate datasets are then compared with a human cerebellum segmented on a population-based MNI template, providing a framework for systematic cross-species analysis.

This integrated approach enables harmonized parcellation of cerebellar regions across primates, revealing conserved and divergent anatomical and microstructural features. The resulting cross-species atlases offer anatomically precise references that support translational and comparative studies in motor, cognitive, and systems neuroscience, and provide a foundation for linking nonhuman primate models with human brain organization in future neuroimaging research.

Methods

Nonhuman Primate MRI Acquisition
Two perfusion-fixed adult marmoset brains and one macaque brain were scanned on a 7T MRI scanner using mean apparent propagator (MAP)-MRI at 150 μm (marmoset) and 200 μm (macaque) isotropic resolution. Diffusion-weighted images were acquired with 112–256 directions across multiple b-values (bmax = 10,000 s/mm²), gradient durations δ = 6–8 ms, and diffusion times Δ = 20–28 ms. Voxelwise MAP estimation enabled the derivation of standard diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) metrics—fractional anisotropy (FA), mean/axial/radial diffusivities (MD, AD, RD)—as well as advanced MAP parameters: propagator anisotropy (PA), return-to-origin/axis/plane probabilities (RTOP, RTAP, RTPP), non-Gaussianity (NG), and non-diffusion-weighted T2 images. Fiber orientation distribution functions (fODFs)5 were also computed to capture complex microstructural organization.

 

Histology and 3D Segmentation
After MRI acquisition, brains were processed histologically using multiple stains (parvalbumin, SMI-32, AChE, Cresyl violet, ChAT, including NeuN and Prussian blue)3,4 (Fig. 1A). High-resolution digital images of stained sections were manually registered to the corresponding MRI images, enabling 3D segmentation of cerebellar lobules and deep nuclei. Ex vivo segmentations were then registered with population-based in vivo templates to create stereotaxic cerebellar atlases suitable for cross-species analyses using AFNI.

 

Human Cerebellum Templates
For the human cerebellum, the T1-weighted MNI_icbm152 template6 was used. In vivo direction-encoded color (DEC)7 volumes from the Human Connectome Project and the BigBrain dataset8 were registered to MNI space, achieving high anatomical correspondence (Fig. 1B). This allowed accurate segmentation of cerebellar lobules, fiber tracts, and deep nuclei.

 

Cross-Species Framework
Together, these multimodal datasets create a unified system for cerebellar mapping, allowing detailed comparisons of marmoset, macaque, and human cerebellar structures and supporting the development of anatomically accurate, translationally relevant cerebellar atlases.

Results

We observed pronounced interspecies differences in cerebellar MR signal characteristics, particularly within the deep cerebellar nuclei (DCN). On T2-weighted images, the DCN appeared substantially more hypointense relative to neighboring structures in macaques and humans compared with marmosets (Fig. 1C, D, G), consistent with prior studies linking T2 hypointensity to elevated iron content9,10. Perls’ Prussian blue staining of histological sections from the same specimens confirmed these observations: macaque DCN neuropil showed robust iron labeling, whereas marmosets exhibited minimal staining, indicating lower iron accumulation (Fig. 1E, F). These results reveal species-specific cerebellar iron distribution patterns, likely reflecting evolutionary differences in metabolism, development, or function. Such variations directly affect MR contrast and the interpretation of structural and functional imaging. By combining histology with high-resolution MRI, we provide a framework to understand how microstructural differences shape MR signals, enabling more accurate cross-species translational comparisons.

We present a high-resolution, cross-species cerebellar parcellation framework designed for human and nonhuman primate neuroimaging, with direct relevance to applications in structural and diffusion MRI. For marmosets, macaques, and humans, we generated species-specific atlases that provide detailed segmentation of cerebellar vermis and lobules, and the deep nuclei, accompanied by standardized anatomical labeling (Figs. 2A) and multiplanar (coronal, axial, sagittal) visualizations (Fig. 2B) optimized for integration with common neuroimaging workflows.

Using directionally encoded color (DEC) images, we further mapped fiber orientations and delineated nuclear territories within the cerebellum (Fig. 2C). Comparative analyses revealed species-specific differences in cerebellar size, lobular structure, interlobular MR contrast, and fiber-orientation patterns. Notably, macaques exhibited vermal and lobular configurations more similar to marmosets than to humans (Fig. 2A), highlighting both conserved and divergent architectural features across primates. 

To support broad neuroimaging applications in human and nonhuman primate neuroimaging, we performed affine and nonlinear registration of each atlas to individual T1-weighted datasets and population templates spanning diverse ages and sexes. The resulting alignments preserved native morphological details and allowed accurate identification of lobular and nuclear boundaries (Fig. 2D; e.g., Human Cerebellar Atlas or “HCA”). These findings confirm that standard registration pipelines can reliably accommodate cerebellar variability across species, enabling comparative, developmental, and translational analyses.

Conclusion

Our results reveal fundamental interspecies differences in cerebellar anatomy and iron distribution, particularly within the deep cerebellar nuclei, reflecting evolutionary, metabolic, and functional specializations across primates. The accompanying multi-species atlases facilitate translational and developmental neuroimaging studies, support accurate parcellation of cerebellar lobules and nuclei, and enable more precise mapping of structure–function relationships across marmosets, macaques, and humans, advancing our understanding of cerebellar organization and evolution.
