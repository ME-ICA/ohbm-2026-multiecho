Introduction

Modeling large-scale brain network fluctuations over time is essential for characterizing neural function and developing robust brain-based biomarkers. Traditional methods such as sliding-window Independent Component Analysis (ICA) and dynamic ICA have provided valuable insights into time-varying network organization, but their reliance on linear assumptions limits their ability to capture the rich nonlinear relationships that underlie neural dynamics [1]. More recent deep learning approaches, including recurrent and convolutional architectures [2], attempt to model these complexities but often face challenges while preserving spatial structure, encoding long-range temporal dependencies, and generalizing across subjects. Meanwhile, diffusion models provide an alternative by learning data distributions through iterative denoising. They provide superior generative fidelity, improved stability during training, and greater flexibility in capturing complex, multimodal relationships compared to other generative networks. In this study, we use latent diffusion models (LDM) [3] to integrate information from static ICNs and temporally resolved fMRI embeddings to generate smooth and biologically consistent network trajectories. Specifically, we explore the ability of diffusion models to (i) generate temporally smooth dynamic ICNs, (ii) improve subject-specific modeling, (iii) explore different sampling strategies, and (iv) capture nonlinear relationships in the data that traditional models fail to address.

Methods

The dataset consisted of resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) scans from 200 subjects obtained from the UK Biobank database [4]. The rs-fMRI data were processed into overlapping windows with a window size of 61 timepoints and a step size of 1, covering a total of 490 timepoints per subject. Across the 200 subjects, this preprocessing resulted in a total of 86,000 slices. The subject-specific model training used both the windowed rs-fMRI data and their corresponding intrinsic connectivity networks (ICNs). These ICNs were extracted from the preprocessed 4D rs-fMRI data using fully automated, spatially constrained independent component analysis (ICA) via the NeuroMark framework [5]. The ICA procedure utilized the NeuroMark fMRI 1.0 template, which contained 53 reproducible ICNs obtained from a 100-component blind ICA decomposition. Moreover, the ICNs were categorized into seven functional domains: subcortical (SC: 5), auditory (AUD: 2), sensorimotor (SM: 9), visual (VIS: 9), cognitive control (CC:17), default mode (DM: 7), and cerebellar (CB: 4). 

The proposed method combines a Variational Autoencoder (VAE) to learn a latent representation of static ICNs, with a latent diffusion model (LDM) conditioned on sliding-window rs-fMRI data to generate subjectspecific nonlinear dynamic ICNs. The 3D static ICNs are first encoded using a 3D convolutional VAE. The encoder extracts a latent representation for each ICN volume, capturing the essential network features in a lower-dimensional embedding space. Meanwhile, the LDM operates in the latent space of the VAE ICNs. It starts with the static ICN latent vector and integrates conditioning information from the fMRI windows. A learnable delta projection computes a window-specific adjustment for each latent ICN, which is scaled by a learnable parameter alpha and added to the static latent to produce the resulting latent vector that incorporates temporal dynamics for a specific fMRI window. Following that, the model uses cross-attention blocks to align latent ICN tokens with fMRI patch embeddings. This allows the latent ICNs to attend to relevant temporal and spatial signals in the fMRI data. Finally, the model predicts the noise component for diffusion training, allowing iterative denoising to generate high-fidelity samples. The model is trained for 100 epochs with a batch size of 16 and optimized using several complementary losses that ensure accurate ICN reconstruction, alignment with fMRI dynamics, and temporal realism. Diffusion and reconstruction losses preserve spatial fidelity, while contrastive and dynamic losses link ICN structure to global activity and enforce smooth transitions over time.

Results

The performance of the proposed transformer-based latent diffusion framework was analyzed in Fig. 2(A) by comparing subject-specific ICNs generated by the model with ICA-derived ICNs. Results from two subjects across the default mode and subcortical networks showed strong within-subject agreement, indicating accurate reconstruction of individual spatial patterns, while clear between-subject differences demonstrated preservation of subject-level variability. Fig. 2(B) further examines the effect of different sampling strategies used for ICN generation, including Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM) and Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models (DDIM). The sampling strategy in DDIM was more efficient since it generated samples in fewer time steps. However, there was a trade-off in quality when compared to DDPM, as skipping steps led to slightly lower detail and less precision. 

The temporal cosine similarity between consecutive dynamic ICNs generated by the proposed latent diffusion model was illustrated in Fig. 2(C). The similarity remained consistently high (mean=0.92+/-0.03), mostly ranging from 0.90 to 0.96, reflecting strong temporal smoothness and stability. Occasional localized decreases in similarity were also observed, indicating transient network reconfigurations, demonstrating sensitivity to meaningful temporal dynamics. Additionally, the heatmap in Fig. 2(D) showed the cosine similarity between each dynamically generated ICN window and the corresponding static ICN template. The similarity matrix was downsampled by selecting every 10th time window along both axes. Higher values indicate close resemblance to static references, while lower values reflect either gradual shifts in the spatial configuration of ICNs over time or subject-specific deviations. Overall, these visualizations confirm that the model produces temporally coherent ICNs, capturing both stable and dynamic aspects of brain connectivity.

Conclusion

The transformer-based LDMs present a powerful and efficient framework for generative modeling in neuroimaging. By performing diffusion directly in a compact latent space, this approach significantly reduces computational cost while preserving rich spatial and temporal representations of ICNs. The integrated rs-fMRI encoder effectively captures both spatial and temporal dynamics of resting-state brain activity, enabling the model to generate ICNs that exhibit realistic structural coherence and dynamic variability across time. Future work will focus on learning shared latent manifolds across subjects and conditions to capture inter-individual variability and identify potential disease-specific biomarkers.
