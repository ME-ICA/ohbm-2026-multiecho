Introduction

Latent variable approaches are increasingly used in neuroimaging to summarize structural covariance and link brain morphology to theory-based psychological constructs.^1 However, widely used reduction frameworks such as principal component analysis (PCA; included as a benchmark because it remains a common dimensionality-reduction approach in neuroimaging), exploratory factor analysis (EFA), confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), and exploratory structural equation modeling (ESEM), differ in whether they separate common from unique/error variance, permit cross-loadings, and accommodate structured residuals.^2 These differences can yield divergent dimensionality estimates and factor topographies, complicating interpretation in personality neuroscience. Here we provide a systematic, methods-focused benchmark of these frameworks for modeling theoretically defined Reinforcement Sensitivity Theory (RST) neural indicators in structural MRI. Building on a recent latent-variable application in this dataset,^3 we quantify how model choice affects (i) dimensionality detection, (ii) global and local fit, (iii) cross-loading patterns, (iv) factor reliability, and (v) robustness to total intracranial volume (TIV) preprocessing.^4

Methods

Structural T1-weighted MRI from 300 healthy adults (18–49 years) were processed with CAT12/SPM12. Gray-matter volumes were extracted from 21 a priori RST-related ROIs using the Neuromorphometrics atlas. Homotopic ROIs were parceled to reduce redundancy from high interhemispheric correlations. Indicators were analyzed in parallel in (a) non–TIV-corrected form and (b) TIV-corrected form using the power-proportion method.^4 Dimensionality was estimated using exploratory graph analysis (EGA)^5 and Horn’s parallel analysis (PA). Candidate measurement models were then fit in a hierarchical space spanning component-based and latent-variable frameworks: PCA, CFA (simple structure; cross-loadings fixed to 0), EFA (unrestricted loadings), and ESEM (EFA loadings within an SEM framework).^2 Models were specified in both theory-driven 3-factor and data-driven 4-factor forms (M1–M8). The best-performing ESEM solution was extended with a random-intercept method factor and/or correlated residuals (M9–M11) to evaluate the impact of method-related variance and local residual dependencies. Model evaluation used χ²/df, RMSEA, SRMR, CFI, TLI, and standardized expected parameter change (local misfit). Interpretability was quantified by the presence and number of non-target loadings, interfactor correlations, and composite reliability (rcom). Robustness was assessed by agreement of dimensionality and loading patterns across TIV and non–TIV datasets. For descriptive statistics, variable parceling, and internal consistency assessments (Cronbach’s alpha), IBM SPSS software (version 28; IBM Corp.) was used. PCA models were executed with Factor, while the remaining factorial approaches (i.e., CFA, EFA, and ESEM) were computed using Mplus version 8.10. Parallel analysis (PA) was conducted with the EFA.dimensions R package (version 0.1.8.4). The exploratory graph analysis (EGA) was performed using the EGAnet R package (version 2.3.0). Results

Dimensionality procedures converged on a 4-factor solution in 3 of 4 scenarios: EGA identified 4 factors for both datasets, while PA identified 4 factors for non–TIV data and 8 for TIV data, with negligible eigenvalue gaps beyond the fourth factor (Figure 2). Across modeling frameworks, the 4-factor specifications consistently improved fit relative to 3-factor specifications. PCA showed the weakest factorial clarity, yielding 6 non-target loadings in the 3-factor model and 12 in the 4-factor model. CFA improved simple-structure interpretability but fit remained inadequate (non–TIV 4-factor CFA: RMSEA=0.104, CFI=0.916, TLI=0.903), with elevated latent correlations and prominent local misfit consistent with cross-loading suppression. EFA and ESEM showed markedly better fit and more coherent loading patterns, with ESEM providing the added capacity to model structured residuals. In both datasets, the best overall solution was a 4-factor ESEM with correlated residuals (M11): non–TIV χ²(125)=289.2, χ²/df=2.3, SRMR=0.018, RMSEA=0.066, CFI=0.977, TLI=0.961; TIV χ²(124)=302.2, χ²/df=2.4, SRMR=0.029, RMSEA=0.069, CFI=0.952, TLI=0.919. Adding a method factor did not improve fit or clarity, whereas correlated residuals substantially reduced residual misfit. Composite reliability was high for the final ESEM solution (rcom=0.982 non–TIV; 0.969 TIV). Importantly, the recovered dimensionality and loading patterns were qualitatively stable across TIV preprocessing, supporting robustness of the latent structure to volumetric correction.

Conclusion

Methodological choices substantially shape the latent structures recovered from structural MRI covariance. Component-based PCA, which does not separate common from unique/error variance, produced diffuse solutions with many non-target loadings and limited interpretability. Strict CFA simple-structure models showed inadequate fit and local misfit patterns consistent with overly restrictive cross-loading constraints. Flexible exploratory latent-variable approaches (EFA/ESEM) provided superior fit and more interpretable topographies, and ESEM further enabled explicit modeling of structured residual dependence. Across dimensionality, global/local fit, interpretability, reliability, and replication across TIV and non-TIV datasets, a 4-factor ESEM with correlated residuals was the most robust measurement model. These results provide a practical benchmark for structural covariance modeling in personality neuroscience and support prioritizing ESEM (with targeted residual specification when warranted) over PCA or overly restrictive CFA when testing theory-linked neural systems.
