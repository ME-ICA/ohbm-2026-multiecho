Introduction

During development, the thalamus and cerebral cortex form a tightly coordinated system whose nuclei and regions structurally covary in shape and size. This anatomical coupling, termed structural covariance (SC), can be mapped voxelwise by measuring how strongly each thalamic and cortical voxel deviates volumetrically from the population average and statistically associating these deviations across individuals. Given the tremendously large number of pairs of thalamic and cortical voxels, matrix decomposition algorithms are often applied to derive interpretable SC components (“networks”). For instance, networks derived from independent component analysis (ICA), Principal Component Analysis (PCA), Non-negative Matrix Factorization (NMF), Orthogonal Projective NMF (OPNMF), and k-means clustering have all been used to capture structural covariance. Subsequently, their spatial overlap and modularity are shaped by each algorithm’s mathematical constraints.

SC networks are thought to capture age and sex variation and reflect both anatomical connectivity and genetic influences. However, the extent to which each of these biological variables is reflected in the networks produced by various decomposition algorithms remains unclear. As these networks are increasingly studied across healthy and disease contexts, it is critical to understand how the choice of decomposition algorithm relates to the aforementioned biological information. Here, using the Philadelphia Neurodevelopmental Cohort1 (755 typically developing subjects imaged at ages 8-22) to probe the thalamocortical system where connectivity is well understood, we quantified how SC networks derived from different algorithms reflect information on age, sex, connectivity, and gene expression. By comparing these algorithms across phenotypic, connectivity, and transcriptomic benchmarks, we provide a biological basis for selecting methods and interpreting the biological relevance of decomposition-derived SC networks.

Methods

Dataset, preprocessing and data matrices

To compute voxelwise SC, we used unbiased deformation-based morphometry (DBM)2 on T1-weighted images from 755 typically developing participants (8 to 22 years old) from the PNC. After quality controlling for motion and correcting for inhomogeneity, all images were nonlinearly registered to compute an average template, and Jacobian determinant (JD) maps were computed to index local volumetric deviations at each voxel. JD values within anatomically-defined thalamic and cortical masks were extracted and normalized by the corresponding structure volumes to reduce global size differences across individuals. The JD values were subsequently arranged into thalamic and cortical subject-by-voxel matrices. For the age and sex analysis, a joint thalamocortical subject-by-voxel matrix was used for direct integration of demographics. For connectivity and transcriptomics comparisons, a thalamic voxels × cortical voxels covariance matrix was computed by correlating the subject matrices. 

Decompositions/Clustering

ICA, PCA, NMF, OPNMF, and k-means clustering were used to derive matched sets of thalamocortical SC components. The number of components/clusters per method was chosen using repeated hold-out cross-validation. We then evaluated: 1) each algorithm’s sensitivity to age- and sex-related phenotypic variation; 2) its consistency with tractography-derived thalamocortical connectivity profiles; and 3) the degree to which SC networks align with cortical and thalamic transcriptomic profiles. 

Age and sex

We quantified how subject-wise component loadings from each decomposition relate to age separately in females and males. For each algorithm (excluding k-means clustering), we loaded the subject-by-component matrices (treating positive and negative NMF/OPNMF as separate solutions) and matched them to the demographic data. Within each sex and for each component, we fit a simple linear model and extracted the t-statistic for the age term as a signed measure of age-loading association.                                      

Connectivity

To quantify how strongly cortical regions that structurally covary with specific thalamic voxels are also structurally connected, we used the PennLINC thalamocortical tractography atlas3 as the “ground truth” and transformed all SC maps to the atlas template space. For each algorithm and each thalamic component, we created binary masks from the top 15% of the thalamic voxels, which were then used to filter atlas streamlines that either intersected with the mask (ROI condition) or terminated within it (End condition) in DSI studio.4 The resulting tract density images were converted to binary cortical maps indicating the presence or absence of streamlines at each voxel. For each thalamic component, we then quantified the agreement between its cortical SC pattern and the corresponding tract-derived cortical projection map using soft Dice similarity, sensitivity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV), computed separately for positive and negative cortical loadings and then averaged across sign. 

Transcriptomics

Using the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA) post-processed and placed in MNI-space,5 we tested the extent that thalamic and cortical components expressed similar genes. We separately normalized gene expression (17205 genes) for thalamic and cortical AHBA tissue samples, and for each component in each decomposition, computed a component-weighted average expression of each gene. We examined 1) correlations between thalamic and cortical component-weighted average expression across genes, and 2) correlations between the expression of thalamic core-matrix genes (PVALB, CALB1) across component numbers; examining these relationships as a function of decomposition algorithm using linear models. These transcriptomic investigations reveal whether genes are similarly expressed within thalamic and cortical regions that structurally covary.

Results

Age and Sex

Across algorithms, thalamocortical SC loadings showed distinct sensitivity profiles to age- and sex-related variation (Methods Figure, Panel A). PCA components displayed the strongest alignment with both factors, indicating that major PCA axes closely track prominent developmental gradients. ICA showed weaker and more spatially circumscribed associations, with only a few moderate effects, particularly in females. Standard NMF yielded modest and partly opposing associations in the positive versus negative covariance matrices, consistent with its parts-based constraints that emphasize localized deviations. OPNMF demonstrated the lowest overall sensitivity and the least sex-related differentiation, suggesting that combining non-negativity with orthogonality further regularizes the solution, producing smoother, more balanced components that are less dominated by age and sex effects.

Connectivity

SC–connectivity correspondence was modest but reproducible across methods, with clear differences in how algorithms captured tractography-defined organization (Methods Figure, Panel B). ROI-based comparisons favored k-means clustering, which showed the strongest spatial overlap and highest positive predictive value, but also the largest spread in precision across components, consistent with hard partitions that align tightly with core tracts while dropping off more sharply in less well-defined territories. OPNMF and NMF achieved slightly lower peak overlap yet combined high specificity with relatively elevated sensitivity and negative predictive value, indicating that their sparse, parts-based representations capture distributed thalamocortical patterns consistent with both streamline trajectories and termination zones. ICA showed moderate spatial overlap but stable, high precision, particularly for End-based comparisons. PCA generally occupied an intermediate regime across metrics, with reasonable agreement with tractography but less pronounced tract-aligned peaks than clustering or sparse decompositions.

Transcriptomics

Across algorithms, thalamocortical SC networks differed in their correspondence with gene expression, and the pattern depended on whether we considered the full transcriptome or a targeted core-matrix benchmark. In the whole-transcriptome analysis, networks derived from ICA and PCA showed only modest cortex–thalamus transcriptional correlations, whereas NMF, OPNMF, and especially k-means produced larger effect sizes and more components with meaningful variance explained, indicating stronger overall transcriptomic anchoring of their networks. 

When focusing on the thalamic core-matrix axis using genes CALB1 and PVALB, the ranking shifted. K-means most clearly separated core- versus matrix-like thalamic territories, with ICA and PCA showing weaker but discernible divergence between markers. By comparison, NMF and OPNMF exhibited largely flat or only subtle differences between CALB1- and PALVB-related profiles across components. This dissociation indicates that transcriptomic correspondence is not unitary: all-gene correspondence is better in methods with harder boundaries and parts-based representations, whereas CALB1 and PVALB correspondence reflects a more specific, partly overlapping core-matrix axis that is better captured by k-means and by ICA/PCA’s ability to isolate divergent signals. This pattern parallels the other benchmarks: PCA’s strong age and sex sensitivity is consistent with its components emphasizing dominant global gradients, while k-means, NMF and OPNMF, having the strongest correspondence with tractography, preferentially recover sharper, territory-like organization that aligns with connectional structure and broad, regionally coherent gene co-expression.

Conclusion

In this study, we asked: what is the biological relevance of thalamocortical SC networks and how much of it depends on the upstream algorithm? Using voxelwise deformation metrics across typically developing subjects, we addressed this question by comparing five commonly used algorithms across three biological benchmarks: individual demographics (age and sex variation), tractography-derived connectivity, and transcriptomics.

A clear algorithm-specific profile emerged. PCA components were most sensitive to age- and sex-related variation but showed only moderate alignment with tractography and transcriptional patterns, indicating that dominant PCA axes capture major developmental gradients, which might only partially overlap with underlying structural connectome. In contrast, k-means clustering showed strong tractography alignment and high transcriptomic sensitivity, implying that a substantial portion of SC is organized into relatively discrete, spatially coherent units that resemble connectivity-defined territories and transcriptomically coherent fields, but is less suited to broad, continuous demographic gradients. ICA showed modest overall transcriptomic correspondence but well separated PVALB- and CALB1-enriched patterns. Its connectivity profiles were the most stable across components, suggesting that ICA yields comparatively consistent system-level connectivity patterns even when it does not maximize overall connectivity or transcriptomic alignment.

Standard NMF and OPNMF both show strong tractography correspondence, consistent with their nonnegativity constraint producing additive, anatomically interpretable “parts” that align well with thalamocortical connectional territories; their higher sensitivity and specificity for connectivity suggests that a substantial portion of SC is modular and can be recovered with relatively little mixing across systems. NMF achieves this while permitting some overlap between components, which can accommodate shared or graded boundaries across networks, whereas OPNMF adds an orthogonality/competition constraint that forces components to be more distinct and minimally overlapping, sharpening tract-defined modules and reducing cross-talk—especially evident in its particularly robust alignment with connectivity measures.

Together, these findings argue against treating “SC networks” as interchangeable across methods. Structural covariance is a family of related signals whose biological content depends on the lens of decomposition/clustering algorithm. For research questions centered on developmental trajectories, PCA is a natural first choice. For questions concerning connectional or transcriptomic organization, different algorithms emphasize complementary aspects: k-means, OPNMF and NMF yield SC networks that align most closely with tractography, whereas k-means and ICA provide networks that are more sensitive to gene co-expression.

Finally, our results emphasize that algorithm selection in SC studies is a methodological choice with inherently biological consequences. We suggest future work to align decomposition choices with explicit biological targets and avoid cross-study comparisons that ignore algorithmic differences. By mapping these mathematical constraints onto biological readouts, this work motivates SC studies toward a more principled, question-driven use of decomposition-derived networks. Although our work focuses on volumetric thalamocortical SC and the precise balance of biological influences may differ for other structures, the general algorithm-specific profiles we observe are likely to extend in other SC networks. We hope this work can motivate a broader shift toward explicitly studying voxelwise subcortical-cortical SC networks, and towards interpreting their biological relevance in light of the specific choice of algorithm used upstream to the analyses.
