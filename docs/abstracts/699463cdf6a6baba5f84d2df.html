Introduction

Psychology rests on two complementary experimental traditions: psychological tasks (e.g., the Stroop task) that involve comparing the speed and accuracy of responses across conditions in groups of subjects, and self-report surveys (e.g., Grit scale) that aim to capture trait-level individual differences across individuals (Enkavi et al., 2019). Only one of these psychological traditions, psychological tasks, have been frequently used in  fMRI, with the other, self-report surveys, almost completely neglected. However, self-report surveys tend to be much more reliable than task-based measures (Enkavi et al., 2019). Hence, the poor reliability in task-based fMRI measurements (Elliott et al., 2020) may have resulted in part from the field focusing almost exclusively on psychological tasks to the neglect of self-report surveys. 

In the present work, we had a group of human subjects complete a set of 40 self-report survey questions within the MRI scanner. These 40 questions were chosen from 5 surveys related to the psychological construct of self-regulation: Grit Scale (8 questions), Brief Self-Control Scale (13 questions), Carstensen Future Time Perspective Scale(10 questions), Impulsive-Venturesomeness (3 questions), and the UPPS-P Impulsive Behavior Scale (6 questions). Our analysis evaluated differences in the univariate activity across surveys, which would indicate that self-report surveys are a promising and underutilized strategy for interrogating the relationship between psychological functions in the brain. 

Methods

105 participants each completed 2, 90-minute MRI sessions while completing 10 measurements that were putatively related to self-regulation: Attention Network Task, Columbia Card Sorting Task, Dot Pattern Expectancy Task, a towers task, delay-discounting task, two stop signal tasks, Stroop task, cued task-switching task, and the aforementioned 40-question self-report survey medley.  This dataset is described in (Bissett et al., 2024) and all data have been made openly available as a community resource (https://openneuro.org/datasets/ds004636/versions/1.0.4).  Here we focus exclusively on the 40-question survey medley. The fMRI data were acquired with a multi-band (8), single-echo sequence with TR = 680ms and 2.2mm isometric voxels. Additionally, subjects completed T1- and T2-weighted anatomical images. The data were quality-assured using MRIQC and pre-processed using fMRIPrep (Esteban et al., 2019). 10 subjects were removed for poor behavioral performance, poor anatomical scans, or poor functional scans. 

In the survey-medley task, each trial presented the survey question for 8500ms, followed by a blank screen for 500ms plus a variable ITI (minimum of 0, mean of 1s, max of 5s). The time series model included 41 regressors: one for each of the 40 questions and one for response time (RT)  (Mumford et al., 2024).  All regressors were boxcar regressors convolved with Nilearn’s spm hemodynamic response function and analyses used FirstLevelModel.  Onsets corresponded to the question onset times and durations were either the question duration (8500ms) or the RT.

To obtain within-subject questionnaire-specific average activation estimates, a model with 6 regressors was used: indicator regressors for each of the 5 questionnaires and a regressor indicating the number of characters in each question centered by the mean number of characters.  This produced 5 character length-adjusted questionnaire averages for each subject.

A repeated-measures ANOVA (omnibus F-test) was performed using Randomise, with subject indicator regressors and factor-effects regressors coding each questionnaire (1 for the questionnaire of interest, –1 for the baseline, 0 otherwise). TFCE-based inference was restricted to within-subject permutations to preserve dependency. All 10 pairwise two-sided paired t-tests among the five questionnaires were also run with TFCE in Randomise. Using TFCE-corrected maps at p<0.05 for the pairwise comparisons, all possible conjunctions were considered (positive and negative separately) in post hoc analyses to unpack the omnibus F-test result.

A multivariate analysis was conducted by submitting all 10 paired-difference maps to a PCA. Scree plots supported retaining four components, and the resulting component weights and spatial maps were then visually inspected.

Results

Correcting for character count differences between questions, we saw widespread activity (Figure 1 yellow) indicating significant pairwise differences between questionnaire activation estimates. Post-hoc paired t-tests across all 10 pairings were used to further understand the F-test result.  We considered all possible combinations of both sides of the 10 tests and studied all singletons and conjunctions. Of all possible singleton and conjunction results, 6 produced maps with at least one cluster with more than 600 voxels.  All 6 results reflected impulsive activation (upps and impulsive venture) greater than non-impulsive activation (brief, future time, grit).  These individual and conjunctions were: brief < upps, grit < impulsive venture, grit < upps, {brief, future time, grit} < upps (3 contrast conjunction), impulsive venture < upps and {brief, future time, grit} < {upps, impulsive venture} (6 contrast conjunction).   The map for {brief, future time, grit} < {upps, impulsive venture} is shown in the top panel of Figure 1(green regions) superimposed on the F-test result (yellow).

The multivariate result also revealed higher activation for questionnaire averages of impulsive compared to non-impulsive questionnaires in the first principal component (bottom of Figure 1).  Not shown, the second component revealed a network primarily reflecting contrasts involving impulsive venture, the third component highlights differences involving brief and grit and the fourth emphasizes contrasts involving future time and brief. All component maps are available at https://github.com/jaemonj/survey-medley-code/blob/main/analyses/questionnaire_average_multivariate/paired_test_pca.ipynb.

Conclusion

To complement the traditional strategy of running psychological tasks in fMRI, we had individuals complete questions from 5 well-validated surveys related to the construct of self regulation in the scanner. We found widespread differences in univariate activity across questionnaires, indicating that fMRI is sensitive to differences in the constructs captured in different surveys. In follow-up univariate and multivariate analyses, we found many of the voxels showed greater activity to surveys related to impulsivity than those that do not, with the activity primarily in bilateral frontal and bilateral dorsal parietal cortices, consistent with control- and attention-related engagement when responding to impulsivity-related survey questions. This work reveals that self-report surveys, which have been largely ignored in task-based fMRI, as a useful complement to traditional experimental tasks for understanding the neural underpinning of cognitive processes.
