Introduction

Anxiety and depression are leading causes of global health burden and especially prevalent in neurodevelopmental conditions, including autism. However, to pave a way for improved mental health support, we require a better understanding of the neurobiological mechanisms that underpin variations in anxiety and depression symptoms in neurodivergent individuals; and need to determine if these differ from the neurotypical population.

Methods

We integrated structural magnetic resonance imaging, genetic, and longitudinal symptom (Beck Anxiety and Depression Inventories, ~12–24 months follow-up) data from 638 individuals (279 neurotypical, 359 autistic, 6–31 years) in the AIMS LEAP cohort, with replication in an independent dataset (BrainMapASD). We initially identified patterns of variation in cortical and subcortical regional volumes associated with concurrent, changing, and future anxiety and depression symptoms across and within groups using partial least squares regression. We then investigated how these patterns related to expression signatures of genes implicated in anxiety and depression and our participants’ polygenic liability for anxiety and depression (based on the Allen Human Brain Atlas, existing genome-wide association studies, and participants’ DNA).

Results

Across groups, neuroanatomical components captured 26-37% of variance in concurrent, changing, and future anxiety and depression symptoms. Components featured brain regions associated with anxiety and depression symptoms, comprising emotion regulation and attention systems (anterior cingulate, amygdala, insula). Components also highlighted regions linked to either anxiety (vigilance/executive control regions including sensorimotor cortices, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex) or depression symptoms (self-referential processing/language regions, such as dorsomedial prefrontal cortex, superior temporal sulcus, frontal operculum). Findings replicated in BrainMapASD (|r|≤.65, pFDR≤4.92e-4). In non-autistic individuals, baseline symptoms emphasized emotion processing structures (fronto-limbic/salience networks), whereas changing and future symptoms mapped to a broader range of perceptual and cognitive networks (e.g. visual and executive control regions). By contrast, in autistic individuals, symptoms across timepoints were linked to a broad range of neural circuits throughout. Across groups, components spatially correlated with gene expression (|r|=.17-39, p≤3.39e-2) and genetic liability (|r|=.17-.39, p≤3.67e-3) to anxiety and depression. 

Conclusion

Anxiety and depression symptoms are linked to neuroanatomical patterns that vary as symptoms develop. In autism, these patterns differed, potentially due to autism-linked innate neurobiological variation and environmental shaping. Neuroanatomical patterns are shaped by both gene expression and genetic liability to anxiety and depression, suggesting they may represent aetiologically relevant neurobiological substrates through which genomic and environmental factors influence affective symptoms.
