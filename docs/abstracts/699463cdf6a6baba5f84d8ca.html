Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a heterogeneous neurodegenerative disease, marked by considerable variability in clinical presentation and underlying mechanisms. This biological and clinical diversity challenges early diagnosis, accurate prognosis, and the development of effective therapeutic strategies. Consequently, there is a need for biologically informed biomarkers that can capture disease heterogeneity. Neuroimaging has recently emerged as a promising avenue for identifying predictive factors related to ALS risk and progression, yet prognosis remains poor, and the determinants of disease progression are still insufficiently understood.1

Sulcal morphometry, which quantifies cortical folding patterns, may provide new insights into ALS. Sulcal metrics reflect the cumulative effects of cortical development, tension, and atrophy, and therefore capture both neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative processes.2 Although most research in ALS has focused on classical cortical metrics such as thickness and surface area,3 recent evidence indicates that sulcal measures may be more sensitive to biological aging and neurodegenerative change than traditional metrics, demonstrating high heritability and sensitivity to age-related processes.2 

Our recent, yet unpublished, investigation has identified significant disruptions in brain folding patterns, notably within the central sulcus (CS), that correlate strongly with clinical progression. Prior studies show that CS morphology is sensitive to developmental and sensory-motor influences,4 yet detailed modeling of CS shape in ALS has not been undertaken. The present study aims to perform longitudinal modeling of the CS and evaluate its diagnostic and prognostic significance for ALS. Our objective is to identify specific CS morphometric alterations associated with ALS and clinical deterioration over time.

Methods

Participants were enrolled across nine sites of the CALSNIC consortium,5 a multi-site initiative aimed at developing MRI-based biomarkers in ALS. Individuals were prospectively recruited and underwent standardized neurological assessments and structural MRI. ALS diagnosis followed revised El Escorial criteria. Healthy controls (HC) were excluded for neurological or major psychiatric history. Patients with primary lateral sclerosis, progressive muscular atrophy, frontotemporal dementia, or ALS-FTD were excluded from the current analyses. At baseline, 221 patients with ALS and 196 HC were included. A longitudinal subgroup of 84 ALS and 107 HC completed all three visits at baseline, four months, and eight months. 

Motor function was assessed using the ALS Functional Rating Scale-Revised (ALSFRS-R), from which the disease progression rate (DPR) was computed as (48 - ALSFRS-R)/symptom duration. Upper motor neuron (UMN) burden was quantified using a 12-point scale capturing spasticity, hyperreflexia, Babinski reflex, and ankle clonus bilaterally, and UMN and DPR values were dichotomized at the median. Cognitive status was evaluated with the Edinburgh Cognitive and Behavioral ALS Screen (ECAS), and total and subdomain scores were categorized as cognitively impaired or unimpaired using age- and education-adjusted quantile regression norms.

MRI scans were acquired using harmonized protocols on research-dedicated 3T scanners at each site. High-resolution T1-weighted anatomical scans (voxel size= 1×1×1 mm³) were used. Full imaging protocol details are reported elsewhere.5 Preprocessing included intensity inhomogeneity correction and brain segmentation using FreeSurfer (v7.4.1) (https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/). CS morphometry followed the ENIGMA-Sulci protocol (https://enigma.ini.usc.edu/) using BrainVISA (v5.1.2) (https://brainvisa.info/). 

The CS was reconstructed as a 3D mesh, with automatic detection of sulcal extremities and ridge points. Two coordinate systems were defined: an x-axis indexing longitudinal position along the sulcus (0-100) and a y-axis quantifying depth. Depth values were computed from isoparametric lines at each x-position. Curvature profiles were extracted to capture anatomical features including the hand knob and pli de passage moyen (PPFM). The CS typically exhibits an omega-like morphology, transitioning from posterior bending at the hand knob to anterior curvature, producing characteristic local minimum (L1) and maximum (L2). The region surrounding L1 corresponds to hand representation, whereas L2 and the ventral CS relate to orofacial functions. No sharply defined somatotopic boundaries exist. Sulcal profiles were smoothed using a Gaussian filter (σ²=3). Inter-individual variability in CS length was addressed by re-parametrizing profile and depth values via piecewise linear scaling to align L1 and L2 across participants (Figure 1). Quality control adhered to ENIGMA guidelines. Bilateral CS meshes were visually inspected by two independent reviewers (MS and FP) using Anatomist, with manual corrections applied when necessary.

Statistical analyses examined group differences (ALS versus HC) in CS metrics using linear mixed-effects models, controlling for age, sex, handedness, and intracranial volume as fixed effects, and recruitment site as a random factor. Interaction models tested whether associations with clinical variables differed by diagnosis, and subgroup analyses followed identical structures. Longitudinal trajectories were evaluated using mixed-effects models with subject-specific random effects and group-by-time interactions. Repeated-measures correlations were performed using the “rmcorr” package to assess associations between CS metrics and clinical measures across visits. Multiple comparisons were controlled using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR <0.05). Analyses were conducted in R (v4.4.3) using RStudio.

Results

Pointwise comparisons of CS depth demonstrated significant group differences between ALS and HC, with ALS showing reduced depth at multiple positions along both hemispheres (Figure 2A, B). Significant clusters (FDR < 0.05) were mainly observed on the left CS, before the hand knob (corresponding to trunk and proximal limb regions) and after L2 (orofacial regions). On the right CS, similar but less extensive clusters were detected, including a significant ventral region associated with bulbar function. In contrast, group differences in the sulcal profile were localized to similar regions but did not survive multiple comparison correction (Figure 2C, D).

Longitudinal analyses revealed progressive reductions in CS depth in the ALS group, with clusters located between L1 and L2 and extending toward the ventral extremity. Although these clusters did not remain significant after correction, the overall pattern indicated a consistent decline in depth over time, whereas HC showed no clear trend. When modeling the interaction between group and time, significant differences emerged beyond L2 toward the ventral CS, corresponding to regions functionally linked to bulbar and facial motor control (Figure 2E). Fewer clusters were observed in the right hemisphere, with the most prominent changes between the pli de passage moyen and L2. Longitudinal effects in the sulcal profile were limited to small clusters in the dorsal section before L1, bilaterally, and were less robust overall (Figure 2F).

Associations between CS measurements and clinical performance in the ALS group showed distinct spatial patterns. Pointwise correlations were strongest in the right hemisphere. Motor measures, including ALSFRS subscores, UMN burden, and tapping performance, were associated with CS depth in two regions: a dorsal cluster before L1 and a ventral cluster after L2. Worse motor function was associated with shallower sulcal depth. Effects in the left hemisphere were weaker and more focal. Correlation patterns for the sulcal profile were similar but smaller in spatial extent and did not survive correction. Longitudinal correlations between changes in CS depth and changes in clinical scores showed consistent relationships: progressive loss of depth in the right CS was associated with worsening motor performance, including declines in ALSFRS Total, Fine, and Gross subscores, slower tapping, and increasing UMN burden. Correlations involving the sulcal profile were less prominent and limited to small clusters.

Conclusion

This study provides the first pointwise parametrization of the CS in ALS, enabling detailed mapping of sulcal geometry along the motor cortex. By capturing localized variations along the sulcus, this approach offers new insight into region-specific cortical alterations in ALS. The most consistent finding was a reduction in CS depth, rather than changes in curvature, suggesting that sulcal depth is a sensitive morphometric marker of cortical involvement in ALS. Depth reductions were most prominent in regions associated with motor and bulbar function, and longitudinal analyses showed that these alterations progressed over time in parallel with clinical decline. These results indicate that sulcal metrics may serve as dynamic neuroimaging markers of disease progression.

Several limitations should be considered. Although the present sample represents one of the largest ALS cohorts analyzed with sulcal parametrization, longitudinal data were available only for a subgroup, potentially limiting sensitivity to subtle effects. The analysis did not stratify by genetic or onset subtype, and clinical heterogeneity may have contributed to spatial variability in sulcal alterations. Anatomical rather than functional labelling of CS landmarks may introduce minor localization imprecision, and depth estimation remains influenced by surface reconstruction and scanner differences. Furthermore, sulcal geometry alone does not fully capture underlying microstructural or functional mechanisms. Future studies combining sulcal measures with diffusion, cortical thickness, or functional imaging are needed to clarify the neurobiological processes driving these changes.

Longitudinal participants likely represented a slower-progressing clinical subset, as individuals with severe bulbar, respiratory, or behavioural symptoms are less likely to tolerate repeated imaging. Despite this bias, significant depth alterations were still observed, supporting the robustness of CS measures even in milder disease. Expanding longitudinal imaging to patients across a wider range of clinical severity will be essential to determine the full diagnostic and prognostic potential of CS morphology in ALS.

In conclusion, this study represents the first attempt to model the CS in ALS using a pointwise parametrization framework, enabling unprecedented spatial precision in characterizing sulcal morphology. By revealing localized reductions in CS depth and demonstrating their progression over time, our findings highlight sulcal depth as a sensitive marker of cortical involvement in ALS. Because these alterations align with somatotopic patterns of neurodegeneration, sulcal-based metrics hold strong potential for early diagnosis, prognosis, and disease stratification. Nevertheless, further investigations are needed to validate these markers across larger and genetically diverse cohorts, to test their applicability in preclinical or at-risk populations, and to integrate them with complementary imaging and clinical measures. Taken together, this work establishes a refined geometric framework that may advance the development of neuroimaging biomarkers for ALS.
