Introduction

Tau accumulation drives neurodegeneration and cognitive decline in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and preclinical research suggests that tau spreads transsynaptically across connected neurons, facilitated by the presence of cortical amyloid-β.1,2 We translated tau spreading models to human neuroimaging data, showing that tau pathology spreads from circumscribed epicenters to connected regions in AD, following the architecture of functional brain networks.3 To further determine whether the topology of brain networks influences tau spreading dynamics, we investigated whether functional hubs (i.e. regions with strong and extensive connections to other brain regions) accelerate tau spread in AD. Specifically, we hypothesized that more efficient communication from tau epicenters towards hubs that cross-link large-scale brain networks (connector hubs) rather than hubs that interconnect regions mainly within the same network (local hubs) accelerates amyloid-related tau accumulation and cognitive decline (Figure 1). 

Methods

We analyzed baseline amyloid-PET, longitudinal tau-PET and cognitive data from two independent cohorts spanning the AD spectrum (ADNI: n = 325; A4/LEARN: n = 165). Subject-specific tau epicenters were defined as the ten cortical regions in the Schaefer-200 (17-Networks parcellation) exhibiting the highest baseline tau-PET uptake. Functional connector and local hubs were identified using resting-state fMRI–derived network measures from cognitively normal, amyloid-negative ADNI participants to minimize disease-related confounds (n = 69). To model biologically plausible communication between tau epicenters and functional hubs, we used a high-resolution structural connectivity template from the HCP (n = 100) and quantified directed routing efficiency using a navigation-based efficiency metric.4 For each subject, we computed relative connector-hub efficiency (rCHE), defined as the difference in communication efficiency from tau epicenters to connector hubs versus local hubs. Robust regression models tested whether rCHE moderated amyloid-related effects on (i) global tau accumulation rates, (ii) downstream tau dissemination across large-scale functional networks (tau network spread), and (iii) longitudinal cognitive decline. All analyses were replicated across cohorts and controlled for age, sex and clinical diagnosis (ADNI).

Results

 Supporting our hypotheses, we found that the effect of higher baseline amyloid-PET on faster global tau-PET increases was moderated by more efficient communication of tau epicenters towards connector hubs relative to local hubs (ADNI/A4: β = 0.28/0.45, p = 2.1 x 10-6/0.01), such that subjects with higher rCHE showed an amplified effect of amyloid on global tau accumulation rates (Figure 2B). Higher rCHE also predicted higher extents of future amyloid-related tau dissemination across functional brain networks (ADNI/A4: β = 0.55/0.49, p = 0.004/0.02), and faster amyloid-driven cognitive decline (ADNI/A4: β = -0.79/-0.37, p = 0.004/0.02). All p-values were FDR-corrected.

Conclusion

Brain network topology shapes spatiotemporal tau accumulation rates and cognitive trajectories in AD. Specifically, relatively more efficient communication of subject-level tau epicenters with connector hubs amplifies amyloid-related tau accumulation. This suggests that brain network architecture has a profound modulatory impact on amyloid-driven tau aggregation and disease progression in AD.5
