Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is increasingly described as a large-scale disconnection syndrome, in which progressive cognitive decline arises from impaired coordination among distributed brain networks rather than isolated regional damage1. While molecular and structural biomarkers such as amyloid-β and tau proteins provide critical diagnostic information, they offer limited insight into how neurodegeneration disrupts large-scale brain dynamics. Electroencephalography (EEG), as a non-invasive and cost-effective technique, is sensitive to synaptic dysfunction and alterations in neural communication, but traditional EEG connectivity studies in AD largely rely on static measures that average interactions over time. Such approaches neglect the intrinsic temporal variability of functional brain networks, which is essential for adaptive cognitive processing2.
Recent work has emphasized the importance of functional connectivity dynamics (FCD) and introduced fluidity as a metric quantifying the temporal richness and flexibility of network configurations3. Altered fluidity has been observed across neurological disorders, suggesting that deviations from an optimal balance between stability and variability may reflect pathological brain states. Given the well-established slowing of EEG rhythms in AD, characterized by increased low-frequency and reduced high-frequency activity, we hypothesized that AD is associated with frequency-specific alterations in brain fluidity. Furthermore, we hypothesized that these alterations would differentially relate to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers of pathology and to cognitive impairment.

Methods

Resting-state EEG data were collected from 28 patients diagnosed with Alzheimer’s disease and 29 age-matched healthy controls. All patients underwent lumbar puncture for CSF biomarker quantification, including amyloid-β (Aβ42), total tau (tTau), and phosphorylated tau (pTau). Global cognitive function was assessed using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE).
 EEG recordings were acquired using 19 scalp electrodes and sampled at 256 Hz, with eyes closed during rest. EEG preprocessing was performed using EEGLAB and included band-pass filtering, automated artifact rejection, independent component analysis, and interpolation of bad channels. Source reconstruction was carried out in Brainstorm using a realistic boundary element head model and weighted minimum norm estimation, projecting cortical activity onto 68 regions of interest defined by the Desikan–Killiany atlas.
 Source-level signals were filtered into canonical frequency bands (theta: 4–8 Hz; alpha: 8–14 Hz; beta: 14–30 Hz; gamma: 30–40 Hz). Dynamic functional connectivity was estimated using the Phase Locking Value (PLV) computed between all region pairs within sliding time windows adapted to each frequency band4. Functional connectivity dynamics (FCD) matrices were obtained by correlating PLV matrices across consecutive windows. Brain fluidity was defined as the variance of the upper triangular elements of the FCD matrix after removing window overlap bias.
 Group differences in fluidity were assessed using non-parametric permutation testing (20,000 permutations). Associations between fluidity, CSF biomarkers, and MMSE scores were evaluated using Pearson correlation. To assess clinical relevance, a multilinear regression model predicting MMSE scores was constructed including age, sex, total tau, and beta-band fluidity as predictors, and validated using leave-one-out cross-validation. Results

Significant frequency-specific alterations in brain fluidity were observed in Alzheimer’s disease. Compared to healthy controls, AD patients showed significantly increased fluidity in the theta band (p = 0.0018), indicating greater temporal variability of slow-frequency connectivity patterns. In contrast, fluidity in the beta band was significantly reduced in AD patients relative to controls (p = 0.0006), reflecting more rigid and stereotyped fast-frequency network dynamics. No significant group differences were observed in the alpha or gamma bands (Fig. 2a).
 Correlation analyses revealed a selective association between beta-band fluidity and tau pathology. Reduced beta fluidity was significantly and negatively correlated with both total tau (ρ = −0.51, p = 0.03) and phosphorylated tau (ρ = −0.56, p = 0.01), whereas no significant correlations were found with Aβ42 levels (Fig. 2b). Theta-band fluidity did not show significant associations with either biomarkers or MMSE scores.
 Importantly, inclusion of beta-band fluidity in a multilinear regression model significantly improved the prediction of cognitive performance (Fig.2c). The final model explained 49% of the variance in MMSE scores (adjusted R² = 0.34), with beta fluidity providing an independent contribution beyond age, sex, and tau levels. These results were confirmed using leave-one-out cross-validation, demonstrating robustness and generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that Alzheimer’s disease is characterized by a frequency-dependent reorganization of large-scale brain dynamics, marked by increased flexibility at slow oscillatory timescales and reduced flexibility at faster beta rhythms. Critically, reduced beta-band fluidity is closely associated with tau-related neurodegeneration and provides additional explanatory power for cognitive impairment beyond established molecular biomarkers. These results support the conceptualization of AD as a disorder of impaired dynamic network coordination and highlight EEG-derived fluidity as a promising, non-invasive functional biomarker for tracking disease-related network dysfunction.
