Introduction

Hemispheric specialization supports efficient auditory and language processing, yet the degree and the direction of asymmetry vary widely across individuals. Focusing on language, lateralization refers to the asymmetric organization of linguistic functions across the cerebral hemispheres. Most right-handed individuals exhibit right-hemisphere dominance, 4-6% of them, and around 22-27% of left-handed individuals, show atypical patterns involving right-hemisphere or bilateral dominance.1 Auditory processing shows hemispheric specialization: the left auditory cortex is more sensitive to temporal information, while the right is more responsive to spectral features.2 Also, studies such as Villar et al.3 propose that factors as musicianship could be involved in the lateralization of language, identifying musicianship as a distinct pathway toward right-lateralized language. Musicianship, which induces structural and functional plasticity in auditory regions, may further modulate hemispheric asymmetries. However, the interplay between natural (language-related) and experience-dependent (musicianship-related) lateralization remains unclear, particularly when examined using sensitive measures of auditory cortex activity in an uncommon sample such as left-handed individuals.

Within this framework, we examined how musicianship and language lateralization could affect the auditory functional asymmetries using a sparse/sampling fMRI paradigm in left-handed individuals. For that, we quantify the auditory laterality index across key regions (Heschl’s gyrus and superior temporal gyrus (STG)) and language laterality index during the verb-generation fMRI. The study provides a test of whether auditory and language lateralization co-vary and whether the musical expertise strengthens or reorganizes hemispheric auditory dominance. 

Methods

Methods

Seventy-four left-handed participants were recruited, who were classified according to language lateralization and musicianship. Language lateralization was assessed with a verb-generation fMRI task using LI-toolbox for SPM12 (typical: LI >+40; atypical: LI < +40).4 Musicianship was defined as ≥6 years of formal training. Only left-handed were included, based on Edinburgh Handedness Inventory (EHI). Based on these measures the demographics profiles of groups are: 1) Musicians-Typical (MT): n = 13; Age = 24.1 ± 6.65; EHI = 42.4 ± 6.87; 2) Musicians-Atypical (MA): n = 11; Age M = 27.2 ± 6.57; EHI = 47.9 ± 2.3; 3) Non-musicians-Typical (NMT): n = 34; Age M = 23.9 ± 4.49; EHI = 41.7 ± 5.74; 4) Non-musicians-Atypical (NMA): n = 16; Age M = 24.3 ± 6.28; EHI = 44.2 ± 4.4.

Participants completed an fMRI session, which included a structural 3D, verb-generation task, and auditory task. The auditory task, based on the design by Robert et al.5 used a sparse-sampling fMRI protocol (TR=1000ms) in a 12-minute run with 48 blocks (24 experimental, 24 control silence). Each block presented 6 pairs of sounds: 4 combining a degraded and not-degraded version, and 2 with identical degraded stimuli, temporal or spectral degraded, depending on the condition. Each trial lasted 3000ms and contained a 1600-ms silent interval for sound presentation (600ms sound 1, 400ms ISI, 600ms sound 2).

To calculate the auditory laterality index, we computed as (L-R)/(L+R) using Percent signal change (PSC) from each hemisphere of ROIs (Heschl, anterior STG and posterior STG. A mixed-design ANOVA tested effects of Condition (Temporal/Spectral degraded), ROI, Musicianship and Language Lateralization.

Results

Results

The mixed-design ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of Condition (χ² (1) = 20.64, p<0.0001) showing that temporal degraded condition shifted lateralization toward the right hemisphere more than the Spectral degraded condition, and ROI (χ² (2) = 39.13, p<0.0001), reflecting that Heschl’s gyrus shifted lateralization toward the left hemisphere more than anterior and posterior STG. As expected, the Condition x Language x Musicianship (χ² (1) = 6.27, p=0.0123) reached significance indicating that the Condition effect was higher for MT and NMA groups than in the NMT and MA groups. Also, the ROI x Language x Musicianship reached significance (χ² (2) = 7.94, p=0.019), indicating that ROI lateralization differences related across on the combined effect of language lateralization and musicianship. Specifically, the MT, NMT, and MA groups showed greater auditory lateralization toward the left hemisphere in Heschl’s gyrus than in the anterior and posterior STG, compared to the NMA group.

Conclusion

Conclusions

When compared with previous studies with right-handers,5 left-handers show in general a similar pattern of processing of auditory stimuli in the superior temporal gyrus: spectral information is processing in the right whereas temporal information is more processed in the left. However, this similar pattern is less pronounced in left-handed atypical musicians, showing a strengthening of the mirrored manifestation of brain organization in this group.
