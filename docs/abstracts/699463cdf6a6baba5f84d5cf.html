Introduction

Suicide is a major public-health challenge and one of the leading causes of premature mortality worldwide. Major depressive disorder (MDD) is present in around half of individuals who die by suicide, and most patients with MDD experience significant suicidal ideation during the course of illness. Yet, despite extensive clinical and epidemiological work, the neural mechanisms that link depression to suicidal thinking and behavior remain only partially understood. Structural and functional imaging studies have implicated distributed abnormalities in fronto-limbic, default mode, and cognitive control networks in people with suicidality, but findings are heterogeneous and have rarely examined whole-brain network organization. Resting-state independent component analysis (ICA) and functional network connectivity (FNC) provide a data-driven way to characterize large-scale brain networks and their interactions, and may help identify network-level markers of suicide risk within MDD. Here, we applied ICA and FNC analysis to resting-state fMRI data from depressed patients with and without significant suicidality and from healthy controls, testing whether alterations in connectivity among visual, default mode, frontoparietal, semantic, insular, and sensorimotor networks were associated with suicidal ideation severity.

Methods

We analysed resting-state fMRI data from 204 adults, including patients with major depressive disorder (MDD) spanning a wide range of suicidality and age- and sex-matched healthy controls. Suicide attempt history was confirmed by a clinical interview and based on the documentation of suicide attempts in their medical records. Depressive symptom severity, perceived stress, and related clinical characteristics were assessed with standardized rating scales on the day of scanning. Resting-state fMRI data were preprocessed using a conventional pipeline (realignment, normalization, smoothing, and nuisance regression), and group independent component analysis was applied to identify large-scale resting-state networks (visual, sensorimotor, default mode, salience/insula, frontoparietal, cerebellar, subcortical, and semantic networks). For each network, we extracted power spectra as an index of within-network synchrony and computed functional network connectivity (FNC) between all pairs of components. Group differences in network synchrony and FNC were tested using analysis of covariance with age, sex, education, and medication status as covariates, followed by pairwise post-hoc contrasts between healthy controls, MDD with history of attempted suicide, and MDD with no history of attempted suicide. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied across all network-level comparisons, and exploratory correlations were performed between altered connectivity indices and clinical measures of suicidality and stress.

Results

Group ANCOVA on between-network functional connectivity revealed robust main effects of group for a distributed set of FNC pairs linking visual, somatomotor, cerebellar/brainstem, insula, default mode, and frontoparietal/semantic networks (p < 0.05, covariates: age, sex, education, medication). The strongest effects were observed for connections between early visual networks (C01/C05/C15) and higher-order systems, including the opercular network (C14), anterior temporal network (C16), posterior and core default mode networks (C24/C25), and right and left frontoparietal networks (C19/C20/C30), as well as for couplings between somatomotor networks (C09/C10), cerebellar and brainstem networks (C13/C12), and transmodal association systems.

Relative to healthy controls, both MDD groups showed a mixture of increased and decreased connectivity, but with larger deviations in the MDD with suicide attempt group. In particular, suicidal MDD patients showed more pronounced abnormalities in visual–DMN, visual–opercular, somatomotor–DMN, and DMN–frontoparietal connections than non-suicidal MDD, and also differed directly from non-suicidal MDD in several links involving anterior temporal, semantic, and cingulate/control networks. Non-suicidal MDD patients showed intermediate connectivity changes between suicidal MDD and controls for many of these pairs, suggesting a graded disruption of large-scale network integration as suicidality emerges within depression.

Within the combined MDD sample, exploratory correlation analyses indicated that clinical burden was systematically related to FNC patterns. Higher HDRS-17 scores were associated with stronger coupling between visual networks and posterior DMN and anterior temporal networks, as well as increased connectivity between visual/somatomotor systems and cerebellar/insula regions (all p < 0.05, FDR-corrected within measure). Longer illness duration was linked to altered connectivity between DMN and right frontoparietal/semantic networks, and between cerebellar/brainstem, somatomotor and frontoparietal/motor networks, consistent with a progressive reconfiguration of large-scale communication over the course of recurrent depression. Suicidal ideation severity (SSI) showed a distinct pattern: higher SSI was related to increased connectivity between anterior temporal/semantic/insula–motor networks and cingulate circuits, but decreased connectivity between posterior DMN and frontoparietal control networks. Together, these results suggest that suicidality in MDD is associated not only with categorical group differences, but also with continuous network-level signatures that track depressive severity, chronicity, and suicidal ideation.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that suicidality in MDD is linked to disrupted coordination between visual, insular, cerebellar/brainstem, default mode, and frontoparietal networks, rather than to isolated regional abnormalities. Suicidal MDD patients showed more pronounced alterations than non-suicidal patients, and network coupling within these circuits tracked depressive severity, illness duration, and suicidal ideation. These results support the view of suicide risk as a systems-level dysconnection syndrome in MDD and highlight insula–DMN, cerebellar–DMN, and DMN–frontoparietal interactions as candidate targets for future circuit-informed prediction and neuromodulation strategies.
