Introduction

Executive and memory processes related to remembering future intentions while performing unrelated ongoing activities (the “ongoing task”) are collectively known as prospective memory (PM; ^1). High working-memory (WM) load of ongoing tasks is known to affect PM processing performance negatively.^2 Previous neuroimaging studies showed partly distinct brain regions for sustained prospective memory (PM) processes but also a high overlap between WM and PM regions,^3,4 which may underly such interference effects.

This study takes a preregistered network neuroscience approach to investigate changes in the functional brain architecture in response to ongoing WM load as compared to sustained PM demands. Participants performed high- and low-load n-back WM tasks, as well as both conditions with additional zero-back PM intentions. We expect distinct patterns of functional connectivity and network architecture for PM demands, particularly in frontoparietal control networks.

Methods

We analyzed fMRI data of a sample of N = 87 healthy, young participants while they either performed a visual n-back working memory task (1-back and 2-back) alone, or together with an additional prospective intention. Mass-univariate analyses of neural activity were then conducted to show the patterns of significant spatial activation associated with WM load and PM demands. Additionally, we extracted task-specific functional connectivity matrices for each participant by extracting node-specific timeseries for 200 cortical (Schaefer) and 32 subcortical (Tian) nodes and calculating pairwise product-moment-correlations of node’s timeseries. We investigated the role of various resting state networks (RSNs) as defined by Yeo and colleagues (17-network version) by examining whether the mean network-specific functional degree and participation coefficient changes significantly in response to additional PM demands. Significance of these changes in functional architecture was calculated via a paired sample t-test comparing network-specific empirical metrics to the mean randomized network metric of a null model where it was permuted 10.000 times. P-values for RSN-specific analyses were FDR-corrected and declared significant at a level of p < 0.05.

Results

We show that 1) there is a high degree of spatial overlap between activity for WM load and PM demands, observable both in standard mass-univariate analyses as well as in whole-brain functional connectivity patterns of 200 cortical and 32 subcortical ROIs. Additionally, we employed a systems level perspective to investigate the role of the brain’s resting state networks in response to additional PM demands, which might be obscured in mass-univariate analyses. Indeed, two subnetworks of canonical RSNs significantly increase their weighted functional degree (strength of interconnectedness with other regions) in response to PM demands, namely the posterior frontoparietal subnetwork of the control network (t(86) = 3.81, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = .41; p-value FDR-corrected) and the ventral subnetwork of the default mode network (t(86) = 3.17, p = 0.02, Cohen’s d = .34; p-value FDR-corrected). We found no significant difference in resting state networks’ participation coefficients, suggesting that subsections of the control network and default mode network change their overall connectivity strength, but not their functional role in network communication in response to PM demands.

Conclusion

We present data that show a high spatial overlap between regions associated with WM load and with PM demands. This possibly reflects a similar demand structure and close conceptual relationship between WM and sustained PM processes. Nonetheless, additional PM demands revelaed substantial changes in the functional architecture of control and default mode resting state networks as indicated by the applied  network neuroscience approach of whole-brain data. These findings indicate that even for relatively short-term, focal PM demands, the brain implements specific network upregulations that go beyond mere WM processing.

We call for continued research into the neural substrate underlying prospective intentions: future research could elucidate the mechanisms involved in these specific network upregulations in response to PM demand, the influence of PM task characteristics (e.g. PM cue frequency) or how the adaptation of functional architecture changes from a lifespan perspective (e.g. alterations in old age and possible relation to age-dependent changes in behavioral performance).
