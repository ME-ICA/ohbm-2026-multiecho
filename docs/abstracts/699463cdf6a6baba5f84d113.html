Introduction

The primate cerebral neocortex supports an extraordinary cognitive repertoire, yet the spatial organization of cell types and gene expression underpinning these functions and associated behaviors remains poorly understood. A previous study1 of spatially-resolved single-cell transcriptomics (STx) in macaque neocortex revealed diverse and interesting patterns of transcriptomic cell type composition, but it was limited by the absence of integration with standardized MRI that is needed for spatially precise multimodal comparisons. Spatial transcriptomics images, as with other histological modalities, typically are presented only in the 2D ex-vivo space of the donor individual’s tissue sections. To unlock their full explanatory potential, such images need to be mapped accurately into the individual’s in-vivo MRI-derived space and then into a common coordinate framework (CCF) where they can be compared to data from other individuals and different modalities and with existing atlases. A CCF consists of a set of reference images, a coordinate system to indexing and navigation, annotations or parcellations that delineate neurobiologically meaningful areas, and an ontology containing names and other information about the areas, coordinates, and images.  Here, we build on the frameworks presented in our companion poster “Advancing Primate Common Coordinate Frameworks I: Improved NHP MRI templates and atlas”, Hayashi et al., and present (1) resting state fMRI (rs-fMRI) networks from denoised fMRI data for the improved rhesus and cynomolgus macaque CCF cohorts; (2) neocortical STx and fMRI registered with and integrated into the improved volume- and surface-based primate group average CCFs; and (3) preliminary comparisons of cell type composition to individual and group average architectonic and functional MRI.

Methods

MRI collection 

As described more fully in our companion poster, cohorts of rhesus macaques (N = 25; 17 male, aged 10.1±1.8 years), and cynomolgus macaques (N = 25; 11 male, 8.7±4.4 years) were used to generate species-specific non-human primate (NHP) group average CCFspaces. T1-weighted, T2-weighted, and BOLD contrast rs-fMRI images plus B1 transmit field maps were acquired from the anesthetized animals in a 3T human whole-body scanner using a specialized 24-channel NHP coil with previously reported acquisition parameters2.. In addition, one rhesus macaque (male, 6 years) was scanned during awake task (Retinotopy, Visual Motion, Face-Object-Body, Face Motion, Familiar Face, and Color) and resting-state ferumoxytol-weighted fMRI. Structural and functional images were obtained at 0.5 mm and 1.25 mm isotropic resolutions, respectively, to ensure accurate localization to convoluted neocortical folds.  

MRI analysis 

Structural and functional MRI for all animals were processed with modified NHP versions of the Human Connectome Project (HCP) pipelines3. Functional data were mapped onto their respective Mac25Rhesus or Mac25cyno volume and CIFTI CCF spaces with the MSMSulc pipeline. Functional timeseries were denoised using spatial ICA with semi-automated ICA-FIX component classification. On macaque 1, task-based analysis was performed, including both traditional task GLMs and retinotopic fMRI mapping. Temporal ICA was performed on macaque 1 as a model-free fMRI analysis strategy to reveal both task-related and spontaneous functional networks. 

STx  

Following the MRI sessions, the forebrain of macaque 1 was cut into coronal slabs ~4 mm thick and frozen without fixation. In each slab, one 10 um section of the entire slab face was mounted onto 1-3 2x3 cm mosaiced chips for spatially resolved in-situ single-cell mRNA sequencing using the Stereo-Seq STx platform (STOmics). Cells were segmented with the Spots-In-Space Cellpose pipeline (https://github.com/AllenInstitute/spots-in-space) and gene expression readouts obtained for each cell. Preliminary transcriptomic cell type assignments were made using MapMyCells (RRID:SCR_024672) based on the Siletti4 whole-brain hierarchical human cell type taxonomy.  

MRI-STx registration 

Photographs of the posterior unfrozen as well as anterior and posterior frozen slab faces were cropped to their cut faces and their backgrounds removed. Each slab face was registered to the donor’s individual MRI space with a semi-automated gradient-based optimization procedure that matched the gray-matter – white-matter contrast in the slab face photographs to the same contrast in the T1-weighted MRI volumes, Fig. 1A. The section plane placement was solved by optimizing a 3D linear affine and in-vivo vs ex-vivo tissue distortion was accounted for by optimizing a 2D warp field within each fitted plane. The position of the mosaiced chip or slide on the section was expressed as a 2D affine and was obtained by manual alignment. Applying the 3D Section-to-MRI plane affine, within-plane 2D warp field and section-to-chip 2D affine yielded the 3D coordinates for each cell in the individual donor’s volumetric MRI space. These positions were then transferred to the standard CCF space in two ways: to the CCF volume by applying the FNIRT-derived individual-to-CCF 3D warp field, and then to the CCF-meshed surface with a ribbon mapping procedure, Fig. 1B. These projections yielded laminar-subdivision-specific neocortical cell type densities on a reconstructed cortical surface with the CCF mesh. Once in surface CCF space, the cell type densities were interpolated between coronal sections. STx data expressed as continuous density fields on the CCF mesh can be treated analogously to MRI data; for example, its gradient can be computed, and the extent of each cell type can be compared to any atlas in the CCF space. 

Results

fMRI 

For macaque 1, retinotopic fMRI (polar angle) revealed a V1/V2 boundary that agreed closely with the D99 atlas boundary (Fig. 2B). 24 signal temporal ICA components were identified, including visual components, task-related fronto-temporal components, oral-pharyngeal and limb-related sensorimotor components, and a default mode component. In Mac25Rhesus during denoising there were on average 17.8 ± 12.1 signal components per individual and in Mac25cyno there were on average 11.2 ± 8.0 signal components per individual. Group average resting state fMRI data are available for both groups for use in spatial ICA, temporal ICA, Probabilistic Functional Modes (PFMs), and group average functional connectivity. Preliminary spatial ICA decompositions were characterized by largely symmetricfunctional networks; however, there are large numbers of non-signal sICA components, which may arise from autocorrelated unstructured noise and an unevenly sampled cortical surface mesh. 

STx 

By projecting neocortical cell type composition from the macaque 1’s section space to the surface component of the CCF space, we overlaid the areal boundaries of the CCF-registered D995 parcellation onto the cortical surface contours in the mosaiced STx sections and assigned each cell to the corresponding D99 parcel. We identified neuronal subclasses that were preferentially concentrated in functionally distinct regions of visual, motor, or insular cortex. The L4/5 IT CTX Glut subclass (Fig. 2A) was strongly enriched in area V1,as defined by CCF-registered atlas boundaries, consistent with the V1/V2 boundaries observed in gray matter myelination (T1w/T2w) and retinotopic boundaries derived from within-subject antemortem fMRI, Fig. 2B. Furthermore, the gradient (spatial derivative) of L4/5 IT CTX Glut density exhibited a pronounced ridge along the V1/V2 atlas boundary, indicating a rapid transition in the cell type composition at the border between these areas. Similarly, transcriptomic subclass IT-ET Glut ONECUT2 2 (Fig. 2C) was almost exclusive to areas F1, F2, and F3 of primary motor cortex, whereas the CLA-EPd-CTX Car3 Glut subclass was selectively found in the granular and dysgranular insular cortex, Fig. 2D. More broadly, we report inhibitory and excitatory neuronal subclass proportions across the cortex, grouped into 15 regions composed of CCF-registered D99 parcels presumed to be functionally and transcriptomically similar. Notably, primary visual cortex and allocortical regions exhibit the greatest differentiation in neuronal composition relative to other cortical areas.  

Conclusion

While the primate CCFs presented here in and in our companion poster “Advancing Primate Common Coordinate Frameworks I: Improved NHP MRI templates and atlases”, Hayashi et al., are presented as resources, their potential applications are myriad and justbeginning to be explored. The precise registration of ex vivo rhesus macaque spatial transcriptomic data to the in vivo Primate CCFs demonstrated here represent an initial step toward addressing many long-standing neuroscientific questions. For example, is the functional specialization of cortical areas underpinned more by interareal cell type composition differences or by differences in connectivity among and within cell assemblies of similar composition, and what sets of genes are contributing to thedifferentiation of novel cortical areas in recent human evolution? Our preliminary findings already support the hypothesis that areas with distinct cortical architecture exhibit more pronounced differences in transcriptomic cell type composition than regions that differ in functional specializations but share more similar cortical architecture. The work presented here is currently limited to rhesus and cynomolgus macaque, but parallel efforts are underway to apply similar methodology to common marmoset and human, thereby enabling a deeper evolutionary perspective on the interplay between genetics, structure, and function across the primate brains. Taken together, the primate CCFs and associated joint MRI-STx approaches provide a solid foundation for bridging microscale cellular and molecular neuroscience with mesoscale systems and cognitive brain neuroimaging.
