Introduction

The Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA) consists of 3702 tissue samples from which genome-wide gene expression has been quantified. These samples are annotated by the brain region from which they were dissected, and these annotations are derived from a hierarchical nomenclature of regions (“human brain atlas”/“HBA” ontology) [1]. While the AHBA is widely used in neuroimaging due to these samples being placed into a standard (MNI) space, previous works have reported that MNI coordinate-based locations of these samples are inaccurate [2], and a common preprocessing step involves filtering out samples for which coordinate-location doesn’t broadly match dissection structure [3]. 

Surprisingly, the anatomical definitions of regions within the HBA ontology are not available on MRI data, preventing sample coordinate validation and filtering through matching MNI-space location with reported dissection structure. Instead, structures are defined within a closely-related ontology that includes a developmental axis, and are made available in relation to an MNI-space template as part of the Allen Human Reference Atlas [4]. This ontology (“developing human brain atlas”; “DHBA”) names and hierarchically organizes structures differently as compared to the HBA ontology but contains many regions which are nearly identical in spatial definition, based on histological segmentations for which both are available. 

Here, through manual slice-by-slice comparison of structure boundaries in both atlases, we first determine region equivalency across multiple levels of the hierarchies. Then, using these equivalencies, we produce HBA segmentations on an MNI-space template by retaining matching labels from within the Allen Human Reference Atlas and manually segmenting the rest. We demonstrate the utility of this resource by providing pre-filtered lists of AHBA samples where coordinate-location matches the annotated dissection structure, across multiple levels of granularity within the AHBA hierarchy. 

Methods

Equivalency mapping:

We constructed a label-equivalency mapping between the AHBA [1] and the DHBA [4], enabling consistent cross-ontology annotation for downstream comparative analyses (Figure 1). The DHBA provides segmentations of 141 brain regions on the MNI ICBM152 symmetric 2009b MRI template but organizes regions under a distinct tree-based ontology. We first identified overlapping structures already defined in both resources based on name and position in the ontologies alone, resulting in a set of shared structures that could be retained. For the remaining structures, we then performed a structured visual comparison of structure spatial boundaries (available in 2D for both reference atlases via the Allen Institute’s online interactive atlas viewer) using each atlas’ hierarchical tree. Starting at the finest anatomical level, we reviewed each structure to determine whether labels matched in shape and location across a substantial portion of images; structures with substantially similar boundaries across >40 slices were considered matches. When no match was evident at this level, we iteratively reassigned the DHBA label to successive parent structures and repeated comparisons until a match was identified or no suitable equivalent remained. Matched full names and acronyms were recorded in a spreadsheet. Using these correspondences, we relabeled the Allen Human Reference Atlas (i.e. DHBA labels on an MNI space template) to HBA equivalents using structures for which a DHBA-HBA equivalence could be made, flagged regions without HBA counterparts as missing and removed these, and examined related sub-structures to expand coverage when partial correspondence was observed using MRI-guided contextual checks. This intermediate mapping was the starting point of our downstream manual segmentation.

Manual Segmentation:

Following the identification of equivalent regions, retention, and HBA-relabeling of cortical and subcortical structures (e.g., caudate head/body, hypothalamus, piriform cortex, cerebellum), we proceeded with manual segmentation of remaining structures (i.e. those for which equivalencies were not found) on the MNI ICBM152 asymmetric 2009b template in ITK-SNAP. Boundaries were delineated by jointly inspecting T1-weighted, T2-weighted, and proton density images (and comparing these to 2D slices of the AHBA). Co-location of regional boundaries with folding patterns in both the AHBA and on the MNI template provided confidence in regional segmentations. Retained labels (when available from the equivalency mapping) were used only as a starting point and were subject to further refinement whenever AHBA-MRI disagreements were observed (e.g. for boundaries separating inferior frontal gyrus subregions). To support consistency, we performed continuity checks across adjacent slices and cross-plane verification (including sagittal checks) for ambiguous boundaries. When fine micro-parcellations could not be distinguished at the working resolution/contracts, labels were consolidated to a higher-order parent structure, and non-discernable structures were documented and omitted. To manage effort while preserving coverage, segmentation was restricted to the left hemisphere and carried out at a 2 mm between-slice resolution.

Sample filtering:

We used CIC coordinates remapped to grey matter, as described in [2] and shown to be the most accurate representation of sample coordinate-location in MNI space. Out of the 3702 samples, we retained only those for which reported CIC coordinates of samples placed them into an AHRA structure that was equivalent (based on our equivalency mapping) to the reported dissected structure.

Results

Equivalencies were identified between 659/1839 structures in the HBA and 74/3317 structures in the DHBA, across 7 (levels 3-9) and 6 (levels 5-10) levels of structures in the two hierarchical ontologies respectively. Some of these were many-to-many mappings, and many high-level fine structures in the HBA were matched to coarser structures in the DHBA, resulting in an equivalency table of 758 matches (rows). The Allen Human Reference Atlas consists of 141 labels associated with the DHBA and mapped onto the MNI ICBM152 nonlinear symmetric 2009b template. Of these, 68 labels were identified with labels within the HBA and retained. AHRA labels with no matching HBA labels were removed, and manual segmentation was carried out using this partial set of labels. 

Across the MNI-template (coronal slices 68 to 413 corresponding to anterior-posterior world coordinates -100 to 72.5 mm) we completed manual segmentation on one hemisphere (left) using a tiered sampling strategy. We initially segmented every slice from 413-270 (144 slices). Given the high resolution of the ICBM152 2009b template (0.5 mm isotropic resolution), we observed very small differences between slices. Therefore, we proceeded to segment every other slice from 270-262 (4 slices), and finally every fourth slice from 262-68 (~48 slices). In total, this yields ~196 manually segmented slices, with denser coverage in anterior segments of the brain and sparse sampling posteriorly to manage workload. During refinement, 21 anatomical regions required new labels, because they were absent from the computed label set, including the caudate head/body, basal forebrain, hypothalamus, piriform cortex, hippocampal formation, cerebellum, and several insular/temporal subdivisions. Further revisions reduced atlas-template mismatches (notably in frontal/temporal areas) and improved cross-slice boundary consistency. In summary, we have produced labels for HBA-defined regions on an MNI-space template (Figure 2), at a resolution of 0.5 mm x 0.5 mm (in plane) x 2 mm (and higher in anterior regions of the brain).

Finally, we demonstrate the utility of these resources by using the label equivalency mappings to produce filtered AHBA sample sets, retaining only tissue samples in which the coordinate-based locations match the reported dissection structure. Of the 3702 tissue samples in the AHBA, 1942 samples were placed within regions (AHRA) equivalent to their reported dissection structure (HBA). These represent a presumably more accurate list of samples for use in neuroimaging-transcriptomic studies, and are made openly available for use. 

Conclusion

Although the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA) is widely used in neuroimaging, a major difficulty in the integration of postmortem gene expression data into a neuroimaging context is the lack of a direct link between the anatomical location sampled in individual donor brains and their equivalent positions within neuroimaging spaces. Various coordinates have been reported for these tissue samples in relation to multiple MNI space templates, but as these are derived from image registration of donor brains to (often unrepresentative) templates, MNI coordinate-based descriptions can be erroneous [2]. To improve reliability of AHBA data, many suggest removing samples for which reported dissection structure does not match coordinate-based location [3], a step that has only been possible for coarsely defined structures (e.g. dissection structure: cortex; sample coordinate in subcortex) because of the unavailability of MRI-based labels of HBA structures. 

We have produced a resource that overcomes this limitation. This resource, openly available for use, includes: 1) label equivalencies between structures defined within the Allen Institute’s “Human Brain Atlas” (HBA) and “Developing Human Brain Atlas” (DHBA) ontologies, based on name, position, and spatial extents of labels in these ontologies, 2) segmentations of the HBA on the MNI ICBM152 nonlinear asymmetric 2009b template, and 3) filtered lists of AHBA tissue samples, for use in neuroimaging-transcriptomics studies.
