Introduction

Recovery of language skills in individuals with aphasia following stroke varies widely among individuals, and this variability is thought to relate to differences in the brain’s capacity to reorganize after injury. Longitudinal neuroimaging studies have provided insight into the dynamic contributions of left- and right-hemisphere regions during recovery, but the specific mechanisms that support better recovery remain unclear.1-3 In particular, the role of the right hemisphere in recovery continues to be debated, and there is still uncertainty about whether up- or down-regulation of perilesional and contralateral regions is associated with better language outcomes. Most current research on these mechanisms relies on functional MRI (fMRI), and often excludes many people with aphasia due to cost, medical contraindications, anxiety, or difficulty performing tasks inside the MRI scanner. 

We aimed to address this gap by using functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), a more accessible neuroimaging tool, to assess task-based cortical activity in individuals with aphasia. Gilmore et al.3 showed feasibility of using fNIRS to assess brain activation during language task in older adults and six people with aphasia. They found that both perilesional and contralateral regions were activated, including left superior frontal gyrus and left supramarginal gyrus during a semantic judgment task; and left superior frontal gyrus and right precentral gyrus during a picture naming task. Recently, Meier et al.4 examined resting-state functional connectivity using fNIRS in people with aphasia and found that right-hemisphere intra-hemispheric connectivity, as well as inter-hemispheric connectivity, was associated with better language skills. 

Our goal was to examine patterns of brain activity during multiple language tasks to establish typical activation patterns in older adults and to compare those to a larger group of people with aphasia than in previous studies.

Methods

2.1 Participants: Inclusion criteria for individuals with aphasia were aphasia due to left-hemisphere stroke per medical record, no premorbid history of neurological impairment, and adequate vision and hearing for study completion. Inclusion criteria for age-matched controls were no premorbid history of neurological impairment, adequate vision and hearing for study completion, and no cognitive impairment per self-report. Participants were 16 individuals with chronic (i.e., greater than six months) post-stroke aphasia and 21 age-matched control participants.

 

2.2 Behavioral Assessment: The individuals with aphasia completed the Western Aphasia Battery – Revised Part 15 to assess overall aphasia severity.

 

2.3 Experimental Tasks: Experimental tasks were confrontation naming to assess expressive verbal output and semantic feature judgment to assess receptive lexical-semantic skills. In the confrontation naming task, participants were asked to either name common pictured objects (experimental condition) or say “pass” when seeing a scrambled picture (control condition) presented on a computer screen in blocks of five randomized items with a three-second response period per item. The task was presented in two runs, with each run lasting approximately seven minutes and including seven experimental blocks and seven control blocks presented in pseudo-random order. Responses were audio-recorded for later review. In the semantic feature judgment task, participants were asked to decide by button press whether a written semantic feature matched a pictured object (experimental condition) or decide by button press whether a scrambled picture was in color or black and white (control condition) in blocks of three randomized items with a five-second response period per item. The task was presented in two runs, with each run lasting approximately seven minutes and including seven experimental blocks and seven control blocks presented in pseudo-random order (See Figure 1 for visual of experimental task design).

2.4 fNIRS Data Collection and Processing: fNIRS data was collected in a quiet room with the participant seated at a computer screen using a NIRx NIRSport2. Head measurements were taken prior to cap placement to ensure proper cap size. The probe included 44 long-distance channels covering bilateral frontotemporal regions and eight short separation channels. FNIRS data was processed in MATLAB using Homer3, including 1) removal of channels with SNR < 5; 2) conversion of raw data to optical density; 3) motion correction using a spline interpolation method; 4) bandpass filtering to remove signal unrelated to neural activity; 5) conversion of optical density to concentration units; and 6) estimation of the hemodynamic response function using a generalized linear model. The generalized linear model took into account regression of signal from the scalp unrelated to the neural response collected from short separation channels.

 

2.5 Statistical Analysis: Statistical analysis was completed in R v4.3.2. Channel-level data was averaged into four broad ROIs (bilateral inferior frontal gyrus (IFG) and bilateral temporal). Estimated HbO values from 0 s (stimulus onset) to 20 seconds (5 seconds after stimulus offset) were averaged into four five-second epochs across conditions and groups. Then, a separate linear regression model for each region of interest was fit to predict HbO as a function of the three-way interaction of group, condition, and epoch and lower order terms of that interaction. No three-way interactions were significant and so epoch was removed from the interaction with the models then including the two-way condition by group interaction. When this interaction was not significant, it was also removed from the models. Significant interactions were followed up with pairwise testing using the emmeans package in R.

Results

3.1 Behavioral Performance: Individuals with aphasia (11 males, 5 females) were on average 64 years old (SD = 7) and 6 years (SD = 6) post-stroke. Average WAB-R Aphasia Quotient score in the individuals with aphasia was 78.4 (SD = 12.6), indicative of mild aphasia on average. Age-matched control participants (8 males, 13 females) were on average 69 years old (SD = 7). 

In the confrontation naming task experimental condition, mean naming accuracy was 61% (SD = 31) for individuals with aphasia and 98% (SD = 1.7) for age-matched controls. In the confrontation naming task control condition, mean task accuracy was 99% (SD = 3.6) for individuals with aphasia and 100% (SD = .62) for age-matched controls.

            In the semantic feature judgment task experimental condition, mean semantic feature judgment accuracy was 93% (SD = 9.1) for the individuals with aphasia and 99% (SD = 1.8) for the age-matched controls. In the semantic feature judgment task control condition, mean accuracy was 89% for the individuals with aphasia (SD = 18) and 95% (SD = 8) for the age-matched controls.

3.2 Confrontation Naming Cortical Responses: For confrontation naming, there were significant group by condition interactions in the left IFG, left temporal, and right temporal ROIs (all p < .05). Follow-up pairwise testing revealed a condition effect in all three ROIs in the people with aphasia (experimental condition HbO greater than control condition HbO; all p < .05) that was not present in the age-matched controls (all p > .05). In the right IFG, there was a simple effect of epoch with greater HbO in Epochs 2 and 3 relative to Epoch 1 (all p < .05; see Figure 2, Panel A for epoch-averaged cortical response across groups and conditions for the confrontation naming task).

 

3.3 Semantic Feature Judgment Cortical Responses: For semantic feature judgement, there was a significant group by condition interaction in the left IFG (p = .027). Follow-up pairwise testing revealed greater HbO in the control condition for the age-matched group than the individuals with aphasia (p < .001). In the left temporal, right IFG, and right temporal, there was a simple effect of group with greater HbO in the age-matched group than the aphasia group (all p < .05). In the right IFG, there was also a simple effect of epoch with greater HbO in Epochs 3 and 4 relative to Epoch 1 (all p < .05; See Figure 2, Panel B for epoch-averaged cortical responses across groups and conditions for the semantic feature judgment task).

Conclusion

           This study evaluated task-based cortical activity using HbO collected via fNIRS for naming and semantic feature judgment in individuals with aphasia and age-matched control participants. Key findings were: 1) for confrontation naming, condition effects across ROIs in the individuals with aphasia (experimental condition HbO greater than control condition HbO) that were not present in the age-matched group; and b) for semantic feature judgment, a pattern across ROIs for greater HbO changes in the age-matched group than the individuals with aphasia regardless of experimental condition. In the confrontation naming task, the condition-related effects for the aphasia group were expected. However, the lack of condition-related effects in the age-matched group were unexpected. This could potentially be due to low task demand for naming common objects in these healthy individuals. In the semantic feature task, greater HbO in the age-matched group relative to the individuals with aphasia is expected and consistent with prior fMRI literature.2 However, the lack of condition-related effects in either group is unexpected. This could be due to relatively low task demand, as both groups showed high performance accuracy in the task.

            Further data collection will expand the sample size for individuals with aphasia (target N = 20). Further analysis will evaluate a) relationships between lesion location and cortical activity patterns and b) responses for deoxygenated hemoglobin (HbR).

            These results show the potential for fNIRS to evaluate differences in cortical activity for language tasks between individuals with aphasia and healthy control participants. This foundation will enable future longitudinal work tracking recovery and treatment response. Using fNIRS to study aphasia recovery may broaden access to neuroimaging, reduce costs, and provide an objective method for monitoring treatment progress. It may also help clarify whether perilesional or contralesional activation patterns are associated with better outcomes and could eventually inform treatment modifications or individualized care.
