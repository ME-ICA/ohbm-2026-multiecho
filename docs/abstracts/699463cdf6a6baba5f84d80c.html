Introduction

Persistent breathlessness frequently occurs in the absence of objective pulmonary pathology, suggesting that maladaptive central processing of respiratory signals may contribute to symptom persistence. This phenomenon, often referred to as functional or maladaptive breathlessness, is observed across multiple respiratory conditions. COVID-19 provides a useful clinical model for investigating these mechanisms, as a substantial proportion of individuals experience chronic breathlessness following infection despite preserved gas exchange, while the virus itself is known to affect central nervous system function.

Methods

We used ultra–high-field (7 Tesla) resting-state functional MRI to investigate neural correlates of maladaptive breathlessness in 53 individuals recovering from COVID-19, spanning a wide range of acute illness severity and breathlessness, but with limited evidence of ongoing pulmonary impairment. Guided by an action–perception framework grounded in active inference and allostatic–interoceptive models of respiration (Barrett & Simmons, 2015; Kleckner et al., 2017; Seth & Friston, 2016), we quantified 153 resting-state functional connectivity measures among 18 cortical, subcortical, and brainstem regions implicated in respiratory signal integration, visceromotor control, and affective evaluation. Associations between functional connectivity and breathlessness levels were assessed using robust linear models with false discovery rate correction and further examined using non-parametric permutation testing and Bayesian moderation analyses. We tested moderation by general anxiety, treated as a continuous variable, and by prior mechanical ventilation during the acute phase of illness.

Results

Two dissociable connectivity patterns were associated with maladaptive breathlessness. Reduced connectivity between the dorsal periaqueductal gray and posterior insula (dPAG–PoI1) was associated with higher post-COVID breathlessness. This relationship persisted after accounting for anxiety and showed evidence of modulation by prior mechanical ventilation, suggesting disrupted integration of ascending visceral signals within lower-level interoceptive circuits, exacerbated by severe acute illness but not driven by affective factors. 

In contrast, increased connectivity between the basolateral amygdala and dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (BLA–dACC) was also associated with higher post-COVID breathlessness, but this effect was strongly moderated by anxiety and was unaffected by ventilation status. Across robust, permutation-based, and Bayesian analyses, anxiety consistently attenuated the BLA–dACC–breathlessness relationship, consistent with an anxiety-dependent anticipatory bias toward threat-related respiratory predictions.

Conclusion

These findings support a dual-pathway model of maladaptive breathlessness that extends beyond COVID-19 and may generalize to functional breathlessness more broadly. Hypoconnectivity within a brainstem–insular interoceptive pathway, linking the dorsal periaqueductal gray and posterior insula, may disrupt the integration of ascending visceral signals with cortical representations, impairing safety learning and prediction error updating for respiratory sensations. Such disruption is consistent with diminished recalibration of bodily predictions following recovery, particularly after severe acute illness, and may contribute to persistent breathlessness in the absence of ongoing pulmonary pathology.

In parallel, connectivity within a limbic–cingulate visceromotor pathway, linking the basolateral amygdala and dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, contributed to breathlessness catastrophizing in an anxiety-dependent manner. Increased BLA–dACC connectivity was most predictive of catastrophizing at lower levels of anxiety, with this relationship progressively attenuated as anxiety increased. This pattern suggests that affective evaluative circuitry may play a greater modulatory role in shaping breathlessness appraisal when background anxiety is relatively low, whereas high anxiety may impose a ceiling or saturation effect on threat-related processing. Under conditions of elevated anxiety, anticipatory threat bias may already dominate symptom appraisal, reducing the incremental explanatory contribution of variation in BLA–dACC connectivity to breathlessness catastrophizing.

Together, these results suggest that maladaptive breathlessness may arise from the interaction of distinct but complementary neural processes: impaired interoceptive signal integration at lower hierarchical levels and anxiety-dependent modulation of higher-order affective evaluative circuits. The dissociation between dPAG–insula and BLA–dACC pathways highlights that persistent breathlessness cannot be fully explained by affective state alone, but may also reflect functional alterations in the neural representation and updating of bodily signals. This framework offers a neurobiological perspective for understanding maladaptive breathlessness and generates testable hypotheses for future work across conditions in which breathlessness persists despite preserved pulmonary function.
