Introduction

Understanding the computations that support human vision requires models that both predict brain responses and provide interpretable hypotheses about their representational content. Recent work has shown that deep neural networks, particularly convolutional architectures, provide effective feature spaces for voxelwise encoding models (VEMs), which map stimulus features to BOLD responses measured during passive viewing. These models accommodate naturalistic images, generalize to unseen data, and reveal hierarchical correspondences between model depth and cortical organization along the ventral stream.1 Importantly, their performance can be objectively assessed on held-out test data, mitigating reproducibility concerns.

A complementary line of work uses Most Exciting Inputs (MEIs), i.e. images optimized to elicit maximal model-predicted activity, to visualize tuning properties2 and to guide closed-loop neural control.3 However, while MEIs have been explored through high-resolution invasive recordings in non-human primates, their potential in humans through lower-resolution fMRI data remains less clear.

To address this gap, we trained voxelwise encoding models on the Natural Scenes Dataset4 and used MEIs to characterize voxel- and region-level selectivity in humans. 
We extracted features from a pretrained convolutional neural network at multiple depths and learned a mapping function to predict voxel responses. 
MEIs were then synthesized for voxels across early, intermediate, and high-level visual regions, including functionally localized face-, body-, and place-selective areas. 
This approach recapitulates a well-known hierarchical organization through both predictive metrics and image-based readouts, and offers a simple, data-driven method to validate passive-viewing datasets by assessing whether area-appropriate selectivity emerges from independently trained encoders. 

Methods

Methods were adapted from Papale et al. (2025),2 who developed the same modeling and visualization framework for invasive recordings in non-human primates. Our work reproduces and extends these methods to human fMRI to test whether analogous tuning properties emerge at the voxel level.

We analyzed data from one of the eight participants of the Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD),4 which contains measurements of 7T fMRI responses (isovoxel 1.8 mm, TR=1.6 s) of 8 participants who each viewed 9,000–10,000 distinct color natural scenes (22,000–30,000 trials) while fixating centrally and performing a long-term continuous recognition task. Responses were averaged across repetitions to improve signal-to-noise ratio.

All images were normalized before feature extraction. Visual features were obtained from each convolutional layer of a pretrained Inception-V1 (trained on ImageNet). To select the optimal layer for each visual ROI, we performed a grid search using a computationally efficient subset of 10 voxels with the lowest trial-to-trial variability per ROI, varying learning rate and regularization parameters. The layer yielding the highest prediction accuracy in this search was then used for the final model, which was trained on a larger set of voxels within each ROI. Features from the selected layer were batch-normalized prior to model fitting.

Each voxelwise encoding model comprised a set of 2D spatial weights (Ws) matching the pixel grid and a set of feature weights (Wf) corresponding to the number of channels in the selected CNN layer. After training, Ws represented the spatial receptive field of the voxel and Wf described its feature selectivity as a weighted combination of CNN features. The predicted response for a voxel was obtained by convolving Ws and Wf with the batch-normalized feature maps. The loss function combined four terms: (1) prediction error (LMSE, mean squared error between predicted and measured responses), (2) a Laplacian smoothing term (LLaplace) promoting spatial smoothness, (3) an L1 penalty encouraging feature sparsity, and (4) an L2 regularization term applied to both Ws and Wf.

Models were trained using the Adam optimizer (learning rate = 0.0001, batch size = 32), with early stopping based on validation performance. Predictive accuracy was quantified as the cross-validated Pearson correlation between predicted and measured single-trial responses. To focus on reliable signals, the 1,000 voxels with the lowest trial-to-trial variability within each ROI were retained for model fitting.

To probe model tuning, we generated Most Exciting Inputs — synthetic images that maximized voxel responses — by optimizing image representations in the Fourier domain using the Lucent library. The resulting images were qualitatively inspected to characterize the visual properties encoded by individual voxels.

Results

Cross-validated Pearson’s correlation coefficients between predicted and observed responses -obtained through grid search- across some visual areas for each convolutional network layer are presented in Figure 1a. The distribution of the correlation coefficients revealed the expected hierarchy: early visual areas (e.g., V1) were best captured by shallower layers, mid‑level areas (e.g., V4) peaked at intermediate depths, and high‑level ventral regions (e.g., FFC) benefit most from deeper layers. This pattern mirrors the canonical increase in feature complexity along the ventral stream and validates the chosen architecture/training procedure. 

A total of 1,000 voxels per ROI (those with lowest trial-to-trial variability) were modeled, and overall performance is shown in Figure 1b as Pearson’s r between predicted and measured single-trial responses.

To probe model representations, MEIs were synthesized via gradient-based optimization in the Fourier domain under natural-image priors. MEIs revealed systematic differences across regions (Figure 1c):

• Early areas (V1–V2): MEIs emphasized low-level image statistics consistent with contrast, high-spatial frequencies, orientation, and simple texture sensitivity.
• Intermediate areas (V4/LO/V8): MEIs contained more structured motifs—curvilinear elements, object parts, and color-selective patterns—suggesting sensitivity to mid-level shape and chromatic features.
• High level areas (FFC/VVC/VMV2): MEIs contained complex, category-diagnostic arrangements, including face- and place-like patterns aligned with known functional localizers.

These qualitative readouts are consistent with the layer-depth results above: voxels whose MEIs contain richer, more global structure tend to prefer deeper CNN representations, whereas voxels with simpler MEIs prefer shallower features.

Conclusion

We trained voxelwise encoding models using features from a pretrained Inception-V1 network to predict fMRI responses in the Natural Scenes Dataset and evaluated model performance across CNN depths and visual ROIs. The cross-validated correlations revealed a clear hierarchical pattern: early visual areas were best captured by shallow layers, intermediate regions peaked at mid-level depths, and high-level ventral areas showed maximal performance with deeper features. This alignment between CNN depth and cortical hierarchy confirms that the adapted Papale et al. framework generalizes robustly to human fMRI.

Across ROIs, models trained on the 1,000 most reliable voxels achieved substantial predictive accuracy and voxelwise performance maps recapitulated expected functional organization. To characterize representational content, MEIs were generated for individual voxels. MEIs revealed systematic differences across regions: low-level areas exhibited edge- and texture-like patterns, mid-level areas showed more structured, part-like motifs, and high-level regions produced complex, category-diagnostic arrangements aligned with face- and place-selective localizers. These qualitative readouts tracked the quantitative depth preferences, demonstrating that deeper layers yield MEIs with richer global structure.

Together, these results show that encoding models trained on naturalistic stimuli not only predict voxel responses but also yield interpretable visualizations of voxel tuning. Beyond scientific interpretability, MEIs provide a practical validation tool for passive-viewing datasets. Without explicit task labels or new stimuli, MEIs derived from an independently trained encoder recover area-appropriate selectivity in a subject-specific manner. This supports using MEIs as a scalable, data-driven check that the dataset carries the expected information and that the encoder is not overfitting idiosyncratic noise.
