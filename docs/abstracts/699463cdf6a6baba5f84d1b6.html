Introduction

The hippocampus is a key structure in the brain implicated in multiple functions from learning, episodic memory, spatial navigation and anxiety. Anatomically, the hippocampal formation is comprised of thin, intricately folded grey and white matter laminae subdivided into distinct subfields; the dentate gyrus (DG), the Cornu Ammonis subfields (CA 1-3), and the subicular complex (subiculum, presubiculum, and parasubiculum). A key for accurate hippocampus segmentation is the identification on the stratum radiatum and stratum lacunosum moleculare (SRLM). The SRLM is a fine band, that is notoriously difficult to visualise using in-vivo MRI relying on T1w and T2w images. The clear visualisation of the SRLM is critical to accurately and reliably delineate subfields within the hippocampus.1 Spatial resolution is a key factor for successfully visualising the SRLM.2

Often, anisotropic T2w scans with high in plane resolution (0.4 x 0.4 mm) but thick coronal slices (2 mm) are used to identify the SLRM and help to accurately segment the hippocampus, but such scans are essentially useless for any purpose other than segmenting the hippocampus. In contrast, a high resolution and quality T1w dataset with isotropic voxels is useful for multiple analytical purposes.

To process high resolution T1w data (<1mm3) in Freesurfer, the -hires  retains original data resolution; previously, default recon-all would down-sample high resolution data to standard 1mm3 resolution. Previous findings have shown that processing with -hires reduces estimates of cortical thickness by ~20%, likely from reduced partial volume effects.3, 4 Whether similar effects occur in smaller, more complex structures like the hippocampus remains underexplored.

Hence, we explore the impact of processing pipeline differences on hippocampal volume estimates using high resolution (0.75mm3 isotropic resolution) T1w datasets.

Methods

MRI data was acquired for 30 participants (18-55 years old): two high resolution T1-weighted (T1w) images were obtained for each participant using a Philips Ingenia CX 3T MRI system with a 32-channel head coil at NeuRA Imaging Centre in Sydney. The images were acquired at 0.75mm3 isotropic resolution in the sagittal plane using a 3D-MPRAGE sequence with TR = 7.483 ms; TE = 3.41 ms; TFE factor = 157; flip angle = 8°; parallel imaging (CSENSE reduction = 1.5); bandwidth = 217 Hz/Px. Each T1w image scan took 6 min and 43 seconds. 

The two T1w images were averaged to create a higher-quality output image for further processing: brain tissue was extracted using HD-BET, rigidly aligned and averaged in MRtrix, and corrected for intensity inhomogeneity using the N4 bias field correction tool to improve image uniformity and accuracy for subsequent processing. The recon-all module in FreeSurfer (version 7.4.1) was used twice: once with the -hires flag (retaining 0.75mm3 resolution) and once without -hires (automatically down sampled to 1mm3 resolution). All other processing parameters were kept consistent.

Bilateral hippocampi were segmented using Freesurfer’s segmentHA_T1.sh tool. Subfield labels were combined using a simplified segmentation protocol that combined regions into six hippocampal labels and a single amygdala label. Volume estimates were extracted for the whole hippocampus and individual subfields. 

Pipeline effects were assessed using a linear mixed model with pipeline (default vs -hires) and hemisphere fitted as factors and participants as a random effect. Exploratory models tested pipeline × subfield interactions. Total brain volume and age were included as covariates. 

Results

Processing pipeline had a significant effect on volume. Whole hippocampal volume estimates derived from the -hires pipeline (M=3550mm3) were 2.71% (~95.92mm3) larger on average compared to those obtained using the default pipeline (M=3455mm3), t(84)=3.40, 95% CI [40.69, 151.15], p<.001. No pipeline ×hemisphere interaction was observed, indicating this effect was similar across hemispheres, 𝛽 =0.93, t(87)=0.02, 95% CI [-107.79, 109.65], p=0.99. 

Exploratory subfield analyses revealed that pipeline effects were not uniform across hippocampal subfields. The CA complex showed a mean volume reduction of approximately 3.7% under –hires, whereas hippocampal fissure volume increased by more than 50%. Other subfields showed minimal, non-significant changes. Visual inspection indicated that additional fissure voxels under –hires were primarily reassigned from adjacent CA regions, consistent with reduced partial-volume effects and altered boundary placement.

When fissure voxels were excluded from whole-hippocampus estimates, the overall pipeline effect was substantially reduced and no longer significant, suggesting that changes in whole hippocampal volume were driven primarily by CA–fissure boundary realignment rather than global scaling.

Conclusion

This study investigated whether processing pipeline parameters influences hippocampal volume in high resolution MRI data. Repeated measures results demonstrated a clear and systematic effect, whereby addition of the –hires flag to the processing pipeline resulted in whole hippocampus volumes 2.7% larger compared to those processed with the default pipeline (resampled to 1 mm3 resolution). This difference was statistically significant and consistent across hemispheres, indicating that retaining submillimeter resolution during processing can meaningfully impact volume estimates of the hippocampus. For context, the effect found is similar in magnitude to differences of 3% linked to bipolar disorder.5 Thus, using different processing pipelines within the same dataset (or across studies) could potentially lead to false-positives, obscure small effects, or confound the direction of an effect. 

Pipeline effects were found to be primarily driven by subfield-specific boundary realignment, particularly between the CA complex and hippocampal fissure, rather than global scaling effects. These findings demonstrate how choices in processing pipelines can influence both overall hippocampal volume estimates and apparent subfield-specific effects through their impact on boundary definition and partial volume effects. 

From this, we recommend careful consideration and transparency in definitions of whole hippocampus, especially regarding inclusion/exclusion of fissure labels, as well as consistency and transparency of processing pipelines within or across datasets.
