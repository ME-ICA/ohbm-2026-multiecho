Introduction

The layer structure of the cortex varies in a manner linked to function. This variation includes the relative thickness of different layers and of the specific cell populations found in each. Our understanding of this variation in humans has been built to a large degree upon post-mortem anatomical investigations. More recently, MR imaging techniques have developed to the stage where layer-specific data can be obtained from living humans. Such techniques are still limited, however, in what they can tell us about variation in microstructure. Some work has suggested that variation in layer structures is linked to differences in PET-based measures of GABA-A receptors, an important aspect of cortical cytoarchitectural.1 This has relied upon small participant groups and has not taken advantage of newer data allowing testing of specific layer thickness rather than overall cortical thickness. We therefore sought to replicate prior findings in two additional datasets and to integrate information about relative layer thickness. 

Methods

Two datasets were used in the work. Dataset A: 24 healthy adults for whom T1-weighted anatomical images and [18F]Flumazenil PET (FMZ-PET) data were obtained. Dataset B: T1-weighted anatomical images from 100 healthy adults (Human Connectome Project data) plus an openly available FMZ-PET map from healthy adults.2 Cortical thickness was calculated for each dataset with Freesurfer. In addition, layer thickness values were obtained from the Big Brain project.3 Average values for FMZ-PET and cortical thickness were calculated from each dataset for Brodmann regions and for each region of the HCP-MMP1 atlas. Layer thickness values were obtained for HCP-MMP1 atlas regions. Associations between FMZ-PET and cortical thickness and layer thickness were tested in each dataset through linear regression.

Results

FMZ binding within Brodmann regions was negatively associated with cortical thickness in Dataset A (ß = -1.74, t = -6.03, p = 6.92e-09). This association did not differ between hetero- and homo-typical cortex (ß = -0.52, t = –1.35, p = 0.18). The same negative association was seen in Dataset B (ß = -348.8, t = –3.83, p = 0.0012), with no difference between cortex types (ß = -8.58, t = –0.04, p = 0.97). Looking at regional layer thickness within the HCP-MMP1 atlas, an association between FMZ binding and the thickness of layers II (ß = -19.09, t = -3.32, p = 0.0011) and IV (ß = 4.08, t = 2.02, p = 0.045) was observed in Dataset A. An association with layer IV alone was observed in Dataset B (ß = 1383.6, t = 2.38, p = 0.018).

Conclusion

These findings replicate existing work showing an overall negative association between FMZ binding, a measure of GABA-A receptor density, and cortical thickness. It does not replicate a difference in this association between hetero- and homo-typical cortex. They advance this prior work by showing a negative relationship between FMZ binding and layer II thickness (although this was not found in both datasets), along with a positive relationship with layer IV. These associations fit with the known cytoarchitecture of these layers. In particular, it links with known populations of GABAergic interneurons within each. This work complements other research investigating microstructure in the living human brain.
