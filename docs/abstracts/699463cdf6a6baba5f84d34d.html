Introduction

Schizophrenia (SZ) and bipolar disorder (BP) are severe mental disorders that typically emerge during late adolescence or early adulthood. It is important to identify the neurobiological markers of risk preceding SZ and BP onset to understand disease progression and detect at-risk individuals early. Studying enriched cohorts, such as children at familial high risk (FHR), provides a unique opportunity to examine neurodevelopmental markers preceding illness, given the high proportion of individuals at FHR who develop SZ or BP. Among children at FHR for SZ (FHR-SZ) or BP (FHR-BP), an estimated proportion of 10-40% develop a psychotic disorder in adulthood, and more than 50% develop any mental disorder.1 Neurodevelopmental markers associated with FHR notably include structural brain abnormalities, particularly in children at FHR-SZ, such as smaller intracranial volume (ICV) and total brain volume, thinner cortex, and reduced surface area.2,3 These abnormalities, albeit with weaker effect sizes, largely conform to what is observed in chronic SZ and BP.4 Previous work from our lab has shed new light on structural abnormalities related to FHR by examining a large, longitudinal FHR cohort, named the Danish High Risk and Resilience Study (VIA). At neuroimaging baseline, when the participants were ~11-12 years old, we previously reported sex-specific brain differences related to FHR, including smaller ICV, brain volume, cortical volume, and surface area in males at FHR-SZ, and larger brain volume and cortical volume in females at FHR-BP.5 It is important to continue to investigate sex-specific structural features related to FHR given known sex differences in brain structure, brain development, pubertal hormonal changes, and symptom progression in SZ and BP. Here, we follow up on our previous findings by testing four brain measures and their change from baseline at age ~11-12 to follow-up at age ~15-16 years in the full sample and in sex-stratified groups.

Methods

The present study included participants enrolled in the VIA study, a prospective, longitudinal, register-based cohort study which follows a total of 522 participants born to at least one parent with SZ (FHR-SZ, n=202), BP (FHR-BP, n=120), or neither of these disorders (population-based controls, PBC, n=200). In the present study, we analyzed MRI data for 288 participants at neuroimaging baseline (VIA11; FHR-SZ: n=104, FHR-BP: n=68, PBC: n=116; mean[SD] age: 12.1[0.3]; 49.7% female) and for 249 participants four years later (VIA15; FHR-SZ: n=93, FHR-BP: n=61, PBC: n=95; mean[SD] age: 16.1[0.4]; 50.2% female). VIA11 and VIA15 included whole-brain anatomical MRI at the Danish Research Centre for Magnetic Resonance (DRCMR) in Copenhagen or the Centre of Functionally Integrative Neuroscience (CFIN) in Aarhus, Denmark, using 3T Siemens Magnetom Prisma Fit (DRCMR) or Magnetom Skyra (CFIN) MR scanners with harmonized protocols. T1-weighted images were derived from 3D MP2RAGE sequences with fat image navigators for retrospective motion correction. Images were processed using FreeSurfer’s longitudinal pipeline (version 7.4.1), including surface-based nonlinear registration to the 68-parcel Desikan-Killiany atlas. We extracted cortical gray matter volume, white matter surface area, and cortical thickness from each parcel and from each hemisphere, averaging the hemispheres to create whole-cortex measures, and we extracted total brain volume. We obtained intracranial volume (ICV) from FreeSurfer’s samseg stream to use as a covariate. The caregiver-reported Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL) measured overall behavioral problems, and the Children’s Global Assessment Scale (CGAS), based on caregiver and child interviews, measured children’s general functioning in the past month. Analyses were preregistered on Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/nax97/overview) and used the lme4 package (version 1.1-37) in R (version 4.4.2) to fit linear mixed-effects models. All models included random intercepts for family ID and participant ID nested within family ID, to account for within-family effects and repeated measures over time within participants, and controlled for MR site and Euler number, a proxy of image quality. We used η2 to quantify effect sizes, interpreting values above 0.06 as medium and above 0.14 as large effect sizes. First, we ran models with group-by-age interactions to test whether age slopes differed between FHR groups. These models also included group-by-sex interactions to test whether FHR group effects differed by sex, and age-by-sex interactions as a control. Second, motivated by the sex-specific results previously reported in VIA11, we ran models with group-by-age interactions that were stratified by sex. In models stratified by sex, we additionally tested FHR group main effects after removing non-significant group-by-age interactions. We ran all models with ICV as an additional covariate. For each set of models, we controlled for multiple comparisons using false discovery rate (FDR) correction across the four brain measures of interest. We used q<0.05 to determine significance at the FDR level. All p-values reported below are uncorrected p-values. For any main effects of FHR group significant at the FDR level, we subsequently extracted pairwise group differences using the emmeans package (version 2.0.0) in R to identify which group differences were driving the main effect. We used Cohen’s d to determine the effect size of pairwise effects, interpreting an absolute Cohen’s d of 0.5 as medium and 0.8 as large group differences. In an exploratory analysis, for models significant at the FDR level, we tested each of the 68 parcels in the Desikan-Killiany atlas to identify cortical regions driving the group main effects. Lastly, for significant models only, we examined if group effects persisted when adding two additional covariates to account for puberty stage: Tanner stage and the time delay between Tanner assessment and MRI.

Results

The FHR-SZ and FHR-BP groups exhibited higher CBCL total problems scores (ps<0.01) and lower CGAS scores (ps<0.01) than the PBC group, indicating greater behavioral problems and worse general functioning in the FHR groups. In models testing our main hypothesis, that age slopes for the brain measures would differ between the FHR groups, none of the group-by-age interactions were significant (ps>0.075). This indicated that slopes were not significantly different between FHR groups, thus failing to support our hypothesis. We observed the same result in models stratified by sex, where none of the group-by-age interactions were significant (ps>0.059). Simplified sex-stratified models testing main effects of FHR group across VIA11 and VIA15 revealed main effects of brain volume (η2 =0.065, F=6.056, p=0.003) and cortical volume (η2=0.047, F=4.318, p=0.015) in males, which were significant at the FDR level, suggesting stable group differences across age. These group main effects were driven by FHR-SZ males having smaller brain volume (Cohen’s d=-0.524, t=-2.464, p=0.015) and cortical volume (d=-0.405, t=-2.154, p=0.033) compared to PBC males, as well as FHR-SZ males having smaller brain volume (d=-0.851, t=-3.257, p=0.001) and cortical volume (d=-0.648, t=-2.723, p=0.007) compared to FHR-BP males. Brain and cortical volumes in VIA11 and VIA15 are visualized in Figure 1A for each FHR group and for females and males separately. Adding ICV as an additional covariate weakened these effects in males, for both brain volume (η2 =0.021, F=1.888, p=0.154) and cortical volume (η2 =0.005, F=0.386, p=0.680), which were no longer significant. ICV itself showed a main effect of FHR group (η2=0.061, F=5.822, p=0.004), with FHR-SZ males having smaller ICV than FHR-BP males (d=-0.720, t=-2.99, p=0.003) and PBC males (d=-0.574, t=-2.734, p=0.007). In females, the FHR group effects for brain volume (η2 =0.04, F=3.249, p=0.041) and cortical volume (η2 =0.042, F=3.376, p=0.037) became more prominent when adding ICV as an additional covariate, but did not reach significance at the FDR level, hinting at relative volume differences. These results were driven by FHR-BP females having larger brain volume (d=0.343, t=2.352, p=0.02) and cortical volume (d=0.293, t=2.501, p=0.013) compared to PBC females. In models reaching significance at the FDR level (i.e., FHR group main effects in males), accounting for puberty stage strengthened the FHR group main effects for both brain volume (η2 =0.078, F=7.267, p=0.001) and cortical volume (η2 =0.058, F=5.31, p=0.006). Lastly, testing pairwise FHR group differences in males for each parcel in the Desikan-Killiany atlas revealed significantly reduced cortical volume in FHR-SZ males compared to FHR-BP males in 8 parcels at the FDR level: right lateral orbitofrontal cortex, right pars orbitalis, right paracentral cortex, right superior temporal gyrus, right inferior temporal gyrus, left medial orbitofrontal gyrus, left pars orbitalis, and left lateral orbitofrontal cortex. No parcels survived FDR correction for the comparison between FHR-SZ males and PBC males. Pairwise FHR group differences in cortical volume for each parcel for males, as well as females, are visualized in Figure 1B using Cohen’s d.

Conclusion

We found smaller brain and cortical volumes in males at FHR-SZ compared to both FHR-BP and PBC across VIA11 and VIA15, revealing sex-specific FHR group differences which were stable into mid-adolescence. This result largely aligns with our previous baseline findings from VIA11, when participants were ~11-12 years old, and extends them to VIA15, when participants were ~15-16 years old. Previously, in VIA11, we found sex-specific global brain differences in participants at FHR-SZ and FHR-BP, including smaller ICV, brain volume, cortical volume, and surface area in males at FHR-SZ, and larger brain volume and cortical volume in females at FHR-BP. Now, in VIA15, we continue to see smaller brain and cortical volumes in FHR-SZ males, highlighting them in particular as reliable markers of FHR. However, when we controlled for ICV, the group differences in males became non-significant. Accordingly, we found strong FHR group main effects on ICV in both the neuroimaging baseline and the present follow-up study, indicating stable group differences in ICV in FHR-SZ males. This suggests an early developmental effect of FHR-SZ. ICV is considered a neurodevelopmental marker that largely reflects early brain growth, with 80% to 90% of total brain volume attained by age 3 years, which supports the view that group differences in ICV may reflect early neurodevelopmental deviations. Controlling for ICV also revealed, although not significant at a corrected level, group differences in brain and cortical volume in females at FHR-BP compared to PBC, warranting further examination in future studies. We did not observe any FHR group differences in change over time between VIA11 and VIA15 for any of the brain measures, suggesting that the maturation of brain structure between the ages of ~11-12 and ~15-16 likely does not diverge as a function of FHR status. Overall, our findings suggest that structural differences between FHR groups may already be present before age 11. Future FHR cohorts should incorporate MRI as early as infancy or prenatally to determine when global volumetric abnormalities associated with FHR arise. Future studies on the VIA cohort will focus on characterizing the developmental trajectories of those who develop psychotic experiences or Axis I mental disorders, in VIA15 and eventually in VIA19, when the participants will be ~19-20 years old. Data collection for the VIA19 assessment wave is currently ongoing. Moreover, we will characterize the developmental trajectories of those showing cognitive impairment in VIA7, when participants were ~7-8 years old. This will allow us to move beyond the FHR labels and link structural changes with both outcomes later in adolescence and early markers of functional impairment. This future work will help determine the clinical and predictive utility of the sex-specific global volumetric brain differences related to FHR identified here. Ultimately, we aim to identify the neuroanatomical markers of biological vulnerability prior to the potential onset of SZ and BP.
