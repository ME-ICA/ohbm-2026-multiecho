Introduction

In the human brain, somatosensory and motor cortices are organized somatotopically, such that each body part is represented according to its functional relevance and sensorimotor complexity. This organization is highly plastic, as demonstrated by use-dependent plasticity in experts such as musicians, who show enlarged cortical representations of intensively trained hands.² Similarly, motor training, frequently used in medicine or sport, induces functional reorganization within the sensorimotor network by increased cortical extent, greater overlap with adjacent body-segment representations, and enhanced activation during movement of the trained limb.³ However, because these paradigms inherently combine motor commands with sensory feedback, the specific contribution of sensory input to sensorimotor cortical plasticity remains difficult to disentangle. 

The present study aimed to isolate the role of proprioceptive input in driving sensorimotor reorganization by applying repeated proprioceptive stimulation to targeted muscles of the non-dominant leg. Mechanical vibrations were delivered to knee muscle tendons to selectively activate muscle spindles and elicit vivid illusions of movement in the absence of actual movement. Twenty-four healthy participants underwent daily proprioceptive training for two weeks and complete two MRI sessions, before and after the intervention. 

During MRI sessions, six muscles were stimulated in a randomized order : the sartorius, quadriceps, and tibialis anterior of both legs. Because leg representations span the interhemispheric fissure and central sulcus, we employed surface-based fMRI analyses to improve spatial precision and minimize signal mixing across sulcal banks. Univariate and multivariate approaches were used to characterize functional reorganization and somatotopic remodeling within primary somatosensory and motor cortices.

Methods

The study included 24 healthy right-foot dominant adults (mean age: 38.04 ± 10.38 years; 9 females). One participant was excluded due to excessive head motion. Participants completed two MRI sessions separated by two weeks of repeated proprioceptive stimulation.

Protocol of repeated proprioceptive stimulation  

Participants received a three-vibration sequence targeting the non-dominant knee (quadriceps, hamstring, alternating agonist/antagonist), repeated five timesdaily for 40 minutes over 9 days (excluding weekends) (Fig. 1B). 

MRI data acquisition  

Data were acquired on a 3T Siemens Prisma at the Centre IRM-INT (Marseille, France). Each session included anatomical (T1w, T2w) and five task-related functional runs using a multiband gradient-echo EPI. Each run had seven conditions (six muscle-vibration conditions and one No-Vib), pseudo-randomly repeated five times (Fig. 1A). Six pneumatic vibrators were positioned bilaterally over the anterior tibialis (ankle), quadriceps (knee), and the sartorius muscles (hip) (Fig. 1A). Vibrations applied at ~70Hz induced an illusion of limb movement. Placement and frequency were individually adjusted and monitored using sensors; signals were bandpass-filtered (10–300 Hz) and analyzed via Welch's method.

MRI data analysis  

Preprocessing was performed using fMRIPrep. Anatomical data underwent intensity non-uniformity correction, cross-session registration, skull stripping, tissue segmentation, and normalization to MNI152NLin6Asym.     

Cortical surfaces were reconstructed in native space and aligned to the fLR 91k template.   

Functional data underwent motion correction, distortion correction, coregistration, confound extraction, resampling and normalization (to the fsLR 91k template for cortical data and to 2 mm MNI152NLin6Asym for subcortical ones). All data were smoothed with a 4 mm Gaussian kernel smoothing.

Image Quality Metrics from mriqc (estimated on the raw data) were checked for outliers. Participants were excluded if average framewise displacement (FD) exceeded 0.3mm or if >25% of timepoints exceeded 0.2mm FD.

Univariate analysis  

Statistical parametric maps were generated with Nilearn’s GLM, for each participant and session. The design matrix included nine regressors of interest (six vibration conditions, rest, ratings and auditory cue, auditory beep), and 48 noise regressors (motion parameters and aCompCor). For each vibration condition and each session, we computed the contrast Vibration>Rest and thresholded it at p < 0.01, corrected for multiple comparisons by means of False Discovery Rate (FDR). 

Random-fixed effect (RFX) analysis was performed at group-level. Additionally, we compute an RFX all-effects analysis for the dominant and non-dominant leg, in both sessions, to capture the full network activated by an illusion of movement regardless of the vibration condition (Ankle or Knee or Hip).  

For all the statistical analysis, changes between pre- and post-sessions within each ROI were examined using a Wilcoxon paired test, FDR-corrected.. We assessed activation extent, peak activation (POA, highest activation within each ROI), and overlap between limb representations via the Jaccard Index (proportion of grayordinates jointly activated by multiple limb regions divided by their union).

Multivariate analysis  

We performed representational similarity analysis (RSA), a multivariate approach that considers the activity of all grayordinates to quantify the distance between activation patterns of different contrasts. The unsmoothed effect size maps of Vibration-Rest contrasts were used to compute cosine distance between the patterns of activation of the vibration conditions. 

Results

During MRI acquisitions, participants consistently reported clear illusions of movement. Illusion clarity scores did not differ significantly between pre- and post-training sessions. Online monitoring of vibration signals during scanning confirmed that stimulation frequencies for each of the six conditions were highly consistent and did not differ significantly between sessions. To make sure that the results weren't contaminated by head-motions we compared the FD between the two sessions and found a significant difference. So, we checked the FD estimation after preprocessing (fMRI data corrected for head-motion and EPI distortions, spatially recalibrated, unsmoothed) and no differences were found between the two sessions. Significant differences were found for the comparison of the FD estimation on raw versus preprocessed data for both sessions. 

Univariate analyses revealed that movement illusions elicited by all six vibration conditions (“all effects”) activated a widespread sensorimotor network, with preferential contralateral activation in primary somatosensory (S1) and motor (M1) cortices. Following proprioceptive training, a distinct somatotopic reorganization emerged within the contralateral primary sensorimotor cortex (S1M1) for the trained (non-dominant) leg. Specifically, a significant reduction in overlap between knee and ankle representations was observed (Fig. 2B; p = 0.038), whereas no somatotopic changes were detected for the untrained (dominant) leg (Fig. 2A). 

However, analyses of individual activation extent (number of activated vertices per stimulation condition) revealed no significant differences between pre- and post-training sessions. Nevertheless, trends toward increased extent were observed for the hip representation of the untrained leg in contralateral M1 (p = 0.056) and for the knee representation of the trained leg in contralateral S1 (p = 0.056). 

We also observed a significant reduction in POA within the sensorimotor cortex for the trained leg following training. More precisely, POA decreased for the ankle representation in contralateral S1 (p = 0.048), for the knee representation in S1 bilaterally (contralateral: p = 0.028; ipsilateral: p = 0.028) and in ipsilateral S1M1 (p = 0.036), and for the hip representation in contralateral S1 (p = 0.038) and ipsilateral S1M1 (p = 0.033). For the untrained leg, only a trend toward decreased POA was observed for the knee representation in contralateral S1 (p = 0.052). 

Finally, multivariate analyses using RSA revealed robust effects for the trained (non-dominant) leg. Following proprioceptive training, dissimilarity between ankle and knee representations significantly increased in M1 bilaterally (contralateral : p = 0.015; ipsilateral : p = 0.020), as well as in contralateral S1 (p = 0.007) and ipsilateral S1M1 (p = 0.002). In addition, increased dissimilarity between ankle and hip representations was observed in M1 bilaterally (contralateral : p = 0.001; ipsilateral: p = 0.020) and in ipsilateral S1M1 (p = 0.045). 

For the untrained (dominant) leg, RSA revealed more limited effects, with a significant increase in dissimilarity between ankle and hip activation patterns observed in M1 (p = 0.035) and in contralateral S1M1 (p = 0.020). No significant changes in representational distances were detected for the knee and ankle representations in the untrained leg.

Conclusion

First, the present findings confirm that proprioceptive-induced illusion of movement engages the same sensorimotor network as actual movement, despite the absence of overt motor output. Remarkably, only two weeks of repeated proprioceptive stimulation were sufficient to induce cortical plasticity, leading to a reorganization of primary sensorimotor representations that was largely specific to the stimulated leg, with minimal changes observed for the unstimulated limb. 

Our main finding is a reduction in the spatial overlap between ankle and knee representations of the stimulated leg within contralateral S1M1. This reduced overlap indicates a sharper differentiation between segmental representations, suggesting increased specificity within the somatotopic maps. Importantly, this change was not accompanied by a reduction in the relative extent of these representations. Although a trend toward a reduced absolute extent of the knee representation was observed in contralateral S1, this suggests that the decreased overlap may reflect a spatial displacement or a reconfiguration of representational boundaries rather than a simple contraction. This interpretation will be further explored using center-of-mass analyses and shape-based metrics.  

Consistent with this view, representational similarity analysis (RSA) revealed increased dissimilarity between activation patterns of the ankle and knee, as well as between the ankle and hip, following proprioceptive stimulation. These effects were observed bilaterally in M1 and in ipsilateral S1M1 for the stimulated leg. Because RSA using cosine distance is sensitive to spatial pattern differences (while remaining partially sensitive to overall activation levels), these findings support the notion of a refined representational geometry. 

In addition, a general decrease in activation levels for the stimulated leg was observed with significantly lower peaks of activity bilaterally in S1M1 after training. 

The present findings contrast with those from studies investigating the effects of active motor training or repetitive movement, which typically report expansions of cortical representations, increased activation, and greater overlap between adjacent body segments.³ In such paradigms, however, sensory and motor components are inherently intertwined, making it difficult to disentangle their respective contributions. 

Here, by isolating the sensory component through repeated proprioceptive stimulation alone, we demonstrate a distinct form of plasticity characterized by enhanced segregation of adjacent limb representations without increased cortical extent or activation. We propose that this effect reflects improved representational specificity rather than cortical amplification. The divergence from motor-training studies may be explained by the inevitable coactivation of multiple muscles and segments during voluntary movement, even when highly controlled. Repeated coactivation likely drives shared neuronal recruitment, thereby increasing overlap between representations. In contrast, targeting a single body segment through proprioceptive stimulation may promote functional differentiation by selectively engaging segment-specific afferent pathways. 

In conclusion, repeated proprioceptive stimulation of a single body segment is sufficient to refine the somatotopic organization of sensorimotor cortices within a relatively short time frame. This effect cannot be attributed to habituation, as perceptual reports of the illusion remained unchanged, and instead reflects genuine representational specialization. By simultaneously engaging somatosensory and motor cortices without requiring voluntary movement, this approach could be promising for clinical applications, particularly in the context of neurological rehabilitation.
