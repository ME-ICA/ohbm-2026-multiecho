Introduction

Establishing an in-depth understanding of how the brain interacts with peripheral organs is essential for explaining why many neurological disorders are often linked with systemic metabolic, vascular, and inflammatory pathways1. However, most of the current studies rely on linear models that might be underpowered for the latent complex and non-monotonic dependencies across organs. To address this limitation, we applied a novel statistical inference approach of pairwise non-linear dependence analysis2 to large-scale multi-organ imaging data from UK Biobank to uncover multi-organ relationships between the neuroimaging biomarkers and cardiac, liver, and kidney imaging traits. Specifically, we quantified multi-organ relationships using a powerful statistical framework known as binary expansion testing (BET)3. This method enables us to rigorously assess the dependence between any pair of organ traits while also revealing complex, non-linear association patterns that might be affected by underlying or previously unrecognized health disparity risk factors. Taken together, this pilot study yields a novel brain-heart-liver-kidney axis, i.e., an integrated map of organ-to-organ relationships that holds promise for advancing disease prevention, informing therapeutic targets, and guiding early clinical intervention.

Methods

Data description. We analyzed 76,079 UKB participants who had at least one MRI assessment of the brain, heart, liver, or kidney (age: 66.0±7.8; M/F: 51.7%/48.3%). Within this cohort, 76,057 individuals had available brain (B) MRI traits (2,900 traits, UKB Category #508), 73,312 had heart (H) MRI traits (457 traits, Category #102), 69,622 had liver (L) MRI traits (14 traits, Category #126), and 44,310 had kidney (K) MRI traits (17 traits, Category #156), as shown in Fig. 1a. We analyzed imaging-derived phenotypes (IDPs) spanning four organ systems including brain, heart, liver, and kidney. The heart IDPs include over 400 traits capturing chamber structure, function, strain, aortic morphology, and pulse-wave hemodynamics. Brain IDPs comprise ~3,000 structural measures, including global tissue volumes, subcortical nuclei, and regional cortical morphometry across multiple parcellations. Liver and kidney IDPs provide quantitative measures of fat/iron content, tissue relaxation, and renal parenchymal volumes, enabling a holistic multi-organ characterization. Regarding the sample size of subjects with paired multi-organ IDPs, the sample size of in total 6 possible pairwise combinations are shown as: B↔H: 73,297, B↔L: 69,599, B↔K: 44,310, H↔L: 69,564, H↔K: 44,272, L↔K: 44,214. To ensure that analyses between organ-specific traits remain biologically meaningful and statistically reliable, we exclude imaging-quality and acquisition-related readouts. In addition, trait pairs with insufficient sample size (n<30) were removed to maintain statistical robustness and prevent unstable estimates. After applying trait-level filtering and sample-size criteria, the final set of cross-organ trait pairs consisted of 309,255 B↔H pairs, 4,263 B↔L pairs, 4,266 B↔K pairs, 594 H↔L pairs, 639 H↔K pairs, and 9 K↔L pairs. 

Binary expansion testing. For any random variable U∈[-1,1], we consider the binary expansion U= Σ∞d=1 2⁻ᵈ Ad, where Ad are binary variables (aka. data bits of U). The combination of the binary expansion shows that eⁱᵗᵁ =eⁱᵗ ∑∞d=1 (Ad / 2ᵈ)=∏∞d=1 e(iAdt / 2ᵈ) =∏∞d=1[cos(t/2ᵈ) + iAd sin(t/2ᵈ)]. By taking the expectation on both sides, the characteristic function of U is determined by the expectation of the products of the form A₁ A₂ … AD… and can be approximated by the characteristic function of UD=∑Dd=12-dAd, which represents the approximation of U with its first D bits. This binary expansion allows us to reparametrize the likelihood of the contingency tables through a novel binary interaction design equation, where the interaction of delta bits {Ad} associated with random variable U and {Bd} associated with random variable V in the filtration holds complete sufficient statistics for dependency by maximizing the cross-interaction odds ratios3. To that end, we convert the challenging problem of dependence detection into a multiple relatively simple testing problem. Compared to classic χ² tests, our approach is much more efficient and interpretable. 

Explore multi-organ relationships. We applied BET to every possible pairwise multi-organ relationship. The key step in BET is to partition the unit square into different patterns that indicate interesting types of dependence in terms of count, i.e., densities of observations, in different regions. For fast computation these patterns are dyadic in nature. As the example shown in Fig. 1c, unions of shaded blocks represent one region and white blocks are the alternative. Each partition pattern is called a binary interaction design3 (BID). If there is no dependence between two multi-organ traits U and V, the observations are randomly distributed in [0,1]2. Dependence is captured by differences in the counts of shaded versus white regions. For each BID, this difference, called the symmetry statistic S, is positive when the white region is denser and negative when the shaded region is denser. These counts are then tested against the null hypothesis of no difference. 

Results

Model interpretability. The dependence pattern in BID and the associated symmetry statistic score  provide rich information to understand the complex organ-to-organ relationship. As the example shown in Fig. 1c, in the pair consisting of a brain trait (parahippocampal area) and a heart trait (left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) aortic annulus diameter), the selected 4×4 BID highlights a monotonic upward dependence: individuals with larger parahippocampal area tend to exhibit larger aortic annulus diameter. This pattern is reflected by the positive symmetry statistic S=115, with a higher number of observations in the white region (W=407) compared to the shaded region (B=292). If the dependence reversed, density would shift to the shaded blocks and the symmetry statistic would turn negative. To further assess the biological relevance of the BET-identified dependence, we additionally examined how participant sex is distributed across the BID grid. Specifically, we computed the proportion of male participants within each BID cell in the two-dimensional space spanned by parahippocampal area and LVOT aortic annulus diameter (bottom-right of Fig. 1c). This allows us to visualize whether certain regions of the joint trait space are enriched for males or females, thereby providing an auxiliary sex-stratified characterization of the detected multi-organ association.

Computational cost. We applied BET to identify brain–heart-liver-kidney relationships across all imaging traits. Under the same hardware environment (NVIDIA H100 NVL, 8-GPUs, 96GB/per), our method completes 309,255 B↔H pairwise independence tests in 9.94 minutes3, whereas the distance correlation method4 and the mutual information method5 take 17.46 minutes and 293.95 hours, respectively. 

Statistical power. Our methods demonstrate promising robustness and optimal performance, facilitating the discovery of various complex dependency patterns. Our method has found 27,250 significant non-linear B↔H relationships, most of which were unknown. Many of these non-linear dependencies arise from diverse UK Biobank phenotypes, such as sex-dependent physiological traits, cardiometabolic traits, vascular elasticity, body composition, and hemodynamics, that jointly shape cardiac and cerebral morphometry. Unlike conventional correlation analyses that capture only linear associations, BET quantifies the asymmetry in the joint empirical distribution and detects departures from independence. For example, one significant B↔H pair is the relationship between precuneus volume (brain) and the aortic annulus at end diastole phase (heart). As shown in Fig. 2a, these two imaging traits exhibit minimal linear correlation in the observed sample space. In Fig. 2b, their binary expansions reveal a clear quadrant pattern of asymmetric dependence captured by BET model, yielding z-score (z=4.39) , and an extreme asymmetry index of S=117. 

Potential applications. The binary expansion plots in Fig. 2c-d allow us to address the scientific questions such as “What drives this non-linear dependency?”. The dependence pattern showed clear sex-specific stratification: females clustered in the lower-left  U-V space (lower brain and cardiac measures), while males occupied the upper-right (higher values in both). This structured imbalance indicates that multi-organ dependence is largely driven by sex-related variations in structural size and metabolic load, aligning with known differences in cardiac output and brain volume. BET thus reveals latent inter-organ dependencies beyond traditional correlations, offering a mechanistic view of sex-dependent coupling. The male-proportion heatmap (Fig. 2d) further confirms this pattern, demonstrating that the detected dependency arises from a sex-linked multi-organ scaling effect between brain morphometry and cardiac structure. Together, our work sets the stage for identifying latent organ-to-organ dependence.



Conclusion

This pilot study delineates a novel brain–heart–liver–kidney axis, an integrated map of organ-to-organ relationships that bridges systems neuroscience with multi-organ medicine. By characterizing how brain function aligns with cardiac, hepatic, and renal phenotypes, this framework provides a translational pathway for identifying mechanistic biomarkers, refining risk stratification, and informing targeted prevention and early therapeutic intervention. Ultimately, these organ-to-organ signatures have the potential to accelerate the development of clinically actionable tools for improving population health across interconnected organ systems.
