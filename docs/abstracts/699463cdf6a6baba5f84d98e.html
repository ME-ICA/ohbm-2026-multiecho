Introduction

Normative brain age models aim to quantify deviations from normative neurodevelopment and aging patterns using predictive models trained on non-affected populations. While structural imaging has recently seen new work on spatially localized brain age frameworks, functional neuroimaging models still often collapse rich temporal data into static summary features, such as functional connectivity. This reduction may overlook meaningful temporal dynamics in brain aging. We propose a novel architecture that performs downsampling dynamically during training in a branching fashion to produce multiple estimates per sample at varying resolutions.

Methods

For training the normative model, we use a sample of N = 18,084 non-affected individuals from the UK Biobank^1, Human Connectome Project (HCP)^2, HCP-Aging^3, and HCP-Development^4 datasets. We then apply this model on the FBIRN phase III data set^5 to evaluate the efficacy of the model in identifying biomarkers in clinical populations. Proposing a novel, branching architecture for brain age estimation, we combine gated recurrent unit (GRU) networks with branching, learnable convolutional filters to predict brain age across varying temporal resolutions. Rather than producing a single age estimate per scan, our approach enables dynamic brain age estimates across resolutions, capturing fine-grained functional aging patterns and distinct temporal motifs related to brain age gaps. We trained on time series data derived using NeuroMark ICA following uniform fMRI preprocessing across data sets (warping, slice-timing correction, smoothing). For inference on samples with fewer TRs, we zero-pad at the beginning of the time-course. We trained each model for 100 epochs with a batch size of 128, a learning rate of 1e-4, and three layers per GRU block with 512 neurons each. The model with the best validation performance from each fold of 5-fold cross-validation was selected and used for inference, with final predictions averaged across folds. We then perform statistical testing between the estimated brain age delta and diagnosis category and cognitive and behavioral scores, performing FDR correction within models across tested scores. Finally, we compute integrated gradient attributions for each model type to visualize differences in importance across components and time between approaches.

Results

Our dynamic brain age models achieve competitive predictive performance for functional brain age on held-out healthy controls. When consolidating dynamic scores, we achieve a mean absolute error (MAE) of 5.534±0.27, a root mean squared error (RMSE) of 8.861±0.31, and a correlation with chronological age of 0.935±0.01. Furthermore, when applied to independent samples from a holdout schizophrenia study (N = 311), we observe associations with the brain age gap and Schizophrenia associated with diagnosis (corrected q<0.05). Using integrated gradient introspection, we can observe that a single-branch model with no downsampling emphasizes NeuroMark component 2 (sub-cortical), 4 (sub-cortical), and 29 (cognitive control) during inference, while a multi-branch model with an additional branch downsampling at a factor of 2 emphasizes components 52 (cerebellar), 2 (sub-cortical), and 19 (visual). 

Conclusion

This work presents a flexible, temporally resolved framework for modeling functional brain age. By capturing dynamic neural aging trajectories, our models offer new insights into neuropsychiatric disorders and represent a step toward more personalized, time-sensitive brain health assessments.
