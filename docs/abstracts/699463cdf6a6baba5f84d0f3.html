Introduction

Experimental models are commonly used in healthy humans to induce temporary pain. One model uses electrocutaneous high frequency stimulation (HFS) to cause hyperalgesia, mimicking perceptual sensations of heightened pain in response to a previously painful stimulus. Neurobiologically, elucidating the mechanistic underpinning of HFS-induced hyperalgesia is a key goal. 

In pre-clinical models, both superficial and deep dorsal horn neurons demonstrate facilitated responses to single electrical pulses following HFS, indicative of homotopic primary hyperalgesia and heterotopic secondary hyperalgesia, describing heightened painful responses at the site of, and proximal to stimulation, respectively. 

Crucially, mechanistic insights from pre-clinical studies regarding spinal transmission processes and their involvement in hyperalgesia following HFS have not been translated to humans, given limited options to non-invasively examine neuronal activity in the spinal cord. Leveraging recent technical developments in spinal fMRI, here we sought to investigate spinal neural correlates of secondary hyperalgesia in humans. Participants underwent two identical scanning sessions wherein they experienced mechanical stimulation of the volar forearm, before and after receiving HFS. We hypothesised that, after stimulation, (1) behavioural pain ratings in response to mechanical stimulation, and (2) corresponding evoked BOLD responses in the spinal cord, would increase.

Methods

Thirty-two healthy right-handed participants (59% female, mean age ± standard deviation (SD) = 24 ± 5 years) completed a two session study, comprised of a familiarisation and MRI visit.

At familiarisation, participants were screened for ongoing or past health conditions and medication use. All were trained in how to complete the within-scanner task and familiarised with the HFS stimulation protocol. The MRI visit consisted of two scanning sessions separated by a one-hour break, during which participants received sensitising HFS to the right volar forearm. Sensitisation was achieved by delivering five trains of 1-second HFS (100Hz) every 10 seconds at 20 times the participants’ individually titrated electrical detection threshold. Stimulation was delivered via an EPS-P10 electrode (MRC Systems GmbH, Germany).

Pre- and post-HFS scanning sessions were identical and comprised mechanical stimulation of first right (test site) and subsequently left (control site) volar forearms, delivered to one arm at a time. The event-related design fMRI task involved trains of three identical punctate stimuli (0.5s duration each) administered using a 26g MRI-safe computer-controlled von-Frey hair, followed by a rating period of perceived pain intensity (8s duration). Stimulation trains were repeated 12 times, resulting in a total task duration of 11 minutes and 30 seconds.

MRI data were acquired on a 3T GE Signa Premier XT System, equipped with a 48-channel head and neck coil, a 60-channel posterior array coil, and a 30-channel AIR anterior array coil. Functional data included custom shimming procedures4 and covered the whole cervical spinal cord over 38 axial slices, of which the inferior-most slice passed through the middle of the T2 vertebra (267 volumes, TR = 2500ms, TE = 30ms, flip angle = 90°, field of view = 180mm, in-plane voxel size = 1.41mm x 1.41mm, slice gap = 1.5mm, acquisition matrix = 96 × 96, reconstruction matrix = 128 × 128). A T2-weighted structural scan was also acquired (56 slices, TR = 2500ms, TE = 123ms, echo train length = 78, flip angle = 90°, field of view = 300mm, in-plane voxel size = 0.59 x 0.59mm, slice thickness = 0.8 mm, acquisition matrix = 320 x 320).

Pain ratings reported during scanning were analysed using a 2x2 repeated-measures ANOVA, with stimulation site (test vs. control) and time (pre-HFS vs. post-HFS) as factors. fMRI data were motion corrected for x and y translations and smoothed with a 2mm full width at half maximum in-plane Gaussian kernel using tools from AFNI and FSL. Functional data were registered to the PAM50 template via subject-specific T2-weighted volumes using spinal rootlet-based registration available in the Spinal Cord Toolbox5.

Subject-level models (FSL FEAT) included explanatory regressors for stimulation and rating periods, along with nuisance regressors accounting for physiological noise (cardiac, respiratory, cerebrospinal fluid, and white matter signals), in-scan motion, and volume censoring regressors (DVARS > 75th percentile + 1.5 of the interquartile range). Stimulation periods were contrasted against rest to investigate sensory processing. Test-site and control-site analyses were conducted independently. Group-level effects of HFS were modelled using a paired-samples t-test (pre-HFS vs. post-HFS). Additionally, the overall effect of mechanical stimulation delivered during scanning was assessed using a one-sample t-test of the pre-HFS session for each stimulation site. Group-level inference was conducted using randomise (FSL) with threshold-free cluster enhancement (5000 permutations, p-FWE < 0.05).

Results

We observed a significant site by time interaction in subjectively reported pain (F(1,31) = 10.87 p < 0.001), driven by an increase in pain ratings at the test site following HFS (pre-HFS mean ± SD = 13.7 ± 14.9; post-HFS mean ± SD = 19.1 ± 19.7; t(31) = 3.844 p < .001), but not the control site (pre-HFS mean ± SD = 16.5 ± 18.6; post-HFS mean ± SD = 15.9 ± 17.7; t(31) = 0.423 p = 0.337). 

By contrast we did not observe significant between-scan effects of HFS on responses to stimulation, nor to punctate stimulation during the pre-HFS session, at either the test or control sites.

Conclusion

HFS led to an increase in pain ratings in accord with hypothesised mechanical hyperalgesia. This finding echoes preclinical and human literature demonstrating HFS as an effective sensitisation methodology. The expected behavioural change however was not accompanied by a change in spinal cord activation.

Our null finding of changes in cord activity contrast with a previous report of increased cord activity in response to mechanical stimulation following a heat/capsaicin sensitisation procedure, with changes observed at spinal levels corresponding to the stimulated dermatomes3. However, their sensitisation protocol produced a larger behavioural effect, approximately a 15-point increase in pain ratings compared to a 5-point increase observed in our own study. We speculate that a smaller behavioural effect size reduced our ability to detect corresponding changes in spinal-neuronal activity.

Our examination of pre-HFS responses to mechanical stimulation alone also did not result in discernible activity within the cord. BOLD responses in the spinal cord following mechanical stimulation have been described, but both increases and decreases in activation have been reported2. Interpretation of these inconsistent findings, as well as the null finding observed here, contrast with well-established electrophysiology literature describing spikes in sensory neurons in the spinal cord elicited by noxious and non-noxious mechanical stimulation. Equivocation in responses to mechanical stimulation contrasts with thermal stimulation, which has been shown repeatedly to elicit increases in spinal cord BOLD signal2. We speculate that detecting mechanical stimulation using spinal fMRI may present methodological challenges that differs from other sensory modalities.

In conclusion we did not detect a significant effect of HFS on cervical spinal cord responses to mechanical stimulation. We suggest that this null finding most likely reflects methodological limitations, given consistent preclinical reports of HFS induced behavioural hyperalgesia and modulated neural responses in the spinal cord.  We suggest that experimental sensitisation models remain effective tools to study pain in humans behaviourally but suggest that further development of spinal fMRI methodology is necessary to reliably characterize their spinal mechanisms of action.
