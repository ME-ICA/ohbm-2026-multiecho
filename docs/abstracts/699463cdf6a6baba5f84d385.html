Introduction

Fetal MRI has opened the possibility of understanding brain development during gestation with many applications to neurodevelopmental pathologies. Although large cross-sectional databases have been proven very useful, they inherently confound two factors: individual development and the emergence of inter-subject variability. To separate the two, longitudinal data is necessary. Nevertheless, most existing databases offer little to no longitudinal data. In addition, for a precise description of all developmental events, a fine temporal sampling (e.g. once a week) is required. Usually, when longitudinal data are available, they contain at most 2 or 3 timepoints per subject. Such database does not exist and is difficult, if not impossible, to acquire in humans. We present here such fetal database that we acquired and processed in baboons, the largest non-human primate that can be scanned. The database comes with fully reconstructed and preprocessed data and will be made openly accessible.

Methods

We acquired data on 7 female baboons (papio anubis) during their gestation, in the second half of gestation between 13wGA and 24wGA (total gestation time : 26.5 weeks), once a week for 10 weeks each (Fig. 1).  An anesthesia protocol was designed to not arm the fetus and disturb its development, by using dexmedetomidine, the only known non-neurotoxic agent that still induces a good level of sedation1. MR acquisitions were performed on a Prisma 3T scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) using a 32-channel receive-only phased-array spine coil in combination with an 18-channel receive-only flexible body coil. For each session, we acquired T2 HASTE anatomical sequences, diffusion data with a 2D spin echo sequence with reverse phase encoding, with 2 volumes at b=0 s/mm2 and 30 volumes at b=1000 s/mm2, and functional resting state data for a duration of 10mn with TR/TE=2000/30ms. Anatomical and diffusion data were acquired under the form of 6 stacks in 3 orthogonal directions, i.e. 2 stacks per directions. T2w HASTE stacks were acquired at resolution 0.63x0.63x2 mm3, and diffusion stacks were acquired at resolution 1.1x1.1x3 mm3. Functional resting-state images were acquired at 2.5mm isotropic resolution. The sum of all acquisitions for a session was limited to one hour.  

Reconstruction: after acquisition, 2D stacks were used to reconstruct high-resolution isotropic volumes using slice-to-volume super resolution techniques. Specifically, for T2w data we used the Niftymic framework2 and produced 0.5mm3 isotropic data (Fig. 1) in a standard orientation. For diffusion data, after preprocessing of stacks (denoising, artifacts, distortions and movements correction), an equivalent framework was used to reconstruct the b=0 diffusion data at 0.5mm3 isotropic, using the slice-to-volume reconstruction toolkit (SVRTK3). For b=1000 diffusion data, we designed a dedicated reconstruction pipeline based also on SVRTK3 that resulted in a full diffusion-weighted dataset reconstructed at the 0.5 mm isotropic resolution in the same orientation as the T2w data (Fig. 1), using an iterative single-shell reconstruction method that models the signal with spherical harmonics basis3. 

Preprocessing: reconstructed anatomical T2w volumes were segmented in 4 classes (GM, WM, ventricules, CSF) using an in-house trained nnUnet model (Fig.1). Reconstructed diffusion data were preprocessed using MRTRix3, resulting in tensor estimation and fractional anisotropy (FA), colored FA, and mean diffusivity (MD) maps, together with constrained spherical deconvolution (CSD) models to perform tractography (Fig.1). Functional resting state volumes were minimally preprocessed to correct for movements, regress confounds and apply band-pass filters to retain typical resting state frequency bands of interest.

Results

Acquisitions were successfully performed on all 7 female baboons for at least 9 of the 10 timepoints (Fig. 1). Reasons for missing sessions were scanner unavailability or the female being too stressed and resistant to sedation. High-quality isotropic data were reconstructed for each session (Fig.1). Anatomical segmentation reached an average DICE score of 0.942 (up to 0.985 for WM) on a test set. Quantitative metrics were computed over gestation days (such as tissue volumes, cortical and white matter FA and MD) and showed consistency across subjects as well as average trajectories that fit with existing cross-sectional results published in humans4,5 (Fig. 2).

All raw data, as well as reconstructed data and preprocessed data will be made freely available in 2026 in a BIDS compliant structure. Reconstructions pipelines are also available on github repositories : https://github.com/MecaLab/Babofet_DWI and https://github.com/MecaLab/Babofet_T2w/tree/main/nifty_module.

Conclusion

We produced the first high-temporal resolution longitudinal fetal database on non-human primates with T2w and diffusion data at high spatial resolution, as well as resting-state functional data. This dataset will be made available freely to the community and will be instrumental for studying in-utero brain development in primates with a fine temporal resolution as well as characterizing the emergence of variability. Note that providing all data, from raw to reconstructed to preprocessed will also provide essential data for methodological research, such as super-resolution reconstruction or segmentation techniques.
