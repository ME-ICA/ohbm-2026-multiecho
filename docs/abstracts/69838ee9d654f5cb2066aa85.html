Introduction

Human language relies on combinatorial structure supported by a left-lateralized fronto-temporal network^1. A central debate concerns whether this network contains distinct syntactic and semantic subregions or whether these computations are distributed throughout the system. Early fMRI studies suggested syntax-selective hubs within posterior temporal and inferior frontal cortex ^2, while more recent work with individualized functional localization reported substantial overlap between syntactic and semantic sensitivities and found no evidence for dedicated syntactic regions^3. At the same time, neurolinguistic studies using syntactic-movement paradigms and intracranial recordings point to finer-grained functional specialization within these areas.

Ultra-high-field (7T) fMRI provides the spatial resolution needed to reconcile these findings, revealing millimetric heterogeneity within language-related cortex in other domains. We therefore used 7T fMRI with single-subject analyses to test whether (i) classical language regions fractionate into subpopulations with differential sensitivity to syntax and semantics, and (ii) syntactic movement in short, semantically impoverished sentences elicits distinct patterns within these subpopulations. Participants completed a state-of-the-art visual language localizer followed by a reading task involving short sentences with syntactic movement. Our results show interleaved but dissociable neural populations within temporal and inferior frontal language areas, with syntactic movement increasing responses in syntax-sensitive voxels but decreasing activity in semantic-sensitive voxels. These findings support a finer-grained functional architecture of the language network than suggested by standard-resolution fMRI.

Methods

Ethics
Participants gave written informed consent and were compensated. Procedures were approved by the local ethics committee at NeuroSpin (CPP 100055) and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants
Twenty right-handed native French speakers (8 women; mean age 26.1 ± 7.6) meeting standard MRI safety criteria completed the study. All performed one run of Task 1 (RSVP truth-judgment) and four runs of Task 2 (single-glance mini-sentences).

Replication cohort
To validate the results of Task 1, we analyzed data from 13 participants of the iCortex longitudinal study who performed the same RSVP paradigm with a broader stimulus set. Analyses were restricted to the five language conditions shared with the present study.

Data acquisition
Scanning was performed on a 7T Siemens Magnetom using a 1Tx/32Rx head coil. Functional images were acquired with 2D gradient-echo EPI (TR=2000 ms, TE=21 ms, 1.2 mm isotropic voxels, multiband=2, 70 slices). Reverse-phase encoding runs were collected before each task run for distortion correction. High-resolution MP2RAGE anatomical scans (0.65 mm isotropic) were collected mid-session. Stimuli were presented visually on a BOLDscreen 32 display and viewed through a mirror. Total scan time per participant was ~90 minutes.

Experimental design
Task 1: RSVP truth judgment.
Participants judged 100 visually presented sentences across five conditions varying in linguistic structure: consonant strings, word lists, semantically anomalous sentences, generic meaningful sentences, and contextual meaningful sentences. Sentences were matched on length and presented word-by-word (350 ms/word). A true/false response followed each trial. This task served as the language localizer and provided syntax- and semantics-responsive voxels.

Task 2: Single-glance mini-sentences.
Participants viewed brief (200 ms) 3-word stimuli in an RPVP format. Ten conditions were included: three controls (single words, 3-word lists, ungrammatical sequences) and seven grammatical conditions differing by minimal syntactic manipulations (affirmative ± clitic movement; yes/no questions ± verb movement; wh-questions in situ, wh-movement, wh+verb movement). Stimuli were matched on length, syllable count, and letter number. Occasional match-to-sample trials ensured task engagement.

Preprocessing and statistical analysis
Preprocessing used fMRIPrep 22.0.2. First-level GLMs were estimated with nilearn, modeling all task conditions plus motion and physiological confounds; a 2.4 mm smoothing kernel (2× voxel size) was applied. Group-level analyses used second-level GLMs with 6 mm smoothing. Unless noted, statistical maps used voxel-wise p<0.001 and FDR correction at α<0.05.

ROI definition and analysis
Task 1 contrasts identified syntax-responsive voxels (Semantically Anomalous > Word Lists) and semantic-responsive voxels (Generic Sentences > Semantically Anomalous). Voxels exceeding p<0.001 (cluster size ≥4) were intersected with individualized Glasser parcellations restricted to 25 language regions. Beta estimates were extracted at native resolution (1.2 mm). The replication cohort followed the same procedure.

Cross-validation of ROI stability was performed using a leave-one-run-out procedure on the 5-run iCortex dataset. A syntactico-semantic index was computed in each ROI based on responses to lists of words, semantically anomalous sentences, and generic sentences.

Statistical modeling
Beta values for syntax- and semantics-tuned voxels were entered into repeated-measures ANOVAs to assess condition effects across regions and tasks. For Task 2, ROIs were retained if ≥30% of participants contributed both voxel types. Additional ANOVAs examined responses to grammatical conditions and the effect of syntactic-movement load (0, 1, or 2 movement operations).

Results

RSVP task reveals robust syntax- and semantics-sensitive regions
The hierarchical RSVP task elicited strong activations across the left-lateralized language network. Semantically anomalous sentences with preserved syntax reliably activated superior temporal (STG), inferior frontal (IFG), and middle frontal (MFG) regions. Prevalence analysis showed high individual consistency (aSTG: 100%, pSTG: 80%, MFG: 90%, IFG: 90–100%), replicated in a second cohort (n=13). Meaningful generic sentences recruited an extended network, including middle temporal (MTG), angular (AG), and additional MFG regions, again with high prevalence (MTG: 90%, AG: 90%, MFG: 70–85%). These results confirm hierarchical sensitivity of temporal–frontal regions to increasing semantic content.

Individual-subject mapping reveals interleaved syntax and semantic patches
Single-subject maps showed a fine-grained mosaic of syntax- and semantic-selective patches with limited overlap. Interdigitated clusters appeared along the superior temporal sulcus (STS), with local preferences for syntax or semantics varying across individuals but consistently coexisting within classical temporal and frontal regions. These observations motivated a parcellation-based quantification using Glasser regions.

A syntactico-semantic index differentiates language regions
To capture each region’s tuning, we computed an index from 0 (semantic-dominant) to 1 (syntax-dominant). Across 25 Glasser language regions, values ranged from ~0.47 to ~0.80, indicating a continuum rather than a binary division. Posterior STS (STSda, STSdp) and IFG regions (45, 47l) showed the highest indices and largest proportions of syntax-selective voxels (>90%), whereas low-index regions contained predominantly semantic voxels. Index values correlated with voxel composition, revealing a functional gradient across STS, MTG, and IFG.

Cross-validated responses confirm distinct activation profiles
 Using a dataset with 5 RSVP runs, we defined syntax- and semantic-selective ROIs from 4 runs and extracted responses from the left-out run. A repeated-measures ANOVA (condition × voxel-type) across 21 regions (≥60% voxel availability) showed a strong interaction, confirming divergent profiles. Syntax voxels responded similarly to semantically anomalous and meaningful sentences, whereas semantic voxels showed markedly reduced activation for anomalous sentences. These differences were robust across regions.

Mini-sentence task dissociates syntax and semantic networks
We next tested whether these two populations generalize to minimal 3-word sentences with reduced semantics. Across 17 regions (≥30% voxel availability), a significant condition × voxel-type interaction again confirmed distinct tuning. Syntax voxels increased activation for sentences containing syntactic movement (clitic, verb, or wh-movement), whereas semantic voxels decreased activation to the same stimuli. This opposite pattern produced a strong movement × voxel-type interaction, indicating that syntactic structure alone selectively drives syntax-tuned voxels. Additional region-by-condition interactions showed that linguistic computations vary in degree across temporal and frontal regions.

Summary
Together, the RSVP and mini-sentence tasks reveal two interdigitated but functionally dissociable voxel populations within the language network: one driven by syntactic structure—even without meaning—and one driven by semantic content. These findings, enabled by 7T single-subject resolution, suggest a finer-grained architecture than that observed in standard-resolution fMRI studies.

Conclusion

Using ultra-high-field 7T fMRI, we identified two interdigitated but functionally distinct voxel populations within the language network: one driven by syntactic structure alone (“syntax voxels”) and one requiring semantic composition (“semantics voxels”). Across two tasks—a hierarchical RSVP localizer and a mini-sentence paradigm—syntax voxels responded robustly whenever structure was preserved, even without meaning, whereas semantics voxels required meaningful combinations. Cross-validation in an independent cohort confirmed these dissociations, and responses to minimal 3-word sentences showed opposite effects of syntactic movement across voxel types.

These findings echo early proposals of syntactic hubs in posterior STG and IFG based on Jabberwocky, syntactic complexity, and lesion studies, as well as evidence from sign language and syntactic-tree manipulation^2. They also align with neurolinguistic studies of syntactic movement in Hebrew, Japanese, and Korean, which consistently implicate posterior temporal and inferior frontal regions. Rather than isolating a single locus, our results suggest that multiple syntax-tuned patches distributed across temporal and frontal cortex support movement operations with varying sensitivities.

Recent work using individualized ROIs has challenged syntactic specialization, proposing a distributed network uniformly sensitive to syntax and semantics^1. Our results help reconcile these views. Hierarchical contrasts revealed that syntax- and semantics-selective voxels coexist within most language regions, although the semantic population is smaller and harder to detect with broad contrasts. Furthermore, large anatomical ROIs obscure fine-scale heterogeneity: single-subject maps revealed voxel populations crossing classical boundaries. Using Glasser parcellation, we observed graded specializations—for example, posterior STG parcels dominated by syntax voxels and angular gyrus parcels dominated by semantic voxels. This gradient matches intracranial studies showing millimetric specialization for composition, lexical processing, and sentence-level integration.

Our findings underscore the advantages of 7T fMRI for bridging the gap between intracranial resolution and whole-brain coverage. Recent work on the VWFA and mathematical areas has similarly revealed patchwork functional architectures where standard-resolution fMRI suggested homogeneous regions^4. Ultra-high-field imaging enabled us to capture comparable micro-organization within the language network and characterize syntax and semantics with unprecedented spatial precision.

Several limitations warrant mention. Identifying semantics voxels required sufficient meaningful input, as shown by the increased voxel counts in the replication cohort exposed to more sentences. Group-level analyses of syntactic movement were less stable at 7T, reflecting known alignment challenges. Finally, using semantically anomalous real-word sentences rather than Jabberwocky limits direct comparison with pseudoword studies; future work should include both to refine lexical vs syntactic contributions.

In conclusion, this 7T study provides strong evidence for coexisting syntax- and semantics-selective subnetworks within left perisylvian cortex. While they map onto known macroscopic language regions, their fine-scale organization varies across individuals, underscoring the value of single-subject analyses and hierarchically structured localizers. Ultra-high-field fMRI offers a powerful path toward uncovering this architecture and advancing models of how the brain computes linguistic structure and meaning.
