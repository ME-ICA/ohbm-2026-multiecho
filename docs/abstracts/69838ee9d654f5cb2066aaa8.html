Introduction

Fluent speech relies on the brain’s ability to continuously compare predicted sensory consequences of an utterance with the auditory and somatosensory feedback it receives. In feedforward–feedback models, motor commands are guided by internal predictions, while auditory and somatosensory systems monitor the produced signal for mismatches1,2. Discrepancies generate error signals that are relayed back to motor regions to adjust articulation in real time, ensuring accurate and intelligible speech. Perturbing auditory feedback provides a powerful method for probing this monitoring system. Delayed auditory feedback (DAF), in particular, disrupts the temporal alignment between predicted and perceived speech, causing many speakers to slow down3. Yet susceptibility to DAF varies widely, and the neural mechanisms underlying these differences remain poorly understood.

Most models emphasize cortical interactions among auditory, somatosensory, and motor regions, but the roles of subcortical structures and the white-matter pathways linking these systems are less clear. A key candidate is the arcuate fasciculus (AF), a major dorsal-stream tract connecting frontal motor regions with parietal somatosensory and temporal auditory cortices. Its long, anterior, and posterior segments support processes central to speech monitoring, including auditory–motor transformation, somatosensory integration, and error-based correction.

To investigate the neural basis of speech monitoring under temporal perturbation, we combined fMRI, diffusion-weighted imaging, and in-scanner speech recordings during speaking with and without DAF. Our goals were to:
 (1) identify the cortical and subcortical networks engaged during perturbed feedback,
 (2) determine how AF micro- and macrostructure contributes to monitoring, and
 (3) uncover neural substrates underlying individual differences in DAF-induced slowing.

Methods

Thirty-one native Dutch speakers completed an fMRI experiment with three conditions: passive listening, speaking with normal feedback, and speaking with 200-ms delayed feedback. Across trials, participants viewed or heard 20 three-syllable words while sparse-sampling functional MRI captured hemodynamic responses and synchronized audio recordings enabled precise measurement of articulation durations. These durations were used to compute a susceptibility index (SI) reflecting how strongly each participant slowed under DAF. Structural (T1-weighted and DWI) and functional data (BOLD) were acquired on a 3T scanner. fMRI data were preprocessed using fMRIPrep and analyzed with SPM using contrasts between DAF and normal-feedback conditions. SI values were included as a covariate in group-level models to identify neural correlates of individual differences. Diffusion data were corrected for distortions and reconstructed with spherical deconvolution. Deterministic tractography was used to isolate the three major segments of the arcuate fasciculus (anterior, posterior, long). For each segment, tract volume and HMOA (hindrance-modulated orientational anisotropy) were extracted as indices of macro- and microstructural integrity. These measures were correlated with individual susceptibility to DAF.

Results

Speaking under DAF significantly slowed articulation relative to normal feedback (paired t-test: t = 7.859, p < 0.001). The susceptibility index captured substantial interindividual variability in this slowing. At the group level, DAF elicited stronger activation than normal feedback across bilateral auditory regions (STG/SMG), right-lateralized motor and premotor regions (IFG, precentral gyrus, SMA), the cerebellum, and the right anterior insula. These effects reflect heightened reliance on auditory and somatosensory monitoring and increased demands on motor control when temporal predictions are violated. Including SI as a covariate revealed that individuals most affected by DAF recruited a predominantly left-lateralized network, including the left IFG, precentral gyrus, SMG, ACC, and insula, along with the cerebellar vermis. This pattern represents a hemispheric shift relative to the group-average response to DAF, which showed a right-hemisphere bias. Analyses of the AF’s three segments revealed a tract-specific pattern: higher DAF susceptibility was associated with smaller HMOA in the right posterior AF (Spearman p = 0.004, FDR-corrected p = 0.046) and larger right long-segment AF volume, although this latter effect did not survive FDR correction (Spearman p = 0.013, FDR p = 0.078). No significant effects were observed in the left AF or in the anterior segments.

Conclusion

Fluent speech relies on the coordination of feedforward motor predictions with feedback-based error monitoring. DAF disrupts this alignment, forcing the system to reconcile mismatched auditory and somatosensory signals with expected outcomes. By linking behavioral susceptibility to DAF with functional activation and white-matter anatomy, our study shows that resilience versus vulnerability to temporal perturbation depends on distinct neural architectures. Fluent performance under DAF was supported by a right-lateralized feedback-control network integrating auditory, somatosensory, and motor signals, and reinforced by a more robust right posterior arcuate fasciculus. In contrast, individuals who slowed more showed stronger recruitment of left-hemisphere speech-motor regions, likely reflecting poorly inhibited activation across left hemisphere speech motor regions, leading to overactive monitoring processes. 

Examining the AF’s anterior, posterior, and long segments revealed a division of labor: the posterior AF supports cross-sensory integration of auditory and somatosensory error signals, stabilizing fluency under perturbation; the long segment may reflect increased auditory–motor coupling, suggesting that heavier weighting of auditory feedback renders individuals more vulnerable when timing predictions are violated (Figure 1). The anterior AF showed no evidence of contributing to DAF susceptibility.

Together, these results provide a mechanistic account of why some speakers remain fluent under temporal disruption while others do not, highlighting the critical role of right-hemisphere feedback pathways in maintaining stable speech and offering new insights into the neural organization of speech monitoring.
