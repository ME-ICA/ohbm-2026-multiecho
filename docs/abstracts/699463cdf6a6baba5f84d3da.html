Introduction

Fibromyalgia (FM) is a chronic widespread pain disease affecting predominantly women, and involves other symptoms such as fatigue, cognitive impairment, and sleep disturbances1. 

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is an affective mental disorder characterized by low mood, anhedonia, and diminished motivation, involving as well negative views about oneself, the world, and the future2.   

FM and MDD not only share clinical symptoms3, but also an alteration in self-concept. Chronic pain involves a loss of sense of self, intertwining with the disease symptoms through a process known as enmeshment, promoting greater pain-related suffering3. Despite the scarcity of literature in this regard, limited studies have found that in fibromyalgia, the recollection of experiences related to pain catastrophizing has been associated with increased activation in the Posterior Cingulate Cortex (PCC), a key node of the Default Mode Network (DMN) that is strongly involved in self-referential processing and the retrieval of prior personal experiences4. Similarly, MDD has also been consistently associated with an enhanced activation of cortical midline structures conforming the DMN while processing self-referential stimuli, which would explain the constant state of hyper-self-alertness of depression5.

To date, no fMRI studies have examined brain activation in fibromyalgia and depression during illness-related self-judgment, nor has any neural signature been identified that discriminates self-referential processing within a disease context from that occurring in a general context.

We hypothesize that patients will have greater involvement of regions related to the DMN when self-judging in the context of disease compared to a general self-judgement -not semantically related to the disease-, and this will be enhanced in patients -in contrast to healthy women-.

Methods

62 women with FM, 32 with MDD, and 56 age-matched controls (p>0.8) underwent an fMRI task that elicits a process of internally oriented self-judgement. The task consists of blocks of self-judgement of general personality traits (e.g., “disrespectful”, “loyal”, “wise”, “impolite”), self-judgement of disease-related traits (related to aspects concerning both pathologies; e.g., “energetic”, “weak”, “exhausted”, “strong”), and a vowel-counting attentional-control condition. In both self-judgement conditions, participants indicated whether each trait described them by pressing yes (right button) or no (left button). The two conditions were matched in terms of valence (positive/negative traits). In the vowel-counting (control) condition, participants had to ignore the meaning of the adjectives and instead count whether the word contained more than four vowels (yes) or less (no), using the same button mapping.

Functional MRI data was acquired during the task on a Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma Fit 3T scanner using a 64-channel head coil. T2*-weighted functional images were collected with a multiband-accelerated echo-planar imaging sequence optimized for BOLD contrast (TR: 800 ms, TE: 37 ms, flip angle: 45°, matrix: 64×64, voxel size: 3.3×3.3×3.5 mm, 48 axial slices, multiband acceleration factor: 4).

Functional MRI data was preprocessed using the standard pipeline of the CONN toolbox (v23) running on MATLAB R2024b in a BIDS-compliant workflow. First-level analysis were conducted in SPM12. For each participant, condition-specific regressors were modeled and individual contrast images were generated for general self-judgement > vowel, disease-related self-judgement > vowel, general self-judgement > baseline, disease-related self-judgement > baseline. 

A whole-brain support vector machine (SVM) (CANLab Core Toolbox) was trained to identify a brain pattern that distinguishes Self-Disease judgement (disease-related self-judgement > vowel) from Self-General judgment (general self-judgement > vowel), ensuring that the attentional condition served as a common comparator. Models were trained separately for healthy controls, and for the patient group (FM and MDD samples combined).  To assess voxel-wise weight stability, a bootstrap resampling using 5,000 iterations was performed on the SVM model and identified significant voxels surviving FDR correction at q<0.05. 

Using the obtained signatures (one by group), pattern expressions were computed for each task contrast (Self-Disease > baseline; Self-General > baseline) within each group. This quantified how strongly each contrast expressed the signature. These values were then statistically compared within each group to determine whether the signature was significantly expressed more strongly in one self-judgement condition than the other.

The study protocol was approved by the Bioethics Commission of the University of Barcelona and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation.

Results

At a behavioral level, significant differences in FM and MDD (vs. controls) were observed on the endorsement (percentage of “yes” answers) of general traits. The group*valence interaction was significant (F=14.73, p<0.001), indicating that endorsement rates for positive and negative traits varied by group. Specifically, patients endorsed negative traits to a greater extent, and positive ones to a lesser extent. Significant differences also emerged in disease-related traits, where patients endorsed fewer positive traits (T=9.52, p<0.001) and more negative ones (T=11.38, p<0.001). However, the group*valence interaction on the endorsement of disease-related traits was significantly larger (F=108.01, p<0.001) than in the general condition. 

Regarding the support vector machine model, the control-group signature showed high classification performance, with a 93% sensitivity, specificity and accuracy, and a non-parametric AUC of 0.99. The signature for patients also displayed robust performance, with a 91% sensitivity, specificity and accuracy, and a non-parametric AUC of 0.96, indicating strong discriminative capacity in both groups. 

After bootstrap resampling, different stability patterns in the weight distributions were observed. Although healthy controls and patients exhibited broadly similar neuroanatomical involvement, the spatial extent and significance of surviving clusters differed markedly. In controls, significant bootstrapped clusters predicting positively for the condition Self-Disease (Positive effect: 71 voxels, min p-value: 0.00000204) were primarily located in the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and left temporoparietal junction. In contrast, patients showed a more extensive and stable cluster in the precuneus / posterior cingulate cortex, together with a bilateral temporoparietal junction engagement, and a cluster in the left anterior temporal pole (Figure) (Positive effect: 390 voxels, min p-value: 0.00000016). 

Additionally, negative predicting voxels (predicting towards the Self-General condition) were found in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) in controls (Negative effect: 6 voxels, min p-value: 0.00001939), whereas for patients these were also found in the mPFC, in addition to the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) (Negative effect: 148 voxels, min p-value: 0.00000207).

A Neurosynth meta-analytic semantic decoding of the unthresholded (not bootstrapped) signature for controls revealed that the most related terms to the functional map of weights were: sentence, words, frontal, sentence comprehension, linguistic, or reading. However, for patients, most related terms were: precuneus, posterior cingulate, memory retrieval, PCC, recognition, social cognition, default mode network.  

After computing pattern expression values of both contrasts of interest with the obtained signatures, significant differences between both contrasts for the group of healthy subjects (T=153, p<0.001), and for the group of patients (T=75.93, p<0.001) were observed, corroborating that the signatures robustly discriminate between general and disease-related self-judgement processes, both in healthy controls and in patients. 

Conclusion

Behaviorally, patients showed a marked bias toward endorsing more negative and fewer positive traits, particularly when these traits were framed within the context of illness-related behavior. This pattern indicates a stronger internalization of disease-specific attributes and suggests an enmeshment of self-identity with disease symptoms4.

At the brain level, bootstrap analyses revealed a divergence between groups in the stability of voxel contributions to the predictive signature, referring to a mental process of self-judgement that is specific of the “disease behavior”. While both groups showed involvement of regions typically associated with social cognition, patients exhibited substantially greater stability within posterior Default Mode Network (DMN) regions, specifically in the posterior cingulate cortex, consistently related to autobiographical memory and self-related thought5.

The Neurosynth semantic decoding further highlights the qualitative differences between groups in the representations captured by the SVM unthresholded map. In controls, the functional weight map was primarily associated with linguistic and perceptual terms, suggesting that their pattern reflects a more neutral, task-driven processing style focused on the linguistic features of the stimuli rather than on their personal relevance. In contrast, the patient signature was more strongly linked to self-referential and memory-related constructs, indicating that patients engage more autobiographical, internally oriented, and self-referential processes when evaluating illness-related traits.

Taken together, the robust and spatially extended posterior DMN contribution observed in patients—contrasted with its minimal stability in controls—suggests that illness-related self-judgement in chronic pain and depression relies more heavily on memory-based and self-referential systems, accompanied by stronger endorsement of illness-related traits. This pattern highlights a neural mechanism through which maladaptive self-beliefs may be constructed and maintained, with posterior DMN nodes (precuneus, PCC) acting as core hubs for integrating autobiographical and self-relevant information when evaluating illness-related attributes. In contrast, the mPFC would be more engaged in processing general aspects of the self, suggesting a functional differentiation within anterior–posterior DMN nodes, according to the type of self-referential judgement involved.

These findings underscore the importance of targeting self-identity in psychotherapeutic interventions, and provide neurobiological evidence supporting its relevance in clinical populations.
