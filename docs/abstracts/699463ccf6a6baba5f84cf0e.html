Introduction

Aging involves progressive molecular and cellular damage that increases the risk of neurodegeneration, cognitive decline, and dementia [1]. Reliable biomarkers of biological aging are essential to quantify aging processes, assess individual disease risk, and develop targeted interventions.

Brain age, inferred from structural MRI scans using machine learning models trained on healthy individuals to predict age, has been proposed as such a biomarker [2]. These models assume that biological and chronological age align in healthy brains, thereby characterizing normative aging patterns. In order for Brain Age models to be clinically useful biomarkers, they should preferably be able to predict future structural and cognitive decline, and ultimately the onset of age-related disease such as Alzheimer’s Disease. However, despite high accuracy in age estimation, it has been previously shown that cross-sectional brain age models fail to predict disease conversion, gray matter atrophy, or cognitive decline. A likely source of these limitations is that cross-sectional data cannot disentangle confounding baseline effects from age-related biological changes [3].

To overcome these issues, it has been suggested to use multiple MRI scans from the same individuals [4]. Rather than predicting age at isolated timepoints, these methods aim to predict inter-scan intervals, and thereby capture aging-related changes while controlling for confounding baseline effects

In this study, we tested whether longitudinal brain age models [4] can distinguish cognitively normal individuals from those with cognitive impairment. We hypothesized that longitudinal models, due to their proposed advantages,  would demonstrate superior discriminative ability compared to traditional cross-sectional approaches.

Methods

We used 3-Tesla (3T) brain MRI data from the OASIS-3 study (https://sites.wustl.edu/oasisbrains/home/oasis-3/). For model training, we included only cognitively normal individuals with at least two MRI scans. From participants with more than two scans, we generated all possible scan pairs with positive inter-scan intervals, yielding 2,039 scan pairs from 1,000 participants (327 male, 461 female, 212 missing) with a mean age of 68.7 ± 9.8 years; The mean scan interval was 3.42 years, with an average number of 2.35 scans per participant. Inter-scan intervals ranged from 0 to 12.3 years (mean: 4.18 years). 

For model evaluation, we included 41 cognitively impaired (CI) subjects (mean age 74.07, 30 male), and 41 age and sex matched cognitively normal (CN) individuals (mean age 74.03, 30 male). 

We preprocessed all MRI scans using FastSurfer, which performed brain extraction, bias-field correction, and segmentation. We then registered the brain-masked, bias-field-corrected images to the MNI152NLin2009cAsym template space using an affine transformation.

Figure 1 illustrates the general approach used to estimate the pace of aging from longitudinal data: paired scans are processed to extract features, which are then subtracted from each other and used to predict the time interval between scans. The predicted time interval (ṫ2,1) can then be divided by the actual time interval t2,1  to get a measure of estimated accelerated age:

p =ṫ2,1/t2,1     .    (eq. 1)

The predicted time interval ṫ2,1can either be estimated directly from a longitudinal model ṫ2,1=f(x1,x2), or it can be estimated by subtracting the predicted age (ẏ2) at timepoint two (follow-up) from the predicted age ẏ1 at timepoint one (baseline) ṫ2,1=ẏ2-ẏ1.

We evaluated three model architectures for predicting inter-scan intervals from longitudinal MRI data: (1) cross-sectional CNN trained to predict brain age, (2) LILAC Siamese architecture for paired scans to directly predict the time passed, (3) LILAC+ with non-linear prediction layers on top of LILAC. Additionally, we compute the predicted age deviation (PAD) from the cross-sectional model (1) for the baseline scan to compare the longitudinal models to the conventional framework. The prediction error was estimated using the mean absolute error (MAE) in a stratified (baseline age) 5-fold cross validation setting. For the final evaluation, the models were trained on the entire healthy dataset except the matched controls for the 41 participants with CI.

We used linear models with group (CI/CN), baseline age, and sex (M/F) as covariates to test for significant differences in aging pace between groups. Cohen's d was calculated as an unadjusted effect size measure.

Results

The models LILAC+ and LILAC could predict the time elapsed accurately, as indicated by a MAE of 0.99 years and 1.16 years respectively. The cross-sectional CNN had a MAE of 2.86 years. Please refer to Wegmann et al. 2025 [5] for an extensive analysis of the training results. The unadjusted pace of aging for cognitively impaired (CI, n = 41) and cognitively normal (CN, n = 41) individuals is presented in Figure 2. After adjusting for baseline age and sex, the pace of aging as estimated using LILAC was significantly increased in CI compared to CN individuals (difference = 0.223, 95% CI [0.039, 0.406], p = 0.018, Cohen's d = 0.505). The cross-sectional CNN model (difference = 0.255, 95% CI [-0.009, 0.519], p = 0.058, Cohen's d = 0.421), and longitudinal LILAC+ showed a smaller, non-significant difference (difference = 0.171, 95% CI [-0.025, 0.366], p = 0.086, Cohen's d = 0.379). All models demonstrated small-to-medium effect sizes. However, the largest effect size was observed when using cross-sectional baseline PAD to differentiate between the two clinical groups (difference = 2.696 years, 95% CI [0.880, 4.511], p = 0.004, Cohen's d = 0.52).

Conclusion

In this study, we implemented longitudinal models to estimate the pace of aging from longitudinal sMRI scans and evaluated their ability to differentiate between cognitively normal and cognitively impaired participants. The pace of aging estimated using the longitudinal LILAC model was significantly increased in cognitively impaired compared to cognitively normal individuals (p = 0.018, Cohen's d = 0.51). However, cross-sectional baseline PAD performed similarly when differentiating groups (p = 0.004, Cohen's d = 0.52). Estimating the time passed between scans is a more complex task as structural changes within the brain occur on different scales across the human lifespan. To get good estimates of the pace of aging, it is therefore important to have a uniform distribution of both baseline age and time interval in the training data. This is likely not the case in the small training sample used here. Future work should therefore focus on scaling the training data, and then assessing the validity of the pace measures, similar to Dörfel [3], to see whether the pace of aging suffers from similar constraints as cross-sectional brain age.
