Introduction

Language processing relies on two major white matter pathways with complementary functions: the dorsal and ventral streams. The dorsal stream is specialized in sensorimotor mapping, transforming speech sounds into motor commands, and supports speech repetition, articulatory planning, and phonological processing. Its main structural supports are the arcuate fasciculus (AF) and the superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF), connecting posterior temporal and frontal regions. The ventral stream enables sound-to-meaning mapping, supporting comprehension, semantic integration, and higher-level syntax, relying on the inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (IFOF), inferior longitudinal fasciculus (ILF), and uncinate fasciculus (UF) (Saur et al., 2008).

Language lateralization develops ontogenetically from an early interhemispheric predominance, mediated by the corpus callosum, toward intrahemispheric dominance, mainly supported by the left AF. Approximately 94–96% of right-handers, 76–78% of left-handers, and 85% of ambidextrous individuals show left-hemisphere dominance, while a small proportion shows atypical right-hemisphere lateralization (Mazoyer et al., 2014; Knecht et al., 2000), often linked to underdevelopment of the AF (Friederici et al., 2011).

Recently, a reduction in streamlines in the anterior AF has been reported as a potential mechanism underlying rightward lateralization in left-handers (Villar-Rodríguez et al., 2024). Building on this dorsal alteration, the present study aimed to investigate whether atypical language lateralization extends beyond the arcuate fasciculus to ventral language pathways, including the IFOF, ILF, and UF. Specifically, we hypothesized that atypical language lateralization would be associated with structural alterations in ventral language pathways, paralleling those previously reported in the dorsal stream.

Methods

A total of 307 participants were included. All were non-right-handed, resulting in a sample of 267 left-handers and 40 mixed-handers, as assessed by the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory (Laterality Quotient range: −100 to 20; mean ± SD = −73.8 ± 26.9). All participants wrote with their left hand. Ages ranged from 17 to 45 years (mean ± SD = 23.3 ± 5.1), with 148 males and 159 females. Restricting recruitment to non-right-handers increased variability in language lateralization phenotypes and extended previous work.

Language production was assessed using an fMRI verb generation task in which participants silently generated verbs in response to visually presented nouns during active blocks, alternating with control blocks involving aloud reading of letter pairs. The task followed a block design with six active and six control blocks of 30 seconds and was administered in Spanish using E-Prime 2.0.

MRI data were acquired on two 3T scanners. A total of 226 participants were scanned on a GE Signa Architect system using either a 24-channel (n = 145) or 48-channel (n = 81) head coil, while the remaining 81 participants were scanned on a Siemens Prisma system. High-resolution T1-weighted images were acquired for anatomical reference and use in automated tractography. Functional images were acquired during the task using T2*-weighted EPI sequences with comparable parameters across scanners (TR = 2500 ms, TE ≈ 25–30 ms). Diffusion-weighted images were acquired using three DTI protocols corresponding to each acquisition group: GE 24-channel (27 directions, b = 1000 s/mm²), GE 48-channel (50 directions, b = 1000 s/mm²), and Siemens Prisma (60 directions, b = 2000 s/mm²).

Task-related functional activation was estimated using a general linear model contrasting active versus control conditions after standard preprocessing in SPM12. Hemispheric language dominance was quantified using the Laterality Index (LI), computed with the bootstrap method implemented in the LI-toolbox. LI values were derived from activation in the pars opercularis and pars triangularis of the inferior frontal gyrus, and participants were classified as left-lateralized (LI > +40), right-lateralized (LI < −40), or ambilateral (−40 < LI < +40). This resulted in 211 left-lateralized (LL), 48 right-lateralized (RL), and 48 ambilateral (BIL) participants.

Diffusion MRI data underwent standard preprocessing, followed by automated whole-brain tractography and bundle segmentation using the Radwan/KUL_FWT pipeline. Mean tract volume was extracted for each reconstructed tract, and ComBat harmonization was applied to account for multi-scanner effects. Statistical analyses were conducted using a mixed-design ANOVA within the General Linear Model framework, with group as a between-subjects factor, tract and hemisphere as within-subjects factors, and age and total intracranial volume as covariates.

Results

The results of mixed-ANOVA analysis showed a significant interaction between tract hemisphere (Pillai’s Trace = .031, F(2,301) = 4.801, p = .009), indicating higher asymetry between hemispheres in the ILF (Pillai’s Trace = .032, F(1,304) = 9.877, p = .002).

Regarding language lateralization, significant differences were found in the mean tract volume among the LL, RL, and BL categories (F(2,302) = 4.293, p = .015), independent of hemisphere. The estimated marginal means adjusted for the covariates indicated that RL (63,907.863 mm³) had the highest average volume, followed by BL (61,491.941 mm³) and LL (60,283.276 mm³). Pairwise comparisons (post hoc LSD adjustment) confirmed that the significant difference was driven exclusively by the disparity between RL and LL (p = .004), with RL showing a significantly higher tract volume than Group L. No significant differences were found between RL vs. BL (p = .130) or BL vs. LL (p = .338) (see Figure 1).

Conclusion

The results of this study show that left-handed individuals with right-hemisphere language lateralization exhibit a significant increase in the volume of ventral pathway tracts (IFOF, ILF, and UF) compared to left-lateralized left-handers. These findings contrast with previous evidence showing that rightward language lateralization is associated with reduced streamline count and volume in the dorsal pathway, particularly in the anterior segment of the arcuate fasciculus. Taken together, these results suggest a potential mechanism of structural overcompensation associated with atypical language lateralization. Specifically, the ventral pathway may play a compensatory role in auditory comprehension and semantic integration when the dorsal pathway is relatively underdeveloped. Overall, these findings expand our understanding of structural brain plasticity in atypical language lateralization and highlight the importance of considering both dorsal and ventral pathways when investigating the neuroanatomical correlates of language.
