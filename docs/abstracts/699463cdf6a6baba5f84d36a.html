Introduction

Long considered to have only a motor function, the cerebellum has recently emerged as a key structure involved in higher brain functions such as emotion regulation and cognition1. The cerebellum has also been implicated in the pathophysiology of bipolar disorder, a condition characterized by mood instability and cognitive dysfunction². Recent neuroimaging studies have identified alterations in cerebellar cognitive regions in individuals with bipolar disorder, particularly within the posterior cerebellum. However, findings regarding these cerebellar alterations have been inconsistent, likely due to small sample sizes. Here, we investigated cerebellar anatomy in a large multicentric cohort of patients with bipolar disorder, leveraging the ENIGMA Bipolar Disorder dataset.

Methods

T1-weighted MRI scans were acquired on 3-Tesla scanners across 24 international sites and preprocessed with the ACAPULCO pipeline to enable automated parcellation of cerebellar lobules. An automated quality control protocol, following ENIGMA consortium guidelines, was implemented to ensure data reliability. For sites providing raw MRI data, a complementary visual inspection was performed to exclude images with insufficient quality. Statistical analyses were conducted using the Python statsmodel package. We used linear models to assess the effect of diagnosis on cerebellar anatomy, adjusted for site, sex, estimated total cranial volume and age to account for potential confounding factors. Within the bipolar disorder group, we investigated the effects of lithium medication and psychotic features by defining patient subgroups based on the availability of the relevant clinical information. All presented results were corrected for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR) method. 

Results

In our study, we analyzed data from 1397 patients with bipolar disorder (497 receiving lithium treatment, 533 with psychotic features) and 2019 healthy controls (mean age: controls 36 years, patients 38 years). Patients with bipolar disorder showed significantly smaller volumes in the Crus I region  compared to healthy control (β = 288.55, 95% CI [43.04, 534.06], pFDR = 0.037, Cohen’s f² = 0.0016). We also found reduced volume in the posterior vermis in bipolar patients compared to controls (β = 51.4763, 95% CI [9.730, 93.223], pFDR = 0.031, Cohen’s f² = 0.0017). Within the bipolar group, lithium-treated patients had larger anterior cerebellar lobules (β = 240.1276, 95% CI [36.789, 443.466], pFDR = 0.043, Cohen’s f² = 0.0045), and also exhibited larger Lobule IX compared to non-treated patients (β = 206.2110, 95% CI [48.593, 363.829], pFDR = 0.031, Cohen’s f² = 0.0055). Patients with psychotic features exhibited a significantly smaller posterior vermis than patients without psychotic features (β = -114.1788, 95% CI [-203.227, -25.131], pFDR = 0.030, Cohen’s f² = 0.0068).

Conclusion

Altogether, our findings are consistent with those of prior studies reporting smaller cerebellar volumes in cognitive-related posterior regions such as Crus I3. The observed reduction in the posterior vermis is particularly noteworthy, given its proposed role in emotion regulation4. In addition, the association between lithium treatment and larger anterior cerebellar regions replicates our previous findings and supports the idea that lithium has a neuroprotective effect on this cerebellar region5. These results underscore the importance of cerebellar anatomy in bipolar disorder and suggest that specific cerebellar regions may serve as potential targets for future therapeutic interventions. A key limitation of our present study is the inability to compare bipolar disorder subtypes due to the limited number of participants with Bipolar II disorder. Future work will include a Voxel-Based Morphometry analysis of the available raw MRI data to assess whether the present results are replicated at the voxel level.
