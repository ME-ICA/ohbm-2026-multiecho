Introduction

Understanding the neurobiological basis of mental illness remains a major challenge, owing to substantial overlap between diagnoses and pronounced heterogeneity within them1,2. Transdiagnostic concepts have therefore gained traction, emphasizing the need to study individual differences within and across diagnostic boundaries3. Previous work has indicated that distributed brain alterations in mental disorders follow shared, network-based patterns2,4, suggesting that shared symptoms and comorbidity may arise from co-occurring alterations in common systems. Large-scale datasets and individualized models are needed to identify the principles that govern systematic neurobiological variation in mental illness. Here, we adopt a systems-level, lifespan approach to map brain structural heterogeneity within and overlap between mental disorders, leveraging one of the largest transdiagnostic psychiatric imaging samples to date.

Methods

Through global collaborative efforts as part of the ENIGMA consortium (>160 sites), we analyzed region-wise, neuroimaging-derived cortical thickness (CT) estimates from 10,188 individuals with primarily one of six major mental disorders (schizophrenia [n=2753], autism spectrum [n=1612], bipolar [n=1370], obsessive-compulsive [n=1775], generalized anxiety [n=762], and major depressive [n=1916] disorders) and 12,251 control participants without any neuropsychiatric diagnosis (Fig. 1A). Using normative models on the data of 80% of controls, we generated a lifespan CT reference trajectory for the ages 5 to 80 years in 68 cortical regions. We then quantified individualized deviations from the reference cohort for patients and remaining controls as a benchmark for comparisons (Fig. 1B). After initially confirming case-control effects and within-disorder variability, we probed the inter-regional architecture of CT alterations across disorders. This aimed to reveal common organizational principles and a potential underlying network-like structure. We first applied principal component analysis to absolute deviation scores of patients and controls (Fig. 1D&E), revealing dominant axes along which CT deviations co-vary. Next, we mapped spatial patterns of within-disorder variability and between-disorder distribution overlaps, and investigated their alignment with the axes of CT deviation. Lastly, we examined whether extreme deviations (|z| > 1.96) are co-organized within a shared network. Using group-level structural and functional connectivity data from the Human Connectome Project (n=207, 83 males, mean age=28.73 ± 3.73 years), we derived a weighted reference connectome and tested whether extreme deviations tended to co-occur in highly connected regions (Fig. 1E).

Results

We observed that CT deviations in patients and control participants primarily covaried along two cortical axes: one spanning from (para)limbic to heteromodal cortex (PC1), and another contrasting unimodal with heteromodal/(para)limbic cortex (PC2; Fig. 2A). PC1 aligned with patterns of highly connected hubs observed in the normative weighted connectome (r=0.67, p_spin<0.001). PC2 recapitulated a dominant cortical hierarchy of neurobiological organization and maturation (r=0.73, p_spin=0.001)5. While the axes reflected normative patterns of covarying deviations that were also observed in the reference cohort, most disorders expressed a greater magnitude of deviations along these shared axes (Fig. 2A right). Variability within diagnostic groups (i.e., standard deviations) was structured along both axes, particularly the unimodal-transmodal axis (Fig. 2B). Moreover, extreme deviations were highly heterogeneous, maximally affecting the same region in less than 9% of individuals with the same diagnosis (Fig. 2C left). However, extreme deviations tended to co-occur in regions that are strongly connected to each other, indicating they occur in a network-like fashion. Hubs of co-occurring extreme deviations were located toward the heteromodal pole of PC1, a pattern seen across diagnoses (Fig. 2C right). Lastly, our sample of 10,188 patients exhibited pronounced overlap in deviation scores between diagnostic categories (84-95%; Fig 2D). These were highest at the (para)limbic pole and slightly decreased towards the heteromodal pole of the first cortical axis of CT deviation. Findings were not driven by single disorder groups as tested by leave-one-disorder-out sensitivity analyses.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that CT deviations across six neuropsychiatric conditions are structured along two cortical axes. While there is pronounced individual heterogeneity, the axes provide a common framework organizing variability within disorders and overlap across them. Heteromodal regions act as hubs that constrain co-occurring extreme deviations in their network. Overall, our findings support the idea that psychiatric disorders are not defined by discrete, disorder-specific brain alterations; rather, they follow norm-extended patterns of cortical variation that are largely shared across diagnoses. By integrating large-scale data with a transdiagnostic, normative modeling approach, our results provide a principled framework for understanding the organization of structural brain variation in mental illness. Taken together, our findings offer insight into common neurobiological substrates, guiding future efforts to identify shared and distinct mechanisms across psychiatric conditions.
