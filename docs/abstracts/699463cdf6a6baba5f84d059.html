Introduction

The locus coeruleus (LC), known as the “blue spot” for its high neuromelanin content, is a small brainstem nucleus.^1 Its involvement in neurodegenerative and neuropsychiatric disorders motivates the development of LC-focused imaging biomarkers. ^2 Since the LC’s MRI contrast is largely neuromelanin-driven, iron-sensitive MRI imaging offers limited signal intensity-based delineation. Neuromelanin-sensitive MRI (NM-MRI) improves LC visualization building on neuromelanin’s paramagnetic properties and aligns well with post-mortem localization. ^3 Manual LC labelling from NM-MRI is feasible for small samples but impractical for large cohorts, therefore motivating scalable atlas-based or machine learning approaches, although their accuracy depends on representativeness of the used atlas and cohort. The LC’s elongated fusiform shape complicates delineation, and in the absence of non-invasive ground truth, validation strategies remain heterogeneous. These anatomical and methodological challenges necessitate systematic evaluation of different LC delineation strategies.

To address this challenge, we provide empirical evidence guiding to informed decision about the choice of a particular LC atlas by quantifying spatial consistency and alignment accuracy. We systematically evaluated atlas-based and LC-enhanced spatial prior delineation. Using NM-MRI acquisitions with repeated annotations from nine expert raters and comparisons across seven existing LC atlases, we assessed spatial variability and reproducibility. This approach differentiates methodological from anatomical sources of variation. We propose a complementary approach to build a consensus LC atlas within established frameworks that capture inter-individual variability, suitable for future studies including deep-learning segmentation and clinical applications.

Methods

Twenty-four healthy participants (10 females, 14 males; mean age 40.0 ± 16.8 years) were included after removing two volunteers due to inconsistent NM-MRI protocol parameters. The study was approved by the University of Lausanne Ethics Committee and all participants provided written informed consent. MRI data were collected on a 3T Siemens Prisma machine using a 32-channel head coil. The imaging protocol included T1-weighted MPRAGE (TR = 2000 ms, TE = 2.39 ms, TI = 920 ms, flip angle = 9°, 1 mm isotropic) and neuromelanin-sensitive 2D gradient-echo sequences with magnetization transfer contrast (TR = 274 ms, TE = 2.28 ms, flip angle = 40°, voxel size = 0.51 × 0.51 × 3.24 mm; 12 interleaved slices; 5 averages). NM-MRI scans were linearly registered to T1-weighted images with a six-parameter rigid-body transformation and resampled to 1mm isotropic resolution to ensure spatial correspondance.

Manual labelling was performed with two repetitions for each subject in all three planes using the MINC Toolkit. Intra-rater reliability was quantified with the Dice-Sørensen coefficient and inter-rater reliability with Fleiss’ Kappa within a restricted LC region of interest to limit contributions from non-LC voxels. To construct a group-level atlas for method comparison, voxel-wise detection frequency maps were generated for each participant and warped to MNI space using previously estimated diffeomorphic registration fields. These maps were then averaged across subjects, thresholded with Otsu’s method, and scaled or binarized for use as probabilistic or binary atlases. To reduce variability in manual labelling and leverage existing resources, we created a consensus LC reference by integrating existing open-access LC atlases with the group-level LC atlas. We computed overlap across atlases for each voxel and retained voxels appearing in two or more sources to define the consensus LC representation.

We modified and applied two delineation strategies. For the atlas-based approach, we prepared probabilistic label maps which were integrated into the MICCAI 2012 multi-atlas framework. The spatial-prior method used SPM12 unified segmentation with a LC-enhanced prior, modeling NM-MRI intensity distributions with two Gaussians. Both methods were repeated for each open-access, group-level and consensus reference atlas as a seperate prior to allow between-atlas comparison.

Statistical analyses were performed in native and MNI152 space. Validation of atlas-based and spatial-prior approaches included evaluating spatial overlap, anatomical alignment, intensity distributions and boundary distances relative to manual labels and the consensus reference. Metrics for the LC-enhanced spatial method included soft Dice, volume intersection, voxel-wise Pearson correlation, Euclidean distance between centres of mass, Kullback–Leibler divergence and Earth Mover’s Distance. Atlas-based performance was evaluated using Dice–Sørensen coefficient (DSC), volume similarity, Cohen’s κ and Hausdorff Distance. For spatial characterization, we assessed LC extent along rostro-caudal, left–right and anterior–posterior directions using principal component analysis of voxel coordinates, and compared spatial dispersion across methods. All data processing used NumPy and pandas, with visualizations generated using matplotlib, and statistical testing performed with SciPy and statsmodels. 

Results

Manual labelling of the LC achieved an average DSC of 0.69 ± 0.18 (95% CI: 0.67–0.72), reflecting moderate intra-rater agreement, while inter-rater reliability measured with Fleiss’ Kappa also indicated moderate agreement (maximum 0.5). The group-level LC atlas extended rostro-caudally to 12 mm in MNI space. Random sub-sampling validation by generating atlases from subsets of 21 participants showed near-perfect overlap (>99%), indicating robust atlas creation. Cumulative inter-rater agreement and Fleiss’ Kappa confirmed moderate spatial consistency along rostro-caudal, anterior–posterior, and medio-lateral axes.

Atlas-based labelling was assessed by comparing individual atlases to manual labels using DSC. Accuracy varied across atlases, from low DSC for the Lee atlas (0.22 ± 0.07, 95% CI 0.03) to higher DSC for the Ye atlas (0.38 ± 0.11, 95% CI 0.04), with some outliers likely reflecting registration errors or anatomical variability. The Tona atlas showed moderate overlap (0.25 ± 0.09), and the group-level atlas had 0.28 ± 0.09. Cohen’s Kappa values mirrored these patterns. Volume intersection was highest for the Tona and Keren atlases (0.83 ± 0.10) and lowest for the Lee atlas (0.41 ± 0.09) and group-level (0.51 ± 0.11). Boundary distances, including centres of mass and Hausdorff metrics, suggested Keren atlas was most consistent. Compared to the consensus reference, the averaged manual atlas had the highest spatial concordance (mean Dice = 0.63 ± 0.02), the Ye and Keren atlases showed the closest boundaries, while the atlas by Tona and Liu had the largest deviations.

LC-enhanced spatial prior-based delineation using SPM12 tissue classification showed the group-level and Dahl atlases were most accurate and spatially consistent relative to manual labels in native space. Using the atlas by Dahl as prior achieved the highest Soft Dice (0.46 ± 0.14) and volume intersection (90.57 ± 31.31), while the group-level prior showed the highest voxel-wise correlation (0.33 ± 0.21) and lowest KL divergence (3.91 ± 2.47). Analyses of centres of mass revealed outliers for several priors, with Betts atlas showing the greatest variability (6.47 ± 8.58). Earth Mover’s Distance was lowest for Dahl and Ye atlases, though Ye showed higher inter-subject variability. Comparisons to the consensus reference confirmed consistent trends, with voxel-wise correlation decreasing except for the group-level prior, which maintained higher correlations. 

Both methods localized the LC along a comparable rostro–caudal extent, although atlas-based labelling generally produced larger cross-sectional areas. Cross-sectional comparisons across eight atlases revealed substantial heterogeneity in mean area and ±1 SD. In standard space, atlas-based labelling achieved higher agreement, particularly for Lee, Tona, and Ye atlases (>95% overlap across three rostro-caudal tiers), whereas overlap in native space was lower, especially in the rostral tier. Collapsing tiers from three to two provided only minor improvements in overlap and did not resolve inconsistencies.

PCA of voxel coordinates identified three principal axes corresponding approximately to left–right (PC1), rostro-caudal (PC2), and anterior–posterior (PC3), with angular deviations of 23.99°, 64.45°, and 64.36° from AC–PC axes. Density and scatter plots showed that atlas-based voxels were tightly clustered, while spatial prior-based voxels were more widely dispersed along PC1 and PC2. Statistical testing confirmed significant differences in dispersion along PC1 (p = 2.00 × 10⁻³) and PC2 (p = 4.00 × 10⁻²), but not PC3 (p = 0.15). 

Conclusion

We compared atlas-based and spatial prior–guided LC delineation, evaluating each method against both manual labels and a consensus reference atlas to understand how methodological choices influence spatial accuracy in native and standard space. Atlas-based labelling using the manually derived group-level atlas provided the most spatially coherent LC representations and highest concordance with both references, and reduced variability compared with LC-enhanced spatial priors. The Ye and Keren atlases performed well on specific metrics, such as boundary distance, but no single atlas excelled across all measures. These findings emphasise the value of consensus-based and group-level atlases for robust LC segmentation.

The method-dependent differences we observed align with prior work showing that LC localisation approaches identify overlapping but non-identical regions. ^4 Atlas-based labelling showed in the PCA analyses tighter clustering and clearer anatomical separation than the more diffuse spatial prior–based method, particularly along the left–right and rostro–caudal axes. The larger extent of the spatial prior method likely reflects thresholding uncertainty inherent to probabilistic maps. The absence of significant differences along the anterior–posterior axis suggests partial anatomical convergence. Thus, atlas-based labelling may be preferable when spatial precision is essential, whereas tissue classification may benefit applications involving uncertainty modelling.

Despite generally high overlap metrics, boundary variability was common, likely due to differences in atlas construction and demographic composition. Age effects may also contribute, as LC neuromelanin contrast increases with age, atlases including younger individuals may underestimate LC boundaries. Down-sampling all atlases ensured comparability but reduced anatomical detail. These factors should be considered when interpreting atlas differences.

Given the small LC volume, minor boundary mismatches substantially reduce Dice scores. Consensus references, akin to majority-vote schemes, can reduce individual rater bias and enhance robustness by emphasising consistently labelled voxels. This achieves a more generalisable LC representation.

After registration to standard MNI space, spatial correspondence improved most in the central LC, consistent with the region’s stronger signal and lower inter-individual variability. Prior studies show that age- and disease-related LC alterations often localise to central or rostral–middle segments, helping explain atlas discrepancies. ^5 These variations highlight the importance of considering intra-LC spatial heterogeneity.

Native-space variability underscores the need for optimised registration for small nuclei. Outliers in rostral and caudal LC further illustrate the limits of manual labelling and support the use of harmonised group-level atlases tailored to the study cohort.

Several limitations should be noted. Manual labelling, often treated as ground truth, showed inherent variability and limited spatial consistency, especially at LC extremities. Inter-operator variability and low NM-MRI contrast may have introduced noise into the manual reference. The small sample size may limit generalisability, and scanner or demographic effects were not assessed. Down-sampling may have reduced atlas granularity, and the consensus reference assumed equal atlas quality. Deep-learning methods were not evaluated but represent a promising direction.

In conclusion, while manual labelling is common in small cohorts, its reproducibility is limited. Atlas-based approaches offer greater scalability and spatial consistency, particularly when paired with consensus references. Among the included atlases, the group-level manual atlas showed the most robust performance across metrics. These findings highlight the importance of method-appropriate validation and continued standardisation in neuromelanin-sensitive imaging.
