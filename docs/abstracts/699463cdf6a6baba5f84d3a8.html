Introduction

The brain’s responsiveness to the hormone insulin modulates neural circuits that regulate energy balance, reward, and cognition1. Insulin resistance in the brain is associated with metabolic diseases such as obesity and type 2 diabetes, as well as cognitive and psychiatric disorders2,3. Eight weeks of aerobic exercise has been shown to restore insulin action in the putamen and hippocampus of adults with overweight or obesity, correlating with improvements in metabolic, behavioral, and cognitive outcomes4. However, the acute effects of aerobic exercise remain unclear. In this study, we aim to investigate how a single exercise session, performed at different intensities, influences brain insulin action.

Methods

Healthy participants with normal weight were enrolled in a randomized, placebo-controlled crossover study. Twenty-seven participants (13 females; mean BMI 22.01 kg/m² [range: 19.48-24.46 kg/m²]; mean age 29 years [range: 20-52 years]) completed three sessions on separate visits: 43 minutes of high-intensity interval training (HIIT), 60 minutes of moderate-intensity continuous training (MICT) on a cycle ergometer, and a 60-minute passive control condition. Blood samples were collected at multiple time points (see Figure 1). All participants were scanned on a 3.0 T Siemens Magnetom Prisma using a 64-channel head coil. Resting-state cerebral blood flow (CBF) was assessed using pulsed arterial spin labeling (PASL) with a PICORE-Q2TIPS sequence (repetition time (TR) = 3000 ms; echo time (TE) = 13 ms; inversion time TI1 = 800 ms, TI2 = 1800 ms; in-plane resolution = 3 × 3 mm²). Acquisition included 50 label/control pairs, with the first image of the series (M0) used for voxel-wise calibration of CBF maps. A T1-weighted anatomical reference image was obtained for registration and normalization. PASL images were acquired 100 minutes after the exercise or control condition, at two time points: before (pre) and 30 minutes after (post) intranasal insulin administration, to assess insulin-induced changes in CBF. Preprocessing of PASL images was conducted in FSL using the Bayesian Inference for Arterial Spin Labeling (BASIL) toolbox. Whole-brain analyses were performed in SPM12 using a flexible factorial model adjusted for global CBF, with within-subject factors of time point (pre vs. post) and exercise condition (HIIT, MICT, vs. control), and a between-subject factor of sex. Insulin-induced changes in CBF were assessed via the interaction between exercise condition and time point. A voxel-wise threshold of p < 0.001 (uncorrected) was applied, followed by cluster-level correction for multiple comparisons at p < 0.05 family-wise error (FWE) at the whole-brain level. Additionally, small volume correction (SVC) was applied to predefined regions of interest4, including the hippocampus, hypothalamus, midbrain (ventral tegmental area and substantia nigra), and putamen. To correct for multiple comparisons for the four bilateral masks, a Bonferroni-adjusted threshold of p < 0.0125 was used.

Results

Blood lactate and free fatty acid levels were significantly higher after both exercise intensities compared with the control condition (pFWE < 0.001). After exercise (pre) no changes in global CBF were observed (p > 0.05). Regional CBF in the right hypothalamus was significantly lower after MICT compared to the control condition (t = 3.89, pFWE = 0.003, SVC). Exercise did not induce significant effects in any other predefined region of interest or regional CBF. Exercise-induced effects on brain insulin responsiveness were found by a significant interaction between exercise condition and time point in the right putamen (see Figure 2), showing that both exercise intensities decreased insulin-induced CBF in this region compared to control (HIIT vs. control: t = 4.71, pFWE = 0.001, SVC; MICT vs. control: t = 4.55, pFWE = 0.002, SVC). Only HIIT decreased insulin-induced CBF in the left hippocampus (t = 4.31, pFWE = 0.007, SVC) and the left inferior frontal gyrus (IFG)/insula (t = 4.66, pFWE = 0.005, whole-brain corrected) compared to control. Significant sex-specific effects were observed following HIIT, with females showing greater insulin-induced CBF than males in the right substantia nigra compared to control (t = 4.16, pFWE = 0.004, SVC), and in the left medial superior frontal gyrus compared to MICT (t = 4.83, pFWE = 0.004, whole-brain corrected).

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that even a single session of aerobic exercise modulates insulin action in key reward-related and cognitive regions in adults with normal weight. This effect is influenced by exercise intensity and sex. Females showed greater insulin-induced CBF responses to HIIT than males, highlighting the need to investigate the role of sex hormones. This underscores the potential of personalized exercise interventions to modulate brain insulin action. We will next determine whether these neural responses relate to exercise-induced metabolic changes, providing insights into body-brain interactions. Future studies should determine whether these acute neural changes predict long-term effects of exercise on cognition and metabolic health.
