Introduction

Normative modeling quantifies individual brain deviations from healthy population distributions, enabling assessments across neurodevelopmental and psychiatric conditions without requiring large matched control groups.^1 Generalized Additive Models for Location, Scale, and Shape (GAMLSS) offer particular advantages for neuroimaging data, flexibly accommodating heteroskedasticity, non-linear age effects, and non-Gaussian distributions through transformations like Sinh-Arcsinh (SHASH) with manageable compute-times to be deployed at scale.^2 However, robust extreme percentile estimation requires substantially large samples (N≫1000).^3

Practical GAMLSS application in neuroimaging faces two key challenges. First, while natively implemented in R, existing Python implementations provide only limited wrappers without neuroimaging-specific functionality. Second, multi-site studies, essential for achieving necessary sample sizes, introduce scanner-related variability that requires adaptation methods. Beyond these technical barriers, a fundamental question remains unexplored. Subcortical structures show disorder-specific volumetric alterations across neurodevelopmental disorders, with cortical and subcortical abnormalities co-occurring in these conditions.^4 Whether incorporating subcortical volumes (controlling for intracranial volume) improves cortical thickness normative models across multiple disorders has not been systematically investigated.

We introduce pyGAMLSS, a comprehensive Python package addressing these gaps. Our implementation includes transfer learning capabilities for model adaptation to new sites with minimal data. We trained models on N=21,351 healthy subjects across 99 scanners and systematically evaluated whether subcortical features enhance cortical thickness deviation detection across seven neuropsychiatric disorders (N=5,860) and external validation datasets from an entirely unseen distribution of scanners, making our tool deployable on unseen scanners.

Methods

Participants: Training data comprised N=21,351 healthy subjects (ages 5-90 years) from 99 scanners. Test data included patients with ADHD (n=1,568), Alzheimer's disease (n=205), autism spectrum disorder (n=1,074), generalized anxiety disorder (n=190), learning disorders (n=197), major depressive disorder (n=251), and schizophrenia (n=199), along with healthy controls (n=2,176) from 45 scanners (healthy) and 38 scanners (patients). External validation employed 3707 healthy individuals from 19 independent sites, with each site comprising more than 50 subjects.

MRI Processing: T1-weighted structural MRI scans were processed using FreeSurfer 7.1 to extract 68 regional cortical thickness measures (Desikan-Killiany atlas, 34 regions/hemisphere) and 10 bilateral subcortical volumes (thalamus, caudate, putamen, pallidum, hippocampus, amygdala, nucleus accumbens, ventral diencephalon, cerebellar white matter, cerebellar cortex), plus total intracranial volume (TCV). Quality control metrics (Euler number) were computed for each scan.

pyGAMLSS Implementation: We developed a Python package wrapping R's GAMLSS library via rpy2, with enhanced neuroimaging-specific functionality. The package implements SHASH distributions with penalized B-spline smoothers for age effects, sex-specific trajectories, and random intercepts for scanner sites. Features include parallel processing, model persistence, comprehensive diagnostics, centile computation, z-score calculation, brain surface visualization via nilearn, and, importantly, transfer learning. Note: Package and documentation will be published at https://github.com/MHM-lab

Model Comparison: For each cortical region, we compared: (1) Base models with age, sex, site, and Euler number covariates, with TCV adjustment (base_tcv); versus (2) Ten target models each adding one TCV-controlled subcortical volume (target_tcv). Model performance was evaluated using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) in the training set, with lower values indicating a better fit.

Novel Transfer Learning Method for GAMLSS: Our transfer approach adapts pre-trained models to new sites using minimal healthy subjects from target sites. The method: (1) obtains predictions from source model; (2) extracts site-specific random effects from trained model using a proxy reference site; (3) fits adjustment GAMLSS models using a fraction (default 20%) of healthy subjects from new sites; (4) applies site-specific corrections to all distribution parameters (μ, σ, ν, τ); (5) generates corrected z-scores. This enables the deployment of models trained on large reference cohorts to clinical sites with limited samples without complete retraining.

Statistical Analysis: Z-scores were computed for each cortical region. We employed two approaches: (1) Region-wise analysis comparing z-scores between healthy controls and patient groups using independent-samples t-tests; and (2) Proportion-based analysis where we calculated the proportion of subjects with extreme deviations (positive: z>1.96; negative: z<-1.96; extreme: |z|>1.96) across the 68 cortical regions, yielding proportion vectors that were compared between groups using one-tailed t-tests (disorder > healthy). Significance: p<0.05 after Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (33 tests per disorder: 11 models × 3 deviation types). Model improvements were assessed by comparing base_tcv versus target_tcv models on: (1) the number of regions (or deviation types) showing significant disorder-healthy differences; and (2) the magnitude of proportion differences.

Transfer Validation: External validation split each of 19 independent sites (n=3,707 healthy subjects) into evaluation (80%) and transfer (20%) portions. Model performance before and after site-specific transfer adaptation was evaluated using prediction accuracy (R², RMSE, MAE), z-score calibration (mean≈0, SD≈1 for healthy subjects), and distributional fit (logarithmic score).

Results

Model Validation and Subcortical Enhancement

Training data (N = 21,351 healthy subjects, aged 5-90, from 99 scanners) demonstrated comprehensive lifespan coverage (Figure 1a). GAMLSS models demonstrated excellent fit with smooth age trajectories and minimal sex differences in cortical thickness distributions (Figures 1b-c).

A systematic comparison of base models (age, sex, site, Euler number, TCV) versus target models incorporating TCV-controlled subcortical volumes revealed significant improvements, despite BIC penalties for the additional parameters. Notably, each subcortical structure showed distinct patterns of cortical improvement. The nucleus accumbens model (Figure 1d) showed the most significant improvement in bilateral lingual cortex (left ΔBIC=283.6, right ΔBIC=249.7), followed by superior temporal cortex and precuneus. The caudate model (Figure 1e) showed the greatest improvement in bilateral precuneus (left ΔBIC=240.6, right ΔBIC=221.1), followed by bilateral lingual cortex and superior parietal cortex. The amygdala model (Figure 1f) showed the largest improvements overall, concentrated in bilateral superior temporal cortex (left ΔBIC=690.1, right ΔBIC=790.2), followed by inferior temporal and fusiform cortex. Across all models, higher-order association cortices showed substantially greater improvement than primary sensory cortices.

Disorder-Specific Patterns

Region-wise z-score analysis revealed distinct patterns across disorders (Figures 2a and 2 b). In autism, base models identified 4 significantly deviating regions (p < 0.05, Bonferroni-corrected): the right lingual cortex, left superior temporal cortex, left transverse temporal cortex, and left medial orbitofrontal cortex. When caudate volumes were incorporated (Figure 2b), target models identified 5 significant regions, gaining detection in the right paracentral cortex while maintaining sensitivity in all previously identified areas.

The effect of incorporating subcortical features varied meaningfully across disorders (Figure 2c). In Alzheimer's disease, target models consistently detected fewer significant cortical regions than base models, suggesting that subcortical volumes captured variance previously attributed to independent cortical abnormalities. Autism showed the opposite pattern, with selective gains in detected regions when incorporating caudate, cerebellum, and hippocampus volumes—indicating that cortical deviations in autism may be more independent of subcortical morphology. Schizophrenia exhibited a structure-dependent pattern: striatal models (caudate, putamen) increased detection sensitivity, whereas limbic models (amygdala, hippocampus) substantially reduced detected regions, reflecting the differential involvement of these circuits in schizophrenia pathophysiology.

Extreme Deviations and Transfer Learning

Proportion-based analysis across 68 cortical regions (Figure 2d) revealed that extreme deviations (|z|>1.96) were most sensitive to disorder detection, with substantially more significant tests than negative or positive deviations alone (Bonferroni-corrected p<0.05). This pattern was consistent across all disorders and subcortical models.

External validation on OASIS3 (772 evaluation, 193 transfer subjects) demonstrated successful model adaptation (Figure 2e). Transfer learning with 20% local data improved R² substantially across all regions, with association cortices showing the largest gains. Similar improvements were observed across all 19 independent validation sites.

Conclusion

Cortico-Subcortical Integration in Normative Models

Our findings demonstrate that incorporating subcortical volumetric information significantly improves the fit of cortical norms, with each subcortical structure showing distinct patterns of cortical improvement, possibly reflecting specific connectivity profiles. The caudate model showed the most significant improvement in the precuneus, consistent with established corticostriate projections and functional connectivity patterns between these regions. The precuneus connects with posterior thalamic nuclei, including the pulvinar, and shows positive functional connectivity with the ventral caudate as part of cognition-related cortico-basal ganglia loops, while exhibiting distinct connectivity patterns with dorsal caudate subdivisions. The nucleus accumbens model showed the greatest improvement in the lingual cortex, consistent with evidence that NAcc core subdivisions show functional connectivity with the lingual gyrus and visual association cortex, reflecting the role of the ventral striatum in integrating reward-related signals with visual processing. The amygdala model showed the largest overall improvements, concentrated in the superior temporal cortex, a region with dense bidirectional connections to the amygdala that processes auditory, social, and emotional information. The superior temporal gyrus receives projections from and sends inputs to the amygdala, forming a circuit critical for processing socially and emotionally relevant auditory stimuli.

Disorder-SpecificInsights

The divergent effects across disorders provide insights into cortico-subcortical relationships in psychiatric conditions. In Alzheimer's disease and schizophrenia, the reduction in detected cortical regions when subcortical features are incorporated suggests that cortical and subcortical abnormalities share common variance, consistent with coordinated neurodegeneration affecting interconnected structures in Alzheimer's disease and corticostriatothalamic circuit dysfunction in schizophrenia. The contrasting pattern in autism, where the incorporation of specific subcortical volumes reveals additional cortical abnormalities, suggests that cortical and subcortical deviations may arise through more independent mechanisms in this condition. This interpretation aligns with evidence that autism is characterized by altered thalamocortical connectivity patterns, including hyperconnectivity between the thalamus and cortical regions, as well as weaker connectivity between the nucleus accumbens and the precuneus. The structure-specific effects in schizophrenia, where striatal but not limbic models enhanced detection, further underscore the importance of considering circuit-specific pathophysiology in normative modeling approaches.

Extreme Deviations as Sensitive Markers

The superior sensitivity to extreme deviations aligns with theoretical frameworks that emphasize psychiatric disorders as tail phenomena. Extreme deviations captured at the distributional extremes provide greater sensitivity for risk assessment and anomaly identification in psychiatric conditions compared to central tendency measures.^5 Extreme deviations capture bidirectional pathology; both cortical thinning and thickening can reflect disease processes, providing greater sensitivity than analyses focused on a single direction of effect.

Clinical Translation via Transfer Learning

Transfer learning addresses a critical barrier to clinical implementation: most clinical sites cannot collect thousands of subjects for robust normative model estimation. Our approach enables deployment of GAMLSS-based models to new unseen scanners using minimal local data (20%), with consistent improvements across 19 validation sites.
