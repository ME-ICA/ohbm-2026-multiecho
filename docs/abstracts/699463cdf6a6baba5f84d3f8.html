Introduction

Our visual environment is hierarchically organized, composed of global objects built from smaller, local elements. Directing attention at both levels is crucial for accurate perception of the environment. The hierarchical structure of scene organization is commonly examined using Navon figures.1 These stimuli consist of a large letter formed from smaller letters and illustrate the global precedence effect, whereby the overall shape is processed more rapidly than the local elements.1 This global perception is largely mediated by low spatial frequencies (LSF), such that filtering LSF information leads to slower reaction times.2

Visual information is transmitted through two primary pathways. The magnocellular (M) pathway originates in the lateral geniculate nucleus  and is preferentially sensitive to LSF, achromatic, and low-contrast input. It is critical for global perception and projects predominantly along the dorsal visual stream. Disruptions to M processing and global integration have been implicated in reading difficulties.3 Conversely, the parvocellular (P) pathway preferentially processes high-spatial frequency (HSF) information, is sensitive to chromatic contrast in opponent color channels and is largely associated with local processing via the ventral visual stream.4

While previous neuroimaging studies of global/local processing have predominantly focused on the cognitive control mechanisms within the frontoparietal network, the specific role of the M and P visual pathways remains unclear. In the present fMRI study, we explored M and P pathways contributions to global/local processing using biased stimuli. We examined the involvement of visual areas and assessed interactions with the language network. We expected distinct visual cortical contributions for M and P pathway-biased stimuli and predicted significant functional interactions between visual cortex and language regions.

Methods

Forty right-handed participants (M = 26.7 years, SD = 5.2; 26 females) completed an in-scanner event-related fMRI Navon task in which they were instructed to attend to either the global or the local letter while searching for a target letter. The stimuli consisted of magnocellular-biased, parvocellular-biased, and neutral (non-biased) Navon figures (see Fig. 1). The M and P bias was introduced by applying psychophysical thresholds determined uniquely for each participant, converting neutral stimuli into either achromatic magnocellular-biased stimuli or red-green isoluminant parvocellular-biased stimuli. We exclusively used incongruent Navon figures (e.g., a large 'H' made of small 'S's) to ensure participants focused on the specified level, confirming that local and global features were functionally distinct across all conditions.

Whole-brain MRI data was acquired with a 3-T Siemens TRIO whole-body MRI scanner (Siemens Medical Solutions) using a 64-channel whole-head coil. High-resolution MPRAGE T1-weighted structural images were collected with the following parameters: time to repetition (TR) = 2530 ms; time to echo (TE)  = 2.36 ms; flip angle (FA)  = 7°; field of view  (FoV) = 256 mm; voxel size  = 1 mm isotropic; 176 slices. Functional MRI data were acquired in twelve runs using a gradient-echo EPI sequence (TR = 1300 ms; TE = 35 ms; 68 axial slices; 2.4-mm isotropic voxels; 0% gap; FA = 56°; FOV = 210 mm). Each run included 456 volumes; four initial volumes were discarded for T1 equilibration. During each run, 90 trials were presented in random order. Intertrial intervals ranged between 4.5 s and 6.5 s to maximize BOLD signal recovery. 

Preprocessing and analyses were conducted in SPM12. Thermal noise was reduced using the NORDIC (NOise Reduction with DIstribution Corrected) PCA denoising algorithm prior to further preprocessing.5 IImages were slice-timing corrected and realigned using rigid-body motion correction, then smoothed with a 4-mm FWHM Gaussian kernel. Motion artifacts were addressed using ArtRepair, which identified and interpolated problematic volumes based on excessive scan-to-scan motion (>0.1 mm) and global intensity fluctuations (>1.3%). Participants whose data required more than 15% of volumes to be repaired were excluded from the analysis. Repaired functional images were coregistered to structural T1 scans and normalized to MNI standard space using nonlinear deformation parameters.

Statistical analyses used the general linear model (GLM). Functional time series were modeled using individual trial onset times convolved with a canonical hemodynamic response function. The full design included 18 experimental conditions, defined by the factorial combination of Pathway (magnocellular, parvocellular, neutral), Stimulus Type (distractor, filler, target), and Processing Level (global, local). Each trial was modeled as a single event with a fixed duration of 1 s. Motion parameters (x, y, z translations; yaw, pitch, roll) were included as covariates of non-interest. The magnitude of BOLD signal change for all 18 conditions was derived from the GLM.

To investigate the involvement of M and P pathways in global/local processing, we conducted ROI analyses. We examined the BOLD activation in anatomically defined ROIs from the human connectome project atlas. Our ROIs encompassed early and higher-order visual areas (e.g., V1-V4, LO, TO, V3A/B, V6A, V7, V8) in both hemispheres, alongside regions in the language network (e.g., Left Inferior Frontal Gyrus, Insula, Anterior/Posterior Temporal Cortex). BOLD parameter estimates (i.e., percent signal change) from each ROI were submitted to a 3 (Pathway) x 2 (Processing Level) x 3 (Stimulus Type) repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA). To investigate task-dependent interactions between visual cortex and regions along the language network, beta-series correlation functional connectivity (FC) analysis were also conducted.

Results

Participants’ overall accuracy was high (M = 95.6% SD = 3.2% across conditions), indicating that they adequately performed the in-scanner task. A repeated-measures ANOVA indicated a small but significant effect of Pathway (F(2, 52) = 4.10, p = .02). Post-hoc analysis confirmed that the neutral condition (M = 96.4%) yielded significantly higher accuracy than the magno condition (M = 94.4%; p = .015), while the parvo condition was intermediate (M = 95.9%) and did not differ significantly from the other conditions (p ≥ .05).

Repeated-measures ANOVAs on ROIs percent signal change revealed significant main effects of Pathway in both left and right V4 and V8: left V8 (F(1.7, 37.3) = 10.80, p = .00039), left V4 (F(1.7, 37.9) = 7.59, p = .003), right V8 (F(1.9, 43.9) = 13.92, p = 2 × 10⁻⁵), and right V4 (F(1.9, 42.2) = 9.56, p = .0004). Post-hoc analysis revealed that parvocellular-biased (P) stimuli elicited stronger responses than neutral (N) stimuli (ps ≤ .03) in   and stronger responses than magnocellular-biased (M) stimuli (ps ≤ .006). This pattern (P > N; P > M) was consistent across Stimulus types (target, distractor, filler) and Processing levels (global, local).

Of interest, statistically significant right-lateralized Pathway by Stimulus Type interactions were found in right V3A (F(3.0, 63.5) = 2.96, p = .038), right V3B (F(3.7, 81.5) = 2.78, p = .036), right V4 (F(3.0, 66.2) = 2.87, p = .043), right V7 (F(3.3, 74.9) = 3.54, p = .016), and right V8 (F(3.5, 81.6) = 2.73, p = .040). Simple-effects post-hoc comparisons revealed that only the Parvocellular-biased (P) condition showed significant differences, with target trials (t) eliciting greater BOLD responses than both distractor (d) and filler (f) trials (ps ≤ .03). No significant effects emerged for Stimulus within the magnocellular (M) or neutral (N) conditions (ps ≥ .05). This pattern indicates that right-hemisphere visual areas, particularly those influenced by the P pathway, are selectively engaged during successful target identification.

Also, statistically significant Processing Level by Stimulus Type interactions emerged across bilateral dorsal and lateral visual regions, including left V6A (F(1.96, 40.87) = 6.75, p = .003), left V7 (F(1.7, 39.0) = 4.19, p = .03), left V6 (F(2.0, 43.7) = 3.49, p = .04), right LO (F(1.8, 41.6) = 3.72, p = .04), right V3B (F(1.9, 41.2) = 4.06, p = .03), right V6A (F(1.9, 40.4) = 5.30, p = .01), right dorsal transitional area (F(1.6, 35.6) = 3.78, p = .04), right V6 (F(1.7, 38.3) = 6.00, p = .007), and right V3 (F(1.5, 32.9) = 3.6, p = .049). Post-hoc analysis revealed that these interactions were driven primarily by the local Processing condition, where target trials (t) elicited stronger activation than distractors (d) and fillers (f) trials (t > d; t > f; ps ≤ .03). This pattern suggests that visuospatial and motion-related regions are selectively engaged during local identification of relevant stimuli.

Finally, FC analysis demonstrated significant task-dependent interactions between higher-order visual and parietal and frontal language ROIs, supporting the hypothesis that global/local processing is functionally coupled with frontoparietal language regions during the Navon task.

Conclusion

The present work examines the neural mechanisms supporting hierarchical visual processing and the contributions of magnocellular and parvocellular pathways to them. Parvocellular-biased stimuli elicited stronger engagement than neutral or magnocellular-biased stimuli in intermediate and higher-order visual areas. These results indicate that parvocellular-biased input was associated with relatively increased bilateral hemisphere activation in these regions during the task. 

A right-lateralized effect emerged in right higher-order visual areas, where parvocellular-biased stimuli showed enhanced responses specifically during identification of relevant stimuli, which suggests lateralized preference and sensitivity to stimulus distinctions under parvocellular-biased conditions.

The interaction between stimulus type and processing level showed that higher-order visual areas responded more strongly to relevant stimuli during more focused local processing. Since attending to local details generally requires more attentional effort and does not follow the usual global-precedence pattern, this increase in activity suggests that these higher other visual areas are critical when local attention is required. This activation pattern is consistent with functional connectivity results showing relevant interactions of these areas with parietal and frontal regions associated with language processing. Overall, our findings highlight pathway-sensitive and lateralized patterns of activation and coactivation within the visual system, providing a more refined understanding of the neural dynamics supporting hierarchical visual processing. These findings inform ongoing neuroimaging studies in our group investigating the involvement of visual pathways on reading processes.
