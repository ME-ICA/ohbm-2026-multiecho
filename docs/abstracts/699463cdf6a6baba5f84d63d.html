Introduction

The last few years have seen the emergence of network-based analysis of human neuroscience data where resting-state activity has been utilized primarily to segregate brain networks, identify network connectivity and estimate network metrics across various disorders as compared with healthy individuals. Recent approaches have utilized an individual centric approach to identify the structure and organization of various brain networks1 instead of utilizing a fixed brain atlas which would vary across individuals and result in a poor estimation of various network parameters.

But using fixation based resting state data is challenging, with respect to duration required for mapping different brain networks would be dependent on multiple factors including motion, artifacts and the regions required to be mapped amongst others2. Given the unstructured aspect of resting-state there has been a rigorous effort to find alternatives. Many patient populations and children find difficult to lie still in the scanner to collect a robust amount, usually 24-30 minutes, to reliably determine network level delineations. One can even use task structure regressed task-fMRI data to create robust brain maps even in hard to determine subcortical regions3. But still some populations might find it hard to perform various cognitive tasks in the MRI scanner.

Naturalistic stimuli like movie-watching can be a potentially useful alternative, it’s easier for individuals to lie still in the scanner during movie-watching and for longer duration, thus allowing more fMRI data to be collected increasing the power and reliability of the network estimation4. In this study, we show the potential of using movie-watching to determine networks at an individual participant level and compare it with networks estimated using resting-state data suggesting that naturalistic movie-watching paradigms could have substantial promise for collecting better and higher quality data for precision network mapping.

Methods

The Human Connectome Project Young Adult (HCP-YA) (www.balsa.wustl.edu) was utilized in the study. Participants were scanned using 3T Siemens Connectom scanner and a subset of individuals (n=129, age (years): range = 18-35, M = 29.37, SD = 3.2; 51 males), utilized in the study had scanning done on a 7T Siemens Magnetom scanner as well. The participants underwent multiple sessions of MRI scanning which included high-resolution T1w and T2w imaging along functional imaging incorporating four runs of fixation-based resting state (TR=1000 ms, TE=22.2 ms, 1.6 mm isotropic, multiband factor 5, 15 mins each) and four runs of movie-watching paradigm with same parameters. The movie-watching runs composed of clips from Hollywood and indie movies. Each run of 15-mins consisted of 3-4 clips of around 1-4 mins in length with a 20 s gap between each clip.

The resting-state and movie-watching data was preprocessed using the minimal preprocessing pipeline5. Briefly, the pipeline consisted of artifact, slice time, motion and fieldmap distortion corrections followed by registration to the standard template followed by the Freesurfer pipeline. To create brain networks, Multi Session Hierarchical Bayesian Modeling (MS-HBM) was utilized with 15-network configuration established in prior studies3. MS-HBM computes variability in the connectivity profile of a seed region across sessions and individuals and clusters them based on a hierarchical Bayesian model including spatial smoothing and averaged data priors. The pipeline resulted in a 15-network configuration for each participant separately for movie-watching and fixation-based resting state runs with the networks: Somatomotor A and B (SMOT-A, SMOT-B), Visual Central (VIS-C), Visual Peripheral (VIS-P), Auditory (AUD), Pre Motor & Posterior Parietal (PM-PPr), Dorsal Attention Network A & B (dATN-A, dATN-B), Salience/Parietal Memory Network (SAL/PMN), Cingular-Operculum (CG-OP), Frontoparietal Network A & B (FPN-A, FPN-B), Default Network A & B (DN-A, DN-B) and Language Network (LANG). The network overlap within an individual was computed using percentage overlap metric. The movie-watching and resting-state networks estimation was compared to split-half reliability from resting-state data (equating the amount of data across the estimations).

Results

We found a high degree of overlap qualitatively (Figure 1A) between networks estimated using both resting-state and movie-watching data (M = 67.48%, SD = 6.46%). The early sensory and motor networks (SMOT, VIS, AUD) showed a high degree of convergence (70-81 %) whereas the most variability was observed in higher order networks like FPN-A (M=58.14%, SD=9.29%) and DN-B (M=55.07%, SD=8.93%) and DN-A (M=61.14%, SD=8.85%) followed by LANG, CG-OP and FPN-B networks. As compared to the split-half reliability overlap (Figure 1B) from resting-state data (M=69.86%, SD=3.23%), the movie-watching data was close to overall network overlap highlighting the use of movie-watching as a potential alternate to estimate within-individual network definitions.

Conclusion

We highlight a potential to utilize movie-watching based naturalistic data to delineate brain networks. Overall, movie-watching is easier to administer for a large population of individuals with lower motion confounds. Existing naturalistic datasets like the Narratives, Child Mind Institute and others where naturalistic stimuli were recorded with high signal-to-noise ratio and low motion artifacts could be analyzed to study subject level differences and similarities in network organization. The optimal stimuli set is still an active area of research with groups showing that clips having more affective stimuli could help delineate higher order networks like the default mode and limbic network. The burgeoning field of naturalistic neuroscience could utilize such approach to minimize the need for an additional paradigm like fixation-based resting state to study network organization.
