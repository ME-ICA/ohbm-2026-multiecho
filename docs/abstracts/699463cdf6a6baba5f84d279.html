Introduction

Temporal Interference Stimulation (TIS) offers a promising avenue for non-invasive deep brain neuromodulation, specifically targeting network-level pathologies without stimulating the overlying cortex.1,2 However, optimizing electrode configurations for distributed brain networks (e.g., bilateral deep nuclei) poses a significant challenge due to the combinatorial explosion of potential solutions. Existing heuristic methods, such as Genetic Algorithms (GA), are severely limited by computational expense (requiring hours) and the need for high-density electrode arrays, hindering clinical feasibility.3 While deep learning approaches (e.g., Unsupervised Neural Networks, USNN) have increased optimization speed, they lack the on-demand flexibility required to steer stimulation intensity across multiple targets.4 To overcome these limitations, we introduce an end-to-end Reinforcement Learning (RL) framework designed for scalable, steerable, and hardware-friendly multi-target TIS optimization.

Methods

Head Model Construction: We utilized six individualized high-resolution head models from the Human Connectome Project (HCP). T1w and DTI data were segmented into ten tissues using SimNIBS 4.5, with anisotropic conductivity assigned to gray/white matter.5 A standard 10-10 EEG montage (74 electrodes) was modeled.

RL Optimization Framework: The electrode optimization was formulated as a Markov Decision Process. We developed an RL agent incorporating a Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) mechanism. This allows the network to condition the generated electric fields on user-defined target weights, thus enabling precise control over relative stimulation intensities (steerability). The agent's output is a sparse electrode montage (e.g., selecting 16 or 32 electrodes) along with the corresponding optimal currents (Figure 1).

Training & Validation: The agent was trained using the REINFORCE algorithm to maximize a Weighted Multi-target Focality Index (WMFI) while adhering to safety constraints (<2 mA per electrode). We validated the framework on three increasingly complex scenarios: (1) Bilateral Globus Pallidus internus (GPi) co-stimulation; (2) Ipsilateral GPi and Hippocampus (HC) co-stimulation; and (3) A four-target network (bilateral GPi and HC). We benchmarked our RL approach against state-of-the-art GA and USNN in terms of focality, speed, and hardware efficiency.

Results

Optimization Performance: The RL framework generated subject-specific protocols in approximately 2 minutes, significantly faster than GA (~2 hours) and comparable to USNN. In the bilateral GPi task, our method achieved a mean WMFI of 0.890, statistically outperforming both USNN (0.859, p=0.024) and GA (0.795, p<0.001) across all subjects.

Hardware Efficiency: Our framework achieved superior focality using a sparse configuration of only 16 active electrodes. This contrasts sharply with previous methods that require full high-density arrays. For two deep targets, increasing the number of electrodes beyond 16 yielded diminishing returns, validating the RL method's ability to efficiently identify optimal sparse montages for clinical application.

Scalability & Steerability: The RL framework successfully scaled to a complex four-target network (bilateral GPi and HC), achieving distinct focal hotspots with a mean WMFI of 0.783. Crucially, the FiLM module enabled precise “steering” of the electric field. When tested with unbalanced target weights (e.g., 90% left GPi, 10% right GPi), the algorithm accurately shifted the stimulation focus, achieving a modulation selectivity score >1.5, whereas baseline models without FiLM failed to adhere to user constraints (Figure 2).

Conclusion

We propose a novel RL framework that solves the complex inverse problem of multi-target TIS. By integrating FiLM, our method provides the first steerable optimization tool capable of shaping interference fields dynamically based on clinician inputs. The approach is highly scalable (handling up to 4 deep targets), rapid (~2 min), and hardware-friendly (requiring fewer electrodes). This work bridges the gap between theoretical TIS physics and practical, personalized network neuromodulation.
