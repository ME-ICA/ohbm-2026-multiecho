Introduction

We have previously found that resting-state ‘static’ network connectivity is differently altered by low dose psilocybin in non-autistic and autistic people. In general, psilocybin reduced within-network connectivity in non-autistic people and increased between-network connectivity in autistic people. However, this approach does not capture the temporal fluctuations in neural activity that support the reconfiguration of connectivity within and between brain networks in response to pharmacological challenge. Differences in the dynamic landscape of brain network connectivity at baseline have already been reported in autism, including fewer state transitions and atypical fractional occupancy and dwell time metrics1, but whether the dynamic response to psilocybin is also different in the autistic brain is unknown.

Methods

In our ‘PSILAUT’ study2, resting-state fMRI data were acquired from non-autistic (n = 30) and autistic adults (n = 37) following a single low dose of 5 mg psilocybin (COMP360 psilocybin, a proprietary synthetic formulation of crystalline psilocybin owned by Compass Pathways plc, London, United Kingdom) or placebo, administered in a pseudo-randomised order across two separate visits. We identified 13 intrinsic connectivity networks (ICNs) with data-driven group-level concatenated independent component analysis3 and performed dual regression4 to obtain subject-specific ICN spatial maps and ICN-specific timeseries (Figure 1A). Core network strength was calculated as mean contribution of within-network voxels to each ICN (defined as z > 3). We clustered ICN dynamic activity to 7 recurrent brain states (Figure 1B) with K-medoid and characterised fractional occupancy (FO), dwell time (DT), mean L2 norm energy (L2 norm of all ICN activity at each timepoint) and metastability (standard deviation of L2 norm) of each state. We used linear mixed models to analyse group, drug, and their interaction effects on FO and DT. Between-network connectivity was calculated as Pearson’s correlation between ICN timeseries in each state. We extracted the first two principal components (PC) of the between-network connectivity matrix with PCA (Figure 1C) and used linear mixed models to analyse group, drug, and their interaction effects on PCA loadings of the first two PCs.

Results

The core network strength of several higher- and lower-order ICNs was reduced by psilocybin including the default mode (DMN, p = 0.001, pFDR = 0.004), frontoparietal (FPN, left: p = 0.009, pFDR = 0.015; right: p = 0.008, pFDR = 0.016) and visual (p = 0.004, pFDR = 0.008) networks across both groups. Across both groups, the DT (pFDR = 0.015) and FO (p = 0.016, pFDR = 0.079) of a salience-somatomotor state was also reduced by psilocybin (Figure 2A). In contrast, the DT of a dorsal-attention-frontoparietal state was differentially altered by psilocybin (dose x group interaction, p = 0.029, pFDR = 0.203), this was driven by a reduction in DT of this state in autistic (p = 0.042, pFDR = 0.084) but not in non-autistic participants (p = 0.267). There was also a distinct response in the metastability of a whole-brain maximally active state after psilocybin (dose x group interaction, p = 0.028, pFDR = 0.196), which was reduced after psilocybin in non-autistic (p = 0.047, pFDR = 0.094) participants, but not autistic participants (p = 0.284).

Between-ICN connectivity was differentially modulated by psilocybin during the salience-somatomotor (dose x group interaction, p = 0.008, pFDR = 0.028) and whole-brain maximally active state (dose x group interaction, p < 0.001, pFDR < 0.001) (Figure 2B). Between-ICN connectivity (e.g. DMN-FPN, unimodal-transmodal) was shifted in opposite directions in each group during the maximally active state, generally, connectivity was decreased in non-autistic (pFDR < 0.001) and increased in autistic participants (pFDR = 0.021). Despite this distinct connectivity pattern, the L2 norm energy of all states was reduced by psilocybin across both groups.

Conclusion

Psilocybin facilitates more dynamic brain states in autism. Despite a common reduction in core network strength and overall state energy after psilocybin, the autistic brain occupies and transitions between intrinsic connectivity states differently after psilocybin, including reduced occupancy of networks supporting visual attention and cognitive control and increased between-ICN connectivity in autistic participants compared to their non-autistic peers. Elevated between-ICN connectivity in autistic participants after low dose psilocybin aligns with our prior static functional connectivity findings; this pattern has previously been elicited by higher doses of psilocybin (e.g. 25 mg) and is associated with treatment response in depression for example5. Thus, next steps are to explore the potential use of lower doses of psilocybin and evaluate whether facilitating more dynamic brain states with psilocybin has clinical utility in autism.
