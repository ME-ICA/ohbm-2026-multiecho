Introduction

AD, PD, FTD together account for approximately 90% cases of dementia. These diseases show sex-specific patterns of atrophy, associated with cognitive impairment progression. While sex differences have been studied in both AD and PD, there are not many studies focused on sex differences in FTD, probably due to the limited available data. In this study we propose a novel Bayesian model, with strong priors, that allows us to study neuroanatomical sex differences associated with cognitive impairment while accounting for normal ageing , and identify regions significantly associated with cognitive impairment more robustly.

Methods

We modeled the normal trajectories of age- and sex-expected volumes of 76 anatomical regions and then used these trajectories to estimate deviations for diseased subjects. All available T1w MRI scans  were   processed using an automatic segmentation method Assembly Net [1]. This method estimates volumes of 76 anatomical regions, each as a fraction of the total brain volume. 

First, we build trajectories of the normative regional volume change of 76 ROIs using MRI scans of 5691 healthy cognitively normal subjects without neurological abnormalities from the following datasets (N subjects, N scans, age range) :  ADNI (1581, 6308,50-96y), HCP (1113, 1113 28-36y), ICBM (340, 340, 18-80y), NIHPD (439, 1061, 4-22y),  NKI (1218, 2157, 6-85y), UK Biobank (1000, 2000, 46-82y), on the scale of z-scoring against the mean and standard deviation of all scans of control subjects. 

Second, we analyzed MRI scans of subjects from ADNI, PPMI and NIFD, including subjects with disease together with a small number of healthy controls. These include:  CN a𝜷- from ADNI (408, 1445 50-95y), all a𝜷+ subjects from ADNI ( 1017, 3602, 54-96y), HC subjects from PPMI (208, 328 , 40-83y), PD subjects from PPMI (708, 2164 , 40-86y), HC subjects from NIFD (126, 393, 47-85y) , bvFTD subjects from NIFD (59, 160, 46-76) , svFTD subjects from NIFD (38, 127 , 50-75y) and PNFA subjects from NIFD (37, 115, 54-83). 
We calculated deviation from the expected relative volume trajectories of each anatomical ROI, in the form of an age- and sex-controled z-scored difference. 

To analyze patterns of atrophy associated with cognitive impairment progression we developed a multi-variate hierarchical Bayesian regression model.  The dependent variable in this model is the individual z-scored deviation of age- and sex-expected relative volume, and the independent values are the inverted MoCA score (i.e., 30 - MoCA, so the larger values indicate greater impairment), chronological age, sex, and diagnostic group (Healthy Controls from all studies, amyloid positive subjects from ADNI, PD subjects from PPMI and three forms of FTD from NIFD study. Subject-specific and acquisition protocol differences are treated as random effects.  We used regularized horseshoe priors [2] to model the effect of dementia progression as measured by MoCA, and factorized low-rank dense priors to model subject and acquisition protocol differences - level effects and factor-analytic residual covariances for residuals. Results

We identified the following anatomical regions with significant sex effects, shown on Figure 2, in particular,

AD:  faster volume loss in women: Occipital pole, Posterior insula, Sup. frontal gyrus, Middle occipital gyrus, Supplementary motor cortex, Occipital fusiform gyrus ; faster volume loss in men: Precentral gyrus medial segment, Middle cingulate gyrus, Frontal operculum, Lobules VIII-X, Cerebellum GM, Thalamus, Transverse temporal gyrus, Parietal operculum; faster volume increase in men: Lateral ventricle,  Inf. lateral ventricle 

PD: faster volume loss in women: Anterior cingulate gyrus, Precentral gyrus, Medial frontal cortex, Transverse temporal gyrus, Planum polare, Inf. occipital gyrus, Gyrus rectus, Accumbens, Lateral orbital gyrus, Sup. frontal gyrus, Anterior orbital gyrus, Medial orbital gyrus, Orbital inf. frontal gyrus, Thalamus,Frontal pole, Subcallosal area, Posterior insula ; faster volume loss in men: Middle occipital gyrus, Temporal pole, Cerebellum WM, Opercular inf. frontal gyrus, Putamen, Fusiform gyrus, Pallidum, Sup. temporal gyrus, Precuneus, Hippocampus, Entorhinal area, Frontal operculum, Anterior insula, Cerebellum GM ; faster volume increase in  men:   Inf. lateral ventricle, 3rd ventricle, 

bvFTD: faster volume loss in women: Triangular inf. frontal gyrus, Parahippocampal gyrus, Lateral orbital gyrus, Medial frontal cortex ; faster in men: Supplementary motor cortex, Middle cingulate gyrus, Inf. temporal gyrus

svFTD:  faster volume loss in women: Thalamus, Ventral DC ; faster in men: Brainstem, Anterior orbital gyrus 

PNFA: faster volume loss in women: Planum temporale; faster in men: Brainstem, Calcarine cortex, Gyrus rectus

Conclusion

The effect of sex as a biological variable in age-related neurodegenerative conditions impose disease-specific regional vulnerability to neurodegeneration. Our results for sex differences for AD and PD are roughly consistent with previously published results, however our choice of using volumes relative to the ICV, instead of using residuals methods, makes our results slightly more difficult to interpret, in case when the atrophy rate of a specific structure is slower than the overall brain atrophy rate, leading to counter-intuitive relative increase of volume with progression of the disease. 
Previously published sex differences: 

AD:  women show greater atrophy in hippocampus, parietal lobe, and medial temporal structures, while men exhibit more pronounced frontal, temporal, and anterior cingulate atrophy [3]

PD: Males demonstrate significantly greater cortical thinning in the precentral/postcentral gyrus, precuneus, superior frontal gyrus, and multiple temporal and parietal regions as well as atrophy in the cerebellum compared to females  [4]

bvFTD: Women exhibit greater cortical thinning in frontotemporal regions at diagnosis compared to men, particularly in prefrontal and anterior temporal cortex  [5]

svFTD and PNFA: to our knowledge there were no previous systematic studies of the sex differences
