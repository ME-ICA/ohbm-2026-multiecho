Introduction

Empathy is a core competency in the socio-emotional domain and allows us to understand and feel affective states experienced by others. It comprises two sub-components: Cognitive (e.g., emotion recognition, perspective-taking) and affective empathy (e.g., feeling others’ emotional states).1 Previous studies have investigated the neural mechanisms behind empathy in adults as well as in children. For example, using the Inter-Subject Correlation approach, previous research documented that self-reported empathy was positively correlated with similarity in time series of brain activity while watching several emotional movies in adults.2 A study with typically developed children showed that brain regions responsible for understanding others’ thoughts (Theory of Mind; ToM) and their bodily reflexes (often pain) already constitute distinct networks by the age of three.3

While previous research focused on the investigation of empathy in adults and typically developed children, it remains unclear what neural mechanisms underlie empathy in gifted children. This is important because gifted children often face societal stereotypes, particularly the assumption that giftedness is associated with socio-emotional deficits.4 Research on empathy in gifted children has mostly relied on self-reports or behavioral measures.5 Thus, it is worthwhile to examine empathy in gifted children from a neurocognitive perspective. Such approaches may offer more mechanistic insights into empathy in gifted children. However, neurocognitive studies on gifted children are sparse. To address this issue, this study investigated empathy abilities in gifted children using fMRI.

Methods

For participant recruitment, we collaborated with the Hector Children’s Academy (HCAP), which offers enrichment classes to the top 10 % of primary school students in Baden-Württemberg, Germany. The children can voluntarily participate in the HCAP after being nominated by one of their teachers, who is responsible for the nomination procedure. The nomination is made in a non-standardized manner, which means that the teachers were not provided with standardized tests for completing the nomination. The sample in our study consists of 97 healthy children (58 Males) who were nominated by one of their teachers. The gifted children were 8 to 9 years old (M = 107 months; SD = 5.82) and attended the second and third grades of primary school. Children completed questionnaires, behavioral measurements, and the MRI measurement. 

Data collection spanned three sessions, and the data used in this study were collected during the third session. Data included the MRI measurement, a parental report on children’s empathy, and an affective rating task. The MRI measurement took a maximum of 45 minutes and was performed using a 3T Siemens Scanner (Siemens Medical Solutions, Erlangen, Germany). During scanning, the children watched a total of five videos. Among these, they viewed an animated video in which characters experienced several affective states ranging from suffering to happiness (Partly Cloudy, see https://www.pixar.com/partly-cloudy#partly-cloudy-13). The functional scan parameters (EPI) were based on a previous large-scale study with children of the same age (ABCD study). For the anatomical scan (T1-weighted), an MPRAGE sequence was used. After the scanning session, the children re-watched the animated video and rated their affective states on 14 time points during the video using a continuous scale between -0.5 = “not good” and 0.5 = “good”. Four participants were excluded due to missing data in this task (N = 93). Additionally, the parents reported on their child’s empathetic competencies on a nine-point scale (from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”) by using the Griffith Empathy Battery (M = 6.62; SD = 0.89; N = 97). 

We analyzed behavioral data by using standard toolboxes in Python 3.11.12 (e.g., pandas). We examined the similarity in affective dynamics across children. For this, we computed a behavioral version of the Inter-Subject Correlation (ISC; leave one out approach) of children’s affective states during video watching. As an inference test, we used a one-sample t-test for each correlation between the child’s affective time course and the average affective time course. 

We analyzed the fMRI data by using the ISC approach from the BrainIAK toolbox in Python 3.11.12. The goal was to quantify the degree of similarity in the response time course across children. In a first step, we used a grey matter mask from nilearn (icbm152_brain_gm_mask), and resampled it to fit our whole brain mask (MNI152NLin6Asym). After masking the voxels, we conducted a leave-one-out approach, in which we correlated the time course (395 TRs) of a specific voxel with the average time course of the same voxel across all other subjects. This analysis was repeated for every voxel (N = 181,638 voxels) and yielded subject-wise ISC maps. We computed voxel-wise z-scores to perform a one-sample test against zero and corrected using Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR). In a second-level analysis, we estimated a General Linear Model (GLM) containing empathy values, the ISC values, and confound regressors, such as gender, age, and handedness when writing, to test whether the voxel-wise ISC is predicted by empathy assessed via parental report. As a non-parametric inference test, we implemented a threshold-free cluster enhancement (TFCE) procedure with 10,000 permutations.

Results

Generally, children’s affective ratings spanned the entire scale, from the minimum of -0.5 (“not good”) to the maximum of 0.5 (“good”) (see Fig. 2). The average time course showed that children experienced both positive and negative affective states (see Fig. 1). Our findings revealed a significant positive correlation between each child’s affective time course and the average time course across all children. On average, the children showed an ISC of M = 0.81 (SD = 0.15; Min = -0.05; Max = 0.96). A one-sample t-test confirmed that this effect was significantly different from zero (t(92) = 51.50; p < 0.001; d = 5.34). These results suggest that children experienced shared affective dynamics while watching the animated video.

The ISC analysis of fMRI data aimed to quantify the similarity in brain activation across subjects during video watching. We observed significant bilateral synchronization across children (see Fig. 3), with the largest ISC values (r > 0.2) in the occipital visual cortex, lateral temporal cortex, posterior parietal cortex, and medial cortical areas during video watching. These regions are primarily involved in audio-visual processing, consistent with the naturalistic stimuli presented. Additionally, we performed a voxel-wise GLM using subject-wise ISC maps and parental reports of empathy as a predictor, while controlling for children’s age, gender, and handedness during writing. The results did not show a significant effect of children’s empathy abilities on ISC after TFCE correction (p = 0.05; -log10).

Overall, we found significant Inter-Subject Correlations (leave-one-out approach) in both affective ratings and the time course of brain activity during video watching. In contrast, we could not find significant voxel clusters showing activation patterns predicted by children’s empathy assessed through parental reports. 

Conclusion

Human empathy has been studied from a neurocognitive perspective in adults and children over the last decades.2,3 However, little is known about the mechanisms behind empathy for the subgroup of gifted children, even though it is highly relevant for the research field. Gifted children often face stereotypes suggesting that giftedness is associated with socio-emotional deficits.4 Because empathy in gifted children has primarily been investigated by using self-reports5, the current study focused on examining empathy in gifted children from a neurocognitive perspective by using an fMRI approach. 

In general, our behavioral analysis showed that the animated video “Partly Cloudy” led children to experience both positive and negative affective states. Thus, this type of naturalistic stimulus seems suitable for further investigating affective responses in 8- to 9-year-old children. In line with previous findings, our results revealed strong synchronization while watching this video in brain areas responsible for audio-visual processing.2 Consistent with a previous study with children, we observed ISC in empathy networks such as the bilateral temporal parietal junction and medial prefrontal cortex.3 Regarding the behavioral analysis, we found statistically significant ISC in the affective ratings over time across children. Taken together, these results suggest that gifted children are not only similar in their subjective emotional experiences but also in their brain activation patterns when they watch others who emotionally suffer. In contrast, we did not find clusters of voxels whose ISC was significantly predicted by children’s empathy as reported by their parents. This indicates an inconsistency between children’s perspective, via the affective rating task, and parents’ perspective, via the Griffith Empathy Battery. 

From a methodological view, this inconsistency can be explained by the different types of assessments. The Griffith Empathy Battery consists of both affective and cognitive components of empathy, whereas the affective rating task solely measures the affective component. Therefore, it is important to examine whether the pattern is similar when we focus on affective empathy.

Following up on this first attempt to investigate neural mechanisms underlying empathetic competencies in gifted children, we plan several additional analyses. First, we will perform a more robust inference testing of the ISC using the time-shift permutation test from the BrainIAK package, in which the time course of the left-out child is circularly shifted by random intervals before computing the correlation with the average time course of all other children. Second, we will repeat the second-level analyses with other predictors, such as the sub-components of the Griffith Empathy Battery (e.g., cognitive and affective empathy), to examine whether the degree of similarity of brain activity is predicted by the sub-components. As confound regressors, we will include children’s age, gender, and handedness, and the remaining sub-component of empathy. In a third step, we will use children’s performance on the affective rating task and on a ToM task as predictors in the GLM. The goal is to further investigate whether the inconsistency between the parental perspective via the Griffith Empathy Battery and the children’s perspective via behavioral tasks persists.
