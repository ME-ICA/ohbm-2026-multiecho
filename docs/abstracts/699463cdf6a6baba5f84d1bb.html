Introduction

A longstanding hypothesis in neuroscience posits that a cortical region’s functional specialization is determined by its unique pattern of inter-regional connections—its "connectivity fingerprint" (Passingham et al., 2002). However, testing this hypothesis mechanistically in the human brain has proven elusive. Structural connectivity (SC) provides a physical scaffold but lacks the directionality and functional weights required to shape signal flow. Moreover, SC is dominated by local exponential distance rules, often failing to capture the long-range, directed dependencies essential for complex integration. Consequently, the SC is fundamentally limited in its ability to predict the flexible, dynamic functional states observed during cognition. We propose that Integrated Effective Connectivity (iEC; Oh et al., 2024), which estimates directed functional interactions from resting-state fMRI, serves as the generative and intrinsic backbone of cortical functional specialization. While our previous work has shown that iEC fingerprints correlate with functional similarity, we here test a stronger hypothesis: that this intrinsic backbone is causally sufficient to generate the cortex’s diverse functional repertoire. To test this, we embedded the iEC matrix into a whole-brain Hopf model. We found that the iEC backbone successfully reconstructs the task activation maps of diverse cognitive states, significantly outperforming a model based on SC—particularly for spatially distributed, integrative tasks, requiring long-range functional dependencies. Leveraging this generative capacity, we further mapped the cortex's "control topography," revealing a fundamental principle: while simple sensorimotor tasks are driven by local inputs, complex cognition requires synergistic driving from heteromodal areas.

Methods

We utilized resting-state fMRI data from the Human Connectome Project (HCP S1200) to derive the iEC matrix, which is served as the directed intrinsic backbone of the cortex (Fig. 1a). To define the functional targets for generative modeling, we used group-averaged task activation maps from the Multi-Domain Task Battery (MDTB; King et al., 2019), which characterizes functional specialization across 26 distinct cognitive states (Fig. 1b). To test the generative capacity of the iEC backbone, we constructed a whole-brain generative model based on the Hopf model (Fig. 1c). The model couples local regional dynamics through the long-range backbone (either iEC or SC). The critical variable governing system behavior is the local bifurcation parameter (a), which determines the dynamical regime of each region. All regions were initialized in a stable, subcritical state (a=-0.01) representing baseline resting dynamics. We modeled cognitive states as emergent phenomena driven by specific subsets of driver regions shifting into an oscillatory supercritical regime (a>0), propagating activity through the backbone (see Fig. 1c for full model equation). To bridge the gap between simulated time series and empirical maps, we quantified the simulated activation of each region as the mean amplitude of its analytic signal, extracted via the Hilbert transform. This metric captures the magnitude of local oscillations driven by the bifurcation parameter, providing a direct analogue to the intensity of BOLD activation observed in empirical GLM beta maps. We employed a two-stage optimization pipeline to identify the minimal set of driver regions required to reconstruct each empirical task activation map (Fig. 1d). For computational efficiency, the search space was restricted to the left hemisphere: 1) Single-driver mapping (grid search): First, we performed a brute-force grid search across the brain regions to identify the optimal single driver for each task. For every region, we varied the bifurcation parameter a (0.1 to 2.0) while keeping all other nodes at baseline. We selected the region and parameter value that maximized the spatial correlation between the simulated Hilbert amplitude and the empirical task map, 2) Multi-driver synergy (nested optimization): To capture complex, spatially distributed task activation patterns, we implemented a nested bayesian optimization framework. The outer loop used a gaussian process with upper confidence bound acquisition to iteratively select optimal candidate driver sets (up to n=7). This step efficiently explores the combinatorial space of potential driver coalitions. The inner loop employed CMA-ES algorithm to optimize the specific stimulation intensities (a) for the selected drivers. To prevent overfitting, the optimal driver set for each task was determined using the Bayesian Information Criterion. This criterion enforces a parsimonious trade-off between reconstruction accuracy and driver set size, allowing the complexity of the control model to vary naturally across different cognitive conditions in a purely data-driven manner.

Results

The iEC-informed Hopf model successfully reconstructed empirical task activation maps with high fidelity across the 26-task battery (mean r=0.52). Crucially, the iEC backbone significantly outperformed models based on SC (mean r=0.44; Wilcoxon singed-rank, p=0.027) and null iEC (mean r=0.24) (Fig. 2a). Qualitative inspection of representative tasks confirmed that the iEC model accurately reproduces fine-grained topographical details—specifically the recruitment of the temporoparietal junction and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex—that undirected SC models failed to capture (Fig. 2b). We found that the generative superiority of iEC was not uniform but showed unique pattern (Fig. 2c). While iEC outperformed SC in the majority of conditions (18/26), SC achieved comparable or marginally better performance in specific tasks. To explain this heterogeneity, we quantified the "generative advantage" (a difference in the r values) as a function of task focality, measured by the spatial kurtosis of the empirical beta map (Fig. 2d left). We observed a strong negative correlation (r = -0.47, p=0.016; Fig. 2d right): SC suffices for generating simple, spatially clustered activations (high kurtosis), whereas iEC is indispensable for reconstructing spatially distributed functional maps (low kurtosis). This confirms that the iEC backbone is critically required to coordinate the divergent, long-range information flow characteristic of complex cognition. Transitioning from single-region drivers (mean r=0.52) to multi-region (mean r=0.65) coalitions yielded a significant boost in generative accuracy (Wilcoxon singed-rank, p<0.001). However, this gain was subject to diminishing returns; the marginal improvement in reconstruction fidelity saturated between driver set sizes of n=5 to 7 (Fig. 2e). This parsimony horizon suggests that the brain does not require widespread inputs to generate high-dimensional states; rather, it utilizes small, synergistic coalitions of drivers to efficiently steer the global backbone into diverse functional configurations. Finally, we mapped the control topography of the human cortex by analyzing the network membership of the optimal driver sets (Fig. 2f). Sorting tasks solely by the size of their optimal driver set naturally organized them along a gradient of cognitive complexity. This data-driven ordering revealed a striking diagonal shift in the recruitment heatmap. Tasks requiring few drivers were invariably simple (e.g., action observation) and driven primarily by attention (e.g., VAN, DAN) and sensory networks, with virtually no recruitment of the limbic network. Conversely, tasks requiring larger coalitions were cognitively complex (e.g., Theory of Mind) and heavily recruited the hierarchical apex (limbic, FPN). This pattern directly links computational cost (set size) to the biological necessity for heteromodal integration.

Conclusion

We provide causal evidence that the brain’s intrinsic signaling architecture—captured by iEC—serves as the generative backbone of cortical function. Our findings reveal a profound principle of brain organization: despite the vast flexibility of human cognition, the brain's functional repertoire is governed by a stable, intrinsic latent backbone. We demonstrate that diverse cognitive states emerge not from dynamic rewiring, but by systematically shifting the locus of control input atop this fixed scaffold. While simple sensorimotor functions are driven by local sensory inputs, complex integrative cognition requires driving the backbone from its heteromodal apex to orchestrate long-range information flow. This framework bridges the gap between network and cognitive neuroscience, offering a mechanistic account of how a stable intrinsic backbone supports the dynamic flexibility essential for human intelligence.
