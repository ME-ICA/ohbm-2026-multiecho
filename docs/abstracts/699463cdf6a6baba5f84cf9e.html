Introduction

    The human brain is massively expanded relative to our primate ancestors, selectively in associative cortical regions1. The high-order cognitive functions of these regions are non-localized and distributed across the cortical sheet, as reflected by the long-distance functional connectivity profiles characteristic of associative networks1. Since associative regions lack canonical feedforward/feedback circuit properties1 and show functional connectivity patterns which are relatively “uncoupled” from the white matter connectome2, it is thought that their flexible, spatially distributed nature is enabled by reduced structural constraints on their function3. However, an alternative hypothesis suggests that it is not a lack of structure per se, but instead a more intricate structural architecture (efficient parallel circuitry, long-distance white matter tracts1) which favours spatially distributed associative cortex function. Since high-order regions continue developing into adulthood2, adolescence is a critical window for studying the properties facilitating long-distance associative function as it emerges, and psychiatric conditions with developmental origins like psychosis are key examples of how such a property may be disrupted during this critical period. Together, we sought to comprehensively explore the long-distance functional connectivity profiles of associative cortical regions and their underlying associations with brain structure in healthy adults, evaluating how these crucial structure-function relationships are formed in development and broken in psychosis.

Methods

    We leveraged neuroimaging and phenotypic data from the Human Connectome Project Young Adult (HCP-YA, N = 1094, aged 22-35), Development (HCP-D, N = 652, aged 5-21), and Early Psychosis (HCP-EP, N = 119 psychosis patients and N = 57 controls, aged 16-35) datasets. Images were preprocessed with the HCP pipelines for structural, functional (including ICA-FIX), and diffusion data. For each participant, we evaluated the distance dependence of each region’s functional connectivity profile (Figure 1A). Distance was defined as the minimum geodesic distance between the edges of each region of the Schaefer 400 atlas (resampled onto the native surface) calculated with the surfdist toolbox, while functional connectivity was defined as the Pearson correlation between the average time series of a given native Schaefer 400 region pair. We then performed region-wise Spearman correlations to predict functional connectivity to all other within-hemisphere regions from the distance between them. In HCP-YA, we averaged regional Spearman coefficients across participants, where we expected to see less steep distance decays in associative regions relative to sensory ones (i.e., elevated long-distance connectivity). To quantify this relationship, we spatially correlated group-averaged statistics expressed on the fsLR 32k surface with the Sydnor sensory-associative axis (S-A axis)2 using spin tests from the neuromaps toolbox, while also comparing participant averages within the sensory, unimodal, associative, and limbic regions defined by Mesulam’s cortical hierarchy (Figure 1B)3. We then implemented region-wise linear models predicting the distance-connectivity relationship from age in HCP-D (covarying for sex and average framewise displacement) and patient status in HCP-EP (covarying for age and sex), assessing how long-distance function may emerge in development and be disrupted in psychosis.

    Next, we aimed to determine how structural connectivity could explain the long-distance functional profiles of associative regions. Diffusion-weighted images were subjected to anatomically constrained probabilistic tractography, with structural connectivity defined as the sum of streamline weights connecting each pair of Schaefer 400 regions, inversely scaled by node volume. In HCP-YA, we evaluated the distance dependence of structural connectivity using Spearman correlations as above, then implemented rank-transformed linear models predicting functional connectivity from both structural connectivity and geodesic distance, allowing us to infer their roles in predicting function independent of each other. Then, we predicted the distance-structure relationship from age and psychosis status as above. Lastly, we correlated distance dependencies with FreeSurfer’s local gyrification index (both averaged across regions) across HCP-YA, allowing us to infer whether long-distance structural connectivity may play an adaptive role in more folded/expanded cortices4.

    Given that associative functional connections are often far apart when considering the distance between them, we also tested whether these functional connections were actually relatively “close” when considering effective distance through the structural connectome. We implemented the model of Brockmann and Helbing5, previously used to predict disease spread based on the effective distance between locations inferred from air travel. Effective distance was defined by row-normalizing each structural connectome, converting each relative connectivity measure to a distance metric (subtracting each log-transformed measure from 1), and finding the additive shortest path between each pair of regions with Dijkstra’s algorithm. The resulting distance metrics could be used in place of geodesic distance as above.

Results

    In HCP-YA, group-average spatial dependencies in brain function followed a sensory-associative hierarchy (Figure 2A, S-A axis correlation: r = 0.694, pspin < 0.001). Sensory cortical regions were characterized by steep distance decays in functional connectivity (i.e., strong connections to nearby regions), while associative regions were far less distance dependent (i.e., greater connectivity to distal regions). Importantly, once distance was accounted for, greater structural connectivity consistently predicted greater functional connectivity, particularly in associative regions which were not initially well explained by distance (Figure 2B, S-A axis correlation: r = 0.302, pspin = 0.016), suggesting a key role for the connectome in enabling long-distance function.

    In this regard, while associative regions tended to be distally functionally connected, these connections were actually relatively “close” together when considering connectome architecture: when predicting functional connectivity from effective distance through the structural connectome, we saw that sensory and associative regions were far more equivalently distance dependent when compared with our initial analysis (Figure 2C, S-A axis correlation: r = 0.398 for connectome distance dependencies, r = 0.694 for cortical distance dependencies). We also found that more folded brains, as measured by local gyrification index, consistently showed steeper distance decays in structural connectivity, implying that long-distance connectivity may come at an efficiency cost averted in more expanded brains (Figure 2D, r = -0.340, p < 0.001).

    Developmentally, in HCP-D, we saw the emergence of long-distance functional connectivity in regions like the prefrontal cortex (Figure 2E), a highly replicable pattern across two held-out developmental datasets: the Philadelphia Neurodevelopmental Cohort (r = 0.411, pspin = 0.010) and the Healthy Brain Network (r = 0.483, pspin = 0.003). Critically, the emergence of long-distance functional connectivity throughout adolescence was paralleled by the emergence of long-distance structural connectivity, which preferentially occurred in associative regions (Figure 2F, S-A axis correlation: r = 0.388, pspin = 0.027). Lastly, in HCP-EP, we observed trends towards greater long-distance functional (Figure 2G) and structural (Figure 2H) connectivity in psychosis patients relative to controls, suggesting differences in structural and functional network segregation related to the disorder. 

Conclusion

    Distance dependencies in brain function followed a robust sensory-associative hierarchy, where the connectivity patterns of associative regions were far more spatially distributed than their sensory counterparts. Given that long-distance functional connectivity preferentially emerged in regions like the prefrontal cortex during adolescence, it is plausible that spatial dependencies could mark the expanding non-localized cognitive capacities of these regions during the same age window.

    Critically, our results suggest that associative functional connections were not explicitly decoupled from the structural connectome, and instead provide evidence for the proposal of Buckner and Kreinen1 that long-distance structural connectivity enables the spatially distributed function of high-order regions. The structural connectivity between two regions consistently predicted their functional connectivity above and beyond what could be explained by the distance between them, a relationship which was most evident in associative regions. Tellingly, while associative functional connections were distant when considering cortical geometry, our analysis suggested that these connections were actually relatively “close” when considering effective distance through the structural connectome. Further still, long-distance structural connectivity selectively emerged in associative regions during adolescence, perhaps enabling the distributed functional architecture of the high-order cognitive networks developing simultaneously.

    While long-distance structural connectivity may favour the elevated cognitive capacity of associative networks, our results imply that excessive integration across space may have key functional consequences. Psychosis patients showed greater long-distance structural and functional connectivity relative to controls, suggesting a lack of proper network segregation related to the disorder. More heavily folded brains appeared to avoid such a consequence and were instead characterized by preferentially local structural connectivity, perhaps a product of the cortex favouring efficient small-world properties as it expanded4 and averting the adverse effects of excessive integration as a result.

    Together, the spatially distributed nature of associative cortical networks is thought to underlie their elevated cognitive capacity. We propose that long-distance function is driven by an efficient structural connectome architecture which emerges during adolescence. Further, we suggest that excessive long-distance integration may relate to dysfunction in disorders like psychosis, a consequence which may have been avoided by the brain’s preference for local connectivity with greater folding and expansion.
