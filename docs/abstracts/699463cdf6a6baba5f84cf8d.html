Introduction

Gliomas are not randomly distributed across the brain, but preferentially locate to highly connected network hubs,1 and through specialized neuron-glioma synapses, maintain functional connectivity with both adjacent and remote healthy brain regions.2 These clinical behaviors reflect the intrinsic connectivity patterns of the brain’s glioma-susceptible zones and their distinctive microenvironment. Conventional imaging studies often treat tumors as relatively homogeneous masses, overlooking their internal spatial heterogeneity and thus limiting insight into how the microenvironment at the initial site of gliomas shapes progression, therapeutic response, and prognosis. By tracking continuous variations in connectivity strength throughout the entire tumor mass, gradient analysis may identify functional subregions that correspond to different stages of tumor growth.3 Here we aim to use functional connectivity gradients to pinpoint the putative early sites of gliomas and to determine whether these areas exhibit connectivity patterns and gene expression signatures specifically linked to tumor behavior.

Methods

We first applied gradient analysis to resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) data from 192 glioma patients to identify zones of Interest (ZOIs) that may harbor early tumor infiltration, and subsequently validated these zones. These ZOIs represent brain areas intrinsically vulnerable to initial glioma invasion. Next, leveraging normative rs-fMRI data from the UK Biobank and the Schaefer atlas, we calculated functional connectivity between each ZOI and the cerebral cortex. Across a range of correlation thresholds, we counted the number of cortical regions that remained connected to the ZOIs, and further quantified the region number within each of the Yeo 7 networks that maintained connections with the ZOIs, thereby characterizing the large-scale functional connectivity patterns of these zones. We then characterized the microenvironmental signature of the ZOIs by intersecting them with whole-brain transcriptomic data from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA), assessing spatial overlap between ZOIs and cortical regions exhibiting high expression of specific genes or enrichment of particular cell types. We placed particular emphasis on synaptic genes, immune-active markers, and glioma-related oncogenes. Finally, we examined how these molecular and connectomic features relate to patient-level tumor metrics—namely tumor volume, grade, and molecular subtype.

Results

The connectivity signature of the ZOIs was significantly associated with tumor volume, grade, and molecular subtype. Within the visual network (VN), ZOI connectivity was markedly higher in IDH wild-type (IDH-wt) subgroup than in IDH mutant (IDH-mut) subgroup (Fig. 2A). Similarly, high-grade gliomas (HGG) exhibited stronger ZOI–VN connectivity than low-grade gliomas (LGG) (Fig. 2B). In the HGG cohort, the number of regions within the limbic (LIM) and default mode networks (DMN) that connected to the ZOIs increased in proportion to tumor volume (Fig. 2C-D), corroborating the influence of these network connections on glioma progression. The microenvironmental properties of ZOIs likewise shaped tumor initiation and evolution. For instance, spatial overlap between ZOIs and cortical regions with high expression of the synaptic gene BDNF was significantly greater in IDH-mut subgroup than in IDH-wt subgroup (Fig. 2E), and similarly, it was greater in LGG than in HGG (Fig. 2F). In both LGG and IDH-mut subgroup, tumor volume correlated positively with the degree of spatial overlap between ZOIs and regions of high BDNF/NLGN3 expression (Fig. 2G-J). Immune-cell abundance and glioma-related proto-oncogene expression levels also related to tumor volume (Fig. 2K-R), underscoring the pivotal role of microenvironmental factors in the pathobiology of LGG and IDH-mut gliomas. Mediation analyses further indicated that the neural microenvironment of susceptible zones likely modulates glioma progression through specific functional-connectivity pathways (Fig. 2S-V).

Conclusion

This study delineates the functional-connectivity patterns and microenvironmental profile of brain regions intrinsically susceptible to glioma, offering a fresh perspective on the mechanisms that govern tumor development. The observed associations among ZOI connectivity, microenvironmental factors, and clinical tumor metrics indicate that specific network properties may—through intricate interactions—create a permissive milieu for glioma initiation and progression. These findings advance the emerging conceptual framework that bridges connectomics and glioma biology, and may ultimately inspire the development of network-based biomarkers for risk stratification and early detection.
