Introduction

Perception is not merely passive processing of sensory information, but rather the integration of sensory signals with prior expectations1. Growing evidence shows that this process is supported by a hierarchical layer-specific predictive model constructed using unexplained sensory information (prediction errors)2. In a previous (7T) fMRI study, we manipulated predictability using an audio-visual associative paradigm and asked where auditory predictions and errors are processed. Before scanning, participants learned (deterministic) face - syllable associations. During scanning, invalid and omission trials served to investigate error processing (75% valid, 25% invalid/omission). Corroborating hypotheses which involve cortical layers in predictive processing, our results showed stronger superficial layer activity for errors in IFG and contextual effects in superficial layers of temporal areas. The hippocampal sub-field CA2/3, revealed decodable representations for expected syllables. We previously focused on the average (across runs) effects, but when inspecting the evolution of prediction errors (BOLD percent signal of invalid vs. valid trials), this signal decreased across runs, an effect possibly related to model updating (see figure 1A). To test the hypothesis that internal models undergo dynamic updating, we designed a new audio-visual associative paradigm to evaluate the reweighting or the reversing of previously learned associations. Measuring reaction times, we hypothesized that reweighting of previously learned contingencies would be reflected in dynamic changes in reaction time differences between valid and invalid trials with learning. By measuring the same responses before and after sleep, we also aimed at investigating whether memory consolidation would affect these processes at a behavioral level.

Methods

Eighteen participants (12 female, 8 male) were recruited for this pilot study, consented prior to the experiment and received compensation for participating. None of the participants had a history of neurological or hearing disorders. All participants had normal (or corrected to normal) hearing and vision. Data was collected at the audio labs at Maastricht University. The study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee for Psychology and Neuroscience (ERCPN) at Maastricht University. The experiment consists of two sessions (see figure 1B). Each session included a first part (four runs) to train participants on specific audio-visual contingencies (with 100% validity) and a follow up part (composed of eight runs) in which the learned contingencies were reweighted/reversed. The difference between the two sessions was the moment in which previously learned associations would be reweighted/reversed. In one session, participants would reweigh/reverse the contingencies in the same day in which they were learned (i.e. part 2 followed part 1 in the same day), in a second session a different set of contingencies would be reweighted/reversed after sleep (part 2 happened the day following part 1). The two sessions were counterbalanced across participants. The audio-visual contingency consisted of the association of a face (1 second, one of four in each session) and a syllable (150 ms; sa/da or syllables 1/2). During part 1, four face-syllable associations were presented with 100% contingency (e.g. faces A and B predict syllable sa and faces C and D predict syllable da). For reweighting, two of the learned associations (e.g. face A and D) were presented at 75% contingency (i.e. we introduced 25% of invalid trials). For reversal, the other two face-syllable pairs (e.g. face B and C) were reversed (e.g. B predicting da and C predicting sa) and presented at 75% contingency as well. Throughout the experiment, subjects had to respond as fast as possible which of the two syllables they heard. Note that we used a different set of four faces across the two sessions (same day [A,B,C,D] vs. sleep [E,F,G,H]). Within each session and after each part we used a two alternative forced-choice task (80 trials, 20 per face) to measure the accuracy of the learned contingencies by asking the participants to guess which syllable they expected to hear based on the presented face and according to the latest model. We analyzed the responses in the two alternative forced choice by estimating the learned transition matrix X (linking each face to the two syllables) for each subject. We used the Jensen-Shannon divergence (JSD) to quantify the discrepancy between the estimated transitions and the ground truth transitions G. The JSD is a special case of mutual information: JSD(X, G) ≡ I(M ; Z), where M is the mixture distribution of X and G, and Z is a binary random variable that indicates which source distribution generated the transitions. A JSD of 0 means the two distributions are identical and 1 means they are completely distinct. We analyzed the reaction times using ordinary least squares regression with condition, run, and their interaction as predictors.

Results

Behavioral results indicate that participants could learn the correct face-to-syllable associations and later successfully updated their model to match the reweighted/reversed transition probabilities. Figure 2A summarizes the group average of the estimated subject-specific transitions from face [A,B,C,D] or [E,F,G,H] to syllable [1,2]. The white circles next to each matrix represent the subject-wise divergences between subjects’ transition estimates and the theoretical ground truth transition probabilities (learning 1: 0.020 ± 0.057, update 1: 0.065 ± 0.039, learning 2: 0.026 ± 0.071, update 2: 0.096 ± 0.084). Reaction times for precision reweighted pairs (shown in figure 2B) resulted in significant differences between valid and invalid syllables in both sessions of the experiment (session 1: t = 2.229, p = 0.027, session 2: t = 2.018, p = 0.045, no interaction). For face-syllable pairs that underwent probabilistic reversal, there was no significant response time advantage for the new valid syllables compared to the new invalid syllables (session 1: t = 1.779, p = 0.076, session 2: t = 0.484, p = 0.629, no interaction). Despite not being significant, reaction times in the second session (after sleep) were lower on average than in the first session (mean session 1: 516.48 ± 50.74, mean session 2: 501.64 ± 48.11).

Conclusion

The Bayesian brain hypothesis proposes that perception in the brain operates via a probabilistic inference mechanism which integrates sensory information with an internal predictive model. Under this hypothesis models of the external world undergo constant updating driven by error signals formed when presenting unexpected information. In a previous fMRI study we identified signatures of model updating in the magnitude of error responses measured across runs in which a reweighted contingency was presented. Our behavioral results indicate that a similar model update signature can be extracted from behavioral reports. The results of the two-alternative forced choice task indicate that at every phase of the experimental manipulation (after training or after reweighting/reversal) participants held an accurate representation of the expected contingencies. In both learning and update sessions, the empirical estimate of the subject-wise transition probabilities seems to converge to the theoretical transition probabilities. This indicates the participants’ ability to learn and update their predictions in our face-syllable associative paradigm. The significant reaction time difference between valid and invalid syllables is evidence for facilitation of responses due to the predictable nature of the syllable. Despite not being significant, reaction time differences to invalid and valid trials after reweighting showed a decreasing trend over the runs especially in the first session (in line with our preliminary fMRI results). Reversals did not show the a significant increase in reaction times across runs. Sleep resulted in marginally lower reaction times overall, indicating that consolidation may have an effect on predictive processing that is facilitatory independent of the validity of the information being presented. Collecting laminar fMRI data using this paradigm will allow linking behavioral measures of reweighting and reversal to laminar cortical processing to investigate their cortical mesoscopic implementation as well as the effects of memory consolidation on both cortical and hippocampal representations.
