Introduction

Humans exhibit remarkable flexibility in visual word recognition. When faced with words containing small spelling mistakes (“typos”), we can usually still understand them, though at a measurably increased processing cost. Neuroimaging studies consistently report stronger activation for misspelled words, notably within the left ventral occipitotemporal cortex (vOT) and superior temporal cortex (ST), thought to reflect greater neural processing effort.1 These effects are often explained by interactive accounts, where recurrent top-down feedback from language regions modulates lower-level orthographic processing.2 The underlying computational mechanism may be predictive coding, a unified brain theory where recurrent connections minimize prediction errors.3 In the predictive coding loop, higher-level areas use feedback connections to predict the activity of lower-level areas, while feedforward connections carry the residual, prediction errors. To test this hypothesis, we augmented a CNN model of visual word recognition4 with predictive coding-inspired recurrent dynamics5, to enable iterative refinement of word representations. We compared the model's behavioral robustness and internal representations with human behavioral and magnetoencephalography (MEG) data during misspelled-word reading.

Methods

MEG experiment 
We reanalyzed an existing dataset including MEG and behavioral data from 23 participants during a word and misspelled-word reading experiment (Figure 1A).1 The four conditions were real words (RW) and pseudowords generated by substituting one (RL1), two (RL2), or three (RL3) letters in high-frequency Finnish nouns (7–8 letters). Participants silently read stimuli and inferred the base word (Figure 1B) during an MEG recording, with 10% of trials being catch trials to sustain attention. 

The CNN model with predictive coding 
We developed a CNN model augmented with predictive coding layers (PCoders) to enable biologically-inspired recurrent feedback dynamics. The model utilizes a VGG16 backbone, pretrained on ImageNet, with the top convolutional layers and the classifier block retrained to classify images of 1000 high-frequency Finnish nouns. The training target was a soft distribution over the vocabulary based on Damerau–Levenshtein distance, encouraging the model to capture orthographic similarities. The PCoders were integrated into the three linear layers of the classifier block, focusing the recurrent dynamics on the abstract orthographic level (Figure 1C). The feedback weights in the PCoders were trained using a reconstruction objective, minimizing the mean squared error (MSE) between the top-down predicted reconstruction and the actual layer representation. During recurrent iterations, PCoder activity at each time-step was updated by four terms: normal CNN feedforward activity, feedback prediction for the preceding layer, feedforward prediction error, and memory (Figure 1D). The relative contributions of the four terms is determined by layer-specific hyperparameters, which were optimized on RL1 stimuli to minimize cross-entropy loss averaged across timesteps. 

Model–brain comparison 
To assess model–brain correspondence, we used Representational Similarity Analysis (RSA) and linear ridge regression to align model activations with MEG source activations in two regions of interest (ROIs): left vOT and ST. For RSA, Representational Dissimilarity Matrices (RDMs) were computed as Pearson correlation distance across condition pairs. MEG-RDMs were derived from condition-specific, vertex-wise evoked responses averaged across sliding windows (width: 100 ms, step: 20 ms). Model-RDMs were computed from condition-specific unit activations for two states: without feedback and with feedback. RSA score was quantified using Spearman's rank correlation. For linear ridge regression, model activations were used to predict grand-averaged ROI-wise MEG source activations over time using ten-fold cross-validation across stimulus trials. Prediction scores were computed as Pearson correlation between prediction and target. Statistical differences between the two model states for both RSA and regression were assessed using cluster-based permutation tests across participants/folds (p < 0.05).  

Results

Behavioral comparison. Human behavioral data (23 participants) showed a decreasing trend with increasing numbers of replaced letters, while there was no statistical difference between RW and RL1 accuracies (t (22) = -0.31, p = 0.75), underscoring the robustness of human word reading (Figure 2A). Model inference accuracy showed a graded decline across conditions (RW to RL3), consistent with human performance (Figure 2B). Importantly, the predictive coding dynamics led to iterative refinement of representations: accuracy increased across recurrent time steps, plateauing at step 23. Model accuracy showed a consistent decline from RW to RL3 (Figure 2B), but the predictive coding dynamics led to iterative refinement, increasing RL1 accuracy from a feedforward baseline of 0.844 to 0.904 at the plateau (step 23). This improvement arose from the refinement of base-word unit ranks in PCoder 3, where the recurrent dynamics mainly shifted the target word from rank 2 to rank 1 for RL1 (Figure 2C). 

Representational comparison. We examined two left-hemispheric regions of interest (ROIs), vOT and ST, based on their established roles in orthographic and lexico-semantic processing (Figure 2D). Furthermore, MEG evoked responses showed significant divergence between real words and pseudowords in both vOT and ST, with sustained clusters occurring later in vOT (~550 ms onward) than ST (~260 ms onward; Figure 2E). 

RSA revealed that feedback-incorporated model representations aligned more closely with MEG dynamics in both ROIs (Figure 2F), as indicated by the cluster-based permutation tests (p < 0.05). Furthermore, linear ridge regression analysis showed that feedback significantly enhanced predictive performance in both ROIs for PCoder 3 compared to the feedforward backbone (Figure 2G), with prediction scores peaking higher and earlier in left ST (~450–580 ms) than in left vOT (~600–800 ms). 

Conclusion

We found that predictive coding mechanisms can narrow the gap between human brain function and convolutional networks in misspelled-word reading. By extending a CNN model with predictive coding layers, we demonstrated improvements in both behavioral alignment with human performance and representational alignment with MEG responses. These findings provide converging evidence that recurrent feedback prediction and prediction error-correcting dynamics within a predictive-coding framework may constitute key computational principles supporting the human brain’s ability to read under challenging conditions.
