Introduction

Social anxiety disorder (SAD) is a prevalent and serious psychiatric condition, characterized by a debilitating fear of negative evaluation by others. SAD is hard to treat effectively, and due to the typical lifelong and chronic course, the disorder is associated with high personal burden and societal costs. Thus, research into the neurobiological characteristics of SAD is of high importance. 

Sample sizes of neuroimaging studies focusing on individuals with SAD are often small, stressing the relevance and momentum of international collaboration and data-sharing. Recent mega-analytic studies within the ENIGMA-Anxiety working group 1 examined neurobiological characteristics of SAD in pooled data with unprecedented sample sizes, revealing changes in subcortical volumes (1,115 individuals with SAD versus 2,775 controls) 2 and alterations in white matter (WM) microstructure (487 individuals with SAD versus 1,604 controls) 3. It is unclear, however, to which extent these alterations show similarity with changes observed in other psychiatric conditions. Exploring whether neurobiological patterns of SAD are unique or reflect transdiagnostic alterations related to psychopathology in general can be the first step in identifying SAD-specific treatment targets. 

Here, we took advantage of the fact that the ENIGMA-Anxiety analyses were performed within the broader ENIGMA (Enhancing Neuro Imaging Genetics Through Meta Analysis) consortium, using harmonized protocols for data processing and quality control in a broad variety of neuroimaging datasets 4. This enabled us to compare the effect sizes obtained in the ENIGMA-Anxiety case-control comparisons of SAD-related neurobiological characteristics (changes in subcortical volumes and WM alterations) to those obtained in ENIGMA-analyses on other psychiatric conditions. 

Methods

Effect sizes for case-control comparisons related to subcortical volumes and integrity of WM tracts were extracted from publications from the ENIGMA consortium. We focused on data from ENIGMA-disease working groups exploring the neurobiology of DSM-based psychiatric conditions. These included data from the ENIGMA-Bipolar, ENIGMA-MDD, ENIGMA-OCD, ENIGMA-PTSD and ENIGMA-Schizophrenia working groups, as well as data from multiple subgroups within the ENIGMA-Anxiety working group (SAD subgroup, Specific Phobia subgroup, Panic Disorder subgroup). 

Next, we performed correlation analyses between the mega-analytical effect sizes obtained in the ENIGMA-Anxiety SAD-projects and the effect sizes of case-control comparisons of individuals with specific phobia, panic disorder, bipolar disorder, major depressive disorder (MDD), obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD), posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and schizophrenia. For each psychiatric condition, we included effect sizes indicative of case-control comparisons of seven subcortical volumes (derived using the automated and validated segmentation software package FreeSurfer, in accordance with the ENIGMA-standardized protocol for brain segmentation and quality control) and effect sizes reflecting case-control comparisons of fractional anisotropy (FA) values of 24 WM tracts (data processed according to the ENIGMA-DTI protocol using the FMRIB Software Library (FSL)). 

Correlation analyses were performed using the regional vulnerability index package (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/RVIpkg/index.html) 5.

Results

Effect sizes for case-control comparisons of subcortical volumes were, in addition to those resulting from the ENIGMA-Anxiety project on SAD (number cases / number controls: 1,115 / 2,775), available for specific phobia (1,452 / 2,991), panic disorder (1,146 / 3,778), bipolar disorder (1,710 / 2,594), MDD (1,728 / 7,199), OCD  (only adults: 1,495 / 1,472), PTSD (794 / 1,074) and schizophrenia (2,028 / 2,540). 

Effect sizes for case-control comparisons of FA were available for SAD (487 / 1,604), bipolar disorder (1,482 / 1,551), MDD (only adults: 921 / 1,265), OCD (only adults: 700 / 645), PTSD (1,426 / 1,621) and schizophrenia (1,963 / 2,359).

When comparing the effect sizes of subcortical volumetric alterations and WM alterations in participants with SAD with those obtained in ENIGMA-studies of the other psychiatric disorders, effect sizes from participants with SAD were in general lower than those reported in other conditions. For subcortical volumes, largest effect sizes were found in case-control comparisons contrasting participants with and without schizophrenia (mean absolute effect size: 0.24), while effect sizes for SAD (mean: 0.05) were comparable to those obtained in OCD (0.07), MDD (0.05), PTSD (0.07), panic disorder (0.05) and specific phobia (0.06). For FA, mean absolute effect sizes were largest for schizophrenia (0.25), bipolar disorder (0.21) and MDD (0.18), while effect sizes for SAD (0.08) were comparable to those obtained in OCD (0.10), and larger than those resulting from case-control analyses in PTSD (0.03). 

Cross-disorder Pearson’s r correlation analyses of subcortical alterations revealed a high and significant correlation between the pattern of volumetric alterations in individuals with SAD and individuals with specific phobia (r= 0.82, p = 0.023), followed by a high (near significant) correlation between the patterns seen in SAD and OCD (r = 0.74, p = 0.056) (Figure 1A). Other correlations between patterns of subcortical volumetric alteration (SAD and MDD, SAD and schizophrenia, SAD and bipolar disorder, SAD and PTSD and SAD and panic disorder) were not significant. 

In addition, analyses revealed similarities between the patterns of WM alterations. Here, the highest correlation was observed between the WM patterns in participants with SAD and participants with schizophrenia (r = 0.68, p < 0.001). In addition, significant correlations were observed between data from participants with SAD and participants with MDD (r = 0.52, p = 0.009) and bipolar disorder (r = 0.49, p = 0.015) (Figure 1B). There were no significant correlations in WM patterns between SAD and PTSD or SAD and OCD. Notably, SAD, MDD, schizophrenia and BP displayed widespread and more global WM effects, while OCD and PTSD did not. 

Conclusion

These preliminary analyses shed light on the unique and shared neurobiological alterations in patients with SAD compared to other DSM-based psychiatric conditions. The pattern of subcortical volumetric alterations in SAD shows resemblance with patterns seen in individuals with specific phobia and OCD, while alterations in WM integrity in SAD show overlap with the patterns observed in patients with schizophrenia, MDD and bipolar disorder. These initial findings suggest that some of the neurobiological alterations (i.e. subcortical volumes and WM integrity) observed in SAD are reflective of transdiagnostic disease mechanisms, rather than being fully disorder-specific. 

While this analysis made use of the wealth of data within the ENIGMA-consortium, limitations of the present project include the use of main effect sizes of general case-control comparisons, not taking potential confounding factors like comorbidity, age and medication use within the patient samples into account. Thus, more detailed work is needed to explore whether case-control comparisons including only patients without comorbidity yield the same results, to investigate age-specific patterns and to examine the potential effects of medication use. Furthermore, future work should explore which regional differences (i.e. which subcortical areas and which WM tracts) contribute the most to the observed patterns.
