Introduction

The brain and immune system communicate continuously, allowing the central nervous system to sense, interpret, and regulate immune activity—a process known as immunoception. During peripheral inflammation, this interaction triggers an adaptive “sickness behavior” response characterized by fatigue, reduced motivation, and social withdrawal, redirecting metabolic resources toward immune defense. Major depressive disorder (MDD) shares several behavioral and physiological features with inflammation-induced sickness behavior and often presents with elevated circulating cytokines such as IL-6, IL-10, and TNF. This overlap suggests that dysregulated immunoceptive signaling may contribute to depressive symptoms.

Animal studies have shown that a vagus-mediated body–brain pathway links peripheral cytokine signaling to neurons in the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS), hypothalamus, amygdala, and insula. However, in humans, the neural mechanisms underlying inflammation-induced behavioral change remain poorly understood. Mild inflammatory challenges, such as low-dose lipopolysaccharide (LPS) administration, modulate insula activity and functional connectivity (FC), though findings vary in direction and magnitude across studies.

Here, we investigated whether an acute inflammatory challenge with LPS alters FC within an immunoceptive network encompassing the insula, amygdala, hypothalamus, and NTS, and whether these effects differ between individuals with MDD and healthy controls (HC). We hypothesized that the insula would show the strongest FC alterations and that these changes would correlate with increases in circulating cytokines and anhedonia severity, reflecting differential neural vulnerability to inflammatory stress.

Methods

We combined data from two harmonized, double-blind, placebo-controlled studies involving LPS infusion to examine how acute inflammation affects FC in MDD and HC. In total, 192 participants (116 MDD, 76 HC) were randomly assigned to receive an intravenous bolus of LPS (0.8 ng/kg) or saline. 

We acquired resting-state fMRI scans at baseline and 2.5 hours post-infusion, corresponding to the peak of cytokine elevation. Cardiac and respiratory signals were recorded simultaneously during scanning and used for RETROICOR. Blood samples collected at baseline and 1.5 hours post-infusion quantified IL-6, IL-10, and TNF using high-sensitivity immunoassays. We assessed anhedonia using the SHAPS and computed change scores (Δ) as post-infusion minus baseline values for FC, cytokines, and SHAPS. Cytokine values were log-transformed to reduce skewness and approximate normality.

After fMRI preprocessing using AFNI, we assessed how FC responded to immune challenge using voxel-wise linear mixed-effects modeling (AFNI’s 3dLMEr). The model included Drug (LPS vs. saline), Diagnosis (MDD vs. HC), and Time (pre- vs. post-infusion) as fixed factors. Sex, age, and study were included as covariates, and participant-specific random intercepts accounted for repeated measures.

We then evaluated relationships between inflammation-related FC changes and peripheral or behavioral responses by correlating ΔFC with ΔIL-6, ΔIL-10, ΔTNF, and ΔSHAPS. FC values were extracted from clusters showing significant time effects under LPS, and significance was determined using FDR correction (q < .05). Finally, we conducted moderation analyses to test whether baseline cytokine levels or SHAPS scores influenced LPS-related FC changes, controlling for age, sex, and study, and including Diagnosis × Baseline interactions to assess group-dependent effects.

Results

We found a significant Drug × Time interaction driven by LPS but not saline, indicating that FC changes reflected immune activation rather than nonspecific temporal or scanner-related effects. The strongest effects were observed for the insula seeds, and to a lesser extent, the right amygdala, while the left amygdala, hypothalamus, and NTS showed no significant time-dependent changes after correction. Regions showing this interaction were primarily distributed across interoceptive, salience, and affective processing networks (Fig. 1). Across all significant clusters, FC decreased following LPS administration, with no regions showing increased connectivity (voxel-wise p < .001, cluster-level p < .05, cluster size ≥ 53 voxels).

For the right insula, LPS induced FC decreases with the ACC, M1/S1, STG, supramarginal gyrus, dlPFC, visual association cortex, and frontal operculum. The left insula exhibited a similar but smaller pattern, consistent with the right hemisphere’s dominant role in interoceptive integration. These findings indicate widespread reductions in insula-centered connectivity following acute inflammation.

We observed significant correlations between FC decreases and increases in IL-6, IL-10, TNF, and SHAPS across participants who received LPS (Fig. 2A). These results link peripheral immune activation and affective symptoms to neural communication within interoceptive networks.

We also found a significant Diagnosis × Time interaction under LPS, indicating differential FC responses in MDD and HC. For both insula seeds, FC increased in HC but decreased in MDD, particularly within the vmPFC, M1/S1, and dPCC, reflecting divergent neural adaptations to inflammation. Baseline cytokine levels—but not anhedonia—showed modest moderating effects on FC responses, with higher baseline IL-6 predicting smaller FC changes in HC but not MDD (Fig. 2B). Although these effects did not survive correction, they suggest that pre-existing inflammation may shape neural reactivity to immune challenge differently across groups.

Conclusion

Our findings show that the human brain dynamically adapts to acute immune activation through changes in FC, particularly within insula-centered networks integrating interoceptive and emotional information. These neural changes parallel increases in circulating cytokines and anhedonic symptoms, underscoring a tight coupling between immune and affective processes.

Moreover, baseline cytokine levels appeared to influence the magnitude of FC changes, suggesting that individual immune tone and mood state modulate neural sensitivity to inflammatory stress. Together, these findings position the insula as a key neural interface between body and mind, providing a mechanistic framework for how inflammation contributes to depressive symptoms and highlighting potential targets for immunomodulatory interventions in mood disorders.
