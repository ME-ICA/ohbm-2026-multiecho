Introduction

Low-intensity transcranial focused ultrasound stimulation (TUS) is an emerging non-invasive neuromodulation technique with considerable therapeutic potential (1). By delivering targeted acoustic energy to specific brain regions, TUS can transiently alter neural activity with high spatial precision. Compared with other non-invasive brain stimulation (NIBS) methods, TUS offers several advantages: it is safe, painless, capable of modulating both cortical and deep-brain structures and can produce either excitatory or inhibitory effects depending on stimulation parameters (2,3). These properties make FUS a compelling tool for probing human brain function and developing novel clinical interventions.

Recent work has demonstrated that transcranial ultrasound stimulation (TUS), including theta-burst TUS (tbTUS), can induce rapid neurochemical, functional, and behavioural changes in humans (4-6). Studies have shown tbTUS-related modulation of inhibitory and excitatory neurotransmitters, alterations in large-scale functional connectivity, and enhancements in cognitive performance. However, the temporal dynamics and multimodal signatures of these neuromodulatory effects remain poorly understood, particularly when TUS is combined with real-time neuroimaging.

The present study investigates the immediate and prolonged neurobiological consequences of tbTUS by integrating concurrent transcranial ultrasound stimulation with magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS), resting-state functional MRI (rsfMRI), and motor behaviour. By applying tbTUS to the primary motor cortex and assessing neurochemical, network-level, and behavioural responses within the same session, we aim to characterise the mechanisms through which ultrasound induces rapid neuroplasticity in the human motor system. 

Methods

Twenty-one healthy, right-handed adults (11 female; mean age = 30.7 years, range 20–42) completed two experimental sessions in a within-subject, counterbalanced design. In separate sessions, participants received theta-burst transcranial ultrasound stimulation (tbTUS) targeting either the left primary motor cortex (M1) or the left lateral ventricle (control site) (5), with a minimum washout period of five days. Individualised structural MRI was used to guide precise target localisation for each participant.

Each session comprised three components: (i) proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) of left M1 acquired immediately before and after tbTUS; (ii) resting-state functional MRI (rsfMRI), obtained at three time points—baseline (5 minutes before tbTUS), during stimulation (80 seconds), and post-stimulation (15 minutes after tbTUS); and (iii) a visuomotor task performed outside the scanner immediately before and after stimulation to assess behavioural changes. During rsfMRI, participants-maintained fixation on a central cross.

For MRS, a single voxel was positioned over left M1, and concentrations of GABA, Glx (glutamate–glutamine), N-acetylaspartate, creatine, and choline were quantified using standard GANNET procedures.

tbTUS was administered via a 500 kHz single-element transducer using a theta-burst protocol (20 ms pulse duration, 200 ms inter-pulse interval, 10% duty cycle, ISPPA = 54.51 W/cm²; total stimulation duration = 80 s). Stimulation commenced five minutes after the acquisition of the baseline rsfMRI scan, enabling the assessment of both immediate (during tbTUS) and prolonged (post-tbTUS) neural responses.

The visuomotor task was performed outside the scanner. Participants responded using only their right index finger. At the start of each trial, a blue circle appeared in the upper-right corner of the screen, and participants moved the mouse cursor to this location to initiate the trial. Once the cursor reached the blue circle, a red target circle appeared at one of three possible screen locations, selected randomly on each trial. Participants were instructed to move the cursor to the red target as quickly and as accurately as possible. Each session consisted of 90 experimental trials, preceded by a 30-trial practice block to ensure task familiarisation.

Results

M1-targeted tbTUS produced significant behavioural improvements on the visuomotor task, whereas stimulation of the lateral ventricle produced no detectable behavioural change. Magnetic resonance spectroscopy revealed a significant reduction in GABA and an increase in Glx within left M1 following M1 tbTUS, with no corresponding neurochemical modulation after ventricle stimulation. These findings indicate a rapid, polarity-specific shift in local inhibitory–excitatory balance induced by tbTUS.

Resting-state fMRI acquired after stimulation demonstrated widespread functional connectivity (FC) changes specific to M1 tbTUS. Relative to baseline and to the ventricle condition, M1 tbTUS increased FC between M1 and a distributed set of regions including the supplementary motor area, operculum, visual cortex, and cerebellum, while reducing FC with the precuneus and additional cerebellar regions. No significant FC changes were detected during the 80-s stimulation window, suggesting that tbTUS exerts its principal network-level effects immediately following the stimulation period rather than during sonication. Ventricle stimulation yielded no significant FC alterations at any time point.

Together, these results demonstrate that focal tbTUS induces rapid, region-specific neurochemical shifts in human M1 and reorganises large-scale motor-related functional networks in a manner consistent with enhanced motor performance. The combined behavioural, neurochemical, and functional connectivity findings provide convergent evidence for robust, within-session neuroplastic effects driven by brief, targeted TUS.

Conclusion

This multimodal study provides convergent evidence that M1-targeted tbTUS induces rapid neurochemical shifts, alters large-scale functional connectivity, and enhances motor performance. The results extend previous offline tbTUS findings by revealing within-session neuroplastic changes measured concurrently with imaging. These data highlight tbTUS as a powerful tool for probing and modulating human brain function and support its translational potential for cognitive enhancement and neurorehabilitation.
