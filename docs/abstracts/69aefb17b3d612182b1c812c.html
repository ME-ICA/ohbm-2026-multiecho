Introduction

Aberrant resting-state functional connectivity (rsFC) in large-scale brain networks has been well documented in alcohol use disorder (AUD). However, the precise mechanism underlying the dysregulation of autonomic function, particularly the relationship between rsFC and heart rate variability (HRV) through the Central Autonomic Network (CAN), remains poorly understood in AUD. This study aimed to investigate the differences in CAN-based rsFC between young adults with AUD and healthy controls (HC) and to examine how these connectivity patterns differentially relate to HRV metrics.

Methods

A total of 112 young adults (54 AUD and 58 HC) were recruited for comprehensive resting-state fMRI and HRV assessments. Seed-based connectivity analysis was performed using key regions involved in the CAN as seeds. Group differences in rsFC were assessed using a two-sample t-test. Furthermore, voxel-wise correlation analyses were conducted within each group to identify brain regions where rsFC was significantly associated with standard HRV indices.

Results

Compared to the HC group, AUD patients exhibited significant alterations in CAN connectivity. Specifically, we observed lower connectivity between CAN regions and cortical areas (e.g., temporal and frontal cortices), alongside higher connectivity involving subcortical and paralimbic structures (e.g., insula and brainstem). Critically, the relationship between rsFC and HRV indices differed significantly between groups. In HC, HRV indices were broadly and positively correlated with rsFC across several brain regions, including the medial prefrontal cortex, inferior temporal cortex, and middle occipital gyrus, suggesting a robust, integrated brain–autonomic coupling. In stark contrast, the AUD group demonstrated a markedly limited and functionally segregated pattern of brain-autonomic correlation.

Conclusion

These findings provide compelling evidence for disrupted and segregated functional organization within the CAN of young individuals with AUD. The observed attenuated brain–autonomic coupling in AUD, manifested as distinct functional segregation, highlights a failure to effectively integrate cortical regulation with autonomic control. This distinct functional segregation pattern within the central autonomic network may serve as a potential quantitative neuroimaging trait marker of autonomic dysregulation and vulnerability in alcohol use disorder.
