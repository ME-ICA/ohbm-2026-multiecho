Introduction

The superficial white matter (SWM) comprises a layer of short- to medium-range fibers that supports cortico-cortical connectivity near the gyral and sulcal boundaries. Previous neuroimaging studies have largely focused on the morphology and spatial distribution and the functional implications of  SWM integrity. Our prior studies identified key anatomical structures, termed “crossings,” where SWM fibers converge from multiple directions, and demonstrated their relationship to cortical architecture using fiber dissection and neuroimaging analyses  [1, 2]. In this study, we analyzed hemispheric differences in SWM using a network-based approach.

Methods

Diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (dMRI) data from 10 healthy individuals from the Human Connectome Project were analyzed. A total of 1,210 regions of interest (ROIs) were defined in the MNI152 T1-weighted template space at presumed crossing locations across the whole brain (605 ROIs in each hemisphere). For fiber tracking, we applied the probabilistic second-order integration over fiber orientation distributions (iFOD2) algorithm to estimate streamlines (step size = 0.625 mm, maximum angle = 45°, FOD amplitude cutoff = 0.1, number of seedings = 5 million). Streamline lengths were categorized into three groups—5–20 mm, 21–40 mm, and 41–60 mm—representing short-, medium-, and long-length fibers, respectively. We then calculated the number of streamlines for each ROI and cross-tabulated these connections by cortical gyrus. Graph-theoretical measures (“local efficiency” and “strength”) were computed from the connectivity matrix to evaluate network segregation and integration. High-measure ROIs (“hub ROIs”) were defined as those with local efficiency and strength values exceeding the mean plus one standard deviation across participants. The number of hub ROIs and mean graph-theoretical metrics were then compared between hemispheres using the Mann–Whitney U test with FDR correction. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

The frequency of intragyral connections decreased with increasing streamline length, being lowest for long-length fibers. Intragyral connections in the occipital lobe were more frequently observed in the right hemisphere than in the left. After calculating local efficiency and strength, hub ROIs were identified. Hubs associated with short-length fibers were primarily located in primary cortical areas, whereas hubs associated with long-length fibers were distributed mainly within association areas. When comparing the distribution of hub ROIs between hemispheres, the left hemisphere showed a greater number of hubs in the frontal lobe—particularly within the inferior frontal gyrus, corresponding to the frontal language area. In contrast, the lateral occipital lobe, related to visual perception, exhibited a larger number of hubs in both hemispheres, with the strongest trend in the right hemisphere. In the medial occipital lobe, including the cuneus, which is related to reading, the left hemisphere demonstrated a greater number of hubs than the right.

Finally, comparisons of graph-theoretical measures revealed similar tendencies for local efficiency and strength. Values in the occipital lobe for short-length fibers were higher than those in other conditions. Values in the left frontal lobe—especially the inferior frontal gyrus—were significantly high across all tract-length groups (p < 0.0001 for every tract-length group). In the occipital lobe (i.e., the inferior occipital gyrus and gyrus descendens), the right hemisphere showed significantly higher values in all tract-length groups (p < 0.0001 for every tract-length group), whereas the left medial occipital lobe and superior frontal gyrus also exhibited significantly high values compared with the right (cuneus: medium-length, p = 0.00046; long-length, p = 0.0066; medial superior frontal gyrus: long-length, p < 0.0001).

Conclusion

In summary, SWM network organization exhibits marked hemispheric asymmetries, with left-hemisphere dominance in language-related regions and right-hemisphere dominance in visual-related regions. These findings provide new insights into the microstructural and network-level architecture of SWM, highlighting its high-connectivity regions and offering new insights into the brain’s anatomical architecture.
