Introduction

Chronic pain is associated with cognitive impairment, altered neural networks activity, and structural brain changes resembling accelerated aging.¹ The brain age gap (BAG) represents the difference between predicted and chronological age, offering a biologically informed index of brain health derived from neuroimaging data.² Every one-year increase in BAG raises Alzheimer’s risk by 16.5%, and all-cause mortality by 12%, while lifestyle interventions such as smoking cessation and physical activity slowed BAG progression.²

Despite the promise of BAG as a biomarker of health status and functioning, its role in chronic pain and resultant disability is understudied. While small-scale studies report elevated BAG across chronic pain conditions, it remains unclear whether elevated BAG is a common feature of all chronic pain conditions or whether it differs across specific conditions, such as chronic low back pain and migraine.¹ Moreover, most previous pain-related BAG studies relied solely on structural MRI, resulting in an uncertainty as to whether functional connectivity (FC), which captures large-scale network interactions implicated in pain chronification, provides complementary information in understanding how chronic pain affects brain aging patterns.¹ 

Here, we apply an FC–based ridge regression age-prediction model trained on healthy controls (HC) to estimate BAG in CM and CBP. Given the limited FC training sample and known instability of brain-age models trained on small samples, estimated BAG values and their clinical associations are interpreted conservatively.² This analysis represents an initial step towards a larger effort that will integrate multimodal brain-age modeling with substantially larger normative datasets.

Methods

The participants were originally recruited as a part of a study that investigated the effect of experimental pain on cognitive performance in two age-matched patient groups as compared to HC at the University Hospital Essen. Both cohorts were thoroughly characterized regarding mental health comorbidity, clinical features and psychometrics. The age ranges were 20–71 years in HC, 20–68 years in CBP, and 19–75 years in CM. To ensure their comparability in terms of BAG, the mean ages were compared (mean HC=36.41 ± 12.76 (n=44), CM=37.47 ± 14.55 (n=45), and CBP=38.94 ± 14.07 (n=36)). Variances did not differ (Levene’s p = 0.877), and a one-way ANOVA confirmed no significant age differences (F(2,122) = 0.33, p = 0.716), indicating that age is well-matched across groups. Gender distributions were likewise nonsignificant (χ²(2) = 4.25, p = .119). The proportion of females in the CM group was prominently higher, in line with reported higher prevalence of migraine in females compared to males, but the present sample size limits sensitivity to this difference. Given this imbalance, gender influence on age-residualized BAG was also tested using regression analysis.
    Resting-state fMRI data were acquired on a 3T Siemens Skyra scanner using a gradient-echo EPI sequence (TR = 2.3 s, TE = 28 ms). Pipelines Utilizing a Modular Inventory (PUMI) software was used for preprocessing.⁴ To obtain whole-brain connectivity, time series were extracted via the MIST-122 atlas. Using Python 3.11 (nilearn and scikit-learn libraries) partial correlations were computed and Fisher-z transformed, and the upper triangular entries were vectorized, obtaining 7053 features.
    A normative ridge-regression age-prediction model was trained in HC using nested cross-validation (CV), with LeaveOneOut CV in the outer loop, rendering HC performance as a reference for typical ageing. To manage dimensionality and stabilize coefficient estimates, univariate feature selection (SelectKBest, F-regression) was combined with ridge regularization. The number of selected features (20–360) and the regularization parameter α (0.0001–15) were optimized within a five-fold inner cross-validation loop. Age-related bias was removed following Beheshti et al.’s linear delta-adjustment.⁴ Corrected BAG was defined as bias-adjusted predicted age minus chronological age. The optimal ridge model (40 connectivity edges and α=0.1) was used for subsequent validation of BAG in CM and CBP.

Between-group BAG differences were evaluated using ANOVA, Levene’s test, and Cohen’s d. Because age-dependence can persist outside the training sample after linear correction, BAG was additionally residualized within each clinical group using ordinary least squares (OLS) regression before clinical correlation analyses (Benjamin-Yukateli (BY)-FDR corrected). Lastly, model interpretation used ridge coefficients in HC individuals and FC–BAG correlations in patient groups, mapped to seven canonical networks of the MIST atlas. 

Results

Training model
In HC individuals, the age-prediction model achieved RMSE = 11.08, MAE = 9.34, and r  =0.48 (p = 0.001).  The mean uncorrected brain age gap (BAG) was +9.39 years. After linear delta-adjustment, performance improved (RMSE = 5.27; MAE = 4.45; r = 0.92), and corrected BAG was centered at 0.00 years (SD = 5.27).

External validation
In CBP patients, uncorrected RMSE was 13.6, improving to 5.4 after correction (MAE = 4.01; r  = 0.93). In CM patients, uncorrected RMSE was 15.4, improving to 5.7 (MAE = 4.4; r  = 0.94).

Group-level BAG
Uncorrected BAG had the following group-wise mean values (CM: −2.66; CBP: −3.48 years). Corrected BAG values were mildly negative in both clinical groups (CM: −1.89 years, 95% CI: −3.5 to −0.3; CBP: −1.57 years, 95% CI: −3.3 to +0.2). No significant differences in corrected BAG were observed across HC, CM, and CBP (F(2,122) = 1.57, p = 0.212).

Age dependence, clinical associations and features

Corrected BAG showed residual age dependence across CM and CBP (R² = 0.24, p < 0.001). After age residualization, BAG was not significantly associated with variables from the German Pain Questionnaire (DSF), including mean and maximum pain intensity, current pain level, pain-related disability, or pain level on the day of MRI scanning (all q > 0.10). For example, the association between maximum pain intensity and corrected BAG in CM prior to residualization (r = 0.20, p = 0.19) was further attenuated after age residualization (r = −0.06, p = 0.72). Additionally, gender did not explain variability in age-residualized BAG (β = 0.24, p = 0.84), and adjusting for gender did not alter CM–CBP differences. Across groups, BAG-related FC variation involved motor–salience, cerebello-thalamic, and fronto-visual interactions.

Conclusion

This study provides the first simultaneous evaluation of functional-connectivity–based brain age gap estimates in chronic migraine and chronic back pain. The ridge model trained on healthy controls showed improved accuracy after bias correction and performed comparably across clinical groups. CM and CBP exhibited mildly negative BAG values but did not differ meaningfully from HC or from each other, and BAG was not associated with pain severity and disability. These findings are consistent with prior structural MRI findings indicating that BAG in chronic back pain resembles normative values, but contrast with studies reporting elevated BAG in chronic migraine.⁵ Such a discrepancy may be due to limited statistical sensitivity in FC-based estimation or genuine differences between structural and functional aging markers.¹
    Importantly, this work establishes the methodological basis for the following steps, although its interpretation must remain cautious due to the small training cohort and known instability of FC-derived BAG at low sample sizes.² As a subsequent step, increasing training sample size using openly available datasets and integrating structural and functional modalities will clarify whether the present results reflect insufficient power or underlying FC-based ageing patterns. The ongoing project will provide more stable BAG estimates using established high-N training cohorts and will assess whether unimodal or multimodal (i.e., combined functional and structural measures) approaches provide reliable markers of pain-related alterations in brain ageing.
