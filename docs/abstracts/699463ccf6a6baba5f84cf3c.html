Introduction

Major depression (MD) is a highly prevalent psychiatric disorder, characterized by a constellation of affective and cognitive symptoms. A large body of neuroimaging work has shown abnormalities in depression relative to controls in canonical brain networks, particularly within- and among the default mode, frontoparietal, salience, and limbic networks (e.g.,^1). Although traditional connectivity metrics provide valuable insight into brain function, they overlook the moment-to-moment fluctuations that reflect the brain’s complex and adaptive dynamics. Brain signal variability quantifies these temporal fluctuations to provide a direct measure of neural flexibility, giving complementary insight into network-level brain dynamics. Emerging evidence suggests that variability profiles can differentiate depression from controls and also relate to symptom severity. For example, depression has been linked to higher variability in regions within the default mode^2 and frontoparietal networks^3, and greater decreases in limbic variability has been associated with greater symptom reduction following antidepressant treatment.^4 However, results are mixed and are often either restricted to single time-point measures or small, single-site samples. In the current study, we used a relatively large, multisite fMRI dataset to replicate and extend previous variability work. The dataset was collected as part of the Canadian Biomarker Integration Network in Depression 1 (CAN-BIND-1) study^5 and included patients undergoing pharmacotherapy, which is the most common first-line treatment for MD. We examined whether baseline network variability can differentiate treatment responders from non-responders, and characterized the trajectory of change over the course of treatment, providing insight into dynamic brain changes associated with clinical response. Patients completed both resting-state and task-based scans to assess the predictive value of both internally and externally focused states.

Methods

Three fMRI scans (baseline, week 2, week 8) from 88 adults with MD enrolled in the CAN-BIND Phase 1 study were used in this study. Participants were 18–60 years old (55 females, 33 males) and met CAN-BIND diagnostic and exclusion criteria. Patients were treated for 16 weeks with escitalopram, and an add-on of aripiprazole after 8 weeks if symptoms decreased less than 50%.^5 Treatment response at week 16 was defined as ≥50% reduction in Montgomery-Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS), yielding 67 responders and 21 non-responders. 

FMRI data were acquired on 3T MRI scanners at the six sites. Blood‑oxygenation-level dependent (BOLD) images were collected using echo‑planar imaging (EPI) with repetition time = 2 s, echo time = 25–30 ms, flip angle = 75° or 90°, field of view = 256 mm, matrix = 64 × 64, and 34–40 axial slices acquired interleaved. Here we focus on data collected during eyes‑open resting‑state and an affective go/no‑go task. Quality control ensured data comparability across scanners. Preprocessing followed the Ontario Pipeline for Preprocessing of Neuroimaging Data (OPPNI), including motion correction, slice-timing correction, outlier censoring, spatial smoothing, PHYCAA+ physiological noise removal, nuisance regression (motion, global signal), low-pass filtering (0.10 Hz), and normalization to MNI152 space.

For brain signal variability estimation, preprocessed fMRI data were parcellated using the Schaefer-2018 atlas (200 parcels, 7-networks). Mean Squared Successive Differences (MSSD) were calculated for each parcel's time series. MSSD quantifies moment-to-moment brain signal variability. Network-level MSSD was computed by averaging parcel values within each network: dorsal attention (DAN), default mode (DMN), frontoparietal (FPN), limbic (LIM), somatomotor (SMN), ventral attention (VAN) and visual (VIS).This analysis was applied to both resting-state and go/no-go task fMRI data.

Baseline differences and longitudinal trajectories of MSSD were analysed using linear mixed-effects models for each brain network. Models included fixed effects of time, response group (responder vs. non-responder), age and sex, along with their time×response group interaction. Random intercepts and slopes for time were included at the participant level to account for within-subject correlation across repeated measurements.

The mixed-effects model tested three key hypotheses: (1) whether response groups differed at baseline (response group main effect), (2) whether each group showed significant change over the 8-week time (time effects for responders and non-responders), and (3) whether groups exhibited different trajectories (time×response interaction). Statistical significance was set at p < .05 for baseline between-group differences, interactions, and within-group time effects.

Results

Results from our mixed-effects models controlling for age and sex are presented in Figure 1. Resting-state analysis revealed a significant baseline difference in the Limbic network, with responders (N = 67) showing higher MSSD compared to non-responders (N = 21; β = 0.0145, p = .007). This was the only network showing significant baseline differences between groups. Task-based MSSD during go/no-go performance showed no significant baseline differences between responders and non-responders in any network.

Analysis of treatment-related changes revealed convergent patterns across both resting-state and task (Figure 1). In resting-state, responders showed significant decreases in MSSD over the 8-week period in the Control network (β = -0.0031, p = .029) and default mode network (β = -0.0032, p = .049), indicating reduced signal variability with treatment. Task-based analysis revealed similar patterns, with responders showing larger decreases in the frontoparietal network (β = -0.0049, p = .009) and default mode network (β = -0.0050, p = .008). In contrast, non-responders showed no significant changes in any network for either modality (all p > .05). While responders demonstrated treatment-related decreases and non-responders remained stable, the time×response group interactions did not reach statistical significance. 

Conclusion

Our study replicated and extended brain signal variability work in patients with MD, offering insight into the predictive value of baseline neural dynamics and the characterization of treatment-related changes. Specifically, the significant baseline difference in resting-state between responders and non-responders suggests that variability within the limbic network can be linked to future treatment outcome, which is consistent with previous work showing a positive association between baseline limbic variability and pharmacotherapy response. ^4 In addition, decreases in frontoparietal and default mode network variability among responders, observed in both scanning modalities, suggest that pharmacotherapy may promote normalization of neural dynamics in networks supporting cognitive control and self-referential processing, irrespective of whether they are measured during internally or externally focused states using fMRI. Overall, our findings suggest that measures of neural complexity have the potential to provide valuable insight into the pathophysiology of depression and inform future treatment choices.
