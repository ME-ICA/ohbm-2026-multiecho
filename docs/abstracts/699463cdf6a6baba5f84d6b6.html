Introduction

Post-stroke neuropsychiatric symptoms affect a significant proportion of patients1. Although they impede the recovery trajectory, to date, little is known about their predictive factors to guide optimal clinical care. Considering the multifactorial nature of neuropsychiatric conditions, it is challenging to identify brain properties that reliably predict them. It is now clear that no single region plays a predominant role in post-stroke neuropsychiatric outcomes. Given the understanding that brain regions function within functional circuits rather than as independent modules, research into the neural substrates of higher-order thinking and associated disorders has increasingly focused on broader, whole-brain analyses. In light of this, it was proposed that studying the disconnectivity in white matter, or the disconnectome, may result in more accurate predictions of post-stroke neuropsychological status2. Yet, it has not been effective in predicting post-stroke mood impairments such as depression and anxiety to date3. Beyond the context of stroke, research on psychiatric disorders has established that emotional difficulties and associated disorders cannot be localized more specifically than at the level of neural circuits4,5. If non-cerebral factors are excluded - which likely play an equal if not greater role - broader neural patterns may offer a clearer perspective on these conditions than fine-grained calculations at the voxel level.

In this context, we introduced a model to predict long-term post-stroke mood impairments using clinical neuroimaging data. The model performs a linear compression of an entire dataset to extract global diffusivity patterns relevant to mood evaluations.

Methods

Three heterogeneous stroke cohorts (ntotal = 672) were examined, which included mood evaluations six months post-acute stroke unit discharge: the Hospital Anxiety and Depression (HAD) and Center for Epidemiologic Studies–Depression (CES-D) scales. Clinical mean diffusion-weighted MRIs were extracted, with one cohort having a 3T scanner and 1.5 T scanners for the others. All images were three-directional and had b-values of 0 and 1000 s/mm2.

All analysis was conducted on Python. For preprocessing, the images were skull-stripped and normalized. The pipeline was created using all the images from one cohort:

1. Dataset compression: Each subject’s image was taken as a 1-D array of the unraveled intensity values and the combined matrix of the data was compressed through principal component analysis (PCA) to provide 100 components.

2. Component selection: Spearman’s correlations were done between the 100 PCA components and the patients’ mood scales’ scores. Components with a correlation coefficients greater than 0.2 were selected for the specific evaluation.

3. Training: k-nearest neighbors (k-NN) regression was conducted between the significant components and the emotional scores. 

4. Testing: The MRI images of a different cohort were transformed using the original PCA to obtain 100 components and the new scores were predicted with the original k-NN regression parameters. 

The performance was compared with the Disconnectome Symptom Discoverer (DSD)2 and a clinical model: routine evaluations conducted at baseline were taken into a negative binomial mixed model to obtain the best fit - (1) age, (2) sex, (3) baseline neurological status (IQCODE, pre-stroke mRS, admission NIHSS), (4) stroke volume, and (5) baseline cognitive (MoCA) and (6) mood status (only present for population with HAD scores). Three goodness-of-fit indices were used: the pseudo R-squared (R2), the mean squared error (MSE), and the area under the receiver operating characteristics curve (AUC).

Results

Two components associated with mood scores are displayed in Figure 1. For HAD outcomes, clinical models performed best for Anxiety (R2=0.225, MSE=7.13, AUC=0.735) and Depression scores (R2=0.268, MSE=8.17, AUC=0.795). The imaging-based pipeline achieved moderate prediction accuracy for HAD Anxiety (R2=0.064, MSE=16.03, AUC=0.613) and Depression (R2=0.067, MSE=15.37, AUC=0.708) scores and outperformed clinical models that lacked baseline mood status in predicting CES-D scores (Pipeline: R2=0.073, MSE=119.9, AUC=0.611; Clinical: R2=0.022, MSE=119.0, AUC=0.597) . Disconnectome models showed minimal predictive power across all datasets (R2<0.01).

Conclusion

The proposed imaging pipeline demonstrates moderate predictive capacity across independent cohorts and outperforms disconnectome models and clinical models without baseline mood scores. With further refinements, our methodology shows promise in identifying whole-brain components relevant to neuropsychiatric outcomes after stroke.
