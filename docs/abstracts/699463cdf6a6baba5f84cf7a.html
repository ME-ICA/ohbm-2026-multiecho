Introduction

A central goal of fMRI research is to compare activation patterns across subjects using group-level statistical maps, but intersubject variability in cortical anatomy makes accurate alignment challenging. Surface-based analysis improves anatomical correspondence by aligning data along the two-dimensional geometry of the cortical sheet, reducing signal mixing between gyri and sulci. This requires transforming functional data from voxel to vertex space, which depends on accurate cortical surface reconstruction from structural MRI [1]. This surface reconstruction is computationally intensive, contributing to substantial energy use and environmental impact through carbon pollution from electricity generation. FreeSurfer [2], a widely used tool for surface reconstruction, relies primarily on CPU-based computation but includes limited GPU support. Deep learning–based tools such as FastSurfer [3] use GPU acceleration to markedly reduce processing time while aiming to approximate FreeSurfer’s output quality. Although FastSurfer has been validated for anatomical analysis, it has not been shown to provide equivalent outcomes as Freesurfer for fMRI analysis. This study therefore aimed to identify the most efficient and environmentally sustainable processing profiles for cortical surface reconstruction when using FreeSurfer or FastSurfer. At the same time, we sought to establish the equivalence of FreeSurfer and FastSurfer outputs for fMRI analysis.

Methods

An existing dataset [4] consisting of T1-weighted anatomical images and BOLD-sensitive functional images from a body-part movement task was used (76 healthy participants (age 18–89, 35 males, 41 females). To identify the most energy-efficient profile for cortical surface reconstruction, we evaluated 21 processing configurations—15 FreeSurfer (v7.4.1 and v8.1) and 6 FastSurfer profiles. These configurations varied in CPU threading (1, 8, or 16 threads), the use of parallel hemisphere processing, and GPU acceleration (supported in FastSurfer and FreeSurfer v8.1). All runs were executed on a workstation with an 8-core/16-thread CPU and an NVIDIA GPU. Carbon emissions and runtime for each profile were quantified using CodeCarbon [5]. After identifying the optimal profiles, functional data were sampled between the white and pial boundaries (0.2–0.8 cortical depth) and averaged to project volumetric fMRI onto each subject’s surface. Four task fMRI runs—movement of tongue, feet, right fingers, and left fingers—were analyzed. Surface-based GLM analyses were performed in Nilearn at both first and group levels. To compare pipelines, correlations were computed between group-level activation maps across the whole cortex and within the precentral gyrus.

Results

Figure 1A reports the per-subject carbon emissions and processing time for all 21 configurations. The most efficient profile was FastSurfer with GPU acceleration, 8 CPU threads, and parallel hemispheres, producing 21 g CO₂e per subject with a runtime of 25.1 minutes. The top three configurations were all FastSurfer profiles. The best FreeSurfer configuration ranked fourth and corresponded to FreeSurfer v8.1 using 8 CPU threads with parallel hemispheres. A notable trade-off emerged between the second- and third-ranked FastSurfer profiles: FastSurfer with 8 CPU threads and parallel hemispheres (34.7 min) ran faster than FastSurfer with 1 CPU thread in CPU–GPU parallel mode (37.2 min), although the latter generated slightly lower carbon emissions. Figure 1B shows that the two pipelines produced highly consistent results, with correlations of 0.99 between their group-level task-activation maps in both hemispheres, indicating nearly identical activation patterns.

Conclusion

FastSurfer reconstructed cortical surfaces substantially faster and with markedly lower carbon emissions than FreeSurfer. Its top configurations outperformed the best FreeSurfer setup, while producing equivalent functional results (r = 0.99), supporting FastSurfer as the more carbon-efficient choice for surface-based functional analyses. The overall most efficient configuration was FastSurfer with GPU acceleration, 8 CPU threads, and parallel hemisphere processing, demonstrating that GPU use further reduces emissions. Within FreeSurfer, all v8.1 configurations were both faster and more carbon-efficient than v7.4.1. Finally, increasing the number of parallel processes beyond the number of physical cores did not improve performance in either pipeline.
