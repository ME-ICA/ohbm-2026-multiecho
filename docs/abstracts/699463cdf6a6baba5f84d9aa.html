Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative disorder characterized by progressive accumulation of misfolded proteins, particularly α-synuclein, degeneration of the substantia nigra and multiple age-related biological changes including neuroinflammation. Understanding the complex interrelations between biological brain ageing processes and PD pathology could shed new light on its pathophysiology and may identify targetable pathways for preventative intervention strategies. 

There is increasing evidence that the glymphatic system, a more recently discovered brain-wide perivascular CSF-mediated waste clearance network is dysfunctional in Parkinson’s with detrimental neurotoxic effect1. The choroid plexus (CP) serves as a neuroimmune interface, regulating CSF production, cytokine secretion, and immune cell trafficking and was also found to be enlarged in PD2. Dysfunctional interactions between glymphatic activity and CP-mediated immune processes may accelerate structural brain aging and increase vulnerability to cognitive decline, even in the absence of overt cognitive impairment. MRI-derived brain age prediction models are new powerful cumulative markers of biological brain age gap (BAG), capturing genetic vulnerability as well as effects from diseases, lifestyle and even psychosocial vulnerability3,4. While prior studies in cognitively impaired PD5 have linked ALPS to cognition, the relationships among glymphatic function, CP-mediated immune activity, global GM aging, and cognition in cognitively unimpaired PD remain largely unexplored.

This study in elderly cognitively unimpaired participants with and without early PD aimed (1) to examine the association between glymphatic activity, CP-mediated immune processes, and accelerated brain aging (global GM aging), and (2) to determine whether the relationship between glymphatic function and cognition is altered by disease status. 

Methods

This is a secondary analysis of the baseline MRI and clinical features of an on-going longitudinal single-centre cohort study.

Participants

Baseline multimodal features from 133 cognitively unimpaired participants (with Addenbrooke’s Cognitive Examination Revised version [ACE-R] scoring ≥ 88) were included in this study. This contained 61 healthy controls (HC) and 72 PD patients from InsightPD study. 

MRI Acquisition and processing

All imaging was performed on a 3T MRI Premier GE using a 32-channel head coil. Structural and diffusion data were preprocessed using standard pipelines BRC-pipeline (https://github.com/SPMIC-UoN/BRC_Pipeline). 

The multimodal MRI comprised structural MRI (High-resolution T1-weighted 3D MRI) and diffusion-weighted images (three-shell, 116-direction protocol (b = 0 and 2000 s/mm²; voxel size = 2 mm³ isotropic). Preprocessing included eddy-current and motion correction, brain extraction, and tensor fitting using FSL.
For calculating ALPS, regions of interest were placed in projection and association fibre areas near the lateral ventricles. Diffusivity along the x-, y-, and z-axes was extracted, and the ALPS index was calculated as: ALPS=(Dxproj+Dxassoc)/(Dyproj+Dzassoc), where Dx, Dy, and Dz represent diffusivities along the respective axes. All processing was performed using our local pipeline with visual QC of the region of interest placement.

CPV was segmented from T1-weighted images using a 3D U-Net trained on multi-modal MRI data. Segmentations were visually inspected for quality, and two failed datasets were excluded. CPV was normalized by total intracranial volume (TIV) to account for interindividual differences. 

GM-based brain age models used a pretrained machine-learning model trained on >15,000 healthy adults from the UK Biobank4. Multimodal GM features were extracted and harmonised using ComBAT to reduce scanner and protocol-related bias. BAG was defined as model-predicted GM age – chronological age, indicating accelerated GM aging. 

Statistical Analysis
 All statistical analyses were performed in MATLAB R2024a. Linear regression models were used to examine associations between key variables. To explore the relationship between ALPS and CPV, we used linear models including age, sex, and group (PD vs. HC) as covariates, with a Group × ALPS interaction term to assess disease modulation. To address the associations between BAG and ALPS, CPV, an additive model controlling for sex, was evaluated in both HC and PD. Analyses were performed in the full sample (HC and PD participants without cognitive impairment). Finally, linear models were applied to test whether ALPS predicts ACE and whether this association is modulated by PD (Group × ALPS interaction). Age and sex were included as covariates. 

Results

We first examined the relationship between ALPS and CPV across cohorts. In the full cognitively unimpaired cohort, higher ALPS was associated with lower CPV (β = −0.00242, p = 0.00016), after controlling for age, sex, and group. The interaction between group and ALPS was not significant (β = 0.00083, p = 0.258), indicating that the ALPS-CPV association did not differ between PD and HC. Also, there was no difference in ALPS or CPV between the PD and HC groups. Age was positively associated with CPV (β = 2.36 × 10-5, p = 0.00024), while sex and group main effects were non-significant.

Next, our results revealed that lower ALPS was significantly associated with higher BAG in cognitive unimpaired PD (β = -8.0982, p = 0.027), whereas CPV was not significant (p= 0.704). In HCs, neither ALPS (p= 0.607) nor CPV was associated with BAG (p= 0.425). 

Finally, we examined whether ALPS predicts cognitive performance (ACE-R) and whether this relationship is modulated by disease. In the cognitively unimpaired cohort, ALPS was positively associated with ACE in HC (β = 7.487, p = 0.026), but the Group (HC+PD) × ALPS interaction was significant (β = −11.223, p = 0.0047), indicating that the positive association between ALPS and cognition was attenuated in PD. Age was negatively associated with ACE (β = −0.105, p = 0.0021), while sex and group main effects were non-significant.

Conclusion

Our findings provide additional evidence that glymphatic function is inversely associated with CP volume, a marker of neuroinflammation in cognitively unimpaired healthy participants and those with PD. We also found that reduced glymphatic activity predicts accelerated global gray matter aging (higher BAG), and that this effect is stronger in PD participants, indicating early disease-specific amplification of structural vulnerability. Conversely, cognitive performance was positively associated with glymphatic function in healthy controls, but this effect was reduced in PD, suggestinga reduction of protective effects and or emergence of additional pathways contributing to cognitive performance in early PD.

We did not observe a reduction of DTI-ALPS or CPV in early PD despite systematic reviews1,5 reporting consistent lower DTI-ALPS in PD with a moderate effect size. However, DTI-ALPS reduction in PD was found to be correlated with disease duration and severity, which may explain the discrepancy, as our study group comprised participants in early-stage PD. Moreover, there remain technical challenges limiting the reproducibility of DTI-ALPS metrics with biologically unexplained strong asymmetry effects in PD5 and increases in women. Interestingly, in our HC group, there were no differences between males and females.

To our knowledge, our study is the first to reveal an association between impaired glymphatic function and increased BAG in cognitive unimpaired PD. This effect was not seen in HC, nor was BAG associated with CPV in PD or HC. While preliminary, this observation suggests a possible PD-specific mechanism linking impaired waste clearance with accelerated GM aging that may herald future cognitive impairment. Previous longitudinal studies show that elevated BAG predicts faster cognitive decline and higher risk of PD‑MCI, supporting the concept of accelerated gray‑matter ageing early in disease. Studies also show that lower baseline ALPS is associated with poorer composite outcomes, including the development of dementia/MCI and broader “poor‑outcome” trajectories marked by accelerated cortical thinning and iron redistribution. 

Interestingly, we observed an attenuated association between cognitive performance and ALPS in PD compared to HC. This may have been a result of recruiting only cognitively unimpaired participants to this study, but it may point to distinct mechanisms driving cognitive decline in PD. To clarify the role of waste clearance and accelerated brain ageing in driving cognitive decline in PD, well-designed prospective longitudinal studies are needed.  

Together, these findings offer new insights into the complex interplay of the glymphatic-CP-immune interface and its association with accelerated brain aging in early PD.
