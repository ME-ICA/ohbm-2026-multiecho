Introduction

Caffeine, by acting as a competitive adenosine receptor antagonist, relieves inhibitory control in the central nervous system, consequently increasing the release of norepinephrine (NE)1, a neuromodulator for arousal. This pharmacological action is the core mechanism underlying caffeine's robust effects on alertness, vigilance, and enhanced sympathetic drive. 

Extensive research confirms that caffeine modulates attentional and emotional processing in a dose-dependent manner. Behaviorally, a moderate dose (150 mg) increases physiological arousal (skin conductance responses) and augments costly avoidance behavior in a conflict task.2 Furthermore, higher doses (e.g., 250 mg) introduce a negative processing bias, selectively heightening threat-related neural activity and increasing self-rated anxiety.3

Despite these findings, a significant research gap remains: current literature relies predominantly on static stimuli (e.g., emotional faces), failing to capture the effects of caffeine on complex decision-making processes under dynamic, competing motivations. This study addresses this gap by employing a dose-response, double-blinded randomized design (placebo, 100 mg caffeine, 200 mg caffeine) within a novel, dynamic approach/avoidance task that mimics real-world motivational behaviors, offering unique insight into how caffeine-driven arousal modulation biases the prioritization and efficiency of flexible reinforcement processing. 

Methods

Sixty healthy, normal-weight young adults (43 females, 17 males) were recruited from the local community. Eligibility required participants to be non-habitual caffeine users (consuming less than 200mg of caffeine per week) and had no substance dependence or any current/historical neuropsychological conditions. Participants were randomly assigned to one of three intake groups: Placebo, 100mg Caffeine, or 200mg Caffeine. All procedures were IRB-approved, and informed consent was obtained.

On the day of the experiment, participants' baseline conditions were assessed, including self-reported sleep quality and a detailed scanning of caffeine consumption over the past 7 days. Participants then consumed the assigned capsule (caffeine or placebo). The placebo uses the same capsule coating as caffeine but with white corn starch as its contents. Visually, it is indistinguishable from the caffeine capsules. Following a 40-minute absorption period, they completed the approach/avoidance task and they were told that they would receive monetary compensation based on their performance. All participants completed the task in the morning after 9 AM in a lighting- and temperature-matched room. 

The approach/avoidance task was programmed in Python using the Pygame library. The paradigm is shown in Figure 1. The task required participants to control a virtual avatar at the bottom of the screen using discrete left and right arrow keypresses to navigate. Participants needed to dynamically respond to approaching objects in two different strategies: evading threats (demons) and catching rewards (coins). Vertically, the total descent time for the stimuli was controlled as a within-subjects factor: fast stimuli took approximately 4 seconds to reach the screen bottom, while slow stimuli took approximately 10 seconds. During the initial phase (first two-thirds of the screen height), the threat/reward traverse downwards along a complex trajectory with random pendulum (horizontal) sway, combined with mild, player-based tracking if threat/evasion if reward; When the stimulus reached at the lower one-third of the screen, horizontal modulation was significantly enhanced: threats actively pursued the player, while rewards actively evaded the player. This dynamic, escalating interaction requires participants to track the object with sustained attention and rapidly respond to its movement.

The task comprised 160 trials across four runs (40 trials/run) with a total duration of 22 minutes. The 2x2 within-subjects design (Intensity: High/Low; Velocity: Fast/Slow) and the stimuli Type (reward/threat) trials were presented in randomized order with equal frequency across trials (10 trials per condition per run).

Primary dependent variables included total keypresses, reflecting motor output, and escape efficiency, which was calculated by log transformed (accuracy divided by keypress), i.e., ln(mean accuracy/mean keypress), quantifying action effectiveness. Subjective arousal was measured by the average of pre- and post-task VAS-measured arousal levels. 

In all models, Group (100mg, 200mg, Placebo) served as the between-subjects factor, and Velocity (fast, slow) and Intensity (high, low) were included as within-subjects factors. To initially screen for overall drug effects, preliminary analyses were performed on reward-based behavioral indices, including accuracy and total keypress, and escape efficiency using a Mixed-design Analysis of Variance (ANOVA). Subsequently, the total number of keypresses was analyzed via a one-way ANOVA with Group as the sole factor, with Tukey's HSD post-hoc tests employed for specific pairwise comparisons. The primary outcome variable, Escape Efficiency, was initially subjected to a three-way mixed ANOVA. A follow-up one-way ANOVA on the main effect of Groupon overall efficiency was conducted, followed by Tukey's HSD post-hoc analysis to determine significant group differences. 

Results

Preliminary analyses indicated that reward-based behavioral indices (accuracy and keypress) and efficiency metrics showed no significant main effect of drug or interactions with the within-subject factors (all Ps > 0.05). One-way ANOVA showed no significant group differences in VAS-measured arousal score (F(2, 50)=0.26, P=0.77). Consequently, the results reported herein are restricted to the threat trials.

A one-way ANOVA on the total number of keypresses showed a main effect of Group trending toward significance (F(2, 57) = 2.838, P = 0.067). The mean value of keypress times showed that the mean differences between 100mg and 200mg and between 100mg and placebo were 5 and 4.08, respectively. It indicates that the 100mg group exhibited the highest mean number of keypresses. Post-Hoc Analysis showed that the difference between the 100mg group and the 200mg group approached significance (P = 0.073), suggesting a hyperactive response strategy under the 100mg dose (Figure 2a). No significant differences were found between the other groups (P ≥ 0.17).

The primary dependent variable, Escape Efficiency (ln(accuracy/keypress), was subjected to a three-way mixed ANOVA with Group(100mg, 200mg, placebo) as a between-subjects factor and Velocity (fast, slow) and Intensity (high, low) as within-subjects factors. A significant three-way interaction (F(2, 57) = 3.39, P = 0.04) and a main effect of Group(F(2, 57) = 3.48, P = 0.04) were observed on the escape efficiency. The post-hoc one-way ANOVA on Group found that the lowest efficiency was observed in the 100mg group, which was significantly lower compared to the 200mg group (P = 0.05) and the Placebo group (P = 0.06). Efficiency was comparable between the 200mg and Placebo groups (P = 0.99). Given that velocity inherently dictates the temporal window available for behavioral response, separate 2 (Intensity) x 3 (Group) repeated measures ANOVAs were conducted after partitioning the trials based on velocity (slow vs. fast trials), with Intensity serving as the within-subjects factor. This approach was employed to isolate the dose-specific effects of caffeine under varying degrees of temporal pressure. Analysis of the slow trials revealed a main effect of Group(F(2,57)=3.13, P=0.051). Furthermore, an Intensity x Group interaction was observed (F (2, 57)=2.99, P=0.058) (Figure 2b). A similar Group main effect (F(2,57)=2.83, P=0.07) was found for the Fast trials while the Intensity x Group interaction (F(2,57)=1.230, P=0.3) did not reach statistical significance (Figure 2b).

Within the 100mg caffeine group (Figure 2c), Pearson correlation revealed a negative association between overall escape efficiency and the averaged subjective arousal state (VAS score) (r =-0.52, P= 0.02). This association was not found within the other two groups (Ps>0.005; Figure 2d).

Conclusion

This study utilized a high-stakes, dynamically modulated approach/avoidance task to investigate the dose-specific effects of caffeine (placebo, 100mg, 200mg) on human evasive behavior. Our findings reveal a non-linear, dose-dependent relationship between caffeine intake and performance metrics, providing novel insight into how noradrenergic modulation biases flexible reinforcement processing.

Impaired Efficiency at Low Dose: The 100mg caffeine dose significantly impaired overall Escape Efficiency, whereas the 200mg dose did not differ from placebo.

Hyperactivated Strategy Bias: This inefficiency was accompanied by a higher frequency of motor responses (total keypress), suggesting that low-dose caffeine induces a bias toward high motor output, but less effective actions. 

While caffeine is widely believed to improve task performance by increasing alertness or arousal, we showed that a low dose of caffeine induces a specific response pattern that was detrimental to performance during dynamic, transient interactions with target objects. With an association with the subject arousal level, the observed deficit is consistent with the pharmacological action of caffeine in increasing NE release. While the neural mechanisms need to be confirmed by future brain imaging investigations, our findings suggest reconsidering caffeine as a cognitive booster, particularly in high-stakes environments.
