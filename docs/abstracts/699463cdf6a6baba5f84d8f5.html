Introduction

The third trimester of gestation is marked by rapid neural growth, during which large-scale brain networks begin to emerge. Advances in neonatal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) have enabled noninvasive investigation of early structural and functional connectivity, revealing that resting state networks (RSNs) are already present before or at birth.1 These networks show continuity with adult functional architecture while also exhibit developmental features such as spatial diffuseness and limited long-range integration.

Despite these advances, accurately characterizing neonatal functional organization remains challenging. The immature microstructure of the newborn brain - characterized by high water content, low anisotropy and incomplete myelination2 - limits the interpretability of conventional resting-state approaches, which often capture diffuse functional activity without explicitly accounting for the underlying white matter architecture.

The Developing Human Connectome Project (dHCP) provides an unprecedented opportunity to address these challenges by offering a large, high-quality multimodal MRI dataset acquired with neonatal-optimized protocols.3 Within this context, track-weighted dynamic functional connectivity (TW-dFC)4 is particularly well suited to the neonatal brain, as it integrates tractography-derived white matter pathways with dynamic resting state fMRI (rs-fMRI) to constrain functional interactions to anatomically plausible connections.

Here, we combine multimodal MRI from the dHCP with TW-dFC and independent component analysis (ICA) to characterize white matter neonatal resting state networks and their development across late gestational ages (GA). Functionally corresponding RSNs are derived across the cohort and examined spatially and temporally to test associations with developmental variables.

Methods

This study used data from the third public release of the Developing Human Connectome Project.3 From 783 subjects, we included infants with complete, high-quality T2-weighted, rs-fMRI, and diffusion MRI (dMRI) data that passed dHCP QC. Infants with major brain lesions, serious preprocessing issues, or excessive motion (>10% of volumes above a framewise-displacement threshold defined as 75th percentile + 1.5 × IQR) were excluded. The final cohort included 509 newborns (mean post-menstrual age 39.8 ± 3.5 weeks).

We used outputs from the dHCP minimally preprocessed structural, functional, and diffusion pipelines which incorporate neonatal-specific corrections. T2-weighted images served as the structural reference, with standardized tissue segmentation, and GA specific templates. rs-fMRI preprocessing included distortion and motion correction and ICA-FIX denoising. dMRI preprocessing included motion, eddy-current and susceptibility corrections.

Track-weighted dynamic functional connectivity (TW-dFC) was computed by integrating tractography with sliding-window rs-fMRI.4 The neonatal-adapted pipeline included segmentation, multimodal normalization, additional fMRI preprocessing, whole brain tractography, and TW-dFC generation.5 Structural segmentations provided masks for nuisance regression and anatomically constrained tractography (ACT). To ensure robust multimodal alignment, all images were registered to diffusion space and then to GA templates using a single-warp strategy that preserved diffusion orientations. Additional rs-fMRI preprocessing included 3mm spatial smoothing and voxel-wise regression of white-matter and CSF signals. Whole-brain probabilistic tractography was performed using multi-shell multi-tissue Constrained Spherical Deconvolution (MSMT-CSD). Ten million streamlines were generated with ACT and filtered with Spherical-Deconvolution Informed Filtering of Tractograms (SIFT) to a biologically informed set of one million streamlines, then normalized to template space. TW-dFC was computed by averaging dynamic functional connectivity across streamlines traversing each white matter voxel with a 40s sliding windows with 0.98 overlap, to capture temporal variation while maintaining resolution.

Independent component analysis (ICA) of TW-dFC was conducted to derive white matter resting state networks (RSNs) (Figure 1).5 A term-born reference cohort (GA-41; n = 90) was used to estimate 10 group-level components with Group ICA of fMRI Toolbox (GIFT), using two-stage PCA followed by Infomax ICA. ICA was repeated 20 times in ICASSO to assess stability and select reliable components. Subject-level spatial maps and time series were reconstructed for all infants using Group-Information Guided ICA (GIG-ICA), ensuring component independence while maintaining correspondence to group ICs.

Statistical analyses were conducted in MATLAB R2024a. In GA-41, group-level z-maps were obtained via one-sample t-tests, visually inspected and manually labeled using established gray matter RSNs. Pairwise Pearson correlations between component time series generated functional network connectivity (FNC) matrices. Network organization was assessed using Ward’s hierarchical clustering, silhouette scores and graph-theoretical metrics were computed with the Brain Connectivity Toolbox (BCT). At subject’s level, back-reconstructed maps and time series ensured functional correspondence across the cohort. Spatial correspondence to reference ICs was quantified using voxel-wise correlations, and mean activation within each IC mask was extracted. Associations with developmental variables were tested using general linear models (GLM; age, birth weight, sex and head circumference), with FDR and Bonferroni corrections. Subject-specific FNC matrices and BCT metrics were analyzed using the same GLM framework.

Results

Group level analysis of term-born neonates (GA-41; n = 90) identified 10 reproducible white matter RSNs (Figure 2a). All components reflected neuronal signal yet showed spatially diffuse patterns needing high z thresholds for peak isolation. Based on spatial distribution and functional relevance, the components were labeled as: (1) Visual bilateral, (2) Default Mode Network (DMN), (3) Auditory left, (4) Sensorimotor medial, (5) Frontal, (6) Auditory right, (7) Sensorimotor/vestibular, (8) Sensorimotor right, (9) Sensorimotor left and (10) Sensorimotor lateral. 

Functional network connectivity analysis revealed structured interactions among RSNs (Figure 2a). Clustering of FNC matrix demonstrated early modular structure, with strong bilateral coupling among homologous sensorimotor and auditory networks. Sensorimotor components grouped into a dominant module, auditory networks clustered with visual and frontal systems, while the default-mode network showed weak connectivity and segregation. These results converged on an optimal three-module organization with limited cross-system integration.

Graph theory analysis revealed a hub-centric organization dominated by sensorimotor networks, which showed the highest strength (3.35–4.34), local efficiency (0.31–0.41), clustering (0.34–0.46) and centrality (0.30–0.42), reflecting strong within-system integration. In contrast, the DMN exhibited the lowest strength (1.44), efficiency (0.14) and centrality (0.05), consistent with weak integration. At the global level, network topology showed moderate efficiency (0.30) and clustering (0.33), modest modularity (0.26) and small-world organization (1.41), with a relatively long characteristic path length (18.83). 

Across the full cohort (n = 509), subject-specific RSNs back-reconstructed from the GA-41 reference showed strong spatial correspondence across gestational ages, with voxel-wise correlations exceeding 0.5 for most networks (Figure 2b). Spatial similarity increased steeply from very preterm ages, stabilized around term-equivalent age and decayed after term, indicating well conserved RSN topology across neonatal term development. Mean RSN activation decreased significantly with increasing scan age across all networks (Bonferroni corrected p < .01), consistent with progressive spatial refinement. Birth weight showed an independent positive effect on activation in several sensorimotor networks and auditory left, whereas sex and head circumference showed no consistent associations.

Subject-specific FNC matrices obtained by back-reconstructed time series were examined for development effect using graph theory (Figure 2c). Interesting local metrics were strength, local efficiency and eigenvector centrality. The first two showed significant age-related decreases across nearly all RSNs, indicating reduced inter-network coupling and network integration with maturation. Birth weight exerted an independent positive effect on strength in most sensorimotor networks, suggesting selective reinforcement of sensorimotor connectivity by physical growth. Eigenvector centrality showed divergent age-related effects, increasing in sensorimotor networks and decreasing in associative systems, consistent with a redistribution of network influence across development. At the global level, GLM results revealed significant age-related decreases in global efficiency and density, accompanied by increased modularity, promoting segregation of networks. Birth weight, despite correlation with age, showed the opposite trend, promoting network connectivity. Sex and head circumference had no significant effects.

Conclusion

Using multimodal data from the Developing Human Connectome Project, we investigated large-scale functional network development in the neonatal brain across term-born and term-equivalent subjects. By integrating diffusion tractography and resting-state fMRI through track-weighted dynamic functional connectivity, we derived resting state networks using a robust ICA-based framework. These white matter RSNs constrain functional interactions to anatomically supported white matter pathways and show clear functional correspondence with canonical gray matter RSNs.5

We show that reproducible and functionally meaningful RSNs are already present around term-equivalent age, with clear correspondence to canonical adult functional systems. Compared with adult networks, neonatal RSNs display spatially diffuse patterns, a feature consistently reported in neonatal neuroimaging¹ and thought to reflect developmental processes such as synaptic overproduction, incomplete pruning, and immature myelination.²

Our findings show that neonatal networks are already modularly organized, with a strong predominance of sensorimotor networks supported by early maturing white matter tracts. In contrast, associative networks, although detectable at birth, show weaker connectivity and limited integration. This pattern is consistent with previous studies¹ demonstrating earlier maturation of sensorimotor circuits relative to higher-order associative systems supported by the earlier development of thalamocortical projections and corticospinal pathways compared with long-range associative tracts.²

Across the neonatal period, network organization shifted from diffuse, overlapping connectivity toward more focal and segregated interactions. RSN activation, network strength, and local efficiency declined with age, consistent with pruning of redundant connections and increasing selectivity. At the global level, reduced density and efficiency alongside increased modularity further indicate a transition from widespread synchrony to compartmentalized functional modules, reflecting consolidation of local circuits at the expense of global integration.

Maturation effects differed across functional systems. Eigenvector centrality increased in sensorimotor networks but decreased in associative systems, indicating a redistribution of network influence consistent with hierarchical white matter maturation. Birth weight exerted independent effects on network strength and spatial activation, predominantly within sensorimotor networks, indicating that somatic growth selectively reinforces early-maturing functional circuits. This dissociation suggests that growth enhances network robustness rather than accelerating overall refinement, likely reflecting differences in underlying white matter integrity.²

Methodologically, this work highlights the value of TW-dFC for studying the neonatal brain, as it constrains dynamic functional interactions to anatomically plausible white matter pathways, mitigating the limitations of conventional resting state approaches in the context of low anisotropy and incomplete myelination. By explicitly linking functional dynamics to developing structural connectivity, this framework enables a more biologically grounded characterization of early functional network organization and maturation and provides a robust reference for future longitudinal and clinical studies of typical and atypical brain development.
