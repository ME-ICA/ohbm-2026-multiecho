Introduction

Ultra-low-field (ULF) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) enables low-cost, portable neuroimaging in low- and middle-income settings, but reduced signal-to-noise ratio and limited resolution, relative to high-field systems, create a strong demand for super-resolution techniques1. Existing image enhancement methods either reconstruct higher-resolution images from three anisotropic ultra-low-field scans acquired in orthogonal planes (axial, coronal, sagittal)2 or train deep learning models on paired ultra-low- and high-field data. In practice, especially in paediatric population, acquiring three high-quality ultra-low-field scans is challenging, and suitable paired high-field data may not be available. Here, we examine the potential of a deep learning approach, trained without high-field scans, designed to produce higher-resolution output images using only a single ULF scan.

Methods

The MRI data were obtained as part of the Khula Study3, a longitudinal birth cohort in South Africa and Malawi. Six-month-old infants were scanned in Cape Town using a 64 mT Hyperfine Swoop ULF system. Only high-quality T2-weighted ULF scans (1.5×1.5×5 mm) with all three orthogonal anisotropic orientations - axial (AXI), coronal (COR) and sagittal (SAG) - were included, yielding 396 scans from 132 participants after exclusion. 

Pre-processing involved rigid registration of ULF scans to an age-matched template, followed by multi-resolution registration (MRR) to generate a higher-resolution (1.5×1.5×1.5 mm) scan2, which would later serve as a ground truth target. The resulting dataset consisted of 132 pairs of three anisotropic ULF scans (AXI, COR, SAG) and one MRR scan per subject. Data were split into training/validation/test sets of 86/20/26 subjects (65/15/20 %) (Figure 1A). 

To identify the best deep-learning approach for ULF super-resolution, we compared three architectures for mapping randomly sampled single ULF inputs to corresponding MRR targets: a 3D UNet, Attention UNet, and the SwinUNETR-v2 transformer. Models were trained on 86 and validated on 20 subjects for 300 epochs using 1283 patches with extensive 3D augmentation (affine, elastic, flips, bias-field). Architecture selection used L1 loss and four evaluation metrics (PSNR, SSIM, MAE, NRMSE). The best model was further optimised by testing additional losses (L2, L1+perceptual, L2+perceptual) and by comparing training on a single orientation (AXI) versus randomly sampled mixed orientations (AXI/COR/SAG). Rank aggregation across all metrics determined the optimal configuration. The best model was retrained on the combined 106-subject training + validation set (Figure 1A). Final weights were evaluated on the 26-subject held-out test set by generating super-resolved predictions for each orientation and comparing them to both the original ULF scans (to quantify enhancement) and the MRR ground truth (to assess model reconstruction accuracy).

Image quality metrics (PSNR, SSIM, MAE, NRMSE) were computed, and all scans were segmented using SynthSeg+4. Segmentation labels were aggregated into 4 global tissue types, namely, white matter (WM), cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), cortical grey matter (cortical GM) and subcortical grey matter (subcortical GM). In addition to the 4 global tissue types, we also obtained labels for 8 bilateral subcortical regions: thalamus, amygdala, accumbens, hippocampus, putamen, pallidum, caudate and ventral diencephalon. Performance was further evaluated using Dice overlap of tissue labels and correlations of tissue volumes across subjects (Pearson’s r, Lin’s CCC), both on average across mixed orientations (AXI, COR, SAG) and for single orientations (Figure 1B).

Results

The best performing model according to rank aggregation across all metrics was SwinUNETR-v2 with L2 loss supplemented by perceptual loss trained on a mixed input of AXI, COR and SAG ULF scans. The outputs of the model are visually superior and show increased sharpness and detail compared to the original ULF scans (Figure 2A). These visual differences were also reflected in higher PSNR and SSIM and lower MAE and NRMSE values for model outputs compared to ULF input scans (Figure 2A).  

After performing visual quality control of segmentations, we excluded 5 participants with failed segmentations from further analyses. We quantified improvement in tissue segmentation from super-resolution by comparing the Dice overlap of model predictions and MRR scans to the Dice overlap of ULF and MRR scans. For all 4 main tissue types, the mean Dice score increased significantly (p < 0.001): from 0.77 to 0.78 for WM, 0.50 to 0.56 for CSF, 0.72 to 0.74 for cortical GM and 0.79 to 0.82 for subcortical GM (Figure 2B). Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons was applied (i.e. p < 0.05/4 = 0.0125). Within individual orientations, the most noticeable improvement in model outputs was observed for COR and SAG scans, with significant increases in Dice overlap across all tissue types. We performed the same analyses on 8 bilateral subcortical brain regions and here too Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons was used (i.e. p < 0.05/8 = 0.00625). Considering all orientations together, all eight regions showed a significant increase in Dice overlap (Figure 2B). When examining individual orientations, the most noticeable improvement in Dice overlap following super-resolution was observed for SAG scans, with significant increases in Dice overlap across all subcortical regions. 

We further inspected correlations of tissue volume across participants, quantifying improvements in image quality following super-resolution by comparing correlations between model outputs and MRR targets, to correlations between ULF inputs and MRR. Within the 4 global tissue types, across all scan orientations combined, we observed the greatest increases in correlation in subcortical GM, including both linear correlation (Pearson’s r rose from 0.66 to 0.87) and exact agreement (Lin’s CCC rose from 0.47 to 0.71) (Figure 2B). The remaining 3 tissue types already showed high linear correlation and agreement at baseline, and therefore also smaller differences in both measures following super-resolution. When examining correlations within each orientation separately, the same trend was observed for all three orientations, with SAG scans showing the greatest increase in volume correlations of subcortical GM. Across the three orientations combined, improvements were observed for all 8 subcortical regions, with the greatest improvements in the amygdala, accumbens and ventral diencephalon (Pearson’s r rose from 0.44 to 0.71, from 0.55 to 0.86 and from 0.55 to 0.84, respectively) (Figure 2B). Looking at individual orientations, all showed improvement in linear correlation for all subcortical regions, with SAG scans showing the greatest increase of volume correlations in thalamus, amygdala, accumbens and hippocampus, COR scans in pallidum and caudate nucleus, and AXI scans in putamen and ventral diencephalon.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates the efficacy of a deep learning SwinUNETR-v2 model in enhancing the resolution of ULF infant MRI scans, significantly improving tissue volume estimation and segmentation accuracy compared to original scans. Future research could investigate other deep learning architectures for super-resolution of a single ULF MRI scan without paired HF data, such as diffusion and state-space models5. By enabling the reconstruction of higher resolution brain scans from a single ULF scan without paired high-field data, the model not only reduces the scanning time, critical for paediatric imaging, but also paves the way for global adoption of MRI technology in low- and middle-income countries1, potentially advancing neurodevelopmental research in these regions.
