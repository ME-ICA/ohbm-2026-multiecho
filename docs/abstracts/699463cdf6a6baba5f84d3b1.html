Introduction

Stroke leads to highly heterogeneous deficits across motor and cognitive domains, with fewer than 15% of patients achieving complete motor recovery.1 Cognitive impairments, which are often overshadowed by motor deficits in clinical practice, have a substantial impact on how well patients respond to motor rehabilitation and resume everyday activities. Early identification of individualized recovery trajectories across both motor and cognitive functions and their underlying neural mechanisms is therefore essential. Multimodal assessments integrating anatomical and functional MRI, diffusion imaging and EEG provide complementary insights into network reorganization, a critical feature in stroke where lesion heterogeneity adds considerable complexity. When combined with comprehensive behavioral evaluations, longitudinal multimodal profiling may enable discovery of patient phenotypes and predictors of behavioral outcomes.
 Despite this potential, no prior stroke study has incorporated all such quantitative measures across multiple timepoints during the first year of recovery. The present study introduces the first application of similarity network fusion (SNF) in stroke to integrate acute multimodal information and stratify patients based on brain-derived patterns. 

Building on prior applications in neurodevelopmental samples,2 this approach may help to identify multimodal phenotypes that relate to long-term motor and cognitive trajectories, thereby elucidating the neural mechanisms underlying inter-individual variability.
For this purpose, the study leverages the TiMeS project (Towards individualized Medicine in Stroke),3 a large-scale, longitudinal multimodal initiative designed to comprehensively characterize stroke phenotypes using advanced neuroimaging, electrophysiology and extensive behavioral testing across the first year after a stroke.

Methods

44 stroke patients recruited for the TiMeS project with upper-limb motor deficits and heterogeneous lesion locations were included. They were assessed at 4 timepoints: acute (T1, within one week), early subacute (T2, 3 weeks), late subacute (T3, 3 months), and chronic (T4, 12 months). 

Details about data collection and inclusion criteria can be found in (Fleury L. et al.).3 Four complementary modalities were extracted after standard preprocessing: structural connectomes from COMMIT-weighted diffusion tractography4 using a Glasser-based parcellation extended to subcortical, cerebellar, and brainstem regions (377 parcels); resting-state fMRI functional connectivity using the same parcellation; lesion topography quantifying parcel-level lesion involvement; and source-level time–frequency connectivity derived from resting-state EEG (62 sources from Desikan–Killiany–Tourville atlas). Right-hemisphere lesions were mirrored to the left to ensure hemispheric correspondence and avoid trivial clustering by lesion side.
 Longitudinal behavioral outcomes were derived via non-negative matrix factorization from raw test scores, yielding normalized (0–1) domain scores at T1–T4 for motor, attention, executive, language, and neglect functions (0 = best performance, 1 = worst).

SNF was applied to integrate the 4 modalities into a single fused network using 20 iterations of nonlinear message passing (figure 1). For each modality, subject-by-subject cosine distance matrices were converted into affinity networks using K-nearest neighbors’ graphs with varying neighborhood sizes and edge-scaling parameters.  At each iteration, matrices were sparsified by retaining only nearest neighbors, then fused with information from other modalities. A final fusion averaged, normalized and symmetrized all unimodal matrices, yielding a unified similarity network.5

A full grid search tested combinations of neighbors ([2:13]), scaling factor ([0.3:0.8]), and cluster number ([2:7]) using spectral clustering. Cluster quality was assessed by maximizing the eigengap, which reflects well-separated clusters, and minimizing the rotation cost, a discretization-based index measuring misalignment with hard cluster assignments. Rank fusion combined both metrics to select optimal parameter combination.

Cluster-assignment stability was evaluated by repeatedly subsampling 80% of participants twice, computing agreement between clustering solutions and comparing this distribution (1000 permutations) to a null model. A jackknife approach further assessed robustness by computing Normalized Mutual Information (NMI) distributions after removing one participant at a time2 (100 permutations).

Moreover, unimodal clustering was conducted separately for each modality. Resulting cluster assignments were compared with the multimodal solution using NMI to quantify overlap. Additionally, we counted how often the multimodal labels outperformed unimodal labels in terms of standard quality metrics (Silhouette score, Davies–Bouldin, Calinski–Harabasz indices) when used to cluster each individual modality. 

To evaluate whether acute multimodal cluster membership predicted long-term behavioral outcomes, ClusterID was entered as a key predictor alongside demographic and lesion-related covariates (age, education, lesion volume, lesion side). Domain-specific trajectories were modeled using beta generalized linear mixed-effects models (GLMEM) with a logit link, including a random intercept for each subject to account for repeated measures (see formula in figure 2e). Model assumptions were evaluated using DHARMa simulations, including checks for uniformity, dispersion and residual structure. Continuous covariates were standardized, categorical variables were factorized and outliers beyond the 10th–90th percentiles were removed when necessary to meet model assumptions. Model fit for each behavioral domain was assessed using AIC, BIC, and marginal/conditional R².

Results

SNF revealed an optimal 6-cluster solution, with the best-performing parameters identified as 4 neighbors and a scaling factor of 0.80, producing clusters of relatively balanced sizes (see figure 2c). Cluster validity was high: stability analyses showed that empirical distributions differed from the null model, as confirmed by a Wilcoxon rank-sum test (p < 10⁻³⁰; figure 2a). The multimodal solution achieved more compact and better-separated clusters than unimodal approaches (higher DB, CH), while lower silhouette scores reflected the expected complexity of integrating cross-modal information. NMI comparisons demonstrated minimal overlap between unimodal and multimodal clusters (figure 2b), underscoring that the SNF approach captured complementary information not recoverable from any single modality alone.

Clusters displayed distinct multimodal signatures (figure 2c). Functional connectivity differences involved occipital and frontal network synchrony, cerebellar and brainstem integration, and global connectivity (e.g., including hyperconnectivity in C3 and C6), with no major hemispheric asymmetries. Structural connectivity differences were more subtle, with limited interhemispheric or subcortical–cortical variations. EEG connectivity showed marked hemispheric asymmetries, high global activity in C3, increased lesioned-hemisphere activity in C6, and reduced connectivity in C4. Lesion profiles ranged from extensive insular and frontal lesions (C1) to focal lesions (C2), medial prefrontal/temporal (C3), inferior parietal (C4), posterior opercular/auditory (C5) and basal ganglia–dorsal stream–parietal involvement (C6).

Longitudinal mixed-effects analyses showed that acute cluster membership was significantly involved in behavioral prediction, especially at later stages (figure 2d–e). Motor performance varied by cluster and time, with C3 showing early impairment followed by improvement, while other clusters remained stable; education also influenced baseline performance.
Language outcomes were associated with lesion size, lesion side, time and a C3-specific deviation at T3. These patterns suggest that C3’s early hyperconnectivity at T1 may reflect compensatory reorganization that supports delayed but robust motor and linguistic improvement. Attention performance was influenced by age, education, lesion size and time, with C5 showing a distinct decline at T4. This late decline suggests that C5’s acute brain profile may reflect vulnerability in attentional control networks during the chronic stage.
 Executive outcomes were shaped by age, lesion size, timepoints, and cluster-specific deviations of C2, C5 and C6 at T4. These deviations imply that acute connectivity patterns captured by SNF on these clusters have lasting effects on executive function. Neglect outcomes were significantly influenced by lesion size and by membership in C6. The association between C6 and neglect aligns with its dorsal stream and inferior parietal lesion profile, confirming the neurobiological interpretability of the SNF clusters.

Across most behavioral domains, clusters identified at T1 remained associated with outcomes at T3 and T4, suggesting their potential prognostic relevance. Predictive accuracy based on multimodal clustering (computed using leave-one-out RMSE) was comparable across all domains and slightly exceeded the unimodal one for the attention domain. In general, ClusterID was more frequently significant in multimodal models compared with unimodal models particularly at later timepoints. For example, motor outcomes were influenced by T2*C2 only for lesion load; language outcomes were not predicted by ClusterID in unimodal models; attention outcomes were influenced by T3*C2 for structural connectivity, T4*C2 for lesion load and T4*C2 for EEG connectivity; executive outcomes showed effects only at T4*C5 for functional connectivity and T4*C2 for lesion load; neglect outcomes were affected only by T2*C3 for structural connectivity.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the feasibility and added value of integrating acute multimodal neuroimaging and electrophysiology using SNF to characterize inter-individual variability in stroke. By combining functional MRI, diffusion-based connectomics, lesion topography, and EEG connectivity, we extracted patient phenotypes not recoverable from single modalities. Multimodal clustering outperformed unimodal solutions in the prediction of later recovery stages, emphasizing the complementary nature of cross-modal information.
 The showed prognostic relevance of acute cluster membership supports the utility of early multimodal profiling for anticipating motor and cognitive trajectories. 

Limitations include the modest sample size, which may influence robustness metrics and generalizability of the identified cluster partition. Nevertheless, the multimodal phenotypes uncovered here suggest shared and distinct patterns of network dysfunction and point toward individualized, network-based therapeutic strategies.
