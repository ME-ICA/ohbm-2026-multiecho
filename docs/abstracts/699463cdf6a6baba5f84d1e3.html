Introduction

Congenital heart disease (CHD) is the most common congenital abnormality and confers a high risk of neurodevelopmental impairments at school age, including motor coordination difficulties1. MRI at clinical field strengths [1.5 and 3 Tesla (T)] has identified altered structural brain development in fetuses and neonates with CHD including altered white matter microstructure alongside reduced total and regional tissue volumes1. However, structural brain features alone cannot accurately predict neurodevelopmental outcomes in individual infants with CHD.

Functional brain development in neonates with CHD is yet to be fully characterised, however there is some evidence that resting-state functional network topology is altered in fetuses and newborns with CHD. It is possible these changes relate to reductions of GABAergic interneurons in superficial cortical layers of somatosensory, frontal and insular cortices identified in a piglet model of CHD2. The increased signal-to-noise ratio offered by ultra-high-field (UHF) MRI at 7T permits functional MRI acquisitions at submillimetre voxel resolutions. This improves functional brain network characterisation allowing study of specific patterns of activity across cortical depths3. Here we present a pilot UHF resting-state functional MRI study in neonates with CHD. We aimed to explore cortical depth-related functional connectivity within and between left and right primary somatosensory (S1) and motor (M1) cortices in neonates with CHD and heart-healthy neonates.

Methods

Recruitment


19 neonates (13 with CHD requiring surgery or cardiac catheterisation within one year, 6 controls born ≥37.00 gestational weeks) (36.43-45.43 postmenstrual weeks at MRI, 35.29-40.71 gestational weeks at birth, 7 female) were recruited at St. Thomas Hospital and the Evelina London Children’s Hospital. UK NHS ethics committee approval (19/LO/1384) and written informed parental consent were acquired prior to data collection.  

Infants with CHD who had suspected or confirmed genetic diagnoses or previously underwent non-cardiac surgery were excluded.

Data acquisition


Whole brain resting state (rs) fMRI data were acquired on a Siemens 7T system at the London Collaborative Ultra high field System facility (LoCUS) using locally imposed safety restrictions for neonatal UHF MRI4. rs-fMRI was acquired with a 1Tx32RX Nova head coil using two sequences following an upgrade from a Terra to a Terra.X system (both acquisitions: 160 volumes, 0.9mm isotropic voxels, TE=44ms, FOV=125mm, GRAPPA 2, multiband acceleration factor 2): Terra, TR=4210ms; Terra.X, TR=3950ms. Imaging was performed during natural sleep without sedation. Hearing protection was provided using moulded dental putty (President Putty) and inflatable cushions (Pearltec) placed over the ears. Physiological signs were monitored by a nurse and paediatrician trained in UHF neonatal MRI procedures.

MRI Preprocessing


Preprocessing steps were built on previous work on neonatal UHF4, published adult UHF fMRI studies, and established neonatal rs-fMRI pipelines at clinical field strengths. Preprocessing is described in Fig. 1A.

Data were corrected for differences in acquisition time between slices and then brain extracted. Thermal denoising using principal component analysis was applied. Volumes were realigned via Fourier interpolation, using the 80th volume as the reference frame. If this volume showed evidence of motion artifact, a middle volume from a motion-free epoch was selected. Head-motion was removed from data using 24-parameter regression.  

A high pass filter (>0.02Hz) was applied prior to physiological denoising using independent component analysis with FSL MELODIC, with the number of components determined using MELODICs Bayesian dimensionality estimation. Components were visually inspected and those deemed to be non-signal were regressed from the data. Data were resampled to 0.18x0.18mm in the axial plane to improve manual delineation of region of interests (ROIs) in the left and right hand area of M1 and corresponding cortex in S1. ROIs underwent ”layerification” to produce three depth bins per region (deep bin at white matter boundary, mid bin, superficial bin at pial surface) using LayNii3 (Fig. 1B). Varianced-normalised timeseries for deep, mid and superficial depth bins were extracted (Fig. 1C).

Quality Control Metrics


Established functional [framewise displacement (FD), absolute displacement (AD), visual inspection of raw data, regional time series, whole-brain temporal SNR (tSNR)] and laminar-specific (depth bin tSNR and time series) metrics were calculated to assess data quality.

Censoring


Both FD and AD were used to censor volumes exceeding displacement greater than the native voxel size (0.9mm). A censoring threshold at which a dataset is excluded from further analysis of >40% volumes exceeding FD/AD of 0.9mm was applied.

Data Analysis


Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between depth bin time series both within and across left and right M1 and S1. Left deep-right mid and right deep-left mid correlations were averaged. Corresponding mid-superficial and deep-superficial correlations were also averaged. Mann-Whitney U tests were used to (i) investigate depth bin correlation and tSNR differences between infants scanned on the Terra and Terra.X and (ii) test whether depth bin correlations significantly differed between neonates with CHD and controls. As this was an exploratory pilot study p-values were not corrected for multiple comparisons.

Results

Three (1 Terra, 2 Terra.X; 2 CHD, 1 control) datasets were removed due to imaging artifacts on visual inspection of raw data. Three Terra datasets, all from neonates with CHD, were excluded based on a censoring threshold of >40%.  5 Terra and 8 Terra.X datasets (8 CHD, 5 controls) were included in the analysis. A median of 2 volumes per dataset (range 0-24) exceeded FD thresholds (median FD=0.028mm, range 0.015-0.118mm), and 5 volumes (range 0-53) exceeded AD thresholds (median AD=0.113mm, range 0.057-0.675mm).  

tSNR was lower in deep bins [Terra median=119, (range 81-266); Terra.X median=93 (range 77-167)] compared to superficial (p=0.004, Terra median=210, range 164-398; Terra.X; median=169, range 102-234) and mid depth bins [p=0.014, Terra median=169 (range 141-396); Terra.X median=146 (range 97-206)]. tSNR was not different between superficial and mid bins (p=0.151).

There were no significant differences in depth bin tSNR between Terra and Terra.X datasets (deep p=0.222, mid p=0.093, superficial p=0.171). Median whole brain tSNR was also not statistically different between systems [p=0.171, Terra=140 (range 87-205), Terra.X=100 (range 65-133)].

Comparison of cortical connectivity between Terra and Terra.X

In left M1, intraregional correlations were significantly higher post-upgrade to Terra.X  (deep-mid Terra 0.842, Terra.X 0.980, p=0.045; deep-superficial Terra 0.853, Terra.X 0.960, p=0.006; mid-superficial Terra 0.930, Terra.X 0.980, p=0.030). In right S1, there was a trend towards lower deep-mid (Terra 0.954, Terra.X 0.923, p=0.066) and deep-superficial correlations (Terra 0.920, Terra.X 0.700, p=0.093) on the Terra.X. No other intraregional or interregional correlations were significantly different between systems.

Comparison of cortical connectivity between CHD and Controls

Within left M1, deep-mid (p=0.030) and mid-superficial (p=0.006) correlations significantly higher in infants with CHD compared to controls (Fig. 2). There were no other significant intraregional connectivity differences between CHD and controls.

Higher left M1-right M1 correlations were seen in CHD at the following depth bins: deep-deep (p=0.030), deep-superficial (p=0.045) and mid-mid (p=0.045) (Fig. 2). There was a trend towards higher correlations between deep-mid bins (p=0.065). There were no differences in left S1-right S1 or ipsilateral M1-S1 connectivity between groups.

Conclusion

We report correlations between cortical depths within left M1 and between homologous motor cortices were higher in CHD compared to controls, particularly at deep cortical depths. We also report intraregional correlations in left M1 were higher in data acquired on the Terra.X compared to the Terra, with a trend towards lower correlations in right S1, however tSNR across the brain and within depth bins was not different between systems.

The deeper layers of the motor cortex are excitatory, projecting to the striatum, brainstem and spine to generate and control movements. These corticospinal projections are present at birth. The superficial layers, which contain high numbers of GABAergic interneurons develop pre and postnatally, shaping inhibitory control and cortico-cortico integration. The increased deep–depth bin correlations in M1 observed in CHD may therefore reflect altered maturation and reductions in superficial inhibitory circuitry, leading to greater connectivity in deep-layer output networks. This interpretation is supported by pre-clinical work in a piglet model of CHD reporting reduced GABAergic interneurons in cortical layers 2/32.

Altered intra-region and interhemispheric connectivity involving deep layers of M1 may represent early alterations of motor control networks in CHD. Prior work at clinical field strengths has identified changes in motor control circuitry in neonates with CHD including altered functional connectivity between M1 and the supplementary motor area and pallidum, and decreased fractional anisotropy (FA) in the corticospinal tract (CST) and corpus callosum. Our work adds further specificity to the literature suggesting motor control circuitry is altered in CHD by identifying functional connectivity alterations in M1 cortex adjacent to the white matter boundary.

Infants with CHD are at increased risk of developmental coordination disorder (DCD), a condition characterised by poor fine and gross motor coordination. The neuroimaging correlates of coordination impairments in CHD are not known. School children with DCD without CHD show decreased interhemispheric sensorimotor functional connectivity during motor synchronisation5. It is possible our findings of altered deep M1-M1 correlations are an early correlate of coordination impairments in CHD, however further research is needed.

This work has several limitations which are important to acknowledge. This study included data acquired on both Terra and Terra.X 7T systems due to a system upgrade in the middle of recruitment. We identified significant differences in depth-related connectivity between systems, and due to the sample size it was not possible to adjust for this in the analysis. We were also unable to adjust for gestational age at birth or at scan, which could influence connectivity.

Conclusions

This pilot study identifies alterations in resting-state connectivity in the primary motor cortex in neonates with CHD. These changes were localised to deep areas of motor cortex bordering the white matter.  UHF field laminar resting-state fMRI offers novel opportunities to link cellular alterations in animal models to in-vivo human research to better characterise functional alterations in CHD.
