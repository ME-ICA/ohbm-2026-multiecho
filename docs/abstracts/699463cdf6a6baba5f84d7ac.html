Introduction

Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) and MCI in Parkinson’s disease (PD-MCI) are clinical conditions marked by progressive cognitive and functional decline, affecting everyday life aspects, among which there are numerical reasoning, financial decision-making and personal financial autonomy, but can also disrupt large-scale brain networks. Resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) provides a powerful tool to investigate these alterations, yet traditional functional connectivity approaches offer only a static representation of brain organization and may overlook transient temporal fluctuations of the BOLD signal. To better characterize these temporal dynamics, we applied the Leading Eigenvector Dynamics Analysis (LEiDA)^1 framework to rs-fMRI data in MCI and PD-MCI. LEiDA identifies recurrent patterns of whole-brain functional connectivity by capturing instantaneous phase-coherence dynamics through the leading eigenvector of the BOLD signal. This approach enables the extraction of dynamic brain states and quantification of their probability of occurrence and lifetime duration.

Recent LEiDA-based studies have shown significant differences in brain state dynamics between healthy controls, MCI, and Alzheimer’s disease, including reduced global synchronized states and higher lifetime in MCI with respect to the control group^2. Other work also demonstrated that altered switching behaviour or prolonged dwell times in specific states are sensitive markers of impaired cognitive performance and pathological network rigidity in aging and clinical populations^3. Studying these dynamics in MCI and PD-MCI may therefore reveal disease-specific alterations that remain undetected by traditional static approaches and provide mechanistic insight into network adaptability and degeneration. 

The aim is to explore whether dynamic connectivity metrics can reveal additional disease-related alterations and provide complementary insights into the cognitive performance in numerical and financial abilities.

Methods

Structural and functional preprocessing.

Structural and rs-fMRI data from 35 MCI and 24 PD-MCI patients were acquired at IRCCS San Camillo Hospital in Venice on a 3T Philips Ingenia scanner. High-resolution T1-weighted images were obtained with a 3D MPRAGE sequence (0.8mm isotropic). Preprocessing included bias field correction (ANTs), brain extraction, and tissue segmentation (Computational Anatomy Toolbox; segmentation into grey matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid). Resting-state fMRI was collected using a single-shot echo-planar Imaging (EPI) sequence (TR = 2.1s, TE = 30ms, multiband factor 3), yielding 421 volumes at 1.96×1.96×2.4mm³ resolution. Preprocessing comprised slice timing correction, motion correction (MCFLIRT of FSL), distortion correction (TOPUP of FSL), denoising steps including confound regression (white matter and CSF, motion parameters and outlier scans identified with ART) through CONN toolbox, high-pass filter (0.01 Hz) as well as ICA-based artifact removal using ICA-FIX (FSL) and low-pass filter (0.1 Hz).

Behavioural assessment.

All patients underwent a comprehensive neuropsychological assessment, including the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), the informal and formal Numerical Activities of Daily Living (NADL-INF and NADL-FOR)^4 and Numerical Activities of Daily Living - Financial (NADL-F)^5. The NADL-INF assesses everyday numerical competence across domains such as time, measures, transportation, communication, general knowledge, and money, NADL-FOR evaluates formal numerical abilities using structured subtests of number comprehension, transcoding, mental and written calculation. The NADL-F assesses the ability to independently manage everyday financial activities involving both basic (e.g., counting currencies) and advanced tasks (e.g., identifying frauds). NADL-INF, NADL-FOR and NADL-F scores and subscores were adjusted using linear regression for age, sex, education, and MMSE to account for demographic factors and overall cognitive status.

Brain parcellation.

The cerebral cortex of each patient was then parcellated into 100 regions of interest (ROIs) based on the 100-parcels Schaefer functional atlas, by adding 10 subcortical regions extracted from the AAL3 atlas and the cerebellum, for a total of 112 ROIs. Time series were extracted for each brain region in the parcellation by averaging the pre-processed blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) fMRI signal within each ROI.

Dynamic functional connectivity.

Dynamic functional connectivity was assessed using LEiDA^1. For each of the 112 brain regions, the BOLD phase was extracted through Hilbert transform, and instantaneous phase coherence between all region pairs was computed at each time point. The leading eigenvector of the resulting phase-locking (PL) matrix was then obtained for every time frame, providing a low-dimensional representation of the dominant synchronization pattern.

All eigenvectors were clustered using k-means (k from 2 to 20; 50 repetitions per k), and the optimal number of clusters was selected by maximizing both Silhouette and Dunn indices. Dynamic properties of each PL state were quantified through two complementary measures: fractional occupancy represented the percentage of time spent in each state, reflecting its predominance during the scan. State lifetime (or dwell time) captured the average duration of persistent presence in that state, providing an index of its temporal stability.

Correlation analysis.

The correlation between LEiDA-derived fractional occupancy and dwell time of each of the identified PL states and all NADL-INF, NADL-FOR and NADL-F cognitive subscores was performed separately for the MCI and PD-MCI cohorts. Between-group differences in correlation strength were assessed using Fisher’s z-transformation with two-tailed significance testing.

Results

Phase-locking states.

The resulting optimal clustering solution identified seven recurrent PL states, each corresponding to a large-scale brain network, as illustrated in Figure 2. These included: State 1 (Global Mode), State 2 and State 7 (Default Mode subnetworks), State 3 (Somatosensory-motor network), State 4 (Ventral Attention network), State 5 (Frontoparietal network), and State 6 (Visual network).

Fractional occupancy and dwell time.

MCI and PD-MCI comparisons revealed differences in the temporal properties of these PL states. Fractional occupancy showed a significant difference between PD-MCI and MCI conditions in State 7 (Default Mode network), highlighted in green in Figure 2, with PD-MCI patients spending a larger proportion of time in this state compared with MCI. This indicates enhanced predominance of this Default Mode subnetwork in PD-MCI. Dwell time demonstrated robust and persistent group differences: patients with PD-MCI exhibited significantly longer lifetime than MCI patients in all the seven PL states. 

Correlation between dynamic metrics and scores.

Several state–score pairs exhibited differences in correlation strength for both fractional occupancy and dwell time.

For fractional occupancy, significant between-group differences in correlation strength were observed in 7 state–score pairs, predominantly involving the global mode with NADL-FOR addition principles and written calculation, somatosensory-motor network with NADL-INF coins, visual network and the NADL-F  counting of currencies value, default mode network with NADL-INF communication and NADL-F shopping score. In these associations, MCI mostly showed positive correlations with cognitive subscores, whereas PD-MCI displayed negative correlations.

For dwell time, significant between-group differences in correlations were identified in 11 state–score pairs, involving global mode with NADL-INF transportation, default mode network with NADL-F total bills, somatosensory-motor network with NADL-INF communication and NADL-FOR writing arabic numbers, frontoparietal network with NADL-FOR writing arabic numbers and NADL-FOR total transcoding, visual network with NADL-F counting total and NADL-F percentages total, and default mode network with NADL-F shopping total and NADL-F judgments total. Overall, both fractional occupancy and dwell time metrics demonstrated robust and opposite correlations with cognitive measures in MCI compared to PD-MCI, indicating that the functional relevance of specific dynamic states diverges substantially between the two clinical populations.

Conclusion

The findings provide evidence that MCI and PD-MCI differ not only in the overall temporal engagement of large-scale PL states, but also in how these dynamic properties relate to the cognitive profile of each clinical group. PD-MCI patients exhibited a systematic increase in dwell time across all seven PL states and a higher fractional occupancy in the Default Mode network, suggesting a shift toward more stable or less flexible network dynamics. Such alterations may reflect reduced adaptability of large-scale communication patterns in PD-MCI, consistent with the notion of impaired dynamic reconfiguration in neurodegenerative conditions. PD-MCI patients tend to remain for longer periods within specific connectivity states.

The correlations between dynamic connectivity metrics and cognitive subscores indicate that the functional relevance of specific PL states diverges between MCI and PD-MCI, involving multiple large-scale networks.

For fractional occupancy, significant associations were found in the Global state, Somatomotor network, Visual network, and Default Mode subnetwork. These relationships showed opposite directions across groups: MCI patients exhibited mostly positive correlations, whereas PD-MCI patients showed negative ones. This suggests that, in MCI, increased engagement of global, somatomotor, or visual dynamics may be consistent with a compensatory interpretation, whereas in PD-MCI the same patterns may reflect reduced efficiency or maladaptive recruitment, particularly in tasks involving written calculation, coin handling, communication, and shopping-related numerical activities. Alternative explanations cannot be excluded: the cross-sectional nature of the study prevents causal inference, and longitudinal investigations will be necessary to clarify the functional role of these dynamics.

For dwell time, effects were more widespread, involving Default Mode states, Frontoparietal network, the Visual network, and again the Global and Somatomotor states. The frontoparietal network, a key network for executive control and symbolic manipulation, showed associations with writing arabic numbers and reading–writing tasks. This is consistent with evidence linking the frontoparietal network to numerical and orthographic processing, and with reports of compensatory frontoparietal engagement in MCI but reduced flexibility and efficiency in PD-MCI. Accordingly, prolonged engagement of this network may be adaptive in MCI but detrimental in PD-MCI, consistent with reduced cognitive flexibility in the latter. Similarly, associations linking visual and default states with counting, percentages, bills, judgments, and shopping scores highlight the integration of perceptual and self-referential networks during numerical tasks. The inversion of correlation signs between groups indicates that similar PL states may facilitate performance in MCI while reflecting inefficiency in PD-MCI.

Overall, these findings demonstrate that both groups may share the same dynamic states, while differing in their temporal engagement (fractional occupancy and dwell time) and in the cognitive relevance of these states. Dynamic metrics thus capture disease-specific alterations in network flexibility and may provide mechanistic insight into how functional brain dynamics support everyday cognition in MCI and PD-MCI.
