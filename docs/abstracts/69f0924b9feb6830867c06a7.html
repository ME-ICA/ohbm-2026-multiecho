Introduction

By studying changes in gene expression that occur without altering the DNA sequence, epigenetics offers a promising lead in unravelling how biological factors interact with the environment to shape brain development. However, previous studies in imaging epigenetics were often limited to pre-selected candidate genes or brain regions due to the high dimensionality of both the neuroimaging and epigenetic data 1. In consequences, little is known about the epigenetic processes, such as DNA methylation, influencing system-wide brain organization and their interaction with environment. For the present work we highlighted epigenetic sites predicting brain-wide anatomy in children with and without ADHD and investigated the environmental factors during childhood predicting methylation patterns at brain-related epigenetic sites.

Methods

The study included 130 children (72 ADHD diagnosis and 58 typically developing controls) assessed at up to three waves between ages 6 and 10. Assessments captured a wide range of environmental factors, including socio-environmental (peer victimization, family/school environment, socio-economic status), mental and physical health-related (sleep behaviour, BMI, medication, stressful life events, mental health), and external exposures (pollution). At the final wave (age ~10), structural neuroimaging (T1-weighted, diffusion-weighted) and peripheral DNA methylation (saliva sample, EPIC illumina Array) data were collected. T1w images were acquired using MEMPRAGE sequence (TR= 2530 ms, TE= 1.77, 3.51, 5.32, and 7.20ms, FOV= 230×230mm, FA=7 deg, axial slices= 176, matrix size = 256 × 232, voxel size = 0.9 mm3, acquisition time = 6 min 52 s). High angular resolution diffusion imaging data were acquired using the following protocol: b=2800 s/mm2, 60 directions, 4 interleaved b = 0 volumes (TR= 3200 ms, TE= 110 ms, multi-band acceleration factor = 3; voxel size = 2.4mm3, acquisition matrix: 110 x 110). T1w images were processed using FreeSurfer (http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/) before deriving whole brain modulated volumetric map using ANTs 2. DWI images were preprocessed using MRtrix 3 to derive map representing mean Fiber Cross-section (FC) and Fiber Density (FD) per white matter voxel. Methylation data were pre-processed using the R package minfi to derive epigenome wide M values representing the ratio of methylated/unmethylated intensity. As illustrated in Figure 1, parallel Independent Component Analysis (pICA) was employed using Fusion ICA Toolbox (FIT, http://mialab.mrn.org/software/fit/) to derive co-varying components of brain anatomy (modulated volumetric maps, Fiber Crossing, and Fiber Density maps) and DNA methylation. Multivariate random forest analyses were then used via the randomForestSRC package in R 4 to identify and reduce the top methylation predictors for each brain cluster within derived component 

Subsequently, Weighted Correlation Network Analysis (WGCNA) was applied to highest epigenetic predictors to construct co-methylation networks. Module eigengenes, representing the overall methylation pattern for each network, were extracted. Finally, Partial Least Squares (PLS) regression analysis was conducted to identify the environmental exposures most strongly associated with each co-methylation network.

Results

We identified five significant epigenetic-brain co-varying components (padjusted <0.026) encompassing 93 brain clusters and 19,119 unique methylation sites. Random forest analysis further highlighted 908 methylation sites as top predictors. These sites organized into four distinct co-methylation networks (modules), enriched for biological processes including inflammatory pathways, organ development, and brain-specific cellular regulation (padjusted <0.05). All identified co-methylation modules were associated with child mental health metrics including ADHD, ASD, and anxiety symptoms (Variable Importance in Projection (VIP)>1.14) and quality of life (VIP>1.03). The most consistent environmental predictors of module methylation included socio-environmental factors (teacher-child relationship, bullying, parenting style, socio-economic status; VIP>1.08), child health factors (sleep problems, stressful life events; VIP>1.02), and exposure to pollutants (PM2.5 and NO2; VIP>2.21).

Conclusion

Large-scale mapping of epigenetic and brain anatomy associations across childhood revealed patterns of co-varying brain volume, white matter microstructure and methylation in gene regions functionally relevant for biological brain development. Our study highlighted a diverse range of environmental factors during childhood—spanning social, health, and pollutant exposures- linked to methylation in sites co-varying with brain anatomy and linked to neurodevelopmental or mental health outcomes. While some risk factors (e.g., pollutants, economical factors) warrant broader public health awareness, other more modifiable factors (e.g., social environment, sleep problems) could be promising targets for early neurodevelopmental interventions.
