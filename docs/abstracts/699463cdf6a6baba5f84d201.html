Introduction

Retinotopic mapping is an essential tool for characterizing the functional organization of visual cortex. Two common issues in retinotopic mapping studies limit its scope and usability. First, projection geometry inside the MRI bore constrains the portion of the visual field that can be stimulated, leaving large regions of peripheral cortex unmapped. Existing solutions typically rely on laborious and delicate hardware modifications of the projection setup such as near-eye projection systems or wider head coils.^3^4 Therefore, it may be difficult to integrate them in more regular study protocols or scanning sites not focused on retinotopic mapping.

Second, retinotopic mapping is time-intensive, since lengthy acquisitions are often assumed necessary to achieve stable maps. Large-scale studies collect multiple 5-6 minute runs, yielding 15-30mins of data per participant. Although there are indications that early visual cortex can be mapped with relatively short acquisitions at 3T^2, it remains unclear how these findings generalize to ultrahigh field strengths or to the mapping of visual areas with more variable response properties. A systematic quantification of how map quality scales with acquisition duration at 7T is therefore needed.

Methods

The analyses presented in this work draw on two datasets: ten participants from the HCP Retinotopy dataset^1 and seven participants from the denseRetinotopy dataset.^5

To address challenge 1, we first introduce a corner-fixation paradigm that expands effective visual-field coverage using standard projection hardware. In this setup, participants are instructed to fixate at 90% of each screen corner while standard bar stimuli are shown (Fig. 1A). This leads to a more than 3-fold expansion of the stimulated field of view.

To address challenge 2, we evaluated how map quality scales with scan time. To ensure equal representation across stimulus types, individual cycles from bar, ring, and wedge runs were segmented and recombined into 3.2-minute training blocks (Fig. 1B). Incrementally increasing the number of training blocks, we characterized the quality of retinotopic maps as a function of scan time.

Results

For challenge 1, the corner-fixation paradigm substantially extended the mappable periphery. Eccentricity coverage increased from approximately 10 to 20° (Fig. 2A), while producing polar angle and eccentricity maps that remained qualitatively comparable to those obtained under central fixation.

For challenge 2, the minimal amount of scan time of 3.2mins already provided highly reliable retinotopic maps (Fig. 2B). Additional scan time primarily improved population receptive field size while polar angle and eccentricity remained stable.

Conclusion

Our results provide two actionable insights. First, peripheral coverage can be expanded considerably using a simple fixation manipulation that is readily applicable across fMRI environments. Second, retinotopic mapping can be achieved with significantly less time at 7T. Finally, there is a clear way to integrate these insights: Because each fixation location requires only a short acquisition, it is possible to complete a full corner fixation protocol within a 10 minute session. We thus present a framework that simultaneously optimizes visual field coverage and efficiency.
