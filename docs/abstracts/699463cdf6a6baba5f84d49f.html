Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is characterized by early disruptions in the Default Mode Network (DMN) function that reflect both reduced connectivity strength and altered dynamic interactions between large-scale systems1. These changes emerge alongside locus coeruleus degeneration and declining norepinephrine (NE) levels, which destabilize neural communication and may contribute to DMN and Task-Positive Network (TPN) fluctuations. Atomoxetine, an NE reuptake inhibitor, has shown promise in human clinical trials by reducing CSF tau and modulating connectivity patterns in mild cognitive impairment2. Dynamic metrics such as Quasi-Periodic Patterns (QPPs) provide additional sensitivity to AD-related changes by capturing repeating, phase-locked spatiotemporal patterns that can reveal altered DMN organization3.

Rodent studies suggest default mode and task-positive network organization comparable to that observed in humans, supporting translational relevance3. The present work analyzes rodent and human Atomoxetine datasets separately and establishes preprocessing and analytic pipelines needed for eventual cross-species alignment. We anticipate that Atomoxetine will strengthen DMN organization and stabilize dynamic neural states across species.

Methods

Functional MRI was acquired in 15 TgF344-AD rats (9 wild-type, 6 AD-phenotype) under isoflurane anesthesia across three conditions: baseline, saline, and Atomoxetine. During the saline and Atomoxetine scans, animals received a bolus injection (1 mg/kg) of saline or Atomoxetine. All rodent data were preprocessed using the RABIES pipeline5,  which included motion correction, denoising, anatomical alignment to the functional SIGMA atlas, and nuisance regression. Multiple smoothing levels (0.3 mm and 0.5 mm) were tested to evaluate their influence on Functional Connectivity (FC). Extracted ROI timeseries were used to compute FC matrices, and dynamic analyses included both QPP detection and Complex Principle Component Analysis (CPCA) decomposition4. CPCA components captured global signal fluctuations (PC1) and QPP1-like DMN-TPN anticorrelation (PC2). Group averages were computed for both WT and AD animals across all conditions for Functional Connectivity analysis.

Human data were obtained from a Phase II randomized, placebo-controlled Atomoxetine crossover trial in individuals with mild cognitive impairment2. Each participant completed both chronic Atomoxetine and placebo treatments for six months each, with 2-3 resting-state scans per visit. Data were preprocessed using CPAC with motion correction, anatomical Brainnetome atlas registration, nuisance regression, and temporal filtering to parallel rodent analyses. Functional connectivity was computed across whole-brain regions, and dynamic structure was assessed using CPCA. Rodent and human datasets were analyzed independently. A cross-species comparison will next align rodent cingulate and retrosplenial regions with human DMN hubs, then compare FC patterns, and CPCA component similarity to evaluate conserved NE-driven network modulation.

Results

AD rats showed reduced and disorganized default mode network (DMN) connectivity relative to WT rats, consistent with AD-related network degradation. Intra-group FC similarity was higher in AD rats for baseline-saline comparisons (r = 0.90) than in WT rats (r = 0.75), suggesting possible increased network rigidity in the AD condition. Across both genotypes, Atomoxetine was associated with reduced FC similarity, with the largest decreases observed in baseline Atomoxetine comparisons (WT: r = 0.62; AD: r = 0.56), indicating possible treatment-related network reorganization.

Time-varying CPCA revealed genotype-specific spatiotemporal differences. At baseline, WT rats exhibited lower PC1 (global signal) contributions than AD rats, suggesting baseline differences in arousal. Atomoxetine increased PC2 (QPP1) contributions in most WT animals (5/7), but decreased PC2 and PC3 contributions in AD animals. PC2 spatial propagation was consistent with the associated DMN-TPN anticorrelation.

From CPCA, human PC1 was dominated by a global signal–like component, while PC2 corresponded to  QPP1, anticorrelated activity between DMN and TPN regions. Visual assessment of Principal Components suggests that Atomoxetine-treated subjects exhibited relatively greater contributions of PC2 compared to placebo, whereas placebo subjects showed dominance of PC1 with minimal PC2 expression. Across subjects, Atomoxetine was also associated with changes in the relative balance of PC1-PC3 contributions, consistent with modulation of large-scale network dynamics. However, given inter-subject variability, formal quantification of analysis is required before making definitive group-based claims. These observations are consistent with prior evidence that noradrenergic modulation influences large-scale network organization and spatiotemporal stability2.

Consistent with this observation, intra-group FC similarity between baseline and post-drug conditions was higher for atomoxetine (r = 0.8569) than for placebo (r = 0.6068), suggesting greater network consistency following atomoxetine administration. Inter-group comparisons revealed moderate FC similarity between atomoxetine and placebo at baseline (r = 0.5481), which increased post-drug (r = 0.6753).

Preliminary results from both species motivate a full cross-species comparison, which will examine whether Atomoxetine induces consistent dynamic state distributions and FC shifts across rodents and humans.

Conclusion

This study processed and analyzed rodent and human Atomoxetine datasets using aligned preprocessing pipelines (RABIES for rodents and CPAC for humans) and parallel analytic approaches for time-averaged FC and time-varying CPCA. In rodents, AD animals exhibited disrupted DMN connectivity and altered large-scale network organization relative to WT controls. Across both rodent and human datasets, Atomoxetine was associated with condition-dependent changes in FC structure and the relative contributions of dominant cPCA components, though the magnitude and direction of these changes varied across subjects. The established pipelines provide a framework for evaluating noradrenergic-sensitive network features across species. Ongoing analyses will quantify these effects and assess whether network changes are consistent across species.
