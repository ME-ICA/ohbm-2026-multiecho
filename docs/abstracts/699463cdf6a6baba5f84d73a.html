Introduction

Studying structural brain networks has witnessed significant advancement in recent decades. Findings revealed a geometric principle, the exponential distance rule (EDR), showing that the number of neurons decreases exponentially with the length of their axons: p(d) ~ exp(-λd‧d).1 This optimal wiring rule was first observed in the white matter of the macaque, later in the mouse with different λd decay rates.1,2 A recent study suggests that it also holds in marmosets, humans and fruit flies (Drosophila) as well.3 This neuron-level information is used to build a region-level random network model that was able to explain various characteristics of inter-areal cortical networks in macaques, mice, and rats.1,2 The complete mapping of the Drosophila connectome4 allowed us to check the validity of this EDR model in a completely different evolutionary branch. Our results confirm that while many network properties of the region-level connectome can be attributed to geometric factors, there are a few exceptions. A highlighted example is the asymmetry of bidirectional link weights, which we consider to play a crucial role in determining the functional hierarchy of brain regions.

Methods

The EDR-based random network model essentially takes into account the known Dij distances between areas and M number of connections to generate random networks according to an arbitrary EDR with a given λ decay parameter.1 The algorithm can be described as randomly throwing axons (links) with length values generated from the input EDR, between appropriately distanced area pairs (nodes). The process is repeated until the network density matches the real data, with  the direction of each link chosen completely at random and the successive insertion generating weights. The validity of the model can be tested by searching for the optimal λ parameter for which the random networks reproduce network properties found in the real dataset. If the found value coincides well with the empirically determined λd characteristic decay rate, those network properties can be considered to arise from the geometrical constraints described by the EDR.1

As already mentioned before, the EDR model is defined on the larger scale of brain regions. Building this macroscale connectome from the neural connections can be considered trivial under the assumption that one neuron projects from one area to another. However, this is not the case for the tiny brain of the Drosophila, as one neuron can span multiple brain regions (neuropils).4 Taking this into consideration, the authors of the database focused on the synapse locations instead to determine the connectivity matrix of the network of neuropils (projectome).4 For each neuron, the probability of connecting two given neuropils can be expressed using the fraction of input and output synapses in those areas. The resulting probability matrix then can be summed for all neurons, resulting in arbitrary weight values representing connection strength between neuropils.4 In total, there are 75 nodes and 4733 links in the projectome. Given the neuropil size variability and their proportionality with the total number of synapses, we chose to further normalize the connectivity matrix by its columns. This way, the wij normalized weight values in the projectome reflect the probability of information transfer between regions. The Dij neuropil distance matrix required for the EDR model was taken as the euclidean distances between neuropil centers originating from JFRC2 Template Brain dataset.5

A previous study has already confirmed the presence of EDR in the Drosophila.3 However,  its λd characteristic decay rate needed for the validation of the model has not been measured. The FlyWire database allowed us to extract the tree structure (skeleton) for approximately 120 thousand reconstructed neurons including marked synapses. Generally for the Drosophila brain, the somas are disjoint from dendritic and axonic arbors, forming a peripheral layer.4 Considering this, we selected the closest pre- and postsynaptic points to the soma within the tree, and measure the path length between them to get the cable/axon length of the neuron (Fig.1A). The distribution of these values proves to be indeed an exponential decay, fitting for multiple bin sizes resulted in the interval of λd = [18.4, 21.5] mm-1 (Fig.1B). Given that the distances known between neuropils are Euclidean, when building EDR-based random networks, the probabilities of neurons projecting to said distances need to be estimated. For this reason, we needed to re-scale the measured cable distances to match the Euclidean scale. Therefore, we took the Euclidean distances instead between the two synaptic points (Fig.1A), which are strongly correlated with the original values (Fig.1D). The distribution of these Euclidean cable lengths gave also an exponential decay, but with a larger decay rate: λED = [31.4, 36.2] mm-1 (Fig.1C).

In the EDR model, we generated a total of 1000 random networks for every λ = {0, .. 60} mm-1 and measured multiple network properties separately. Then, the results were averaged for each ensemble and compared to the one obtained for the projectome.

Results

We found that the EDR model effectively reproduced numerous binary (unweighted) network properties of the projectome for λ = 33 mm-1, a value close to the measured λED. These properties included standard metrics from network science: degree distributions, number of uni- and bidirectional links, average path length and clustering coefficient, distribution of triangular motifs and lastly clique sizes.

For properties based on link weights, we got mixed results. In the case of weight distribution, the weaker links present in the projectome were not necessarily found in the EDR model networks. Similarly, the weighted out-degree (out-strength) distribution were not reproduced by the model. Given that projectome link weights are calculated from synaptic connections, which were originally predicted by convolutional neural networks, we argue that some of these weaker weights can be artifacts of imprecise detection. Another contributing factor could be the thresholding of synaptic connections with less than 5 synapses recommended by the authors of the database,4 that result incomplete link weights. Interestingly, for network properties based on the weighted shortest path lengths like their distribution or communication efficiency values, the EDR model with λ = 33 mm-1 matches the projectome. This further supports our previous argument, as shortest path length tend to be more robust, following stronger connections within the network.

Many of the general properties of the projectome can be replicated by the EDR model, however there are a few exceptions as well. This is expected, as the model takes into account only the distances between areas, the number of connections (density), and the distance rule itself; all the other factors are random in the network generation process. For example, even though the degree distribution was well reproduced, the values for individual nodes (neuropils) were not due to this randomness. Looking at the modular structure of the projectome using hierarchical clustering method, we got a realistic picture with symmetric organization and clusters localized in space. The former was not found for the EDR model networks. Our most important finding was related to the asymmetry of connections. We introduced a metric quantifying the weight asymmetry of bidirectional links: ASYMij = |wij – wji|/(wij + wji). The distribution of these values revealed that there are a large number of asymmetric connections in the Drosophila projectome that were not replicated by the pure geometrical EDR model (Fig.2A). We hypothesize that these connections are related to an actual functional hierarchy of brain areas. One supporting fact is that when we look at the homotopic links only — connecting left and right parts of the same area — the asymmetry disappears (Fig.2B). The two parts having symmetric connection indicates that they are also on the same hierarchical level, which is likely considering their similar functionality. In order to gain insight into this structure, we built a new network with unidirectional links that characterize information flow between areas. The direction of these new links is given by the direction the original weight was larger in the projectome, and the weight is given by the previously introduced  ASYMij metric. In- and out-strength values in this new network can give us a view on which areas tend to have more in- and outward projections, so their possible ranking as well. What we noticed was that this ranking of areas is strongly symmetrical, also the in- and out-strength values are anticorrelated, indicating well defined levels in this functional hierarchy (Fig.2C). For example, visual areas on the sides and olfactory on the top tend to have the highest out-strengths, meaning a low ranking in the hierarchical structure. This seems to match the idea of sensory areas sending more information to the central brain, further justifying the use of asymmetry measure in determining functional hierarchy.

Conclusion

Our findings further support the EDR model  as a suitable null model for analyzing region-level brain networks across diverse species. The importance of the null model lies in its ability to facilitate the identification of functionally significant features not caused by inevitable geometric constraints. One example shown is the pronounced asymmetry of connection weights, likely important for functional hierarchy. We present a first attempt to build this hierarchy of brain regions using the introduced asymmetry measure. With further development, this approach could be applied to human brain connectivity data to compare structural hierarchy with brain activity patterns, potentially reveling new insights on how structural organization shapes brain function.
