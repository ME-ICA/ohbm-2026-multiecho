Introduction

As we age, cognitive performance declines in multiple domains, including memory, executive and perceptual-motor function. However, current approaches for assessing cognition, such as neuropsychological testing or task-based fMRI, face several limitations, including variability in patient effort, learning effects, and lack of standardization. These challenges highlight the need for complementary approaches that provide standardized, reproducible, as well as clinically meaningful measures of cognition. Here, we present in-silico cognition, a reservoir computing framework that models cognitive task performance and provides standardized measures of memory, executive, and perceptual-motor function. 

Methods

We quantified in-silico cognition using four computational tasks spanning different cognitive domains: memory capacity (Fig. 1) and delayed match to sample (memory), Go/No-Go (executive function), and random dot motion (perceptual-motor function). For each participant, we constructed anatomical brain connectomes using diffusion-weighted imaging. Then, we used reservoir computing, a machine-learning framework that permits us to analyze how information flows between regions in the anatomical connectomes, to evaluate their capacity to perform each of the above tasks1. We evaluated this performance in two cohorts. The primary dataset was the Cam-CAN2 lifespan cohort, which includes 636 healthy participants (18-88 years) who completed diffusion-weighted imaging, resting-state fMRI, and magnetization transfer imaging. We validated our findings in the LEMON3 cohort (n = 226), consisting of 154 young (20-40 years) and 72 older (55-80 years) adults.

To determine whether computational task performance captures age-related variation, we trained a multilayer perceptron deep-learning model to predict chronological age from the in-silico measures. We assess the clinical implications of these measures by using partial least squares to test whether in-silico cognition was associated with cognitive performance and different brain imaging measures derived from gray matter atrophy, anatomical, and functional connectivity, as well as with the integrity of locus coeruleus, a key and small subcortical nucleus that is responsible for most of the noradrenergic neurotransmission in the brain. Finally, to investigate the biological mechanisms underlying in-silico cognition, we examined whether these measures were linked with certain gene-expression profiles, using transcriptomic data for 10,027 genes from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA).

Results

In-silico cognition measures declined with age and showed significant associations with neuroimaging measures and cognitive performance. The global and regional memory capacity showed the strongest decreases with age, particularly in the frontal and parietal regions (Fig. 2a). The spatial patterns of this age-related variation were accurately predicted by the transcriptomics model, achieving a correlation between predicted and true regional memory capacity of 0.53 ± 0.05. The strongest contribution in these predictions was by genes involved with signaling transduction processes in inhibitory neurons. 

Using the memory capacity values to predict individual chronological age (Fig. 2b), we observed a strong correlation between true and predicted age (0.72 ± 0.04) across 10-fold cross-validation (Fig. 2c). In the replication cohort, memory capacity also showed a high discriminative accuracy for distinguishing young and older adults (AUC: 0.908 ± 0.054), correctly classifying 80.3% of the old and 92.7% of the young individuals.

Global memory capacity was further associated with white-matter integrity in tracts connecting parieto-occipital and frontal regions (Fig. 2d), as well as resting-state functional activation signals in sensorimotor, visual, and attention networks. Finally, regional memory capacity in regions involved in visual and emotional processing, memory, and multisensory integration of facial stimuli was linked to individual cognitive performance on a face recognition test (Fig. 2e), which is commonly used to assess age-related decline in cognitive memory.

Conclusion

In-silico cognition, and especially the memory capacity, emerges as a robust marker of aging and showed a correlation with cognitive performance on tasks targeting the corresponding domains. Additionally, these findings highlight the biological significance of computational measures derived using reservoir computing. By showing the utility of in-silico cognition, our insights pave the way for the wider integration of computationally simulated cognitive tasks, enabling scalable, standardized, cost-efficient assessments that are directly translatable into clinical practice.
