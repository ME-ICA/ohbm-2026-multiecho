Introduction

In shift workers, night shifts are consistently linked to poorer executive and attentional performance, especially with repeated or long shifts [1]. Beyond the cognitive impairments, our previous study showed that short-term night shifts altered the resting-state connectivity of medical staff [2]. However, the neurobiological mechanisms driving the cognitive response time and subsequent recovery effects after night shifts remain under-explored, especially for the gut-brain interactions.

Therefore, we recruited Taiwanese medical staff and tested their brain functions and fecal samples following night shifts and a period of recovery. We used a Numeric Stroop task during fMRI, combined with stool microbiome and short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) profiling across pre-shift, post-shift, and recovery sessions. We aimed to characterize the longitudinal dynamics of inhibitory control and brain recruitment, and to explore whether individual differences in neural restoration co-vary with gut microbial diversity and SCFAs.

Methods

We recruited 12 medical personnel from hospitals in Taipei for a longitudinal repeated-measure study across three time points: (a) pre-shift (baseline), (b) post-shift (after ≥4 consecutive night shifts), and (c) recovery (after ≥3 rest days). All 12 participants completed the fMRI scanning and fecal sampling across all sessions. Due to technical issues with data logging, valid behavioral data (reaction time and accuracy) from the Numeric Stroop Task were available for only 9 participants.

MRI data were acquired using a 3T PRISMA scanner (National Taiwan University). The task-fMRI protocol utilized a gradient-echo EPI sequence (voxel size=3.44×3.44×3.4 mm³; TR/TE=2000/32 ms; FA=77°) with a scan duration of 4 minutes and 48 seconds. During the scan, participants performed the Numeric Stroop Task via E-Prime 2.0. Data were preprocessed using the CONN toolbox, with first- and second-level analyses performed in SPM12. In the brain, the cingulo-opercular network (CON), spanning the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), insula, and inferior parietal lobule (IPL), are central for conflict monitoring and interoception. Crucially, the insula serves as a primary hub for integrating visceral signals, a key pathway in the gut-brain axis [3,4]. ROI extraction was conducted using the MarsBaR toolbox, specifically targeting the Insula, IPL, and ACC.

Statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.9. A one-way repeated-measures ANOVA was employed to assess longitudinal changes across the three sessions. This was applied to the whole cohort (N=12) for neuroimaging metrics (ROI beta values), SCFA concentrations, and gut microbial alpha diversity. For behavioral metrics (Stroop RT/Accuracy) and any correlation analyses involving behavioral performance, the analysis was restricted to the valid sub-cohort (N=9). Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to examine associations between brain activation, gut microbial features (diversity, taxonomy, SCFA), and cognitive performance (p < 0.05 uncorrected).

Results

The Numeric Stroop Task revealed a pattern of maintained performance during the shift, followed by significant improvement. While the Stroop interference effect (Incongruent vs. Congruent) persisted across all phases (p < 0.04), no significant inhibitory deficit was observed immediately post-shift compared to baseline. Instead, Incongruent reaction times showed a significant long-term main effect (F2,16 = 4.52, p = 0.028). Crucially, reaction times became faster during the recovery phase compared to the post-shift phase (Post vs. Recovery: t8 = 3.62, p = 0.007), indicating a restoration effect that emerged after recovery (Fig. 1a). In parallel, gut microbial profiling identified a significant longitudinal shift in alpha diversity (Simpson Index: F2,22 = 5.62, p = 0.011) (Fig. 1b). Post-hoc comparisons indicated that diversity decreased significantly immediately after night shifts (Pre vs. Post: t11 = 2.73, p = 0.020) and remained suppressed during the recovery period (Pre vs. Recovery: t11 = 2.69, p = 0.021), rather than returning to baseline.

ROI analysis revealed a striking, synchronized 'suppression-rebound' dynamic across the CON. During the acute shift phase (Pre-to-Post), significant hypoactivation was observed concurrently in the right IPL (t = 3.49, p = 0.015, Bonf. corrected) (Fig. 2a) and right ACC (t = 4.91, p < 0.01, Bonf. corrected) (Fig. 2c), indicating a systemic dampening of the conflict-monitoring circuitry. This suppression was followed by a robust restorative rebound during the recovery phase (Post-to-Recovery), where activation levels in three nodes surged significantly (right ACC: t = -4.17; right IPL: t = -5.38; left Insula: t = -4.62; all p < 0.005, Bonf. corrected) (Fig. 2a, 2c, and 2e) to return to baseline levels.

During the recovery phase, changes of brain activation were significantly associated with gut microbial markers. The magnitude of rebound activation during the recovery phase in the right IPL was positively correlated with the increase in fecal Butyrate levels (r = 0.78, p = 0.014) (Fig. 2b). Conversely, the amplitudes of both the right ACC (r = -0.774, p = 0.014) (Fig. 2d) and the left Insula (r = -0.714, p = 0.031) (Fig. 2f) during the recovery phase showed negative correlations with changes in Simpson diversity.

Conclusion

We characterize a synchronized "suppression-and-restoration" pattern governing the recovery from night shifts. Our data indicate that the acute phase is characterized by a systemic hypoactivation of the conflict-monitoring circuitry (ACC, SMA, Insula), which occurs even when behavioral performance remains statistically stable. Crucially, the subsequent behavioral improvement during recovery is accompanied by a vigorous neural rebound in these same regions, suggesting that post-shift hyperactivation represents a concurrent neural restoration process. Furthermore, these brain-function dynamics appear to be linked to the gut ecosystem. The correlation between elevated fecal butyrate and stronger parietal recruitment hints that specific microbial metabolites may support the neural resources associated with network recovery. In parallel, the inverse relationship between microbial diversity and cingulate rebound suggests that the magnitude of neural recruitment during recovery varies with the state of the gut ecosystem. Collectively, these findings move beyond simple fatigue models, proposing a framework where neural and microbial recovery processes are closely intertwined in the resilience against circadian disruption.
