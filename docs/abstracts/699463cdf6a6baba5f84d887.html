Introduction

Cortical oscillations in the beta (15-30 Hz) frequency range are increasingly studied for their role in top-down control of neural circuit functions. Beta activity has been shown to be inversely related to neural processing in functionally specific brain regions during motor1 and speech2 processes, suggesting a general inhibitory role of the signal. In addition, it has been suggested that beta modulations signal predictive properties of incoming sensory stimuli, including speech2. While much of the work with beta oscillations has focused on modulations of average spectral power, recent consensus dictates that beta oscillations manifest as brief ‘beta bursts’ with distinct timing and frequency properties3. However, associations between these properties and cognitive processes beyond the motor domain remain to be resolved. In the present work, we aim to investigate the role of beta bursts in naturalistic speech processing. We explore the role of beta bursts in tracking speech processing at the word and phrase level, as well as the role of beta bursts in signalling the predictive context of upcoming words. 

Methods

MEG data was obtained from 11 healthy adult participants while listening to TED talk recordings. Participants listened to 7 talks, each approximately 10 minutes in duration. MEG data was cleaned and projected to the cortical surface using MRI-based anatomical reconstructions. We then extracted MEG time series data from 200 regions of interest and detected bursts in each time series using a simple amplitude-thresholding procedure. To assess the involvement of beta bursts in natural speech tracking, we compared beta burst rates during periods of continuous speech and periods of silence (i.e., pauses), and during short intervals before, during, and after presentation of individual words.  

To investigate the role of beta bursts in predictive speech processes, we trained a large language model to predict upcoming speech tokens in the audio recordings, and outputs were used to derive proxy measures of the contextual uncertainty surrounding each word. We then assessed the relationship between word uncertainty and beta burst dynamics. Finally, we used a self-exciting point process approach to explore the role of beta burst timing in top-down and bottom-up relationships between cortical regions and assessed changes in these relationships with uncertainty level.

Results

Throughout natural speech, beta bursts showed a strong inverse relationship to auditory input, increasing in bilateral auditory and language regions during pauses and decreasing during periods of prolonged speech. Furthermore, the onset of each word was associated with a small reduction in beta bursting, suggesting a further role in word-level processing (see Figure 1).

With respect to predictive context, beta burst rate exhibited an unexpected, inverted U-shape relationship with uncertainty level such that moderate uncertainty words were associated with elevated beta bursting compared to both low and high uncertainty words (see Figure 1). Evaluation of the temporal evolution of beta burst dynamics with respect to word onset revealed that low uncertainty (i.e., predictable) words elicited an early beta burst suppression, before word onset, while high uncertainty (i.e., unpredictable) words elicited a later, but prolonged beta burst suppression. In contrast, moderately uncertain words were associated with a slight increase in beta bursting prior to word presentation, reflecting a possible predictive signalling role.

Spatial analysis revealed bidirectional influence of bursts in auditory and sensorimotor brain regions suggesting roles of beta bursts in signalling top-down predictions as well as communicating bottom-up feedback throughout the auditory-motor network. Bottom-up influence from the auditory cortex was elevated in both low and high uncertainty contexts compared to moderate uncertainty, while top-down influence into the auditory cortex was greatest in low uncertainty contexts.

Conclusion

The results suggest that beta bursting plays a regulatory role in speech processing by modulating inhibition in functionally-specific brain regions to enable word- or phrase-level linguistic processing. In predictive speech processing, beta bursting has a multifaceted role, acting to either reduce inhibition prior to word presentation - enabling early processing of highly predictable words, or to increase inhibition immediately prior to the presentation of moderately predictable words to conserve resources when a reasonable prediction exists. Together, our results shed light on the temporal dynamics of speech processing and demonstrate the integral role of beta bursting in signalling predictive speech features.
