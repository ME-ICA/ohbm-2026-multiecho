Introduction

Neural activity is often assumed to respond reliably to identical stimuli, yet such consistency is non-trivial for recurrent, high-dimensional and noisy cortical dynamics. Without reliable representations, perception and learning would be fundamentally unstable. However, most theories treat this consistency as a given rather than a property that must itself emerge. 

Recently, we proposed the selective consistency hypothesis: the brain initially behaves as a variable system, and experience selectively stabilises its dynamics so that only particular, repeatedly encountered stimuli evoke reproducible trajectories while overall variability is preserved1. This consistency is not the well-known “feature selectivity” of neurons or task-specific computation, but rather a dynamical substrate that enables downstream inference, such as prediction and learning.

Here we report our two complementary approaches to testing this hypothesis. First, we examined whether unsupervised plasticity in a recurrent network can self-organise stimulus-specific convergence—selective consistency. Second, we asked whether human EEG during a noise-repetition-detection (NRD) task shows analogous selective consistency, and how this relates to individual learning ability and intrinsic network dynamics.

Methods

 We combined a behavioural paradigm (Fig.1a)2, recurrent-network modelling, and a human EEG experiment. Across both projects, participants or models performed an auditory NRD task. Each trial consisted of either repeated-noise (RN) stimuli—concatenated 500-ms noise segments—or pure noise (N). Within each block, a small subset of RN and N stimuli repeatedly reappeared as “reference repeated noise” (RefRN) and “reference noise” (RefN). Some previous studies reported an improved repetition-detection accuracy specifically for RefRN.

Simulation (Fig.1b)

We implemented recurrent networks with fixed input and output weights. Networks varied in spectral radius, modulating their baseline balance between stability and variability. Plastic versions incorporated a weak Hebbian-like rule that modified recurrent connections during brief exposure to NRD stimuli (N, RN, RefN and RefRN); static networks lacked plasticity. After training, all weights were frozen, and the model was tested on new N/RN stimuli and the learned RefN/RefRN. Selective consistency was quantified as the correlation between early portions of the output during the first and second 500-ms segments of each trial. Surrogate-based nonparametric tests assessed effects of stimulus type, plasticity and spectral radius.

EEG (Fig.1c)

Twenty-four adults (19 females and five males, mean age = 35.7 (±8.5) years, range 21–45) performed the NRD task during EEG recording (pre-registered sample size3). Preprocessing included epoching, band-pass and notch filtering, and ICA. Analyses targeted auditory and fronto-parietal regions. For each trial, multichannel L1 distances between corresponding EEG segments were computed to index within-trial neural dissimilarity. Linear mixed-effects models tested the effects of stimulus type (N/RN/RefN/RefRN), subjective repetition report, and learning status. Inter-trial phase coherence (ITPC) during repetitive segments was analysed in parallel. And a cluster-based Monte Carlo permutation test for the statistical comparison. Resting-state EEG from a separate run was used to estimate each participant’s 1/f spectral exponent as an index of intrinsic network dynamics (subcritical–supercritical). We related this index to individual NRD learning.

Results

Simulation

Plastic recurrent networks rapidly developed selective consistency for a single repeated-noise exemplar. After a small number of exposures, correlations between the first and second segments were markedly higher for RefRN in plastic than in static networks, whereas no such effect emerged for RN, RefN or N. This convergence was a population-level property and did not require optimisation algorithms. Varying the spectral radius revealed that selective consistency peaked near mildly supercritical dynamics, where variability supports flexible responses but trajectories can still stabilise. Subcritical regimes compressed stimulus differences, whereas strongly supercritical regimes produced excessive variability, preventing convergence.

EEG

Behaviourally, repetition-detection performance improved selectively for RefRN, with substantial inter-individual variability.

In auditory regions, mixed-effects models showed significantly reduced L1 distances—i.e., more reproducible segment-wise patterns—selectively for RefRN, particularly when repetition was perceived and when the stimulus had been well learned. This neural consistency was absent for RN. In contrast, fronto-parietal regions showed greater L1 distances for well-learned RefRN trials perceived as repetitive, consistent with decision-related differentiation rather than sensory convergence.

Scrutinisation of ITPC during the repetitive segments, which was reported to be higher for RefRN in a previous study 4, found that an improved value for RefRN was not consistently observed, but only in the trials when the stimulus was well learned, and repetition was perceived.

Finally, resting-state spectral exponents showed a robust behavioural–physiological link: learning ability was highest in participants whose intrinsic dynamics were closest to criticality, whereas more subcritical or supercritical dynamics were associated with reduced learning and weaker selective consistency.

Conclusion

Across modelling and human EEG, we identify a previously unrecognised form of experience-dependent neural stability: selective consistency. Both the recurrent network and the human auditory cortex developed stimulus-specific convergence for a single noise exemplar, despite its statistical similarity to other stimuli and despite the absence of feedback. This stability emerged from unsupervised plasticity in the model and manifested as reproducible segment-wise EEG patterns in humans.

Selective consistency provides a mechanistic account of how reliable sensory representations can arise, without relying on a modification of an internal model. Rather than updating internal generative predictions, experience reduces variability stimulus-dependently in early sensory activity, thereby increasing representational precision. This may enable the successful detection of repetition even for unpredictable stimuli such as white noise.

The phenomenon generalises beyond NRD. Without consistency, each encounter with the same stimulus would be treated as a new, unrelated event, preventing gradient-based or prediction-error–based learning in conventional theories from operating. Yet our studies indicate such consistency may itself be an acquired dynamical property. Selective consistency offers a principled framework for understanding how the brain determines which inputs should acquire representation and be learned.

Finally, the dependence on near-critical dynamics in both projects suggests that selective consistency is maximised only within an optimal dynamical regime. Deviations from this regime—observed in ageing, developmental conditions or neurological disorders—may impair selective consistency, resulting in chronically low representation precision. Such a reduction in precision could, in turn, contribute to a wide range of functional deficits. Therefore, these findings position selective consistency as a fundamental dynamical prerequisite for perception.
