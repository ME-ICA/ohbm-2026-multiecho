Introduction

Child maltreatment is a large public health concern; in the U.S., an estimated 12-16% of children are investigated and have confirmed exposure.^1 Maltreatment exposure is associated with adverse physical and mental health outcomes including internalizing and externalizing disorders. During adolescence, neurodevelopmental processes such as synaptic pruning and myelination may buffer or exacerbate these negative effects of maltreatment. White matter serves as the structural connections linking brain regions constituting neural circuits. Adolescents exposed to maltreatment generally have reduced fractional anisotropy, a rough scalar measure of white matter integrity, along fronto-striatal (e.g., internal capsule, external capsule, anterior corona radiata) and fronto-limbic (e.g., anterior corpus callosum, cingulum) fibers compared to non-exposed peers.^2 Paradoxically, some have identified higher integrity (i.e., hypermyelination) at uncinate and superior longitudinal fibers.^2 In sum, these integrity alterations are associated with higher rates of internalizing and externalizing behaviors, and deficits in inhibitory control, working memory, and emotion regulation.^2 However, many studies rely on retrospective reports of adverse childhood experiences to determine exposure which are broader than child maltreatment and are subject to recall bias. The present study sought to leverage advancements in tractography and a large cohort of children and adolescents to evaluate differences in white matter integrity between children exposed and unexposed to maltreatment.

Methods

The objectives of this study were to 1) identify changes in white matter structural integrity measured by quantitative anisotropy in children and adolescents exposed to maltreatment compared to unexposed peers using correlational tractography; and 2) extract more integrity information by exploring differences between groups using measures of diffusivity and isovolumetric fraction. Participants aged 8-13 (MMale,Maltreated = 11.20; MFemale,Maltreated = 11.62; MMale,Non-Maltreated = 10.68; MFemale,Non-Maltreated = 10.84) comprised a state-wide, prospective cohort of N = 700 children with (n = 526) and without (n = 173 child maltreatment exposure. Children with a past-year history of a maltreatment investigation were identified via Pennsylvania’s Child Welfare Information Solution database, providing an objective report of maltreatment exposure. T1-weighted MRI and diffusion weighted imaging data were available for N = 549 participants. Diffusion weighted imaging data were preprocessed using QSIPrep, a workflow including correction for intensity non-informities, realignment, nonlinear registration to MNI space, brain extraction, segmentation, and denoising. N = 523 participants (nMaltreated = 418; nNon-Maltreated = 105) had complete, preprocessed diffusion weighted imaging data suitable for tractography (Figure 1). After preprocessing, image quality was evaluated using a machine learning-guided quality control metric computed from motion and other corrective parameters.^3 Multiple quality control thresholds (0.50, 0.60, 0.70, 0.80) were tested to maximize study power and image quality. 

White matter fibers were reconstructed using deterministic tractography with DSI Studio.^4 Herein, diffusion orientation distribution functions are generated using generalized q-sampling imaging. This creates scalar metrics such as quantitative anisotropy, an analog to fractional anisotropy representing white matter integrity, used to guide tractography. Correlational tractography^4 was used to relate white matter integrity to child maltreatment exposure (binarized). Rather than comparing integrity at the voxel-level, this method computes differences along the tract (fixel-wise). These fixel-wise differences were related to maltreatment exposure using multiple linear regression. The dependent variable was quantitative anisotropy computed from deterministic tractography. These models were covaried for age, sex, body mass index, socio- economic status, and mean framewise displacement. Permutation testing (n = 5000) with a false-discovery rate of padj < .05 was used to ensure correction for multiple comparisons. These models included data that surpassed each quality control threshold to test the reliability of findings. Resulting white matter fibers were pruned using topology-informed pruning with 16 iterations. To test the effect sizes of these differences, the t threshold was increased from 2.5 to 3.0 with a length threshold of 20 voxel distance at the whole-brain level. Post-hoc, ROI-level correlational tractography sought to examine patterns in white matter integrity change within the region at which associations between maltreatment exposure and quantitative anisotropy were found using a quality control threshold of 0.50 and t threshold of 3.0. In these analyses, regression model design and parameters were identical except a length threshold of one voxel distance was used given the smaller spatial extent (Figure 2E), and outcome measures included mean diffusivity, axial diffusivity, radial diffusivity, and isovolumetric fraction.

Results

As the quality control threshold was increased, fewer white matter tracts were positively associated with child maltreatment exposure. Using a t threshold of 2.5, the spatial extent decreased from 16,530 to 3,009 tracts (Figure 2A-D). However, this spatial extent overlapped among each quality control threshold. Using a threshold of 0.50, the top five tracts positively associated with child maltreatment exposure included the left retrolenticular part of internal capsule (96.78% coverage), left posterior thalamic radiations (67.78% coverage), right posterior limb of internal capsule (66.46% coverage), left posterior corona radiata (66.14% coverage), and the left posterior limb of internal capsule (61.84%; Figure 2A). Using a threshold of 0.80, the left retrolenticular part of internal capsule (48.55% coverage) and right posterior limb of internal capsule (49.90% coverage) were still associated, as well as nearby tracts such as the left (39.16% coverage) and right cerebral peduncles (39.92% coverage; Figure 2D). Using a t threshold of 3.0, the spatial extent of white matter tracts positively associated with child maltreatment exposure was smaller (zero [threshold > 0.80] to 523 tracts). The spatial extent decreased as the quality control threshold was increased, with a similar pattern of overlapping regions per threshold (Figure 2E-H). Increasing the quality control thresholds from 0.50 to 0.70, the top five tracts positively associated with child maltreatment exposure included the left retrolenticular part of internal capsule (0.50: 42.76% coverage; 0.70: 19.61% coverage), left cerebral peduncle (0.50: 37.26% coverage, 0.70: 37.64% coverage), left posterior limb of internal capsule (0.50: 19.49% coverage; 26.83% coverage), left corticospinal tract (0.50: 8.42% coverage; 0.70: 14.60% coverage), and the left posterior corona radiata (0.50: 7.39% coverage; 0.70: 7.17% coverage; Figure 2E, 2G).

Post-hoc analyses examined associations between child maltreatment exposure and other white matter integrity metrics within the region resulting from a quality control threshold of 0.50 and t threshold of 3.0. Child maltreatment exposure was associated with decreased mean diffusivity in 202 tracts, increased axial diffusivity in 1,604 tracts, decreased axial diffusivity in 1,223 tracts, increased radial diffusivity in 336 tracts, and increased isovolumetric fraction in 7,012 tracts (Figure 2I-M). Importantly, post-hoc tract counts can be greater than whole-brain level tract counts as the length threshold was decreased from 20 to one voxel distance.

Conclusion

Using a quality control threshold of 0.50 (to maximize study power) and a higher t threshold (to retain associations with larger effect sizes), correlational tractography revealed higher quantitative anisotropy in left-lateralized, posterior white matter tracts in CM-exposed compared to non-exposed children. Although these quantitative anisotropy trends are associated with greater white matter integrity,^4 diffusivity metrics suggested heterogeneous patterns within this region (Figure 2I-L). Child maltreatment exposure was associated with higher axial diffusivity, a traditional marker of axonal coherence, in inferior portions as the white matter fibers funnel from the left posterior limb of internal capsule to the cerebral peduncle. In medial portions of this region, child maltreatment exposure was associated with lower mean diffusivity, together a profile of higher white matter integrity.^5 Superior portions of the region consisting of posterior corona radiata fibers had lower axial and higher radial diffusivity values, which was expected given the fanning fiber organization of this tract.^5 Radial diffusivity values at more inferior portions of this region followed expected patterns, whereby they increased as corticopontocerebellar fibers integrate through the peduncles to the corticospinal tract. This could indicate white matter pathology or consolidation of fibers as they funnel from motor and sensory cortical regions towards the internal capsule and corticospinal tract. Pathological processes such as edema or intracellular swelling may be further supported by increased isovolumetric fraction across the entire region in maltreatment-exposed youth compared to non-exposed youth. These left-lateralized structural alterations are associated with changes in attention, cognitive control, sensory integration, and language processing.^5 These neurobehavioral associations could in part explain greater risk for externalizing disorders (e.g., attention, conduct, substance use) among children and adolescents exposed to maltreatment.^1-2.

This study benefited from the correlational tractography procedure, which provides better anatomical specificity on white matter integrity differences compared to voxel-wise approaches such as tract-based spatial statistics.^4 Repeating whole-brain analyses with increasing quality control^3 and t thresholds demonstrated reliability of differences accounting for scanner-related artifacts (i.e., minimizing false positives). The large sample size and objective reporting of child maltreatment exposure maximized study power and minimized false negatives in findings. Theoretical limitations included assumptions in relationships between diffusivity and anisotropy values and white matter pathology. In vivo histological studies are needed to confirm the validity of these relationships. Methodological limitations include an inability to infer temporal ordering between maltreatment exposure and white matter changes. In addition, the novelty of correlational tractography limits the available guidance on appropriate parameters and conclusions to draw. We attempted to bolster validity and reliability of our findings through quality control and t-value thresholding, but our approach applied to correlational tractography is unprecedented. Future studies will incorporate multiple waves of diffusion weighted imaging data using differential tractography, a repeated-measures extension of correlational tractography,^4 to clarify the temporal ordering between maltreatment exposure and white matter structural integrity changes over the course of adolescence.
