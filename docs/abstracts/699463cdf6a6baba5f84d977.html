Introduction

In our previous research we reported Blood-brain barrier (BBB) leakage in schizophrenia-spectrum disorders (SSDs) compared to healthy controls, based on dynamic contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (DCE-MRI) 1. However, the underlying mechanisms and etiology of this finding remain unclear. In this study, we aim to investigate these mechanisms using a transcriptomics approach and perform subgroup analyses to better understand the clinical phenotype associated with BBB leakage in SSDs.

Methods

Eighty-one individuals with SSD and 52 healthy controls underwent high-resolution DCE-MRI at 3T to quantify BBB permeability using voxel-wise Ktrans maps. Group differences were assessed using voxel-wise ANCOVA. Patients with markedly elevated BBB leakage were identified and clinically characterized. Regional specificity was determined using atlas-based analyses. Imaging transcriptomics linked spatial patterns of BBB leakage to gene expression from the Allen Human Brain Atlas, followed by gene set enrichment analyses. Spatial correlations with PET-derived neurotransmitter maps were assessed using the neuromaps framework.

Results

Replicating our prior findings, patients with SSD showed increased BBB permeability relative to controls; however, this effect was driven by a distinct subgroup comprising approximately 9% of patients. BBB leakage was predominantly localized to subcortical and brainstem regions, including the thalamus, hippocampus, amygdala, and midbrain nuclei. The leaky subgroup was older and showed trends toward longer illness duration and greater cumulative antipsychotic exposure. Imaging transcriptomics revealed enrichment of glial and vascular cell-type gene signatures (astrocytes, microglia, endothelial cells) in regions with increased BBB leakage, alongside negative enrichment of neuronal gene sets. Spatial correlation analyses demonstrated significant correlations with PET-derived dopaminergic, serotonergic and cholinergic maps.

Conclusion

The findings confirm BBB dysfunction in a subgroup of individuals with SSD, and further suggest that BBB dysfunction is not a ubiquitous feature of SSD but possibly characterizes a biologically distinct SSD subgroup marked by subcortical localization, glial molecular signatures, and convergence with monoaminergic and inflammatory systems. These findings support a stratified view of SSD pathophysiology and suggest that BBB leakage may define a mechanistically relevant subtype with implications for targeted, personalized interventions.
