Introduction

Rasmussen syndrome (RS) is a rare neurological disease characterised by the progressive atrophy of one cerebral hemisphere.1 Age at onset varies, but RS is more common and severe in children.2 Despite immunotherapy, disease progression often continues, causing refractory seizures, hemiparesis and cognitive decline.2 Hemispherotomy can halt seizures and disease progression but guarantees hemiparesis and hemianopia, with risk of further cognitive deficit. Language fMRI is often included in the pre-surgical planning for RS (and other epilepsies) in order to map language function and avoid resection of eloquent cortex.

In focal epilepsy, atypical language lateralisation (right-sided/bilateral) is reported at a higher incidence compared to typical development, suggesting a process of reorganisation in response to disease.3,4 Having atypical handedness (i.e. left-handed/ambidextrous) has been shown to predict atypical language lateralisation in these cohorts.4 Clinical observations in RS of increased use of the previously non-dominant hand may demonstrate an atypical handedness switch over disease course, possibly linked to epilepsia partialis continua affecting the dominant hand.2

Both the progression and the scale of the disease in RS provide a unique opportunity to study the process of reorganisation over time in a developing brain. Here, we present a longitudinal and cross-sectional retrospective analysis of the pre-surgical language lateralisation in all children with RS who underwent language fMRI at a single-centre in the last 20 years. We explored the effects of atrophy side, disease duration and handedness at scan on the lateralisation of language. We hypothesise that the side of atrophy influences language lateralisation and that longer disease durations and changes in handedness are associated with atypical language lateralisation in this cohort. Currently, there is no published study on a pre-surgical fMRI cohort of this size in RS.

Methods

36 children diagnosed with RS who underwent clinical language fMRI as a part of pre-surgical evaluation at Great Ormond Street Hospital, London, UK, were included in this study. Some children had multiple fMRI acquisitions and so longitudinal data is also considered in this analysis. FMRI and clinical data (atrophy side, disease duration and handedness at scan) were extracted from existing databases and patient notes. Clinical fMRI protocol varied throughout the time period studied (2006 – 2025) but all children included in this analysis completed a covert verb generation run (task: silently generate a verb in response to a noun; contrast: white noise) in a 1.5T (Avanto) or 3T (Prisma) scanner. A T1-weighted structural MRI scan was acquired contemporaneously for each patient’s run. FMRI data was pre-processed in SPM12 (Wellcome Centre for Human Neuroimaging at University College London, https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/doc/).

Laterality indices (LIs) were calculated using the LI toolbox for activity within our masked region of interest (ROI).5 LI values are on a scale from -1 to 1, from complete right hemisphere lateralisation to complete left hemisphere lateralisation. Values from -0.2 to 0.2 typically denote bilaterality. We selected an ROI that included an extended Broca’s area, to allow for variations in anatomy caused by atrophic changes in the affected hemisphere of many RS patients. This mask was devised from normalised typically developing paediatric brains to match the age of our cohort, as visualised in Figure 1A.  

Two linear mixed effects models were employed to explore the effect of disease duration on language lateralisation in RS. Fixed effects of our first model were disease duration, atrophy side (left vs. right) and an interaction of atrophy side and disease duration, with subject as a random effect to account for multiple assessments. We then employed a second linear mixed-effects model in the left-sided atrophy group to include handedness switch as a factor in our analysis, as preliminary analysis suggested handedness switch was predominantly featured in left- over right-sided RS cases. Fixed effects in this second analysis were set as disease duration and handedness switch (yes vs. no), with subject again as a random effect.

Results

We performed a cross-sectional and longitudinal retrospective analysis of the language lateralisation of 36 children with a diagnosis of RS (9 right-sided, 27 left-sided) who underwent pre-surgical language fMRI of sufficient quality at Great Ormond Street Hospital. Patients had a mean age of 12.9 years (range 6.5 – 17.7) at fMRI acquisition (longitudinal patients averaged across timepoints), and were majority female (n = 22 females, 61%). Twelve patients had multiple fMRI scans, totalling 53 fMRI acquisitions. 17/36 patients (47%) showed atypical language lateralisation (bilateral/right-hemisphere dominance) on their final scan, all of whom had left-sided RS (see Fig. 1C for an example patient). All patients in this RS cohort who switched their handedness (n = 11) had left-sided RS, a previous history of right-handedness and were left-handed or ambidextrous at the time of fMRI. Eight of the 11 who switched their handedness also showed atypical language lateralisation.

Visual inspection of residual plots (for both our two models) suggested homoscedasticity and normality in our data, and tests of multicollinearity showed low correlation between our variables.

Our analysis revealed a significant effect of atrophy side on lateralisation index (β = 0.42, SE = 0.18, t = 2.32, p = 0.028), which suggests that those with right-sided RS were more left-lateralised for language than those with left-sided RS (Fig. 2). We found no significant interaction effect between atrophy side and disease duration, suggesting that the progressive nature of the disease does not necessarily predict a change in language reorganisation in those with left-sided RS. 

We performed a second linear mixed-effects model in just our left-sided RS group (N = 27, timepoints = 43) to include handedness switch as a factor in our analysis. This model found a significant effect of handedness switch on language lateralisation (β = -0.60, SE = 0.15, t = -4.10, p < 0.001), suggesting that those who switch their handedness (from right to left/ambidextrous) are more likely to have more atypical language lateralisation (bilateral/right-sided) (Fig. 2). As with the previous model, we found no effect of disease duration on language lateralisation.

Conclusion

Here we report the impact of RS, a disease of progressive atrophy of one cerebral hemisphere on language lateralisation in previously normally developing children. As expected, we found an increased prevalence of atypical language lateralisation (bilateral/right-sided dominance) in this RS cohort (17/36, 47%) compared to that previously reported in a typically developing population, and similar to that seen in other focal epilepsy cohorts.3,4

Our results show a complete adherence to left-sided language dominance in our right-sided RS group. This finding is consistent with the assumption that these patients may have already developed with a dominant left hemisphere for language and remain as such in the presence of right-sided atrophy. In contrast, in our left-sided RS group, we report atypical (bilateral/right-hemisphere) language dominance (compared to typical language dominance of the left hemisphere) in 17/27 patients (63%), suggestive of reorganised function. We also report that those who have a switch in handedness during the disease course are significantly more likely to show an atypical language lateralisation, which suggests a link between hand motor and language dominance, such that the mechanisms by which one dominance shifts in the presence of hemispheric disease may be followed in the other. Interestingly, in our left-sided RS group, the prevalence of atypical language lateralisation (63%) and atypical handedness (41%) is higher than that reported in other left-sided focal epilepsy cohorts (34% atypical language lateralisation, 22% atypical handedness).4 This suggests the scale of the disease (hemispheric) and mechanism of pathology (atrophy) in RS is more disruptive to motor and language networks than that of focal epilepsy and therefore provide a greater push towards a reorganisation of function.

However, there are those cases with left-sided RS in our cohort that remained left-dominant for language, despite years of disease (see Fig. 1B for an example patient). Reasons for these divergent groups (those that reorganise language and those that do not) remain unknown, but this would explain our lack of evidence of disease duration as a predictive factor in language lateralisation shift. Planned work will continue to investigate this rare cohort, to explore whether there are other clinical or structural markers that differentiate between reorganised and non-reorganised patients in RS, as well as expanding our scope to characterise the pre- and post-surgical neuropsychological consequences of this disease.
