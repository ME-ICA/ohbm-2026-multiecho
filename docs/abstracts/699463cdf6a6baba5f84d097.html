Introduction

Unsupervised anomaly detection (UAD) methods based on variational autoencoders (VAEs) are used to detect rare events, such as lesions in medical images, by learning a model on healthy data only. The model is trained on healthy data to learn the healthy distribution. At inference, given an image (which may be abnormal), we assume the model reconstructs the image as if it were healthy, and the difference between the input image and its pseudo-healthy reconstruction is used to obtain an anomaly map.

Defining a proper criterion to select methods and their hyper-parameters in UAD remains an open question. In VAE reconstruction-based methods, models are often selected based on the reconstruction error of healthy inputs. However, recent work shows that models selected at the beginning of the training procedure detect anomalies better than fully trained models, despite a lower reconstruction quality1. But this loss in reconstruction quality is detrimental to the interpretability of the anomaly maps as the pseudo-healthy reconstructions may look quite different from patient data. This observed trade-off reveals a misalignment between the auxiliary task used for training models (healthy reconstruction) and the inference task (anomaly detection). Adding to the complexity of model selection, recent work has revealed that detection performance can exhibit large variability depending on the random seed.

Motivated by these observations, we establish key criteria for voxel-level UAD methods: 

  1. high-quality reconstruction for interpretability, 
  2. strong anomaly detection performance, and 
  3. robustness to seed variation.

We focus on 𝛽-VAE models and study  the performance variability across different seeds through an analysis of the latent space, and show that the models that least separate normal and abnormal images in the latent space produce the best anomaly maps. From this analysis, we propose a maximum-a-posteriori method to optimise the latent embeddings. 

Methods

We use a dataset of 3D FDG PET scans from ADNI that are co-registered, averaged, and uniformized to the same resolution. We pre-process the images using the pet-linear pipeline from Clinica, which performs affine registration to the MNI space, cropping and intensity normalization using the average PET uptake in the cerebellum and pons regions.

We focus on the 𝛽-VAE model as a recent benchmark demonstrated its good performance for detecting dementia-related lesions in FDG PET images2. We denote by x̂ the VAE-reconstruction of an image x. 

We evaluate our model's ability to reconstruct pseudo-healthy images from abnormal samples. Since ground truth pairs of abnormal and healthy images are not available, we use a simulation framework to simulate realistic hypometabolism characteristic of Alzheimer's Disease (AD) with increasing levels of severity (denoted by AD-30 and AD-50)3. We denote by x’=x+ε the simulated abnormal version for a healthy image x. 

We design the anomaly maps as the residual between the input image x and its pseudo-healthy reconstruction given by the VAE x̂, i.e. r = x - x̂, which we normalize using the mean (μ) and standard deviation (σ) of residuals obtained on a healthy validation set, i.e. r̃ = (r - μ) / σ. 

We define the following metrics:

  • Healthy-to-healthy MSE (hhMSE): the MSE between a healthy input x and its reconstruction x̂.

  • Abnormal-to-healthy MSE (ahMSE): the MSE between the pseudo-healthy reconstruction obtained from a simulated abnormal input x̂’ and its ground truth healthy counterpart x.

  • Ratio MSE (rMSE = ahMSE / hhMSE): ratio between the abnormal-to-healthy MSE (ahMSE) and the healthy-to-healthy MSE (hhMSE), which measures the reconstruction healthiness. We want this ratio to be close to 1.

  • Voxelwise average precision (AP): computed between the ground-truth anomaly mask ε and the absolute value of the anomaly map |r|. 

Latent space analysis

First, we study the relative position between embeddings of abnormal samples and embeddings of normal samples in the latent space. We model the latent embeddings distributions with multivariate Gaussian distributions and we compare them using the 2-Wasserstein distance. More specifically, we compare the latent distributions of healthy test samples and simulated AD-30 and AD-50 samples. To account for normal variability, we divide by the distance between the healthy training and validation distributions. Our results of this analysis (detailed in the next section) confirm that there is a detrimental offset of the embeddings of abnormal samples compared to the healthy latent distribution.

Latent maximum-a-posteriori approach

From this latent analysis, we hypothesize that the embedding given by the encoder of the abnormal image may not be the best embedding to generate its pseudo-healthy reconstruction. We propose to use a maximum-a-posteriori (MAP) optimization to find the best latent embedding within the healthy latent distribution. First we model the latent healthy distribution using the training set embeddings of a trained VAE model. Then we perform an optimization of the latent embedding in order to maximize (1) its probability under the healthy distribution, and (2) a data attachment term to the input image to remain subject-specific. More specifically, we use a Total Variation loss between the input image and its pseudo healthy reconstruction.

To model the healthy latent distribution, we use either Gaussian mixture distributions or distributions parameterized with Masked Autoregressive Normalizing flows4. We fit the distributions on the training embeddings and select the one that maximizes the likelihood of the validation embeddings.

The proposed method is similar to another MAP approach which performs an optimisation on x̂ directly rather than on the latent embedding5. We compare this method (restoration-VAE) to our approach. 

Results

Latent space analysis

We train 30 models of 𝛽-VAE (with latent dimension 64 and 𝛽=10) and report the Wasserstein distance and the healthiness ratio rMSE (which characterizes the quality of the pseudo-healthy reconstructions). We see from Figure 2 that: 

(1) there is a lot of variability depending on the random seed, 

(2) for all models, there is an important distance between healthy and abnormal latent distributions (at minimum 10 times the distance between the healthy training distribution and the healthy validation distribution), and 

(3) the models which least separate the normal and abnormal latent distributions reach better performance. 

This confirms our hypothesis and justifies our approach to optimize the latent codes to reconstruct better pseudo-healthy reconstructions.

Latent maximum-a-posteriori approach

Our results demonstrate that improved latent embeddings can substantially enhance pseudo-healthy reconstruction quality and downstream detection performance. 

Initial MAP-based results support this trend, achieving improved reconstruction compared to the typical 𝛽-VAE approach on AD-30 (ahMSE: 0.0011 vs. 0.0016) and comparable performance on AD-50 (ahMSE: 0.0018), while consistently improving AP on both AD-30 (0.67 vs. 0.55) and AD-50 (0.89 vs. 0.84).

Compared to the restoration-VAE baseline5, which performs the optimisation directly in the image space, our method achieves comparable or better reconstruction quality and consistently higher AP. On AD-30, we match ahMSE (0.0011) while substantially outperforming in AP (0.57). On AD-50, despite slightly higher ahMSE (0.0014), we still obtain superior AP (0.82).

Conclusion

We analyzed the performance variability across different random seeds and found it to be strongly linked with the distance between healthy and abnormal latent distributions. This observation confirms the presence of a detrimental offset in abnormal latent embeddings relative to healthy ones, which our approach aims to reduce. Motivated by these findings, we investigate maximum-a-posteriori strategies to optimize the latent embeddings of abnormal images, leading to improved pseudo-healthy reconstructions. 

Future work will focus on further improving the restoration process by exploring alternative approaches that better preserve subject-specificity in the latent space, and consequently in the pseudo-healthy reconstructions. This includes investigating reconstruction metrics beyond Total Variation, as well as enriching the prior with subject-specific information or additional imaging modalities. We also plan to better optimize the weighting parameter λ balancing reconstruction error and prior regularization, and to further refine the optimization procedure itself through learning-rate tuning and the use of appropriate scheduling strategies.
