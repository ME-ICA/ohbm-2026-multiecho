Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a heterogeneous condition involving affective, cognitive, and biological alterations that extend beyond mood symptoms. MDD is associated with disruptions of large-scale brain networks supporting emotional regulation, cognitive integration, and interhemispheric communication, with white matter (WM) abnormalities reported across diffusion MRI and ENIGMA studies1, 2. However, linking these network-level structural alterations to clinical and biological phenotypes remains a challenge.

Diffusion MRI tractometry enables quantification of microstructural properties along anatomically defined WM bundles, providing an interpretable framework for examining network-specific alterations and integrating imaging findings with detailed clinical phenotyping. Beyond neuroimaging, multidomain assessments incorporating cognitive testing, mental health measures, and peripheral blood biomarkers offer complementary insight into the biological correlates of MDD. Plasma biomarkers indexing amyloid processing, tau phosphorylation, axonal injury, and glial reactivity are particularly relevant in late-life depression, where affective symptoms may overlap with age-related neurobiological changes3.

In this study, we applied diffusion MRI tractometry together with multidomain cognitive assessments and plasma biomarkers to investigate tract-specific WM alterations in MDD. Using fiber density and cross-section metrics to capture microstructural and macrostructural bundle properties4, we hypothesized selective alterations in commissural and association pathways supporting posterior and interhemispheric network integration.

Methods

We collected clinical and neuroimaging data from two partially overlapping cohorts: a clinically well-characterized subgroup with complete multidomain assessments and plasma biomarker data (22 MDD, 41 healthy controls [HC]) and a larger imaging cohort including all participants with usable diffusion data (46 MDD, 41 HC).

Multidomain phenotyping encompassed mental health, cognition, and peripheral biomarkers. Depression and anxiety severity were assessed using validated self-report and clinician-rated scales (Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale; Hamilton Depression and Anxiety Rating Scales). Cognitive function was evaluated using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment for global cognition and a comprehensive neuropsychological battery assessing memory, executive function, processing speed, working memory, semantic fluency, and facial memory. Plasma biomarkers were quantified using ultrasensitive immunomagnetic reduction assays, measuring β-amyloid species (Aβ42, Aβ40), total tau, phosphorylated tau-181 (p-Tau181), neurofilament light chain (NfL), and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), following established protocols3.

Diffusion MRI was acquired on a 3T Siemens Skyra scanner using a multi-shell protocol (30 and 64 directions at b = 1500 and 3000 s/mm², respectively; 2 b=0 volumes; 2-mm isotropic). Preprocessing followed the MRtrix3 pipeline, including denoising, motion and distortion correction, bias-field correction, and multi-shell multi-tissue constrained spherical deconvolution. Fiber orientation distributions were registered to a study-specific population template, and fixel-wise fiber density and cross-section (FDC) metrics were computed4. White matter bundles were segmented into 72 tracts using TractSeg5, and tract-averaged FDC was extracted by projecting fixel metrics onto tract-specific streamlines. Group differences were assessed using parametric or non-parametric tests as appropriate, with effect sizes quantified using Cohen’s d.

Results

Demographic, cognitive, mental health, and biomarker characteristics are summarized in Table 1. MDD and HC groups were well matched for age, education, and global cognitive performance. As expected, individuals with MDD exhibited significantly greater depression and anxiety severity and higher subjective cognitive complaints. Objective cognitive differences were selective, with reduced category fluency in MDD, while measures of global cognition, processing speed, and working memory did not differ between groups.

Plasma biomarkers showed no group differences in amyloid measures (Aβ42, Aβ40), total tau, NfL, or GFAP, indicating the absence of overt neurodegenerative pathology. However, p-Tau181 and the p-Tau181/Aβ42 ratio were significantly lower in MDD (Table 1).

Diffusion MRI tractometry revealed a significant reduction in FDC in the splenium of the corpus callosum in MDD compared with HC (p=0.023, Cohen’s d=−0.50). This effect localized to posterior interhemispheric fibers connecting parietal, temporal, and occipital cortices. No other tracts reached statistical significance, although some association and commissural pathways exhibited medium, subthreshold effect sizes.

Conclusion

The selective reduction in splenial FDC indicates compromised white matter architecture within posterior interhemispheric pathways in MDD. This finding is consistent with large-scale reports implicating callosal and posterior WM abnormalities in depression1, 2. Notably, our results emerged in a clinically well-characterized cohort without differences in age, education, or global cognition and in the absence of widespread neurodegenerative biomarker abnormalities, suggesting that posterior callosal alterations are unlikely to be driven by co-morbid dementia or generalized neurodegeneration. The largely preserved plasma biomarker profile supports this interpretation. Although the reductions in p-Tau181 may reflect subtle neurobiological stress or altered tau-related processes associated with late-life depression3, they do not constitute an Alzheimer-type biomarker signature. By integrating diffusion MRI tractometry with multidomain cognitive and molecular characterization, our study identifies posterior callosal alterations as a potential structural substrate of depressive psychopathology.
