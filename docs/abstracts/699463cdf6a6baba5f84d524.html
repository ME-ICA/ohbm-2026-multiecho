Introduction

Machiavellianism is a personality construct used to characterize individuals who use manipulation against other people's interests to achieve one's own personal goals1. Machiavellianism is one of the Dark Triad personalities, which has been related to detrimental effects, including unethical behaviors, mental health problems, damaged interpersonal relationships, and impeded performance and effectiveness. Despite the development of various Machiavellianism scales encompassing different factors, overlaps and distinctions among these factors obscure understanding of the conceptualization and dimensionality of Machiavellianism. 

Personality neuroscience aims to map neural correlates to personality traits, with the hope of helping “test key hypotheses from trait theory and ultimately identify such neural trait systems” 2. Although scattered studies indicate the neural foundations of Machiavellianism3,4,5. The neural signatures of Machiavellianism subdimensions remain largely unknown.

Regarding the inconsistent dimensionality of existing scales and the unclear neural underpinnings of processing Machiavellianism dimensions, the current research aims to delineate the dimensionality of Machiavellianism by delving into its psychometric and neurocognitive characteristics.

Methods

In Study 1, we provided a comprehensive yet concise model of the dimensionality of Machiavellianism via a psychometric approach. We recruited 834 Chinese participants from Credamo (a Chinese online survey platform), and required them to complete four Machiavellian scales (97 items in total) that are most popular in existing literature. We first conducted an explorative factor analysis (EFA) to identify the number of factors; then, item analysis was performed to produce a short version of the Machiavellianism scale, and an EFA was reconducted to examine the item loading. Finally, we conducted a confirmatory factor analysis to examine the structure of the short version scale using an independent sample from Prolific (n = 495).

In Study 2, we uncovered the neural correlates of each Machiavellian dimension when participants processed dimension-specific information using a task-based fMRI. We recruited 88 participants (average age of 23.55 years old; 56 females) and asked them to rate 97 Machiavellian statements (same as Study 1). Regarding the referent condition, we operationalized high- and low-Machiavellians, as well as baseline. We chose two prototypical high- and low-machiavellians, Cao Cao and Guan Yu, well-known to Chinese people; self serves as the baseline. To sum up, participants were asked to rate 97 Machiavellian statements for each referent, leading to 291 items in total. To ensure the generalizability of our results, we compared high- versus low-degree of Machiavellian, as well as self versus other referents. 

Functional images were taken using a multiband (4 factors) echo-planar gradient sequence (TR, 1000 ms; echo time, 29 ms; flip angle, 60°; field of view, 192 × 192 mm; axial slices, 60; voxel size, 2.2 × 2.2 × 2.2 mm). The slices were collected using an interleaved ordering protocol. We preprocessed the fMRI data using SPM12. The first ten seconds of each run were discarded. Functional images underwent slice timing correction to account for differences in acquisition timing, followed by spatial realignment to the first volume and unwarping to correct for nonlinear distortions caused by head motion or magnetic field inhomogeneities. The functional images were coregistered to the individual’s structural image. The functional images were then normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space, with interpolation to a voxel size of 2 × 2 × 2 mm, using the forward deformation parameters derived from the segmentation process. Finally, the images underwent spatial smoothing with a 6-mm full-width at half maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel. 

A GLM was constructed for each participant with 15 task-related regressors and six motion-related regressors. For the ‘evaluating for self’ condition, we time-locked the onset to the presentation of the statement and with the statement presentation and grouped statements classified under a specific dimension from Study 1 into a single regressor. To control for motion artifacts, six motion correction parameters were included as covariates of no interest, along with a constant term per session. To pinpoint brain activity triggered by a specific dimension, we compare the target dimension against other dimensions across the different referent conditions: self, Cao Cao, and Guan Yu. We reported results surviving after a voxel-level height threshold at p < .001 and cluster-level family-wise error (FWE) correction at p < .05. 

In Study 3, we employed a connectome-based predictive modelling (CPM) to reveal whether and how resting-state functional connectivity predicts specific Machiavellianism dimensions. We employed 106 healthy right-hand participants (73 females; mean age = 24 years), who underwent an 8-minutes resting-state scan and finished the same Machiavellianism scales as Studies 1 and 2. The scan parameter, preprocessing, and measurement were the same as Study 2. 

Results

In Study 1, the EFA results indicated five Machiavellianism dimensions, including Desire for status, Amoral manipulation, Distrust of others, Planfulness, and Agency. By using CFA to compare the five-factor model with other alternative models (i.e., a null model, a common factor model, a hierarchical five-factor model), we found that the five-factor model has the best fit. The short version of the five-factor Machiavellianism scale showed acceptable reliability, and convergent, divergent, and criterion validity.

In Study 2, univariate analyses showed that the Status dimension correlated with increased activity in the superior temporal gyrus; the Distrust dimension with the middle temporal gyrus; the Amorality dimension with the dorsomedial prefrontal cortex and striatum; the Agency dimension with the insula, inferior parietal gyrus, and dorsomedial prefrontal cortex; and the Planfulness dimension with the orbitofrontal area and inferior frontal gyrus. Our findings confirmed that each Machiavellianism dimension is distinct by recruiting specific neural signatures. 

Study 3 revealed that the right TPJ and limbic-motor networks negatively predict Status, whereas rTPJ, mPFC, and default networks positively predict Status. Distrust was negatively predicted by the cerebellum-mPFC and cerebellum-limbic networks. We did not find neural networks that contributed to the prediction of individuals’ self-ratings in Amorality, Agency, and Planfulness.

Conclusion

Our research builds on existing scales to develop a comprehensive five-factor model of Machiavellianism with robust validity, resolving inconsistencies in its dimensionality and providing a coherent framework and practical tool for personality research. Task-based fMRI delineates the neural organization of these dimensions, offering biological support. Task-free fMRI further shows that status-seeking and distrust can be predicted from intrinsic connectivity, suggesting that spontaneous brain states may predispose some aspects of personality. Methodologically, our interdisciplinary approach showcases how neuroscience may inform social science.

Practically, given the links between Machiavellianism and mental disorders and disruptive behavior, our findings may have implications for intervention strategies.
