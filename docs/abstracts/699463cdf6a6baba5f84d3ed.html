Introduction

Artifact removal in BOLD signals is an important technique in fMRI studies, and it is particularly true for model-free, data-driven functional connectivity analysis. To effectively perform the data cleanup, it is important to identify the source and property of the noise components. One of common artifacts is from the participant’s head motion.

Multiple approaches have been proposed to identify and remove the motion artifacts by regression of different noise components, for example, motion regressors from motion correction algorism, noise identified by Independent Component Analysis (like FIX in FSL), white matter signals, corticospinal fluid signals, and the global signal. 1 Lately, the scrubbing, or censoring, method was proposed to remove those data points contaminated with severe motion. 2

Byrge and Kennedy 3 presented a method to quantify the temporally extended components, following the different ranges of framewise displacement (FD). The study verified that the epoch pattern was consistent with multiple popular preprocessing pipelines, and among different participants and different datasets. Since the pattern follows the FD ranges, it is natural for the authors to conclude that the temporally-lagged pattern reflects motion artifacts. However, the observation that the smallest “artifacts” was related to a medium FD range, rather than the minimal FD range, strongly challenges the conclusion.

In the current study, we try to find the source of this consistent patten by using simulated BOLD time series.

Methods

We simulated the BOLD time series by convolving the Hemodynamic Response Function (HRF) with onset of events which happen randomly in time and with random amplitude. The simulation was performed in Matlab (Mathworks), as shown in the following equation:

               y(t) = s(t) * h + ε(t)

where t represents time, y(t) is the simulated timer series, s(t) is the stimulus matrix in dimension of number of time points (time interval is the Repetition Time, or TR) by number of voxels, h is the HRF, * denotes the convolution operation, and ε(t) is the Gaussian noise term. The stimulus matrix s(t) contains mostly zeros, and with random event amplitude at random time points.

We applied the analysis on the simulated time series in a similar way to what was used in Byrge and Kennedy study. 3 Fig. 1 is one of the figures in that study, and we cite it here to explain both the method and the FD-related epoch pattern. The computed mean cortical BOLD signals from resting state scans were z-scored first, and then different epochs were extracted. The epochs whose starting points had similar FD values were averaged and 14 FD ranges were used to compute 14 epochs. A clear pattern between the epochs and the FD ranges could be seen in the figure.

If the FD-related pattern was the reflection of motion artifact, we would expect the minimal motion (the dark blue epoch in Fig. 1) would cause minimal artifacts. However, one of the medium motion ranges produced the close-to-flat epoch (a trace in cyan in Fig. 1).

Our simulated time series was motion free, hence there were no FDs. We computed the root mean square difference of the neighboring time points, in the same way as the DVARS computation in a real fMRI dataset 1 and used the “DVARS” in place of the FDs used in Byrge and Kennedy study. 3

We divided those DVARS into different ranges, and computed the average epochs for each DVARS range, in the identical way to Byrge and Kennedy study. In short, the simulated time series was z-scored, and epochs were repeatedly extracted starting from each time point. For example, we extracted an epoch starting from point 5 and lasting 45 seconds (number of points of the epoch would depend on the choice of TR). This epoch would fall into one of DVARS ranges based on DVARS value at point 5. Then those epochs within a DVARS range would be averaged to generate one epoch corresponding to that DVARS range.

Most parameters in our simulation were flexible, including TR, amplitude of events, HRF shape, etc. This allowed us to test the potential effect of different parameters on the expected epoch pattern.

Results

The main goal of this study was to generate similar epoch patterns to those in the previous study 3 from the simulated data. For this purpose, we used similar parameters to the previous study, including TR=0.72 s, 14 ranges of DVARS, and 45 seconds of epoch. Fig. 2 shows those 14 epochs with event amplitude (or beta) shifted 10% to positive from zero (see detailed explanation later). The figure legend shows those 14 DVARS ranges.

We tried to manipulate multiple parameters in the simulation, including the TR, the number of DVARS ranges, the HRF shape (canonical vs single gamma), and the noise term within a limited range (20%) relate to the signal, and the epochs would change accordingly. However, the epoch pattern for those DVARS ranges always persisted.

This pattern from the simulated data showed great similarity to that in Fig. 1, especially for the epoch segments between 6 and 30 seconds. This strongly suggests that the observed pattern should not be attributed to the motion artifact, but is, at least partially, the intrinsic property of the BOLD signal.

Among the parameters in the simulation, the sample of the event amplitude turned out to be critical for the observed pattern. Although both commonly used uniform and normal distributions would generate similar epoch pattern to that shown in Fig. 2, the mean amplitude was the key parameter. If the mean amplitude was positive, the observed pattern would show up. If the mean amplitude was zero, the pattern would disappear, and the epochs would overlap with each other, with minor fluctuations. As expected, if the mean amplitude was negative, the pattern would flip upside down (no figure is shown due to the limited number of figures).

This result from our limited simulation strongly suggests that the observed epoch pattern is a result of the neuronal activity with positive or positive dominant responses.

There is a major difference between the real BOLD and our simulated BOLD. In real BOLD, the magnitude of motion can be quantified by FD during motion correction, or by DVARS, 1 the temporal derivative of time series. FD and DVARS are similar, especially for relatively large motion 1. In our simulated motion-free data, there is neither FD nor DVARS from motion, so the DVARS we used here are purely the temporal derivative of signals. Here raises the question: how do we explain the pattern related to FD in the real BOLD?

In the real BOLD, FD is computed from 6 motion parameters (3 translations and 3 rotations) from motion correction algorithm. One may think that FD is a pure reflection of motion and does nothing to do with signals. This is not true. The reason is that motion correction algorithm tries to align the two volumes by minimizing the difference between the two volumes. In other words, the algorithm assumes that any difference between the two volumes is due to motion. This assumption is of course wrong in theory, due to the neuronal response over time, but in practice it has minimal consequences since the neuronal response is a tiny fraction of the BOLD signal. Both FD and DVARS from the real BOLD may contain not only motion information, but also information from the neuronal signal, although it may be only a tiny fraction. We explain the epoch pattern observed in the real BOLD as a reflection of this tiny fraction of signal after many epochs within the similar FD range are averaged.

Since the epochs are extracted from the mean BOLD signal, it makes sense that, if the global signal regression (GSR) is performed, the pattern will be attenuated or removed. 3 In the current study, we found the pattern contains, at least partially, the neuronal signal. The GSR, which attenuates or removes the epoch pattern, will result in signal loss. Although our simulation result does not provide sufficient evidence to recommend against GSR, we strongly recommend researchers to consider the benefits and costs of GSR, a controversial factor mentioned in other literatures. 4-5

Conclusion

Our study indicates that the pattern for temporarily-lagged epochs exists in the simulated BOLD data, so the pattern is, at least partially, an intrinsic property of the BOLD signal.

Any method aiming to remove this pattern may risk removing signal. The global signal regression, while effective to remove artifacts, will attenuate or remove this patten, and should be used with care.
