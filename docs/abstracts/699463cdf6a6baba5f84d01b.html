Introduction

How do large-scale brain networks interact to enable cognition? Correlated oscillations, a mechanism for inter-areal interactions, can be expressed as phase coherence or amplitude co-fluctuations. While considerable work has investigated the functional roles of phase coherence1, little is known about whether and how the coincidence of high-amplitude oscillations across the cortex can impact cognition. What has been shown in recent years is that time-averaged amplitude coupling networks are linked to perception, memory, and decision-making and dissociated from phase coupling in task, rest and disease. Further, the coupling mode is dynamically modulated at low frequencies (< 0.1 Hz), influenced by neuromodulatory systems but also displays high-amplitude transient activity. However, what dynamic features of large-scale activity co-fluctuations can aid efficient cognition and behavior?

Recent studies resolving coupling modes dynamically over time can help to elucidate the role of amplitude co-fluctuations in cognition. It has been shown that transient high-amplitude events interfuse sustained bandlimited neuronal activity and could aid inter-areal interactions2,3. Due to time-varying event-rates, transient high-amplitude activity can be mistaken for sustained activity. Moreover, high-amplitude events can originate from both, oscillatory or aperiodic transients. High-amplitude oscillations reflect rhythmic excitability fluctuations without necessitating firing rate modulations, and display narrow-band spectral power increases. Yet, aperiodic activity, another meaningful feature of healthy brain function, is linked to time varying firing rate fluctuations that, too, can display transient increases in filtered neuronal activity4. Taken together, transiently strong oscillatory- and aperiodic- as well as sustained low-amplitude activity provide potentially distinct information channels and time-scales for neuronal communication and efficient cognition.

Methods

We recorded MEG from twenty participants (9 female, agemean = 28.05 years ± ageSTD 4.6 years) to non-invasively assess high-amplitude events in the healthy human brain. During the recording the participants performed a challenging three-alternative decision-making task comparing the contrast of three Gabor patches (medianaccuracy = 77%, rangeaccuracy = 66–86%) presented at different peripheral locations equidistant from fixation. The experiment was approved by the ethics committee of the Hamburg Medical Association and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants gave their written informed consent and received monetary compensation for their participation.

We defined functional coupling dynamically, i.e. time-resolved for each sample (sampling rate = 400Hz), via coinciding events as well as instantaneous amplitude correlations. We applied oscillatory and aperiodic high-amplitude event detection using instantaneous frequency stability on source-reconstructed (individual head models from T1-weighted MRI, DICS spatial beamforming) cortical activity over a broad range of frequencies (2–64 Hz, quarter octave steps, Morlet's wavelets). To discount volume conduction effects we applied signal orthogonalization.

Further, we trained for each participant inverted encoding models (IEM) on a separate functional localizer task to decode the momentary focus of attention during decision-making. We used the IEM decoding to track brief changes of covert visual-spatial attention, i.e., attentional saccades, that can occur at any time during the trial. Hereby, covert attention either reallocated between (switch) or refocused on the same (stay) decision alternative5.

We hypothesized that coinciding high-amplitude events, i.e., oscillatory and aperiodic, enable the rapid reconfiguration of cortical networks required for flexible behavior. To test this hypothesis, we leveraged the spatio-temporally precise magnetoencephalography (MEG) recordings with whole-brain coverage to comprehensively unravel sustained and transient components of cortical activity during the challenging cognitive task. We tested dynamic coupling differences related to decision-making behavior (correct vs. error trials) and around attention reallocation between decision alternatives. Specifically, we utilized transient high-amplitude events, i.e. oscillatory or aperiodic, coinciding in distant areas to define functional networks and contrast our findings to sustained amplitude co-fluctuations.

Results

We validated the novel functional coupling approach in three complementary ways. First, we showed that local high-amplitude oscillatory event-rates overlapped well with previously reported cortical generators of oscillatory activity. Second, we demonstrated that time-averaged networks of amplitude coupling components were reliable and, particularly, that transient oscillatory networks predicted previously reported amplitude coupling features. Third, we established that amplitude coupling network components were reliably and dynamically modulated during the task. We then moved on to relate the different coupling measures to different aspects of cognition.

First, our results show that transiently increased oscillatory network activity can be predictive of behavior. Transient high-amplitude oscillatory networks were modulated up to 1.5 seconds before a correct choice was made. Coincident high-amplitude oscillatory event rates increased particularly in the alpha and beta range (8–23 Hz) when participants made a correct choice. An extended network of behaviorally relevant coupling hereby spanned the medial- and lateral parietal as well as lateral prefrontal areas and the temporo-parietal junction. This network appeared to be stable over time and was quantitatively up-/down-regulated throughout the decision phase.

Second,  our findings further demonstrate that decision-related functional networks dissociated temporally, spatially, spectrally and in magnitude between transient (events) and sustained (event-free) amplitude coupling modes. Consequently, particularly transient oscillatory amplitude coupling components might echo large-scale interactions related to decision behavior.

Third, our result showcased that attention reallocation modulated transient oscillatory amplitude coupling. Transient oscillatory event coincidences were amplified in an extended visual, medial parietal and lateral prefrontal network predominantly in the theta and alpha frequency range at the moment of attention reallocation. The coupling effects for attention refocusing were generally weaker. Further, by excluding high-amplitude events, sustained amplitude coupling displayed little attentional modulation. These findings further underline the functional, spatial and spectral dissociation between transient and sustained neuronal dynamics and their putative roles in large-scale cortical interactions during cognition.

Conclusion

The exchange and integration of information over long distances within the brain is a prerequisite of flexible cognitive processing. Precise temporal relationships of neuronal activity, i.e., phase coupling, between distant areas has been associated with fostering and parsing this communication. However, little has been known about the functional role of co-fluctuations of local activity, i.e., amplitude coupling, in cognition and long-range communication. Leveraging the rich spatio-temporal resolution of non-invasive electrophysiological measurements we here demonstrated that transient oscillatory dynamics are a central hallmark of large-scale neuronal interactions. We established that brief events of high-amplitude oscillatory activity govern the network architecture during cognition. Hereby, high-amplitude oscillatory network activity was relevant to decision behavior, indicative of better performance. Further, networks of coincident oscillatory events were transiently modulated during attention allocation. Sustained low-amplitude co-fluctuations, on the other hand, showed a weaker modulation with cognition while displaying compatible reliability. Moreover, the associated networks supporting cognition were spatially and spectrally distinct between sustained and transient oscillatory coupling components. We, thus, argue that not only sustained activity but particularly the orchestration of transient oscillatory events can facilitate inter-areal coupling. Our findings, thus, provide a novel framework integrating dynamic large-scale amplitude co-fluctuations with neuronal circuit mechanisms.

Moreover, our study showed that amplitude coupling is a dynamically rich intrinsic coupling mode that is dominated by the coincidence of transiently high oscillations. By identifying high-amplitude oscillatory events coinciding over the cortex we could reliably explain previously reported amplitude coupling networks in the delta, theta, alpha and beta frequency ranges with only a fraction of the data. Importantly, we highlighted that not every high-amplitude event is oscillatory and that transient aperiodic activity might itself be a distinct feature of neuronal communication. Additionally, sustained amplitude coupling displayed reliable yet dissociable network features. Averaging neuronal activity and functional coupling over extended time periods might thus lump together various informative and dissociable processes. Particularly during cognition sustained and transient coupling components might cater to qualitatively distinct functions and information processing time scales. Our results highlight that efficient cognition and behavior might rely on information channels forming through co-occurring transient oscillations. We, thus, propose that networks of coincident oscillatory events are a key signature of inter-areal interactions during cognition.
