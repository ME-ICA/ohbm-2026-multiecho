Introduction

Accurate left–right orientation is a fundamental assumption in neuroimaging, yet violations of this assumption remain difficult to detect.^1 Left–right orientation errors commonly arise from incorrect or missing header information during image acquisition or DICOM-to-NIfTI conversion. Because of the brain’s approximate bilateral symmetry, left–right flips are often not apparent on visual inspection and can therefore propagate unnoticed into downstream analyses. Brain asymmetry is a core organizational feature of the human brain and underlies functional specialization, neurodevelopmental processes, and vulnerability to neurological and psychiatric disorders.^2 Alterations in structural, microstructural, and functional asymmetry are increasingly studied as sensitive biomarkers neurodevelopment, aging and diverse psychiatric disorders.^3 Consequently, even a small number of misoriented images can systematically bias asymmetry measures and distort group-level inferences. Despite these risks, left–right orientation checks are not routinely incorporated into large-scale neuroimaging pipelines. Here, we apply an automated, alignment-based left–right consistency check ^1 to multimodal brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans from the UK Biobank, in order to demonstrate the necessity and scalability of incorporating left–right flip detection as a standard quality control step, particularly for large-scale neuroimaging studies.

Methods

This study used 37,690 T1-weighted structural MRI (T1w) scans along with their corresponding diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI, N = 37,278) and resting-state functional MRI (rsfMRI, N = 37,690) scans from the UK Biobank (Application Number 25641). All imaging data were acquired across UK Biobank imaging centers on Siemens Skyra 3T scanners using standardized acquisition protocols.^4 Left–right orientation inconsistency between T1w images and their EPI data (DWI b0 or rsfMRI) were assessed using a volumetric alignment–based flip check implemented in AFNI (align_epi_anat.py -check_flip).^1 The method compares the cost function of alignment between a T1-weighted MRI scan and its corresponding echo-planar imaging (EPI) scan with that obtained when the T1-weighted image is left–right flipped: a lower cost for the flipped (or original) configuration indicates a probable left–right orientation inconsistency, and datasets were accordingly classified as DO_FLIP or NO_FLIP, respectively. Alignment was performed using the LPC+ZZ cost function, in which local Pearson correlation (LPC) measures patch-wise intensity correspondence across modalities, and the ZZ component provides a robust multi–cost-function initialization to improve alignment stability for multimodal images with differing tissue contrasts.^5 Importantly, this procedure detects relative left–right orientation inconsistency between paired images and cannot determine which modality is absolutely misoriented. Before implementing the left-right flip check, T1w images were skull-stripped and DWI images were corrected for eddy currents, head motion, and 3D gradient distortion in UK Biobank. For DWI, volumes with b < 50 s/mm² were extracted and averaged to generate a mean b0 EPI image for alignment. For rsfMRI, a mean EPI volume was computed across time. In both analyses, T1w images and EPI-derived volumes were deobliqued and cropped to their minimal bounding boxes prior to alignment to remove oblique orientation metadata and excess background, thereby improving alignment robustness and computational efficiency.

Results

A graphical summary of the alignment cost comparisons used for left–right flip detection is presented in Figure 1. For the T1w–DWI analysis (N = 37,278), 37,275 scans (99.99%) were classified as left–right consistent (NO_FLIP), while 3 scans (0.008%) were identified as left–right inconsistent (DO_FLIP). For the T1w–rsfMRI analysis (N = 37,690), 37,665 scans (99.93%) were classified as NO_FLIP, and 25 scans (0.07%) were classified as DO_FLIP. For T1w–DWI, NO_FLIP datasets showed substantially lower alignment costs for the original configuration compared with the flipped configuration (original: −1.38 ± 0.19; flipped: −0.39 ± 0.069). In contrast, DO_FLIP datasets exhibited lower alignment costs for the flipped configuration, but with much smaller cost differences (original: −0.073 ± 0.021; flipped: −0.080 ± 0.014). Similarly, for T1w–rsfMRI, NO_FLIP datasets demonstrated lower alignment costs for the original configuration (original: −0.36 ± 0.080; flipped: −0.19 ± 0.037), whereas DO_FLIP datasets showed lower costs for the flipped configuration with relatively small original–flipped differences (original: −0.11 ± 0.034; flipped: −0.12 ± 0.031). Across both modalities, NO_FLIP cases exhibited large cost differences favoring the original configuration, whereas DO_FLIP cases showed smaller but consistently reversed cost differences. Such subtle magnitude of these differences in DO_FLIP datasets supports existing observations that left–right orientation inconsistencies are difficult to identify through visual quality control alone.^1

Conclusion

By applying an automated left–right flip check at population scale, this study provides empirical evidence that UK Biobank multimodal MRI data exhibit a high level of left–right orientation consistency, reflecting the overall robustness of the UK Biobank imaging acquisition and preprocessing pipelines. At the same time, the presence of a small number of subtle orientation inconsistencies highlights that even well-curated large-scale datasets are not immune to such errors. Our findings therefore support the reliability of UK Biobank imaging for large-scale neuroimaging research, while also emphasizing the value of incorporating automated left–right orientation checks as a routine quality control step. This is particularly relevant for future large-scale neuroimaging studies focused on brain asymmetry and other laterality-sensitive analyses, where undetected orientation inconsistencies, even if rare, could bias downstream inferences.
