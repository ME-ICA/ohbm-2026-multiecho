Introduction

Relief from pain is a fundamental component of affective and motivational behavior and central to effective therapeutic interventions of clinical pain states. However, its neural basis remains poorly understood. Previous neuroimaging research has identified robust brain-based markers of ascending nociceptive processing, such as the Neurologic Pain Signature (NPS)1, and modality-general representations of affective valence, such as the valence model of Lee et al., (2024)2. 

Previous studies propose that pain relief is neither a simple reduction of nociception3 nor equivalent to hedonic reward4 (e.g., induced by chocolate liquid, as in Lee et al., 2024). Therefore, distinct neural circuits may underlie the processing of pain relief, which may not be fully captured by either the NPS or Lee-Valence model.

In the present study, we aimed to construct a multivariate signature from task-based fMRI that captures both transient aggravations and relief from ongoing pain. First, we examined whether the NPS and Lee-Valence model can capture changes in perceived pain and valence during relief from ongoing pain in our paradigm. Then we developed a new multivariate brain signature, the PAin and RElief Signature (PARES), that aims to predict pain rating changes both during pain increase and pain relief. We evaluated the predictive performance of the new signature both for the pain increase and relief conditions via cross-validation and in an external validation using a clinical dataset. Finally, we investigated which neural systems preferentially encode pain increase and relief.

Methods

61 healthy individuals and 58 patients with chronic back pain (CBP) participated in an fMRI pain conditioning paradigm5. Thermal hyperalgesia was induced using capsaicin cream, after which a tonic heat stimulation, which is normally non-painful, produced a moderate yet safe pain experience on the capsaicin pretreated forearms. Next, participants received three cued conditions: pain increase, pain relief, and no-change baseline, with temperatures calibrated individually (Fig. 1a). Participants rated pain intensity and valence via a Visual Analogue Scale.

Individual univariate activation maps in the three conditions were used to predict pain ratings for each neural signature: NPS, Lee-Valence model, and the novel signature PARES developed in this study. Signature responses for the NPS and Lee-Valence model were computed as the dot product between predictive weights and beta-maps. Performance of all signatures was evaluated with identical measures: intra-individual (within-participant) and inter-individual (between-participant) correlations between observed and predicted ratings, and within-participant condition-classification accuracy using paired t-tests. Because PARES is designed to predict the change in pain experience as compared to the tonic pain baseline, for this signature we additionally evaluated how well the sign of the prediction (positive or negative) classifies pain increase and relief conditions at the within-subject level. All signatures were further evaluated in an independent dataset running the identical experimental paradigm in 58 CBP patients. PARES was trained and validated with cross-validation in the first sample with healthy controls. Therefore, the second sample served as an independent external validation sample. Statistical significance of all performance metrics was evaluated via permutation tests.

PARES was trained using Ridge regression with nested leave-one-participant-out cross-validation to predict changes in subjective pain intensity ratings relative to individual tonic pain baseline (Δpain increase, Δpain relief, Δno change) (Fig. 1b). During training, the model blind to condition information. The training procedure provided (regularized) predictive weights for each brain voxel. We identified voxels with significant contributions to the predictions by bootstrapping with 10,000 iterations using the best hyperparameter from training. To obtain accurate p-values, we fitted a generalized Pareto distribution to the extreme tails of bootstrapped weight distributions. We then corrected for multiple comparisons across voxels by computing false discovery rates (FDR). To further interpret multivariate patterns, we computed Haufe-transformed weight maps within each FDR-significant voxel using mass-univariate linear regression models, to quantify the condition-specific contributions (pain increase or relief vs. no change). For visualization purposes, we extracted peak positive and negative Haufe t-values in each FDR-significant cluster (with a cluster extent threshold of KE > 10 voxels) to characterize its pain increase vs. relief response profile in each PARES region and visualized these in a 2-dimensional coordinate system encompassing the pain increase and relief contributions. 

Results

As expected, the NPS response was greater for pain increase as compared to no change (Fig. 2a; t = 9.483, p < .0001, in line with pain intensity ratings) corroborating the vast evidence for the robustness of NPS to detect nociceptive activity. However, no significant difference in the NPS was found between pain relief and no change (t = -1.404, p = .9207). We observed similar results for the Lee-Valence model, with the greater signature response to pain increase than no change (Fig. 2b; t = 2.94, p = .0033, in line with valence ratings) but no significant difference between pain relief and no change (t = -2.80, p = .9972). These results were replicated in the CBP sample, with equivalent results in terms of statistical significance. 

In contrast, the newly developed pain signature, PARES, demonstrated robust performance across all metrics (Fig. 2c). For the internal validation, our model predicted overall Δpain ratings both at the intra- (r = .679, p < .0001) and inter-individual levels (r = .445, p < .0001). Although condition information was unseen during model training, signature response was significantly greater for pain increase (t = 7.255, p = .0001) and significantly lower for relief (t = 2.434, p = .0093), both compared to no change condition. Furthermore, the sole sign of the PARES prediction yielded a significant inter-individual classification accuracy to differentiate between pain increase and pain relief (accuracy: 78.7%, p < .0001), with 75.4% sensitivity, 82.0% specificity and area under the curve 86.0%. The results were replicated in the external validation sample, suggesting the PARES’s robust performance in capturing experimentally induced pain relief.

In the PARES predictive patterns, areas with positive weights indicated increased pain intensity (regardless of condition). These areas primarily overlapped with the NPS patterns, e.g., the insula, dACC, thalamus, S2, PAG and cerebellum. Negative weights were found in the OFC, vmPFC, amygdala, hippocampus, S1/IPL and SPL (Fig. 2d), typically associated with affective and motivational processes and valuation. Within FDR-significant voxels, we estimated condition-specific Haufe-weights and disentangled the clusters by comparing the overall contributions to pain increase and relief (Fig. 2e).

Pain-preferential regions were identified as those contributing more to pain increase than relief. Among regions with increased activation during pain increase were the mINS, S2, thalamus, pINS, cerebellum, dACC and precentral gyrus. Pain-preferential regions showing deactivation included the vmPFC, sgACC, hippocampus, SPL and SMG. These regions substantially overlapped with the NPS patterns, confirming dominant engagement of nociception in pain increase. 

Relief-preferential regions showing activations during pain relief but low responses during pain increase were primarily involved in the frontoparietal network (OFC, IPS, SFG, MTG and MFG), while deactivations were found in the brainstem, M1 and PCC. We found some relief-preferential regions were weak or absent in the NPS and Lee-Valence model patterns, likely implying why neither of them captured relief. These findings suggest that relief is encoded as an intrinsically safe and appetitive signal rather than a mere absence of nociception or equivalence to explicit rewards. 

Finally, we observed several regions expressing opposing yet comparable-magnitude responses between pain increase and relief (S1, IPL and pINS), and activity in these regions is heterogeneous, which is consistent with their roles in somatotopic sensory integration and updating, attention-driven shifts during pain-relief transitions.

Conclusion

Using the heat/capsaicin model of hyperalgesia to experimentally induce pain increase and relief within a unified sensory modality and experimental framework, we (i) confirmed that existing neural signatures of nociceptive processing and gustatory-related hedonic experience do not capture the subjective experience of pain relief; (ii) constructed a novel task-based neural signature of pain that reliably reflects pain perception in both the pain increase and relief conditions and (iii) contrasted the engagement of the involved brain regions in the two conditions. 

Our results with the NPS and Lee-Valance model are in line with our hypotheses that pain relief is more than a simple downscaling of nociception but is also different from gustatory-related hedonic reward (e.g., induced by chocolate liquid, as in Lee et al., 2024) and underscore the need for a multivariate pain signature that also captures processes underlying pain relief. 

Internal and external validation results confirmed that the newly developed signature PARES successfully fills this gap. It not only provides a similar predictive performance to the NPS in the pain increase condition, but extends it to the domain of pain relief. The predictive effect sizes are large in both conditions, albeit somewhat weaker in relief, suggesting that PARES has potential as a novel, robust research tool in basic and translational pain research. Importantly, the sign of the PARES scores can be interpreted as a general indicator for differentiating pain increase from pain relief - a feature that may display high translational utility.

Detailed analysis of the signature weights highlights a coordinated engagement of nociceptive, evaluative, attentional, and affective-motivational systems. Pain-preferential regions aligned with classical nociception, whereas relief-preferential and opponent regions involved areas previously associated with safety signaling, value updating, and context-sensitive sensory integration.

Together, these findings advance our understanding of relief from sustained pain and establish PARES as a promising tool for probing pain–relief dynamics at both within- and between-individual levels. This work lays the groundwork for future translational research aimed at developing brain-based markers of therapeutically induced analgesia, which may hold substantial values in both preclinical and clinical contexts of drug development and other therapeutic interventions.
