Introduction

Cerebrovascular risk factors are known to compromise white matter microstructure and are thought to contribute to cognitive decline through the disruption of brain connectivity.1 Large population-based studies, including the UK Biobank, have demonstrated that higher vascular burden is associated with significant reductions in white matter integrity using diffusion-weighted MRI metrics.2 However, the mechanisms linking vascular risk to white matter damage remain unclear.

Systemic inflammation has been proposed as one potential pathway. Chronic low-grade inflammatory processes have been linked to vascular dysfunction, endothelial damage, and neuronal ageing, and blood-based proinflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) have been associated with vascular risk and alterations in brain structure. These findings raise the possibility that inflammation may contribute indirectly to white matter microstructural damage, with potential implications for cognitive decline and risk of dementia in later life.

Despite this, the role of systemic inflammation as a mediating mechanism has not been tested in a large population-based sample. Previous work in the UK Biobank has demonstrated that vascular risk and inflammatory markers are independently associated with poorer white matter microstructure, but whether inflammation explains the association between vascular burden and white matter integrity remains unclear. This study extends previous findings by deriving latent measures of vascular risk, systemic inflammation, and white matter integrity using principal component analyses, and testing a mediation model to examine whether systemic inflammation mediates the relationship between vascular burden and white matter integrity in mid- to late-life adults without known vascular disease. Clarifying these pathways could help to define early biomarkers of cognitive vulnerability associated with vascular risk.

Methods

The sample was taken from the larger UK Biobank imaging cohort (N ~ 90,000). The final sample comprised of 7,330 adults (aged 45-85 years, mean = 65.3, SD = 8.1; 53.1% female) who had complete data on vascular risk, inflammatory markers, diffusion MRI, age, and sex. Participants with a history of neurological or neurodegenerative disease, stroke or transient ischaemic attack, head or brain injury, central nervous system infection, or other major neurological conditions were excluded.

Cerebrovascular burden was quantified using principal component analysis (PCA) of established vascular risk factors, including mean systolic and diastolic blood pressure, body mass index, waist-to-hip ratio, smoking status, and the use of cholesterol, blood pressure, or diabetes medication2. The first PCA demonstrated shared variance across these measures and was interpreted as a composite of vascular risk.

Systemic inflammation was assessed using blood-based inflammatory markers. A primary inflammation component was derived using a second PCA of CRP, IL-6, and TNF-α, and demonstrated shared inflammatory variance across markers. To maximise sample size and available data, analyses were repeated using CRP alone as a broader but less specific indicator of systemic inflammation.

White matter integrity was assessed using diffusion-weighted MRI. Fractional anisotropy (FA) and mean diffusivity (MD) were extracted from major anterior association and projection tracts, including the genu of the corpus callosum, internal capsule, corona radiata, superior longitudinal and fronto-occipital fasciculi, and the uncinate fasciculus2. Diffusion measures from the baseline imaging visit were used where available, with substitution for the follow-up visit when baseline imaging data was missing. FA and MD values were combined using PCA to derive a single diffusion component per participant, to represent white matter microstructural integrity.

Structural equation modelling was then used to test whether systemic inflammation mediated the association between vascular risk and white matter integrity. Models were estimated using maximum likelihood with 1,000 bootstrap draws for indirect effects and adjusted for age and sex. All analyses were conducted within the UK Biobank Research Analysis Platform (UKB-RAP)3, using R and the lavaan package was used for structural equation modelling.4

Results

Greater vascular burden was strongly associated with poorer white matter integrity (β = 0.42, p < .001). Vascular burden was not significantly associated with systemic inflammation (β = -0.02, p = .19), and inflammation was also unrelated to diffusion measures (β = -0.01, p = .28). Consistent with this, the indirect (mediated) effect of inflammation was non-significant (βindirect ≈ 0, p = .48), while the direct vascular-diffusion association remained robust (βdirect = 0.42, p < .001). Older age and male sex were associated with lower white matter integrity.

Principal component analyses showed that the vascular composite explained 38% of variance across risk indicators, the inflammation component explained 40% of variance across inflammatory markers, and the diffusion component explained 56% of variance across white matter measures. Repeating the mediation analysis using C-reactive protein alone produced a comparable pattern of results, indicating that the absence of mediation was not driven by missing cytokine data.

Conclusion

Although CRP, IL-6, and TNF-α capture aspects of low-grade systemic inflammation, they may primarily reflect metabolic inflammatory tone rather than immune or endothelial processes directly relevant to cerebrovascular brain injury. The absence of mediation suggests that vascular risk influences white matter integrity largely through direct vascular or metabolic mechanisms, rather than through systemic inflammation as measured here.

These findings replicate robust associations between higher vascular burden and poorer white matter integrity in midlife and older adults, but indicate that systemic inflammation does not explain this relationship. It is also possible that blood-based inflammatory markers in large population cohorts do not adequately capture central inflammatory processes relevant to white matter damage.

These results highlight the importance of cerebrovascular health in maintaining white matter integrity and suggest that targeting vascular risk factors may be critical for reducing vulnerability to cognitive decline and dementia, even in the absence of higher systemic inflammation.
