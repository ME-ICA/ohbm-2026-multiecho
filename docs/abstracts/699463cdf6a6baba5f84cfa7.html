Introduction

The neonatal period is a critical epoch marked by rapid cortical development that lays the foundation for cortical function across the lifespan1. Of particular importance is the development of sensorimotor function, which underpins the emergence of fundamental goal-oriented behaviours such as feeding and grasping. The importance of development during this period is emphasised by permanent motor difficulties or cerebral palsy following perinatal brain injury, and the association between early motor development and later adverse neurodevelopmental disorders.

In adults, sensorimotor information is processed across a brain-wide network of connected cortical areas with feedforward and feedback information flow between primary and associative regions2. However, understanding of how this cortical circuitry is established in the neonatal period has been constrained to postmortem anatomical studies, with limited in vivo and/or longitudinal insight due to the relatively large voxel sizes of conventional functional MRI (fMRI), especially with respect to the smaller neonatal brain. This can be overcome through the increased sensitivity, signal-to-noise and spatial specificity afforded by ultra-high field (UHF; 7T) fMRI, which enables localisation of functional activity at a sub-millimetre level3. This allows direct studies of cortical circuitry at different cortical depths by exploring corresponding depth-resolved patterns of functional connectivity (FC). Here, we harnessed the mesoscale resolution afforded by fMRI at 7T to characterise the functional architecture of the sensorimotor circuit at rest across the neonatal period.

Methods

Participants

Data were collected from 12 healthy neonates (33.17–46.71 weeks post-menstrual age (PMA) at scan, 27.71-39.29 weeks gestational age (GA) at birth, 6 female) recruited from the postnatal and neonatal wards at St. Thomas Hospital, London, and 3 adults (29–49 yrs at scan, 2 female). National ethics committee approval (NHS REC: 19/LO/1384) and written consent (from parents in the case of neonatal participants) were acquired prior to data collection.

Data acquisition

Whole-brain sub-millimeter resolution fMRI data were acquired on a 7T system (MAGNETOM Terra.X, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, DE) with a 1TX-32RX Nova Medical head coil (Wilmington, MA, USA) during natural sleep for neonates and at rest for adults. After in-house specific absorption rate (SAR) and safety modelling for 7T neonatal scanning, locally imposed safety measures were implemented5. BOLD fMRI data were acquired with a GRE-EPI sequence over 8 min 58 s with 0.8 mm isotropic resolution and 2.99s TR, while other sequence parameters were adjusted to optimise for subject age-specific brain tissue properties and size (neonates/adults: TE=50/25 ms, matrix=156x156x87 / 260x260x120, in-plane acceleration=2/4, slice acceleration=3/3, volumes=180/180). A reversed-phase encoding EPI image and high-resolution anatomical data were also acquired in all participants.

Data preprocessing

All fMRI data were pre-processed using custom, age-appropriate pipelines6 adapted for resting state (rs)-fMRI data. In brief, data underwent brain extraction using FSL’s BET, slice-timing correction with FSL’s slicetimer, thermal denoising with NORDIC, motion correction by realignment of each volume to a motion-free reference volume using FreeSurfer’s implementation of AFNI’s 3dVolReg followed by 24-parameter motion regression, global mean intensity normalisation and high-pass filtering at 0.01 Hz. Physiological noise was removed by regressing out physiological noise components estimated using ICA with FSL’s MELODIC tool constrained to 60 components. Data from 3 neonates were excluded from data analysis due to excessive motion (> 40% volumes with framewise-displacement (FD) > 0.3 mm and/or absolute displacement (AD) > 0.8 mm), leaving a final dataset of 9 neonates and 3 adults.

Functional connectivity analysis

Data were up-sampled to 0.16 mm x 0.16 mm x 0.8 mm to avoid singularities at the cortical boundaries. Regions of interest spanning the full depth of the cortex were manually defined in the hand area of S1 and M1, then split into three equivolume depth bins using LayNii (https://github.com/layerfMRI/LAYNII) (Figure 1A). Automatic segmentation of the thalamus was performed on T2-w structural data using BOUNTI (https://github.com/SVRTK/auto-proc-svrtk) in neonates and on T1-w structural data using the FreeSurfer recon-all pipeline in adults. Thalamic segmentations were registered to EPI distortion corrected EPI images (corrected with AFNI’s 3dQwarp and reverse phase encoding EPI images) using boundary-based registration with Freesurfer in adults, and FSL in neonates. The inverse of the EPI distortion correction warp was applied to the segmentations in EPI space to improve the alignment with the native (non-corrected) EPI data (Figure 1B). Normalised BOLD timeseries were extracted from each depth bin of M1 and S1, as well as whole cortical thickness ROIs in M1 and S1 and the thalamus (Figure 1B). Depth-resolved FC between cortical depths in M1, S1 and whole cortical thickness ROIs in M1 and S1 and the thalamus was assessed using pair-wise Pearson correlation with global signal confound regressors and volume censoring for excessive motion (FD > 0.3 mm and AD > 0.8 mm). 

Statistics

Age-related effects on depth-resolved FC were assessed using linear regression. FC values were Fisher z-transformed, and ordinary least squares linear regression relating FC to PMA was performed for each depth bin in M1 and S1 – whole cortical thickness target ROIs in M1 and S1 and the thalamus. 

Results

Despite the thinner cortex in the neonatal period compared with adults (~1.5 mm vs. ~2.5 mm), the increased spatial resolution afforded by 7T fMRI enabled cortical depth-resolved FC analysis in neonates. Depth-resolved rs-FC across the sensorimotor network (thalamus, M1 and S1) differed in neonates compared with adults (Figure 1C). The superficial depth bin in both M1 and S1 showed maximal FC with all target ROIs in neonates; whereas adults showed more variable depth-dependent FC profiles in both M1 and S1, with the maximal connectivity also seen at the middle and deep depths (Figure 1C). 

Cortical depth-resolved FC showed region-specific PMA-related changes in neonates, which was significant within the middle and deep depths only (Figure 1D). In M1, increases in FC were seen with PMA between middle M1 - contralateral S1 (p=0.030) and ipsilateral S1 (p=0.029); and between deep M1 – ipsilateral S1 (p=0.024). In S1, increases in FC were seen between deep S1 – contralateral S1 (p=0.006), ipsilateral M1 (p=0.016) and contralateral M1 (p=0.016); as well as between middle S1 – contralateral S1 (p=0.013), ipsilateral M1 (p=0.028) and contralateral M1 (p=0.032); and between superficial S1 – contralateral S1 (p=0.025). No changes in FC were seen between any depth bin in M1 or S1 and the thalamus. 

Conclusion

We show that it is possible to resolve depth-dependent functional connectivity of the sensorimotor system in the neonatal brain, despite the markedly thinner cortex relative to adults. Neonates exhibit maximal functional connectivity at superficial cortical depths of M1 and S1, with less regional specificity in depth-resolved connectivity patterns than seen in adults. This is consistent with less differentiated functional architecture in early development, followed by experience-dependent remodelling of sensory circuits. 

Region and depth-specific increases of M1 and S1 cortico-cortical functional connectivity were seen with PMA both within and across hemispheres. These effects were predominantly evident at deep and middle cortical depths, suggesting depth-specific refinement of cortical circuitry across this period. In contrast, no age-related changes were found in M1 or S1 FC with the thalamus, likely reflecting the earlier maturation of thalamocortical connectivity. 

These findings demonstrate that depth-resolved functional connectivity is already present at birth, with cortical circuit-level development of FC occurring across specific periods within the neonatal period. This work highlights the potential of sub-millimetre fMRI to characterise the early evolution of cortical circuitry across depths, providing a framework for identifying developmental windows of vulnerability and understanding how disruptions during these windows may influence later neurodevelopmental outcomes.
