Introduction

Neurodevelopmental Disorders (NDDs), including autism spectrum disorder (ASD), attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), show substantial genetic, behavioural, and neuroanatomical heterogeneity.  This heterogeneity limits precision medicine approaches, motivating efforts to identify biologically coherent subgroups.  Recent work from our group has demonstrated that structural MRI phenotypes in 135 different NDD relevant mouse models can be clustered into stable neuroanatomical subgroups that translate directly to large human NDD cohorts1. In humans, these subgroups are transdiagnostic, including individuals with ASD, ADHD, OCD, and other NDDs, suggesting shared biology across diagnoses.  The shared neuroanatomy in these cross-species clusters has been linked to specific molecular signatures, including chromatin and transcriptional pathways, synaptic mechanisms, and various signaling pathways.

In that work, the NDD clusters/subgroups diverge in cortical gray matter (GM), white matter (WM), and cerebellar volume.  Here, we further investigate this neuroanatomical pattern across subgroups to better understand the balance between the white matter, the cortex, and cerebellum in NDDs.  White matter development is tightly related to cortical growth during childhood and may provide a better understanding of subgroup specific brain organization.  Our goal is to determine how WM-GM relationships differ across these neuroanatomical clusters, providing group specific neurodevelopmental trajectories that may further refine biologically grounded subtyping approaches.

Methods

Participants were drawn from 3 specific clusters that were translationally matched across species from within the Province of Ontario Neurodevelopmental Disorders (POND) dataset1.  These clusters were chosen based on having a translational match between clusters in the mouse models, POND dataset, and a second human dataset (Healthy Brain Network).  This three-way match added substantial weight to the authenticity of our subgroups.

For this work, 399 NDD participants aged 5-12 years old were included. The images were collected at three separate sites across Ontario: The Hospital for Sick Children, Holland Bloorview Kids Rehabilitation Hospital, and Queen’s University. All participants previously completed high-resolution T1-weighted MPRAGE sequence with 0.8 mm isotropic resolution used for anatomical measurements and a subset (n=331) completed Diffusion Tensor Imaging (DTI) with 2.0 mm isotropic resolution and b-values of 0 (18 images), 1000 (39 directions), 1600 (41 directions), and 2600 (59 directions) used for structural connectivity measures.  For the T1 weighted images the volumes were assessed using deformation-based morphometry to derive local measures of absolute and relative volume differences for each participant (for full details please refer to Ellegood et al. 2025)1. For the DTI images, we used FSL (https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk) to calculate FA maps and Multi-Modal Registration Framework (MMORF, https://git.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/MMORF )2 to register the T1 and diffusion tensor of an individual together towards a reference atlas (https://git.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/oxford-mm-templates).  The Johns Hopkins University (JHU) WM atlas3 was used to assess 48 different white matter structures and calculate mean FA values across the 331 NND participants. For each individual, we assessed overall white matter (WM), cortical gray matter (GM), cerebellar volume, and the mean FA values in 48 different white matter tracts.  

Initial assessments of volume revealed overall group differences and found correlations between WM and cortical GM across the entire dataset.  We further repeated these analyses within each individual cluster.  Parallel correlation tests were conducted for the cerebellum with cortical GM, and for the cerebellum with WM, producing correlation coefficients, confidence intervals and associated p-values for each cluster. We then fit cluster specific linear models predicting cortical GM from WM, cerebellar volume from WM, and cerebellum volume from cortical GM.  Each model was estimated separately within each cluster, allowing slopes and intercepts to vary between groups. Finally, we tested whether the relationship between WM and cortical GM differed between clusters.  We compared a model containing only main effects of cortical GM and cluster against a model including an interaction term (cortical GM x cluster).  A significant interaction indicated that the slope linking WM to cortical GM varied across the clusters.  Slopes for each cluster were extracted from the interaction model to quantify these differences directly.

To assess WM microstructure, we performed a multivariate ANOVA (MANOVA) on the mean FA value of the 48 different regions with cluster as the predictor to test for coherent microstructural differences between the subgroups.  We then ran univariate ANOVAs on each region with FDR correction to identify tract level differences driving the multivariate effect.  To visualize and interpret the multivariate FA patterns we used a linear discriminant analysis (LDA) on the 48 ROIs.  LDA coefficients (LD1, LD2) were used to identify the regions with the largest absolute contributions to subgroup separation and cluster-wise means.

Results

Across the three neuroanatomical subgroups, we observed differences in both absolute volumes, as well as in the coupling between cortical gray matter, cerebellar volume, and white matter pathways (Figure 1A).  

The absolute volumes of the cortical GM in the three clusters were significantly different measured by an ANOVA (631 ± 10 cm3 (Mean ± 95% CI), 669 ± 9 cm3, and 662 ± 13 cm3,  p=1.6x10-7) for cluster 1, 2 and 3 respectively.  The absolute volumes of the WM and cerebellum also differed significantly across the three clusters (WM: 426 ± 9 cm3, 455 ± 10 cm3, and 479 ± 14 cm3, p=0.04; Cerebellum: 130 ± 2 cm3, 131 ± 2 cm3, and 135 ± 3 cm3, p=4.3x10-8) again for clusters 1, 2, and 3 respectively.  The relative volumes (% total brain volume) were slightly different with the cortical GM and the WM matter showing significant differences across clusters (Cortical GM: 44.5 ± 0.2 %, 45.1 ± 0.2 %, and 43.3 ± 2 %, p=3.6x10-11; WM: 30.0 ± 0.3 %, 30.5 ± 0.3 %, and 31.3 ± 0.2 %, p=0.003) but not the cerebellum (9.2 ± 0.1 %, 8.9 ± 0.1 %, and 8.9 ± 0.1%,  p=0.08).  A MANOVA indicated further that there was a significant difference in the patterns of these 3 regions across clusters (Wilks, F~10.2, p=7.4x10-11).  Correlations and linear models indicated that WM was strongly associated with cortical GM (r ~ 0.61, p < 2.2x10-16), while cerebellar volume was largely independent of both the cortical GM and WM (r ~ 0.02, p=0.71; r ~ -0.01, p=0.85, respectively).  Cluster specific slopes for WM ~ Cortical GM were 0.47, 0.74, and 0.65 for clusters 1, 2 and 3 respectively, with a significant interaction effect across clusters (p=0.025).

FA analyses provided converging and complementary evidence of subgroup differences. Overall, the mean FA between clusters was insignificant (p=0.15, Figure 1B). However, a MANOVA across the 48 regions was found to be highly significant (Wilks, F~3.3, p=2.4x10-16), indicating microstructural differences across clusters.  Univariate ANOVAs (with FDR correction) identified multiple tracts with significant cluster effects.  These regions included several cerebellar related tracts (middle – p=0.002, superior – p=0.01, inferior – p=0.02), the genu of the corpus callosum (p=0.001), the corona radiata (p=0.006), and the cingulum bundle (p=0.001).  The LDA on the 48 regions produced clear separation of the three clusters in discriminant space (Figure 1C).  Inspection of the LD coefficients revealed that the LD1 was heavily influenced by tracts such as the corona radiata (LD1 coeff -11.0), external (9.1) and internal (9.0) capsules, and the superior longitudinal fasciculus (-6.9), whereas LD2 was more influenced by cerebellar tracts (inferior cerebellar peduncle - LD2 coeff 20.4, superior -5.1, and middle 18.2) and brainstem pathways like the cortical spinal tract (10.3).  In terms of cluster level FA profiles, cluster 1 exhibited higher FAs in the cerebellar peduncles and the fornix, cluster 2 exhibited a higher FA in the uncinate fasciculus and the lowest FAs in the cerebellar peduncles, cluster 3 showed higher FA in the cingulum bundle, the corona radiata and the cortical spinal tract and lower FA values in the corpus callosum.  

These FA signatures contrasted our volumetric findings with cluster 1 having the smallest overall white matter and cerebellum volume but the highest FA values in the cerebellar peduncles. Cluster 2 on the other hand despite having the largest overall cortical volume tended to have intermediate FA values with the only increase in FA compared to the other clusters in the uncinate fasciculus.  Cluster 3 had the highest overall white matter volume despite having the lowest FA values in the genu and splenium of corpus callosum.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that neuroanatomically defined, cross species subgroups show divergence not only in regional volumetric organization but also in white matter microstructure, suggesting that these clusters reflect meaningful differences in development.  The identification of distinct WM-GM coupling across clusters, combined with tract-specific FA signatures, indicates that each subgroup may follow its own trajectory of cortical expansion, myelination, and long-range connectivity maturation.  This raises the possibility that these subgroups represent distinct neurodevelopmental groupings shaped by unique combinations of molecular, synaptic, and axonal processes.

The FA findings provide additional insight.  Cluster 1, which showed the smallest overall white matter and cerebellar volumes, nonetheless had a higher FA in the cerebellar peduncles and in limbic-brainstem pathways such as the fornix.  This pattern may reflect a developmental profile in which specific subcortical and cerebellar brainstem tracts mature more efficiently despite reduced global volumes.  Cluster 2, despite having the largest cortical volumes, showed mostly intermediate FA across most tract with the uncinate fasciculus as the primary pathway showing elevated FA relative to other clusters.  This may signal a developmental emphasis on frontotemporal connectivity, but without the broader myelination seen in the other groups.  Cluster 3, which had the largest white matter volumes, showed higher FA in the cingulum, corona radiata, and corticospinal tract, but lower FA in callosal regions, particularly in the genu and splenium.  This combination suggests a developmental trajectory consistent with strong intra-hemispheric and projection system maturation, but possibly delayed development of the inter hemispheric communication.

Taken together, these patterns underscore that volume and microstructure do not align uniformly across clusters.  This dissociation raises the possibility that these subgroups correspond to distinct underlying cellular or molecular programs.  The previous cross-species alignment with these subgroups and subgroups of mouse models also indicates that these volume and FA signatures map onto specific molecular mechanisms. Cluster 1 with the smaller volume and increase FA in the cerebellum may be linked to chromatin modification and the immune system.  Cluster 2 with the largest cortical volumes and the increase FA in the uncinate fasciculus may be linked to signaling by wnt and mapk.  Cluster 3 on the other had with the largest white matter volumes and greater intra-hemispheric FA values may be linked primarily to changes in the synapses.

These findings have several implications for future research.  First, incorporating volume and microstructural features seem to be essential for biologically grounded subtyping in NDDs, as each metric can capture a different dimension of brain organization.  Second, the divergent WM-GM coupling across clusters suggests that developmental trajectories will be imperative for understanding how these subgroups emerge.  Longitudinal studies in humans and mice from these groups could reveal whether the clusters differ in timing of cortical growth, peak myelination, or synaptic pruning.  Third, the distinctive FA signature highlight potential neurobiological targets for intervention studies with a more precise grouping.  Overall, these multimodal convergence patterns strengthen the interpretation that these clusters reflect meaningful and potentially biologically grounded neurodevelopmental subgroups, which may offer a pathway towards more precise and mechanistically informed interventions.
