Introduction

Bilingualism is associated with adaptive changes in brain regions supporting language and executive control, yet the magnitude of these effects varies based on individual experience.1 A critical factor likely influencing these structural adaptations is typological distance—the degree of structural similarity between a bilingual’s known languages. Navigating languages from distinct linguistic families (e.g., English-Chinese) may impose different neural demands compared to managing languages with shared structural roots (e.g., English-French).2 However, it remains unclear which linguistic environment drives greater neuroplasticity. Specifically, does the cognitive demand of managing typologically distinct languages (Inter-family bilingualism) result in more pronounced structural differences than managing typologically similar ones (Intra-family bilingualism)? Utilizing the large-scale ABCD dataset, this study investigates whether Inter-family or Intra-family bilingualism exerts a greater influence on cortical surface area and tissue intensity in late childhood.

Methods

We analyzed structural MRI data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study (Release 5.0). Participants were excluded if they lacked complete structural MRI data or failed manual quality control and protocol compliance checks, resulting in a final high-quality structural sample of 11,924 children. From this cohort, we identified bilingual participants using the Youth Acculturation Survey; children were excluded from the final analysis if they did not report speaking or understanding a second language besides English. The remaining 4352 bilingual children were classified into two groups based on the linguistic distance of their second language from English: (1) an Intra-family group (n = 222) speaking French, a structurally similar Romance language, and (2) an Inter-family group (n = 211) speaking Mandarin, a typologically distinct language. Structural T1-weighted images were processed using the standard ABCD pipeline, including gradient nonlinearity and intensity non-uniformity corrections.3 We extracted cortical surface area, white matter intensity (WMI), and gray matter intensity (GMI) from regions of interest (ROIs) defined by the Desikan–Killiany atlas. Analyses focused on two a priori networks: the Language Network (e.g., middle temporal, pars opercularis/triangularis gyri) and the Multiple-Demand (MD) Network (e.g., caudal middle frontal, inferior parietal gyri). Visual cortex and Default Mode Network regions served as controls. Linear Mixed Models (LMM) were employed to estimate group differences, controlling for age, sex, and intracranial volume, with subject and site included as random effects.

Results

The Inter-family group exhibited significantly greater structural metrics across specific network nodes compared to the Intra-family group (all p<.05), with control analyses of visual and default-mode regions showing no group differences (all p>.05), confirming network specificity. Figure 2 summarizes the principal cross-network group differences.

Cortical Surface Area: In the Language Network, Inter-family bilinguals showed larger surface area in the left middle temporal gyrus (t=4.17, p<.001). In the MD Network, group differences were also significant in the inferior parietal lobule bilaterally (left: t=2.98, p=.003; right: t=3.20, p=.001).

White Matter Intensity: A significant Group effect emerged in the Language Network (F(1,427)=10.31, p=.001), with Inter-family bilinguals showing higher intensity in the left middle temporal (t=-4.05, p<.001), pars orbitalis (t=-3.29, p=.001), pars triangularis (t=-2.85, p=.005), and superior temporal (t=-2.90, p=.004) regions. In the MD Network, the overall Group effect was nonsignificant (p>.05), though the caudal middle frontal gyrus showed a trend-level difference. 

Gray Matter Intensity: Significant group effects were localized to the left middle temporal gyrus (F(1,427)=4.77, p=.029). 

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that the typological distance between a bilingual’s known languages is a significant factor in shaping brain structure during development. The observed increases in cortical surface area and tissue intensity among Inter-family bilinguals imply that managing typologically distinct linguistic systems may impose unique computational demands, resulting in distinct patterns of neuroplasticity compared to managing similar languages. These results indicate that bilingualism should not be viewed as a uniform experience; rather, future neurodevelopmental research should account for the structural relationships between languages to fully capture the spectrum of bilingual adaptation.
