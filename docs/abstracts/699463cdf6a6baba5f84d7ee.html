Introduction

The hippocampus is a crucial structure for learning, memory, and spatial cognition 1. Although critical aspects of human hippocampal development occur relatively early in gestation, the consequences of premature birth on its development remain poorly understood.  Studying the neonatal hippocampus is technically challenging: it is a small, structurally complex region that demands a level of precision that traditional imaging has struggled to achieve. Ultra-high field MRI significantly improves signal-to-noise ratio, enabling the sub-millimetre spatial resolution, and therefore has the potential to sensitively detect subtle hippocampal changes during early development.

In this study, we combined 7T MRI with a neonatal-specific implementation of HippUnfold 2 to characterise subfield level hippocampal growth during the earliest period of postnatal life, with a particular focus on preterm neonates.

Specifically, this study addressed two complementary aims. First, we evaluated the performance of HippUnfold on 7T T2-weighted neonatal data, assessing its ability to reliably segment hippocampal subfields despite the unique tissue properties of the neonatal brain and the potential for high-field imaging distortions (figure 1). Second, we sought to replicate previously reported volumetric patterns 3, relating changes in volume to key developmental variables, including postmenstrual age (PMA) at scan, time ex-utero, and gestational age (GA) at birth.

Methods

Data Acquisition:

66 neonates (median GA at birth = 34.43 weeks, range: 27.71 - 41.57 weeks, median PMA at scan = 38.14 weeks, range: 33.57 - 52.86 weeks, 38 males, 27 females) were imaged during natural sleep on a 7 T MRI scanner (MAGNETOM Terra, Siemens Healthineers) using a 1TX-32RX Nova Medical head coil (Wilmington, MA, USA), under local safety conditions (REC: RE/LO/1384) 4. 2D-TSE sequences (TE = 156 ms, acquired resolution = 0.6 mm, slice thickness = 1.2 mm) were acquired in at least two orthogonal planes.

A 3D volume with 0.5 mm isotropic resolution was created using slice-to-volume reconstruction from the 2D TSE acquisitions 5. Hippocampal and dentate gyrus (DG) segmentation was then performed with HippUnfold (https://hippunfold.khanlab.ca/en/latest/), using a U-net model trained specifically on neonatal T2-weighted data 2. 

Analysis:

Volumetric analyses were performed following visual quality control (QC) of the segmentations. 

To characterise age-related changes in hippocampal volume, linear versus quadratic regression models were first evaluated using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). 

Relationships between PMA and hippocampal volume were then quantified using linear mixed-effects models, with subject included as a random intercept (Figure 2A). Wald tests were used to assess the significance of fixed effects, and multiple comparisons were controlled using a false-discovery-rate procedure.

Inter-regional covariance of hippocampal subfields was assessed by pairwise Pearson correlations between subregional volumes (Figure 2B). Hemispheric asymmetry was quantified using the laterality index, LI = (L–R)/(L+R), for both the whole hippocampus and individual subfields (Figure 2C).

To specifically examine the effects of time in utero and time ex utero on volume and shape measures, we used a linear mixed-effects model of the form: 

~ b0 + b1(tIU) + b2(tXU) + b3(P) + b4(tXU * P) + e

(tIU is gestational age at birth, tXU is postnatal age, and P is prematurity). Normality of data was assessed by plotting the residuals of all data points calculated from the regression model used and fit to y=0. 

To evaluate how subregional growth scales with total hippocampal expansion, we computed log-log regressions between each subregion’s volume and the global hippocampal volume. The resulting slope parameter (b) indicates hypometric (<1), isometric (~1), or hypermetric (>1) scaling.

All statistical analyses were conducted using python statsmodels. 

Results

HippUnfold demonstrated robust performance on 7T neonatal T2-weighted data, with only three scans failing visual QC due to severe pathology. This yielded 73 volumetric data points from 66 neonates (median GA at birth = 34.43 weeks, range 27.71-41.57; median PMA at scan = 38.14 weeks, range 33.57-52.86; 42 male, 31 female scans).

Absolute volumetric growth of the hippocampus (figure 2A) and all subregions except CA3 best fit a quadratic model. Subregion-to-whole hippocampus volume ratios did not significantly change with PMA. In addition, sub-regional volumes were strongly inter-correlated (except CA3) (figure 2B), suggesting subregional growth scales isometrically with the hippocampus as a whole. Consistent with this, log-log scaling analyses indicated largely isometric growth across subregions (CA1 b = 0.934, CA2 b = 0.970, CA3 b = 0.823, CA4 b = 0.984, DG b= 1.010, subiculum b = 1.119).

There was no clear evidence for lateralisation in hippocampal or subregional volumes, with the exception of the subiculum, which tended to be larger in the right hemisphere (figure 2C). 

Uterine growth rates were higher for the entire hippocampus and all subregions than ex-utero growth rates. Interestingly, neonates born prematurely displayed significant catch-up growth in CA1, CA2, dentate gyrus, and subiculum (Figure 2D). Notably, the ex-utero growth rate of CA1 in prematurely born babies was very close to the corresponding in utero growth rate (19.4 mm3/week, 21.6 mm3/week respectively), indicating that CA1 exhibits comparatively rapid postnatal growth in preterm infants.

Conclusion

Hippocampal absolute volumes followed a quadratic growth trajectory with increasing PMA, consistent with developmental patterns previously reported in 3T neonatal MRI studies 3. Subregional volumes were strongly correlated with total hippocampal volume, and most subfields grew isometrically with the hippocampus as a whole. This proportional scaling suggests that, over the early postnatal period, subregions expand in a coordinated manner rather than exhibiting region-specific volumetric divergence.

Our findings further suggest a compensatory growth mechanism in CA1, CA2, dentate gyrus, and subiculum in babies born preterm. Whereas ex utero growth rates in term-born neonates were typically slower relative to in utero rates, preterm infants exhibit accelerated postnatal volumetric growth. 

While statistical trends with PMA were significant, the variance explained indicates that age alone is an insufficient predictor of hippocampal volume. The substantial inter-subject variability suggests that additional factors, such as genetic influences or perinatal clinical conditions, likely contribute meaningfully to shaping hippocampal size during this developmental window.

To conclude, these results provide a refined characterisation of early hippocampal development, highlighting robust global growth patterns while suggesting that specific subregions may show heightened vulnerability, and possibly adaptive responses, in the context of prematurity. Future studies are needed to determine whether this catch-up growth reflects genuine restoration of typical development or altered maturation with consequences for later neurodevelopment.
