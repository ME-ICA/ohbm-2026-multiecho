Introduction

A major goal of neuroscience is to identify biomarkers that can reliably predict brain function and disease with high precision using non-invasive methods. EEG is a prime candidate in this respect, and, over the years, it has been used to derive an ever-increasing number of features and putative markers. Ad hoc signal characteristics have been considered as interpretable alternatives to deep learning-derived features, which often achieve high predictive performance but are difficult to interpret. Yet, even these ad-hoc features create a high-dimensional “multiverse” of data, spanning feature, temporal and spatial dimensions. Dimensionality is not the only challenge: individual features, even when targeting the same aspect of brain function, typically exhibit low mutual correlations.^1.

This raises a fundamental question: what is actually being captured across this expanding feature space, and are meaningful latent factors being overlooked? It is possible that coherent neural patterns are expressed across multiple EEG features and across spatial and temporal dimensions that are often neglected.

In this modeling work, we assessed the extent to which latent multivariate patterns can be extracted from the spatial distribution of multiple existing EEG features, and whether such latent representations provide higher predictive value than 1) individual features alone 2) spatially coarse representations of the same features. As a proof of principle, we focused on chronological age as the target variable.

Methods

Using two publicly available eyes-closed resting-state EEG datasets (dataset 1: Dortmund Vital Study, DVS.^2, N = 599, age range: 20-70 years; dataset 2: LEMON.^3, N = 186, age range: 20-77 years), we extracted a set of commonly used EEG features derived from the literature.^1. The datasets were selected for their broad age coverage, which allowed us to benchmark different EEG-based models in their ability to predict chronological age, a variable that has been widely used as a target for EEG-derived biomarkers. 

In each dataset, we first evaluated previously reported features’ mutual correlations.^1. We then conducted an age-prediction benchmark comparing four spatial dimensionality-reduction strategies. Starting from 138 features derived per EEG channel and subject, these strategies included: (i) simple averaging across channels, (ii) principal component analysis (PCA) applied to channel-level features, (iii) PCA applied to channel covariance matrices, and (iv) projection into a Riemannian tangent space to better preserve spatial structure. 

Across all models, subsequent analytical steps were held constant. These included a second-stage feature reduction using PCA followed by ridge regression to predict age, evaluated using 10-fold cross-validation. To characterize the accuracy–complexity trade-off, we systematically varied the number of retained components during the feature-reduction step. Predictive performance—quantified by the coefficient of determination (R²) and mean absolute error (MAE)—was compared against single-feature models and against a state-of-the-art covariance-based approach relying on spectral power features.^4. 


Results

As previously reported.^1, feature correlations were generally low in both datasets (quartiles Q1, Q2, Q3; DVS: -0.17, 0.04, 0.25; LEMON: -0.26, 0.08, 0.34). In the benchmarking analyses, single-feature models yielded relatively low predictive performance (max R²=0.24). Multi-feature models diverged in the level of complexity at which performance reached an asymptote. The model based on a Riemannian representation, implementing a geometry-based reduction of the spatial covariance structure, reached its performance plateau at a lower level of complexity and outperformed the other models considered. The reference model based on spectral power features.^4 performed better than all other models considered, albeit at the cost of a substantially higher level of complexity.

Conclusion

The generally low correlations within the feature set replicate previous findings supporting the view that EEG features span a highly heterogeneous space and that individual features may not map onto common aspects of brain function.^1. The clear predictive advantage of multi-feature models over single-feature approaches suggests that information relevant to the target variable—here, chronological age—may be embedded in more complex, multivariate latent structures. The Riemannian model’s superiority in terms of parsimony and performance highlights the importance of accounting for the spatial structure of EEG signals, beyond approaches relying on single electrodes or scalp-averaged features. However, this model still underperformed relative to the reference model based on spectral power features.^4, which appears to outperform the others by leveraging both spatial and temporal dimensions. Thus, although preliminary and restricted to age prediction, our benchmarking results indicate that EEG-based predictive models could be enriched by exploiting latent multivariate structure across feature sets, motivating future efforts to integrate spatial and temporal dimensions.
