Introduction

Stroke is the leading cause of long-term disability in the United States. Understanding how stroke leads to death and disability supports acute triage, personalized care, and prognosis. In research, it informs brain–function relationships, guides targeted treatment development, and defines benchmarks for clinical trials. The most common outcome index is the modified Rankin Scale (mRS), recorded 90 days after stroke. mRS ranges from 0 (no disability) to 6 (death). Despite being coarse and subjective, it is easy to collect and widely used, making it the standard measure of medium- and long-term function. ^1

Traditionally, outcome prediction often depends on clinical experience, which makes it variable and imprecise. Previous studies ^2 ^3 have predicted mRS using clinical variables (sex, age, comorbidities, glucose level, onset-to-treatment time, acute mRS, NIHSS) and imaging-derived injury characteristics (infarct and hypoperfusion volumes, infarct location via ASPECTS, radiomics Despite some success, prior studies were limited by small and heterogeneous samples, subjective imaging assessments, reliance on expert-only metrics, lack of real-world representativeness, and insufficient independent validation. Most importantly, no public, easy-to-use tool exists for non-expert clinicians to generate real-time prognosis, limiting clinical impact.

Recent advances in artificial intelligence enable the integration of multimodal clinical and imaging data. This study compares classifiers for predicting 90-day mRS using clinical variables and automatically extracted image features that capture the influence of the acute stroke core and hypoperfused tissue on long-term outcomes. Ultimately, we aim to build a system that automatically combines imaging and clinical information to provide accurate, clinically relevant prognosis, supporting treatment planning and patient care.

Methods

We used a publicly available dataset containing clinical and imaging data from 2,888 acute stroke patients who underwent clinical evaluation and MRI at the Johns Hopkins Stroke Center ^4. The sample flow is shown in Fig. 1A.

Four clinical features were included in the prediction models: the NIH Stroke Scale (NIHSS, a standardized clinical tool used to quantify stroke severity by assessing neurological deficits at acute stage), Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), Body Mass Index (BMI), and age. These variables were selected for their clinical relevance and strong support in prior studies. To incorporate spatial information, lesion vectors were computed by quantifying the lesion volume within anatomically defined arterial territories. Because each territory supports different functional regions, the location of infarct and hypoperfusion strongly influences prognosis. We utilized a digital arterial atlas ^5 that defines 15 vascular subregions, as detailed in Figure 1B. Lesion and hypoperfusion loadings were obtained by intersecting each patient’s normalized binary masks with the atlas in MNI space and calculating the percentage of affected tissue in each region of interest (ROI).

We performed five experiments (Table 1) to assess how clinical features, lesion-core loadings, and hypoperfusion loadings contribute to long-term dysfunction and how combining them improves classification. Following prior work, mRS was grouped into two classes: 0–2 as favorable (independent function) and 3–6 as unfavorable.

Five classification models were tested. Logistic Regression (LR) served as the baseline linear model. Random Forest (RF), XGBoost (XGB), and LightGBM (LGBM) were selected for their robustness and ability to capture nonlinear interactions, with XGB and LGBM providing gradient-boosting optimization. A multilayer perceptron (MLP) was included for its capacity to model complex multimodal relationships. SMOTE was applied to address class imbalance. All models used stratified five-fold cross-validation on the training set to keep class proportions consistent and improve the stability of performance estimates.

Results

The accuracies of our classifiers are shown in Table 1. The results indicate that encoding ischemic lesions within fifteen arterial territories improves performance compared with using clinical variables alone. Spatial information adds prognostic value by capturing details not reflected in clinical data. Combining lesion vectors, hypoperfusion vectors, and clinical variables achieved robust performance even with a smaller sample, demonstrating the usefulness of integrating anatomical, vascular, and clinical markers for more accurate mRS prediction. Our results are also consistent with prior studies using large datasets. ^3

Feature analysis using SHAP, detailed in Figures 2A and 2B revealed significant contributions from clinical, lesion, and hypoperfusion variables. Clinical factors, especially NIHSS, strongly influenced outcomes, as expected. However, lesion and hypoperfusion involvement in key territories, such as thalamoperforating, posterior and vertebrobasilar territories, and lenticulostriate regions, also predicted poorer long-term prognosis. The model based only on lesion loading assigned the highest weights to the thalamoperforating (PCTP), inferior cerebellar (ICA), lateral lenticulostriate (LLS), anterior cerebral artery (ACA), and basilar (BAS) regions. When combining lesion and hypoperfusion loadings, the regions with the strongest contributions included hypoperfusion in the lateral lenticulostriate (LLS), superior cerebellar (SCA), medial lenticulostriate (MLS), MCA parietal (MCAP), and PCA temporal (PCAT) territories, along with lesion loading in the thalamoperforating (PCTP) region.

Conclusion

The results show that adding lesion loadings to clinical variables improves mRS prediction, and that hypoperfusion features provide additional gains, reaching similar accuracy even in a smaller cohort. Together, these findings highlight how clinical, lesion, and hypoperfusion information strengthen outcome estimation. The feature analysis clarifies the biological impact of stroke on function: beyond expected clinical variables such as NIHSS and age, lesion and hypoperfusion involvement in thalamoperforating, posterior and vertebrobasilar territories, and lenticulostriate regions contributed to mRS prediction. These regions align with critical functional areas where damage is known to worsen short- and long-term outcomes. The feature analysis also serves as a quality check, confirming that the models rely on meaningful, clinically relevant information.

This study focused on clinical and imaging data available on arrival and before treatment. Other work has included variables such as treatment choice and hospital stay, which are only known later. We plan to include these variables in future models to assess their effect on outcome, enabling mRS prediction both at initial assessment and at discharge and allowing comparison between the two stages.

Although we used manual annotations here, we plan to retrain our models for automated segmentations of lesion core and hypoperfusion maps extracted with our previously developed tool, ADS (NITRC: Acute-stroke Detection Segmentation (ADS): Tool/Resource Info). Our long-term goal is to add a fully automated mRS prediction module to ADS, giving clinical researchers a baseline prognosis to help tune trial parameters and, ultimately, support personalized therapeutics and family communication for life planning.
