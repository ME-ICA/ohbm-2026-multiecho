Introduction

Many primate and human studies investigating the neural correlates of perceptual decisions have supported an intentional framework, proposing that decisions are encoded in brain regions associated with the required response1. However, in most paradigms, decisions are tightly coupled to motor responses, making it difficult to pinpoint neural markers of perceptual decisions independent of sensory or motor processes.

Two recent fMRI decoding studies used a vibrotactile delayed match-to-comparison (DMTC) task that rendered the decision orthogonal to stimulus order and motor response. Nevertheless, they decoded binary decisions from brain regions associated with the required motor response – specifically the frontal eye fields (FEF) for saccades, the dorsal premotor cortex (PMd) for button presses and the posterior parietal cortex (PPC) for both2,3.

Since DMTC tasks are typically implemented in the vibrotactile domain, it remained unclear to which degree these findings were modality-specific. A recent visual adaptation of the same DMTC paradigm using button presses as responses revealed similar results, with the PMd and intraparietal sulcus (IPS) being predictive of binary decisions4. Together, these findings suggest that if a response effector is pre-specified, perceptual decisions are encoded in an effector-dependent manner – independent of stimulus order, motor response and likely sensory modality.

However, it remains unclear, whether neural representations show similar differences and overlaps across effectors in the visual domain as compared to the vibrotactile domain. To address this, the present fMRI study used a visual DMTC task using saccades as responses, enabling a direct cross-effector comparison with the previous button-press version4. Using whole-brain multivariate pattern analysis (MVPA), we tested whether decision-related information is represented in effector-dependent brain regions and how these representation differ and overlap across effectors.

Methods

A total of 31 healthy, right-handed volunteers completed the fMRI experiment. Based on a pre-defined exclusion criterion (less than 50% correct responses in at least one stimulus pair), eight participants were excluded, resulting in a final dataset of 23 participants (15 females; mean age = 28.35 ± 5.68 years).

Participants performed a visual DMTC task that was adapted from a previous study with the key difference that responses were made via eye movements instead of button presses. The task design was originally adapted from a vibrotactile DMTC paradigm2 and allows to separate the decision from potential confounds related to stimulus order and motor responses. In our implementation, participants compared the frequencies of two sequential visual flicker stimuli (f1 and f2). Specifically, f1 was 16, 20, 24 or 28 Hz, and f2 was 4 Hz higher or lower than f1, resulting in eight different frequency combinations. For a detailed overview of the task, see Fig.1.

After training the task on a separate day, participants completed six experimental runs inside a 3T MR scanner. Each run included 64 trials, each featuring a unique combination of frequencies, rule cue, matching cue and target screen. The flicker stimuli were displayed to the left and right of a central fixation cross using light-emitting diodes (LEDs) and participants’ eye movements were recorded with an MRI-compatible eye tracker.

Preprocessing and general linear model (GLM) analyses were carried out with SPM12. Functional images were realigned and co-registered with the structural image and a GLM was estimated to obtain voxel-wise beta estimates. The two regressors of interest captured the binary outcome of correct decisions (“higher” vs. “lower”) at the onset of the decision phase and were convolved with the hemodynamic response function. Incorrect decisions were modelled separately, and six head-motion parameters and a run constant were included as nuisance regressors.

Decision-related activation patterns were identified using an MVPA whole-brain searchlight approach with a searchlight radius of 4 voxels. We implemented a support vector machine (SVM) classifier in The Decoding Toolbox using a six-fold leave-one-run-out cross validation scheme to obtain voxel-wise activation patterns with above-chance decoding accuracies for distinguishing between the decisions (higher vs. lower). The accuracy maps were normalized, smoothed and entered into a one-sample t-test to identify local brain activation patterns that showed significant above-chance decoding across participants. Statistical results were thresholded at p < 0.001 (voxel-level) and corrected for multiple comparisons at the cluster-level using family-wise error (FWE) correction at p < 0.05. The anatomical regions were identified with the JuBrain Anatomy Toolbox and the HCPex atlas was additionally used to identify eye-movement related regions.

To rule out that decision-related activation patterns were confounded by imbalances in motor responses or task rules, we performed three additional control analyses. Two decoding analyses were implemented to identify brain regions encoding information about saccades or task rules during the decision phase. Additionally, the main decoding analyses was repeated using a subsampling scheme that balanced response direction and task rule across choices and runs.

For cross-effector comparisons, accuracy maps from the present study were combined with data from the prior button-press study4. With second-level t-tests in a flexible-factorial design, we tested for stronger decision-related representations in saccade- versus button-based responses and vice versa, using small-volume correction (SVC) in the expected effector-dependent regions. Furthermore, a conjunction analysis against the conjunction null hypothesis was applied to identify brain patterns predictive of perceptual decisions across both effectors.

Results

Participants performed the task with a mean accuracy of 85.1% (SD = 5.5%) and a mean response time of 365 ms (SD = 82 ms). To examine potential effects of task rule (compare f1 against f2 vs. f2 against f1), stimulus order (f1 > f2 vs. f1 < f2), and f1 frequency (16, 20, 24, and 28 Hz) on performance, we conducted a three-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) on arcsine-transformed data. This ANOVA showed the same main effects and interactions as the previous study4, with no main effect of task rule, but a main effect of stimulus order (F(1,22) = 33.96, p < 0.001), with higher accuracies for f1 < f2 trials. This difference was slightly more pronounced for rule 2, indicated by a significant task rule × stimulus order interaction (F(1,22) = 4.945, p = 0.037). Furthermore, the performance decreased decreased with increasing f1 frequency in f1 > f2 trials but remained stable in f1 < f2 trials, indicated by a significant main effect of f1 frequency (F(3,66) = 12.423, p < 0.001) and a significant interaction between stimulus order and f1 frequency (F(3,66) = 4.975, p < 0.001).

To identify regions encoding perceptual decisions independently of stimulus order and effector, we applied a whole-brain SVM searchlight analysis during the decision phase. We identified seven clusters that reliable predicted the binary decision (cluster-level FWE corrected at p < 0.05). These clusters were predominately located in bilateral posterior parietal cortex, such as the IPL and premotor cortices, spanning the frontal and supplementary eye fields. For a comprehensive details on all significant clusters, refer to Fig. 2. SVM analyses with 3- and 5-voxel searchlight radii produced similar patterns, confirming the robustness of our findings.

We performed two additional control analyses to identify brain activation patterns predicting the eye movements and task rule during the decision phase. Saccade responses (left vs. right) could be decoded from occipital regions, as well as parts of the left primary motor, primary somatosensory cortex and the FEF. Activation patterns predicting the rule (compare f1 against f2 vs. f2 against f1) were observed in the bilateral PPC and premotor cortices. The results of the subsampling control analysis were highly consistent with our main analysis, but given the strongly reduced number of trials, not all relevant clusters survived cluster-level FWE correction. Nonetheless, at an uncorrected cluster threshold of p < 0.001, the same key regions as observed in the main analysis were involved.

To compare decision representations across effector modalities, we combined the present dataset with the previous button-press version4. A two-sample t-test, using small-volume correction based on HCPex atlas regions, identified a significant cluster in the right FEF for the contrast “eye > hand”. No other clusters survived the whole-brain FWE correction, suggesting that specifically activation patterns in the right FEF were more predictive of perceptual decisions when responses are given via saccades. No significant clusters emerged for the contrast “hand > eye”, neither within the left PMd under SVC, nor at the whole-brain level. The conjunction analysis between the current dataset and the button-press data set4 revealed one significant cluster in the left IPS, indicating a parietal representation of perceptual decisions that is consistent across different effectors.

Conclusion

The present fMRI study used a visual DMTC task that rendered decisions independent of stimulus order and motor response to investigate whether saccade-reported perceptual decisions are encoded in an effector-dependent manner. Consistent with our hypotheses, the SVM MVPA searchlight approach revealed above-chance decoding accuracies in bilateral FEFs and PPCs. By using the same task paradigm as a previous study4 but with saccades instead of button presses as the response modality, we were able to identify similarities and differences between the two effectors. Cross-study second-level t-tests indicated that the right FEF but not the left FEF is more involved in decisions performed with saccades as compared to decisions with button-presses, while there was no preferential involvement of the left PMd for button presses as compared to saccades. However, the absence of the left PMd and the left FEF effects does not necessarily indicate a lack of preferential involvement. As these regions lie in close proximity, the combination of spatial smoothing and the searchlight approach make it challenging to find differences between these two areas. A conjunction analysis between the two studies revealed an overlap in the left IPS, suggesting that the IPS is involved in perceptual decisions across both effectors.

Although the decision was rendered independent of specific saccade responses, activation patterns in the FEFs were still predictive of binary decisions, suggesting an effector-dependent but motor response-independent representation. Rather than encoding concrete saccade plans, the FEF appears to retain abstract, categorical information that is nevertheless tied to the oculomotor system, indicated by the selective involvement of the FEF for saccadic as compared to manual responses. Similar differences in the vibrotactile domain2,3 suggest that this principle generalizes across sensory modalities. Such effector-dependent yet response-independent encoding could reflect an intermediate stage of the sensorimotor hierarchy in which decisions are already assigned to an effector, thereby facilitating their later transformation into specific motor plans and actions upon presentation of the response mapping.

Activation patterns in the PPC were shown to predict binary decisions not only when responses were given with saccades but also in the previous button-press variants, although with deviations in peak locations, with the IPL being more involved for saccades and the IPS for button-press. Importantly, the conjunction across effectors revealed an overlap across effectors in the IPS, suggesting that the representations in the IPS are more abstract and at least partly independent of the effector and the sensory modality. While seminal primate studies have attributed signals in lateral intraparietal neurons (LIP, the monkey equivalent of the IPS) primarily to saccade planning and preparation1, more recent evidence indicates that activity in LIP neurons encode categorical decisions independent of effectors or sensory modalities5. This aligns with the overlap across effectors in the IPS observed in the previous vibrotactile studies2,3 as well as in our visual variants4. Rather than reflecting an effector-dependent code, the IPS may encode stimulus-category information at an earlier level that can be flexibly mapped onto different motor systems.

Overall, our results indicate that premotor regions such as the FEF represent decisions in an effector-dependent, but motor-response independent manner, if a response effector is specified. On the other hand, representations in posterior parietal regions, particularly the IPS, generalize across effector and are thus more abstract and at least partly effector-independent. Notably, both forms of encoding remain independent of stimulus order, motor response and likely sensory modality.
