Introduction

Attention is a fundamental brain function that supports perception, decision-making, and social behaviors. In humans, it develops in childhood, stabilizes in adulthood, and declines with age. Dysfunction at any stage can impair daily functioning. Yet research is constrained by human longevity, is often cross-sectional—making it challenging to distinguish stable attentional phenotypes from transient fluctuations—and is confounded by factors such as education and social status that introduce variability unrelated to attention. These constraints make it difficult to determine whether observed behavioral differences reflect stable attentional mechanisms or context-dependent variability. Linking behavior to its underlying brain networks is therefore essential for understanding attentional trajectories and their evolution. We hypothesized that, as in humans, sustained attention in primates follows a nonlinear developmental trajectory, with parallel neural changes shaped by hierarchical status.

Methods

We investigated two species of semi-free-ranging macaques—Macaca mulatta (n = 12, 5 females, ages 1.87 – 5.68 years) and Macaca tonkeana (n = 32, 12 females, ages 1.93 – 22.29 years)—living in rich social groups with unrestricted access to touchscreens for automated testing2 (Fig. 1a). This setup enabled animals to voluntarily engage with cognitive challenges in a naturalistic environment, bridging the gap between controlled experimentation and real-world behavior. To assess sustained attention and inhibitory control, animals were engaged in a touchscreen-based adaptation of the 5-Choice Serial Reaction Time task3 (Fig. 1b), a cross-species analogue of the human Continuous Performance Test that requires participants to sustain vigilance and respond to infrequent or briefly presented target stimuli1. 

We then collected resting-state fMRI data of 27 animals (18 M. tonkeana and 9 M. mulatta, 18 females, age range 1.73–23.15 years). Of these, 11 also belonged to the behavioral cohort. To examine whether functional connectivity within attention networks relates to behavioral performance, we integrated behavioral data from this subset with behavioral metrics extracted within a six-month window preceding each subject's scan date. 

Results

We found that attention follows an inverted-U trajectory throughout the macaque lifespan, improving from childhood through adolescence (Fig. 1c, left), peaking in early adulthood (around 8.0 years), and declining in old age. This pattern closely parallels human development, in which sustained attention similarly peaks in young-to-middle adulthood before declining with age4. Distinct phenotypes emerged, including a subset of monkeys with persistent attentional deficits. Social status significantly influenced performance (Fig. 1c, right), and fronto-parietal connectivity—unlike purely visual connectivity—showed the same nonlinear rise and decline (Fig. 1d), robustly predicting attentional profiles.   

Conclusion

Together, these findings reveal fundamental principles of how attention develops, declines, and varies across individuals and social contexts, offering novel insights into the lifespan dynamics of cognition and its evolutionary foundations.
