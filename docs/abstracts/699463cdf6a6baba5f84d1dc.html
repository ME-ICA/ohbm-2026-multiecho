Introduction

Mood-congruent emotional processing bias is a core feature of Major Depressive Disorder (MDD), shaping attention, perception, and motivational responses and contributing to persistent symptomatology. 1,2 However, most neuroimaging research has relied on static emotional stimuli, limiting ecological validity. Dynamic emotional scenes may better capture affective demands encountered in real-world contexts. 3 In addition, cognitive control deficits and altered speech characteristics are frequently observed in MDD and may interact with emotional processing biases. 2,4,5 The present study used a naturalistic, multimodal approach to investigate behavioral, neural, and speech-based indicators of mood-congruent bias in MDD. We hypothesized that individuals with MDD show reduced performance and altered neural responses during unconscious emotional conflict, particularly when positive scenes are interrupted by negative subliminal cues.

Methods

A total of 126 right-handed adults (MDD: n=60; healthy controls: n=66), aged 18-55 years, completed a novel subliminal emotional priming paradigm during fMRI (Figure 1). Participants viewed emotionally positive or negative videos while facial expressions (happy/sad; 16.7 ms) were presented every second, either congruent or incongruent with the video content. The emotional expression of primer / video is either the same (no-conflict trial) or different (conflict trial). Participants classified the emotion of the video as either “happy”, “sad”, or “neutral” before they give a subjective rating on their own affective experience. Reaction times (RT) and accuracy were analyzed using linear mixed models. Functional MRI data were preprocessed in SPM12 and modeled using a full factorial design (primer × video emotion × group), FWE-cluster-corrected at p<0.05. Outside the scanner, participants narrated positive and negative autobiographical events; speech features were extracted and examined in relation to psychological measures. All procedures were approved by the institutional ethics committee and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. 

Results

Across participants, congruent trials produced faster and more accurate responses than incongruent trials (p<0.001). MDD participants showed slower RTs (p=0.013) and reported higher subjective difficulty (p<0.001) in emotion classification. Accuracy impairments were most pronounced when happy videos were disrupted by sad primes (interaction p=0.025). Neuroimaging revealed a main group effect with increased activation in the right inferior and middle occipital and inferior temporal gyri in MDD vs. controls (pFWE<0.05, Figure 2). Task-related contrasts demonstrated broader recruitment of frontal, parietal, temporal, and insular regions during emotional conflict (incongruent > congruent), indicating increased cognitive demand. Speech in MDD was characterized by reduced verbosity and loudness; however, associations between speech markers and emotion-regulation measures were not moderated by group after correction for multiple testing.

Conclusion

MDD is associated with heightened neural effort, reduced behavioral performance, and negative emotional interpretation during dynamic emotional conflict. Increased right occipito-temporal activation suggests compensatory processing mechanisms in MDD. The results support the view that cognitive control and emotional bias mechanisms jointly contribute to maladaptive mood-congruent processing patterns in depression and may reinforce symptom persistence. These findings highlight the value of naturalistic, multimodal paradigms for characterizing affective dysfunction in MDD and may inform future data-driven diagnostic tools integrating neuroimaging, behavior, and speech.
