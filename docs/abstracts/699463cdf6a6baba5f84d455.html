Introduction

Structural connectivity underpins the brain’s ability to generate behaviour, yet mapping these networks in larger primates remains challenging. To build accurate wiring diagrams, efforts are underway to acquire multimodal datasets integrating MRI with microscopy. These data enable deep phenotyping of selected brains to provide species-level “road maps”. However, robust tools for joint analysis of multimodal data remain under-developed.

We previously introduced a framework1 for jointly reconstructing “hybrid” fibre orientations from MRI and microscopy, merging the micrometre-scale microscopy details with the 3D nature of dMRI. After demonstrating this approach in a single macaque brain, we now extend this framework to improve its robustness, flexibility, and generalisation across datasets. We also include new features that enable more principled weighting of MRI and microscopy and better handling of regions with less-reliable microscopy.

A major development is the integration of polarisation-sensitive optical coherence tomography (PS-OCT). PS-OCT provides micrometre-scale microstructure and dense whole-brain imaging but typically yields only 2D fibre orientations or limited 3D information via specialised setups. By jointly modelling PS-OCT and dMRI, our method enables 3D fibre-orientation reconstruction and subsequent microscopy-informed tractography. These PS-OCT datasets are being acquired as part of a consortium effort (Centre for Mesoscale Connectomics), with numerous brains planned in the coming years, including macaques and humans, and brains sectioned along different axes. This presents an opportunity for detailed brain characterisation, motivating the development of robust and generalisable joint-analysis tools.

Here we develop an open, flexible tool that fuses MRI and PS-OCT to generate microscopy-informed connectomes, supporting broader deployment across primate datasets and enabling neuroanatomical insights.

Methods

DIFFUSION MRI ACQUISITION

High-resolution postmortem dMRI data were acquired from a macaque brain at 0.4-mm isotropic resolution using a 10.5 T scanner2. A diffusion-weighted steady-state free-precession (DW-SSFP) protocol was used with two shells (effective b = 3000 and 5700 s/mm²), including 6 b0 volumes and 60 diffusion-weighted volumes per shell. Image reconstruction followed the pipeline described in [3]. Fibre orientation distributions were estimated via the ball-and-stick model (BAS) using SSFP response kernels4 with 3 fibre populations per voxel, and 50 orientation samples per population (150 samples total).

PS-OCT DATA

PS-OCT provides depth-resolved measurements of birefringent tissue, here acquired in the same brain as the diffusion MRI with an effective voxel size of 5.5 × 5.5 × 3.3 µm. After imaging the tissue surface, a 200 µm layer was removed and the newly exposed surface was imaged. For each surface, en-face retardance and optic-axis orientation maps were obtained by weighted-averaging the PS-OCT data across depth. Although PS-OCT provides 3D volumetric data, the current system gives reliable estimates of the in-plane orientation of the optic-axis, but not its through-plane component5.

DIFFUSION MRI AND MICROSCOPY CO-REGISTRATION

Co-registration of the PS-OCT and dMRI data was achieved by reconstructing a stack of PS-OCT slides and transforming this stack into the 3D MRI space. Individual PS-OCT slices were stacked after estimating translations and rotations between successive cross-polarisation slides and the resulting transformations were concatenated to align all slides with the central slide. Next, this 3D microscopy volume was affine-transformed to the 3D MRI space using FSL FLIRT. PS-OCT orientations were rotated according to the affine transformation matrix (Figure 1B).

HYBRID ORIENTATION CONSTRUCTION

In each voxel, spatially resolved in-plane orientations were taken from the PS-OCT data. Then, samples from the BAS model fitted to the voxel dMRI data were used to provide a distribution of out-of-plane orientations. These were matched to the corresponding in-plane samples using a criterion that combines both the most similar in-plane angles and volume fractions of the BAS samples. Hybrid orientations were only constructed in pixels where the PS-OCT retardance exceeded a pre-specified threshold, to avoid including unreliable microscopy. The orientations were then combined over local neighbourhoods into fibre orientation distributions at a higher resolution than the dMRI data, here 0.2 mm. 

Results

Co-registration produced close alignment of 327 PS-OCT slices and MRI across the whole brain (Figure 1B), with tissue boundaries well matched in all axes (the PS-OCT was acquired coronally. This enabled robust voxel-wise joint modelling.

The resulting hybrid orientations (Figure 2) follow expected neuroanatomical patterns and faithfully trace known white matter pathways. Importantly, our hybrid reconstruction allows us to super-resolve neuroanatomical details, achieving an 8-fold improvement in spatial resolution compared with diffusion MRI (0.4 -> 0.2mm). This improved resolution reveals fine anatomical features which are difficult to discern from the diffusion MRI alone, including the detailed subcortical architecture and connectivity (white arrows) and diencephalic pathways (far right panel). These improvements are made possible by the precise spatial localisation provided by PS-OCT. 

Notably, though the PS-OCT provides only coronal 2D orientations, the hybrid approach resolves many through-plane (AP) fibres, including those in the fornix, subcortex and projecting into the brainstem. Among these are fine, tightly organised anterior–posterior fascicles transversing the thalamus and caudate, which cannot be reliably resolved from either modality in isolation (due to the 2D nature of the PS-OCT and limited spatial resolution of the MRI), highlighting the power of their combined analysis.

Conclusion

We demonstrated precise MRI-microscopy co-registration and whole-brain hybrid diffusion MRI–PSOCT fibre orientation reconstruction with super-resolution in a macaque brain. Our multimodal fusion framework produces high-resolution fibre orientations that better capture anatomical details and small fibre bundles which are challenging to detect from diffusion MRI alone. The hybrid outputs can be directly input to tractography, paving the way for future reconstruction of a microscopy-informed connectome. Future work aims to construct mesoscale whole-brain connectomes in macaque and human brains, and to validate these results using paired tract-tracing data.
