Introduction

Coarse-grained information, such as the average response across vertices within a cortical region, typically reflects broad category distinctions (e.g., faces), whereas fine-grained information embedded in multivariate response patterns carries more detailed distinctions within the category, such as identity, head angle, and gender. Prior work shows that fine-scale functional architecture exhibits substantial individual variability, often obscured by idiosyncratic functional–anatomical alignment and recoverable through hyperalignment.1 Prior works have linked idiosyncratic responses to naturalistic stimuli to behavioral traits at the parcel level, emphasizing coarse-scale information. Here, we apply hyperalignment to examine responses to a 3.5-minute cartoon movie clip (“The Present”)  in 300 children and adolescents (ages 5-21, 90 females) and assess how individual differences in coarse- and fine-grained  movie-viewing responses relate to individual differences in chronological age. 

Methods

We analyzed data from the Healthy Brain Network (HBN) Biobank2, which includes children and adolescents (ages 5-21) with diverse clinical profiles recruited across four sites in the New York City area. The HBN initiative, launched by the Child Mind Institute, provides rich  multimodal data to facilitate developmental and clinical research. This study was approved by Chesapeake Institutional Review Board. Preprocessed structural data (Freesurfer 6.0.1) and raw BIDS-formatted data were downloaded from the Reproducibility Brain Charts (RBC)3 site, which curates four developmental neuroimaging datasets with harmonized phenotypes and extensive quality-control metrics. Functional and structural images were then preprocessed with fMRIPrep (version 25.0.0) using default parameters, followed by custom Python scripts for downstream processing.These steps included motion correction, brain extraction, and bias-field correction of T1-weighted images. The left and right cortical surfaces from each subject were registered to the onavg-ico32 surface template4 via a one-step resampling process based on cortical folding patterns (anatomical alignment). The final discovery dataset included 300 subjects who completed both movie fMRI runs (“Despicable Me” and “The Present”), and passed RBC structural and functional quality control from the RUBIC, CBIC, and CUNY sites up to HBN Release 9. T1-weighted images were acquired with 0.8×0.8×0.8 mm resolution (TR = 2500 ms, TE = 3.15 ms, TI = 1060 ms, flip angle = 8°). BOLD images were acquired with 2.4×2.4×2.4 mm resolution (TR = 800 ms, TE = 30 ms, flip angle = 31°, multiband acceleration = 6). A full description of HBN study design and imaging data acquisition is available at the HBN data portal and in the project overview2. 

Functional hyperalignment was performed using connectivity patterns to ico-8 vertices from an independent “Despicable Me” movie run (~10 min) in each HBN subject using a generalized procrustes transformation.  Subjects were aligned to a whole-brain functional template constructed from an independent adult dataset collected during viewing of “The Grand Budapest Hotel”5. This dataset (“Budapest”) included 25 participants (13 female, mean age = 27.52 years) scanned at the Dartmouth Brain Imaging Center using a 3T Siemens Magnetom Prisma with a 32-channel head coil. The study was approved by the Dartmouth Committee for the Protection of Human Subjects. These data were preprocessed with a pipeline similar to HBN using fMRIPrep 20.2.7 and registered to the onavg-ico32 surface templates. Connectivity data from all 5 movie runs (~1 hr) in 10 of the adults in the Budapest dataset were used to construct a functional template.

Full fine-grained responses consisted of the multivariate response patterns within a searchlight, which can be separated into coarse-grained responses, defined as the mean fMRI BOLD response time-series across all vertices in a searchlight, and residual fine-grained responses, obtained by subtracting the searchlight mean from each vertex’s response.1 Searchlights were centered on 1,210 onavg-ico8 vertices with a 20-mm radius applied to data registered to onavg-ico32 surfaces4. Intersubject correlation in movie-evoked responses (rISC) was calculated as the Pearson’s correlation of fMRI responses in each subject pair. The resulting response individual-differences matrices (IDMs) summarize pairwise rISC across all 300 subjects (Figure 1A). An age-difference IDM was calculated as the negative absolute values of chronological age differences for each pair of subjects, such that smaller age differences yield values near zero and larger differences yield more negative values (Figure 1B). Due to the symmetry in the IDMs, at each searchlight, the lower triangles of the response IDMs were correlated with the lower triangle of age-difference IDM using Spearman’s rank correlation. Statistical significance was assessed using two-tailed Mantel tests with 10,000 permutations.

Results

rISC across searchlights doubled from r = 0.022 ± 0.016 in anatomically aligned data, to 0.045 ± 0.030 in hyperaligned data (example searchlight in Figure 1A). Additionally, the correlations between response IDMs and the age-difference IDM were higher after hyperalignment (rho = 0.040 ± 0.023 to 0.053 ± 0.023,  t(1209) = 46.34, p < 0.001, Figure 1C-E). The percentage of searchlights with a significant correlation with the age-difference IDM increased from 63.1% in anatomically aligned data to 79.1% in hyperaligned data. 

Since hyperaligned rISCs contain intersubject correspondence for both coarse-grained and fine-grained rISCs, we calculated rISCs for searchlight mean time-series (coarse-grained) and for residual time-series after subtracting the searchlight mean (residual fine-grained; Figure 2A) to distinguish the contributions of these two spatial scales of functional architecture. Coarse-grained responses showed stronger synchronization (r = 0.094 ± 0.053) than fine-grained responses (r = 0.008 ± 0.007), reflecting the data smoothing inherent in coarse-scale analyses. Coarse-grained response IDMs correlated with the age-difference IDM in the ventral and lateral temporal, lateral occipital and medial parietal cortices (Figure 2B). Residual fine-grained IDMs had generally stronger correlations with the age-difference IDM, especially in superior and inferior prefrontal cortices, regions previously reported to have higher variation across individuals and to be associated with maturity and executive function in children, as well as medial parietal and frontal cortices (Figure 2C). Hyperalignment led to a small increase in the correlation of  coarse-grained response IDMs with the age-difference IDM after hyperalignment (rho = 0.028 ± 0.023 to 0.032 ± 0.024, t(1209) = 20.27, p < 0.001, Figure 2B) but a larger increase in the correlation of the residual fine-grained response IDMs with the age-difference IDM (rho = 0.033 ± 0.018 to 0.065 ± 0.021, t(1209) = 84.79, p < 0.001, Figure 2C). This suggests that residual fine-grained responses after hyperalignment reveal more developmentally relevant information, possibly reflecting changes in functional architecture due to learning. 

Conclusion

In sum, hyperalignment uncovers developmentally meaningful fine-scale neural signatures that are largely obscured by anatomical alignment alone. Residual fine-grained patterns, in particular, show strong age-related organization, emphasizing the value of multivariate functional alignment for developmental neuroscience. More broadly, these findings suggest that age-related changes in cognition and experience may be encoded in individualized, fine-scale cortical architecture.
