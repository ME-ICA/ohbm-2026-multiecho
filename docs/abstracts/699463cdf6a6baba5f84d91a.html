Introduction

Stroke is a leading cause of disability worldwide. Non-invasive brain stimulation, such as repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS), is a potential adjunct therapy for motor rehabilitation when delivered prior to skilled motor practice. After stroke, rTMS may alter excitability by reducing contralesional hemispheric inhibition. Continuous theta burst stimulate (cTBS) is a patterned form of rTMS that can be delivered in a short amount of time, but individual responses vary. Previous work showed that motor cortical interneuron connectivity is a factor in predicting response to cTBS.1 Single pulse TMS delivered in the anterior-posterior (AP) direction results in longer latency motor evoked potentials (MEPs) than the posterior-anterior (PA). Latency differences are attributed to polysynaptic distance from corticospinal neurons. Activation of corticospinal neurons is measured by pulses directed lateral-medial (LM).2 Healthy individuals showing longer AP-LM MEP onset latencies are more likely to show the expected reduction in excitability following cTBS, likely due to recruitment of cortical regions further from M11. The relationship between AP-LM MEP onset latency and response to cTBS has not been examined after stroke where damage to the brain and recovery is variable. 

Resting state functional MRI (rsfMRI) maps network connectivity changes at the individual level. Past studies used rsfMRI to probe changes in the sensorimotor network after multiple rTMS sessions in individuals with stroke;3 however, the immediate effects of cTBS on cortical activity have not been examined. 

The current study aimed to understand if resting state functional connectivity in the sensorimotor network after cTBS is associated with AP-LM MEP latency after stroke. We predicted that individuals with stroke showing longer AP-LM MEP onset latency in the contralesional hemisphere would exhibit reduced connectivity between M1 and sensorimotor regions after cTBS.

Methods

Data from 24 chronic stroke participants were used (8 females, mean age = 67.51 ± 12.84 years). A within-subjects design was employed wherein all participants completed three sessions (Figure 1). A separate TMS mapping session measured directional TMS MEP onset latencies. This was followed by two MRI sessions consisting of: acquisition of structural T1-weighted (T1) and rsfMRI (10 minutes), followed by sham or cTBS over the contralesional cortex, and a repeat T1 and rsfMRI. The order of sham and cTBS stimulation was randomized across participants. 

MEP Onset: 20 single pulses of TMS were delivered over the M1 representation of the contralesional extensor carpi radialis (ECR) muscle in the AP, PA, at 110% of resting motor threshold and in the LM direction at 150% of resting motor threshold. A Magstim 2002 unit and figure of eight coil were used for stimulation delivery. MEP responses were analyzed using a combination of the TMS Toolbox and visual inspection.3 MEP onset was defined where the MEP, greater than 0.5 standard deviation compared to the background muscle activity, began relative to the TMS artefact. Onset time for each direction was averaged over trials and latency differences between mean AP and LM, and PA and AP were calculated.

Resting State: Participants laid supine in an MRI scanner (Phillips 3.0T with an 8-channel sensitivity encoding head coil [SENSE factor = 2.4] and parallel imaging). Structural, high-resolution T1 image anatomical and resting state functional (blood-oxygen level dependent scans [10 minute] with a single shot EPI sequence) images were collected. During rsfMRI scanning subjects were instructed to look at a fixation cross, think of nothing, and stay awake. Brain-extraction and segmentation were completed using FSL (version 6.0.7.11) for structural data and CONN (version 22.v2407) for functional data. Functional image pre-processing was completed with CONN and all images were normalized to MNI space. Brains with left hemisphere stroke were flipped along the x-axis prior to statistical analyses, such that all lesions were represented in the right hemisphere. Seed regions in the contralesional hemisphere were defined a priori based on the canonical sensorimotor network and regions known to be critical for motor recovery and compensation. These included the precentral gyrus (PreCG), postcentral gyrus (PostCG), juxtapositional lobule (SMA), superior frontal gyrus (SFG), and middle frontal gyrus (MidFG) using the Harvard-Oxford Atlas in CONN. ROI-to-ROI connectivity analysis was conducted for four contralesional sensorimotor regions (PreCG to PostCG, SMA, SFG, and MidFG). Individual Fischer-Z scores for each region, indexing connectivity, were extracted. Change in connectivity was measured as the difference in Fischer-Z score post and pre cTBS. All statistical analysis were conducted in RStudio (version 2024.12.0+467). 

cTBS: Active or sham cTBS was applied at 80% RMT over the same non-paretic ECR M1 hotspot used for directional TMS mapping. cTBS consisted of three pulses at 50 Hz delivered in 200 bursts at a rate of 5 Hz (40 second duration). Stimulation was delivered with a Magstim Rapid2 (Magstim, UK) via a figure 8 air cooled coil. A custom, identical looking and sounding coil was used to produce the sham stimulation.

Results

A linear mixed-effects (LME) model was used to determine whether resting-state connectivity changes between M1 each ROI were moderated by individual AP-LM MEP onset time. Each model included stimulation type, AP-LM onset latency, and their interaction as fixed effects with a random intercept for participant, accounting for within-subjects design. Average AP-LM MEP onset time was 3.44 ± 2.14ms compared to the average PA-LM being 2.23 ± 1.73 ms, confirming successful preferential recruitment of direction sensitive cortical neurons.

Analysis of the four sensorimotor connections revealed that AP-LM MEP onset time moderated change in connectivity between PreCG and PostCG for sham vs. active stimulation. No significant interaction effects were observed for the SMA, SFG, or MidFG connectivity. Results from the LME model revealed a significant stimulation type by AP-LM onset time interaction (β = -0.076, SE = 0.032, p = 0.027) (Figure 2). There was a significant main effect of stimulation type at the intercept (AP-LM MEP latency = 0), indicating less change in connectivity in the active stimulation compared to sham  (β = 0.27, SE = 0.12, p = 0.036). However, neither interaction nor main effect reached the threshold for statistical significance after FDR correction for multiple comparisons (padj=0.108; padj=0.144), suggesting a trend in our data. 

Conclusion

Overall, we found that the response of the sensorimotor network, specifically from the contralesional preCG to the postCG, to a single bout of cTBS was associated with AP-LM onset time. We observed a trend in our data such that longer AP-LM onset times were associated with decreased connectivity between these regions after cTBS, relative to sham stimulation. Other contralesional sensorimotor regions (SMA, SFG, MidFG) did not exhibit change in connectivity following cTBS. Our results are to be interpreted with caution considering the effects did not survive correction for multiple pairwise comparison.

In previous work, longer AP-LM onset time in young, healthy individuals was an indicator of response to cTBS1 (a decrease in MEP amplitude). We are the first to investigate this property in stroke and to show an effect using resting state fMRI. Our findings trend towards this same conclusion, showing that individuals with longer AP-LM MEP onset times elicited from the contralesional hemisphere did exhibit decreased functional connectivity between the PreCG and the PostCG, after cTBS delivery over the contralesional M1. Functionally, these ROIs represent connectivity between the motor and somatosensory cortices. Recent work using probabilistic tractography modelling of TMS stimulation in the AP direction showed selective activation of excitatory projection fibres from M1 to the primary somatosensory cortex4. Previous literature has proposed that longer AP MEP onset times are attributed to greater synaptic distance from the CST. While high intensity LM stimulation can preferentially recruit CST neurons, lower intensity PA and AP MEP onset timing may reflect preferential recruitment of mono and polysynaptic activation to the CST, respectively. Longer AP-LM MEP onset suggests a larger synaptic distance to the CST and a higher likelihood of contralesional recruitment of cortical areas by M1. Changes in M1 connectivity as a result of stroke and subsequent recovery may impact response to cTBS. Our data indicates that recruitment of these sensorimotor cortical areas, specifically areas in the postcentral gyrus, may be a prerequisite for expected response to cTBS. Continuing to build long-term neurophysiological profiles of individuals with stroke will help identify key characteristics for targeted TMS therapy and confirm this trend.

Importantly, our data suggest that immediate response to cTBS and individual variation may be detected through rsfMRI and MEP onset, similar to data from young healthy individuals. We provide evidence for further investigation into TMS MEP onset as an indicator for typical cTBS aftereffects in stroke, which is necessary to improve efficacy of cTBS delivery for motor rehabilitation. As cTBS grows in use as an adjunct therapy for motor rehabilitation, it is important to understand how it changes intrinsic sensorimotor brain connectivity and continue to define who may benefit from this type of intervention.
