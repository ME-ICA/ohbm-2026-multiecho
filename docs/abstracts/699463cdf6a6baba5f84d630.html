Introduction

Major depression (MD) is a prevalent and debilitating disorder. Its symptomatology, frequency, and treatment efficacy rates are connected to individual factors such as sex and gender, age, MD duration, degree of treatment resistance, chronicity and/or severity, and physical and psychiatric comorbidities. Among these factors, sex differences have drawn increasing attention as MD is characterized by prominent sexual dimorphism related to presentation rates, with differences becoming noticeable during puberty, and becoming less prominent after menopause.^1 Females are twice as likely to be affected by MD and experience more comorbid conditions than males, but males are more likely to die from depression-related suicide.^2 Differences in brain function also exist. For example, a recent multisite fMRI study examined resting-state functional connectivity and identified sex differences mainly among the default mode (DMN), frontoparietal network (FPN), and visual (VIS) networks.^3 Importantly, while prior work has focused on sex differences in symptoms and brain features at single time points, little is known about sex differences in how treatment modulates network connectivity over time. In the current study, we used a multisite resting-state fMRI dataset collected as part of the Canadian Biomarker Integration Network in Depression 1 (CAN-BIND-1) study from patients undergoing pharmacotherapy.^4 We examined sex-differences in brain features at baseline prior to treatment and tracked whether pharmacotherapy differentially modulated network connectivity over the course of treatment.

Methods

Three fMRI scans (baseline, week 2, week 8) from 88 adults with MD enrolled in the CAN-BIND Phase 1 study were used in this study. Participants were 18–60 years old (55 females, 33 males) and met CAN-BIND diagnostic and exclusion criteria.  Patients were treated for 16 weeks with escitalopram, and an add-on of aripiprazole after 8 weeks if symptoms decreased less than 50%.^4

FMRI data were acquired on 3T MRI scanners at the six sites and included 10 minutes of eyes‑open resting‑state acquisition. Blood‑oxygenation-level dependent (BOLD) images were collected using echo‑planar imaging (EPI) with repetition time = 2 s, echo time = 25–30 ms, flip angle = 75° or 90°, field of view = 256 mm, matrix = 64 × 64, and 34–40 axial slices acquired interleaved. Quality control ensured data comparability across scanners. Preprocessing followed the Ontario Pipeline for Preprocessing of Neuroimaging Data (OPPNI), including motion correction, slice-timing correction, outlier censoring, spatial smoothing, PHYCAA+ physiological noise removal, nuisance regression (motion, global signal), low-pass filtering (0.10 Hz), and normalization to MNI152 space.

For functional connectivity estimation, preprocessed resting‑state fMRI data were resampled to 1-mm isotropic resolution and parcellated using the Schaefer-2018 atlas (200 parcels, 7-networks); for each parcel, mean time series were extracted and 200×200 Pearson correlation matrices were computed, with the diagonal zeroed out and computed Fisher’s z‑transformed correlation matrices.

To examine sex differences at the level of large‑scale networks, parcels were grouped into the dorsal attention (DAN), DMN, FPN,limbic (LIM), somatomotor (SMN), ventral attention (VAN) and visual (VIS) networks, and we calculated within‑network connectivity (within FC), between‑network connectivity (between FC), positive‑only connectivity and segregation indices (SI). Baseline functional connectivity differences were assessed using independent t-tests and Cohen’s d (d), with multiple comparisons controlled and corrected (q) using the Benjamini–Hochberg False-Discovery Rate (FDR) method.

To determine whether pharmacotherapy differentially altered brain networks over time in females and males, we evaluated connectivity trajectories across baseline, week 2, and week 8. Changes were modeled using linear mixed-effects models with fixed effects of time, sex, and their interaction (time×sex), and participant-level random intercepts. 

Results

We identified baseline functional connectivity differences across networks. Notably, males exhibited significantly higher Within FC and Within FC Positive values in the DMN (q < 0.01), suggesting stronger internal coherence of this network at rest. Additionally, Between FC Positive values were consistently higher in males across all networks (e.g., DAN: q = 0.004; DMN: q = 0.0009; FPN: q = 0.0009; LIM: q = 0.002; SMN: q = 0.003; VAN: q = 0.003; VIS: q = 0.004), indicating greater inter-network synchronization. SI Positive values also revealed sex differences favouring females, particularly in VIS (q = 0.002), SMN (q = 0.022), and FPN (q = 0.022).

Cohen’s d values for each measure–network combination further revealed large effect sizes were in the DMN for Within FC and Within FC Positive, across several networks (e.g., DAN: d =0.85; DMN: d = 0.84; FPN: d = 0.74; VIS: d = 0.66). Negative values in SI Positive for certain networks (e.g., VIS: d = -0.81; SMN: d = -0.60; FPN: d = -0.58) suggest relatively higher network segregation in females. Overall, the effect sizes were moderate to large. 

To investigate longitudinal changes in network function, we fit linear mixed-effects models for each network measure with time×sex interaction terms, using subject ID as a random effect to account for repeated measures. Importantly, VIS SI Positive showed a significant main effect of time (p = 0.001) and a significant time × sex interaction (p = 0.002), indicating that males and females followed distinct trajectories across the 8-week period. At baseline, females exhibited higher VIS SI Positive values than males (baseline sex difference: q < 0.01), reflecting greater network segregation at the start of treatment. Over time, however, the two groups moved in opposite directions. Females showed a gradual decline from baseline to week 2, followed by a slight stabilization toward week 8, resulting in an overall decreasing trend across the treatment period. In contrast, males showed an initial slight decrease at week 2 but then showed an increase from week 2 to week 8. These opposing patterns produced a convergence of VIS SI Positive values by week 8, with males rising toward values comparable to females, despite beginning the study at significantly lower levels. The interaction results therefore reflect sex-specific changes of visual network segregation, with males exhibiting a stronger treatment-related increase compared with the more stable trajectory seen in females.

VIS Between FC Positive showed a significant main effect of time (p = 0.019) and a significant time × sex interaction (p = 0.039), indicating that males and females changed in different directions over the treatment period. At baseline, males showed higher VIS Between FC Positive values than females (baseline sex difference: q < 0.05). Across time, males showed a gradual decline from baseline through week 8 and females, in contrast, showed a marked increase from baseline to week 2, followed by relative stabilization between week 2 and week 8. In the LIM SI Positive network, a significant main effect (p = 0.016) and interaction (p = 0.017) were observed, with males showing progressive increases in segregation and females showing reductions.

Conclusion

We examined sex-differences in large-scale functional connectivity to understand whether males and females enter treatment with distinct network configurations, and how reorganization in these networks evolve over the course of treatment. Our findings demonstrated distinct sex-specific large-scale connectivity patterns at baseline; males exhibited higher functional connectivity within the DMN, and females exhibited greater network segregation among SMN, FPN, and VIS. Overall, these are similar to the networks highlighted by Wang et al.,^3 but methodological differences likely led to some inconsistencies in the specifics of connectivity profiles. We next examined if males and females followed distinct patterns of functional network reorganization over eight weeks of treatment and showed that VIS and LIM followed different trajectories. For the VIS network, females and males exhibited segregation index and functional connectivity differences at baseline and converged with reorganisation by week 8. The LIM network also showed differences in segregation indices at baseline, but convergence was greatest at week 2. The prominence of VIS network changes over the course of treatment were somewhat surprising yet consistent with previous work showing abnormally enhanced activity and connectivity of visual regions in MD,^5 as well as sex differences in VIS connectivity.^3 Taken together, our results suggest that males and females may enter treatment with distinct network configurations and follow distinct patterns of functional network reorganization during pharmacotherapy treatment. These findings have implications for potential sex-specific therapeutic strategies, which may improve clinical outcomes for a prevalent and disabling condition.
