Introduction

Patients suffering from hearing loss have difficulties in understanding spoken language which results in an impaired ability to comprehend semantics. Cochlear implants (CIs) are an effective treatment for restoring hearing and improving speech understanding. However, there are still major individual differences in speech intelligibility outcomes following CI-treatment and the underlying neural mechanisms remain to be fully understood. Correlations between hearing ability and cognitive abilities could help to explain the variability between individuals in postoperative speech comprehension following cochlear implant treatment.

This project investigates neural processes (electrophysiological and vascular correlates) involved in semantic processing during rehabilitation after CI surgery and examines the potential impact of additional cognitive training on speech comprehension.

Methods

Two groups of patients (>50 years) with bilateral hearing loss who have been fitted with a CI are studied. All patients participating in the study undergo hearing training as part of the clinical routine. While the hearing training group (n=13) receives additional hearing tasks to perform at home after surgery, the cognitive training group (n=16) uses an app-based training programme designed to improve cognitive skills at home.

Six months after CI activation, we simultaneously record electroencephalography (EEG) and functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) while participants listen to semantically correct and incorrect sentences. Event-related potential (ERP) analyses are performed to investigate fast dynamic neural processes, and haemodynamic responses (fNIRS) are analysed to localise the underlying brain regions in the two groups. 

Results

Our preliminary ERP results indicate an early starting and more positive response to semantically incorrect compared to correct sentences in both groups which potentially indicates a P300 ERP component. Patients who used the cognitive training app appear to show an additional trend towards a delayed onset of the N400, which typically indicates the processing of semantics in individuals with normal hearing. Patients in the cognitive training group showed larger negative amplitudes for semantically incorrect sentences compared to correct ones. This typical language-related process does not seem to occur in patients in the hearing training group.

Preliminary fNIRS results also indicate stronger activation in the cognitive training group than in the hearing training group. This appears to be especially apparent when processing semantically correct stimuli.

Conclusion

The possible P300 with greater positivity when processing semantically incorrect sentences compared to correct ones probably reflects an attentional response rather than a linguistic process. This indicates that rehabilitation continues to be an active process six months following surgery. The tendency towards an N400 observed in the cognitive training group suggests the initiation of linguistic processes. This could imply that semantic processing improves after six months of cognitive training and would also align with the preliminary fNIRS results we found.

This project uses EEG and fNIRS to investigate the importance of brain adaptation after CI surgery. Preliminary results suggest that CI users exhibit altered neural processing of semantics compared to individuals without hearing loss. The potentially enhanced and more efficient semantic processing observed in the cognitive training group may help disentangle the relationship between cognitive abilities and speech comprehension.

Further analyses and our ongoing project will provide a better understanding of individual adaptation processes after cochlear implant operation and may contribute to the optimization of hearing rehabilitation.
