Introduction

Functional MRI  encodes identity, cognitive states, behavioral traits, and pathology. Yet current approaches isolate single dimensions, requiring separate models for fingerprinting, task decoding, behavioral prediction, and clinical diagnosis. This fragmentation limits efficiency, obscures relationships between dimensions, and prevents identification of distinct neural substrates. A unified framework of multi-task learning would enable discovery of shared and dissociable mechanisms underlying brain function across individual, cognitive, and clinical domains. Recent deep learning methods have advanced single-task fMRI analysis but remain task-specific and do not leverage both spatial and temporal information. Mamba-based architectures offer efficient long-sequence modeling¹, yet existing variants target individual objectives without multi-task scalability. No framework jointly models temporal dynamics and functional connectivity (FC) to reveal how brain organization relies on distinct or overlapping neural circuits.

We present MAMBAxBrain, a dual-stream framework integrating Mamba's selective state-space model¹ for temporal modeling with FC-based spatial encoding. Through learnable fusion and task-specific heads, a single backbone achieves state-of-the-art performance across four domains: brain fingerprinting, cognitive task decoding, reaction time prediction, and schizophrenia diagnosis. Multi-task experiments demonstrate positive transfer across tasks, with joint training improving generalization to unseen objectives. Interpretability analyses reveal task-specific neural signatures: individual identity relies on higher-order networks, cognitive states engage sensory-motor systems, reaction time prediction depends on subcortical-motor-limbic circuits, and schizophrenia classification identifies disrupted top-down control of sensory streams. Multi-task learning enables discovery of biologically dissociable mechanisms underlying diverse dimensions of brain function.

Methods

Dual-stream architecture.

MAMBAxBrain integrates temporal dynamics and functional connectivity through a dual-stream neural architecture. The temporal stream employs Mamba's selective state-space model (SSM)¹ to capture long-range dependencies in BOLD time series with linear computational complexity. For an input fMRI segment X ∈ ℝ^(L×D) (L timepoints, D regions), the SSM recursively updates hidden states through input-dependent transition matrices, enabling selective propagation of salient temporal features while attenuating noise. The FC stream computes pairwise functional connectivity from the same input using Pearson correlation, vectorizes the upper-triangular connectivity matrix, and encodes it through multilayer perceptrons. A learnable fusion module concatenates both embeddings and applies nonlinear transformation to produce a unified spatiotemporal representation z. Lightweight task-specific heads then map z to predictions for each objective, enabling multi-task learning with a shared backbone (Figure 1a-c).

Datasets and preprocessing.

We utilized 770 subjects from the Human Connectome Project Young Adult (HCP-YA) dataset², each with 4 resting-state and 14 task fMRI runs across 7 cognitive tasks (motor, working memory, gambling, language, social, relational, emotion); a subset of 45 subjects were rescanned 4-6 months later (HCP Test-Retest) for cross-session generalization assessment. A separate clinical cohort from UCSF comprised 93 healthy controls and 45 schizophrenia patients. HCP data were processed using the Micapipe pipeline³, yielding time series from 114 regions (100 cortical parcels from Schaefer atlas⁴, 14 subcortical from FreeSurfer⁵). Clinical data were processed using fMRIPrep with 86 regions (Desikan-Killiany atlas). All time series underwent bandpass filtering (0.01-0.1 Hz for HCP, 0.01-0.25 Hz for clinical), ICA-AROMA denoising, and z-score normalization per region.

Task configuration and sampling.

A sliding-window approach generated fixed-length samples from variable-length time series. Window sizes were task-optimized: 100 timepoints for fingerprinting and cognitive decoding, 464 for reaction time prediction, and 70 for schizophrenia classification. Stride was fixed at 1, generating 3.4M samples for fingerprinting/cognitive tasks, 4,347 for reaction time, and 5,796 for schizophrenia. The large sample sizes for classification tasks reflect the sliding-window strategy applied to multi-session data.

Experimental design.

Three experimental paradigms evaluated model performance. (1) Single-task experiments assessed in-domain accuracy on HCP-YA using 70:30 train-test splits (60:40 for schizophrenia). (2) Cross-session experiments evaluated generalization by training on HCP-YA and testing on HCP Test-Retest data (45 subjects scanned 4-6 months later). (3) Multi-task experiments examined transfer learning by training on base tasks (fingerprinting alone, fingerprinting+cognitive decoding, or all three HCP tasks jointly) and fine-tuning on held-out tasks using 20% of target data. Performance was compared against state-of-the-art baselines including SPDNet, BrainNetTransformer, standard Mamba, and FST-Mamba.

Model training and interpretation.

All models were implemented in PyTorch and trained on NVIDIA RTX 3080 Ti GPU using AdamW optimizer. Learning rates ranged from 1e-4 to 8e-4, batch size was 128, and training ran for 60 epochs with early stopping. For multi-task training, task-specific losses were combined with weighted composite loss, assigning higher weight to regression tasks for balanced optimization. Interpretability was assessed by computing edge-importance weights from learned FC representations, aggregating parcel-level scores into canonical resting-state networks, and mapping spatial distributions of predictive features.

Results

Single-task performance and cross-session generalization

MAMBAxBrain achieved state-of-the-art performance across all four tasks (Figure 1d-f). On in-domain evaluation (HCP-YA dataset), brain fingerprinting accuracy reached 96%, cognitive task decoding 99.5%, reaction time prediction R²=0.92, and schizophrenia classification 91% (AUC=0.95) (Figure 1d). These results exceeded all baselines (Figure 1d), with improvements of 5.6% for schizophrenia diagnosis and 3.0% for reaction time prediction over the next-best method (FST-Mamba). Under cross-session evaluation on independent HCP Test-Retest data collected 4-6 months later, performance remained robust: fingerprinting accuracy was 95% and cognitive decoding 99% (Figure 1e-f). Critically, accuracy drops were minimal (1.13% for fingerprinting, 0.54% for cognitive decoding; Figure 1e-f), whereas the best baseline (FST-Mamba) showed substantially larger declines (7.15% and 2.70%, respectively), demonstrating superior cross-session stability.

Multi-task learning and transfer

Multi-task experiments revealed positive transfer across objectives. When trained solely on fingerprinting, the model achieved 96% accuracy but failed to generalize to unseen tasks (cognitive decoding: 46%, reaction time R²=0.25). Joint training on fingerprinting and cognitive decoding improved both tasks (95% and 99%, respectively) while substantially boosting reaction time prediction to R²=0.72 without direct training, demonstrating successful transfer of behavior-relevant representations. Training on all three tasks jointly maintained high accuracy and further improved reaction time to R²=0.91, indicating that incorporating complementary tasks promotes learning of richer, more transferable spatiotemporal features.

Task-specific neural signatures

Interpretability analyses revealed highly dissociable, biologically plausible circuit mechanisms underlying each prediction task (Figure 2). Individual identity relied on higher-order association cortices, specifically connections within and between the default mode network, control network, and dorsal attention network (Figure 2a). In striking contrast, cognitive task decoding was driven by primary sensory-motor systems, predominantly the visual, somatomotor, and dorsal attention networks, reflecting stereotyped engagement of task-positive regions (Figure 2b). Reaction time prediction depended on a distinct subcortical-motor-limbic circuit, with critical features involving subcortical connections to somatomotor, ventral attention, and limbic networks, consistent with classical motor execution pathways and arousal-dependent processing speed (Figure 2c). Schizophrenia classification identified disrupted top-down control circuits linking frontoparietal and cingulo-opercular hubs (anterior cingulate cortex, insula) to auditory and visual association regions (superior temporal sulcus, fusiform gyrus), aligning with the dysconnection hypothesis (Figure 2d).

Conclusion

We present MAMBAxBrain, a unified neural framework that addresses a fundamental challenge in neuroimaging: the lack of computational models capable of jointly analyzing the multidimensional information embedded in fMRI data. By integrating Mamba-based temporal modeling with functional connectivity analysis through a dual-stream architecture, MAMBAxBrain achieves state-of-the-art performance across four representative domains—individual identification, cognitive task decoding, behavioral prediction, and clinical diagnosis—within a single, scalable backbone (Figure 1d-f). The framework demonstrates exceptional cross-session generalization, with minimal performance degradation (1.13% and 0.54% drops; Figure 1e-f) compared to substantially larger declines in baseline methods, indicating robust learning of stable spatiotemporal representations that transfer across scanning sessions. Critically, multi-task learning experiments reveal that these dimensions are not independent but share transferable features within a common representational space. Joint training on fingerprinting and cognitive decoding enabled successful prediction of reaction time (R²=0.72) without direct training on behavioral data, demonstrating positive transfer across individual, cognitive, and behavioral domains. This finding suggests that seemingly distinct neuroimaging objectives can be unified within a shared multi-task framework, enabling more efficient model development and revealing relationships between dimensions that remain hidden in single-task approaches.

Beyond achieving superior accuracy, interpretability analyses demonstrate that MAMBAxBrain learns biologically dissociable mechanisms for each task rather than relying on diffuse, task-agnostic features (Figure 2). The framework correctly identifies that individual identity is encoded in higher-order association networks (default mode, control, dorsal attention; Figure 2a), cognitive states engage primary sensory-motor systems (visual, somatomotor; Figure 2b), behavioral speed depends on subcortical-motor-limbic circuits reflecting arousal and motor execution pathways (Figure 2c), and schizophrenia manifests as disrupted top-down control linking frontoparietal and cingulo-opercular hubs to sensory streams (Figure 2d).

MAMBAxBrain represents a significant step toward a foundation model for fMRI analysis, demonstrating that diverse neuroimaging objectives spanning identity, cognition, behavior, and pathology—can be solved within a unified, interpretable, and biologically grounded framework. This work provides a scalable approach for multi-task fMRI modeling and establishes a foundation for future development of generalizable neuroimaging biomarkers applicable across cognitive neuroscience, behavioral prediction, and precision psychiatry.
