Introduction

Sign language experts engaging in a signed conversation maintain eye contact, using central vision to process facial expressions, and peripheral vision to process manual signs. This visuo-spatial language communication relies heavily on information from both faces and hands for language comprehension. How does this unique visual requirement change the way the visual world is represented in the brain? Specifically, we ask - How do face and hand representations in visual cortex adapt to allow for efficient sign language processing? Are those changes in visual representation consistent in both hearing and deaf sign language experts, or distinct when comparing deaf and hearing signers? These questions can shed important light on our brain’s ability to adapt to our visual requirements in two unique populations and settings- (1) when they arise in childhood alongside auditory deprivation, and (2) in licensed Sign Language Interpreters with 10+ years of experience, who learnt sign language in adulthood. We hypothesised that experienced signers would have larger representations of hands- specifically in lateral visual regions which are known to specialize in processing objects in motion 1. 

Methods

To test this hypothesis we collected structural and functional 3T-MRI scans from 33 healthy adults- 11 deaf sign language experts (DE; F=8, mean age=46), 11 hearing sign language experts (HE; F=11, mean age=47), and 11 hearing controls with no sign language experience (C; F=7, mean age=45). To identify regions selective to different visual categories, we used an fMRI Visual Category Localizer 2, including the following categories: faces, hands, feet, full bodies (faces cropped out), houses, pseudowords, cars, and a baseline gray image. Using a GLM, we fit predictors to the data and estimated within each voxel, the response amplitudes for each condition (betas), and residual variance of each voxel’s time course. The beta values and residual variance from the GLM were used to generate contrast maps comparing responses in the different conditions (visual categories). In this work, we focus on three contrasts: (1) face vs. all other categories (2) hands vs. nonlimb categories, and (3) house vs. all other categories (scene representations). Individual contrast maps were transformed to FreeSurfer’s fsaverage space for further analyses to allow for group comparisons. In this common cortical space, we thresholded each individual’s contrast t-maps at T>3, and used anatomical landmarks previously described 3 to separate the activations on the ventral and lateral surfaces of the high level visual cortex. To test whether there are differences in category representations on the ventral or lateral surfaces, we performed a bootstrap analysis (1000 iterations), estimating the sampling distribution of the number of vertices selective to each category in our experimental groups. In every iteration, for each of our three contrasts (face, hand, place), we sampled 8 participants from a single group (out of 11; with replacement), and estimated the average number of vertices in this thresholded contrast in each hemisphere in the ventral and lateral surfaces. Then we calculated for each condition the group differences (deaf experts minus controls, and hearing experts minus controls) for each iteration, to create a bootstrapped distribution of the group differences. Finally, we compared our group-difference bootstrapped-distributions to zero, quantifying the confidence intervals (CI) and p-values under the null hypothesis that there is no difference between the groups. To further examine whether sign language experience affects the specialization of category selectivity in high level visual cortex, we tested the overlap of two visual categories. Specifically, we quantified for each participant the number of vertices that were significantly selective (T>3) in both of the following contrasts: (1) hands vs. nonlimb categories, and (2) house vs. all other categories. The same bootstrapping methodology was then used to test the group differences in the overlap of the hand and house representations.

Results

We found that sign language experts had increased hand representations in high level visual cortex compared to controls with no sign language experience, with no significant group differences in face representations. Specifically, our bootstrapping analysis revealed no difference in the number of vertices selective to faces on the ventral nor the lateral surfaces across groups (p>0.2). However, hearing sign language experts (HE) had significantly more hand representations on the ventral surface compared to controls (C) on both hemispheres: in a bootstrapping analysis (1000 iterations), hearing experts demonstrated 359 more hand-selective vertices on the ventral left hemisphere (CI [1 713], p=0.023), and 290 more vertices on the ventral right hemisphere (CI [-23 614], p=0.035), compared to the control group. On the lateral surface, we saw consistent yet insignificant effects. Deaf sign language experts (DE) had significantly more hand representations compared to controls (C), specifically on the lateral right hemisphere (mean difference = 1292 vertices, CI [350 2235], p=0.007) and the ventral left hemisphere (mean difference=371, CI [174 578], p<0.001), with consistent yet insignificant increases in the lateral left and ventral right surfaces (p=0.1). As expected, in our control analysis comparing place representations (house-v-all contrast) between hearing sign language experts (HE) and controls (C), we found no group differences in the number of vertices (p>0.2). However, when comparing deaf sign language experts (DE) with controls with no sign language experience (C), we find that deaf experts showed significantly less place representations on the lateral surface bilaterally (left hemisphere mean difference = -547 vertices, CI [-921 -139], p=0.003; right hemisphere mean difference = -440 vertices, CI [-899 -9], p=0.024), with consistent yet insignificant reductions on the ventral surface (p>0.15). Does this increase in hand representation alongside the decrease in place representation suggest more specialization on the lateral surface? One way to address this question is to explore the extent of the overlap between those categories. More overlap would suggest an increase in dual coding, whereas less overlap would suggest more specialization of hand representations. When we quantify the overlap between hand and house/scene representations we find that indeed, deaf experts (DE) have less overlap of hand and house/scene selectivity on the lateral surface compared to controls (left hemisphere mean difference =-49, CI [-92 -4], p=0.01; right hemisphere mean difference = -30, CI [-60 -2], p=0.016), suggesting more specialization for hand processing. This difference is specific only to the lateral surface, which is known to specialize in processing objects in motion 1, with no differences observed in the hand-house overlap on the ventral surface (p>0.27). Interestingly, hearing sign language experts (HE) did not show similar decreases in hand-house overlap (p>0.2). This finding suggests that deaf signers might develop more specificity for hand representations on the lateral surface, such that lateral regions that tend to represent both hands and houses/scenes become more hand selective.

Conclusion

This is the first study to our knowledge to quantify visual category representations in the high level visual cortex of both hearing and deaf sign language experts. We demonstrate an increase in hand selectivity in high level visual cortex, most prominent on the lateral surface which is known to specialize in processing objects in motion 1. This increase in representation of hands appears to come at the cost of representing scenes on the lateral surface in deaf participants compared to hearing individuals, suggesting functional pruning of non-social visual category representations likely happens during childhood. Interestingly, despite having learned sign language in adulthood, the hearing sign language experts demonstrated significantly more surface area representing the hand in ventral temporal cortex, challenging the theory of a critical window of visual plasticity during childhood 4,5. The fact that deaf signers show less overlap between visual categories in the lateral visual stream suggests a sharpening of categorical representations. This novel study sheds important light on how sign language experience changes the way that the visual world is represented in the brain, and more broadly, how our visual brain adapts to unique visual requirements.
