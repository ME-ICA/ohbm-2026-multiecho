Introduction

Positron emission tomography (PET) enables in vivo quantification of molecular targets, such as tau aggregates in Alzheimer’s disease (AD). The standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) is widely used to summarize tracer uptake but is sensitive to the accuracy of PET-MRI registration, which maps anatomical regions onto PET images (Schwarz et al., 2017). Small PET-MRI misalignment can occur, e.g. from the cross-modality differences in spatial resolution, but it is unclear how much these impact quantitative measures. To fill this gap, we simulated variations in PET-MRI alignment to emulate inter-rater variability during visual QC and systematically assessed the impact of these subtle misalignments on SUVR estimation.

Methods

PET images were obtained from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI, 18F-flortaucipir tracer, N=834; Jack et al., 2008). Participants were grouped by cognitive status and tau profile. During preprocessing, each T1-weighted MRI was aligned to baseline PET by optimizing seven cost functions, among which the best alignment was selected by human raters through visual QC.

A subset of ADNI was QC-ed by two human raters. Discrepancy between their chosen cost functions defined the boundary of our simulations.

To simulate realistic QC-induced misalignments, random rigid-body perturbations were applied to T1 segmentation with rater-determined alignment (Fischl et al., 2002). Perturbations were parameterized by framewise displacement (FD), a standard head motion metric (Power et al.,2012), and sampled uniformly between 1-5 mm (Figure 1C). Three scenarios were simulated: translation only, rotation only, and combined. SUVRs were recomputed for each perturbation, and percentage changes relative to the rater-determined SUVRs were calculated.

Results

In our data, test-retest variability was approximated by the variation across PET frames within the same subject (Figure 1A). At the whole-brain level, simulated PET–MRI misalignments exhibited an average variance of 1.11% (Figure 1B), comparable to our estimated test-retest variability (1.06%; Figure 1A) and to the range of 0.1%-12% test-retest variability according to the literature (Devous et al., 2018; Lois et al., 2024).

However, at the regional and individual levels, even minor misalignments (FD = 1–2 mm) can produced a maximal difference around 0.3 in SUVR unit, which can translate to up to ~20% in low uptake regions (Figure 2A). SUVR variability scaled with both displacement magnitude and disease severity: participants with higher tau burden (MCI+, AD) showed larger deviations than tau-negative controls (CN-), suggesting that regions with steep tracer gradients are sensitive to spatial error. These findings can be reproduced by the SUVR difference introduced by two human raters (Figure 2B). 

Spatial mapping of mean SUVR percentage changes (Figure 2C) identifies regions most sensitive to misalignment. Collectively, these findings indicate that visually subtle registration differences can substantially affect SUVR estimates in specific areas. Greater caution is warranted, particularly when individual (vs. group-level) or region-wise (instead of whole-brain level) measures are critical.

Conclusion

Although SUVR variations introduced by subtle PET–MRI misalignments align with test-retest variability at the whole-brain level, they become critical at the regional level. This underscores the need for rigorous visual QC, particularly in AD research, where individualized patterns of regional tau accumulation and spread are critical.
