Introduction

        Children born preterm (< 37 gestation weeks) account for about 10% of global live births and face increased risks of neurobehavioral impairments, including learning difficulties and ADHD. 1,2 While previous research has focused on high-risk preterm cohorts with additional complications (e.g., very preterm, very low birth weights, with neurological or other medical injuries), the unconfounded links between prematurity and neurobehavior remain not well understood. Specifically, the subtle, latent impacts on children born preterm without overt medical issues are often overlooked, even though these effects may increase vulnerability to later environmental stressors. To address these gaps, this study leverages the Adolescent Brain and Cognitive Development (ABCD) study3, the largest prospective cohort of brain development in the United States, to examine the unique neurobehavioral patterns associated with preterm birth. We implement a rigorous systematic confounding control approach to isolate the effects of preterm birth in school-age children who were born preterm without postnatal medical and since-birth developmental complications. This study aims to identify unconfounded neurobehaviors that underline childhood susceptibilities as a result of premature birth in a non-medically complex preterm cohort and thus facilitate more targeted and effective early interventions.

Methods

The participants involved in the study were collected from the publicly available ABCD cohort, consisting of nearly 12,000 children aged 9-11 years from 21 educational sites across the United States. The ABCD Study received institutional review board (IRB) approval at all participating sites and obtained informed consent/assent from participants at the time of data collection. A total of 2208 children born preterm and 9525 children born full-term were identified from the baseline recruitment in the ABCD 2020 Data Release 3.0. The preterm/full-term criteria were based on the ABCD Parent-Report Medical History Questionnaire. We excluded participants with any DSM-V diagnosis of psychiatric disorders, postnatal complications (blue at birth, slow heartbeat, convulsions, required oxygen, blood transfusion, incubator, Rhesus incompatibility, any infections or serious illness within the first 12 months of life), or presence of any brain injury, resulting in 417 preterm-born children and 1851 full-term born children.

            Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) data were acquired from participants at 9-11 years using multiband EPI with slice acceleration factor of 3 and 96 diffusion directions, seven b = 0 frames, and four b-values (6 directions with b = 500 s/mm2, 15 directions with b = 1000 s/mm2, 15 directions with b = 2000 s/mm2, and 60 directions with b = 3000 s/mm2). The standard ABCD processing pipeline includes eddy current distortion correction,   distortion correction, registration to structural MRI, tensor fitting and modeling, fiber tract segmentation, and automated QC. Analyzed DTI measures include fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity (MD), axial diffusivity (AD), and radial diffusivity (RD), extracted from a total of 30 white matter regions with 15 major cortico-cortical, cortico-subcortical, and subcortical tracts throughout the whole brain. We excluded participants with incomplete imaging records, with abnormal MR findings, data that failed raw image quality checks (QC), and post-processing QC, resulting in 306 preterm-born children and 1420 full-term-born children.

            The cognitive measurements were from the NIH Toolbox, including the pattern comparison speed test, inhibitory control and attention test, card sort test, and picture vocabulary test. The raw scores for all tests were converted to age-adjusted standard scores for the analyses. Detailed information about these tests can be found on the NIH Toolbox website: https://www.nihtoolbox.org/. 

We performed one-to-one propensity score matching (PSM) 4 between the preterm and control groups without replacement. A logistic regression model was used to predict group membership based on key demographic and clinical covariates, including age, sex, two socioeconomic factors, and handedness. From the model, a propensity score was calculated for each participant. Each preterm subject was then matched to a control subject with the closest propensity score, using a caliper width of 0.2 standard deviations. A final eligible Preterm group (N=306) was identified, and a matched Control group (N=306) was obtained (Table 1). 

We performed two levels of statistical analysis to determine the effect of preterm birth on the neurocognitive behavior. For the primary analysis, we performed logistic regression to identify significant brain regions (FA in white matter regions that serve as predictors) to distinguish the Preterm vs. Control groups, controlling for all the covariates and global FA values. We adopted a stepwise forward likelihood ratio selection method to select the most significant predictors and performed multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni method. The secondary analysis is based on the correlation analysis between the identified brain region and the cognitive measurements within-group and between-groups to explore the altered neurobehavioral relationship between preterm birth and the full-term control group.

Results

In the primary analysis, we observed that the left anterior thalamic radiation (L-ATR), left uncinate fasciculus (L-UNC), and the right cingulate-cingulum (R-CC) were significantly associated with the differentiation of the preterm and control groups. The unstandardized beta (B) weight parameters in the logistic regression model with the surviving significant tracts are: L-ATR: B = -12.516, SE = 4.253, Wald = 8.661, P < 0.003; L-UNC: B = -9.169, SE = 3.737, Wald = 6.020, P < 0.014; R-CC: B = -5.419, SE = 2.410, Wald = 5.054, P < 0.025. Group comparisons reveal that FA in the L-ATR is significantly lower in the preterm group compared with the control group, controlling for the global FA value (P < .05, non-parametric one-way ANCOVA). For the FA in the L-UNC and R-CC, marginally significant group differences are observed (P <.07) with consistently lower FA values in the preterm cohort (Figure 1B). Supplementary assessments for the RD, MD, and AD reveal significantly lower AD in the preterm group in all the identified tracts (L-ATR, L-UNC, and R-CC; Figure 1C). 

The brain-behavior correlation (secondary) analysis with the above-identified tracts showed that the control group had a significantly low-degree positive correlation between the performance on the pattern comparison speed test and FA in the L-ATR (r = 0.15) and L-UNC (r = 0.17) (P < .05). No other correlation was found with other cognitive measures. Between-group assessments reveal a significant interaction effect of group by level of the pattern comparison speed test on the FA in L-ATR, which suggests altered neurobehavioral relationships between pattern comparison speed test and preterm birth (P < .05, non-parametric two-way ANCOVA). A significantly reduced association with the pattern comparison speed test is observed in the preterm group.

Conclusion

This study identified distinct neurobehavioral biomarkers of prematurity by implementing a rigorous confounding control approach within the large-scale ABCD dataset. We found that school-age children born preterm, even free from major medical and neurological complications, exhibit alterations in white matter microstructure within key fronto-subcortical pathways, including L-ATR, L-UNC, and R-CC. The identified tracts play a particularly important role for communication between the primary (e.g., sensory, arousal, and attention) and the secondary (e.g., feedback, control, and decision making) cognitive processing. Within these tracts, we found altered brain-behavior white matter microstructure, particularly ATR integrity, in regulating the attention and processing speed performance in preterm children. These results explain an increased risk of attention- and sensory-based dysfunctions, which frequently develop in preterm cohorts, thus providing new knowledge about the unconfounded neurobehavioral pathological processes of premature birth. By isolating the unconfounded effects, our findings reveal that premature birth itself leaves a lasting neural imprint that may heighten susceptibility to environmental demands. Findings of this study shed new light on the possible development of effective and targeted interventions, as well as preventive measures, for preterm populations.
