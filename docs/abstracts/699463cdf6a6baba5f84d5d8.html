Introduction

The structural reserve of the motor system is increasingly recognized as playing a seminal role in compensating for motor impairment after stroke.1 However, which cortical and subcortical motor networks support motor function early after stroke remains largely unknown. Therefore, this study aimed to elucidate which aspects of structural connectivity within major motor networks, including the cortex, the basal ganglia, and the cerebellum, explain variance in upper limb motor control early after stroke.

Methods

Structural connectivity and hand motor control were assessed in 23 acute stroke patients using diffusion spectrum imaging (DSI) and the Action Research Arm Test (ARAT). Structural connectivity was quantified using a recently introduced compartment-based approach, analyzing tract-based anisotropy within non-lesioned unidirectional white matter voxels.1–3 Tract templates were created via deterministic fiber tracking using a normative diffusion template4 informed by multiple anatomical priors to guide and constrain the tracking algorithm.  We assessed fiber tracts within four motor networks: (1) Cortical tracts between ipsi- and contralesional pre- and primary motor areas, (2) cortico-cerebellar tracts, (3) basal ganglia-cortical and (4) basal ganglia-cerebellar tracts. Correlation analyses were performed to assess associations between structural connectivity and motor control. Additionally, partial correlation analyses allowed us to account for the potential influence of corticospinal tract (CST) integrity on the association between structural reserve and motor control.

Results

Hand motor control in acute stroke patients was positively associated with structural connectivity of several motor network connections, with distinct patterns emerging for cortical and subcortical pathways. High levels of intra- and interhemispheric primary- and premotor cortical connectivity were associated with higher degrees of hand motor control. Furthermore, structural connectivity of the primary motor cortex (M1) and the supplementary motor area (SMA) with the cerebellum, as well as of M1 and the basal ganglia (including putamen, thalamus, globus pallidus internus, and subthalamic nucleus (STN)) was related to hand motor control. In contrast, no such associations were found for basal ganglia-SMA connectivity as well as for basal ganglia-cerebellar tracts (including STN, substantia nigra, and putamen). Partial correlation analyses revealed that the findings above were highly dependent on the integrity of the CST, highlighting that the cortical and subcortical compensation of hand motor control critically depends on remaining CST fibers to convey descending motor commands early after stroke.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that the successful compensation of hand motor impairment early after stroke relies on the interplay of cortical, cortico-cerebellar, and cortico-basal ganglia loops. Conversely, subcortical circuits, including cerebellar-basal ganglia pathways, were not associated with hand motor control. Hence, early compensation of complex hand motor control appears to depend strongly on the computational flexibility of cortical motor regions, which may facilitate updating of motor control policies in interaction with subcortical motor regions. In particular, rerouting of motor commands on the cortical level may help to access remaining CST fibers in order to generate descending motor output signals.5 In contrast, subcortical circuits alone seem to lack the flexibility to support early reorganization without the rerouting capabilities and redundancies of the cortex. In summary, our findings advance our mechanistic understanding of how the motor network supports early compensation of hand motor control and suggest that future therapeutic strategies may benefit from specifically targeting the highlighted cortical loops to enhance recovery in acute stroke patients.
