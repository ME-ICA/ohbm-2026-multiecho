Introduction

Major depressive disorder is a complex condition with heterogenous aetiology and heterogenous symptomatic profiles. Patients with major depressive disorder exhibit unique combinations of symptoms, represented by a wide spectrum of depressive phenotypes (e.g. mood swings, sleep disturbances, motor dysfunction, fatigue, appetite loss, and cognitive impairment). Studies have proposed risk factors and identified correlates of depression based on brain, psychological, lifestyle, genetic, and environmental phenotypes. Recently, there has also been a growing interest in investigating the relationship between depression and immuno-metabolic biomarkers, to deepen the understanding of the potential biological mechanisms behind depression1. Nevertheless, present findings of association of depression with both brain features and immuno-metabolic biomarkers remain weak2,3. Furthermore, the heterogeneity of depressive symptoms complicates the discovery of unique biomarkers and the understanding of neurobiological substrates1. 

In this study, we propose that the association with biomarkers can be better characterised by disentangling specific dimensions of depressive phenotype (within the subclinical and clinical population). Using a data-driven approach, we identified the mood and energy dimensions of depressive symptoms in an adult population cohort. We then show that these depressive symptom dimensions have distinct patterns of associations with immuno-metabolic and multimodal brain biomarkers.

Methods

Data from 21,705 participants aged 45-83 years in the UK Biobank4 were used. Depressive symptoms were collected from four mental health questionnaires: the Recent Depressive Symptoms (RDS-4), Neuroticism (N-12), Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), and Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD-7). We focused on participants with at least one depressive symptom from any of the questionnaires, including both participants with major depressive disorder (clinical MDD) and participants without MDD diagnosis (subclinical).

To use independent subsamples for deriving and characterising depressive symptom dimensions, we used a subset of 2500 subjects with missing immuno-metabolic phenotypes who were not used in subsequent analyses. The depressive symptom items were standardised to have the same scale. We applied agglomerative hierarchical clustering of the depressive symptom items, by minimising the variance of clusters based on Euclidean distances. The final clustering threshold is empirically determined at 60% of the maximum distance. We identified two most distinct clusters representing the mood and energy dimensions of depressive symptoms respectively (Figure 1A). For each dimension, a sum score over all individual items in the cluster was computed for each subject. As sex differences in depressive symptoms are widely recognised and present in our data, we performed all further analyses in female and male individuals separately. 

For the immuno-metabolic markers, we used measures mainly from the blood assays and the abdominal imaging derivatives. By probing markers specifically related to peripheral inflammation or metabolism, we selected 17 markers of blood cell count, 25 blood metabolic markers, and 17 markers of body fat. In addition, four commonly used ratios of blood cell count were computed including systemic immune-inflammation index, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio, and lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio. For the brain markers, we examined grey matter volume (GMV) for 148 cortical and 36 subcortical areas defined based on the FreeSurfer surface parcellation and subcortical segmentation. We also examined intracellular volume fraction (ICVF from diffusion MRI) from 48 white matter regions defined based on the JHU white matter atlas. ICVF serves as a potential indicator of cellular swelling due to neuroinflammation, representing changes in tissue microstructure associated with activated immune cells.

The association between each depressive symptom dimension and each biomarker was assessed with Pearson’s correlation. We adjusted all biomarkers by age and age squared as covariates with a simple linear regression. Furthermore, grey matter volume measures were adjusted by the subject-wise total intracranial volume, to control for the potential confounding effect of head size. Finally, all p values were corrected for multiple comparisons by controlling the false discovery rate using the Benjamini-Hochberg method.

Results

Our clustering analysis found a total of eight clusters of depressive symptoms, among which we identified two most distinct clusters representing the energy (pink) and mood (green) dimensions (Figure 1A). The energy dimension includes items from both the PHQ-9 and RDS-4 questionnaires encompassing fatigue, sleep disturbances, appetite troubles, concentration loss, and psychomotor dysfunction. The mood dimension involves PHQ-9 items representing depressive ideation such as anhedonia and suicidal thoughts. While both symptom dimensions consist of mainly PHQ-9 items, they demonstrate the heterogeneity of depressive symptoms even under the same assessment scheme.

The energy and mood dimensions are significantly correlated with most body fat biomarkers, as well as several blood immuno-metabolic markers such as white blood cell and neutrophil count, in both the female and male groups (Figure 1B). The magnitude of correlations are generally higher with the energy dimension compared to with the mood dimension. In addition, the energy dimension is significantly associated with insulin-like growth factor 1, triglycerides, and monounsaturated fatty acids in both sexes, as well as with C-reactive protein, gamma glutamyl transferase, saturated fatty acids, mean corpuscular haemoglobin count and volume, and platelet count in female participants. For the mood dimension, significant association in female participants was also observed for C-reactive protein. Among male participants, significant correlations are observed for glucose and glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) with the energy dimension, as well as for neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio with the mood dimension.

Brain grey matter and white matter features generally showed wider patterns of brain correlates with the energy dimension than with the mood dimension, and more correlations in the female sample than in the male sample. Regarding grey matter volume, similar associations were found for both symptom dimensions and for both sexes in the subgenual cortex, the left superior temporal sulcus, and the right olfactory sulcus (Figure 2A). Among female participants, significant correlations are additionally found in the left fusiform gyrus and right postcentral sulcus for both symptom dimensions. Moreover, the energy dimension correlates with grey matter volume in the occipital pole, the right intraparietal sulcus, the right central sulcus, the right anterior cingulate cortex, the right rectus gyrus, the right caudate, the putamen, and the right inferior lateral ventricle, while the mood dimension correlates with grey matter volume in the right anterior lateral sulcus and the left cerebellum. The associations are mostly negative suggesting that lower grey matter volume is associated with higher depressive symptom scores, although positive associations are also observed for the occipital pole, the caudate, the putamen, and the inferior lateral ventricle.

The intracellular volume fraction measures are associated with the energy dimension in both sex groups in the splenium of corpus callosum, the right cingulum hippocampus, the right fornix cres/stria terminalis, the superior, middle, and superior cerebellar peduncle, the left corticospinal tract, the cerebral peduncle, the right posterior limb of internal capsule, and the medial lemniscus (Figure 2B). Additionally, the energy dimension significantly correlates with intracellular volume fraction in the right superior fronto-occipital fasciculus in the female sample. In the male sample, significant associations between the energy dimension and intracellular volume fraction can also be found in the pontine crossing tract, the uncinate fasciculus, the right sagittal stratum, the external capsule, the left anterior limb of internal capsule, and the right retrolenticular part of internal capsule.

Conclusion

In this study, we mapped the immuno-metabolic and brain correlates of two distinct dimensions of depressive mood and energy symptoms. We thus demonstrated that associations with both body immuno-metabolic biomarkers and brain measures can be better characterised by examining depressive symptoms that are specifically energy-related. In addition, we found prevalent sex differences often manifested as higher correlations between the body or brain correlates and depressive symptoms in the female group. This suggests that depression in female may be more related to specific immuno-metabolic pathways and better evidenced with brain structural markers. 

In particular, we found significant peripheral markers of inflammation in relation to depressive energy symptoms, such as adiposity, C-reactive protein, triglycerides, and white blood cell count. Our results suggest that higher levels of inflammation and body fat and lower levels of insulin growth factor 1 are associated with worse depressive energy symptoms. Rather than considering depression as a single construct, we showed that a more specific dimension of depressive symptoms provides more identifiable body correlates. Furthermore, our analysis extended the association between immuno-metabolic biomarkers and depression to the larger group of subclinical individuals. Potentially, further research into the relationship between immuno-metabolic dysregulation and depressive phenotype in the healthy population can contribute to the prevention of complex interactions driving individuals towards clinical depression.

Previous research into brain markers of depression is characterized by inconsistencies across studies3. By disentangling two different dimensions of depressive phenotype, we showed that the more specific energy dimension provides more identifiable brain correlates. In particular, worse energy symptoms are related to lower grey matter volumes in the temporal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, and the motor cortex, as well as higher grey matter volume in the occipital pole and dorsal striatum. These regions discovered are mostly consistent with previous literature reporting on brain grey matter correlates of major depressive disorder5. The reduced grey matter volume in these regions may also suggest a related decrease in cognitive control, emotion regulation and motor functions.

In the white matter, worse depressive energy symptoms are related to higher intracellular volume fraction in the tracts that connects to the hippocampus, the cerebellum, the brain stem, and the thalamus. As a marker of cellular swelling and neuroinflammation, the increased intracellular volume fraction indicates the presence of inflammation in the sensorimotor pathways, body-brain axis, the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, and the limbic pathway. While the mechanisms behind the interaction between inflammation, brain, and depression is still importantly discussed, our results suggest that neuroinflammation and brain pathways related to sensorimotor function can be better investigated in relation with the energy symptoms.

In conclusion, our work elucidated the association of depressive symptoms with immuno-metabolic and brain markers in a large cohort of clinical and subclinical individuals. By disentangling the various dimensions of depressive symptoms, we uncovered a set of energy related symptoms that are more specifically related to immuno-metabolic biomarkers and brain structure. Our findings motivate the need to move beyond depression diagnosis towards depressive symptom profiling to investigate the complex and heterogenous mechanisms underlying depression.
