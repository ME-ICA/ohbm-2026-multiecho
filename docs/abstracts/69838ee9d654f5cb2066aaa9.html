Introduction

Understanding infant brainstem pathologies remains challenging because the brainstem is a densely organized structure containing numerous small gray-matter nuclei interwoven with adjacent white-matter tracts. Conventional MRI, even ex vivo, offers limited contrast in infants due to minimal myelination, reducing the ability to resolve these structures. To overcome this limitation, our lab employs polarization-sensitive optical coherence tomography (PSOCT) to image postmortem infant brainstems. PSOCT provides intrinsic contrast derived from optical scattering and birefringence, enabling clear visualization and segmentation of nuclei and fiber pathways independent of myelination. Our long-term goal is to use this modality to build a high-resolution atlas of the infant brainstem. Because PSOCT has not previously been applied to infant brainstem tissue, an essential first step is to validate PSOCT-based segmentation against histology, the established gold standard. In this work, we present our initial validation efforts and assess the accuracy and reliability of PSOCT for infant brainstem mapping. 

Methods

We applied our serial-sectioning PSOCT workflow to two postmortem infant brainstems: a 7-month-old male and a 1-month-old male, both immersion-fixed in 10% formalin. The brainstems were subdivided into blocks approximately 1–1.5 cm in height and embedded in agarose to stabilize the tissue during imaging and sectioning. Each block was imaged volumetrically using PSOCT optimized for high-resolution volumetric reconstruction1, and the tissue was simultaneously sectioned into 50-µm slices for downstream histology. Each section was allocated into one of four histology series: Nissl stain for “healthy” neurons, myelin basic protein (MBP) immunohistochemistry for myelinated fibers, Tryptophan Hydroxylase 2 (TPH2) immunolabeling for serotonergic neurons, and one unstained for reserve series (Figure 1). 

Following PSOCT acquisition, all volumes were manually annotated by trained raters to identify major brainstem nuclei, white-matter tracts, and cranial nerves. These manual labels were done solely based on PSOCT contrast, the scattering coefficient which needs to be validated. 

To establish ground-truth anatomical labels, the histological sections were digitized and registered to their corresponding PSOCT slices. Because infant tissue is exceptionally fragile, the physical sections often exhibited nonuniform distortions such as local tearing, stretching, or slight misalignment introduced during mounting and staining. To address these variations, we evaluated both affine and nonlinear registration strategies. We used pipelines developed by Iglesias et al.2 and Huszar et al3. 

We generated ground truth segmentation maps from each histological series and compared them with the manual PSOCT-based labels, after both affine and non-linear registration. PSOCT segmentation performance was quantified using the Dice overlap coefficient computed for each anatomical structure across the dataset. This allowed us to assess the degree to which PSOCT-based identification of nuclei, tracts, and cranial nerves correspond to conventional histological ground truth and to evaluate the feasibility of using PSOCT as a standalone modality for high-resolution infant brainstem mapping. 

Results

Figure 1 illustrates representative scattering-coefficient reconstructions obtained from one block in each case, demonstrating the high contrast achievable for differentiating nuclei, tracts, and cranial nerves even in extremely delicate infant tissue. 

For each block, the Nissl, MBP, and TPH2 histological series were registered to the corresponding PSOCT volume using both affine and nonlinear transformations (Figure 2). Qualitatively, the nonlinear approach generated smoother and more anatomically consistent reconstructions and appeared to better capture the complex deformations introduced during section mounting, staining, and drying. These distortions were especially pronounced in the youngest tissue, which was more prone to tearing, folding, and local stretching. 

To quantitatively assess registration quality, we selected a set of fiducial landmarks manually across the entire slice area, not limited to the outer boundary but also within internal regions where deformation can be substantial. Landmark distances after registration confirmed that nonlinear registration predominantly outperformed affine transformations, producing lower residual errors across all staining modalities. Among the stains, Nissl sections yielded the highest overall registration accuracy. This is likely because Nissl staining is performed on tissue already mounted on slides, whereas MBP and TPH2 immunostaining involves free-floating sections that undergo additional handling steps, increasing the risk of tissue distortions that are more challenging to be fully compensated by either affine or nonlinear algorithms. 

We then evaluated the correspondence between anatomical segmentations produced independently from PSOCT and histology. Segmentation was performed on the PSOCT-derived scattering-coefficient volumes and, separately, on each matched Nissl section. As illustrated for one representative block in Figure 2, nuclei identified on PSOCT (top-left) show strong visual agreement with the corresponding Nissl-based segmentation (top-right). The middle panel highlights the volumetric overlap after affine alignment, demonstrating excellent correspondence even for complex structures such as the inferior olivary nucleus (ION), whose highly convoluted geometry poses a particular challenge for registration. 

Quantitatively, Dice overlap coefficients were computed for all segmented nuclei across both cases. Dice values of 0.70 or higher were obtained for all but one structure, the arcuate nucleus. This nucleus is among the smallest examined and is located along the ventral aspect of the pyramidal tract, a region especially vulnerable to compression and shear during sectioning. As a result, even minor registration inaccuracies disproportionately reduced Dice overlap for this structure. For all other nuclei — including those that are larger and more geometrically complex ones — the strong agreement between PSOCT-based and histology-based segmentations demonstrates the reliability of serial-sectioning PSOCT for high-resolution mapping of the developing human brainstem.

Conclusion

Our results demonstrate that histological sections can be reliably registered to the corresponding PSOCT volumes, enabling direct, voxelwise comparison between microscopic ground truth and optical measurements. Across multiple specimens and anatomical regions, non-linear registration consistently outperformed affine transformations, providing improved alignment of nuclei, fiber bundles, and fine anatomical boundaries. Using these registered datasets, we validated PSOCT-derived tissue segmentation based on the scattering coefficient against the histological gold standard. The strong correspondence between modalities confirms that PSOCT can accurately delineate key brainstem nuclei and tracts without the need for staining. 

Together, these findings support the feasibility of using PSOCT as a foundational modality for constructing a high-resolution infant brainstem atlas. The combination of volumetric imaging, intrinsic contrast mechanisms, and accurate spatial correspondence with histology positions PSOCT as a powerful tool for characterizing the organization of the developing brainstem with unprecedented detail. 

Future work will expand this framework to a larger cohort of infant brainstems to capture anatomical variability and improve statistical robustness. We also plan to integrate automated segmentation methods based on machine learning to scale the pipeline and reduce manual annotation demands. Ultimately, the PSOCT-based anatomical labels and fiber-orientation features will be transferred first to ex vivo MRI and, subsequently, to in vivo MRI, enabling multimodal harmonization and broader applicability to clinical and developmental research. This progression will lay the groundwork for a comprehensive, cross-modal infant brainstem atlas that bridges microscopic structure with macroscopic imaging and supports future studies of early-life neurodevelopment and pathology.
