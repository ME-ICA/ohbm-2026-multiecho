Introduction

Decision making frequently requires brain to arbitrate between the drive to pursue rewards and the need to avoid losses. Striking this balance relies on the allocation of limited cognitive resources to support goal-directed behavior. In individuals with schizophrenia spectrum disorders (SSD) which are marked by profound disturbances in cognition, this equilibrium is altered; the ability to pursue rewards is blunted, while loss avoidance remains intact or is even heightened1,2. This asymmetry implies that cognitive resources for goal-directed behavior are disproportionately diverted toward avoiding losses at the expense of chasing rewards3. However, this ‘resource-reallocation’ hypothesis remains untested. Validating this requires computational models that can pinpoint how different motivational contexts modulate this behavior.

Therefore, to test this, we employed the two-stage decision-making task4 to dissociate two decision making strategies: goal-directed, model-based (MB) and habitual, model-free (MF) learning. As MB learning is cognitively more demanding and relies on dopaminergic signaling, both often compromised in psychosis, we predicted that individuals with SSD would exhibit impaired MB control, in line with previous findings. Crucially, we investigated whether this control is differentially modulated when chasing rewards versus avoiding losses, and if such a cognitive bias is reflected in clinical symptoms.

We hypothesized that individuals with SSD would exhibit preserved MB control for averting losses, but attenuated control for securing rewards. Neurally, we anticipated this asymmetry would manifest as stronger activation in prefrontal hubs in anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) during loss avoidance compared to reward pursuit as these regions are implicated in decision making (Fig:1a). Further, we also expected that a greater bias would track with a heavier burden of negative symptoms.

Methods

We recruited 50 individuals with SSD from the University Hospital of Psychiatry in Zurich, and 48 healthy controls (HC). Diagnosis was established with a structured clinical interview, and the recruited patients were clinically stable at the time of testing. Individual symptom profiles were characterized using the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS) and the Brief Negative Symptom Scale (BNSS) which assess symptoms across negative domains such as avolition, asociality, and anhedonia In addition, AMDP (Association for methodology and documentation in psychiatry system) ratings were obtained at baseline and at the follow-up assessments at 3,6,9 and 12 months from the subset of individuals with SSD (n=25) who attended follow-up visits.

Task: We employed a modified version of the two-stage task4, replacing abstract fractals with concrete 'spaceships' and 'aliens' to enhance intuitiveness and engagement5 (Fig: 1b). Participants completed 200 trials each for reward and loss conditions in separate blocks while inside the MRI scanner. A 3 Tesla MRI scanner was used to acquire structural, resting state and task-based fMRI data. The order of data acquisition was counterbalanced across participants.

Behaviour Analysis: After task completion, participants’ choice patterns were analyzed through complementary approaches: raw behavioral indices and reinforcement-learning (RL) models. For raw behavioral indices we computed stay-probabilities (StP), which reflected the probability with which individuals stick with their previous choices, if the transition in the last trial was rare or common (Fig:1b). Additionally, to track the latent structure of decision-making processes using modelling, a hierarchical Bayesian model was employed.This model summarizes decision making dynamics from the history of choices made by the participants using seven parameters, including ω, (0=pure MF, 1=pure MB), which quantifies the balance between MF and MB learning. 

fMRI Analysis: Data underwent quality control and standardized preprocessing via fMRIPrep. To investigate neural activity associated with task conditions we performed an event-related generalized linear model (GLM) analysis in FSL-FEAT, utilizing trial-by-trial reward prediction errors (RPEs) derived from the computational modelling (Fig:1c). Regressors included second-stage and outcome onsets, modulated by model-free RPEs and the unique model-based contribution (MB minus MF). First-stage selections and missed trials were treated as nuisance variables, and all inputs were convolved with a standard hemodynamic response. Following the framework of Daw et al., 2011, we specifically estimated the βMB coefficients corresponding to MB RPEs. We then defined activation as “loss-bias” if these βMB coefficients showed greater sensitivity during loss than reward pursuit. Similarly, we defined ‘reward-bias’ as the inverse of this (Fig:1d).

Region of Interest (ROI) analysis: We extracted the coefficients βMB from the ROI masks of ACC, OFC and dlPFC. Each ROI was further divided into left and right masks to allow for lateralized analysis. We also conducted an exploratory whole-brain analysis which might overlook regions other than the ones specified in our hypothesis.

Clinical ratings analysis: We initially explored associations between context-dependent biases (both neural and behavioral) and clinical variables using Spearman’s rank correlations to ensure robust estimates. Relationships reaching significance were subsequently validated using GLMs, adjusting for age, sex, antipsychotic dosage, and illness duration. Additionally, we conducted a longitudinal analysis to determine whether baseline context-biased activation in these ROIs predicted the trajectory of negative symptom features (AMDP) at follow-up.

For all analyses involving multiple comparisons, p-values were false discovery rate (fdr) corrected. Statistical significance was defined as a fdr-corrected p < 0.05.

Results

Behavior Results: Analysis of raw behavioral metrics indicated that individuals with SSD exhibited globally reduced MB control compared to HC in both reward (p = 0.005) and loss (p = 0.014) conditions. Numeric trends suggested that HCs deployed greater MB control during reward pursuit whereas individuals with SSD showed the reverse pattern. However, these raw context-dependent differences did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.28) (Fig:2a). We subsequently analyzed the parameter ω using a reinforcement learning model to better capture the latent structure of the decision-making process. This more sensitive computational approach confirmed the behavioral trends: individuals with SSD exercised significantly greater MB control in the loss condition (p < 0.05), while HCs were more goal-directed in the reward condition (p < 0.05). This difference in context-dependent biases between groups was statistically significant (p < 0.05) (Fig:2b). Additionally, we found no significant link between ω and age, sex, disease duration or antipsychotic dosage.

Neural activations: HC showed a significant reward bias in the ACC across both hemispheres (signrank test; left and right: p = 0.001), which stood in contrast to the loss bias exhibited by individuals with SSD. We found a similar pattern in the DLPFC, where HC displayed a bilateral reward bias (signrank test; left: p = 0.002; right: p = 0.01). The reward bias in the left hemisphere of controls was significantly higher from the numerical loss bias observed in the SSD group (ranksum test: p = 0.02, Fig. 2b). Furthermore, HC demonstrated a significant reward bias in both hemispheres of the OFC (signrank test; left: p = 0.001; right: p = 0.01), whereas individuals with SSD showed a significant shift toward a numerical loss bias (ranksum test; left: p = 0.001; right: p = 0.002, (Fig. 2e-i). In terms of localization, context-biased activation for both groups appeared primarily in the central portions of the DLPFC and OFC. Within the ACC, individuals with SSD exhibited loss-biased activation predominantly in dorsal regions, whereas reward-biased activation in HCs was stronger in ventral regions. Complementing our a priori ROI analysis, the exploratory whole-brain analysis revealed that in SSD, βMB values were significantly higher in the loss condition relative to the reward condition within the ACC, posterior cingulate cortex (PCC), and temporal regions. Conversely, regions where reward-related βMB estimates exceeding those for loss were limited to occipito-temporal areas. In contrast, healthy controls displayed a more widespread activation pattern where βMB was elevated for rewards compared to losses in the ACC, PCC, thalamus, and angular gyrus, with only a small occipital-pole cluster showing a preference for loss encoding (Fig 2j,k) .

Clinical results: We found that only the context bias in the right ACC (rACC) exhibited a significant negative correlation with anhedonia severity (rs = -0.41; p = 0.048; Fig. 2c). To confirm the robustness of this finding, we regressed anhedonia on rACC context bias while adjusting for age, sex, illness duration, and antipsychotic dose; the bias remained a significant negative predictor (β = –4.26  1.51 SE, p = 0.008). Extending this to our longitudinal subset, baseline rACC context bias prospectively predicted negative symptom outcomes over the follow-up period (mean 3–12 months). ANCOVA revealed that greater loss bias in the rACC was associated with worse baseline-adjusted symptom severity. This predictive effect was most pronounced for the domain of motivation (β= 1.83, 95% CI [1.20–2.46], p = 3.18 x 10-5) with significant effects also observed for social engagement (β = 1.11, 95% CI [0.31–1.92], p = 0.03, Fig. 2d). 

Conclusion

The present study provides converging behavioral and neural evidence that SSD are characterized by a systematic re-allocation of goal-directed cognitive resources away from reward pursuit and toward loss avoidance. In contrast to HC, who preferentially deployed model-based strategies to secure gains (reward-bias), individuals with SSD exhibited a loss-bias, by mobilizing MB control primarily when avoiding losses. Notably, while raw behavioral metrics indicated only non-significant trends, computational modeling uncovered a robust dissociation. By accounting for the full history of choices and estimating latent parameters, the RL model provided the superior sensitivity required to isolate these specific goal-directed mechanisms.

This reward bias in HC and loss bias in SSD was mirrored at the neural level by preferential engagement of the bilateral ACC, OFC and left DLPFC during model-based learning, regions known to orchestrate cost-dependent allocation of cognitive resources, value representation and prospective planning of goal-representation respectively. These findings are supported by previous research which observes blunted OFC and DLPFC responses to reward outcomes in schizophrenia and extends the view by demonstrating that neural computations which drive decision-making are context-dependent and tuned towards avoiding losses in psychotic disorders. Specifically, this bias appears to be driven by heightened valuation of avoidance in the OFC, coupled with increased recruitment of DLPFC resources to plan these strategies. 

Our results also support the central role of ACC dysfunction in psychotic disorders as the loss bias observed in this region correlated with negative symptom domain. Functionally, ACC acts as a gateway, calculating the "expected value of control" to decide if investing cognitive effort is justified. We propose that a loss-biased valuation in this region monopolizes limited cognitive resources into preventing negative outcomes leaving insufficient capacity for goal-directed reward pursuit and manifests clinically as anhedonia. Importantly, this is not a static feature, our longitudinal data show that this ACC profile predicts the future trajectory of negative symptoms, supporting its utility as a neurocomputational biomarker for patient stratification.

We believe that these findings regarding decision-making biases may hold translational utility. Firstly, it offers a quantifiable metric of goal-directed control, this framework could contribute to patient categorization and the assessment of treatment outcomes. Secondly, the association between the neural loss bias in ACC to anhedonia suggests that restoring this balance potentially via neuromodulation might help address motivational deficits. Theoretically, this also adds a new dimension to the active-inference model of psychosis, refining our understanding of how reduced policy precision interacts with distinct reward and loss contexts.

This study has several limitations. First, the task design employed in this study is simplistic and fails to capture the more complex and rich reward and loss outcomes encountered in daily life. Secondly, as the study is cross-sectional, we cannot establish whether loss-biased planning predates illness onset, emerges progressively or reflects a secondary adaptation to chronic disability. Finally, the medication effects of participants cannot be truly excluded from our behavioral and neural findings, however, we found no associations between context-dependent brain activation or behavior results with medication effects.
