Introduction

Schizophrenia Spectrum Disorders (SSD), which include schizophrenia (SZ) and schizoaffective disorder (SZA), share core positive and negative symptoms, although SZA is further distinguished by prominent mood and affective disturbances that often blend in with psychotic episodes. Growing evidence implicates cerebellar dysfunction in SSD1, consistent with the cerebellum’s role in higher-order cognitive and affective processes that are disrupted in these conditions. Within this framework, the cerebellar deep nuclei, the dentate and interposed nuclei, are of particular interest, as they serve as the major output hubs of the cerebellum. These nuclei integrate cortical, subcortical, and cerebellar inputs and project widely to cerebral and limbic regions, while simultaneously providing feedback to the cerebellar cortex. Their central position in cerebellar-cortical communication underscores their potential relevance to the dysconnectivity theory of SSD.

Recent neuroimaging studies have reported abnormal functional connectivity (FC) of the dentate nuclei in SZ2. However, few studies have examined both functional and structural alterations in the dentate and interposed nuclei across SZ and SZA subtypes.  Therefore, the properties of these nuclei, particularly their microstructural characteristics, remain largely unexplored.

To address these gaps, we employed multimodal neuroimaging to characterize dentate and interposed nuclei in SZ and SZA. Resting-state fMRI was used to assess intra-cerebellar FC of the bilateral nuclei, while diffusion kurtosis imaging (DKI) was used to quantify their microstructural properties via axial (AK), radial (RK), and mean kurtosis (MK), which are more suitable than the conventional diffusion tensor metrics for these grey matter structures.  Integrating these modalities allowed us to investigate how structural and functional alterations in cerebellar nuclei contribute to disrupted connectivity patterns in SZ and SZA.

Methods

A total of 110 participants (56 controls, 24 individuals with schizophrenia (SZ), and 30 with schizoaffective disorder (SZA)), aged 18–35 years, were included from two datasets (53 from Dataset A; 57 from Dataset B). Both datasets were acquired on Prisma Siemens 3T scanners using identical imaging protocols. Dataset A was acquired using a 64-channel head coil, and Dataset B using a 32-channel coil.

High-resolution T1-weighted MPRAGE scans were collected for anatomical reference (0.8 mm isotropic). Resting-state fMRI data were acquired using an EPI sequence (TR/TE = 800/37 ms, flip angle = 52°, 2 mm isotropic). Multi-shell diffusion data (b values = 1000, 1500, 2000, 3000 s/mm²; TR/TE = 3230/89.2 ms; 1.5 mm isotropic) were collected to estimate the kurtosis metrics. Both anterior–posterior (AP) and posterior–anterior (PA) phase-encoded acquisitions were obtained for fMRI and diffusion data in Dataset A; Dataset B included field maps with AP and PA orientations.

All imaging data underwent consistent preprocessing pipelines across datasets. Functional data were distortion-corrected using topup, then preprocessed and denoised in CONN. Diffusion scans were corrected for motion, susceptibility-induced distortions, and eddy currents using topup and eddy in FSL. Dtifit was used to derive diffusion maps (AK, RK, and MK).

Images from both modalities were normalized to 2 mm³ MNI space. Cerebellar regions of interest (ROIs) were defined using the Diedrichsen probabilistic cerebellar atlas3, which includes the left and right dentate and interposed nuclei (Figure 1). FC values were then extracted from all the cerebellar ROIs and converted to FC z-scores (Fisher z-transformed correlation coefficients). 

The same atlas was applied to extract diffusion metrics (AK, RK, and MK) from the kurtosis maps (Figure 1). Regional outlier removal was performed within each dataset, followed by merging and harmonization across datasets using NeuroCombat. 
 
 For statistical analysis, region-wise ANCOVA was performed for each diffusion metric and FC z-score to examine group differences among healthy controls, SZ, and SZA, while controlling for age and sex. Post hoc comparisons were evaluated using an FDR-corrected threshold of p < 0.05, and effect sizes were reported as Cohen’s f for the pairwise tests.

Results

As shown in Figure 2.A, functional connectivity patterns of the dentate and interposed cerebellar nuclei differed significantly across diagnostic groups. In SZ, spider plots demonstrated reduced connectivity (hypoconnectivity) between the right dentate nucleus and multiple cerebellar cortical subregions compared to healthy controls and SZA, accompanied by increased connectivity (hyperconnectivity) between bilateral interposed nuclei and the cerebellar cortex compared to SZA. In SZA, widespread hypoconnectivity was observed between the left dentate nucleus and various cerebellar regions, as well as between bilateral interposed nuclei and the cerebellar cortex.

The strongest group effect that survived ANOVA post hoc testing emerged for right cerebellar lobule X (Cohen f=0.28), indicating a moderate-to-large group effect on functional connectivity between the left dentate nucleus and this region (Figure 2.B). For the right dentate nucleus, the largest group-related effect occurred for its FC with the right lobule VIIb (Cohen f=0.21), though this did not survive correction for multiple comparisons. For the right interposed nucleus, shown in Figure 2.C, the largest group effect was noted for its FC with the left cerebellar lobule VIIb (f=0.30). Across these regions demonstrating group effects in FC, the SZA group showed pronounced hypoconnectivity compared with both the SZ and control groups.

Diffusion kurtosis imaging revealed a consistent pattern of increased kurtosis metrics in the SZA group relative to SZ and controls (Figure 2.D). This elevation was evident across AK, RK, and MK for bilateral dentate and interposed nuclei. The most prominent group effects were detected for the AK of the left interposed (f=0.306) and dentate (f=0.299) nuclei, followed by the right dentate (f=0.294) and interposed (f=0.257) nuclei. For the left dentate nucleus, the group exerted a moderate-to-large effect for RK (f=0.313) and MK (f=0.302). 

Conclusion

This study identifies distinct alterations in cerebellar nuclei connectivity and microstructure across SZ and SZA compared with healthy control participants. Our multimodal findings indicate that SZA exhibits more extensive and pronounced cerebellar nuclei abnormalities than SZ, both functionally and structurally.

In SZ, functional connectivity analyses revealed hypoconnectivity between the right dentate nucleus and ipsilateral cerebellar cortical subregions, such as right crus I and lobule VI, paired with hyperconnectivity between bilateral interposed nuclei and the cerebellar cortex. Our results are consistent with previous work showing hypoconnectivity of the dentate nucleus with crus I in drug-naïve first-episode SZ patients4.  In contrast, SZA showed widespread hypoconnectivity of the left dentate nucleus and bilateral interposed nuclei with multiple cerebellar regions, including the right lobule X and left lobule VIIb, two areas involved in sensorimotor and cognitive processes5. These regions showed moderate-to-large group differences, with SZA consistently showing decreased connectivity compared with SZ and control groups. 

Diffusion kurtosis imaging complemented these findings, showing increased AK for bilateral dentate and left interposed nuclei in SZA compared with healthy controls, along with increased RK and MK in the left dentate nucleus compared to SZ. Increased kurtosis is assumed to reflect greater tissue complexity, potentially from changes in cellular packing or glial processes. Both inflammation and increased neurite dispersion may result in increased kurtosis.  Cerebellar nuclei microstructural alterations observed in SZA could impair the precision and timing of cerebellar output signals, thus affecting the cerebello-thalamo-cortical loops and resulting in the observed FC hypoconnectivity. 

Together, these results provide convergent evidence for structural and functional deficits within cerebellar nuclei in SZA. The combination of reduced cerebellar nuclei connectivity and increased microstructural complexity suggests disorganized local cerebellar circuitry that may compromise effective communication within the cerebellum as well as with cortical regions. These findings suggest that SZA involves distinctive cerebellar dysfunction that appears to be more prominent than in SZ and underscore the cerebellum as a potential region differentiating SSD subtypes.
