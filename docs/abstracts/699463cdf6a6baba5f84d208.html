Introduction

Network control theory (NCT) provides a framework for quantifying the energetic cost required for the brain to transition between functional activation states.1 NCT has been applied broadly in neuroscience, from characterizing aberrant brain dynamics in neurodegenerative and psychiatric conditions,2 to explaining how neuromodulation and pharmacological perturbations reshape the brain’s energy landscape.3 Transition energy (TE), derived from structural connectivity and functional brain states, offers a mechanistic marker of brain dynamics that may change across the lifespan from childhood to old age. However, lifespan analyses spanning multiple large neuroimaging cohorts must account for nonlinear age effects, sex differences, motion, and site-related variability. Here, we used generalized additive models (GAMs) to characterize nonlinear lifespan trajectories of TE and related state dynamics across harmonized multi-cohort Human Connectome Project (HCP) lifespan datasets.

Methods

We analyzed structural and resting-state functional MRI data from the HCP-Development (n=608), HCP-Young Adult (n=958), HCP-Aging (n=716), and HCP-Disease cohort (healthy controls subset; n=95). Structural connectomes and resting-state fMRI time series were calculated regionally using the FS86 atlas, and site effects were corrected using CovBat which adjusts for site variance but tries to retain age and sex-related variance.4 Regional brain activity vectors were clustered via k-means clustering to derive four brain states (Figure 1) with interpretable network profiles (DMN±, VIS±).5 NCT was used to calculate TEs, or the estimated energy needed to complete state transitions, between every pair of brain states. TEs between pairs of states were averaged across all pairs and then summed across all regions (global TE) or within functional networks (network TE). We modeled age effects on global and network TEs using GAMs, with terms for age, sex, motion, and age-by-sex interactions. The same GAM models were fitted on state-level metrics including fractional occupancy (FO) and dwell time (DT).

Results

Global transition energy (TE) showed a significant nonlinear association with age (GAM smooth for age: edf ≈ 10.3, p < 0.001). The age trajectory was characterized by an early-life stabilization phase, in which TE remained relatively constant from adolescence through early adulthood, followed by a transient local elevation in the third decade and a mild attenuation thereafter that was mostly driven by males’ somatomotor, visual, and frontoparietal network TE. Beginning in midlife, TE increased progressively with age, with the steepest rises observed in later adulthood. This pattern indicates a multi-phasic aging trajectory rather than a monotonic or linear change, consistent with age-dependent reorganization of large-scale brain dynamics.

Sex exerted a significant main effect on global TE, with females exhibiting lower TE than males across much of the lifespan, driven by the cerebellum and dorsal attention networks. Motion effects were small and not significant after harmonization, consistent with prior linear models. A modest age-by-sex interaction was detected, reflecting subtle differences in the curvature of age-related trajectories rather than differences in overall direction. Allowing for nonlinear age effects improved model fit relative to OLS (pseudo-R² ≈ 0.144).

At the network level, age-related increases in TE were widespread but heterogeneous. Default mode, visual, somatomotor, frontoparietal, dorsal attention, and ventral attention networks showed consistent upward age trends, whereas cerebellar TE exhibited a weak downward trajectory. State-level dynamics also varied with age: fractional occupancy decreased for a visual-dominant state, while dwell time shortened for sensory states and lengthened for a default mode–dominant state, suggesting age-related shifts in both the energetic landscape and temporal organization of brain states.

Conclusion

Across four harmonized cohorts, NCT-derived measures reveal robust, nonlinear age-related changes in brain dynamics. TE was generally flatter from childhood to mid-adulthood, with linear increases after age 50 mostly driven by the somatomotor, dorsal attention, and default mode networks. Additionally, males had a non-linear increase in TE around age 30, driven by the somatomotor, visual, and frontopareital networks. These findings highlight TE as a sensitive, multi-scale marker of lifespan brain dynamics that appear to be nonlinear and sex- and network-specific. Understanding the development of brain dynamics across the lifespan is critical if we are to understand how brains develop and better characterize windows of vulnerability to disorders.
