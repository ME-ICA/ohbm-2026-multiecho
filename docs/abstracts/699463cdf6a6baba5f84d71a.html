Introduction

Pain perception reflects distributed neuraxial processing: nociceptive signals are encoded in the spinal dorsal horn, relayed to supraspinal "pain-matrix" systems, and continuously shaped by descending modulation. Yet most multivariate fMRI biomarkers are brain-centric (e.g., NPS,1 SIIPS2) and rarely incorporate spinal contributions, limiting mechanistic interpretability and clinical translation. Spino-cortical fMRI work has begun to show that brain-spinal interactions shape pain modulation,3 motivating biomarkers that capture coordinated signals across the neuroaxis. Leveraging simultaneous brain-cervical cord fMRI, we developed the Corticospinal Pain Intensity Pattern (CsPIP) to decode subjective pain from integrated brain and spinal activity and to bridge evoked and intrinsic signatures (e.g., resting-state fALFF4). Across five independent cohorts (Total N=320, Fig. 1a), we tested whether CsPIP (i) outperforms brain-only and spinal-only models, (ii) generalizes across modalities and contexts (heat, electrical; pain vs itch; observed pain), and (iii) tracks neuromodulation-induced analgesia and chronic pain abnormalities expressed in resting-state spectral and dynamic states.

Methods

Participants completed simultaneous corticospinal fMRI on 3T Siemens Prisma scanners with 64-channel head/neck coils using a customized SMS-EPI protocol optimized for brain-cervical coverage (FOV 192x192 mm2; 70 axial slices, 4-mm thickness spanning C1-C7 and cerebrum; 1.5x1.5 mm2 in-plane; multiband GRAPPA factor=2; flip angle 80 degrees; 230 volumes; TR/TE=2680/27 ms; dual phase-encoded references for topup). Respiration and pulse were recorded (400 Hz) for physiological noise modeling. Corticospinal data were preprocessed separately (deepbet/FSL/SCT; dcm2niix conversion), including PNM-based physiological regressors (32 terms), distortion correction, slice-timing correction, and registration to MNI (brain) and PAM50 (spinal). Brain images were smoothed (5-mm FWHM) and high-pass filtered (100 s); spinal images underwent cord-restricted motion correction and 2D smoothing (3-mm FWHM) plus slice-wise CSF noise regression (highest-signal decile). First-level GLMs produced stimulus-evoked COPE maps; group activation used FLAME (brain; Z>3.1, p<0.05) and randomize TFCE (spinal; 10,000 permutations, C6 gray-matter focus).

Study 1 (heat pain; n=92 after exclusions) provided discovery data: individualized calibration established temperatures evoking NRS ~7/10, followed by counterbalanced left/right forearm stimulation and 0-10 pain ratings. CsPIP was trained with LASSO-PCR (Fig. 1c; alpha=1; all PCA components) on vectorized corticospinal COPEs using a 2:1 participant-level train/test split (train n=61; hold-out n=31) and participant-stratified 10-fold CV. Brain features were restricted to a predefined univariate activation mask, whereas no a priori mask was applied to the spinal cord due to its smaller voxel count. Prediction was evaluated with Pearson r and mean absolute error; 10,000 value-permuted CV iterations established a null distribution, and 10,000 bootstraps identified stable weights (thresholded at z>3.1 with corrected cluster inference). Signature expression was computed as dot products between CsPIP weights and task or resting maps. 

External validation included Study 2 (factorial heat pain/itch; n=39) with condition-wise prediction, 2AFC/AUC specificity tests, and single-trial analyses (2340 trials) using mixed-effects models of ratings on CsPIP * condition. Study 3 (electrical pain; n=50) tested modality generalization and quantified region-wise similarity between heat-trained CsPIP and an independently trained electrical-pain pattern (275-parcel atlas; Bonferroni). Study 4 (pain empathy; n=58) assessed sensitivity to observed pain. 

Benchmarking compared CsPIP with brain-only and spinal-only models and with established cortical biomarkers (NPS1, SIIPS2). Analgesia sensitivity was tested in a Study 1 TENS subcohort (active n=30; sham n=31) using group x side x time ANOVA and correlations between delta-CsPIP and delta-pain. 

Clinical utility was assessed in IBS patients (Study 5; n=83) scanned at rest pre/post 18-session electroacupuncture or sham treatment: CsPIP was applied to fALFF maps (0.01-0.13 Hz) and to dynamic resting states estimated within CsPIP voxels via PCA (20 components) and a Gaussian HMM (hmmlearn; diagonal covariance; EM up to 1000 iterations; K=5 selected by cross-validated log-likelihood), with Viterbi decoding to derive state occupancy and state-wise CsPIP predictions.

Results

Univariate nociceptive responses replicated canonical pain processing and revealed robust spinal encoding: pain activated bilateral insula/dACC/thalamus and somatosensory cortices (brain; Z>3.1, p<0.05), and produced significant cervical gray-matter effects including C6 dorsal-horn activation (TFCE-corrected p=0.02) with concurrent ventral-horn deactivation (Fig. 1b). 

In Study 1, CsPIP significantly predicted pain intensity in participant-stratified CV (Fig. 2a; r=0.36, p<0.0001) and generalized to the hold-out set (Fig. 2b; r=0.47, p=0.0001). Stable predictive weights spanned anterior insula, dACC, SMA/cerebellum, and dorsal-horn segments (C2/C6), with network-level emphasis in somatomotor and ventral-attention systems; weights overlapped canonical pain signatures yet showed distinct contributions in motor cortex (Fig. 1d). In Study 2, behavioral manipulation was robust (high pain 6.89+/-1.54 vs low pain 4.99+/-1.42; t(38)=10.77, p=4.12x10^-13). CsPIP predicted individual pain sensitivity (Fig. 2c; high pain r=0.52, p<0.001; low pain r=0.34, p=0.04) and showed specificity: high pain was classified above chance against low pain (AUC=0.64, p=0.032), low itch (AUC=0.73, p<0.001), and high itch (AUC=0.67, p=0.010). Single-trial analyses demonstrated condition-dependent corticospinal coupling (CsPIP * condition beta=0.31, z=7.31, p<0.001): within-subject prediction was reliable for pain (mean rho=0.16; t(38)=4.63, p=4x10^-5) but not itch (mean rho=0.04; p=0.16), and differed between pain and itch (t(38)=2.51, p=0.017). 

CsPIP generalized across nociceptive modality in Study 3 (Fig. 2d; electrical pain r=0.45, p=0.001); 81 atlas regions showed significant pattern similarity between heat-trained CsPIP and an independently trained electrical-pain pattern (Bonferroni), with overlap enriched for perception/cognition terms (Fig. 2e-f). And CsPIP responses were not related to empathy ratings (observed painful r=0.03, p=0.81; painless r=0.02, p=0.88). 

In the TENS subcohort, analgesia depended on group, side, and time (F(1,59)=14.584, p<0.001): active TENS reduced treated-side pain (t(29)=6.740, p<4.284x10^-7) without contralateral change (Fig. 2g), and delta-CsPIP tracked treated-side relief (r=0.36, p=0.049) but not sham. 

In IBS, CsPIP applied to resting fALFF predicted baseline pain severity (Fig. 2h; r=0.33, p=0.04) in low burden group, exceeded the cortical NPS (r=0.22, p=0.17), and captured longitudinal treatment response (Fig. 2j; delta-CsPIP vs delta-pain r=0.25, p=0.04) in electroacupuncture group. HMM analyses (K=5) revealed state-resolved links to symptoms: a pro-nociceptive state showed positive pain association (Fig. 2m; State 5 r=0.22, p=0.04) whereas an antinociceptive state showed the opposite (State 1 r=-0.29, p=0.01).

Across models, CsPIP outperformed brain-only (training r=0.31; Study1 test r=0.45; Study2 high-pain r=0.35) and spinal-only (training r=0.29; Study1 test r=-0.08) decoders. Relative to established cortical biomarkers, NPS showed modest task prediction (Study1 test r=0.28) and limited treatment sensitivity, while SIIPS predictions were largely non-significant outside high-pain conditions. Linear LASSO-PCR also outperformed nonlinear SVR/RF models, which showed reduced internal accuracy and failed to generalize (external correlations near zero or negative).

Conclusion

CsPIP is a neuraxial, multivariate pain biomarker derived from simultaneous brain-spinal fMRI that reliably decodes subjective pain intensity, generalizes across nociceptive modalities, and is specific to first-hand pain relative to itch and observed pain. By explicitly integrating spinal features, CsPIP outperforms brain-only and spinal-only approaches, supporting the view that pain intensity is represented by coordinated corticospinal patterns rather than isolated cortical maps. 

Importantly, CsPIP expression tracked neuromodulation-induced analgesia and clinical pain dynamics. In healthy participants, changes in CsPIP responses selectively mirrored TENS-evoked pain relief on the treated limb but not sham or contralateral control conditions, indicating that the pattern is sensitive to targeted engagement of descending modulatory pathways and spinal gating mechanisms. In IBS, corticospinal CsPIP expression derived from low-frequency resting-state activity predicted baseline chronic pain severity and closely followed treatment-induced improvement, outperforming a cortical biomarker and demonstrating translational utility from experimental pain to clinical symptom burden. 

HMM further revealed recurrent corticospinal states characterized by high versus low CsPIP expression, with a pro-nociceptive state and an antinociceptive state explaining both static spectral abnormalities and inter-individual variability in chronic pain. This neuraxial, state-resolved perspective suggests that chronic pain may reflect not only elevated tonic excitability but also biased changes in high-pain network configurations and reduced access to antinociceptive states—features that are, in principle, targetable by state-informed neuromodulatory or pharmacological interventions. 

Taken together, our findings establish CsPIP as a neuraxial fMRI biomarker that (i) captures distributed corticospinal representations of pain intensity, (ii) generalizes across tasks, modalities, and cohorts, and (iii) indexes both acute and chronic pain as well as treatment-induced change. Beyond refining mechanistic models of descending pain control, this work supports the use of simultaneous corticospinal imaging and pattern-based analysis as a framework for precision pain medicine—enabling stratification of patients by corticospinal phenotype and rational selection or monitoring of neuromodulation strategies that target specific spinal and supraspinal nodes in the pain pathway.
