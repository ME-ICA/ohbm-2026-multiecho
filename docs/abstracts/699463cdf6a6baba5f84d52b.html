Introduction

Brain function is the outcome of the coordinated information processing by its various components. Traditionally, the cerebral cortex has been associated with different functions such as sensory, cognitive and conscious processing, while subcortical structures and the cerebellum have been assigned either supporting or highly specific purposes. However, recent investigations have shown a multifunctional view also for subcortical structures, with even neural correlates for cognition (once believed an exclusive realm of the cortex) robustly reported in the cerebellum and in various subcortical regions. In order to explain this multifunctional nature of the various brain's structures, it is necessary to better understand how they are interconnected and how white-matter fibers form pathways that allow them to work together. 

Studies of structural connectivity (infered white-matter tracts from diffusion imaging) for the human brain have so far focused mainly on cortico-cortical connections. Integration of subcortical and cerebellar regions into the analyses has been rather anecdotal, typically considering subcortical structures (e.g., the entire thalamus or hippocampus) as a single node of the network. In order to overcome this gap, in the present study we investigated the structural connectome of the human brain comprising the cortex, the cerebellum and nine subcortical nuclei. With the entire brain parcellated into 718 regions of interest (ROIs), this network presents sufficient resolution to "zoom-into" the connectivity of the subcortex and the cerebellum, allowing us to parcel the subcortical structures into tens of ROIs and thus investigate their inter-/intra-connectivity patterns.

Methods

Structural connectivity data was obtained via diffusion-weighted imaging for 32 healthy human participants from the Human Connectome Project (HCP) and parcellated into 718 regions of interest (ROIs). Of these, 360 correspond to cortical, 233 to subcortical, and 125 to cerebellar cortex1. This parcellation provides the internal division of subcortical structures into several ROIs such as the bilateral nucleus accumbens (n=13), brainstem (n=47), caudate nuclei (n=17), diencephalon (n=40), hippocampus (n=29), globus pallidus (n=20), putamen (n=18), thalamus (n=38), and amygdala (n=11). Tractography data was filtered in order to exclude false-positive tracts related to cerebellar connections2. A population averaged, binary structural adjacency matrix was estimated employing a distance-aware consensus algorithm.

Network analyses were performed using the Python package pyGAlib (https://github.com/gorkazl/pyGAlib), including the characterization of the role individual ROIs play for the global connectivity3 and the rich-club analysis over various k-density thresholds. Community detection, instead, was performed using the leidenalg Python package (https://pypi.org/project/leidenalg/). 

Finally, integration and segregation of the brain-wide network were estimated using the definitions introduced in Ref.4. This definition accounts for the propagation of external stimuli throughout the network and the subsequent collective responses by groups of brain regions. To do so, in the present study we employed a stimulus-response linear dynamical approach that allows to estimate the influence that one node j exerts over another i in terms of the responses measured at i, due to external stimuli applied on j. In particular, integration is measured as the sensitivity of one node (or of a set of selected nodes) to external stimuli applied at the rest of the network, and segregation is defined as the loss of communication (influence) suffered between the modules of a network, after a node (or a set of nodes) has been lesioned.

Results

We began by inquiring whether the anatomical division of the brain into cortex, subcortex and cerebellum is also reflected in the organization of its structural connectivity. For that, we compared the traditional division of the brain into cortex, subcortex and cerebellum (anatomical division) to the modular architecture revealed by a community detection algorithm (network division), see Figure 1. The network division comprised of five macrocopic communities of intermixed cortical, subcortical and/or cerebellar components. We found that this division represents a more accurate viewpoint of how the global brain communication pathways are organised than the traditional view represented by the anatomical division into cortex, subcortex and cerebellum.

A closer look into this organization also showed that subcortical nuclei are internally heterogeneous meaning that, internally, their ROIs present a diversity of connectivity profiles and contribute differently to the global wiring. Not suprisingly, we found that the brain-wide network contains a large communication core (a rich-club supramodule), but in contrast to previous studies, we could show that this communication core is not only made of cortical and subcortical ROIs, but it is in fact dominated by the subcortex. But, which subcortical structures govern the rich-club? Our analyses revealed that, contrary to previous reports suggesting a central role of the thalamus and the basal ganglia for global communication and integration, we found that no individual subcortical structure lies at the center of the network. Instead, the global  rich-club is spatially delocalised with all subcortical structures contributing a few of their ROIs to the global rich-club, while the rest of their ROIs are more locally connected.

Finally, we applied in-silico stimulations protocols alongside stroke-like lesions. This showed why focal subcortical lesions are significantly more detrimental for brain function than cortical or cerebellar lesions alone.

Conclusion

A major challenge for neuroscience today is to better understand how cortex, subcortex and cerebellum work together. This anatomical division is an essential notion of the brain's organization, evidenced by their differentiated cytoarchitecture, cellular composition and evolution. However, there is more to the brain's structure than meets the eye, hidden in the complex manner that white matter shapes the pathways between distant regions, and only discernible through suitable network analyses. The present study reveals a dual view of how the human brain is organised, with the connectivity forming an intermixed architecture underneath the traditional anatomical view into cortex, subcortex and cerebellum. Both views are complementary, demonstrating the necessity to integrate global information beyond cortex-centric views to understand the brain’s function. 

Given the need of the brain to integrate sensory information from different modalities, initially processed by specialized regions, the existence and function of multimodal "convergence zones" has long been debated. The presence of subcortical hubs has been reported by studies focusing on specific structures. However, our results clearly identify (1) the subcortex at the center of the global rich-club, and (2) that the subcortical component of the rich-club is decentralised, with all subcortical structures participating on it while previous reports suggested the thalamus or the basal ganglia as those lying at the center of the global connectivity. This is very relevant and unpredicted by previous observations, which lacked of sufficient resolution. Our analyses reveal an internal diversity of connectivity patterns within each and all of the subcortical structures. That is, within each subcortical structure, some ROIs are more locally connected (an indication of are more specialised or segregated function) while other ROIs are globally connected (indicating their role for the interexchange of information with other parts of the brain and integration). These observations reveal the structural organization that allows the cortex, the cerebellum and the subcortical structures to participate and coordinate with each other on multifunctional processing, beyond the specialised functions traditionally associated to them separately.
