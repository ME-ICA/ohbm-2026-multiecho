Introduction

The mechanism of psychedelic drug action is a dynamic and rapidly evolving area of neuroscience. Two major lines of investigation currently dominate the field: (1) molecular and cellular studies performed in laboratory systems1,2, and (2) human neuroimaging research that focuses on large-scale functional brain networks3-5. Despite considerable progress in both domains, there remains insufficient understanding of how the molecular and cellular substrates of psychedelics relate to the whole-brain network effects observed in humans. In this study, we address this gap by focusing on the intermediate spatial scale of local brain regions (<1 cm³), asking whether psychedelics exert a common mesoscale effect that links micro-level receptor actions to macro-level network dynamics.

Methods

We analyzed functional magnetic resonance imaging data from humans exposed to the classical psychedelics dimethyltryptamine (DMT), lysergic acid diethylamide (LSD), and psilocybin, as well as the non-classical psychedelics nitrous oxide and ketamine. For each drug, we quantified regional homogeneity (ReHo) to assess the synchrony of local neural activity at a small spatial scale across cortical and subcortical regions. To examine dynamic changes, we computed time-resolved ReHo and global functional connectivity (GFC) using sliding-window approaches. We then performed dominance analysis to determine the relative contribution of a broad set of neurotransmitter receptors to ReHo alterations. In addition, density maps of neuronal and non-neuronal cell types were included to evaluate cellular contributions to psychedelic-induced changes in local synchrony.

Results

Across all five drugs and both drug classes, we found that psychedelics consistently reduced regional homogeneity, indicating disrupted local synchrony in small-scale brain regions. These reductions were extensive across cortical regions but occurred only sparsely within subcortical structures. Dynamic analyses of both ReHo and global functional connectivity revealed an inverse pattern: large-scale connectivity increased as local synchrony declined, suggesting a coordinated reorganization of brain activity across spatial scales. Dominance analysis showed that DMT, LSD, and psilocybin exhibited the strongest associations with 5-HT receptor families, whereas nitrous oxide and ketamine showed the largest associations with the NMDA receptor. Both neuronal cells, including interneuron populations, and non-neuronal cell types were significantly linked to psychedelic-induced disruptions in local synchrony, indicating that multiple cellular pathways contribute to these effects.

Conclusion

Across five psychedelics spanning two major drug classes, our findings demonstrate that a diverse set of molecular and cellular actions converge on a common outcome that involves disrupted synchrony in local brain regions. This shared mesoscale mechanism provides a potential bridge between molecular receptor engagement and drug-specific changes in global functional connectivity. By identifying local synchrony as a unifying intermediate process, our results offer a framework for understanding how heterogeneous molecular events produce the characteristic whole-brain network signatures of psychedelic states.
