Introduction

Ovarian hormone fluctuations coincide with dynamic changes in brain organization, reflecting an organism’s adaptive response to hormonal transition states. Such neuroendocrine plasticity may help explain sex differences in cognitive, affective, and behavioral processes across both health and disease. In humans, the menstrual cycle offers a natural experimental framework for examining how variations in steroid hormone levels relate to brain function. The hippocampus, crucial for memory, spatial navigation and affective regulation, is densely populated with estrogen and progesterone receptors and exhibits cycle-related variation across multiple structural and functional neuroimaging parameters. Notably, ultra-high field (7T) structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with fine-grained mediotemporal cortex segmentation has revealed distinct, subfield-specific patterns of volumetric change in response to ovarian hormone fluctuations1. In the present work, we extend these findings by analyzing unpublished resting state functional 3T MRI data from the same sample to characterize hormone-dependent changes in hippocampal connectivity. Using a multimodal analyses approach (graph theoretic measures, seed-based functional connectivity, hippocampal-cortical gradients), we aim to characterize short-term plastic changes in hippocampal network integration throughout the menstrual cycle. Elucidating these dynamic connectivity patterns may advance our understanding of endocrine modulation of the hippocampus, and how this shapes behavioral adaptivity throughout the menstrual cycle.

Methods

Sample

Participants were assessed at six menstrual cycle time points: menstrual (< 5 days menses onset; “M”), pre-ovulatory (≤ 2 days before ovulation; “PREO”), (peri-)ovulatory (≤ 2 of ovulation; “O”), post-ovulatory (≤ 2 days after ovulation; “POSTO”), mid-luteal (6-8 days after ovulation; “MIDL”), and pre-menstrual (≤ 2 days before next menses onset; “PREM”). The final sample included 27 participants. Nineteen participants had data at all six time points. The remaining participants had data for five (N=2), four (N=1), three (N=1), two (N=1) or one (N=3) time points. In total, the dataset comprised 136 observations (24 M and O measurements, 22 PREO, POSTO, MIDL and PREM measurements).

Endocrine measures

Participants' menstrual cycles were continuously tracked using repeated intravaginal ultrasounds and ovulation testing. At each assessment, fasting serum samples were collected and analyzed via liquid-chromatography mass-spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) to quantify estradiol (E2), progesterone (P4), luteinizing hormone (LH), and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), and to confirm current cycle phase. For statistical analyses, E2 values were square-root transformed and P4 values were log-transformed to correct for non-normal distributions. 

Neuroimaging measures

At each assessment, participants underwent MRI scanning on a Siemens MAGNETOM Verio 3T machine, including anatomical MPRAGE (TR = 2,300 ms, TE = 4.21 ms, FOV = 176 mm x 240 mm x 256 mm, flip angle = 9°, voxel size = 1 x 1 x 1 mm3) and resting-state functional MRI (TR = 1,400 ms, TE = 30 ms, FOV = 202.4 mm x 202.4 mm x 147.2 mm, flip angle = 69°, voxel size: 2.3 x 2.3 x 2.3 mm3) sequences. Preprocessing was performed using CONN (conn22v2407; in SPM12; Matlab R2024b), including realignment using fieldmaps, as well as a unified normalization and segmentation approach. Sequences were then denoised and bandpass filtered (0.01-0.1 Hz). First- and second-level analyses comprised hypothesis-based seed-to-ROI (region-of-interest) and exploratory seed-to-voxel functional connectivity analyses using the right and left hippocampus as seed regions. Eigenvector centrality (EC) values were derived via the fast EC mapping (fastECM) algorithm, using the bilateral hippocampus as the primary ROI. In upcoming analyses, we will also apply gradientography to assess cycle-related changes in hippocampal-to-whole-brain functional connectivity gradients.

Linear mixed-effects models

Linear mixed-effects models (LMEs) of seed-based functional connectivity (from CONN first-level functional connectivity values) and EC (from fastECM) were built in R (lme4 package), with subject as random intercept. Three separate models were built with E2 or P4, or their interaction, as fixed effects, respectively. To evaluate potential effects of menstrual cycle time point beyond hormone levels, additional LMEs included time point, alone, or in interaction with hormone levels. ROIs were selected based on previous literature. Here, we expected a modulatory role of P4 on hippocampal functional connectivity with dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (middle and superior frontal gyrus) and somatosensory regions (pre- and postcentral gyri)3, and a modulatory role of E2 on hippocampal functional connectivity with amygdala, nucleus accumbens, anterior and posterior cingulate cortex, precuneus, superior parietal lobule and frontal orbital cortex4,5 (preregistration: https://osf.io/kwzxg). Significance levels were FDR corrected to account for repeated testing.

Results

Hippocampal eigenvector centrality

We could not replicate previous findings on a P4 modulation of hippocampal EC.3 Instead, right hippocampal EC was significantly predicted by the interaction of E2 and time point (F(5, 103.68) = 3.062, pFDR = 0.026; random intercept SD = 1.30 × 10-5), when controlling for P4. Follow-up contrasts indicated that effects were driven by negative effects of E2 during ovulatory (β = −7.03 × 10-6, 95% CI [−1.20 × 10-5, −2.11 × 10-6]), post-ovulatory (β = −6.50 × 10-6, 95% CI [−1.12 × 10-5, −1.84 × 10-7]) and pre-menstrual (β = −1.47 × 10-5, 95% CI [−2.26 × 10-5, −6.77 × 10-6]) time points. In addition, a positive effect of the pre-menstrual relative to the menstrual time point was observed (β = 1.44 × 10-4, 95% CI [6.99 × 10-5, 2.25 × 10-4]). No effects were found for the left hippocampus seed.

Hippocampal seed-based functional connectivity

P4 showed no significant effects on hippocampal connectivity with our hypothesized ROIs. However, in exploratory analyses, significant effects of time point on left middle frontal gyrus connectivity were revealed when controlling for E2, for left (F(5, 108.73) = 2.693, pFDR = 0.049; random intercept SD = 0.079) and right (F(5, 109.84) = 2.736, pFDR = 0.049; random intercept SD = 0.076) hippocampus. In both models, this main effect was driven by a decrease in functional connectivity from menstruation to pre-ovulation (left: ß = -0.279, 95% CI [-0.492, -0.067]; right: ß = -0.298, 95% CI [-0.518, -0.077]).

Our findings did not confirm an association of E2 with connectivity strength for any of our hypothesized hippocampus connections. Instead, again, in exploratory analyses of hippocampus-precuneus connectivity, a significant main effect of time point was observed in models accounting for P4 levels, for both hippocampus seeds (right: F(5, 105.89) = 2.391, pFDR = 0.043; left: F(5, 105.49) = 2.815, pFDR = 0.040). For the right hippocampus, this effect was driven by the post-ovulatory time point, at which functional connectivity was significantly lower compared to menstruation (β = -0.137, 95% CI [-0.241, -0.032], random intercept SD = 0.100). For the left hippocampus, it was driven by both the post-ovulatory (β = -0.128, 95% CI [-0.231, -0.025]) and mid-luteal (β = -0.225, 95% CI [-0.413, -0.038]; random intercept SD = 0.107) time points. In the left hippocampus model, a main effect of P4 was observed which did not survive FDR correction (β = 0.028, 95% CI [-0.034, 0.090], pFDR = 0.056).

For the anterior cingulate cortex, additional LMEs including P4 and time point revealed significant effects of time point on bilateral hippocampal functional connectivity, both as a main effect (right: F(5, 113.36) = 3.012, pFDR = 0.014; left: F(5, 108.83) = 3.437, pFDR = 0.013), as well as in interaction with P4 levels (right: F(5,114.37) = 3.782, pFDR = 0.007, random intercept SD = 0.060; left: F(5, 108.96) = 2.912, pFDR = 0.017, random intercept SD = 0.092). More specifically, functional connectivity between right hippocampus and anterior cingulate cortex was significantly predicted by joint effects of P4 and time point (P4 * M: ß = -0.102, 95% CI [-0.175, -0.029]; P4 * PREO: ß = 0.177, 95% CI [0.082, 0.272]; P4 * O: ß = 0.109, 95% CI [0.021, 0.196]; P4 * POSTO: ß = 0.123, 95% CI [0.042, 0.204]; P4 * MIDL: ß = 0.175, 95% CI [0.082, 0.269]; P4 * PREM: ß = 0.096, 95% CI [0.011, 0.181]), as well as by the main effect of the pre-ovulatory time point (ß = 0.159, 95 % CI [0.031, 0.288]). Left hippocampal functional connectivity with anterior cingulate cortex was predicted by effects of P4 during the ovulatory (ß = 0.096, 95% CI [0.007, 0.184]), post-ovulatory (ß = 0.084, 95% CI [0.002, 0.165]), and mid-luteal (ß = 0.163, 95% CI [0.068, 0.258]) time points, and by independent effects of the mid-luteal time point (ß = -0.361, 95% CI [-0.582, -0.140]).

Conclusion

The hippocampus exhibits remarkable structural and functional neuroplasticity, responding not only to to exogenous stimuli such as learning or chronic stress but also to endogenous physiological rhythms, such as endocrine fluctuations. Using a dense-sampling, repeated-measures design across the menstrual cycle in 27 participants, we assessed how hippocampal functional connectivity and network centrality vary as a function of ovarian hormone dynamics.

Our findings show that E2 negatively modulated right hippocampal EC, with effects varying by menstrual cycle phase and strongest during ovulatory and pre-menstrual days. P4 modulated bilateral hippocampal functional connectivity with the anterior cingulate cortex, also in a phase-dependent manner, such as exerting negative effects during menstruation and positive effects in other phases for the right hippocampal seed. 

Additional significant main effects of cycle phase emerged for bilateral hippocampal connectivity with left middle frontal gyrus and the precuneus. Here, effects were only detectable when models accounted for ovarian hormone fluctuations, underscoring the complexity of hormone-cycle phase interactions and the relevance of careful hormone-informed modeling to reveal subtle, phase-dependent connectivity changes.

Despite all participants being regularly cycling and our direct confirmation of ovulation status via LH tests and ultrasounds, we observed substantial inter-individual variability in E2 and P4 trajectories. These findings highlight the need to capture both intra- and inter-individual endocrine variation when studying menstrual cycle effects on the brain. In this well-characterized sample, the hippocampus showed subtle but significant hormone-dependent changes in functional connectivity, demonstrating its capacity to adapt to short-term endogenous hormone shifts. Future analyses will extend these findings by investigating menstrual cycle–related alterations in hippocampal–cortical gradient organization. Taken together, such a multimodal analysis approach provides new insight into the scale and specificity of healthy neuroendocrine plasticity.
