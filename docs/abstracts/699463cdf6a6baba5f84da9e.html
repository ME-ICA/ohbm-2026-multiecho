Introduction

The cerebellum, located beneath the cerebrum and connected to the brainstem and cerebral hemispheres, plays a fundamental role in the coordination of motor, cognitive, and autonomic functions. Its main communication pathways are the superior, middle, and inferior cerebellar peduncles (SCP, MCP, and ICP), which, together with other cerebellar connections, enable efficient information transfer to and from the cerebellum, thereby supporting proper motor, cognitive, and autonomic functioning in humans^1.

Cerebellar connectivity has been studied using diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (dMRI) tractography, addressing both specific pathways and broader cortico-ponto-cerebellar projections and cerebro-cerebellar connections. Post-mortem and microsurgical studies have provided detailed descriptions of anatomically and functionally relevant tracts and peduncles, revealing associations with neuropsychiatric disorders such as autism, ADHD, bipolar disorder, and schizophrenia. 

However, most existing atlases focus on individual structures and lack a comprehensive population-based mapping of cerebellar connectivity^2. Our work addresses this limitation by constructing an atlas of 20 human cerebellar fiber tracts derived from 20 subjects from the HCP S1200 dataset. Furthermore, to enable atlas-based tract segmentation in new subjects, a leave-one-out approach was employed to define tract-specific distance thresholds. The resulting thresholds were evaluated in 10 independent subjects from the same database, with visual inspection of the results to ensure anatomical consistency of the fascicles. This approach aims to provide a robust resource for identifying and studying cerebellar connectivity in both healthy populations and clinical contexts.

Methods

Cerebellar connection atlas. Data from 20 healthy adults from the Human Connectome Project (HCP) 1200 database were used. The database includes T1-weighted structural images and diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (dMRI) acquired on a 3T Siemens Connectom scanner (voxel size: 1.2 × 1.2 × 1.2 mm). Diffusion images were acquired with approximately 90 gradient directions at three b-values (1000, 2000, and 2995 s/mm²) and were preprocessed using the HCP pipeline^3.

We selected 20 cerebellar connections, connecting key cerebral and cerebellar structures, including the frontal and parietal lobes, left and right cerebellum, ventral dentate nucleus, red nucleus, and lateral occipital areas, in both the left and right hemispheres (Figure 1).

First, we performed the segmentation of the region-of-interest (ROI), using a combination of automatic and manual methods.  Each ROI was validated by an anatomy specialist to ensure accurate anatomical representation. Automatic segmentation was used for pre-segmented structures available in existing atlases, while manual segmentation was applied to cerebellar nuclei and cerebellar peduncles.

Deterministic tractography was performed using DSI Studio^4 to reconstruct fibers for the 20 selected connections, using specific ROIs. Each resulting bundle was reviewed and filtered to remove duplicate streamlines and fibers outside the expected anatomical trajectory. Finally, tracts from the 20 subjects were merged to generate a single representative tract per connection, forming the final atlas. Each tract was transformed into MNI space. A refinement process was then applied to remove minor artifacts resulting from the merging procedure.

Cerebellar bundles segmentation. To segment cerebellar connections in new subjects based on the created atlas, a spatial proximity-based segmentation method was applied^5. For each fiber from the subject’s whole-brain tractography, the maximum Euclidean distance to each fiber in the atlas bundles is computed. Fibers are assigned to the atlas bundle with the highest similarity, provided that the distance to the closest atlas bundle is below the tract-specific distance threshold. All atlas and subject fibers need to be resampled to 21 equidistant points.

To define the thresholds, we used the leave-one-out approach, based on deterministic tractography calculated with DSI Studio. For that, 20 atlas versions were generated, each systematically excluding one subject. This ensured that during automatic segmentation, each subject was evaluated using an atlas constructed without their own data, thereby guaranteeing validation independence.

Determining the optimal distance threshold for each tract required an iterative tuning process. An initial distance of 15 mm was selected to capture inter-subject anatomical variability. At each iteration, similarity between the automatically segmented tract and the manually segmented tract used during atlas construction was evaluated using the DICE coefficient computed on fiber density maps. If the resulting DICE coefficient was below 0.8, the distance was reduced by 1 mm, and the segmentation process was repeated. This procedure was applied to all 20 tracts across the 20 atlas subjects. The distance corresponding to the highest DICE coefficient prior to performance degradation was selected as the optimal value for each tract.

To assess atlas generalizability, the optimized distance thresholds were applied to an independent set of 10 additional HCP subjects not involved in atlas construction. Deterministic tractography for these subjects was generated using fixed initial parameters (tracking threshold: 0.04; angular threshold: randomly selected between 45° and 90°) to establish a baseline for future comparisons. 

Finally, segmentation results for these 10 subjects were visually evaluated by neuroanatomy experts to verify anatomical consistency and reproducibility of the proposed segmentation method.

Results

The final atlas comprises 20 cerebellar tracts, with the number of fibers varying according to the anatomical complexity and volume of each connection. Most fascicles contain between 5,000 and 10,000 fibers, whereas those associated with the cerebellar peduncles reach approximately 20,000 fibers due to their greater density and anatomical extent (see Figure 1).

To implement the leave-one-out approach for the bundle segmentation distance threshold, it was necessary to trace each fiber from the merged tracts back to its subject of origin. It is important to note that during the merging process, some fibers were modified due to redundancy removal or trimming of segments to preserve the anatomical coherence of the atlas. As a result, not all fibers from the merged atlas could be identified in their corresponding original manually segmented tracts. Fibers that could not be matched to their subject of origin were excluded from the atlas. During this process, 95% of the fibers in the merged atlas were successfully identified. Fiber loss ranged between 300 and 1,000 fibers per tract. The CTC and SPNC tracts exhibited greater loss of original information and were therefore reconstructed to ensure the robustness of subsequent analyses.

The optimal distance thresholds, determined using the leave-one-out approach, ranged from 5 mm to 12 mm, depending on the anatomical characteristics of each tract (see Figure 2.a). These values are consistent with those reported in previous fiber atlases of deep white matter bundles (10-18 mm) and short association fibers (6-8 mm). Because manually segmented fascicles were altered during the merging and refinement process, DICE coefficients considered acceptable or representative of the best results obtained ranged between 0.5 and 0.75. However, some fascicles did not reach these thresholds with the tractography parameters employed. The most challenging tracts were the DRTC, with an average DICE coefficient of 0.3, and the OPC, with 0.4. These difficulties suggest that these fascicles may require adjustments to tractography parameters or alternative segmentation strategies.

When applying the optimized distance thresholds to the 10 independent subjects, most tracts were successfully segmented. However, the DRTC tract consistently presented difficulties in being identified across all test subjects, with one or both hemispheres missing in some cases. This result is consistent with the low performance observed during the distance threshold optimization phase.

Visual evaluation revealed that the tracts with the highest anatomical consistency were the ICP, PPC, and CTC (see Figure 2.b). These fascicles exhibited clear and reproducible morphology across subjects, validating the robustness of the proposed method for these specific connections.

Conclusion

This work presents an atlas of 20 human cerebellar fiber tracts constructed using high-quality data from the Human Connectome Project. The implementation of a leave-one-out approach enabled the determination of tract-specific optimal distance thresholds, establishing a systematic methodology for the automatic segmentation of cerebellar connections. While some tracts demonstrated satisfactory reproducibility in external subjects, others require additional adjustments to tractography parameters or segmentation strategies. Nevertheless, this work represents a significant step toward the development of a robust resource for studying cerebellar connectivity in both healthy populations and clinical contexts.

The results demonstrate that most cerebellar tracts can be reproducibly segmented using the proposed optimized radii, with fascicles such as the ICP, PPC, and CTC showing excellent anatomical consistency across subjects. However, other tracts, such as the DRTC, posed greater challenges, exhibiting lower DICE coefficients and consistent identification difficulties. This suggests that the anatomical complexity and tract-specific characteristics of certain fascicles require additional considerations and more sophisticated segmentation strategies beyond search distance threshold optimization.

Validation in 10 independent subjects confirmed the atlas’s ability to generalize to most connections, while also highlighting methodological limitations for tracts with smaller volumes or greater morphological complexity. Importantly, the deterministic tractography parameters used to generate the fibers play a critical role in the quality of the final segmentation, creating opportunities for methodological improvements.

Future work will include more subjects and systematically explore the effects of different tractography parameter configurations on segmentation quality for the most challenging tracts, aiming to identify optimal combinations that improve reproducibility, particularly for fascicles such as the DRTC. In addition, complementary segmentation strategies will be evaluated to better capture inter-individual anatomical variability. Finally, applying the atlas-based segmentation of cerebellar connections to clinical populations with cerebellar involvement is a priority to assess its utility in neuropsychiatric and neurodegenerative disorders characterized by altered cerebellar connectivity.
