Introduction

Skilled musical performance relies on multisensory feedback and precise sensorimotor integration.1 When playing string instruments, musicians continuously monitor auditory, visual, tactile, and proprioceptive feedback to guide their movements. Investigating the neural mechanisms supporting real-world string instrument performance using fMRI poses substantial technical challenges, such as the compatibility of instruments with MRI, unnatural playing postures, limited space for bowing movements, gradient noise, head motion induced by bowing, and the difficulty of providing real-time sensory feedback. Consequently, most previous studies have focused on passive listening or motor imagery rather than actual performance.2 Here, we take the first step in examining the neural correlates of naturalistic Erhu performance with fMRI. The Erhu, a traditional Chinese string instrument, presents particular challenges for fMRI studies due to its bow length and bowing techniques. We used a direct-view setup to allow a near naturalistic playing posture, enabling performers to receive visual feedback of their hand movements. Furthermore, MR-compatible headphones provided real-time auditory feedback during performance. Using a phase-encoded fMRI design,3 we captured the spatiotemporal dynamics of sensory-motor integration as amateur Erhu players reproduced melodies following auditory presentation of short musical phrases, under four conditions that systematically varied the availability of auditory and visual feedback. This paradigm enables the tracking of information flows across the brain as sensory feedback conditions change, revealing the neural basis of multisensory and sensorimotor integration during naturalistic string instrument performance, situating music performance within auditory-motor integration models developed for speech, particularly those involving the Sylvian-parietal-temporal area (Spt).4,5

Methods

Sixteen amateur Erhu players (age: 21.25 ± 3.3) participated in the study. All participants were native Chinese speakers, right-handed, and had normal or corrected-to-normal vision.

During each 16-s trial, participants listened to 8-note Erhu melodies for 6 seconds and then reproduced the melody at a similar tempo under four feedback conditions: (1) eyes open, playing with both visual and auditory feedback; (2) eyes open, playing a hairless bow with visual feedback only; (3) eyes closed, playing with auditory feedback only; and (4) eyes closed, playing without any visual or auditory feedback with a hairless bow. Each trial within the same condition was repeated 16 times in a 256-s scan, which itself was repeated twice during an fMRI session.

Participants were scanned in a Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma 3T MRI scanner at the Centre for Cognitive and Brain Sciences. Prerecorded Erhu melodies and participant-produced sound were delivered and recorded using an MR-compatible active noise-cancellation system (OptoActive II, OptoAcoustics Ltd.). Participants wore earplugs and headphones, and the gradient noise was attenuated by acoustic foams lining the scanner bore. Head motion during Erhu performance was minimized with a custom-molded thermoplastic mask. The Siemens 32-channel head coil was tilted in a direct-view configuration to provide an unobstructed line of sight to the instrument and hand movements. Participants lay supine and played a modified Erhu equipped with nylon strings and a 30-cm bow; a separate hairless bow was used in conditions without auditory feedback. Together, these measures allowed continuous fMRI acquisition during realistic Erhu performance under different visual and auditory feedback conditions.

Functional images were acquired using an echo planar imaging (EPI) sequence (55 axial images, matrix size = 64x64, voxel size = 3x3x3 mm, TE = 30 ms, TR = 1 s, 256 TR per image). The time series of fMRI data were analyzed using Fourier transform. The amplitudes and phases of significant periodic activations at the task frequency (16 cycles per scan) were displayed on the cortical surfaces reconstructed from the structural images of each participant.

Results

Regardless of visual or auditory feedback, Erhu performance commonly activated the supplementary motor area (SMA), pre-SMA, middle to posterior cingulate cortex, dorsal and ventral premotor cortex (PMC), anterior insula, primary sensorimotor cortex (SMC), posterior parietal cortex (including the superior and inferior parietal lobules; SPL and IPL), superior temporal cortex (STC), area Spt,4,5 secondary somatosensory cortex (S-II), and the supramarginal gyrus (SMG) in all four conditions.

In eyes open conditions, regardless of the presence of auditory feedback, finger and bowing movements activated the peripheral representations of early visual areas V1, V2, and V3, as well as motion-sensitive areas V3A, V6, V7, cIPS, LIP1, anterior precuneus (aPCu), and the middle temporal complex (MT+). Compared with the conditions without auditory feedback, real Erhu performance with auditory feedback (regardless of eyes open or closed conditions) further activated the inferior frontal gyrus (IFG) and frontal operculum (FOP).

Analysis of phase-encoded activation patterns revealed several streams of traveling waves3 propagating across the cortical surface. The first stream emerged at SMA and propagated toward the midcingulate cortex (MCC). The second stream emerged at the dorsal premotor cortex (area 6a) and propagated toward the hand representations in the primary motor cortex. The third stream emerged at the IFG and propagated toward the ventral premotor cortex and reached the primary motor cortex. The fourth stream emerged at the FOP and propagated toward the insula. Lastly, a stream emerged at the primary auditory cortex, passed the Spt at the posterior lateral sulcus, and reached the SMG and IPL.

Conclusion

In this exploratory study we addressed technical challenges of overt string-instrument performance in the MRI scanner by using a direct-view setup that allowed Erhu performers to see their finger and bow movements while minimizing their head motion. Visual feedback produced activation changes confined to motion-sensitive visual areas and did not significantly alter the broader sensorimotor-auditory activation patterns, suggesting that the brain’s auditory-motor circuitry for overt-string performance is robust to added visual information. A common network consisting of the SMA, pre-SMA, MCC, PMC, insula, SMC, SPL, IPL, STC, Spt, S-II, and SMG was engaged across all four feedback conditions. These regions exhibited a range of activation phases during real-time Erhu performance that resulted in traveling waves propagating across the cortical surface. Several streams of traveling waves originated from the SMA, dorsal PMC, IFG and ventral PMC, FOP, and primary auditory cortex, respectively. Situated on the stream propagating from the auditory cortex to SMG and IPL, area Spt emerged as a key hub linking auditory predictions to sensorimotor integration during string instrument performance.
