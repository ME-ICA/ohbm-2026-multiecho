Introduction

Midlife (40 - 65 years of age) is a critical stage in adulthood when age-related episodic memory decline is first detectable.1 Midlife is also the age that most females with ovaries experience spontaneous menopause. At midlife, females move from pre- to peri- to post- menopause, and experience declines in centrally circulating estradiol-17b (E2) which is neuroprotective for memory. Compared to premenopausal females and middle-aged males, postmenopausal females perform worse on episodic memory tasks for spatial source.2 

Individual differences in spatial source memory have been associated with differences in hippocampal volume.3 The hippocampus is critical for episodic memory, and it is composed of subfields that play functionally distinct roles in memory processes. Yet it remains unclear whether and how age, sex, and menopause status affect hippocampal subfield volumes, and their associations with episodic memory, at midlife. To address this knowledge gap, the current study aims to determine how biological sex and pre- compared to post-menopause status affected: (1) age-related differences in spatial source retrieval during a spatial source memory task midlife, (2) age-related differences in hippocampal subfield volumes and (3) the association between hippocampal subfields and spatial source retrieval accuracy. 

Methods

All participants in this study are part of the larger ongoing Brain Health at Midlife and Menopause (BHAMM) cohort study.^3 We collected ultra-high-resolution T2-weighted (T2-w) structural MRI scans for the hippocampal subfield segmentation (TR 2500ms, TE 198 ms, 320 slices of 0.60 mm thickness, voxel size = 0.6  mm3, FOV = 206 mm, acquisition time = 13 min) from 132 cognitively unimpaired middle-aged adults (38 males, 49 premenopausal females, 45 postmenopausal females; aged 39.55–65.46 years). Participants completed a face-location spatial source memory task where they were asked to encode faces presented one at a time in one of four quadrants on a screen and later recall the face and its associated location during retrieval (spatial source retrieval accuracy). Task difficulty was also manipulated such that all participants were required to encode and retrieve 6 face-locations in easy task versions and 12 face-locations in the hard task versions. Correct spatial source accuracy (CS; proportion of correct face-location associations retrieved) was the behavioural outcome variable. 

We trained a machine learning model (Automated Segmentation of Hippocampal Subfields; ASHS) to create a customized-automated segmentation pipeline to extract subfield volumes for cornu ammonis (CA) 1, CA2/3/-dentate gyrus (DG), and subiculum.4 To create our custom atlas, we first applied an existing ASHS atlas trained on the same manual segmentation protocol but on a different MRI resolution to 20 MRI scans, as a starting point.5 These 20 initial segmentation outputs were manually corrected by two raters to ensure anatomical precision with our isotropic scans and were used to re-train ASHS (see Figure 1). Volumes from left and right subfields were summed and adjusted for intracranial volume.

Two multivariate analyses of variance (MANOVA) were conducted using R v4.4.3 to test for sex differences in CS and subfield volume, respectively. To assess age effects on CS accuracy and subfield volumes, we ran linear mixed effect regression (LMER) and linear model (LMs), respectively. If sex differences in CS and volumes were observed, we conducted a final LMER to determine if sex differences in CS were predicted by sex differences in volumes. Age was included if age was identified as a significant predictor in any model. Task difficulty (easy, hard versions) was included in all models where CS was the outcome measure. Education was a covariate in the sex analyses due to significant educational differences between males and females. All predictors were standardized.

To determine menopause effects, MANOVAs were conducted to test for differences in CS and subfield volume between pre- and postmenopausal females. LMERs and LMs were used to determine the effect of menopause status on CS, subfield volumes, and the association between volume and CS were examined using the same analytic procedures as those used to explore sex differences. However, because menopause status and age are collinear variables, within menopause group analyses were conducted to examine age-effects on CS and subfield volumes.

Results

Sex differences in CS and Hippocampal Subfields: There was no significant sex difference in CS accuracy (p = .38) or hippocampal subfields (p = .84) according to MANOVA. The sex and age LMER on CS identified a significant sex-by-age interaction (β = -.04, p = .02), task difficulty-by-age interaction (β = -.01, p = .03), task difficulty (β = .09, p < .001), and education (β = .06, p < .001) effects. Within-sex LMERs on CS indicated that the sex-by-age interaction was due to a significant age effect in females (β = -.07, p < .001), but not males (p = .77). The within-sex LMERs on CS also identified a significant effect of task in both males (β = .09, p < .001) and females (β = .09, p < .001), and a significant task-by-age interaction in females (β = -.02, p = .02).

The LMs examining sex and age effects on subfield volumes found no significant effect of sex nor sex-by-age interactions. All subfields showed significant age effects  (CA1 β = -.35, p < .001; CA 2/3/DG, β = -.34, p < .001; subiculum (β = -.24, p = .02), though the age effect on subiculum volume did not remain significant after Bonferroni corrections (α = .017).

Menopause status effects on CS and Hippocampal Subfields: MANOVA indicated significant group differences in CS accuracy (p < .001). The LMER predicting CS found a significant effects of menopause status (β = .07, p < .001), task difficulty (β = .10, p < .001), and task difficulty-by-menopause status interaction (β = .02, p = .003). Post-hoc analyses indicated that premenopausal females had significantly greater performance compared to postmenopausal females on both the easy (estimated mean difference = 0.18, p < .001) and hard (estimated mean difference = 0.09, p = .001) task runs. Within-menopause group LMERs on CS indicated that postmenopausal females had a significant effect of age (β = -.10, p = .03) and task difficulty (β = .08, p < .001). Premenopausal females only had a significant effect of task difficulty (β = .12, p < .001), not age (p = .16). 

Between group MANOVA on subfield volumes was significant (p = .002). Univariate ANOVAs on subfields indicated postmenopausal females had significantly smaller  CA1 (p < .001) and CA2/3/DG volumes (p = .006) compared to premenopausal females. The within-menopause group LMs examining the effect of age on subfields found that only postmenopausal females exhibited a significant age effect in CA1 (β = -.99, p = .001) and subiculum volumes (β = -1.61, p = .001). There were no significant age effects on subfield volumes in premenopausal females.

Brain-behavior associations in postmenopausal females: Since only females showed age-related differences in CS compared to males, and this effect was only observed in postmenopausal females, brain-behavior LMERs analyses were conducted only in postmenopausal females to test whether CS was predicted by age, task difficulty, CA1 or subiculum, and interactions. Chi-square analysis indicated the full interaction model for CA1 was significantly different from a null (intercept only) model (χ2(7, N = 45) = 57.16, p < .001). Similarly, the full interaction model for subiculum was significantly different from a null model (χ2(7, N = 45) = 56.96, p < .001). Following a stepwise approach to model fit testing, our full interaction models did not significantly improve model fit compared to a reduced interaction model where we excluded interaction terms with age for both CA1 (χ2(3, N = 45) = 1.49, p = .68) and subiculum (χ2(3, N = 45) = .48, p = .92), thus we report results from the reduced interaction models. There were task difficulty-by-volume interactions for both CA1 (β = -0.2, p = .008) and subiculum (β = -.02, p = .004) on CS accuracy (see Figure 2). However, within task difficulty (easy, hard) LMs on CS predicted by CA1 or subiculum were not significant in the easy (CA1 β = -0.01, p = .76; subiculum β = -0.005, p = .85) or hard (CA1 β = 0.04, p = .11; subiculum β = 0.04, p = .061) task versions.

Conclusion

Our findings point to midlife as a period when sex differences in spatial source memory are apparent. While we did not find significant mean differences in spatial source memory between males and females, we found a significant sex-by-age interaction on memory performance. Specifically, we found that females performed significantly worse with age compared to males, indicating that older females performed worse on our face-location memory task than younger females. Consistent with past research, we found that menopause status affected spatial source memory such that older postmenopausal females performed significantly worse than premenopausal females on both easy and hard versions of the memory task. 

The current study also delineated the differential impact of age, sex, and menopause status on subfields and their relationship to spatial source memory. Although there were no sex differences in hippocampal subfield volumes, we observed significant age-related differences in CA1 and CA2/3/DG.  When we examined females by menopause status, we observed that postmenopausal females demonstrated age-related effects in CA1 and subiculum volumes, but premenopausal females did not show these effects. Since only postmenopausal females exhibited age-related differences in CS we explored brain-behavior associations only in them. We found that both CA1 and subiculum volumes interacted with task difficulty and raw plots indicated that larger volumes in these subfields were associated with better memory performance in postmenopausal females, but only on the hard versions of the task. Thus, smaller volume in CA1 and subiculum in older postmenopausal females were thought to be associated with worse memory performance when the task became challenging. However, when we probed these effects within task difficulty models, the effect of CA1 or subiculum did not significantly predict CS accuracy in the hard task, which may have been due to issues of power. In summary, our findings emphasize the need for sex-informed and menopausal consideration in cognitive neuroscience and aging research and call for more nuanced models that move beyond binary sex comparisons to incorporate life-stage and hormonal context.
