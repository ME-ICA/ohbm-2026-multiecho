Introduction

Early-life stress (ELS) has been consistently linked to widespread, brain-wide structural changes. Across human and animal studies, ELS has been linked to changes in volume of the amygdala and hippocampus (1), alongside alterations in fronto-striatal and limbic regions such as the anterior cingulate cortex, caudate nucleus, dorsal striatum, and broader limbic system (2). Cortical thinning has also been reported in regions including the orbitofrontal cortex and precuneus following experiences of abuse, poverty, and emotional maltreatment (3,4).

Currently, much of the existing research has focused on the consequences of individual stressors within relatively narrow demographic groups, such as institutionalised samples or adults who have been exposed to emotional abuse in their youth. Consequently, there remains a limited understanding of how cumulative exposure to ELS influences brain development more broadly, particularly across key development stages. Importantly, brain regions mature at different rates, with the prefrontal cortex the last to reach full maturity as it continues to develop throughout adolescence (5). This prolonged developmental trajectory represents an extended window of neuroplasticity during which ELS may have particularly pronounced effects. In line with this, reductions in cortical thickness within the PFC, and more specifically the orbitofrontal cortex, have been observed in children exposed to early institutionalisation, abuse, and deprivation (1,4). Consequently, the present study investigates the cumulative effects of ELS in a large-scale sample scanned at multiple stages of adolescence to characterise how brain structure is altered during this sensitive period of neurodevelopment.

Methods

Data for this analysis were obtained from the IMAGEN consortium, comprising 2,000 adolescents recruited across seven sites in Europe. After assessing the completeness of the data and performing a visual inspection of the MRI scans, the final sample consisted of 1,214 participants (male = 582, female = 632). T1-weighted structural MRI scans were acquired at three time points during adolescence per individual: with average ages of 14.41 (±0.39) at baseline, 19.04 (±0.74) at follow-up 2, and 22.91 (±1.13) at follow-up 3. At the second follow-up (age 19), participants also completed the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (Bernstein et al., 1999). In this sample, the average CTQ score was 6.76 ±7.97 (range: 72). 

Voxel-based morphometry (VBM) analyses were conducted using SPM25. Firstly, all scans were visually inspected and quality checked. Then, scans at all timepoints were aligned within each subject to reduce intra-subject variability. This was achieved through serial longitudinal registration, where a midpoint average template was calculated for each subject, along with Jacobian determinant maps. During this stage, scans were also bias corrected. Once registration was complete, these individual average templates were then segmented into probability maps of their tissue classes: grey matter, white matter, CSF, bone and skull. er493

A geodesic shooting algorithm was then applied to non-linearly align the brain data, producing an estimated population template. This was used as it has been demonstrated to outperform creating DARTEL templates. A voxel-wise deformation field vector was then applied to the segmented grey matter scans at all timepoints to normalise these images to the standard MNI152 template. This method ensures all scans are warped consistently, reducing within-subject variability. Global normalisation values were then calculated to control for total intracranial volume in the model. Images were then smoothed using an 8mm FWHM kernel. After preprocessing, the general linear model was fitted to the preprocessed images. A multiple regression model controlling for sex and recruitment centre was then conducted to identify voxel-wise volume differences associated with ELS

Results

At the voxel-wise level, three regions demonstrated significant bilateral reductions in volume associated with ELS: the supplementary motor area (p < .001), the paracentral lobule (p = .001), and the precuneus (p = .001). An additional twelve regions demonstrated reductions unilaterally. In addition, twelve regions exhibited unilateral volume reductions. In the right hemisphere, reduced volume was observed in the superior parietal gyrus (p < .001), angular gyrus (p < .001), middle occipital gyrus (p < .001), inferior temporal gyrus (p < .001), and middle temporal gyrus (p < .001). In the left hemisphere, significant reductions were identified in the middle cingulate gyrus (p < .001), precentral gyrus (p = .001), medial orbitofrontal gyrus (p = .001), anterior cingulate gyrus (p = .001), and medial frontal gyrus (p = .001). Conclusion

In conclusion, the present findings suggest that adolescence represents a period of neurodevelopment during which certain brain regions may show altered trajectories in volume following ELS. These reductions appear not to be randomly distributed but aligned with specific large-scale networks, particularly the default mode and somatomotor networks, highlighting potential susceptibility of these networks during this developmental period. 

To examine these effects, the longitudinal data were condensed into a cross-sectional VBM approach using serial longitudinal registration, allowing for the assessment of average structural changes across the cohort. However, by doing so, we are overlooking potential trajectory changes that occur between these time points. Therefore, building on these results, a second VBM model is currently in progress, using a linear mixed-effects approach to investigate developmental trajectories over time and examine the role of cumulative ELS exposure. This approach will further these findings by providing insight into how cumulative ELS influences the development of brain structure across different stages of adolescence and into early adulthood.
