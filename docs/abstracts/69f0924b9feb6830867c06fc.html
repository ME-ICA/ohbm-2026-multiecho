Introduction

Psychedelic compounds such as psilocybin produce profound and transient alterations in perception, cognition, and self-experience, yet the systems-level neural mechanisms underlying these effects remain incompletely understood. Animal studies have shown changes in electrophysiological power in specific frequency bands and functional MRI (fMRI) studies in humans have shown that psilocybin modulates large-scale brain networks, including changes in default mode network integrity and global integration. However, prior work into the frequency-specific effects of psilocybin on fMRI data is limited. 

Blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) fMRI signals exhibit structured fluctuations mostly reported in the frequency range 0.01Hz to 0.1Hz, though the upper limit tends to vary between studies. Rather than estimating frequency-agnostic measures, characterizing how pharmacological perturbations reshape the spectral power of BOLD activity, as well as the connectivity patterns embedded within specific frequency ranges, may provide a further detailed description of altered brain states. 

Here, we investigate the acute effects of psilocybin on frequency-specific BOLD dynamics using a dense resting-state fMRI dataset comprising 28 healthy participants, each scanned before and 4-8 times acutely following oral psilocybin administration. We first quantify voxel-wise spectral power of BOLD time series to assess how psilocybin alters the spatiospectral distribution of spontaneous neural fluctuations. We then examine changes in functional connectivity by applying generalized eigendecomposition to cross-spectral density matrices, identifying connectivity patterns that maximally differentiate narrowband connectivity from broadband, noise-heavy connectivity. This approach allows us to isolate frequency-specific connectivity patterns while minimizing motion-related broadband contamination. 

Methods

In total, 165 fMRI scans (5 or 10 minutes, TR=2s) were acquired across 28 participants in two different scanners. Each participant was scanned once before and multiple times up to 350 minutes after oral psilocybin administration. Each scan was followed by a blood measurement for the plasma psilocin level (PPL), i.e., the concentration of the psychoactive substance in the blood, which is directly related to the intensity of the subjective experience. We used fMRIprep (v25.1.3, defaults) for fMRI preprocessing and quantified motion summary statistics for each scan: mean framewise displacement (FD), max FD, and pct_outliers (ratio of volumes with FD>0.5mm). We discarded motion-heavy scans (maxFD>3mm or pct_outliers>20%), leaving 125 scans after quality control. Motion parameters and aCompCor (5Wm and 5CSF components) were regressed out to reduce residual non-neural physiological signal. 

Multitaper spectra with 8 Slepian tapers were estimated for each voxel using nitime and subsequently parcellated into 200 cortical parcels1 and 7 cortical networks2. For each network and frequency, we fitted log10(power) using a linear mixed effects model with age, sex, scanner, meanFD, maxFD, pct_outliers, and PPL as fixed effects and subject as random intercept. We also fitted a secondary model without motion-related nuisance regressors. We corrected p-values using max-T permutation testing across frequencies (20 separate “bins”) and Bonferroni across 7 networks. We also fitted the statistical model to log10(power) from each cortical parcel and each of three frequency bands: Slow-3 (0.67Hz-0.2Hz), Slow-4 (0.025Hz-0.67Hz), and Slow-5 (0.01Hz-0.025Hz) to increase spatial precision while reducing spectral precision to not exaggerate the number of statistical tests applied. Here, p-values were corrected using max-T across parcels and Bonferroni across frequency bands. 

Using the obtained spectra we also quantified spectral entropy as the Shannon entropy of the spectra between 0.01Hz and 0.2Hz normalized to represent a probability distribution. We fitted the statistical model above to the entropy and corrected using max-T correction across parcels. 

To obtain a motion-agnostic assessment of functional connectivity, we computed the generalized eigendecomposition 

RWΛ=SW,

where W are eigenvectors that explain variance in the “ratio” between the narrowband covariance S and the broadband covariance R with the assumption that motion will be encapsulated in the broadband covariance matrix. When R and S are of the same scale, eigenvalues in L are directly interpretable as variance ratios, i.e., if λi>1 the corresponding eigenvector explains more variance in S than in R. 

Narrowband covariance matrices are not easily computed in fMRI data due to the short acquisition sequences, causing ringing effects from filtering to dominate the filtered signal. Instead, we computed the cross-spectral density matrix using multitaper cross spectra implemented in nitime. For each of 20 frequencies, we computed the generalized eigendecomposition between the narrowband (FWHM=0.01Hz)  and the broadband (0.01Hz-0.2Hz) covariance matrices and saved all eigencomponents with eigenvalue larger than one. All covariance matrices were normalized to unit trace before the eigendecomposition. 

Eigenvectors are orthonormal in broadband-space but not Euclidean space. Instead, we computed A=R1/2WΛ1/2 such that A represents a weighted basis set in the Euclidean space. Since the eigenvectors are not necessarily ordered the same way across scans, we computed the generalized orthogonal Procrustes alignment between A across all scans. This allowed us to quantify, for each frequency, the distance from the scan-specific weighted basis to the average across scans. Subsequently, we also computed the Procrustes distance between the average frequency-specific weighted basis to that of all other frequencies. 

Results

We found that log10(power) was negatively associated with PPL (p_perm_bonf<0.05) in all cortical networks at slow frequencies (0.01Hz-0.03Hz) and in higher-order networks (attention, control, default) at a wider range of slow frequencies (0.01Hz-0.08Hz). If not including motion summary statistics as nuisance regressors, log10(power) was significantly negatively associated with PPL in higher-order networks at slow frequencies (0.01Hz-0.04Hz) but significantly positively associated with PPL in all networks at fast frequencies (0.12Hz-0.22Hz). These findings indicate that psilocybin induces a brain-wide (but strongest in higher-order networks) flattening of the BOLD spectra, i.e., a reduction of low-frequency power and an increase in high-frequency power, the latter being associated with motion. 

When increasing the spatial resolution to 200 parcels, we found that (with motion nuisance regressors) Slow-4 and Slow-5 frequency band power were significantly negatively associated with PPL in most non-motor and non-limbic parcels with a stronger effect size in a wide range of frontal and parietal parcels. In these areas and strongest in the parietal cortex, we also found spectral entropy to be positively associated with PPL, again indicating a flattening of the BOLD spectrum particularly strong in transmodal regions. 

We observed the leading generalized eigenvalues of narrowband to broadband covariance to linearly increase across all frequencies, possibly indicating that the broadband covariance is more similar to slow frequencies. Interestingly, we found that leading eigenvalues were significantly positively associated with PPL at slow frequencies (0.01Hz-0.04Hz) but negatively associated at fast frequencies (0.08Hz-0.2Hz), indicating that Psilocybin leads to more distinct connectivity at slow frequencies but more broadband-like connectivity at higher frequencies. The same pattern was observed in the subspace distance from each scan’s frequency-specific subspace to the Procrustes-defined frequency-specific mean across all scans; this distance was significantly positively associated with PPL as slow frequencies (0.01Hz-0.05Hz) and again at fast frequencies (0.14Hz to 0.20Hz), indicating that psilocybin’s effects are more strongly exerted at slow and fast frequencies while middle frequencies (0.06Hz-0.13Hz) are less affected. 

When aligning both across scans and frequencies, we found leading eigenvectors to generally be similar in the range 0.01Hz to 0.07Hz across all scans (Pearson’s correlation > 0.8). At baseline, the leading eigenvector after alignment contrasted frontoparietal and temporal from sensorimotor areas in the brain. At peak psilocybin (~80 min post-administration), the frontoparietal areas were less distinguished. 

Conclusion

Here we provided a cross-frequency analysis of the spectral content of BOLD signals and their relation to the acute psilocybin experience. One previous study3 analyzed an aggregate measure of BOLD spectral power (average power between 0.01Hz-0.08Hz) in relation to the effect of music on the psilocybin experience, and several studies have analyzed psilocybin effects on canonical electrophysiological frequency bands. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of the full spectrum of spectral effects of psilocybin on human BOLD fMRI.

We found spectral power to decrease at slow frequencies following psilocybin administration, indicating a weakening of the BOLD response at these frequencies. Psilocybin has been posited to reduce cerebral blood flow, which correlates well with our findings. 

One theory of the effect of psilocybin, supported by multiple studies, is that the drug increases brain entropy, i.e., a measure of randomness in the brain. Here we find an increase in spectral entropy, a metric that quantifies the flatness of the spectrum, supporting the theory of increased brain entropy. However, most studies typically filter their fMRI data around 0.01Hz-0.1Hz, which includes the bandwidth wherein we observed reduced spectral power. The fMRI data was rescaled to percent change, meaning that reduced spectral power is directly related to a decrease in percent signal change and therefore a decrease in signal to noise ratio (SNR). Thus, other findings on psilocybin in fMRI data, including the relation to temporal complexity or entropy, may be explained by decreased SNR in data rather than increased neural “randomness”, such that these effects are better explained by noise in measurements rather than noise in brain biology, thereby offering a contradictory explanation for these findings. Future studies looking into the relation between cerebral blood flow, BOLD signal power, and subsequent quantifications are needed to confirm this relationship. 

We observed an opposing relation between psilocybin’s effect on narrowband connectivity at slow frequencies as opposed to fast frequencies. One explanation for this finding is indeed the reduced signal power at slow frequencies leading to the broadband covariance matrix, which was used as the reference in the generalized eigendecomposition, to be increasingly affected by higher frequencies following psilocybin. When not including motion summary statistics as nuisance regressors, we observed a statistical relation between spectral power and PPL at fast frequencies. Combined with the observation that leading eigenvalues were negatively associated with PPL, i.e., psilocybin causes narrowband connectivity at these frequencies to be more “like” broadband connectivity, which is assumed to encapsulate motion, we believe that motion effects on fMRI data are strongest at these frequencies (0.10Hz-Nyquist). This finding resonates with previous findings in the fMRI literature4,5. However, we assumed that the broadband covariance matrix predominantly fully captured non-neural noise, which is a limitation of our study. 

In conclusion, we find that psilocybin acutely reduces raw BOLD spectral power at slow frequencies in conjunction with reduced frontoparietal connectivity and that motion is particularly associated with spectral power at fast frequencies leading to reduced distinction between narrowband and broadband connectivity at these frequencies.
