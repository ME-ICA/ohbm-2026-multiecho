Introduction

Recent advances in face recognition technology have increased the potential that research participants could be re-identified from brain MRI, PET, or CT. Our group have tested this hypothesis using leading face recognition software from Microsoft Azure, and we found that the software was able to correctly and automatically match participants’ brain MRI with face photographs in up to 98% of 182 participants. PET and CT also matched at up to 42% and 78% respectively1. Related work by Steeg et al. also found that non-expert researchers were able to correctly identify 2 out of 4 brain MRI participants using only public web-page face recognition databases containing over 800 million photographs, in under 10 minutes2. These findings have led more neuroimaging datasets to consider using face de-identification (aka “de-facing”) software before public data sharing to protect the privacy of research participants.

Methods

Our group have developed software called mri_reface that replaces faces, ears, and teeth in brain and head imaging with imagery from an average template. Unlike other software, mri_reface more thoroughly replaces larger portions of the face, and its outputs are designed to maximally resemble a natural image. Comparisons by our group and by others have shown that mri_reface provides relatively greater protection against re-identification (by removing and replacing more identifiable features) and relatively smaller effects on brain quantification software (by more resembling an unmodified image with natural looking but different face anatomy, better matching analyses software’s training data and assumptions). Replicating our original face recognition experiments using de-identified images from mri_reface yielded only 8% correct matching, vs. other software still matched at up to 38%3,1. Analyses from our group and others have shown that effects on brain measurements from FreeSurfer, SPM, and FSL are smaller with mri_reface than with other software, and effects from all tested de-facing software are far below scan-rescan measurement variation3–5. We have also found that mri_reface had no significant effects on either the findings or the statistical power of relevant brain measurements for studying AD, DLB, and PSP. 

Results

Many large US-based aging and neurodegeneration open datasets historically did not de-face brain MRI or PET, but after considering recent advances, this trend has changed. Many large open neuroimaging datasets have begun using mri_reface for all identifiable brain images, including ADNI, SCAN, ALLFTD, A4, MCSA, CLARiTI, and NAPS2.  We have found that de-facing is warranted for most MRI sequences including structural T1-w, T2-w, FLAIR, T2*-w (susceptibility), and perfusion, all PET (amyloid, tau, and FDG), and all CT, and we have developed and validated support for all of them in mri_reface. The only exceptions have been functional MRI, diffusion MRI, and some specific older perfusion (ASL) sequences, where the face is not identifiable. 

Conclusion

We have developed mri_reface to promote and facilitate increased and wider data sharing with fewer restrictions and delays, and several datasets have shared more images more widely only through confidence in its protection. It is publicly available and free for non-commercial research use: https://www.nitrc.org/projects/mri_reface, and it supports automated de-facing of NIfTI (i.e. BIDS) or DICOM images (with preservation of all metadata) for all indicated brain imaging types.
