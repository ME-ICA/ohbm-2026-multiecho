Introduction

Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) is the most effective intervention for treatment-resistant depression and psychosis with remission rates up to 80% and decades of clinical application.1 Yet, its mechanism of action remains poorly understood. Neuroimaging suggests that ECT induces structural and functional changes in brain regions that are embedded in neural networks that have a causal role in treatment response.2 These effects are often interpreted in the context of neuroplasticity, with proposed mechanisms including increased neurotrophin signaling, neurogenesis, synaptogenesis, and glial proliferation.3 These processes may arise from cytokine-induced transcriptomic and proteomic modifications and modulate neural signaling by affecting neurotransmitter and neurosteroid systems.3 However, these components may not be universally necessary for treatment response, and evidence indicates that they may operate through partially independent pathways.4 The lack of integrative, large-scale studies makes it difficult to assemble these diverse findings into a coherent model of ECT’s mechanism of action.

Methods

Two independent literature searches identified studies investigating longitudinal ECT-induced structural or functional alterations. Activation likelihood estimation (ALE) meta-analysis and coordinate-based network mapping (CNM) were used to generate brain maps of structural, functional, and network-level brain adaptations (Fig. 1a). Anatomical labeling was conducted using the JuBrain Anatomy Toolbox, and network-level changes were related to established functional brain systems, employing the Network Correspondence Toolbox.

To assess potential drivers of neuroplasticity, we examined how the unthresholded maps of structural alterations were associated with specific cell types using the Virtual Histology approach (Fig. 1b).5 Gene expression distributions were obtained from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA) using the abagen toolbox and filtered for biological relevance (top 50% of genes with the highest differential stability) and consistency (correlation r≥0.52, one-sided p≤0.05 with the independent BrainSpan atlas). Genes were categorized into nine major brain cell types using the Zeisel cell-type taxonomy, and mean correlations with structural alterations were computed, with significance assessed via permutation testing. To further characterize these structural alterations, we examined their association with neurotrophins (BDNF, NGF, NTF3, NTF4) and their receptors (NTRK1, NTRK2, NTRK3, NGFR) using AHBA gene-expression distributions and Ridge regression. 

To extend these molecular and cellular associations to a mechanistic, system-level framework, we characterized all ECT-induced brain adaptation maps using normative neurotransmitter receptor and transporter distributions derived from the neuromaps software package. We employed dominance analysis for each ECT map, informed by neurotransmitter distributions, to quantify overall and individual contributions of neurotransmitters to ECT-induced adaptations. Associations with electrophysiology and mental processes were assessed using correlation analyses and spin tests implemented in neuromaps, leveraging normative MEG maps and meta-analytic cognitive maps from the Neurosynth database. 

To test the clinical relevance and specificity of convergent brain alterations, we analyzed a separate dataset of fast-acting antidepressant interventions (HCP-PDC 1.0 Release; Fig. 1c). Depressed patients underwent MRI and clinical assessment (Quick Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology, QIDS) before and after ECT (n=28), ketamine (n=52), or sleep deprivation (n=54). Structural T1-weighted images and resting-state functional MRI data were longitudinally processed using established pipelines (CAT12.9 and DPARSF). Individual pre-post differences in (i) gray matter in regions of convergent structural alterations, (ii) fractional amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations in regions of convergent functional alterations, and (iii) connectivity within significant networks were assessed, yielding a multimodal patient-by-feature matrix. Dimensionality was reduced using principal component analysis, retaining the first two components per domain based on scree plot inspection, to balance their contribution and mitigate overfitting. For each intervention, Ridge regression models predicted residualized QIDS change from multimodal PCA components, controlling for age and sex. Treatment specificity was assessed using multinomial logistic regression to discriminate between intervention groups using identical predictors. 

Results

Convergent ECT-induced morphological brain changes (≈1100 subjects, 45 experiments, 408 foci) emerged in an expansive right-hemispheric cluster covering temporal pole, temporoinsular transition zone, and amygdalohippocampal, as well as the ventral striatum and the subgenual cingulate cortex (Fig. 1c, right) - regions implicated in depression and targeted by deep brain stimulation for treatment-resistant depression. Virtual histology revealed significant positive associations between ECT-induced structural changes and interregional gene expression profiles specific to astrocytes (r=0.29, pperm=0.001, FWE corrected) and microglia (r=0.30, pperm=0.001, FWE corrected; Fig. 2a), consistent with a potential role of glial plasticity in ECT-induced remodeling. In addition, structural changes showed strong spatial correspondence with neurotrophin-related gene expression (R2=0.61).

Convergent functional changes (≈1200 subjects, 103 experiments, 340 foci) were identified in the left middle temporal and right insular cortices, bilateral temporoparietal junction, medial frontal pole, right hippocampus, and in anterior midcingulate cortex (Fig. 1a, right), a known target in cingulotomy for treatment-resistant depression. Both structural and functional alterations are embedded in the default mode, salience and visual networks – networks known to be altered in depression. Those network-level alterations are accompanied by widespread antidromic electrophysiological changes in all frequency bands (all |r|>0.45, pspin < 0.05, FWE corrected), indicating the engagement of broad-scale circuit dynamics. 

Across modalities, ECT-induced alteration maps show strong associations with neurotransmitter distribution (adjusted R2=0.33-0.72), with the serotonergic 5-HT1A, dopaminergic D2, and μ-opioid receptors emerging as the dominant contributors (Fig. 2b). These neurobiological signatures were further linked to emotion-related processes including ‘anxiety’(r=0.47), ‘emotion’ (r=0.55), ‘empathy’(r=0.41), and ‘stress’(r=0.58), indicating a multifaceted impact on affective brain systems. 

To assess the clinical relevance and specificitiy of multimodal features from regions of convergent ECT-induced brain adaptations (Fig. 2c), we evaluated their association with clinical outcome across fast-acting antidepressant interventions. Ridge regression model performance and permutation testing revealed the strongest – and only significant – association for ECT (R2=0.51, pperm=0.022) with clinical outcome, indicating an ECT-specific response signature (Fig. 2d). In the ECT group, functional domains – including fALFF and connectivity – contributed most strongly to the model, highlighting their role in rapid clinical response. Multinomial logistic regression demonstrated robust discrimination of ECT from other interventions (pperm<0.001, Fig. 2e), with gray matter alterations emerging as the most discriminative feature domain, supporting a distinct mechanism of action specific to ECT, rather than a general marker of improvement. 

Conclusion

Our findings delineate a convergent, multimodal neurobiological signature of electroconvulsive therapy, linking mesoscopic brain adaptations across structure, function, and network organization to underlying cellular, molecular, and electrophysiological processes. ECT-induced structural alterations co-localize with astrocytic and microglial gene expression and neurotrophin-related signaling, supporting a model in which coordinated glial, inflammatory, and BDNF-TrkB-dependent plasticity mechanisms – rather than isolated neurogenesis – drive treatment-related brain remodeling. These distributed structural and functional changes converge on limbic hubs embedded within the default mode and salience networks, align with emotion-related processes and co-localize with 5-HT1A receptor density, consistent with a normalization of pathological affective circuits. The clinical relevance of these adaptations is underscored by their selective associations with symptom improvement and their specificity to ECT. 

Altogether, these multimodal brain adaptations indicate that ECT engages widespread neuroplastic mechanisms rather than isolated regional effects. By integrating evidence across imaging modalities and biological scales, this framework reconciles prior inconsistencies from single-modality studies and underscores the value of integrative approaches for precision psychiatry. We anticipate that delineating the neurobiological mechanism of action of ECT will provide a blueprint to guide the development and refinement of therapeutic strategies targeting these molecular and network-level substrates in refractory psychiatric disorders.
