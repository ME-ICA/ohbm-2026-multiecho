Introduction

Magnetoencephalography (MEG) offers unique spatiotemporal resolution for understanding brain function, but this advantage has mostly been limited to studying adult cognition. Previous-generation systems required cryogenic cooling in bulky, rigid helmets sized for adult heads—far from infant-friendly. Optically pumped magnetometers (OPMs) have changed this, enabling lightweight sensors in flexible configurations that can be customised for smaller heads. This opens the door to developmental neuroscience, allowing us to capture brain dynamics in early infancy with unprecedented spatiotemporal resolution.

Recent studies have demonstrated the feasibility of OPM-MEG recordings in early infancy,¹⁻³, yet no commercial infant setups exist. As laboratories independently develop solutions, openly sharing implementation knowledge is key. Here, we contribute to this effort by providing a practical guide for establishing infant OPM-MEG research, sharing our setup specifications and protocols developed through iterative prototyping with several families. We present a proof-of-concept study demonstrating successful acquisition of auditory-evoked responses in 2-month-old infants, along with open-source analysis scripts.

Methods

Our process.                Our setup and protocols were developed through interdisciplinary collaboration between physicists and neuroscientists at the University of Birmingham, refined over three rounds of prototyping with researcher and parental feedback at each iteration.

Setup.              We designed a setup for supine recording in infants aged 0–4 months (Figure 1). A rigid prototype helmet (FieldLine Medical; 45 cm circumference; SLA resin; edges lined with 2 mm wool felt) was mounted on a custom MEG-compatible bed (82 × 54 × 93 cm, modelled on IKEA Gulliver). The helmet housed 50 rubidium-based OPM sensors (FieldLine Medical) distributed across the entire scalp, extending coverage beyond previous neonatal setups to include occipital sensors. A moveable platform with 3D-printed spoon-shaped head support and memory-foam mattress enabled precise positioning. A pillow placed around the infant's body and attached to the mattress increased comfort and minimised body movement. A small gap between scalp and helmet permitted air circulation. The system was housed in a magnetically shielded room (Mu Room Small, Cerca Magnetics; 2.2 m inner height) without active shielding. Auditory stimuli were delivered via a piezoelectric speaker (Sonitron, 84 dB) mounted at ~2 m. A ceiling-mounted webcam enabled behavioural monitoring.

Protocols.                    We developed protocols and openly available materials (screening forms, cheat sheets, recording logs) for recruitment, data acquisition, and feedback collection. Eligibility screening addressed infant factors (newborn hearing screening, developmental milestones) and MEG-specific criteria for both infant and caregiver (metal implants, claustrophobia). Pre-visit guidance on metal-free clothing minimised preparation time. Sessions involved two researchers: a session lead (with infant research experience) accompanying the family inside the shielded room throughout recording, and an OPM-MEG operator managing the system, video recording, stimulus delivery, and equipment monitoring (sensor on-time, helmet temperature). 

Validation paradigm.          Infants completed ~25-minute sessions comprising auditory oddball tasks and resting-state recordings. Pure tones (500/750 Hz; 100 ms; ~60 dB) were presented in 60-trial blocks with 75/25 frequent/infrequent ratio, ISI 500–600 ms (jittered), minimum 2 frequent tones between infrequent tones, and frequency roles counterbalanced across blocks. Breaks were offered between blocks.

Analysis pipeline.                    Preprocessing included bandpass filtering (0.1–40 Hz) and ICA for cardiac artifact removal. Notably, no EOG, ECG, or HPI coils were used to minimise attachments to the infant. Bad sensors were marked but not interpolated given low sensor density. Empty-room recordings were acquired after each session. Epochs (-100 to 600 ms) underwent manual artifact inspection. For ERF analysis, epochs were baseline-corrected (-100 to 0 ms) and trial numbers were equalised across conditions by random subsampling. For MVPA, we used sliding estimator decoding with a logistic regression classifier to discriminate frequent versus infrequent tones at each time point (-100 to 500 ms, no baseline correction). Performance was evaluated using area under the curve (AUC) with stratified 5-fold cross-validation. Discriminant activation patterns were extracted to visualise classification-relevant spatial activity 4. All code is openly available.

Results

Feasibility and family experience.       Parental feedback (n=7 respondents) confirmed high satisfaction: 100% rated pre-visit information as very clear, 100% rated infant comfort during positioning as very comfortable, 83% rated comfort during data acquisition as very comfortable, and 83% indicated they would be very likely to participate in future OPM-MEG studies.

Data quality.               7 infants have been tested using our setup and protocols (4 female; M=60 days, range: 48–83 days; all term-born). Of these, one participant was excluded due to irrecoverable noise in the recording. Cardiac artefacts displayed stereotyped topography across participants and were readily identifiable via ICA, enabling successful removal without requiring EOG or ECG recordings. Signal quality supported detection of evoked responses at the individual participant level.

Qualitative validation of neural signal acquisition.    We first examined event-related fields for characteristic patterns of auditory evoked responses. For all six participants included in the analysis, we observed temporal response morphologies and topographic distributions qualitatively consistent with auditory cortical sources (Figure 2A). However, because infants were positioned differently within the helmet, it was not possible at this stage to meaningfully average sensor data across participants or select consistent sensor locations for comparing frequent versus infrequent tones. To circumvent this limitation, we harnessed MVPA, which identifies the configuration of temporal and spatial features that best discriminates between conditions at the within-subject level, independent of absolute sensor positions 5. This analysis revealed that, across the group, decoding accuracy peaked at 234 ms post-stimulus—consistent with the expected latency range for infant mismatch responses (Figure 2B). For 5 of 6 participants, peak decoding exceeded the 0.5 chance level. Furthermore, for most participants, discriminant activation patterns at this latency exhibited dipolar topographies predominantly over temporal regions, consistent with auditory cortical generators of deviance detection.

Conclusion

We provide a comprehensive, openly available framework for establishing infant OPM-MEG research, comprising hardware setup specifications, protocols optimised through iterative processes incorporating researcher and family feedback, and an analysis pipeline. 

Our proof-of-concept data demonstrate that this framework enables the acquisition of interpretable neural signals in 2-month-old infants, including auditory-evoked responses with expected temporal dynamics and topographies, and multivariate discrimination of stimulus categories at latencies consistent with infant mismatch responses.

One important limitation should be noted: our validation dataset is small (n=6), precluding formal statistical inference. Instead, we relied on qualitative validation at the individual participant level. This approach is appropriate for a methods-focused proof-of-concept study, but future work with larger samples will be necessary to characterise effect sizes and establish statistical benchmarks.

Key methodological challenges remain for the field. Coregistration—aligning sensor positions with brain anatomy—is complicated by the absence of standardised fiducial landmarks in infants and the impracticality of acquiring individual MRIs: future work could leverage 3D photogrammetry combined with age-appropriate MRI templates . Source reconstruction requires infant-specific forward models accounting for differences in skull thickness, fontanelle presence, and tissue conductivity: emerging infant MRI atlases offer promising avenues. Head movement tracking is particularly challenging given that infants cannot follow instructions: optical tracking or video-based motion estimation could enable offline correction.

Despite these open challenges, our framework provides a foundation that can be adapted to different hardware configurations and age groups. We hope this work reduces barriers to entry and accelerates progress toward understanding the developing brain.
