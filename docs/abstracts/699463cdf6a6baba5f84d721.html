Introduction

Resting-state EEG has shown strong potential as a candidate biomarker for ALS. Source-level analyses further revealed disease-specific network changes, including increased theta-band amplitude envelope correlation and reduced beta-band imaginary coherence across motor and non-motor regions1, with alterations persisting longitudinally.

Temporal analyses of EEG microstates provide key insight into network dysfunction in ALS. While the four canonical microstates (A–D) are consistently observed, ALS patients show marked alterations in their temporal characteristics which correlate with motor and cognitive decline, indicating disruptions across both sensory and higher-order networks.2 Yet, current interpretations of microstate classes rely largely on paradigm-studies, without direct anatomical mapping.

Source localisation of microstates addresses this gap by linking rapid EEG topographies to their neural generators, offering a framework to interpret transient dynamics in terms of underlying brain anatomy. Although EEG–fMRI studies have related microstates to known resting-state networks, fMRI’s limited temporal resolution constrains its ability to capture microstate-scale dynamics. EEG-based inverse modelling preserves millisecond-level precision and enables estimation of microstate generators directly.3

In this study, we combined EEG source-level time courses with microstate sequences to identify the cortical regions underlying each microstate class in ALS and HC groups. By characterising disease-related alterations in microstate generators, we aim to clarify how spontaneous neural activity is reorganised in ALS and to advance source-level markers of disrupted neural signaling.

Methods

Ethical approval was obtained from the St. James’s Hospital / Tallaght University Hospital Joint Research Ethics Committee. Individuals with ALS or ALS-FTD were recruited from the Irish National ALS Clinic using revised El Escorial and Strong criteria; those with other motor neuron disorders, other medical conditions, or neuropsychiatric symptoms were excluded. Age-matched HC without neurological or psychiatric diagnoses were recruited from a volunteer database. 

High-density (128-channel) resting-state EEG was recorded from 99 ALS patients and 78 HC at the St. James’s Hospital Clinical Research Facility, Dublin, Ireland. Participants sat with eyes open and fixated on a central cross while three 2-min blocks were recorded using a BioSemi ActiveTwo system (512 Hz). Short breaks ensured alertness and comfort.

Preprocessing followed established procedures.1,2 Recordings were visually inspected and artefactual epochs were removed based on amplitude, mean shift, variance, and band-variance thresholds (3.5 Z-score). Signals were downsampled to 256 Hz, band-pass filtered (1–97 Hz) and notch-filtered at 50 Hz. Noisy channels were identified using PREP pipeline criteria and interpolated; sessions with >11 removed channels were excluded. A common average reference was applied.

Microstates were computed using the Microstate EEGlab toolbox. EEG topographies (1–30 Hz) at peaks of global field power, from ALS and HC groups combined, were clustered using polarity-invariant modified k-means into four canonical microstate classes (Figure 1). A Gaussian-weighted smoothing window (five samples) was applied, and each timepoint assigned to the best-matching prototype using global map dissimilarity.

In parallel with microstate computation, EEG sources were estimated using a linear constraint minimum variance beamformer and an ICBM152 template-based headmodel, yielding time courses for 90 brain regions. Because microstate labels and source signals share the same temporal scale, brain regions generating each microstate could be directly estimated. Resting-state generators were identified via statistical parametric mapping between microstate time courses and source activity.3 A general linear model (GLM)

 ηₘ = Χ·βₘ + ε

was fitted, where ηₘ is a binary indicator of microstate m and Χ contains regional source activity. No intercept was included. A first GLM on concatenated data produced weights β_all for each microstate; only FDR-corrected significant weights (q < .05) were retained, representing the regions contributing most to each microstate topography in the combined ALS-HC group.

Reproducibility was assessed by fitting the same GLM for each participant i to obtain βᵢ. Bootstrapping (n=1000) was performed by repeatedly sampling individual participants and Z-scoring contribution maps against the full cohort. A Contribution Stability Index (CSI) was computed by dividing β_all Z-scores by the standard deviation of the bootstrapped Z-scores. Steps above were repeated for ALS and HC separately, and for two HC subgroups (HC 1–40, HC 38–78).

Spatial similarity of microstate generators between groups and subgroups was evaluated using Spearman correlations (two-tailed, α=0.1). Significance was assessed using Moran spectral randomization Null models (10,000 permutations) to account for spatial autocorrelation, as implemented in the BrainSpace toolbox.4 The same Null framework was used to test group differences in microstate sources (one-tailed, α=0.1).

Results

The clustering algorithm revealed four optimal microstate prototypes for the HC and ALS groups. These four maps respectively explained on average 56 (±0.2) % of the global variance for the HC group and 54 (±0.2) % of the global variance for the ALS group. Similar to what is commonly described in the literature, the topographies are reliably observed across conditions.

Four sources generating the four canonical microstates were estimated by associating the microstates' timecourses with the brain sources' timecourses. The resulting Contribution Stability Indexes represent the level of reproducibility across participants. Only reproducibility scores associated with significant GLM coefficients of activation (q<.05) were kept. The index values above threshold (|z| >1) represent the most reproducible contributing brain areas during microstates.

We noticed a high degree of overlap between microstate sources, as observed in previous studies.3 The major areas of overlap were found in precuneus, frontotemporal regions and hippocampus. We then compared the microstate sources from two HC subgroups and from the ALS versus the HC group. Among HC subgroups, no significant spatial correlations were found between estimated microstate sources. A significant spatial correlation (r = 0.25, p=0.03) was detected between microstate class A in the ALS group and microstate class B in the HC group. Despite high similarities in microstate topographical maps between the groups, distinct microstate brain sources were identified in the ALS and HC groups. The major differences between ALS and HC microstate sources were located in the frontotemporal region, the left amygdala and the bilateral somatosensory motor network (Figure 2).

Conclusion

EEG microstates, reflecting transient topographies generated by phase-locked synchronised oscillations across distributed neural sources, differ in coverage, duration, and transition profiles in ALS and relate to motor and cognitive decline.2 Understanding how these altered dynamics map onto underlying neural generators is essential for interpreting disease-related disruptions in large-scale brain networks. Previous EEG–fMRI studies linked canonical microstates to auditory, visual, salience, and attention networks, but their limited temporal resolution constrains interpretation. Here, EEG-based source localisation enabled a more direct characterisation of the generators underlying microstates in ALS versus HC.

Across all participants, distinct source configurations were observed for microstates A–D, partially consistent with prior findings.3 Microstate A involved temporal and occipital regions; microstate B showed contributions from occipital and cingulate areas, consistent with visual network involvement; microstates C and D engaged bilateral fronto-parietal regions, aligning with salience/default-mode and attention networks, respectively. 

However, overlap across microstate generators, reported widely in the literature3, underscores the difficulty of attributing each microstate to a single, isolated network. Microstates are not temporally independent, and correlations between their time courses likely contribute to this overlap. Limited reproducibility between HC subgroups suggests difficulty in dissociating microstate sources, a finding likely exacerbated by the small subgroup sizes. Despite this variability, certain hub-like regions consistently contributed across microstates, including the precuneus, frontal cortices, and hippocampus, structures with dense structural and functional connectivity networks. Such recurrent engagement supports the view that microstates reflect dynamic transitions within broadly distributed, multifunctional cortical systems rather than isolated modules.3

Although ALS and HC showed highly similar microstate topographies, the underlying neural generators differed. Notably, microstate A sources in ALS were more strongly correlated with microstate B sources than with the corresponding class, indicating a reorganisation of the cortical systems supporting otherwise similar scalp topographies. In HC, microstate A relied more on temporal pole and occipital regions, whereas in ALS, motor regions contributed more strongly. This shift may explain the cross-class correlation between ALS microstate A and HC microstate B, the latter showing stronger sensorimotor involvement.

The ALS group exhibited increased contributions from bilateral somatosensory cortex across several microstates (A, B, D). These microstates were previously shown to have altered temporal characteristics in ALS2, and increased source-level involvement may reflect compensatory or disinhibited activity. Despite structural atrophy in somatosensory regions, functional imaging studies have demonstrated increased connectivity and heightened excitability in ALS, consistent with loss of inhibitory regulation or compensatory recruitment.5

The finding that similar scalp patterns arise from different underlying generators in ALS raises the possibility that altered regional participation disrupts the coordination of large-scale networks, even when topographies appear intact. These results point toward a fundamental reorganisation of resting-state network dynamics in ALS and highlight the value of source-level analysis for identifying disease-specific changes. Future work should investigate whether direct analysis of source-reconstructed signals improves sensitivity to neurodegenerative network dysfunction.
