Introduction

Immune dysregulation-related neuroinflammation is thought to play a role in the pathophysiology of psychosis1, but its functional consequences remain to be fully determined. Extracellular free water (FW), which can be estimated using free water mapping, has been proposed as a proxy measure of neuroinflammation2. Whole brain, white matter (WM), and gray matter (GM) FW have been found to be higher in individuals with psychosis compared to healthy controls3. Moreover, recently, high gray matter FW was shown to be associated with better treatment outcome in early psychosis. Psychosis is also considered to be a disorder of brain activity and connectivity, and many resting-state functional imaging studies have reported aberrant brain activity and connectivity in individuals with psychosis compared to controls, including studies on intrinsic brain activity as measured by fractional amplitude of low frequency fluctuations (fALFF4). To advance our understanding of the functional consequences of neuroinflammation in psychosis, this study compared GM and WM FW between individuals with early psychosis and healthy controls, and examined their relationships with psychotic symptoms and fALFF in the Human Connectome Early Psychosis sample. We hypothesized higher GM and WM FW, lower fALFF in individuals with psychosis, and a negative association between FW and fALFF.

Methods

This study analyzed MRI and clinical data from 118 individuals with early psychosis [EP: 28 affective (primary mood disorder with psychotic features), 90 non-affective (primary psychotic disorder based on SCID-5-RV5; 45 women, mean age (SD) = 22.7 (3.6)] and 53 healthy controls [HC: 18 women, mean age (SD) = 22.7 (3.6)], collected by the HCP-EP (release 1.1, August 19, 2021). Diffusion MRI data was preprocessed using preprocessing and analysis of q-space images (QSIprep6), which also generates GM and WM masks. FW was estimated using a Diffusion Imaging In Python (DIPY7) pipeline. fALFF was estimated using ENIGMA (Enhancing Neuro Imaging Genetics through Meta-Analysis) HALFpipe8, which generates z-scored fALFF (zfALFF) images by normalizing fALFF images by each individual subject’s whole brain fALFF mean and standard deviation. Both FW and fALFF measures were averaged within the GM and WM masks for each subject. Positive and negative symptom severity were based on the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS9 sum of items 1 to 7, and 8 to 14, respectively). ANCOVAs tested for effects of diagnosis (early psychosis, healthy control) on FW (GM & WM) and fALFF with age, sex, and site included as covariates. Kendall partial correlations examined relationships between FW (GM & WM) and PANSS positive symptoms, negative symptoms, and fALFF (GM & WM). Threshold-free cluster enhancement (TCFE10) analyses (p<0.05, 5,000 permutations) examined group differences (EP vs. HC) in fALFF, and relationships between FW (GM & WM) and voxelwise fALFF.

Results

Both GM FW (t=2.17, p=0.032) and WM FW (t=2.46, p=0.015) were higher in individuals with early psychosis compared to healthy controls. WM FW showed a significant positive correlation with positive [r=0.14, p=0.031] but not negative psychotic symptoms.

Individuals with EP showed significantly lower regional fractional fALFF compared to healthy controls (TCFE, p<0.05). The most prominent areas with lower fALFF were in posterior cortical areas, including bilateral precuneus/cuneal cortex (clusters 28, 29, 30, 31), postcentral/precentral gyrus (clusters 19, 20, 21, 22, 26, 28, 29), and lateral occipital cortex (clusters 25, 27, 30, 31). Lower fALFF was also found in supracalcarine / intracalcarine cortex (clusters 16, 18, 23, 24) and superior parietal regions (clusters 17, 19).

Both GM FW (t=-7.27, p<0.001) and WM FW (t=-6.86, p<0.0001) showed significant negative associations with fALFF (p<0.0001). Voxelwise analyses of the relationships between GM FW and WM FW and fALFF showed key regions involved in psychosis including the middle frontal gyrus / dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, superior frontal gyrus, anterior cingulate cortex, and superior parietal lobe (TCFE, p<0.05).

Conclusion

This study replicates prior findings of higher GM and WM FW in early psychosis based on high-quality HCP-EP diffusion MRI data and found a positive relationship between WM FW and positive symptoms that corroborates an earlier finding in chronic psychosis. The study also replicates findings of lower fALFF in psychosis compared to healthy controls. Critically, it  found significant regional associations of GM FW and WM FW with spontaneous brain activity -here measured with fALFF. These findings suggests possible new avenues for the treatment of the functional consequences of neuroinflammation in psychosis using neuromodulation.
