Introduction

In light of demographic changes, promoting cognitive health in older age is pivotal for independent living and quality of life [1]. To this end, interest in identifying markers for age-related cognitive decline is growing, since early identification of cognitive decline is key to developing successful prevention strategies. In this regard, machine learning (ML) tools are becoming increasingly important, as they can identify patterns in high-dimensional spaces and perform analyses at an individual level. However, so far ML results based on brain metrics have not yet been successful [2]. At the same time, other, non-brain factors such as demographics have been reported to better predict cognitive functioning in both younger and older adults. Recently, the Lancet Commission on Dementia emphasised the importance of multiple risk factors in aging and cognitive dysfunction, i.e. in dementia cases, which typically emerge at different stages of life, e.g. education in childhood and hypertension in middle age [1]. These findings highlight the importance of conducting age-specific predictive analyses and systematically comparing the predictability of cognitive performance across different age groups, which requires large sample sizes. The current study investigated the predictability of cognitive test scores using MRI-derived structural brain data, health-related variables, demographics, and cognitive performance across the lifespan (age range: 25–74) in a large cross-sectional sample (n = 21,877) from the population-based German National Cohort (NAKO Gesundheitsstudie, NAKO [3,4]) using ML. Specifically, the study aimed to systematically compare ML outcomes across the following categories: 1) Age decades (total sample vs. age decades), 2) distinct modalities (structural brain data, health, demographics, cognition) and 3) different cognitive tests (executive functions, language, motor speed, episodic and working memory).

Methods

Data for the current analyses were derived from NAKO, which collected data across 18 different study centres in Germany, with five dedicated MR imaging sites (Augsburg, Berlin, Essen, Mannheim & Neubrandenburg), each equipped with the same MR scanner (3 Tesla, MAGNETOM Skyra, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) and using the same scanning protocol [4]. A final sample of 21,877 participants (44 % females, Mage= 48.9, SDage = 11.4) was stratified into five age decades (25-34 years; 35-44 years; 45-54 years; 55-64 years; 65-74 years). Structural brain data was captured at the whole-brain level in brain summary statistics, including total 1) cortical GMV, 2) subcortical grey matter volume, 3) cerebellar grey matter volume (left and right), 4) total cerebral white matter volume, 5) estimated total intracranial volume together with 6) white matter hyperintensity volume. Health-related data included lifestyle behaviour as well as medical conditions: a) body mass index, b) smoking status, c) hypertension, d) intake of antihypertensive medication, e) intake of cholesterol reducing medication, f) diabetes diagnosis, g) increased blood lipids, h) alcohol consumption, i) left hand grip strength and j) right hand grip strength. Demographic data included i) age, ii) sex, iii) educational level, iv) socioeconomic status, and v) employment status. Cognitive performance tests [5] included 1) a semantic fluency test [number of correctly named animals within one minute], 2) a verbal episodic memory test [Immediate Recall: sum of words remembered from a word list (12 items) in two trials; Delayed Recall: sum of words remembered of a word list in delayed recall], 3) an executive function test [Stroop; Time difference between naming ink colour of words with incongruent colour meaning and naming of colour of differently coloured boxes (Time T3 - Time T2)], 4) a verbal working memory test [Digit Span; longest sequence of digits correctly repeated backwards] and 5) a motor speed test [Purdue Pegboard Test; number of correctly placed pin pairs in 30 seconds]. A ML approach was chosen to investigate the predictability of cognitive test performance from brain structural, health-related, demographic and cognitive data across different age decades. ML performance estimations were compared across four different algorithms, i.e. Ridge regression, linear Support Vector Regression (linSVR), Elastic Net (EN) regression and Random Forest (RF) regression, which are commonly employed in ML neuroimaging studies. In turn, ML model performance was evaluated using a repeated nested 10-fold cross-validation (10 repeats). Hyperparameter optimization was performed in the inner cross-validation to avoid data leakage (5-fold CV). For performance estimation coefficient of determination (R2) was calculated. To assess the contribution of each input feature to the prediction, permutation feature importances were calculated on the testing dataset, for all targets and algorithms in each sample.

Results

Across all cognitive tests, input features and algorithms used, best prediction performance was found in the total sample. Here prediction levels reached up to R2 = 0.32 and explained up to 10 % more variance in the total sample compared to the different age decades (Total: R2 range: 0.02-0.32, Decades 1-5: R2 range: -0.03-0.22). This effect was found particularly for executive functions, immediate and delayed episodic memory (Total: R2 range: 0.06-0.32, Decades 1-5: R2 range: -0.01-0.22). In general, immediate episodic memory was best predicted (R2 range: 0-0.32), while motor speed and verbal working memory showed the lowest predictability (R2 range: -0.03-0.18). Furthermore, we found lower prediction performance for executive functions and motor speed in the oldest age group compared to the other age decades (Decade 5; R2 range: -0.01-0.08, Decades 1-4: R2 range: 0-0.14). ML outcomes for delayed episodic memory performance and semantic fluency, in turn, were found to be lower in the youngest participants (Decade 1: R2 range: 0-0.14) compared to all others (Decades 2-5: R2 range: 0-0.18). 

In addition to this, we revealed highest predictability from demographic and cognitive data (R2 range: 0.02-0.24) outperforming brain structural and health-related data (R2 range: -0.03-0.09). Addressing the combination of different input features modalities (i.e. brain structural, health-related, demographic, or cognitive data) revealed an additional benefit, particularly combining demographic and cognitive data as input features to predict immediate and delayed episodic memory (Unimodal: R2 range: 0-0.24, Multimodal: R2 range: 0.02-0.32). Finally, zooming into the single input features revealed age to be most relevant in the prediction of executive functions, immediate and delayed episodic memory, but only in the total sample. For all other decades and cognitive tests, age was found to be of less importance. Across decades, episodic memory performance appeared to be particularly important in the prediction of semantic fluency performance and vice versa. The combination of sex, body mass index and smoking status was found exclusively among the top ranked features for motor speed. Educational level and socioeconomic status were found to be relevant particularly for performance in semantic fluency, verbal working memory, and episodic memory. Cortical and subcortical grey matter volume appeared to be partially important for predicting performance in semantic fluency, motor speed and verbal working memory.

Conclusion

The results of the present study revealed systematic divergences in prediction performance across the adult lifespan (age range: 25–74), with variability observed across age groups, modalities, methodological approaches, and cognitive outcome measures. Overall, the total sample yielded higher predictive accuracy compared with analyses restricted to individual age decades. Age constituted the most influential predictor variable, consistent with prior research encompassing the entire adult lifespan. However, this effect diminished when examined within narrower age strata. Although predictive performance was largely comparable across age groups, slight discrepancies emerged between the youngest and oldest cohorts. These differences may reflect cohort-specific influences and correspond with the age-dependent susceptibility to lifestyle risk factors reported by Livingston et al.[1]. Moreover, the findings substantiate the assumption that demographic and cognitive variables possess superior predictive utility relative to brain- and health-related measures. This pattern aligns with an increasing body of literature demonstrating the substantial explanatory power of demographic characteristics for individual differences in cognitive performance. Beyond demographic factors, additional cognitive indicators were identified as conveying essential information for predicting specific cognitive test scores, likely due to their inherent interdependencies, as documented in previous research. For instance, reciprocal predictive associations have been observed between executive functions and semantic fluency.

The present results further reinforce earlier evidence suggesting that associations between structural brain metrics and cognitive performance across the lifespan are negligible. This implies that the coupling between brain structure and behavioural outcomes may be relatively modest and shaped by complex, multidimensional mechanisms. Consequently, further empirical work is required to elucidate these intricate interactions and to refine theoretical models of brain-behaviour relationships.

In conclusion, the findings underscore the prominent role of demographic and cognitive variables in predicting cognitive performance, while simultaneously highlighting the limitations of the examined brain- and health-related measures. The combined investigation of the entire adult lifespan in conjunction with age-specific subsamples represents a promising avenue for future prediction research. Such an approach may enable the identification of previously undetected developmental or cohort-specific patterns and contribute to a more nuanced understanding of the determinants of cognitive trajectories across adulthood.
