Introduction

Understanding how the brain’s cortical organisation enables cognition remains a central challenge for neuroscience. Two key aspects of this question are 1) “why are cognitive networks anatomically-located where they are?” and 2) “how are the computations required to solve cognitive tasks distributed across brain networks?”. 

Two modern approaches to neural modelling each excel in identifying either the computations1 or the spatial activity patterns underlying cognition2, respectively. Current recurrent neural network (RNN) models can capture the compositional nature of computation during the performance of higher cognitive tasks1. However, they largely overlook anatomy, limiting their comparability with experiments. Cortex-wide dynamical models increasingly integrate anatomical features including connectivity, dendritic spines and receptors, but are limited to simple cognitive tasks2. Here, we introduce Cortically-Embedded Recurrent Neural Networks (CERNNs), which embed artificial neural networks into a species-specific cortical space, enabling the incorporation of anatomical constraints and facilitating direct comparisons of models to cortex-wide neural connectivity, activity, dynamics and behavior during complex cognitive tasks. 

By analysing many iterations of CERNNs trained on 26 cognitive tasks, and testing multiple brain-inspired objectives (e.g. energy minimization, wiring cost minimization, macroscopic gradient alignment), we generate testable hypotheses as to why distributed cognitive networks arise, and how they implement complex cognition.

Methods

We trained CERNNs with human or macaque cortical geometry to perform multiple visual and somatosensory cognitive tasks (e.g. working memory, response inhibition, decision-making). Additionally, on some training runs we added brain-inspired terms to the objective function, including penalising metabolic cost (firing rates), wiring cost (long-distance connections), deviations from macroscopic gradients and other brain-inspired costs. Human and macaque CERNNs shared identical architectures and objectives, differing only in cortical geometry and anatomical gradients. 

Model architecture

CERNN integrates RNNs with cortex-wide neuroscience data through four key brain-inspired constraints (Figure 1a,b):

1. Leaky RNN units (neural populations) are embedded at locations defined by the cortical geometry

2. Inputs target only primary sensory units

3. Outputs are read from primary motor units

4. Deviations from selected biological constraints are penalized during training via additional loss terms. 

This set-up forces the network to deal with brain-like problems that are alien to typical RNNs, such as the propagation of sensory activity across the cortex to the primary motor cortex in addition to solving the cognitive aspects of the task. These constraints are therefore likely to significantly alter the RNN dynamics. 

Anatomical wiring cost penalty

Unit locations derive from group-average macaque3 and human cortical geometry. Units are evenly spaced geodesically across the cortical surface in both species. We calculated the geodesic distances between pairs of units along the cortical surface to obtain the distance matrix D, and penalized the use of long-distance connections. 

Integration of dendritic spine count gradients in the macaque and human

Dendritic spines index excitatory synapses and increase along cortical hierarchies. We used macaque spine counts (27 regions) and inferred remaining macaque/human values from the MRI T1w/T2w signal, which inversely correlates with spines. In CERNNs, we model the spine count as proportional to the total strength of the incoming weights for each unit, and penalize deviations from the empirical spine count gradient. 

Training CERNNs to solve cognitive tasks

We trained CERNNs with backpropagation-through-time to minimize total loss L, which contained Ltask and the other brain-inspired losses (Figure 1c). Here we are interested in the constraints on biological activity during cognition (e.g. terms in the Loss function), but do not attempt to model biologically-realistic learning.

Results

Across species and tasks, CERNNs trained under anatomical and metabolic constraints spontaneously developed connectivity and dynamics matching several features of empirical cortical organization.

Behavior

Individual CERNNs learned to perform all 26 tasks with 95% accuracy or above.

Comparison with macaque connectivity data

We compared learned macaque CERNN weights compared to retrograde tract-tracing data from Henry Kennedy’s lab4, specifically the 40-area subgraph with complete bidirectional weights. In networks that penalized long-distance connections with an L2-style regularizer, and penalized deviations from the spine count, we observed  an exponential decay of connectivity strength with distance (in both macaque and human models). We also observed that the learned connectivity matrices of networks based on human cortical geometry are sparser than macaque, consistent with comparative anatomy (Fig 2a,b). Strong recurrent connections were observed among prefrontal and parietal units in both species’ models. Feedback connections in the models (e.g. top-down from associative to sensory areas, defined based on the fraction of supragranular labeled neurons in the tract-tracing data) were more likely to be inhibitory compared to bottom-up connections (Fig 2d), consistent with recent electrophysiological and neuroimaging reports. 

Emergent specialization of cognitive networks

We analysed CERNN on multisensory context-dependent delayed decision-making (two visual, two somatosensory inputs), the most challenging task in the battery. For analysis, units were grouped into canonical brain networks5. Across human/macaque embeddings and both task variants, we observe distributed dynamics across cognitive networks (Fig 2e,f).

CERNN sensory networks consistently display stimulus-locked transient activity, indicating sensory encoding (Fig 2f). The somatomotor (including premotor/motor cortex) activity consistently ramps from the time of the second stimulus (when the decision can be made) and is maintained through the second delay until the 'go' period, consistent with a motor preparation signal (Fig 2f). Lesioning the dorsal attention network and frontoparietal network but not other associative networks causes severe loss of performance in the network shown in Figure 2c. Nonetheless, the precise dynamics in associative networks varies across network instances. We observed dynamics consistent with working memory, decision-making, and motor preparation in the FPN and DAN in significant subsets of trials. This variability of dynamics may indicate that additional aspects of anatomy should be integrated to constrain the roles of higher cognitive networks. However, this may also reflect the natural variability in the spatial location of these networks across individuals. 

Conclusion

The CERNN framework can be extended to other species and integrate further transcriptomic and anatomical data. These anatomically grounded models generate testable predictions for cortex-wide imaging, recordings and perturbations, providing a bridge between computational, cognitive and systems neuroscience. These results suggest that distributed cognitive networks may arise naturally as the brain attempts to solve complex tasks under wiring constraints with systematic gradients of single neuron properties. More broadly, CERNNs constitute a framework by which artificial neural networks can be integrated with cortex-wide neuroscience data to produce anatomically-specific testable hypotheses across species.
