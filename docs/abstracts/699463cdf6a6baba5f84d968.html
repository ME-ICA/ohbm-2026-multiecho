Introduction

 Physiological states (e.g., sympathetic arousal under a departure from thermoregulatory optimum, or conversely parasympathetic engagement under a return to such optimum) are emergent properties of shifting connectivity configurations. Transient reconfigurations of functional connectivity (including both correlations and anti-correlations) allow the central nervous system to flexibly allocate resources depending on behavioral and physiological context. What features distinguish sympathetic and parasympathetic functional network configurations? To answer this question, we applied machine-learning based classification to zero lag functional connectivity data estimated from in vivo fMRI time series. The fMRI data were co-acquired while participants were subjected to a whole-body thermoregulatory challenge1.fMRI time series extracted from 246 cortical (Brainnetome2) and 16 brainstem (Harvard AAN3) regions were separated into epochs of sympathetic and parasympathetic response (based on concurrently acquired skin temperature vectors). For each subject and each condition functional connectivity matrices were constructed using zero-lag correlation and were Fisher’s-z transformed before being submitted for linear support vector machine (SVM)-based classification. We used an ensemble approach with cross-validation (CV) and label shuffling to robustness against overfitting. Results revealed that the majority of the most salient features for classification were the connectivity of inter- and intra-brainstem and subcortical pathways. The evoked connectivity of these region pairs were more correlated during parasympathetic than during sympathetic conditions. 

Methods

The Human Investigation Committee of Wayne State University authorized all study procedures. Consent was obtained from 61 adults (mean age: 25.1 + 3.4 years, age range: 20 - 31 years). Body mass index (BMI) and body fat % were in the normal range (BMI = 22.7 + 2.1 kg/m2, body fat % = 24.0 + 4.3%), subjects were unmedicated and free of neurological or psychiatric disorder. fMRI was acquired using a 3T Siemens Verio with a 12-channel volume head coil (TR: 2.6s, TE: 29ms, FOV: 256x256mm2, acquisition matrix: 128x128, 36 axial slices, voxel dimensions: 2 x 2 x 3mm3). During fMRI we used an oscillating thermal challenge; subjects wore a specialized whole-body garment through which they were exposed to cold or neutral temperature water. Body temperature was monitored using an MRI-compatible GaAs crystal sensor located at the tip of an optical fiber cable (temperature was recorded at 30 s intervals). The paradigm consisted of two 5-minute cooling periods interspersed within warming periods (5 min) that allowed the system to return to homeostatic baseline (25 minutes). 

MR images were preprocessed using the FSL pipeline (Version 6.0.7.18). From each subject’s functional scan, and for each image, we calculated the raw average fMRI signal in each of 246 cortical regions of the Brainnetome atlas2 and the 16 regions in the brainstem 3. Next, time series were formed from extracting the BOLD data from each of the 262 regions. Onset vectors (from changes in body temperature) were used to identify time intervals indicating Sympathetic (body temperature falling) and Parasympathetic (body temperature rising) conditions. We estimated the full functional connectivity matrix (34,191 unique elements) in each condition using zero-lag correlations [transformed (Fisher’s z) to stabilize the variance and normalize the feature distribution]. 

The vectorized upper triangle of the FC matrices were classified using linear kernel support vector machines (SVM) which allow for interpretation of the feature weight vector post hoc4 (see Figure 1). This weight vector assigns a weight to each feature in the dataset, where a larger weight magnitude reflects greater feature importance4. Feature weights assume salience because they can allow identification of features that have the largest impact on the SVM. We randomly selected 90% of the samples for SVM training. We then applied a StandardScaler to the connectivity features using the mean and standard deviation from the training set. 

We found an optimal SVM (high accuracy without overfitting) by training and scoring SVMs with a wide range of hyper-parameters. Each candidate SVM was evaluated using 10-fold cross-validation to obtain a robust estimate of generalization performance while maintaining computational efficiency. To check whether classification performance exceeded chance, we implemented a null check where each candidate SVM was retrained on data based on shuffled temporal labels (10 independent iterations). For each of the 10 independent iterations, we again performed 10-fold CV. After candidate SVMs were scored, we saved the hyper-parameters from the SVM with the highest average CV accuracy that also passed the null check. These hyper-parameters were used to generate 100,000 SVM’s with different random states, that were then submitted to CV and a null check. This ensemble method follows the general bootstrap aggregation strategy which increases stability of feature selection. We saved the weight vector from models that passed the null check and with a cross-validation accuracy greater than or equal to 70%. Next, we ran a conservative one sample t-test (p<.05, Bonferroni corrected) to eliminate non-significant features across all the weight vectors. The remaining features were averaged and sorted by weight to determine their relevance for classification. We next parsed 

the significant features into classes based on the sign of the weight, and the average sign of the feature in each condition.

Results

The best performing hyperparameter's yielded a 69.23% average CV accuracy. After bootstrapping we retained the weight vectors from models which met or exceeded the 69.72% accuracy (the closest to 70% for our sample size of 109) and the 6,530 SVMs had an average CV accuracy of 69.75%. The t-test (p< 0.05, Bonferroni corrected) revealed that nearly all (>99.5%) of features had significantly nonzero weight distributions. This motivated an analysis of the magnitude of the mean weight for each feature. The difference in magnitude of feature weight between the top-weighted and the least-weighted feature in the 99th percentile (0.0081) was similar to the difference between the feature at the 99th percentile threshold and the feature at the 50th percentile threshold (0.0085). This indicates that the weight distribution was heavily skewed and that the 99th percentile constitutes a substantial proportion of the feature weight distribution. As such, it was determined that the features of greatest significance to our model were those in the 99th percentile by weight and that these features warranted further investigation. To achieve this, we split the significant features into four types. Type I: FC was positive (i.e., correlated) in both conditions and greater during parasympathetic response (Quadrant I). Type II: FC was positive (correlated) in both conditions and greater during sympathetic response (Quadrant II). Type III: FC was negative (i.e., anti-correlated) during both conditions and greater during sympathetic response (Quadrant III). Type IV: FC was negative during both conditions and greater during parasympathetic response (Quadrant IV). These four types accounted for ~85% of the significant features, and a majority of significant features were Type I (~57%). Furthermore, the majority of these Type I features involve pathways between regions in sub-cortical nuclei and brainstem. Finally, an increase in positive FC during parasympathetic responses (Type I and Type IV) accounted for ~68% of significant features. 

Figure 2 provides an exposition of the lobe and regional contributions. The bar graphs depict the normalized lobe frequencies (i.e. the ratio of significant features with at least one region in the lobe to all possible features with at least one region in the lobe). As seen, Type I (upper right quadrant) has the highest normalized frequencies of regions in the brainstem (2.17%) and subcortical nuclei (1.52%) confirming that the SVM associated greater correlations in these regions during the para-sympathetic condition. The most represented regions, highlighted in the glass brains, are the ventral tegmental area (VTA) and the left hemisphere ventromedial putamen, which appear 32 and 31 times, respectively, in the set of Type I features.

Conclusion

Our goal was to determine features that discriminated network configurations evoked by sympathetic or parasympathetic thermoregulatory responses. Our results reveal that a high proportion of these significant features (connectivity between region pairs) were those with a positive FC in both conditions and greater functional connectivity during the parasympathetic as opposed to the sympathetic condition. Moreover, regions in the brainstem and subcortical nuclei were notably represented in these effects. Our work highlights a) the highly flexible nature of network configurations during physiological challenge, and b) the sensitivity of ML-based classification to identify the features that most saliently distinguish between these configurations.
