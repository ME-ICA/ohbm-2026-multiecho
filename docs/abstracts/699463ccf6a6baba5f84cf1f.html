Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is an immune-mediated disease of the central nervous system (CNS) that affects over 2.8 million people worldwide1. Despite the more recent availability of high-efficacy disease-modifying therapies, individualized treatment approaches remain challenged by the heterogeneity of clinical presentations in people with MS (PwMS) and a lack of accurate biomarkers to predict individual disease progression over time.2 Conventional magnetic resonance imaging (cMRI) has played an increasingly prominent role in MS diagnostic criteria3 over successive iterations due to its ability to reveal the volume, spatiotemporal distribution, and types (e.g., gray vs. white matter, activity) of CNS lesions. Concurrently, research has shown that several quantitative MRI (qMRI) methods offer improved sensitivity and/or specificity to various aspects of tissue microstructure that allow quantification of subtle damage in the white matter lesions (WMLs) characteristic of MS, and in normal appearing white matter (NAWM)4. Moreover, driven by their ability to handle large, complex datasets and identify non-linear patterns that traditional statistical models might miss, the application of predictive deep learning models to forecast trends in medical imaging datasets has expanded. Our aim was to employ a convolutional neural network deep learning architecture (specifically a U-Net) to predict the evolution of person-specific MS WMLs over two years using baseline cMRI or multi-modal qMRI data.

Methods

We included 35 participants with MS from the CCOMS Study cohort with baseline and 2-year follow-up data5. cMRI images (baseline T1-weighted, T1-w and T2-FLAIR), qMRI maps (baseline DTI-derived axial diffusivity, radial diffusivity, mean diffusivity, and fractional anisotropy; myelin-water-imaging-derived myelin water fraction, intra- and extra-cellular water fraction, geometric mean T2; T1-weighted/T2-weighted ratio mapping; multiecho gradient echo based R2*, and quantitative susceptibility mapping), binary global WM mask (baseline), and binary WML masks (at baseline and 2-year follow-up) were pre-processed and nonlinearly normalized to MNI152 template space5.

Two U-Net models were trained: one with each participants’ baseline WM masks and all ten baseline qMRI maps, and another with each participants’ baseline WM masks and both baseline cMRI images, following intensity normalization wherein each participant’s T1-w and T2-FLAIR images were rescaled between 0-1. Both sets of MRI inputs (Figure 1A) were then cropped by the baseline WM mask to narrow the field of view and concatenated with the baseline WM and WML masks in the fourth dimension. The model output (Figure 1B) was a disease evolution map (DEM) with 4 classes defined by subtracting the follow-up WML mask from the baseline WML mask (i.e., “shrinking” (voxels = -1), “stable” (voxels = 0), “growing” (voxels = +1), and background (everywhere else within WM) regions). The model architecture (Figure 1C) was a basic 3D U-Net initialized by the function ‘unet3d’ in MATLAB (R2024b) with the following configurations: encoder depth of 3, 64 filters in the first convolution layer, iso-filter size of 3, and the option “same” for the convolution padding. We employed the Adam-W optimizer to yield more consistent regularization and better generalization. Despite using a quad-GPU workstation (4 x NVIDIA RTX8000 GPUs with NVLink interconnects), the entire 4D volume needed to be subsampled into 4D blocks with each spatial dimension specified as 32 for training; and, to mitigate potential biases due to coverage and/or edge effects caused by idiosyncratic subsampling, resampled blocks with 50% volume overlap between adjacent blocks were added to the training dataset.  All 4 GPUs were run simultaneously, but with a deterministic allocation algorithm to enable result replication and controlled evaluation across models. The key hyperparameters were chosen for all model training as follows: mini batch size of 128, learning rate of 5e-3, maximum epoch of 20 (with data shuffled at each epoch for each worker), weight decay factor of 0.01, gradient decay factor of 0.9, squared gradient decay of 0.999, and epsilon of 1e-8. The objective function was set to minimize the 4-class cross-entropy between the subsampled 4D ground truth DEM and predicted DEM blocks (which were ultimately reassembled into a whole brain DEM by placing each 4D block back into its original position).

Models were evaluated (internally) using the leave-one-out cross-validation (at the participant level, N=35). During every iteration, the overall Dice similarity coefficients (DSCs) of each model (OverallqMRI and OverallcMRI) were computed for each hold-out individuals’ between the predicted follow-up WML mask and ground truth (wholistically accounting for shrinking, stable, and growing WML regions). However, as a global control and to quantify how much change there actually was, we also computed DSCs between each participants’ baseline and follow-up WML mask (Real Change). OverallqMRI and OverallcMRI were then compared to Real Change using one-tailed (assuming models would learn at least some temporal changes), Wilcoxon signed-rank tests paired for each individual; and then compared to each other using a two-tailed Wilcoxon signed-rank test. To elucidate how overall performance was achieved, regional DSCs of shrinking, stable, and growing regions of each participant’s WML mask were assessed within and across models.

Results

The oversampling method yielded a total of 18,710 4D blocks across the entire 35-person dataset. As shown in Figure 2A, the qMRI model (DSC median [IQR]: 0.72 [0.60,0.80]) and cMRI model (0.73 [0.64,0.79]) were able to learn spatiotemporal changes in MS WML maps, with both models significantly outperforming the DSCs of baseline WML masks (0.65 [0.55,0.74], OverallqMRI p-value = 3e-6; OverallcMRI p-value = 2e-6). However, the overall predictive performances of the qMRI and cMRI models were not significantly different from each other (two-tailed, p = 0.36). In Figure 2B, when qualitatively comparing the class-specific DSCs of the three different region classes, there was consistency between the two models. Stable WML regions had the best results (qMRI model = 0.86 [0.79,0.89]; cMRI model = 0.86 [0.80,0.89]), followed by shrinking WML regions (qMRI model = 0.65 [0.59,0.73]; cMRI model = 0.66 [0.61,0.72]), and growing WML regions had the lowest DSCs (qMRI model = 0.40 [0.29,0.48]; cMRI model = 0.44 [0.34,0.51]). These trends were also apparent when comparing the actual and predicted 3D disease evolution maps (DEMs), which consistently showed high correspondence (Figure 2C). 

Conclusion

Using a relatively simple U-Net architecture with baseline MRI inputs has shown the potential to predict WML evolution over two years (Figure 2C). The fact that both models consistently produced higher DSCs than baseline WML masks indicates future clinical potential in predicting spatiotemporal changes in WMLs over the course of months-to-years and could (along with other clinical measures) be used to guide individualized treatment plans. 

Notably, the positive predictive results from both models were largely driven by exceptionally high DSCs among stable WML regions, with generally medium (though occasionally high) predictive accuracy among shrinking WML regions, and relatively modest predictive accuracy among growing WML regions (as shown by the lower median). Although there may be inherent limitations explaining this finding (e.g., known regional MRI insensitivity variations in NAWM, which our model was poorly equipped to learn due to the need to segment into smaller 4D blocks), future efforts should be made to improve the predictive performance of growing WML areas and assess the model's generalizability using an external dataset.  

Perhaps somewhat surprisingly, the predictive performance between the qMRI and cMRI models was not significantly different. From the perspective of practical application to clinical practice, this finding is relevant since T1w and T2-FLAIR images are routinely acquired for patient diagnosis and treatment monitoring. Even assurances of the prediction of lesion stability with a high degree of accuracy through the use of these methods on baseline MRI characteristics being routinely available, without the need for additional advanced imaging methods, is of value in clinical practice. With widely available (at least) cMRI images with larger sample sizes and potentially more time frames, this result has clearly demonstrated the scalability of future studies. However, more work is needed to improve the predictive performance of growing WML areas since prediction of lesion growth at the individual level with a high degree of accuracy will set the stage for trials of individualized approaches to optimized treatment strategies for PwMS. Whether achieving this goal can be achieved through the use of much larger data sets with cMRI measures or will require use of other qMRI methods (or both), remains unclear. Ideally more powerful high-performance computing clusters would enable the models to be trained on whole-brain MRI data (to account for expected intensity differences across different brain regions).

Overall, our findings demonstrate the potential for baseline MRI data to predict the evolution of MS white matter lesions, which in turn aid MS prognosis and guide individualized therapeutic approaches for improved patient outcomes.
