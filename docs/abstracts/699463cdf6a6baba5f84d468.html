Introduction

Emotional experiences unfold dynamically during naturalistic listening, engaging neural systems that change across the adult lifespan1. Prior work using narrated stories has shown that listeners show changes in valence and arousal as the story unfolds2. Yet, the neural representations of these two affective dimensions during continuous, real-world comprehension remain poorly understood. EEG offers high temporal sensitivity to fast neural activity generated in response to emotional fluctuations, allowing us to analyze rhythms through spectral analysis. Since different brain rhythms are involved in distinct processes, a mechanistic description of how affect is encoded in the brain requires an understanding of how the arousal and valence of naturalistic stimuli relate to the spectral representation of neural activity. Yet, little is currently known about how valence and arousal modulate the spectral content of neural activity in naturalistic contexts, or how these relationships vary with age3.  Theories of aging suggest that emotional processing is reorganized across adulthood, potentially altering how the brain encodes affective information. However, no study has tested whether age modifies the moment-to-moment coupling between EEG spectral power and valence and arousal ratings during naturalistic narratives. Here, we examined whether both emotional valence and arousal modulate EEG spectral activity during story listening, and whether the strength of these effects or their spatial and spectral patterns differ across young, middle-aged, and older adults. By integrating high-density EEG with segment-level human affective ratings during narrative listening, we aimed to identify age-sensitive neural markers of affective processing. 

Methods

We analyzed EEG recordings from 90 adults (ages 20–80; female: N = 64, male: N = 26) from the Aging Brain Cohort at the University of South Carolina (ABC@USC)4 dataset. EEG were recorded using an actiCHamp amplifier system (Brain Products GmbH, Munich, Germany) with a 128-channel actiCAP slim active electrode array. Since electrode positions were not digitized, we used the standard CACS-128 montage (Easycap) for analysis. EEG was sampled at 500 Hz, high-pass filtered at 1 Hz, and low-pass filtered at 100 Hz. Data was preprocessed with the PyLossless5 pipeline. Segment-level valence and arousal ratings were obtained from an independent cohort of 545 raters stratified into young (20–40; N=162), middle-aged (40–60; N=105), and older adults (60–80; N=278) groups. Among the raters, the young adult group had a mean age of 23.05 years (SD = 5.72) and included 195 females and 75 males; the middle-aged group had a mean age of 48.51 years (SD = 5.38), with 88 females and 74 males; and the older adult group had a mean age of 65.55 years (SD = 4.37), comprising 77 females and 26 males. Because the EEG and rating cohorts were collected in separate samples, EEG participants were young (N=36), middle (N=11), and older (N=43), and affective ratings were matched per age group. For each story segment, we computed multitaper spectral power from 5 to 100 Hz and interpolated power spectral density (PSD) estimates onto a 1-Hz frequency grid. This step was necessary to uniformize the frequency of samples computed on segments of variable lengths. PSD values were extracted for each segment, channel, and frequency and combined with demographic information (age and sex). Subject-level Pearson’s correlations between PSD and valence or arousal across segments produced subject × channel × frequency correlation maps. Group-level analyses were conducted based on (1) pooled ratings, (2) age-stratified subsets, and (3) sex-stratified subsets. Statistical significance was assessed using cluster-based permutation testing (2,000 permutations) with channel adjacency determined by MNE-Python for the CACS-128 montage to correct for multiple comparisons across channels. To correct for multiple comparisons across the four canonical frequency bands (theta: 4–7 Hz, alpha: 8–12 Hz, beta: 13–30 Hz, and gamma: 31–80 Hz), we applied a Bonferroni-corrected cluster-wise threshold of α = 0.0125 (i.e., 0.05/4).  

Results

Across the full sample (N = 90), correlations between valence ratings and EEG spectral power were small (|r| < .02) across all frequency bands, and no clusters survived Bonferroni-corrected significance testing. Age-matched analyses within young, middle-aged, and older adults showed similarly small valence–PSD correlations, with no significant clusters in any group. Sex-stratified analyses also yielded no significant valence-related spectral effects. Arousal demonstrated widespread associations with EEG spectral power. In the overall analysis, higher arousal was associated with reductions in theta and alpha power and modest increases in gamma power, with significant clusters observed in two of the four canonical frequency bands after Bonferroni correction (theta: p = 0.0005; alpha: p = 0.0040) while the two other bands were significant before but not after correction (beta: p = 0.0145; gamma: p = 0.0200). These effects were spatially extensive, indicating strong and coherent arousal–spectral coupling across the scalp. 

Sex-stratified analyses revealed that both females and males exhibited strong theta-band effects, each showing a significant theta cluster at the corrected threshold (p = 0.0005). Females also showed a significant gamma-band cluster (p = 0.0085), whereas males did not exhibit significant gamma effects. Several alpha- and beta-band clusters were present in both groups, but did not survive correction for multiple comparisons. Overall, both sexes demonstrated arousal-related spectral modulation, with differences reflected in the magnitude of the effect and band involvement rather than the presence or absence of the effect. 

Age-matched analyses showed that the arousal–PSD effect was present across all age groups but varied in magnitude and spatial distribution. Young adults exhibited theta (p = 0.0340) and gamma (p = 0.0245) clusters that were significant before but not after correction; middle-aged adults showed the strongest theta effects (max |r| ≈ .17; p = 0.0029); and older adults displayed a significant effect in the theta band (p = 0.0005), after multiple comparison correction. Additionally, for older adults, the alpha (p = 0.0260) clusters were significant before correction but not after. Thus, in the present dataset and under the analysis framework used here, valence–spectral correlations did not reach statistical significance, though these findings do not preclude the possibility that valence-related spectral effects may emerge under alternative analytic approaches or experimental conditions. 

Conclusion

In conclusion, our results show that emotional arousal was reliably associated with changes in EEG spectral power during narrative listening. Arousal-related decreases in theta and alpha power and increases in gamma power were evident across all age groups and both sexes, indicating a stable neural signature of arousal across the adult lifespan. Although age and sex influenced the magnitude of these effects, middle-aged adults showed the strongest theta modulation, and females exhibited most pronounced gamma involvement. Valence–PSD associations were small and did not reach statistical significance in the current analyses, and should be interpreted cautiously, as valence effects may depend on methodological choices or signal features not fully assessed here.
