Introduction

Sex differences in brain aging are increasingly recognized, yet most neuroimaging-based brain age models remain sex-agnostic and provide static estimates that overlook biologically relevant modifiers such as sleep and hypoxia-related vulnerability. Deep learning approaches using structural MRI have demonstrated high accuracy in brain age prediction; but growing evidence suggests that neuroanatomical features underlying these predictions differ by sex. Prior studies have linked sleep disturbances and sleep-disordered breathing to hippocampal atrophy and white matter injury, with women potentially exhibiting greater vulnerability during midlife. However, it remains unclear whether sex-specific brain aging signatures identified by deep learning correspond to differential susceptibility to sleep- and hypoxia-related longitudinal brain structural decline. In our study, we established sex-specific neuroanatomical signatures underlying brain age estimation using deep learning. We then examined whether sleep-related vulnerability, using a symptom-based hypoxia-risk proxy predicts longitudinal hippocampal atrophy and white matter hyperintensity progression in a sex- and age-dependent manner.

Methods

As methodological groundwork, we developed a sex-stratified deep learning framework using T1-weighted MRI from the UK Biobank. A three-dimensional convolutional neural network (3D-CNN) was trained to predict chronological age from whole-brain structural MRI volumes, enabling estimation of sex-specific brain aging patterns. Model interpretability was examined using gradient-based activation mapping to identify neuroanatomical regions contributing to age prediction in women and men. In addition, conditional generative adversarial networks (cGANs) were employed to simulate sex-specific brain aging trajectories, providing a dynamic representation of morphological change across adulthood.

To examine the biological relevance of these sex-specific aging signatures, we conducted a complementary longitudinal analysis in a repeat-imaging subset of UK Biobank participants with baseline assessments (2006–2010) and two structural MRI scans: an initial imaging visit (2014–2020) and repeat imaging (2019+). Sleep-related vulnerability was operationalized using a symptom-based hypoxia-risk proxy reflecting sleep-disordered breathing–related burden. Brain outcomes were derived from the UK Biobank imaging pipeline, with longitudinal changes in hippocampal volume and white matter hyperintensity burden assessed to evaluate sex- and age-dependent associations with subsequent brain structural decline.

Results

Deep learning–based brain age modeling revealed distinct sex-specific neuroanatomical signatures underlying age prediction, with differential contributions from medial temporal and limbic regions. Simulated aging trajectories generated by the cGAN further demonstrated divergent morphological aging patterns between women and men, supporting the presence of sex-specific brain aging processes.

In longitudinal analyses, sleep-related hypoxia-risk burden showed differential associations with subsequent brain structural changes across sexes and age ranges. These associations were most evident in regions implicated by the deep learning–derived aging signatures, including the hippocampus and white matter, and were not fully captured by conventional linear modeling approaches. In women, greater decline in sleep duration over midlife was associated with exaggerated brain age acceleration and reduced hippocampal volume in the 50–60 year age window. These associations were not captured effectively by linear models, highlighting the importance of nonlinear, sex-dependent modeling approaches.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that sex-specific brain aging signatures identified using deep learning provide important context for understanding longitudinal vulnerability to sleep-related brain structural decline. Our findings suggest that sleep-related risk factors may differentially modulate brain aging trajectories in women and men, particularly during midlife. This framework demonstrates how advanced neuroimaging models can be utilized to generate biologically informed hypotheses linking sleep pathology to longitudinal brain aging and provides a foundation for future work examining causal pathways to neurodegeneration.
