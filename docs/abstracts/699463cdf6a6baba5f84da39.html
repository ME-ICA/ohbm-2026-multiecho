Introduction

The elevated risk of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) risk in women may be partially related to their higher exposure to gender-linked psychosocial stressors and the rapid loss of estrogen during their menopausal transitions1. Chronic stress and sex-hormones impact memory-related brain structure and function, particularly in the medial temporal lobe (MTL), an area that is also vulnerable early in AD2. However, little is known about how gender-linked stressors influence MTL neural activity during memory processes, and whether these effects differ by hormone levels. In this study, we recruited 52 middle-aged women with a family history of AD to test whether gender-linked stressors and menopausal stages interactively impact task-evoked neural activations during lure discrimination, a challenging memory process that is affected in early AD.

Methods

Chronic and acute stressor counts and severities were quantified using the STRess and Adversity INventory (STRAIN). We focused on gender-influenced domains (reproduction, treatment/health, other relationships, death) and social psychological characteristics (entrapment, interpersonal loss, role-change)1. Separate composite indices for chronic and acute stressors were calculated by summarizing all relevant indices. Menopausal stage was determined using Stages of Reproductive Aging Workshop (STRAW) criteria3.

Functional MRI (fMRI) data were acquired during a modified mnemonic similarity task (MST)4, on a 3-Tesla Siemens Skyra scanner (TR=1.485s, 2mm isotropic, 524 time-frames x 3 runs). During the task, participants were asked to remember details of a set of everyday objects, followed by recognizing them as identical (targets), similar (lures) or new (foils, Figure 1A). Neural activity during lure discrimination was quantified using the contrast LureCR (corrected identified lures) vs. LureFA (falsely rejected lures). Post-hoc univariate ANCOVAs were first performed to evaluate whether menopause stage and gender-linked chronic stressor count (either across domains or characteristics) interactively predict neural activity in each contrast-activated region. Age, education, and racial groups were used as covariates. Multivariate canonical correlation analysis (CCA) were then used to jointly evaluate associations between all gender-linked stressors (i.e., acute and chronic, across domains and characteristics) and neural activities in activated MTL regions. Figure 1C summarizes post-hoc analysis pipeline and results on activated brain regions.

Results

Perimenopausal women (N=11, 50.09±3.78 years old (y.o), 86%±5% accuracy) showed significantly better task performances than postmenopausal women (N=26, 54.92±2.42 y.o, 79%±9% accuracy), but there were no significant differences between perimenopausal and premenopausal women (N=15, 44.8±5.56 y.o, 83%±7% accuracy). Task performance was further negatively correlated with the severity of gender-linked acute stressors across social-psychological characteristics (partial correlation (partial-r) =-0.35, p=0.05).

Significant brain activations in the prefrontal, MTL, visual areas were observed during lure discrimination (p<0.001, cluster-size>49, Figure 1B). For gender-linked chronic stressors count across domains, post-hoc univariate ANCOVA revealed a significant positive association with brain activities in the Visual areas (Calcarine: partial-r=0.32, p=0.02; Lingual: partial-r=0.31, p=0.02) and Supra Marginal area (partial-r=0.40, p=0.007), yet no moderation effect of menopause stage was observed. No significant association was found between gender-linked stressors, either across domains or characteristics, and neural activities in the medial temporal lobe (MTL) using univariate analysis.

With multivariate CCA, a trend-level significant mode of covariation was found linking combined gender-influenced stressor levels and lure discrimination activities in MTL (cca-r=0.50, pfwe=0.08, permutation-based5, Figure 2A). Except the middle temporal gyrus, MTL regions negatively contribute to this mode, with most significant contributions seen in the activated right hippocampus (r=-0.24) and right amygdala (r=-0.54, Figure 2B). On the other hand, gender-linked chronic stressors, across the social psychological characteristics contributed most significantly and positively (count: r=0.80; severity: r=0.78) towards this mode of covariation, followed by chronic stressors across domains (count: r=0.52; severity: r=0.52) and acute stressors across characteristics (count r=0.50; severity r=0.42, Figure 2B). This mode of covariation was further driven by perimenopausal women (Figure 2A bottom). A voxel-wise examination in the hippocampus revealed that for pre/post-menopausal women, more lifetime chronic stressors were related to greater hippocampal activities, whereas peri-menopausal women demonstrated significant opposite associations (Figure 2C).

Conclusion

Our study first demonstrated robust MTL activations during lure discrimination in middle aged women at risk of AD. Our findings further suggest that gender-linked chronic stressors may be related to this neural activity. Although univariate analysis showed only limited associations outside the MTL, multivariate method identified a marginally co-varied pattern in which higher chronic stressors were linked to lower activations across hippocampus and amygdala regions. This covarying mode appeared to be most significant in perimenopausal women, who also demonstrated highest task performance and opposite stress-activation associations in the hippocampus. These results indicate possible stress- and menopausal-related influences on MTL functions during midlife, although future work with larger samples and direct hormone measures is needed to clarify these relationships and their relevance to AD risks.
