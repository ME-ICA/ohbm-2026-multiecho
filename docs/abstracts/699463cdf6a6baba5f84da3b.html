Introduction

With the findings about the critical role of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) for brain clearance and associated pathologies, such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD), the scientific attention to CSF is increasing. Variations of peri-vascular space, where CSF flows, across the transitions of sleep stages, and its hydrodynamics were associated with accumulation of brain waste.^1 Also, weaker coupling between ventricle and brain was found in AD patients than in healthy controls,^5 and more recently decomposed into multiple brain-CSF modes with Leading Eigenmode Dynamics Analysis (LEiDA) on fMRI.^3 Beyond the function of brain clearance, CSF has high mechanical mobility, which is different from grey matter and white matter, and greatly affects fMRI signals. However, the dynamics of CSF, besides its link with cardiac and respiratory systems, has not been thoroughly explored due to its complexity, being discarded as physiological noise in standard fMRI.

Recent advances in fMRI temporal resolution show ultra-slow oscillatory modes extending across the whole brain, differentiated from respiratory and cardiovascular frequency bands.^2 We hypothesize that the ultraslow whole-brain correlations in fMRI signal are related with CSF hydrodynamics. The standard understanding on fMRI signal is blood oxygenation level dependent signal (BOLD), but CSF flow also affects the fMRI signal.^4 We aim to investigate the principles governing ultra-slow, long-range oscillatory fluctuations in human CSF dynamics.

Methods

We collected ultrafast Echo-Planar Imaging (EPI) fMRI (TR/TE=68/25ms) from 5 healthy controls on selected slices of the ventricles (lateral and fourth) and spinal cord. We bandpass-filtered the fMRI scans from 0.01 to 0.2 Hz and analyzed signals in voxels in the superior sagittal sinus, fourth ventricle, brain, and empty background.

Results

Figure 1 (a) shows the ultraslow fMRI signal in the coronal section, and (b) shows the ultraslow fluctuations from the voxels of different regions: 1) superior sagittal sinus, 2) 4th ventricle, 3) brain, and 4) background. The signal from the superior sagittal sinus was reduced to one fifth for visibility. It suggests that the highest amplitude of ultraslow activity is in  the superior sagittal sinus, preliminarily supporting the hypothesis that CSF is linked with the ultra-slow activity in the neurological system. Figure 1 (c) gives the relative power spectrum density from the four regions, differentiating several peaks in the ultraslow band. Even though regions 1) and 3) have high anti-correlation, −0.60 of Pearson’s rho, they show different frequencies of peaks. Also, the different spectral properties from the superior sagittal sinus and the ventricle give  suggests the existence of multiple modes in the ultraslow frequency band, requiring further mode decomposition analysis.

Conclusion

These results show that ultrafast fMRI is a powerful tool for the investigation of CSF dynamics in humans. The highest amplitude of the signal in the superior sagittal sinus implies that fMRI images CSF hydrodynamics as well as the contents of deoxygenated haemoglobins in the blood vessels. We will proceed to analyze the ultraslow eigenmodes with the LEiDA algorithm and frequency-specific oscillatory mode patterns.
