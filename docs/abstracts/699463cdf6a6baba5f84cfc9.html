Introduction

Diffusion MRI is sensitive to differences in microstructural tissue properties. Recent work in healthy adults has shown that a small set of latent factors interpretably describe spatial relationships between diffusion statistics in cortex, with some factors corresponding to established cortical gradients of organization and development.1 Related work on developmental variation along cortico-cortical white matter tracts found that maturation patterns of superficial tract microstructure align with these cortical gradients, reflecting that cortical grey and white matter microstructural organization and development are closely related.2 It remains unclear how these microstructural relationships generalize beyond cortex, motivating a need to characterize diffusion statistic covariance jointly across cortex, subcortex, cortico-cortical association tracts, and subcortico-cortical projection tracts. Factor analysis addresses this need by summarizing correlated diffusion statistics into a smaller set of latent dimensions, unifying multiple indicators of diffusion properties based on their shared methodological and microstructural sensitivities. Characterizing diffusion statistics’ typical variability and covariability from healthy populations may have important clinical applications. Evidence from epilepsy patients suggests that deviations from typical microstructural organization correspond with lesion locations and help explain surgical outcomes.3,4 Quantifying how latent families of diffusion statistics are altered in epilepsy may therefore help prioritize which measures are most sensitive to clinically relevant microstructural alterations.

Methods

Data and Preprocessing: We leveraged multi-shell diffusion MRI data from 271 healthy controls from the University of Pennsylvania (Penn, n=45), Human Connectome Project Young Adult (HCP-YA, n=69), and Human Connectome Project Aging (HCP-Aging, n=157) datasets and 89 temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) patients with unilateral seizure onset (n=55 left, n=34 right). Data were preprocessed with QSIPrep.5 Diffusion models were fit with QSIRrecon, producing 27 diffusion statistic maps across 5 models: diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), diffusion kurtosis imaging (DKI), generalized q-sample imaging (GQI), neurite orientation dispersion and density imaging (NODDI), and mean apparent propagator MRI (MAP-MRI). Diffusion statistics were averaged within 156 cortical and subcortical grey matter regions (4S156 atlas) and 156 white matter segments (HCP-1065 atlas), which included 52 unilateral projection and association tracts divided into thirds based on automatic fiber quantification (AFQ) along-tract sampling. Scanner-related differences were harmonized with CovBat. Normative modeling: Normative diffusion values were modeled as a function of age (spline fit) and sex using generalized additive models, and deviations in patients were expressed as z-scores. Factor analysis: To capture interregional patterns of covariance, factor analysis was performed on diffusion statistic z-score correlations in controls using ordinary least squares estimation with promax rotation. The number of retained factors was determined based on the elbow of the scree plot. For each subject, region-specific z-scored diffusion statistics were multiplied by factor loadings to compute factor scores. Factor scores in epilepsy patients were z-scored relative to control factor score distributions per region of interest. Abnormalities across factors were summarized using the Mahalanobis distance (DM). Statistical comparisons: Paired t-tests were used to assess for mean DM differences between regions ipsilateral and contralateral to the clinically-determined epileptogenic hemisphere, excluding regions which were bilateral or had no contralateral counterpart. Paired t-tests were used to assess for absolute factor z-score differences per region between factors. Independent t-tests were used to assess for mean DM differences across patients between grey matter and white matter regions.

Results

Factor interpretations. 4 factors explaining 86% of diffusion statistic covariance in controls. Factors were interpreted to reflect “overall”, “anisotropic”, “non-Gaussian”, and “spin density function (SDF)-based” diffusivity, denoted F1-F4 respectively, based on the statistics with the strongest loadings onto each factor. Abnormalities by factor: The overall diffusivity factor showed the most extensive abnormalities across TLE patients (F1 vs. F2: t(311)=17.01, F1 vs. F3: t(311)=13.37, F1 vs. F4 t(311)=8.44, all p<0.001). The overall diffusivity factor was dominated by MAP-MRI return-to-plane probability (RTPP), a statistic quantifying the likelihood that water molecules return to the principal diffusion plane. RTPP had an extremely high loading (0.99), indicating that variation in this factor is almost entirely captured by RTPP. Additional strong loadings on this factor were observed for axial diffusivity (AD) from DKI (loading=-0.94) and DTI (loading=-0.93), and mean diffusivity (MD) from DKI (loading=-0.90) and DTI (loading=-0.87). Abnormalities by tissue type: Abnormalities were more extensive in white matter than grey matter (t(310)=-12.14, p<0.001). Abnormalities by region: For both left and right temporal lobe epilepsy subgroups, abnormalities across factors were more extensive ipsilateral than contralateral to the clinically-determined epileptogenic hemisphere (Left TLE, t(135)=7.89, p<0.001; Right TLE t(135)=9.15, p<0.001), although contralateral abnormalities were also observable. In grey matter, abnormalities were most extensive in temporolimbic and default mode regions, particularly the ipsilateral hippocampus (Left TLE DM=0.58, Right TLE DM=0.98) and middle temporal gyrus (Left TLE DM=0.62, Right TLE DM=0.77). In white matter, abnormalities were most extensive in the ipsilateral anterior thalamic radiation core (Left TLE DM=0.96, Right TLE DM=1.29), frontal end of the inferior fronto-occipital fasciulus (Left TLE DM=0.74, Right TLE DM=0.97), and temporal end of the uncinate fasciculus (Left TLE DM=0.68, Right TLE DM=0.86). Importantly, abnormalities were considerably heterogeneous across factors, epilepsy subgroups, and patients.

Conclusion

This study shows that a small number of latent factors can recapitulate complex diffusion MRI covariance patterns across cortical, subcortical, and tract-based regions. Four factors captured most of the shared variance among diffusion statistics and corresponded to partially separable families of diffusivity-based measures of the tissue microenvironment. By applying these latent factors to describe abnormalities in temporal lobe epilepsy patients, we found that overall diffusivity alterations in white matter were particularly extensive. Microstructural abnormalities across factors mapped onto temporolimbic and frontotemporal circuit abnormalities which are well-established features of temporal lobe epilepsy. Collectively, these results illustrate how latent diffusion factors can serve as an organizing scaffold for summarizing normative microstructural variability to better localize and interpret disease-related changes at the whole-brain level.
