Introduction

Over the past two decades, the Open Science movement has driven the creation and growth of large-scale open-data initiatives that have transformed how data are collected, shared, and used, by making data accessible and reusable and research more transparent, equitable, and collaborative. Large resources such as the UK Biobank, OpenNeuro, the Human Connectome Project, and INDI/FCP have enabled researchers to advance knowledge of brain structure and function. Similarly, initiatives like ABCD, ADHD-200, the NKI Rockland Sample, and the Brazilian High-Risk Cohort (BHRC), among others, have advanced knowledge of brain development and psychiatric disorders across the lifespan through transdiagnostic and longitudinal data. The strength of these initiatives lies not only in their large sample sizes but also in the depth of phenotypic characterization and multimodal data availability.  

In 2015, the Child Mind Institute (CMI) launched the Healthy Brain Network (HBN), a large-scale data-sharing initiative which releases multimodal datasets while capturing a broad range of clinical psychopathology in children and adolescents.1 HBN openly releases rich multimodal data, integrating deep phenotypic characterization with neuroimaging, genomic profiling, and real-time activity monitoring that can be used for a variety of studies, such as clinical and psychiatric biomarker discovery, neurodevelopmental, cognitive and learning research, multimodal integration, and neuroimaging method development. Additionally, since HBN collects standardized neuropsychiatric assessments and uses harmonized MRI acquisition protocols, these data can be integrated with other existing neuroimaging and psychiatric datasets. 

Ten years after enrolling our first participants, we reflect on how HBN is contributing to brain sciences by accelerating clinical research, advancing multimodal data collection, expanding data accessibility and use, and promoting data-science innovation and education.

Methods

HBN accelerates clinical research while enhancing accessibility to diagnostic evaluation 

HBN utilizes a community-referred recruitment model by encouraging participation of families in New York (US) who have concerns about mental health in their children (https://fcon_1000.projects.nitrc.org/indi/cmi_healthy_brain_network/Project.html). The aim of the HBN initiative is to enroll up to 10,000 participants aged 5-21.

Families enrolled in the study complete 3-4 visits for a total of approximately 13 hours. Visits include mental-health assessment, cognitive testing, learning evaluation, physical assessment, genetic sampling, imaging, actigraphy, and ecological momentary assessment (Fig.1). At the end of the study, families are compensated for their time and receive a no-cost personalized feedback report about their child, signed by a licensed clinician. The report contains diagnostic evaluations, treatment recommendations, and referrals to local resources, which can be used to require school-based accommodations, specific treatments, individual therapy, and school counseling.  

Through its comprehensive in-person and virtual assessment protocol, HBN has acquired phenotypic data from more than 140 instruments spanning multiple domains (Fig.1), making the dataset an invaluable resource for clinical research across a wide range of topics.  

HBN advances multimodal data collection, accessibility, and usage 

The HBN initiative was designed from inception to enable the integration of diverse data modalities (https://fcon_1000.projects.nitrc.org/indi/cmi_healthy_brain_network/Data.html). Openly released imaging data comprises high density EEG (128 channels), eye-tracking, and structural and multi-band functional MRI, acquired during resting state, cognitive tasks, and a naturalistic video-watching paradigm (Fig.1). These data support multimodal analyses and integration with deep clinical phenotyping. Collection of biological samples, such as saliva, blood, teeth, and hair, provides the opportunity to quantify genetic markers that inform individual differences in neurodevelopment and environmental exposure. Finally, actigraphy and ecological momentary assessment data support the identification of ecologically valid and high-resolution patterns that are often missed in laboratory clinical assessments.

HBN promotes data-science innovation and education 

HBN data is not only used in clinical, research, and academic settings, but is has recently been made available through data-science competitions to accelerate innovation in data-science and AI, and to provide data and resources that support the training of the next generation of data scientists, with an emphasis on inclusivity and diversity in the field. Between 2023 and 2025, the Child Mind Institute played a leading role in organizing 6 data-science competitions in the mental health space, leveraging phenotypic, neuroimaging, and digital technology data collected as part of HBN (https://fcon_1000.projects.nitrc.org/indi/cmi_healthy_brain_network/OpenScience.html).2,3  

These data-science competitions enabled participants from a wide range of backgrounds, such as neuroimaging and non-neuroimaging, and across multiple levels of expertise, from high school students up to professionals, to access multimodal data and engage with real-world mental health and neuroscience questions, creating a unique opportunity to crowdsource novel approaches beyond traditional academic practices. Data for these competitions were specifically curated for the target audiences and made “machine learning-ready". Furthermore, in collaboration with the hosting platforms and collaborators (e.g., Kaggle, Codabench, Women in Data Science), methods to quantify results and select competition winners were tailored to the competition’s aims.

Results

Over the past 10 years, HBN has evaluated more than 8,000 children and adolescents, often providing their first clinical diagnosis, and has built strong trust and credibility with participating families, many of whom would otherwise have limited access to mental-health assessment. Families have responded extremely positively to the initiative, and a subset has gone on to participate in additional CMI studies, which further leveraged the transdiagnostic richness of the sample thus enabling more targeted clinical questions. 

The extensive transdiagnostic and multimodal dataset has been released through 11 successive data releases. Anonymized phenotypic data is openly accessible on the LORIS data-management platform upon signing a data usage agreement that governs responsible use and minimizes participant risk. Deidentified neuroimaging data is openly available through the international neuroimaging data-sharing initiative (INDI/FCP) and nemar.org, enabling thousands of researchers and clinicians worldwide to advance neuroimaging and psychiatric research without the economic or time burden of primary data collection. To date, more than 500 laboratories around the world have accessed HBN phenotypic data, and HBN datasets have been cited in over 600 scientific publications.

Data science competitions organized by CMI using HBN data have engaged thousands of participants and teams worldwide and have driven advances across multiple areas: improving state-of-the-art methods for sleep-window detection with actigraphy data (1,877 teams); generating insights to inform future studies on safe technology use (3,559 teams); testing hypotheses on the contribution of various sensors to detecting body-focused repetitive behaviors (2,657 teams); developing EEG foundation models for applications in computational psychiatry (1,183 teams); and expanding data-science education opportunities to broaden participation and inclusivity, particularly in addressing socially and clinically relevant questions related to women’s brain health (1,130 teams).2,3

From a neuroimaging perspective, HBN has openly released more than 3,900 MRI datasets and over 4,000 EEG datasets, marking the completion of a long-standing data-collection phase. As part of the Reproducible Brain Chart (RBC) project (https://reprobrainchart.github.io/), the HBN dataset is also being released with other large neuroimaging dataset, with data curated, preprocessed, and harmonized across datasets. This was specifically designed to facilitate data reuse, cross-study integration, and AI-driven analyses.4  

Current efforts are centered around releasing previously unreleased data, such as actigraphy data, which will enable high-resolution and ecologically valid characterization of behavior over time. The HBN actigraphy dataset comprises wrist-worn accelerometry data over at least two weeks, offering a detailed quantification of sleep-wake patterns and rest-activity fragmentation. These measures capture dimensions of behavior that are often inaccessible through in-clinic or in-lab assessments and are highly relevant for psychiatric research, particularly for conditions marked by disruptions of sleep and daily functioning. Actigraphy data are being released in both raw and preprocessed formats, followed by manually curated sleep windows (over 25,000 nights reviewed).5 Summary features include standard sleep metrics (e.g., sleep onset, sleep offset, and sleep duration) and physical activity metrics (e.g., time in sedentary/light/moderate/vigorous activities). For example, Fig.2 illustrates a 24-hour activity trajectory stratified by day of the week, sex, and age. This newly released dataset exemplifies the multimodal capabilities that HBN offers: when linked with the phenotyping and multimodal measurements available in HBN, it provides a way to study how online measures, such as sleep and daily motion, contribute to clinical, cognitive, and neurobiological variations.

Conclusion

Although HBN began as a community-serving program and open data-sharing initiative, it has continued to evolve in parallel with rapid advances in neuroscience and psychiatric research. These developments have shaped how HBN collects, curates, and disseminates data, and have opened new pathways for leveraging this large-scale transdiagnostic resource. Moving forward, HBN aims to finalize the release of multimodal datasets acquired asynchronously (such as EMA and genetic profiles) and will prioritize the collection of new data through the in-house multimodal brain/body imaging (MoBI) lab, which enables the synchronous capture of brain activity and behavior. Furthermore, it will incorporate the development of diverse, smaller ad-hoc studies that require deep phenotyping. Finally, current efforts are focused on developing methods to share PHI-dense qualitative data (e.g., audio, video, text) already collected but never publicly released, through federated data-sharing approaches and the creation of synthetic datasets. These represent concrete next steps in the HBN’s commitment to providing state-of-the-art and openly accessible datasets to the broader research and data-science communities.
