Introduction

The period from childhood to early adulthood represents a critical window for structural brain maturation, characterized by ongoing refinement of cortical and subcortical regions. These changes involve progressive myelination and microstructural reorganization that both shape and are shaped by brain function. The T1w/T2w ratio provides an in vivo proxy of regional microstructure, primarily reflecting myelin content, with additional contributions from iron and tissue composition that change during development. While cortical development has been extensively studied,^1,2,3 cortico-subcortical co-maturation remains less explored,^4 despite its essential role in cognitive development.^5 Understanding coordinated structural changes across the cortex and subcortex is therefore key to characterizing brain development. Here, we aimed to map shared trajectories of cortical and subcortical maturation and to study how these synchronized patterns relate to macroscale cortical organization.

Methods

To measure structural maturation across childhood and early adulthood, we used the T1w/T2w ratio, a myelin proxy, obtained from the HCP-Development (5–21 years) and HCP-Young Adult (22–26 years) cross-sectional cohorts (N = 959, Fig. 1A). T1w/T2w ratio images were parcellated using the Glasser atlas for cortical regions and the Melbourne (Tian S4) atlas for subcortical regions. Age-related trajectories of the T1w/T2w ratio were estimated for each cortical and subcortical parcel using generalized additive models (GAMs), and first derivatives of the T1w/T2w ratio (ΔT1w/T2w) were computed to quantify regional maturation rate. Cortico-subcortical co-maturation was quantified by comparing the first derivatives of T1w/T2w trajectories across cortical and subcortical parcels. For each age at which the maturation rate was estimated, we computed the difference between the maturation rate of each cortical parcel and each subcortical parcel, yielding an age-resolved cortex x subcortex difference matrix. Similarity between regions was then defined as the negative absolute difference in maturation rates, such that higher values indicate more synchronized maturation. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed in two stages. First, PCA was applied to the ΔT1w/T2w trajectories to identify clusters of cortical and subcortical parcels exhibiting similar rates of maturation. These clusters capture regions that undergo coordinated changes in myelination and microstructural development. Second, PCA was applied to the cortico-subcortical co-maturation matrix to extract latent axes summarizing synchronized patterns of structural maturation across cortex and subcortex, reflecting how co-maturation varies across age bins: 8-10, 13-15, 18-20, and 23-25 years. To contextualize these axes with overarching patterns of cortical organization (T1w/T2w ratio, gene expression, functional gradients, neurosynth maps, and cortical thickness) from Neuromaps, Pearson’s correlation coefficients (r) were calculated, with p-values obtained via spin-test correcting for spatial autocorrelation using 1000 permutations.

Results

Using GAMs, we estimated age-related T1w/T2w trajectories for each cortical parcel and subcortical region. We observed that T1w/T2w ratios generally increased with age across most cortical and subcortical areas. ΔT1w/T2w, representing maturation rate, varied regionally: pre-frontal cortices exhibited lower or more protracted ΔT1w/T2w profiles, consistent with delayed maturation trajectories. By comparison, subcortical regions showed a pronounced decline of ΔT1w/T2w (Fig. 1B–C). The first PCA on ΔT1w/T2w trajectories (Fig. 1D) revealed partial segregation of regions by cytoarchitectural class in PCA space. Insular and limbic cortical parcels overlapped with subcortical regions, whereas other cortical classes were more widely dispersed, reflecting divergence in maturation patterns across regions. Subsequent PCA analyses showed a transient period of synchronized maturation during early adolescence (13-15 years), forming a latent structure of coordinated change that weakened during later adolescence (Fig. 1E). To contextualize the latent axes of cortico-subcortical co-maturation derived across the full age range, we correlated the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) with multimodal maps of myelin, gene expression, functional gradients, neurosynth meta-analytic activations, and cortical thickness (Fig. 1F). PC2 showed robust associations with myelin and gene expression, functional gradients, and neurosynth patterns, indicating that this axis captures synchronized maturation patterns aligned with structural, molecular, and functional organization. In contrast, PC1 exhibited low correlations with most features (all p > 0.05), except for cortical thickness (r = -0.37, p = 0.003). This pattern suggests that PC1 may reflect a cortico-subcortical developmental axis that is not well captured by measures that are predominantly cortical.

Conclusion

Our findings revealed a selective, temporally constrained coupling of cortical and subcortical maturation during adolescence. Developmental refinement of cortical systems occurs in parallel with changes in subcortical regions, particularly within limbic and insula regions. This coordinated maturation aligns with established structural, molecular, and functional brain organization. Together, these results provide novel insights into how coordinated structural maturation shapes brain organization and ultimately supports neurocognitive development.
