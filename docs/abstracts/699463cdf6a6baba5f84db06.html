Introduction

During adolescence, a constellation of changes pose a risk for the emergence and worsening of sleep problems for many adolescents. This risk is exacerbated in youth with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), comprising ~9% of adolescents. Adolescents with ADHD report far worse sleep quality and more difficulty waking up compared to their non-ADHD peers. In addition, youth with ADHD are also at increased risk for facing academic challenges, and even dropping out of school during adolescence relative to their peers. Despite the prevalence of poor sleep for adolescents with ADHD, the neurobiological mechanisms linking sleep academic challenges in this population are poorly understood.
Reading, a fundamental skill for academic success, requires a balance of externally-focused attention toward incoming information and internally-focused attention to integrate new information with existing knowledge. The balance of externally- and internally-focused attention is neurobiologically facilitated by interactions across several large-scale brain networks the dorsal attention network (DAN, externally-focused attention), the default mode network (DMN, internally-focused attention), the fronto-parietal network (FPN), and the salience network (SN). Poor sleep disrupts integration and segregation of these networks. Furthermore, organization of the DAN, DMN, FPN, and SN is aberrant in individuals with ADHD. Here, we test a model wherein poor sleep affects the balance of functional brain connectivity underlying externally- and internally-oriented attention during a reading fMRI task, clarifying how poor sleep impacts specific network pathways that underlie the balance of attention.

Methods

A preliminary dataset of 12 adolescents (age 13-16) with ADHD were used for the current analyses. From each subject we collected self-reported and parent-reported measures of sleep problems and disordered morning routine (does your child have time to eat breakfast, how long does it take them to leave the house etc. Participants underwent a functional MRI scan where they completed up to 3 9-minute runs of a reading task where they had to make a rhyming judgement or a semantic judgement. fMRI data were preprocessed using open, reproduceable field standard pipelines (fMRIprep and eXtensible Connectivity Pipeline-DCAN). Processed functional runs were concatenated and parcellated into 14 functional brain networks. From this parcellation, regions comprising the DAN, DMN, FPN, and SN were used for further analyses. Average time courses of brain activity were extracted from each region across all the combined tasks, and Pearson’s correlations were calculated for each pair of brain regions resulting in a correlation matrix for each pair of pain networks (SN-DMN, SN-FPN etc.). Global efficiency was calculated to index integration between network pairs and system segregation was calculated to index segregation between networks. Integration and segregation of network pairs was correlated with reported sleep problems and disordered morning routine to test the link between multiple domain of poor sleep and network organization underlying the balance of attention.

Results

Preliminary results show a negative relationship between system segregation of the SN-DMN and disordered morning routine (r(10) = -0.61 p = .03), such that adolescents with a more disordered morning routine saw less separation between the SN and DMN while reading. Additionally sleep problems were negatively related to integration of the SN-FPN r(10) = -0.76, p = .004, such that adolescents with more sleep problems saw less integration between the SN and FPN during reading.

Conclusion

These preliminary findings suggest a neurobiological pathway by which poor sleep impacts learning and academic success in adolescents with ADHD. We find both poor sleep and disordered morning routine impact connectivity of the SN, through poor segregation from the DMN and poor integration with the FPN. Future analyses will increase the sample size and test for a moderating role of these neurobiological pathways between sleep and academic achievement. Overall, this work stands to provide novel evidence for how poor sleep confers risk for academic challenges in youth with ADHD.
