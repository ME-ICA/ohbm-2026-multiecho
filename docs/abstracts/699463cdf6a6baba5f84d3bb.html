Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic, immune-mediated neuro-inflammatory disease characterized by demyelination, inflammation, and axonal degeneration, causing lesions across the central nervous system (CNS)1. A wide range of symptoms and functional impairments may develop in the mobility domain, such as balance and postural control. Thus, preventing falls in people with MS (PwMS) is crucial. Motor-cognitive interaction has been extensively investigated as a possible determinant to mobility impairment in PwMS. Traditionally, the assessment of cognitive-motor interaction involves the execution of motor and cognitive tasks in isolation (single-task (ST)-condition) and in combination (dual-task (DT)-condition). However, previous studies focused on the comparison between the single-task motor (STm) and the DT, identifying the DT cost as the impact of the cognitive task on the motor performance. Moreover, no clear association has been found between the risk of falling and increased DT costs in PwMS2.

The literature recommends the use of dynamic tasks that alter balance and postural control to simulate daily living situations. However, assessing brain activity during walking presents technical challenges, as it requires the use of wireless devices and the procedure is susceptible to motor artifacts3,4. Recently, advances in wearable electroencephalography (EEG) technology have enabled the recording of brain activity during dynamic tasks, facilitating the extraction of mental workload metrics based on power spectral densities during task execution. 

We aim to extract mental workload indexes during cognitive and motor tasks performed in isolation (ST) and in combination (DT) to assess motor-cognitive interaction in PwMS. We employed a wearable EEG system suitable for EEG recordings during walking. 

Methods

Twenty-three participants were included in the study (females: 11; age (mean [standard deviation]): 57.4 [9.6] years; Kurtzke Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) (median [25th, 75th percentiles]): 5 [4.125-6.00]). Participants were recruited at the Neurorehabilitation Unit (AOUI Verona, Italy) and the Multiple Sclerosis Center at the Neurology Unit (AOUI Verona, Italy), and 6 of them at the Ferrara University Hospital (Ospedale Sant’Anna, Ferrara, Italy). 

All participants provided written informed consent after receiving a full explanation of the study procedures. Data collection was conducted in accordance with the protocol approved by local ethics committee of the University of Verona (Protocol 232CET). 

EEG was recorded using a wearable gel-free device composed of one ground electrode, one reference electrode, and eight active electrodes: AFz, AF3, AF4, AF7, AF8, Pz, P3, and P4 (https://www.mindtooth.com/). The sampling frequency was set to 125 Hz. In the left part of Figure 1 the proposed methodology is shown. 

The protocol consisted of 4 phases: a resting-state eyes open, a single-task cognitive (STc), a STm, and a motor and cognitive DT. STm, STc and DT were presented in a randomized order. Resting data lasted 90 s. In the STc, participants were asked to count aloud a serial subtraction of seven starting from a random 3-digit number for 90 s. In the STm, subjects performed the timed-up-and-go (TUG) task for 5 trials, separated by a rest of 10 s. Finally, during the DT, they performed the TUG while counting a serial subtraction of seven. The TUG is a mobility assessment task in which the participant begins seated, then stands up, walks 3 m, turns around, and returns to the chair to sit down again. 

EEG data were baseline-corrected and filtered using a 5th order high-pass Butterworth filter (cut-off 3.5Hz) and a 5th order low-pass Butterworth filter (cut-off 30 Hz). We used the independent component analysis (ICA) analysis to reduce movement artifacts and eye blinks.

To deal with residual artifacts, we selected the 15th-85th percentile range of signal amplitude for each EEG channel. Values exceeding the 85th percentile by more than 1.5 times this range or falling below the 15th percentile by the same margin were classified as outliers. The signal without outliers was then reconstructed via interpolation.

To compute the power spectral density (PSD) in theta (4-8 Hz) and alpha (8-12 Hz) frequency bands using Welch’s method, a sliding window of 250 samples (2 s) with an overlap of 249 samples was used. When the sliding window covered an interval where more than 25% of window data samples were outliers or a set of at least 30 consecutive outliers (as described above), the window was excluded from the PSD computation. 

For each trial and each EEG channel, we computed the median PSD in theta and alpha, and we averaged these values across the 5 trials in STm, STc and DT conditions. Then, the task load index (TLI) was computed as the ratio between the theta power in the frontal lobe and the alpha power in the parietal lobe, which has been shown to increase as the task becomes more demanding. According to previous literature, the channel AFz was used to extract the PSD in theta band, and Pz in alpha band5.

Normality of EEG data was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. To compare the four conditions, a non-parametric Friedman test for repeated measures was applied. Finally, post-hoc analyses were performed with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, and the p-value for statistical significance was corrected for multiple comparisons using Bonferroni’s method.

Results

We implemented a procedure based on wearable EEG data to assess mental workload in PwMS. Overall, the procedure lasted around 40 to 45 min. The right panel of figure 1 shows the PSD values in theta and alpha bands and TLI over 5 trials for each participant and condition. Statical significant differences are marked with an asterisk. 

Statistical analyses found significant condition effects in TLI (χ2=39.99, p<0.001) and theta PSD (χ2=30.81, p<0.001), but not for alpha PSD. In theta PSD, post-hoc analyses revealed statistical differences between 1) STm and resting (p<<0.05); 2) STc and rest (p=0.001); 3) DT and resting (p<<0.05). Analyzing TLI, post-hoc analyses revealed statistical differences between 1) STm and resting (p<<0.05); 2) STc and rest (p=0.007); 3) DT and resting (p<<0.05); 4) STm and STc (p=0.0017); 5) DT and STc (p=0.00047). 

Of note, all task conditions were different from resting EEG, confirming the robustness of the extracted measures. Moreover, the results highlighted that the single cognitive task (STc) is statistically different from the tasks involving motor components. As expected, higher TLI was observed with increasing task complexity. 

Conclusion

The study introduced an innovative framework for investigating motor-cognitive interaction in PwMS using wearable devices during mobility tasks. TUG represents an important aspect of MS-related mobility impairment and probably a target for fall-prevention strategies. The use of wearable EEG can have a remarkable impact in this field of study, establishing the groundwork for personalized approaches in the management of motor-cognitive interaction alterations in PwMS.

The EEG analyses suggested a significant effect of condition on  theta-band activity and on the TLI. Particularly, no differences were found in TLI between DT and STm, whereas both differed from the STc. These findings may be consistent with the hypothesis that a walking task, such as the TUG, requires a high mental load. Thus, this may prevent subjects from recruiting additional resources to cope with the added cognitive task in the DT condition.

The main limitation is the absence of a healthy control group. Indeed, most of the studies on EEG correlates of mental workload were performed on healthy subjects and functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) in PwMS has shown cortical activation, in prefrontal cortex and supplementary motor areas during walking DT paradigms. 

We plan to address these issues in the future by recruiting additional PwMS and controls, thereby increasing the statistical power of the analyses and investigating EEG-based workload correlates with clinical outcomes, demographic data, and the risk of falls. Moreover, the inclusion and analysis of a group of healthy controls may provide further information for the interpretation of our results, possibly discriminating whether the increased activation found in the STm condition is likely to be attributed to MS-related disfunctions or to a specific motor-related effect.
