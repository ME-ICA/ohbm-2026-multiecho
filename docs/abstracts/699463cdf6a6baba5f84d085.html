Introduction

One of the criteria of substance use disorder is compulsive drug seeking and use despite the awareness of detrimental consequences driven by intense substance craving. Dual-process models of addictive disorders conceptualize this maladaptive behavior, usually described as a “compromise of willpower” stemming from a dysregulation in the interplay of two neural systems: bottom-up, and top-down. The stimulus-driven impulsive system becomes hypersensitive to drug cues, growing hyperactive and automatic. The reflective system (or top-down), goal-oriented and responsible for inhibitory control and planning, is weakened and compromised. A promising therapeutic avenue to address this neurocognitive imbalance involves targeted interventions aimed to rectifying this imbalance by the strengthening of the reflective system, such as through Cognitive Remediation Therapy.

Methods

The study merged data from two randomized controlled trials ^1,2. A total of 74 participants with tobacco use disorder and 77 participants with alcohol use disorder were included. The clinical intervention consisted of a six-week program: a smoking cessation protocol for Tobacco Use Disorder participants and inpatient therapy for those with Alcohol Use Disorder. All participants received Treatment-As-Usual and were randomized into an experimental group with adjunctive chess-based cognitive remediation therapy over six weeks, or a control group with no additional therapy (Treatment-As-Usual only). To assess the neural and cognitive effects of the intervention, all participants underwent comprehensive neuropsychological testing and functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI) at pre- and post-intervention. During the fMRI measurement cue-reactivity, stop-signal and n-back tasks were carried out with a 3T whole- body tomograph (MAGNETOM Prisma; Siemens, Erlangen, Germany). T2* weighted multiband echo planar images (mb-EPI) using a multiband acceleration factor 6 was acquired in a transversal orientation 20° clockwise to AC‐PC (anterior commissure - posterior commissure) line covering the whole brain (Repetition Time (TR)=869 ms, Echo Time (TE)=38 ms, 60 slices, slice thickness=2.4 mm, voxel size 2.4×2.4×2.4 mm, no interslice gap, field of view (FoV)=210 mm, matrix size 88×88, acquisition orientation T>C, interleaved slice order, acceleration factor slice=6, flip angle=58°, bandwidth=1832 Hz/Px, prescan normalise, weak raw data filter, LeakBlock kernel, fat sat). This short TE and the 20° flip to AC‐PC orientation is chosen to minimise susceptibility artefacts. In addition, a T1- weighted 3D MPRAGE (Magnetization Prepared— RApid Gradient Echo) dataset consisting of 208 sagittal slices (slice thickness 1 mm, 1×1×1 mm voxel size, FOV 256×256 mm2, TR=2000 ms, TE=2.01 ms, Inversion Time (TI)=800 ms, flip angle=8°) was acquired. Using the software package G*Power, the sample size calculation was conducted for the main primary outcomes, where we expected a minimum effect size of f=0.2 for all constructs (analyses of variance (ANOVA) with repeated measures, within- subject and between- subject factors and interactions). 

Results

Our results demonstrate that adjunctive Chess-Based Cognitive Remediation Therapy significantly enhanced treatment outcomes relative to the Treatment-As-Usual alone. Clinically, the Chess-Based Cognitive Remediation Therapy group demonstrated significantly reduced relapse rates in both Tobacco Use Disorder and Alcohol Use Disorder cohorts, as well as lower craving scores in the Tobacco Use Disorder group. Neuroimaging analyses revealed that Chess-Based Cognitive Remediation Therapy modulated key brain networks: it potentiated activity within the motor inhibition network, in Alcohol Use Disorder participants and led to a more efficient neural decrease (i.e., reduced resource expenditure) during a working memory task in Tobacco Use Disorder participants. Furthermore, Chess-Based Cognitive Remediation Therapy conferred additional benefits on cognitive and affective measures, enhancing cognitive flexibility in Tobacco Use Disorder, and self-reported life satisfaction and sustained attention in Alcohol Use Disorder.

Conclusion

These findings demonstrate that Chess-Based Cognitive Remediation Therapy is an effective adjunctive therapy for substance use disorder, producing meaningful clinical improvements in relapse and craving, alongside measurable enhancements in neural efficiency and cognitive-affective functioning. These multi-level benefits underscore its potential as a practical and engaging strategy to rebalance the impulsive-reflective system dichotomy in addiction. By targeting core neurocognitive mechanisms, Chess-Based Cognitive Remediation Therapy represents a valuable addition to standard treatment, with promising implications for long-term recovery. Future research should focus on elucidating the specific causal pathways linking neural changes to clinical outcomes.
