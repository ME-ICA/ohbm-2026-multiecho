Introduction

Functional connectomes (FCs) extracted from fMRI data are highly individual — each person’s FC pattern tends to be distinct, so that one can reliably identify a person based on their FC versus those of others. However, the neurobiological origins of this “fingerprint-like” uniqueness remain ambiguous. One hypothesis proposes that FC fingerprinting arises from consciousness. An alternative hypothesis argues that the uniqueness instead reflects a stable, intrinsic organization of brain networks that persists regardless of immediate cognitive content. To adjudicate between these ideas, we examined FC fingerprints across different sleep stages using a two-night concurrent EEG-fMRI dataset from healthy subjects. In doing so, we leveraged the natural variation in consciousness across the sleep-wake cycle — from wakefulness, through progressively deeper non-REM sleep (N1 → N3), during which consciousness gradually diminishes, and into REM sleep, when an altered consciousness partially emerges. If conscious experience drives the uniqueness of FCs, then the ability to correctly identify individuals should gradually decline as consciousness fades and then partially recover during REM sleep. Alternatively, if a person’s unique, intrinsic brain-network architecture is the main source of individual specificity, then FC fingerprinting performance should remain stable across all sleep stages.

Methods

Data were drawn from a previously published two-night EEG-fMRI sleep study (Fig. 1A),2 in which 12 healthy, non-sleep-deprived participants (mean age 24 ± 3.5, 8 female) slept in a scanner for two consecutive nights (8 h/night). Sleep staging was performed in 30-s epochs according to AASM criteria. We preprocessed the fMRI data with afni_proc, including RETROICOR and global signal regression (TR = 3 s). Analyses focus on Night 2 (Night 1 served as an adaptation night); all participants showed REM sleep, but one subject’s REM data were excluded due to excessive motion.

Rather than static connectivity, we used dynamic functional connectivity (DFC) estimated with a 90-second sliding window (30 TRs) moved in 30-s steps (10 TRs), corresponding to three successive sleep epochs with the same sleep stage.3 For each window, we computed full inter-regional correlation matrices with 302 ROIs, including 45,451 unique connections (edges). This set of edges will be referred to as “DFC vector” or “DFCV”.

For identification analysis, we followed the procedure of Finn et al. (2015), see Fig. 1B&C. We also measured “self-self” correlations (within-participant, diagonal elements in Fig. 2A) and “self-other” correlations (between participants, off-diagonal elements in Fig. 2A). Identification was successful when a participant’s self-self correlation exceeded all self-other correlations (Fig. 2B), with significance assessed via the rank-sum test. We also computed differential power (DP) to quantify how well each edge identifies individuals.

In addition, we employed a multilayer perceptron (MLP) to classify individuals based on a single 90-second DFCV rather than average correlations across DFCVs, using scikit-learn’s neural_network module. For each sleep stage, we trained the MLP with five-fold cross-validation, then tested it on the remaining four sleep stages and on all five stages from Night 1.

Results

Identification rates are higher when using N3 sleep episodes as the database compared to Wake. As shown in Fig. 2A, correlations among participants in the Night 2 data were elevated when N3 served as the database. Notably, self-other correlations were significantly elevated when using N3 (rank-sum test, p < 0.0001), indicating greater inter-participant similarity (Fig. 2B). Concurrently, the difference between self-self and self-other correlations was larger for N3 than for Wake (rank-sum test, p < 0.01), which is reflected in the higher identification rates observed during N3 (Fig. 2C). An additional analysis using Night 1 data as targets (with Night 2 as the database) confirmed a numerically higher identification rate for N3 relative to Wake, as well as higher rates for N2 and REM sleep stages compared to Wake. Next, we explored which cortical networks or subcortical regions contributed most to identification for each sleep stage. During N3 sleep, the hippocampus emerged as a key contributor: a pattern that was unique to this stage. We then trained MLP classifiers based on a single 90s DFCV separately for each sleep stage using five-fold cross-validation. The best parameters for models trained on both Wake and N3 were identical (α = 1, two hidden layers each of size 20). When tested on other stages and on Night 1 data, the model trained on N3 outperformed the one trained on Wake: average identification rates were 0.958 for Night 2 and 0.959 for Night 1.

Conclusion

Our results challenge the notion that consciousness is necessary for FC fingerprinting. In contrast, we observed that FC fingerprinting actually improved during both N3 and REM sleep compared with Wake, despite substantial differences in levels of consciousness. In deep sleep (N3), when consciousness is greatly diminished, participants’ connectivity patterns became more similar overall, yet, paradoxically, the ability to identify individuals increased. The functional connections that contributed most to this improved identification involved coupling between the hippocampus and unimodal sensory networks, a pattern not seen in other sleep stages, suggesting a reconfiguration of intrinsic brain-network organization during deep sleep. While prior studies of FC fingerprinting under anesthesia have yielded mixed findings, with some demonstrating reduced identifiability,4  and others preservation of individual signatures.5 Our study differs in that it takes advantage of naturally occurring fluctuations in consciousness across sleep stages and avoids the confounds introduced by using different anesthetic agents. Moreover, a multilayer perceptron trained on deep-sleep data was able to correctly classify individuals from brief (~90-second) fMRI segments, demonstrating that reliable individual identification can be achieved even with short measurement windows.
