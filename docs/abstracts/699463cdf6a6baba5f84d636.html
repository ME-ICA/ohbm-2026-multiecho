Introduction

Puberty marks a critical developmental period characterised by profound biological and neurocognitive changes. During this phase, the brain undergoes substantial reorganisation, particularly within white matter (WM) pathways that support large-scale communication across distributed neural systems.1 Prior work has shown that WM microstructure generally strengthens with advancing pubertal stage. However, far less is known about how the topological organisation of the WM connectome, its global integration and regional segregation changes during this transition. Normative developmental studies indicate a shift from more segregated, locally specialised networks in childhood toward increasingly integrated and efficient networks in adolescence and adulthood.2,3 This reconfiguration is reflected in reductions in modularity, alongside gains in global efficiency, enabling more economical communication across the brain. Such network-level refinement is thought to support the emergence of higher-order cognitive and emotional capacities.2 Despite these insights, the extent to which pubertal maturation specifically shapes WM network topology, and how these changes manifest at both global and nodal levels, remains poorly understood. This study aimed to understand the network and nodal level brain network changes in relation to pubertal development using graph theoretical analysis.

Methods

This study used data from the NeuroImaging of the Children’s Attention Project (NICAP), a longitudinal community-based cohort of children with and without ADHD in Melbourne, Australia.4 Participants completed up to three assessments between 9.5 and 14.5 years of age, spaced approximately 18 months apart. Pubertal stage at each timepoint was measured using the parent-reported Pubertal Development Scale (PDS), which captures development across five indices of pubertal maturation. For the present analyses, children with ADHD and those with MRI data failing quality control were excluded. The final sample included 91 healthy children (184 observations) aged 10.43 years at baseline (range: 9.58–11.86). 

T1-weighted anatomical MRI data were pre-processed and parcellated into 164 regions (148 cortical, 14 subcortical, 2 cerebellar regions) using the Desikan–Killiany atlas in FreeSurfer. These parcellations were converted to MRtrix3 format to define network nodes. Diffusion-weighted images were pre-processed using MRtrix35 and FSL. Fibre orientation distributions were estimated using single-shell 3-tissue constrained spherical deconvolution, and anatomically constrained tractography was performed to generate 20 million streamlines. SIFT2 was applied to enhance biological accuracy of streamline counts. Structural connectivity matrices were constructed as weighted, undirected graphs. Network analyses were performed in GRETNA, and network visualisations were produced using BrainNet Viewer. Four global (global efficiency, normalised clustering coefficient, normalised shortest path length, and assortativity) and four nodal metrics (nodal efficiency, clustering coefficient, degree centrality, and betweenness centrality) were computed across thresholds from 0.05 to 0.50 (step 0.05). The area under the curve (AUC) for each metric was extracted for statistical modelling. 

Given evidence for nonlinear developmental effects, generalised additive mixed models (GAMMs) were fitted in R using each graph metric as the dependent variable. Models included smooth terms for age and PDS and a random intercept for participant ID to account for repeated measures. Analyses were conducted separately in males and females to capture sex-specific developmental effects. Nodal results were corrected for multiple comparisons using FDR correction.

Results

Global metrics: In males (Figure 1a), pubertal stage showed a positive association with global efficiency (edf = 1.00, F = 4.21, p = .04) and a negative association with average clustering coefficient (edf = 1.81, F = 6.13, p = .002). In females (Figure 1b), only clustering coefficient showed a significant negative association with pubertal stage (edf = 1.87, F = 3.40, p = .03), suggesting sex-specific developmental trajectories. 

Nodal metrics: In males,  pubertal stage exhibited significant nonlinear associations across nodal metrics. Eleven nodes (predominantly left-hemisphere) showed effects in betweenness centrality, while widespread bilateral associations were observed for degree centrality, clustering coefficient, and nodal efficiency. However, after FDR correction, only the left angular gyrus remained significant for degree centrality (Figure 1c). In females, pubertal stage demonstrated widespread bilateral nonlinear associations across all nodal metrics. Following FDR correction, only left transverse temporal region remained significant for degree centrality, whereas 62 nodes retained significant associations in clustering coefficient (Figure 1d).

Conclusion

The present study provides new longitudinal evidence that pubertal maturation contributes uniquely to the reorganisation of large-scale white matter network topology, beyond age-related developmental effects. In males, higher pubertal stage was associated with greater global efficiency and lower clustering coefficient, reflecting increasing network integration alongside reduced local segregation, a pattern consistent with models proposing enhanced long-range communication during adolescence. At the nodal level, pubertal stage was associated with nonlinear changes in nodal topology in both sexes, with more extensive effects observed in females after correction for multiple comparisons. The persistence of these associations after accounting for age underscores puberty as a distinct biological process contributing to white matter network maturation. Together, the results highlight puberty as an important driver of structural network reorganisation during early adolescence, complementing normative age-related brain development.
