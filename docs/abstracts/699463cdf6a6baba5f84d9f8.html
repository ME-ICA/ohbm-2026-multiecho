Introduction

The diagnosis of dementia remains challenging due to overlapping clinical and pathological features, particularly in early stages¹,². Advances in neuroimaging and fluid biomarkers have progressively shifted diagnostic frameworks toward biologically based definitions³–⁶. Among neuroimaging modalities, structural MRI and [¹⁸F]FDG-PET are the main indicators of neurodegeneration. While visual interpretation remains the clinical standard, quantitative approaches may enhance objectivity, reproducibility, and standardization, yet their added value in practice remain debated. This study tested the hypothesis that combining metabolic and structural information from simultaneous FDG-PET/MRI, analyzed both visually and quantitatively, could improve the differentiation of major dementia syndromes: Alzheimer’s disease (AD), frontotemporal dementia (FTD), and Lewy body dementia (LBD).

Methods

Subjects
 Two-hundred-seventy-four patients, diagnosed according to consensus criteria, were recruited in monocentric SOCRATES cohort at tertiary memory clinic, comprising 206 patients with AD (174 amnestic AD, 19 logopenic variant primary progressive aphasia (lvPPA), 13 posterior cortical atrophy (PCA)), 44 with FTD (21 behavioral variant FTD (bvFTD), 16 semantic dementia (SD), 7 nonfluent variant PPA (nfvPPA)), and 24 with LBD.

Image Acquisition
 All subjects underwent simultaneous FDG-PET/MRI on an integrated 3T GE Signa system including 3D T1-weighted sequence for morphometry and FDG-PET for metabolic assessment.

Visual Analysis
 Two neuroradiologists independently performed blinded qualitative reading of FDG-PET and T1-weighted MRI. Patterns of hypometabolism and atrophy were evaluated across 46 regions of interest (ROIs) consistent with standard clinical practice reflecting functional brain regions. Each ROI was rated on a three-point scale: 0=normal, 1=mildly decreased, 2=severely decreased.

Quantitative Analysis
 Quantitative processing of T1-weighted MRI and FDG-PET was performed using an inhouse pipeline combining BrainVisa and SPM 12 (Figure 1). A custom atlas was generated via a Python script merging regions from the Automated Anatomical Labeling Atlas version 3 (AAL3) within SPM12 for ROIs to match those used in visual analysis. Mean gray matter (GM) volume and normalized FDG uptake were extracted for each ROI after intersecting with GM masks.

Statistical Analysis
 Candidate ROIs were first selected using univariate analyses. Multivariate multiblock modeling was then performed using DIABLO framework from the mixOmics R package⁷, with three components per model. Model performance was assessed by repeated five-fold cross-validation (100 iterations), using AUC values as the main metric. Three model configurations were tested: (1) four-block including each modality/approach separately, (2) two-block combining modalities, and (3) two-block combining approaches. The most discriminant ROIs were identified from component loadings contributing to group separation. 

Results

Both quantitative and visual metabolic components strongly contributed to group separation, primarily driving the distinction of FTD from other groups, while AD and LBD showed additional regional differences; volumetric blocks contributed less overall&(Figure 2). The multiblock model revealed that metabolic features were the main discriminants between syndromes (range AUC=0.86[95% CI:0.84-0.88] to AUC=0.96[95% CI :0.95-0.97]). FTD was characterized by alterations in the temporal pole, anterior cingulate, and caudate, whereas AD and LBD showed distinct patterns in posterior cingulate, temporal mesial, and occipital-parietal regions. Among all modalities, visual FDG-PET achieved the highest classification performance, particularly for FTD (AUC=0.96[95%CI:0.95-0.97]). Quantitative metabolic data also showed slightly lower discriminatory power, though non-significatively (AUC=0.95[95%CI :0.95-0.96]). Volume-based analyses demonstrated lower discriminative power comparatively to FGD-PET, while being significatively superior for visual analysis vs quantitative for AD and FTD (AUC=0.79 [95%CI:0.77–0.81] and AUC=0.89 [95%CI:0.88–0.91] vs AUC=0.68[95%CI:0.65–0.71] and AUC=0.81[95% CI:0.78–0.83]). Combining metabolic and volumetric data improved performance compared to volume alone, particularly in quantitative models especially for AD and LBD (AUC=0.73[[95%CI:0.71–0.75] vs AUC=0.68[95% CI:0.65–0.71] and AUC=0.80[95%CI:0.78–0.82] vs AUC=0.67[95% CI:0.62–0.71] ), but offered modest benefit over metabolic imaging alone for FTD.

Conclusion

Metabolic imaging provided the strongest discrimination among dementias, confirming its central diagnostic role. Structural data contributed less but retained diagnostic value, especially in visual readings with atrophy patterns remained disease-specific. Across modalities, visual interpretation outperformed quantitative analysis, particularly for FTD, demonstrating the strength of expert evaluation. Quantitative metrics, offered standardized measures that improved model robustness and may facilitate harmonization across centers. The use of identical ROIs for both visual and quantitative analyses allowed a direct comparison between approaches within the same anatomical framework. This showed that modalities and reading methods revealed complementary regional information, with metabolism reflecting function and volume structure, supporting the benefit of combining both approaches.

Visual assessment remains the most accurate and clinically interpretable method for differentiating major dementias, while quantitative analysis adds objective and standardized support. Using a shared regional framework confirmed their complementarity, and integrating both in PET/MR workflows combines expert interpretation with quantitative precision for consistent, clinically meaningful dementia imaging.
