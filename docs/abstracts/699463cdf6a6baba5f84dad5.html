Introduction

Quantitative MRI (qMRI) has the potential to provide reproducible imaging biomarkers, yet scanner and site variability still limit data comparability across research and clinical studies. Addressing this variability requires calibration standards, harmonised Quality Assurance/Quality Control (QA/QC) procedures and coordinated technical expertise supported by automated analysis pipelines. In Australia, the National Imaging Facility (NIF) represents the peak, national, advanced biomedical imaging capability across a network of partner research and clinical sites. To pursue harmonisation at the national level, a NIF supported initiative is being undertaken to develop consistent protocols and analysis pipelines.

Methods

As part of a national initiative supported by the NIF, a Gold Standard Phantoms’ Spirit phantom1 was distributed to each imaging site across Australia. Each phantom contains NIST-certified solutions from the same batch to ensure uniform reference materials. This includes vials for relaxometry and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) mapping, plus two nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) tubes of ethylene glycol (CH₂OH)₂. Relaxometry vials hold milli-molar manganese chloride (MnCl₂) in deionised water for quantitative T₁ and T₂ mapping, while ADC vials combine MnCl₂ with polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) to modulate viscosity and water diffusivity. The ethylene glycol tubes yield a well-characterised, temperature-dependent NMR signal, providing an internal calibration reference. Figure 1 depicts a photo of the phantom1 and an ADC diffusion map and T1 MPRAGE of the phantom with roughly equivalent slices. Standard operating procedures for reproducible phantom positioning, cross-vendor optimisation, automated analysis pipelines, and standardised performance reporting against NIST-traceable reference values are being established interoperable for each site and specific instrument being used.

Results

This collaboration between researchers across Australia is developing a framework for qMRI QA/QC using the Spirit phantom as a reference. This framework will be accessible to NIF nodes across Australia, as automated analysis pipelines will be incorporated as XNAT2 containers to be operated against acquired MR data. Automated reporting includes generation of a report detailing the relaxation and ADC fits, error bounds for the fits and comparison to the NIST values for each vial. A secondary pipeline is being developed that will aggregate these data and reports across all participating sites for long term metrological purposes.

Conclusion

We present current progress, outline the next stages of development, and discuss how this approach will facilitate long-term standardisation of qMRI practice to enhance data comparability and reproducibility of imaging biomarkers in research and clinical settings.
