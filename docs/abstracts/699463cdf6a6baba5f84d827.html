Introduction

Language is a defining human capacity that supports communication, thought, and identity. Early lesion studies established that speech production and comprehension rely on specific left-hemisphere regions. Modern neuroimaging has expanded this classical model, revealing a left-lateralized fronto-temporo-parietal network. Dual-stream frameworks further distinguish dorsal pathways for phonological–articulatory processing and ventral pathways for semantic comprehension^1. Phonological encoding, ie transforming abstract word forms into articulatory plans, is central to language production, inner speech, and verbal working memory. It consistently engages a left-dominant network encompassing Broca’s and Wernicke’s areas, supplementary motor, and parietal cortices during tasks such as reading, naming, and rhyme judgments^2.

Understanding these functions also requires examining their anatomical substrates. Cortical folding patterns, particularly sulcal depth, have emerged as predictors of functional specialization, reflecting early genetically guided programs described by the protomap model. Concepts such as sulcal roots and pits capture folding units whose depths correlate with sensory and higher-order cognitive functions. Recent work shows that even tertiary sulci relate to individual differences in abilities such as working memory and reasoning, suggesting sulcal depth is a generalizable marker of structure–function organization^3,4.

However, whether these relationships extend to language, and specifically to phonological encoding, remains unknown. The present study integrates structural and functional imaging to test whether sulcal depth correlates with brain activation during a rhyming judgment task. This cross-modal approach aims to clarify how genetically anchored cortical morphology shapes individual variability in language processing and informs mechanisms of vulnerability or resilience in language-related disorders.

Methods

Thirty-two healthy adults performed a rhyming judgment task during fMRI. Functional data were analyzed in FSL using general linear models to identify task-related activations. Anatomical T1-weighted images were processed with FreeSurfer to extract vertex-wise sulcal depth and other cortical metrics.

The group-level activation map for the contrast of interest was projected onto FreeSurfer surface space, and only vertices showing significant activation were retained for correlation analysis. A vertex-wise correlation was then computed between beta values (brain activity) and sulcal depth (Sulc values).

Statistical significance was assessed with permutation testing using a cluster-based correction (initial threshold p < 0.001, 1000 permutations). Only clusters larger than five contiguous vertices were considered, ensuring robust results

Results

Functional activation associated with phonological encoding showed the expected pattern: robust engagement of the left inferior frontal, precentral, postcentral, supramarginal and superior temporal cortices, as well as the supplementary motor area, alongside bilateral activity in the middle cingulate and additional left cerebellar and calcarine regions as well as in the basal ganglia (figure 1a). The cross-modal correlation analysis revealed that sulcal depth was significantly and selectively associated with task-related activation in a circumscribed cluster within the left inferior frontal gyrus, pars opercularis, a core component of Broca’s area (figure 1b). No other classical structural measures (thickness, surface area, volume) showed significant correlations with neural activity. 

Conclusion

These findings provide novel evidence that the structural basis of phonological encoding is rooted in early-developing cortical folding patterns. The task elicited robust activation across the phonological network, yet the structural–functional association emerged only in the pars opercularis, a key subregion of Broca’s area. That this region, central to speech production, was the only one showing a significant link with sulcal depth, a feature shaped early in neurodevelopment, supports the idea that core linguistic abilities may be partly genetically predetermined. 

These results align with theories proposing that cortical expansion and folding contribute to high-level cognitive functions and highlight how prenatal developmental processes can influence adult language abilities. By correlating activation with sulcal depth, we show that anatomical–functional coupling can be detected even in complex cognitive domains such as language.
