Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a prevalent and debilitating psychiatric condition associated with aberrant functional network organization, as measured by resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI). The growing availability of large-scale rs-fMRI datasets has enabled the development of deep-learning architectures for diagnosing MDD and predicting its severity. Among them, the graph convolutional networks (GCNs) have gained extensive attention due to their ability to incorporate spatial information into models. However, when constructed from multi-site datasets, GCN models face substantial challenges from inter-site variability, which limits adaptability to local datasets under the conventional pooled-data training approach. Accordingly, we proposed a novel regression framework that integrates domain adaptation during training to predict depressive severities across heterogeneous imaging sites.

Methods

We utilized 16-site rs-fMRI data from the Rest-meta-MDD dataset, comprising 835 MDD patients, with data parcellated by the Dosenbach atlas. Functional connectivity graphs derived from rs-fMRI were used as model inputs, with a GCN as the base learner2. We adopted a model-agnostic meta-learning framework1 to explicitly model the heterogeneity inherent in multi-site data by treating each imaging site as an independent learning task. During meta-training, 12 sites were used to construct episodic tasks. In each episode, one site was randomly sampled to form a 5-subject support set and a non-overlapping 10-subject query set. Site-specific adaptation and cross-site generalization were optimized on the support and query sets, respectively. Meta-gradients accumulated across 20 episodes were used to update model parameters per meta-iteration, with this process repeated for 100 meta-iterations. During meta-testing, 4 held-out sites were evaluated independently: model was first adapted using 5 fixed support samples, then evaluated on the remaining subjects. Model performance was assessed using root mean square error (RMSE) and Pearson correlation between predicted and observed HAM-D scores. The model demonstrating the lowest averaged RMSE and highest correlation across 30 independent runs was selected for external validation on an in-house dataset of 110 patients with late-life depression. We compared meta-learning performance against a transfer-learning baseline using paired t-test. For external validation, the in-house dataset was split into meta-validation (80%) and meta-testing (20%) subsets, where 20 samples from validation subsets were randomly selected for model adaptation. This splitting procedure was repeated 30 times to ensure robust model comparison.

Results

Figure 2 shows that meta-learning achieved a significantly lower RMSE (6.56 ± 0.45 ) compared to transfer learning (7.24 ± 1.33, p = 0.005 ), along with higher Pearson correlation (0.11 ± 0.19 vs. 0.01 ± 0.20, p = 0.023). Notably, meta-learning models exhibited reduced RMSE variance, highlighting their improved reliability under cross-site domain shifts.

Conclusion

When validated on an external dataset, meta-learning models achieved more accurate and reliable severity predictions than transfer-learning models. These findings highlight meta-learning as a promising paradigm for neuroimaging-based clinical modeling under realistic small-sample scenarios.
