Introduction

Attention is a fundamental component of cognition, and its variability is closely linked to learning efficiency, executive functioning, and a wide range of neuropsychiatric conditions.^1 EEG is widely used to investigate neural dynamics associated with attention. At the same time, Graph Signal Processing (GSP) provides a framework for graph-spectral signal analysis that embeds the brain's connectivity topology into the computation, thereby enabling the extraction of graph-domain features that traditional time–frequency methods cannot adequately characterise.^2 Although GSP has shown potential in describing network-level dynamics from resting-state EEG, resting data alone may not fully reveal the task-related neural mechanisms that directly support attentional processing. The symbol search task is susceptible to individual differences in selective attention and processing speed, and the behavioural distinction between correct and error trials offers valuable information for examining network changes across different attention states.^3 However, global dynamical brain network features from multi-state EEG have not been sufficiently explored in the context of attention research. In this study, we analysed EEG data collected during the resting state, together with EEG recorded during correct and error-symbol search trials, enabling a direct comparison between intrinsic neural activity under varying attentional demands. We extracted a comprehensive set of GSP-based graph spectral features across these multi-state conditions. We systematically compared feature groups across states, assessing both statistical differences and their ability to discriminate between high- and low-attention participants using classification analyses. We further examined their associations with attentional performance to provide a more comprehensive characterisation of the graph spectral features that reflect individual differences in attention.

Methods

EEG data were obtained from the HBN-11 dataset^4, which contains recordings from 430 participants aged 5 to 22 years with a balanced distribution of males and females. From this cohort, 84 participants with valid EEG segments in all three states, including resting state, correct symbol search trials, and error symbol search trials, were retained for analysis to ensure comparable data across conditions. Attention performance was measured using the Attention Problems subscale of the CBCL questionnaire, which provides a validated index of attentional functioning. Participants within the highest 25 percent and lowest 25 percent of the CBCL score distribution were categorised as low-attention and high-attention groups, respectively, resulting in 21 participants in each group. As illustrated in Figure 1, EEG recordings were first band-pass filtered and processed using automated artefact rejection to remove segments contaminated by noise. The cleaned data were then segmented into epochs corresponding to resting and task conditions and decomposed into five canonical frequency bands: δ (0.5–4 Hz), θ (4–8 Hz), α (8–13 Hz), β (13–30 Hz), and γ (30–45 Hz). For each participant, each EEG state, and each frequency band, band-limited EEG amplitudes across all N channels were extracted to form multichannel time series, which served as inputs for connectivity estimation and graph-based analysis. Functional connectivity matrices were computed using standard Pearson correlation between all pairs of EEG channels, separately for each participant, each state, and each frequency band. This procedure yielded, for every condition, a symmetric matrix describing the strength of statistical association between channels. To construct a sparse functional brain graph, the absolute correlation values were ranked and only the strongest proportion of connections (top 30 percent) was retained as edges in the adjacency matrix, while weaker connections were set to zero. For each state and frequency band, the EEG amplitudes across all channels were treated as a graph signal, which was projected onto this basis to obtain a graph-spectral representation. From these projections, spectral energy at each graph frequency was computed and normalised across frequencies to form a spectral energy distribution. Based on these graph signal representations, five GSP-derived features were extracted for each participant, EEG state, and frequency band: (1) Graph Smoothness (SM), which quantifies how gradually the signal varies across connected nodes, with higher values indicating stronger variation between neighbouring channels; (2) Total Variation (TV), which reflects the overall roughness of the signal relative to the connectivity structure by aggregating differences in amplitude across all connected node pairs; (3) Spectral Entropy (SE), which is computed from the normalised spectral energy distribution using Shannon entropy and measures how widely energy is distributed across graph frequencies, with higher values indicating a more dispersed and complex spectral profile; (4) Degree-Centrality-Weighted Amplitude (DC), which summarises the contribution of highly connected nodes by weighting signal amplitudes according to their node degree; and (5) Graph Bandwidth (BW), which captures the highest graph frequency that retains non-negligible spectral energy and thus reflects the upper bound of spatial detail present in the graph signal. For each participant and each state, the five GSP features computed across the five frequency bands were assembled into a 5 × 5 feature matrix, yielding FR, FC, and FE for the resting, correct, and error conditions. These three matrices were then stacked into a 3 × 5 × 5 representation, which was vectorised and used for classification analyses comparing high- and low-attention groups.

Results

Figure 2 presents the experimental results based on multi-state GSP-derived EEG features for attention analysis. Figure 2(A) shows the ten-fold cross-validation performance of the SVM classifier across different feature configurations. Using single-state features, namely resting (FR), correct (FC), or error (FE), produced accuracies of approximately 0.655, 0.595, and 0.665, respectively. When feature sets were combined, the classification performance improved substantially. For example, FR + FC achieved an accuracy of 0.735, FC + FE reached 0.785, and the combination of all three states (FR + FC + FE) performed best with an accuracy of 0.860. Relative to the single-state inputs, the multi-state combinations improved accuracy by roughly 0.195 to 0.265, demonstrating that the three EEG states capture complementary neural information. Significantly, the multi-state inputs also consistently enhanced precision, recall, and F1-score, suggesting that integrating information from both resting-state and task-evoked neural dynamics produces more stable and discriminative behavioural signatures. These results together highlight that attention is not captured solely by baseline neural functioning or by isolated task responses, but instead requires considering how the brain transitions across distinct cognitive states. Building on these classification results, we further examined how individual GSP-derived features varied across groups and conditions. Figure 2(B) displays violin plots of SE across the five canonical frequency bands, revealing characteristic differences in spectral energy distribution between high- and low-attention participants. SE reflects the complexity and diversity of neural activity fluctuations within each band. Although differences appear modest in both the resting state and the correct trial condition, more precise and more systematic distinctions emerge during error trials, particularly within the alpha band. This heightened separation during errors suggests that SE becomes more sensitive when attentional control is disrupted or challenged. The pattern also aligns with well-established findings that alpha-band activity is tightly linked to attentional modulation, cognitive resource allocation, and the regulation of sensory inhibition.  To further characterise spatial patterns associated with attentional differences, Figure 2(C) presents alpha-band topographic difference maps (low minus high) across the resting, correct, and error states, computed using DC. In the resting state, differences are relatively modest and primarily localised to occipital regions, showing no clear spatial organisation. During correct trials, these differences broaden into posterior parietal regions, suggesting a shift in attentional resource allocation, although the spatial structure remains somewhat diffuse. A more structured and pronounced distribution emerges during error trials, in which the most considerable low-minus-high differences occur over frontal regions, forming a spatially coherent pattern that reflects increased frontal participation as attentional demands escalate. This frontal alpha modulation is consistent with alpha-ERD theory^5, which proposes that reductions in alpha activity signal heightened cognitive effort and engagement. Overall, the differences in the graph-spectral features derived from DC indicate that the distinction between high- and low-attention participants becomes most apparent during search failures, and that using resting-state features as a reference further enhances this separation. These findings highlight the potential of multi-state GSP features for distinguishing attentional performance.

Conclusion

This study investigated the associations between attention performance and GSP features derived from multi-state EEG, demonstrating that these features provide meaningful discrimination between high- and low-attention participants. Multi-state feature combinations showed stronger discriminative ability than any single-state input, and the most pronounced group differences emerged in the error condition, particularly in alpha-band spectral entropy and corresponding topographic patterns, revealing clear divergences in the neural dynamics associated with attention performance. These findings highlight that attention is not represented by a single neural state but rather emerges from the interplay between resting baseline activity, successful task processing, and moments of performance breakdown, all of which contribute complementary information to GSP-based representations. Several limitations should be noted. The final number of included participants was relatively small, requiring validation in larger cohorts to establish the robustness and generalisability of the findings. Additionally, several individual factors were not incorporated into the analysis, including genetic or clinical background, medication history, and behavioural indicators such as symbol search response speed or task engagement metrics. These factors may modulate the neural signatures of attention and could account for variance not captured in the present model. Another methodological limitation concerns the imbalance between correct and error trial courts across participants, which may bias the estimation of state-specific features and classification results. Future work would benefit from improved trial balancing, stratified sampling strategies, or statistical correction methods to address this issue. Moreover, the current analysis focused exclusively on static GSP features. Incorporating dynamic graph-based metrics, cross-frequency interactions, or connectivity perturbation models may reveal additional dimensions of neural organisation relevant to attention. Despite these limitations, the study demonstrates the utility of GSP features as a principled framework for representing EEG signals in a manner that integrates both spectral and spatial structure. By embedding brain network topology into the analysis, GSP enables the extraction of features that capture how neural signals propagate and reorganise across cognitive states, offering insights beyond those provided by conventional time–frequency measures. This approach holds particular promise for understanding attentional variability, as it highlights state-dependent signatures that may otherwise remain obscured. More broadly, the sensitivity of GSP-based features to attentional differences suggests potential applications in the early identification and auxiliary diagnosis of attention-related disorders such as ADHD, where disrupted network organisation and atypical spectral patterns are consistently reported. Future studies applying multi-state GSP analysis to clinical populations may help elucidate mechanistic pathways underlying attentional impairments and support the development of more precise, network-informed diagnostic tools.
