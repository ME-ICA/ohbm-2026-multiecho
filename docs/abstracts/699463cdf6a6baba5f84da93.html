Introduction

Multimodal data fusion is a powerful technique for extracting shared and complementary information about the brain that is captured across neuroimaging modalities. Independent component analysis (ICA)-based approaches are among the most widely utilized methods for multimodal fusion, as they are data-driven, robust to noise, and capable of identifying complex, hidden linkages of varying strengths across high-dimensional datasets. However, the data-driven nature of ICA fusion approaches can make comparisons across analyses difficult. In this work, we utilize resting state functional MRI (rsfMRI) and structural MRI (sMRI) scans from >15,000 subjects to generate a multimodal structure-function template that can be used to guide ICA fusions of new datasets. 

Methods

We utilize rsfMRI data as well as sMRI to extract static functional network connectivity (sFNC) and gray matter volume (GMV) maps from 16,530 healthy control subjects from UKBiobank to generate the template. We iterated over 5 sample sizes and 11 model orders to identify an optimal set of joint sources that were reproducible across batches. For each sample size in [N = 1000, 2000, 3000, 4000, 5000] we randomly sampled without replacement from the unaffected subjects to generate two non-overlapping batches of size N. We then applied joint ICA1 to each batch at each sample size across model orders [C = 5, 8, 10, 12, 15, 18, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40] for a total of 110 joint fusions. Joint sources were matched across batches for each sample size and model order using a greedy matching approach on the average Pearson correlation of spatial maps for the FNC and GMV components. For validation, we applied the template via MOO-ICAR2,3 in three datasets not used for training: UKB Batch3 (N= 5000), HCP (N = 833), and A4 (N = 1099) (Fig 1B).

Results

We found the 5K sample and model order = 30 components yielded good  cross-batch correspondence (r = 0.64-0.94, r-mean = 0.83) and IC specificity, and was thus selected as the multimodal template (Fig 1A). Validation in UKB Batch3 shows strong correspondence of estimated ICs with the template and across subsets, even at small sample sizes (r = 0.78 at N = 100 subjects;  r > 0.91 N >= 250 subjects) (Fig 2A). Validation in novel cohorts from HCP and A4 datasets showed similarly high correspondence to the template, (r = 0.81, 0.95, respectively) and repeated scan experiments in HCP showed near-perfect pairwise correspondence (r > 0.99) of group level component maps in the same subjects (Fig 2B). Using linear models, we identified 29/30 ICs in UKB Batch 3, 14/30 ICs in HCP, and 23/30 ICs in A4 with significant (pFDR < 0.05) associations of loadings with age (adjusted for sex) (Fig 2C). In an age-matched case-control sample in the A4 dataset (N = 340 amyloid elevated, N = 340 amyloid not elevated), we found 2 ICs (11 & 17) with significant (pFDR < 0.05) associations of loadings to the Mini Mental State Exam (MMSE) total score, and 1 IC (21) with significant group differences in loadings.

Conclusion

We present the first multimodal template for guided FNC + GMV fusion. When estimating a higher number of joint components via blind ICA, larger sample sizes showed substantially improved replicability. By using a guided approach, we show this template can boost performance and increase the robustness of estimating higher numbers of joint components from lower sample sizes. When applying this template in three datasets, resultant components exhibit high correspondence to the template even in small sample sizes, and subject-level loadings from template-derived ICs show significant associations with age and cognitive scores, highlighting their biological and clinical relevance.
