Introduction

Adolescence represents a critical neurodevelopmental window characterized by heightened sensitivity to peer influence and significant challenges in emotion regulation, coinciding with the onset of most internalizing psychiatric disorders.^1 Despite this, the mechanisms through which social information shapes emotion regulation across development remain poorly understood. This gap is particularly salient given that standard clinical interventions are often less effective during adolescence, underscoring the need to identify developmentally sensitive modulators of regulatory control.

In this study, we examined whether peer-derived social information modulates cognitive reappraisal in a developmental cohort comparing children (9-12 years) and adolescents (13-16 years). Participants completed an fMRI-based reappraisal task with two key conditions: social reappraisal, in which regulation was preceded by a peer-informed auditory reappraisal cue in addition to a written instruction, and non-social reappraisal, in which the identical instruction was presented via text alone. 

Our objective was to characterize the neural substrates of social emotion regulation across development. We hypothesized that adolescents would exhibit a greater benefit from social reappraisal compared to children, reflecting their heightened social sensitivity. At the neural level, we further hypothesized that this benefit would be associated with increased engagement of prefrontal regulatory regions and social processing networks during social reappraisal in adolescents.^2 Together, this work aims to clarify how social information modulates emotion regulation circuitry during a key neurodevelopmental window. 

Methods

Participants: A total of 126 right-handed, Dutch-speaking participants were recruited: 72 children (9–12 years old) and 54 young adolescents (13–16 years old). Participants were screened via telephone for prior psychiatric conditions, medication use, and standard MRI contraindications. Written consent was provided by legal guardians and/or participants, depending on age. Participants received 40 Euros compensation. The study was approved by the local ethics committee (METC Leiden Den Haag Delft; NL80802.058.22). 

General Procedure: Before the session, participants were randomly assigned to either the social group or the non-social group. All MRI data were acquired on a Philips Achieva 3T MRI scanner at the LIBC in Leiden, following mock scanner training. The emotion regulation task followed a fear-conditioning task not reported here. After scanning, participants completed standardized questionnaires assessing pubertal development, peer influence, psychopathology symptoms, early-life stress, resilience, sleep quality, and IQ. Legal guardians provided additional reports on life events, socioeconomic status, and sleep quality. Saliva samples were collected to index pubertal development.

Emotion Regulation Task: Participants performed a cognitive reappraisal paradigm comprising 54 trials (View Negative, View Neutral, Reappraise Negative) using DAPS-selected images. On a view trial, participants were instructed to “look at the picture” and on a reappraisal trial they were instructed to “try to think of this as just a picture”.^3 Trial order was pseudorandomized with no more than two consecutive trials of the same type. The core manipulation occurred during the 5-second instruction phase: the social group received a pre-recorded auditory reappraisal cue from an age-matched peer in addition to written instruction, whereas the non-social group received written instructions only. Participants were informed that the auditory cue was provided by a peer to support them during the session. This design isolates the contribution of social information to reappraisal-related neural engagement while holding reappraisal content constant. Regulation difficulty was assessed using subjective difficulty ratings and skin conductance responses.

Data Acquisition: A high-resolution T1-weighted anatomical scan was acquired (1.1 mm isotropic) using a gradient echo sequence. Functional data were obtained using a T2*-weighted EPI sequence (TR=2200 ms; TE=30 ms; 40 slices; 2.75 mm isotropic). Field maps were acquired to correct for susceptibility distortions, and a resting-state scan was collected at the end of the session.

Preprocessing: Imaging data were preprocessed using fMRIPrep (mapped to MNI152NLin2009cAsym) and re-sampled to 2 mm isotropic voxels. Functional images were smoothed with a 6 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel (SPM12). Applying exclusion criteria for motion and data quality resulted in complete fMRI data for 92 participants.

fMRI Analysis: Instruction, audio, image presentation and rating period of each trial were modeled as boxcar regressors convolved with a canonical HRF. Separate regressors were made for each task condition (Reappraise Negative, View Negative, View Neutral). Six motion parameters and spike regressors were included as nuisance covariates. Group-level analysis employed an SPM full-factorial model with age group (children, adolescents) and condition (social, non-social) as factors. Analyses focused on a priori defined ROIs derived from the Schaefer and Melbourne atlases, including regions implicated in emotion regulation and social cognition (amygdala, thalamus, hippocampus, anterior insula, PCC, ACC, dorsomedial, ventromedial, ventrolateral and dorsolateral PFC). Statistical significance was assessed using small-volume FWE correction at p<.05.

Results

Behavioral Data: A 2 x 2 x 2 repeated measures ANOVA on difficulty ratings (trial type x social condition x age group) revealed a significant main effect of trial type (F(1, 105) = 11.22, p < 0.001) and a significant age group x trial type interaction (F(1, 105) = 9.39, p = 0.003). Neither the main effect of age (F(1, 105) = 1.91, p = 0.170) nor any interaction involving the social condition factor reached significance. Follow-up analysis of the age group x trial type interaction indicated divergent patterns across development: adolescents rated reappraisal trials as significantly more difficult than the view trials (t = -4.06, p < 0.001, df = 50), whereas children showed no significant difference between trial types (t = -0.395, p = 0.694, df = 57). These results indicate greater perceived regulatory effort in adolescents, while children's difficulty ratings are generally undifferentiated across task demands.

fMRI Data: To establish general task-related activation, we first examined the neural response to viewing negative images (View Negative > View Neutral), which elicited significant responses across a broad affective and cognitive network, including the ACC (MNI: 10, 22, 38; Z=3.78, p=.025), dlPFC (MNI: 56, 26, 24; Z=4.41, p=.005), vmPFC (MNI: 8, 54, 20; Z=4.64, p=.002), dmPFC (MNI: 8, 54, 26; Z=5.31, p=.000), vlPFC (MNI: 58, 26, 20; Z=4.3, p=.003), amygdala (MNI: 26, -2, -18; Z=5.95, p=.000), caudate (MNI: 14, 4, 10; Z=4.97, p=.000), hippocampus (MNI: -32, -18, -12; Z=4.46, p=.001), nucleus accumbens (MNI: 18, 16, -6; Z=4.34, p=.001), putamen (MNI: 20, 2, 4; Z=5.08, p=.000), and thalamus (MNI: 4, -6, 6; Z=5.18, p=.000). The general reappraisal contrast (Reappraise Negative > View Negative) did not yield greater activation in predefined ROIs. Conversely, the inverse contrast (View Negative > Reappraise Negative) revealed greater activity in the NAc (MNI: -6, 10, -2; Z=3.14, p=.04). 

Examining social and developmental effects during passive viewing of negative images, the nonsocial group showed greater amygdala activation than the social group (MNI: -30, 2, -16; Z=3.53, p=.016). Additionally, children exhibited greater amygdala activity than adolescents (MNI: 24, -6, -26; Z=3.16, p=.05), consistent with prior developmental findings.^3 No significant age group x social condition interaction was observed during passive viewing. 

During reappraisal, the social condition elicited greater activation than the nonsocial condition across several social and executive regions, including the ACC (MNI: -8, 26, 44; Z=4.46, p=.005), PCC (MNI: 2, -28, 36; Z=3.37, p=.042), mPFC (MNI: 4, 50, 6; Z=3.59, p=.031), and dmPFC (MNI: -14, 32, 54; Z=3.78, p=.035). No regions showed greater activation for the nonsocial relative to the social condition. Although no main effect of age group was observed during reappraisal, a significant age group x social condition interaction emerged in the hippocampus (MNI: 26, -28, -14; Z=3.58, p=.026). Follow-up analysis indicated that adolescents - but not children - exhibited greater activation in ACC (MNI: -8, 24, 46; Z=4.15, p=.007) and dmPFC (MNI: -8, 30, 54; Z=3.92, p=.021) during social compared to nonsocial reappraisal, suggesting enhanced recruitment of regulatory and social-cognitive regions in response to peer-derived cues. 

Conclusion

This study provides evidence that social context and neurodevelopmental stage jointly modulate the neural systems engaged during cognitive emotion regulation. By directly comparing children and adolescents, and isolating the contribution of peer-derived social information, we demonstrate that the presence of social cues alters neural engagement during both affective processing and reappraisal, with distinct developmental profiles.

At the behavioral level, adolescents—but not children—reported greater subjective difficulty during reappraisal relative to passive viewing. This finding indicates a developmental divergence in perceived regulatory effort, suggesting that adolescents experience cognitive reappraisal as more demanding than simple stimulus viewing. Importantly, this effect was independent of social condition, indicating that the observed behavioral difference reflects age-related variation in subjective effort rather than modulation by peer cues.

At the neural level, expected task effects confirmed the validity of the paradigm, with viewing negative images eliciting robust engagement of subcortical affective regions and prefrontal control networks. While reappraisal did not produce increased activation relative to viewing in predefined ROIs, reduced NAc activity during passive viewing compared to reappraisal suggests a modulation of affective salience across the cohort.

Crucially, we observed both developmental and social modulation of affective processing. Children exhibited greater amygdala activation than adolescents during negative viewing, consistent with established developmental differences in emotional reactivity. Additionally, reduced amygdala activation in the social relative to the nonsocial group during negative viewing suggests that the presence of a peer-related cue modulates affective responses, independent of explicit regulation demands. 

During reappraisal, the social condition exerted a robust influence on neural engagement. Social reappraisal elicited greater activation than nonsocial reappraisal across regions implicated in social cognition and cognitive control, including the ACC, PCC, and medial prefrontal cortex. These findings indicate that peer-derived cues modulate the recruitment of networks associated with self-referential processing and regulatory control during cognitive emotion regulation.

Importantly, developmental differences emerged in how the social condition shaped the regulatory circuitry. A significant age group × social condition interaction in the hippocampus suggests differential integration of contextual information during reappraisal across development.^4 Follow-up analyses revealed that adolescents—but not children—selectively recruited the ACC and dmPFC during social relative to nonsocial reappraisal. Given the roles of these regions in contextual integration, self-referential processing, and goal-directed control,^4,5 this pattern suggests that adolescents are more sensitive to social context during emotion regulation at the neural level.

Together, these findings indicate that adolescence is characterized by heightened neural responsiveness to social information during cognitive emotion regulation, reflected in increased engagement of prefrontal and medial cortical systems when regulation is socially framed. Our results suggest that social cues modulate the recruitment of control and social-cognitive resources during adolescent reappraisal. This developmental sensitivity to social context may represent an important mechanism through which peer environments influence emotional processing during adolescence. Further analyses are needed to clarify the network-level mechanisms through which social cues modulate regulatory processes during adolescence, including how interactions among prefrontal, limbic, and social-cognitive regions support this developmental sensitivity.
