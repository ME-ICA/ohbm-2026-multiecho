Introduction

Mirror therapy has been increasingly applied as a rehabilitation approach for upper limb motor recovery after stroke. By providing visual feedback of movements performed by the unaffected hand, mirror therapy generates a visual illusion of movement in the affected limb, which has been suggested to support neuroplastic reorganization. With advances in technology, the mirror therapy paradigm has been extended by virtual reality (VR), enabling more immersive visual experiences.1 Increasing evidence supports the therapeutic potential of virtual reality–based mirror therapy programs in stroke rehabilitation.

However, most existing mirror therapy and VR-based mirror therapy approaches primarily emphasize unilateral movements of the affected hand, often overlooking the natural cooperative use of both upper limbs that characterizes everyday functional activities. To better enhance immersive sensorimotor engagement underpinning real-world bimanual task demands, we developed a Virtual Reality–Based Generative Bimanual Cooperative Task-Oriented Mirror Therapy (VRGBCTMT) system. By integrating hand tracking with virtual reality technology, this system enables synchronized bimanual interaction while delivering coordinated multisensory feedback, including visual, auditory, and tactile inputs.

We hypothesized that training with the VRGBCTMT system would be able to facilitate the engagement of brain regions related to sensorimotor network. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the underlying neural mechanisms associated with VRGBCTMT using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI). Two stroke cases were reported to illustrate task-related brain activation patterns following VRGBCTMT training.

Methods

Development of the VRGBCTMT system. The VRGBCTMT system was developed using a standalone head-mounted virtual reality platform (Oculus Quest 3; Meta, USA) (Figure 1). Oculus Quest 3 is an all-in-one VR device that integrates onboard processing, storage, and display capabilities, enabling immersive virtual reality experiences without external computers or tracking hardware. The system employs built-in inside-out tracking technology (Oculus Insight), which uses embedded cameras and sensors to track head position and upper-limb movements within the virtual environment. This markerless configuration allows naturalistic interaction and unrestricted movement, supporting clinically feasible implementation.

Hand interaction within the VRGBCTMT system was facilitated using handheld controllers. The affected hand held the controller to receive system-generated sensory feedback, even in the absence of active movement, allowing multisensory input while minimizing physical demands. The headset was equipped with an adjustable head strap and integrated audio output to ensure participant comfort and stable immersion during task execution. Collectively, these features supported synchronized bimanual interaction and immersive task engagement central to the VRGBCTMT paradigm. This integrated system architecture enabled cooperative, task-oriented bimanual training scenarios intended to engage sensorimotor networks relevant to functional upper-limb use.

fMRI imaging acquisition and analysis. Magnetic resonance imaging data were acquired using a GE MR750 3T scanner (GE Healthcare, Waukesha, WI, USA) equipped with a 32-channel head coil. Functional MRI data were collected using a T2*-weighted echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence to measure blood oxygen level–dependent (BOLD) signals. Acquisition parameters were as follows: repetition time (TR) = 2500 ms, echo time (TE) = 30 ms, field of view (FOV) = 240 × 240 mm², matrix size = 80 × 80, flip angle = 90°, and isotropic voxel size = 3 × 3 × 3 mm³. Stroke lesions were manually delineated on FLAIR T2-weighted images by a trained researcher (Y. Kang) to generate lesion masks. All imaging data underwent standard preprocessing procedures, including denoising, eddy-current correction, head motion correction, and magnetic field inhomogeneity correction, to reduce potential sources of error in subsequent analyses.

Functional MRI task scans were conducted using a block design. During scanning, participants performed a hand grasp–release task at a frequency of 2 Hz. In the active movement condition, participants executed grasp and release movements in response to visual cues displayed on a projection screen. In the passive movement condition, an MR-compatible device passively moved the participant’s hand at the same frequency. Each fMRI run consisted of alternating task (grasp–release) and rest blocks, with each block lasting 20 seconds. Four task–rest cycles were performed per condition, and each hand was tested in two separate runs.

All fMRI preprocessing and statistical analyses were performed using SPM12. Functional images were spatially normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) standard space. Task-related brain activation was examined at the individual level using a general linear model (GLM), with head motion parameters included as regressors of no interest. Statistical significance was defined using an uncorrected voxel-wise threshold of p < 0.001 combined with a minimum cluster size of 10 voxels to ensure robustness and interpretability of activation patterns.

Outcome assessment. Participants’ motor ability was assessed using the Fugl-Meyer Assessment–Upper Extremity (FMA-UE) motor subscale, a widely used standardized behavioral assessment for evaluating upper-limb motor function after stroke. The FMA-UE demonstrates excellent test–retest reliability. Muscle tone was assessed using the Modified Ashworth Scale (MAS).

Results

Two stroke patients completed 18 intervention sessions of motor training using the VRGBCTMT system and exhibited markedly different levels of motor impairment at baseline. Case 1 (41 years old; stroke chronicity: 87 months; hemorrhagic stroke) presented with mild motor impairment (FMA-UE motor score = 56) and mild muscle tone in the affected upper limb, as indicated by Modified Ashworth Scale (MAS) scores of 1 for both the elbow and wrist flexors. In contrast, Case 2 (55 years old; stroke chronicity: 52 months; hemorrhagic stroke) demonstrated severe motor impairment at baseline, with an FMA-UE motor score of 26, while exhibiting a similar level of mild muscle tone (MAS = 1 at both the elbow and wrist flexors).

Following the intervention, motor performance of the affected upper limb in Case 1 remained unchanged (FMA-UE = 56), although the participant subjectively reported slight improvement. In Case 2, an improvement in motor performance was observed, with the FMA-UE motor score increasing from 26 to 29. Muscle tone, as assessed by MAS, remained unchanged for both the elbow and wrist flexors in both cases after the intervention.

Task-related fMRI revealed increased brain activation following the intervention in both cases compared with baseline (Figure 2). In Case 1, activation was observed in the contralateral primary motor cortex during movements of the affected hand under both active and passive grasp–release conditions at baseline. Following VRGBCTMT intervention, additional activation emerged in the ipsilateral motor cortex and the supplementary motor area during task performance.

In Case 2, no discernible task-related activation was observed at baseline during active movement conditions. After the intervention, increased activation was identified in the ipsilateral cerebellum during active movement. Moreover, during passive movement conditions, increased activation was observed in the bilateral sensorimotor cortices following the intervention.

Conclusion

This case-based study demonstrates that the VRGBCTMT system can be feasibly implemented in individuals with stroke and may modulate task-related brain activation patterns, despite heterogeneous baseline motor severity. Task-related functional MRI revealed increased and more distributed activation in the sensorimotor network following the intervention in both participants.

Importantly, the post-intervention activation patterns were consistent with the design principles of the VRGBCTMT system, which emphasizes bimanual cooperation, multisensory feedback, and task-oriented interaction. These findings suggest that such a mirror therapy paradigm may engage bilateral sensorimotor networks relevant to motor performance of the affected hand. Notably, in the severely impaired case, post-intervention activation emerged predominantly in the cerebellum. Prior studies have highlighted the critical role of the cerebellum in motor planning and sensorimotor integration. In the context of severe motor impairment, cerebellar recruitment following VRGBCTMT training may reflect enhanced immersive sensorimotor experiences that support the integration of sensory input and motor planning processes, potentially facilitating motor execution of the affected hand.

In sum, these preliminary findings indicate that VRGBCTMT may provide a useful experimental framework for probing neural mechanisms of sensorimotor engagement after stroke, particularly across different levels of motor impairment. Future studies with larger samples and quantitative neuroimaging analyses are warranted to determine whether VRGBCTMT-induced neural reorganization translates into meaningful functional recovery and to clarify its role in individualized neurorehabilitation strategies.
