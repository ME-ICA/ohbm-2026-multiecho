Introduction

The neural factors of neuromuscular control of lower limb movements are poorly explored. 

Ankle sprains are the most common lower limb musculoskeletal injury in physically active people and limit neuromuscular function at the site of injury and impair whole-body mobility1. A multi-centre cohort showed that 47% of ankle sprain injuries had poor recovery 3-months post-injury, and 25% had not fully recovered 1 year later2. Chronic injury (>9 months) may involve central adaptation, alteration of motor unit recruitment strategies, and impaired neural drive to muscle. 

This work aims to establish the feasibility of measuring functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) BOLD responses and concurrent electromyography (EMG) during a unilateral lower limb flexion task. This will in future allow the assessment of cortical adaptations for over the course of rehabilitation interventions.

Methods

Data Acquisition: Ten healthy participants (4 female, age 27±8 years (mean±stdev) were recruited to the study and gave informed consent. A voluntary lower limb unilateral flexion task was performed using a Trispect module (Ergospect, Innsbruck, Austria) at 1 bar pressure and 15% of maximum voluntary contraction (MVC) (15% mean: 0.72±0.16 mean (stdev)). MVC was estimated for each participant at the start of the scan session whilst supine on the scanner bed. The fMRI paradigm comprised 8 blocks of unilateral flexion (20s) comprising a rhythmic push cued by a white circle that presented on a screen (1s every 2.5s) and a rest period (20s). Pairs of EMG-electrodes were attached to the gastrocnemius of the right and left leg for concurrent EMG during the fMRI task using commercially available MR compatible equipment (BrainAmp ExG MR amplifier and software, Brain Products GmbH, Munich, Germany). In addition, a 3D Acceleration Sensor (Brain Products GmbH) was placed on the instructed leg only and used to capture leg movements in three dimensions. For each leg, two fMRI runs were performed.

Data Analysis: EMG signals were digitised and stored at a sampling rate of 5,000 Hz. The data were corrected for fMRI artifacts, and to reduce movement artifacts, bipolar derivations for each muscle were high-pass filtered at 10 Hz, and rectified. The amplitude cross correlation was calculated between EMG channels from each leg to assess the degree of muscle coactivation.

fMRI data were analysed in SPM123. Thermal noise was removed using NORDIC4, and ICA-AROMA was used to remove head movements covarying with each “push”. Standard preprocessing comprising motion correction, spatial and temporal filtering was performed. fMRI data was analysis using a GLM modelled of either: 1) a boxcar of task paradigm; or 2) accelerometer trace from the instructed leg; or 3) the EMG trace from the instructed leg - as the input convolved with a HRF with motion parameters input as covariates of no interest. T-stats from each participant’s 1bar fMRI task (L and R) were assessed. These were masked by the Harvard-Oxford atlas (Pre & Postcentral Gyrus, SMA) for both L and R responses to compare the contralateral to ipsilateral response. 

Results

Group level maps showed significant fMRI responses in the pre-/post-central gyrus and supplementary motor area (SMA) in response to plantar flexion of the unilateral lower limb. Using the accelerometer trace or EMG as the input into the GLM resulted in smaller statistically significant clusters, with the accelerometer resulting in a greater response than EMG. EMG traces revealed coactivation of both legs in some individuals in response to the instructed unilateral tasks, as evidenced by high cross correlations of EMG traces between instructed and uninstructed legs (sub04, 05, 06, 09). 

fMRI responses showed that the unilateral 1bar left leg task had greater overall response (both hemispheres) than the 1bar right leg task (p=0.002). Overall, to both tasks, the contralateral hemisphere had greater responses than the ipsilateral hemisphere (p=0.0002). 

Studying interaction effects of task and hemisphere revealed that the 1bar left leg task had greater contralateral responses than the 1 bar right leg task contralateral responses (p=0.008). Additionally, the 1bar left leg task had greater contralateral responses than its ipsilateral responses (p=0.001). While the 1bar left leg task also had greater contralateral responses than the 1bar right leg task ipsilateral responses (=0.0003). (Matlab: ANOVAN, corrected for multiple comparisons, Bonferroni). 

Conclusion

Cross correlation between EMG traces of both legs during the unilateral task showed that multiple participants moved their uninstructed leg. The use of an accelerometer as a GLM regressor produced similar estimates as the EMG and a standard boxcar.

fMRI responses showed a typical contralateral preference to unilateral pushing, however, several participants had symmetrical activity, particularly during the right leg contraction; while hemispheric dominance is less pronounced in lower limbs5, this may reflect ipsilateral recruitment for balance control or postural balance and stabilisation. This study shows that central and peripheral aspects of motor function can be identified in a healthy cohort, and this methodological approach will now be applied in studies of unilateral injury.
