Introduction

Object categorization occurs very early in the visual system and represents one of the most fundamental processes in everyday life. In the early days of fMRI research, simple visual stimuli such as checkerboard patterns were among the first paradigms used to probe these processes. Category-specific brain regions often form distributed networks that show preferential activity for particular stimulus classes, such as faces, bodies, or houses. 

To study these networks, researchers have frequently employed functional localizer scans. A functional localizer typically presents blocks of the condition of interest alongside one or more control conditions. Such paradigms have been instrumental in defining the face1, body2, and place3 networks.

Interestingly, these networks consistently show bilateral activation in early visual processing regions, with robust involvement of the occipital cortex and fusiform or parahippocampal gyrus. The core face network faciliates face perception through the interaction of bilateral regions in the occipito‑temporal cortex, including the occipital face area (OFA) and the fusiform face area (FFA). The core body network regions, showing higher activation for images of bodies or body parts than for other types of objects, also lie in the occpito-temporal cortex. They are the extrastriate body area (EBA) and the fusiform body area (FBA). The areas of the core place network reveal more activation for houses than for example faces and comprise of the occipital place area (OPA) and the parahippocampal place area (PPA). 

Despite their anatomical adjacency and their common involvement in categorization processes, however, the face, body, and place networks have largely been investigated in isolation4. To close this gap, this study wants to investigate the mechanistic commonalities and differences between the networks.  Thus, our goal is to exploratively compare the network dynamics for faces, bodies and houses stimuli using Dynamic Causal Modeling (DCM).

Methods

2.1 Participants

The dataset comprised 109 healthy volunteers (68 females, 41 males; mean age = 24.6 ± 3.7 years). The study was approved by the local ethics committee of the Medical Faculty at the University of Marburg (file reference 160/13 version 2). 

2.2 Experimental procedure

Participants completed localizer scans on a 3T Siemens scanner. Block‑design paradigms presented faces/bodies, houses/chairs, and scrambled images. Each block lasted 14.5 s and contained 20 stimuli (300 ms each, followed by fixation ~425 ms). Nine blocks per category were acquired, interleaved with baseline fixation blocks.

2.3 MR Data Acquisition and Preprocessing

Functional images were acquired with a T2*‑weighted EPI sequence (TR = 1450 ms, TE = 25 ms, voxel size = 3 × 3 × 4 mm³, 30 slices). Structural images were collected using a 3D MP‑RAGE sequence (TR = 1900 ms, TE = 2.54 ms, voxel size = 1 mm³, 176 slices). Preprocessing in SPM12 included discarding the first four volumes, realignment, co‑registration, segmentation, normalization to MNI space (2 mm³ voxels), and smoothing with a 6 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel.

2.4 Statistical Analysis

A univariate general linear model (GLM) was specified with regressors for main, control, and scrambled conditions, convolved with the canonical HRF. First‑level contrasts (‘main > control & scrambled’) were computed and entered into group‑level t‑tests.

2.5 Dynamic Causal Modelling Analysis

With the help of the DCM12.5 (7479) implementation in SPM12 (v7738), we constructed one full DCM for each category (faces, houses and bodies). The full model was kept identical over all network types.

2.5.1 Determination of ROIs and extraction of time-series

All our networks included six regions: two input regions from the early visual cortex (EVC), two occipital regions (first-order regions) and two regions from the fusiform gyrus or the parahippocampal gyrus (second-order regions). Group coordinates were defined from group activation maps (FWE‑corrected, p < 0.05) using WFU PickAtlas (calcarine, occipital, fusiform and parahippocampal gyrus).

Once we got the group coordinates we used SPM ROI search to jump to the nearest local maxima within a 15 mm sphere in uncorrected first‑level t‑maps (p < 0.001). Time‑series were extracted from 6 mm spheres, mean‑centered, and adjusted for effects of interest.

2.5.2 Model Construction

Identical full models were built for faces, bodies, and houses. Inputs drove bilateral EVC, which projected forward to first‑ and second‑order regions. All homologous interhemispheric and core intrahemispheric connections were modelled. Modulatory effects of the main condition were applied to all structural connections except EVC self‑inhibitions. This yielded bilinear, single‑state, deterministic DCMs for each participant. 

2.5.3 Model Estimation

The 109x3 models (for each participant one face, place and body network) were estimated using the variational Laplace algorithm, returning log model evidence and posterior parameter estimates. These estimated parameters serve as input for the subsequent PEB analysis.

2.6 PEB Analysis

Group‑level connectivity parameters were estimated using parametric empirical Bayes (PEB) 5, enabling random‑effects GLM analysis of DCM parameters. An intercept model assessed commonalities across participants. Bayesian Model Reduction (BMR) was applied to identify the most relevant connections by systematically comparing reduced models to the full model.

2.6.1 Family Analysis

Beyond exploratory BMR, we tested specific hypotheses. Four model families were defined: (i) connections between second‑order regions (top), (ii) connections between first‑order regions (bottom), (iii) intrahemispheric links in the left hemisphere (left), and (iv) intrahemispheric links in the right hemisphere (right). Each family contained three variants (two unidirectional, one bidirectional). Families were combined into a mixed model space of 82 models (3 × 3 × 3 × 3 + 1 null).

Results

Our objective was to investigate the network dynamics that underlie early object categorization in the human brain. We first examined average modulatory changes across participants, then reduced full models into sub‑networks. Finally, we reduced models into families.

3.1 Model averages over all DCMs  

The average modulatory connectivity in the bodies, faces and places networks showed positive input connectivity from EVC to first and second-order regions. Left-to-right connections between second-order regions were positively modulated, while right-to-left connections between first-order regions were negatively modulated. Intrahemispheric connections revealed negative feedforward and largely (with the exception of rFBA ro rEBA) positive feedback connections from first to second-order regions. Self‑inhibition decreased in left second‑order regions and increased in right first‑order regions. For right second‑order and left first‑order regions, modulatory input produced mixed effects: inhibition was enhanced in rFBA, rFFA, and lOFA, but reduced in rPPA, lEBA, and lOPA.

3.2 Exploratory Bayesian Model Reduction

BMR identified the most essential connections for each category (see Fig. 1). The body network was most reduced, retaining nine modulatory connections, while face and place networks retained twelve each. Across the three different networks many commonalities were found. 

All networks showed positive forward connections from EVC to second‑order regions (FBA, FFA, or PPA). These connections were bilateral for the face and place networks but restricted to the left hemisphere in the body network. In addition, self‑inhibition of the left second‑order region was consistently decreased across all stimulus types. A positive left‑to‑right interhemispheric integration remained between second‑order regions, while right‑to‑left integration between first‑order regions was negatively modulated.

Within hemispheres, the face and place networks revealed negative feed-forward and positive feed-backward coupling between core regions. Differences between networks were most evident in the interactions of the first‑order regions (EBA, OFA, OPA). The EVC showed connections to these regions only in the body network and in the left‑hemisphere place network.

3.2 Family-wise Bayesian Model Reduction

In addition to the exploratory BMR, we also tested four predefined families (top, bottom, left and right) on intrahemispheric and interhemispheric connections. For each family, connections were tested in both unidirectional and bidirectional forms.

Overall, the family‑wise analysis highlighted network differences more clearly. While bidirectional connections generally showed the highest probabilities, distinct preferences emerged (see Tbl. 1). The body network showed preferred left‑to‑right second‑order connections (FBAs, Pp = 0.80) and feedback from second‑ to first‑order regions (lFBA → lEBA, Pp = 0.86). The face network favored left‑to‑right second‑order connections (FFAs, Pp = 0.86), while other families remained bidirectional. The place network showed strong right‑to‑left interhemispheric connections between first‑order regions (OPAs, Pp = 0.83). These configurations achieved the highest free energy across all 82 models (F(bodies) = 4.64, F(faces) = 1.84, F(places) = 1.59).

Conclusion

In this study, we examined the dynamics of core categorization networks for bodies, faces, and places using DCM. We assumed a fully connected network for all three stimulus types and observed many commonalities in the signs of PEB parameters as well as in the surviving BMR connections across these networks.

Object stimuli strongly increased input connectivity from the EVC to either first‑ or second‑order regions, indicating that core regions receive preferred stimuli for processing. The analysis also revealed enhanced left‑to‑right connectivity at the second‑order level and reduced right‑to‑left connectivity at the first‑order level, suggesting that hemispheres may operate differently across these stages. Specifically, right occipital areas appeared to decouple from their left homologues, whereas left fusiform and parahippocampal regions strengthened interactions with their counterparts. Positive intrahemispheric feedback propagation—only partially preserved in the body network—may support more fine‑grained analysis or object detection in occipital areas. Conversely, negative feedforward coupling could reflect a reduction in expectation signals driving second‑order regions.

We also identified distinct preferences for unidirectional versus bidirectional connectivity. Feedback connections played a greater role in the body network, whereas both feedback and feedforward pathways contributed to the face and place networks. Moreover, body and face networks favored unilateral connections between second‑order regions, while the place network showed this preference at the first‑order level. These findings may point to different hierarchical dynamics across categorization networks.

Overall, our study demonstrates that categorization processes for different stimulus types share common network dynamics but also exhibit distinct differences that warrant further investigation.
