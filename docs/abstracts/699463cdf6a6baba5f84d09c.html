Introduction

Fetal brain development shapes the earliest architecture of human cognition, yet its rapid and heterogeneous maturation remains difficult to characterize. In developmental neuroimaging studies1, cortical surface atlases are fundamental for spatial normalization, enabling population-level analyses and delineating fine-grained typical and atypical developmental trajectories. Conventional atlas construction methods rely on repeated group-wise registration at each age group, resulting in atlases that lack temporal continuity, consistency, and correspondence. These limitations hinder our ability to precise mapping of fetal cortical developmental trajectories. We aim to address these issues by constructing a set of high-quality spatiotemporal fetal cortical surface atlases using an advanced deep learning method.

Methods

We used 242 T2-weighted fetal MRI scans and corresponding cortical surfaces from 231 participants in the Developing Human Connectome Project (dHCP)2, including longitudinal observations spanning from 21 to 37 gestational weeks. Cortical surfaces were mapped onto a sphere, and average convexity and mean curvature features were computed as geometric features to characterize folding patterns across gestational ages3.

To capture spatiotemporally continuous, consistent cortical development, we adapted a spherical deep-learning framework inspired by our infant 4D atlas construction method4. By leveraging rich information in the whole dataset, the model jointly learns (1) an age-conditioned atlas generator and (2) a spherical registration network that aligns individual surfaces to the atlas space. Longitudinal scans from the same fetus were grouped during training, allowing the model to better incorporate a temporal-consistency constraint that enforces meaningful anatomical correspondences across gestational weeks.

Surface alignment quality was evaluated using across-subject feature similarity in terms of Pearson’s correlation coefficient. The resulting atlases were generated continuously across gestational weeks and preserved fine-grained folding patterns essential for fetal neuroimaging studies.

Results

Across 21–37 gestational weeks, our fetal cortical surface atlases better captured the dynamic development of folding patterns, comparable to those obtained by a widely adopted conventional method, i.e., Spherical Demons5 (Fig. 1a). Our atlases provided sharper and more anatomically resolved representations of local cortical geometry (Fig. 1b). In our atlases, fine-scale folding structures are more clearly delineated (gray arrows), and the developmental features are more coherent across gestational weeks (white arrows).

Cross-subject alignment accuracy further demonstrated these advantages (Fig. 2). At early gestational ages when cortical folds have relatively simple shapes, the two atlases showed similar performance. As cortical folding complexity increased with development, our atlases consistently enabled higher alignment accuracy across subjects. This pattern indicates that the improved anatomical detail and temporal consistency of our atlases provide a more reliable and developmentally faithful reference for fetal cortical mapping.

Conclusion

We introduce a set of spatiotemporal fetal cortical surface atlases from 21–37 gestational weeks. By unsupervised learning of rich knowledge in the whole dataset, these atlases offer substantially improved anatomical detail and spatiotemporal consistency compared with atlases built by the conventional approach. This resource establishes a continuous, anatomically precise developmental reference for the fetal period, enabling more accurate surface normalization and more sensitive detection of emerging cortical organization in studies of typical and atypical fetal brain development.
