Introduction

Chronotype is shaped by complex interplay of endogenous and exogenous factors and is increasingly realized as a key factor related to our daily life and health outcomes. Recent advances in neuroscience have enable researchers to explore brain structure and function in greater depth, revealing links between chronotype and brain circuits including limbic system, basal ganglia, and prefrontal cortex. These reports suggested potential neural manifestations of chronotype which may explain its associations with emotional regulation, reward processing, as well as cognitive function1. Additionally, genome-wide studies indicate that chronotype is heritable, offering insights into its underlying biological mechanisms and links to other traits or diseases2. Our recent multi-modal cross cohorts’ study has identified five distinct, biologically valid chronotype subtypes, highlighting underappreciated sources of population variation that echo the rhythm of people’s inner clock3. However, the genetic architecture underlying this heterogeneity remains elusive. To investigate the potential heritable components and genetic consequences of these chronotype subtypes, we co-analyzed three brain imaging modalities and genotype data from 27,030 UK Biobank participants. Here, we characterized the distinct heritable structures of five latent brain subtypes, and examined their genetic correlations with brain phenotypes, behavior outcomes, as well as health indicators. Overall, our holistic study reveals both nature and nurture sides of chronotype heterogeneity within the population, providing a novel framework to disentangle the complex genetic and neural pathways linking circadian rhythms to mental and physical health.

Methods

Following protocols from our previous studies1,3, we excluded participants with inconsistent chronotype assessments or history of shift work experiences. This yielded 27,030 UK Biobank participants with complete data for structural MRI, diffusion MRI, and resting-state fMRI. Of these, 26,409 participants of European ancestry had quality-controlled genotype and imputed data (V3). 

To identify brain patterns associated with chronotype, we used partial least squares (PLS) correlation analysis to capture the most explanatory projections of brain features in relation to chronotype3. Statistical robustness was assessed via 1,000 non-parametric permutation tests, resulting five significant components. Subject-specific latent scores were extracted from these components to serve as phenotypes for downstream genetic analyses.

UK biobank genotyped and imputed data were filtered with standard QC criteria in PLINK2, specifically with MAF ≥ 0.001, Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium test P ≥ 10-10, genotyping missing rate and sample missing rate ≤10%, imputation information score ≥0.8 in real data analyses. All participants are European-ancestry individuals. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) for the five latent brain subtypes of chronotype were conducted using the fastGWA function in GCTA software, including the top 10 PCs as covariates. GWAS summary statistics were munged using the 1000 Genomes Project EUR reference panel. 

To evaluate tissue-specific heritability enrichment, we generated annotations for 49 GTEx V8 tissues based on the maximum causal posterior probability of cis-SNPs within 95% credible sets derived from CAVIAR fine-mapping method. Partitioned heritability for five subtypes was analyzed by implementing stratified LD score regression (s-LDSC), conditioned on the baseline v1.2 model4. Further transcriptome-wide association studies (TWAS) were subsequently performed across these tissues by using FUSION software5. 

Finally, to assess genetic overlap with other phenotypes, we aggregated GWAS summary statistics for 3,935 brain imaging-derived phenotypes (IDPs) from UK Biobank Oxford Brain Imaging Genetics Server - BIG40, 1,880 traits from Neale Lab, and 38,345 harmonized GWAS results from GWAS Catalog. After munging and checking the heritability z-scores (Z > 4) of those GWAS summary statistics, we calculated genetic correlations between our five chronotype subtypes and the traits that met inclusion criteria.

Results

PLS analysis identified five distinct brain patterns associated with chronotype. As established in our previous work3, these subtypes represent: subtype 1 is a night owl pattern linked to higher cognitive ability but emotional regulation deficits; subtype 2 is a night owl pattern associated with depression and cardiovascular risks; subtype 3 is a early bird with healthier profile; subtype 4 is a female-dominated early bird pattern associated with depression mood; and subtype 5 is male-dominated night owl pattern associated with high testosterone level and high risk-taking.

Common-variant heritability (h2SNP) analyses revealed moderate heritability across all subtypes (subtype 1: h2SNP = 0.128+/- 0.021, subtype 2: h2SNP = 0.167+/-0.023, subtype 3: h2SNP = 0.113 +/-0.023, subtype 4: h2SNP = 0.146 +/- 0.022, subtype 5: h2SNP = 0.105+/-0.021). After correcting for multiple comparisons, we observed significant heritability enrichment in the tibial artery, skeletal muscle, and thyroid for Subtype 2, and in the tibial artery and basal ganglia for Subtype 5. 

TWAS revealed distinct biological mechanisms. Subtype 1 top hits clustered on chromosome 2 (ZNG1B, LINC02966, FOXD4L1) and showed upregulation in the thyroid, a key regulator of metabolism. Notably, the lead SNP rs1823125 is located near the established circadian gene PAX8, underscoring a direct biological link. Conversely, Subtype 2 also highlighted thyroid involvement (ZNG1B, LINC02966) but with a hypo-active signal relative to Subtype 1; significant signals in the tibial artery (VCAN), hippocampus (CRMA), and adrenal gland (KLHL2) genetically corroborated its phenotypic associations with cardiovascular risk and depression. Subtype 3 exhibited signals in whole blood (OGFRL1), skin (FUBP1), and skeletal muscle (PICK1), pituitary (TMEM201/SAMP1), aligning with a high cellular integrity profile. Subtype 4 showed top hits in vagina (S100A9), breast (PM20D1), cerebellum and basal ganglia (RPTN, CRCT1), and adipose tissues (G0S2), linking to inflammatory state and hormonal sensitivity, mirroring its female-predominant and depressive behavioral expression. Subtype 5 exhibited signals in the adrenal gland (LINC00475), prostate (LINC00475), and substantia nigra (SFSWAP), tightly linking to dopamine activity, suggesting a genetic basis for the higher energy and risk-taking behaviors observed in this group.

Genetic correlation analyses confirmed significant overlap between chronotype subtypes and brain imaging phenotypes, particularly in diffusion and structural MRI. Furthermore, Subtype 2 exhibited significant genetic correlations with depression, reinforcing the underlying shared biological mechanism between this type of eveningness and psychiatric health.

Conclusion

Our holistic analysis reveals that chronotype heterogeneity is underpinned by distinct genetic and neural architectures, providing detailed perspectives on the complex interplay between the nature and nurture sides of chronotype. Importantly, we identified biological dissociations within night owl patterns, distinguishing between metabolic-circadian coupling (subtype 1), vascular and stress-response dysregulation (subtype 2), as well as dopaminergic pathways (subtype 5). Similarly, we delineated divergent early bird profiles, contrasting a resilient pattern (subtype 3) against a female-predominant pattern linked to hormonal and inflammatory pathways (subtype 4). These insights challenge the one-size-fits-all view in chronotype studies, suggesting that effective health interventions could benefit from looking beyond behavioral sleep patterns to the underlying biological mechanisms, paving the way for future precision chronotherapeutics.
