Introduction

With over half of the global population proficient in more than one language, there has been much effort to discover if and how a dual-language experience affects brain structure. Prior brain imaging studies have compared bilinguals and monolinguals on gray matter volume1 (GMV) and cortical thickness2,3 (CT), but convergence of results across studies (within each metric) has been low. This raises the question whether inconsistent findings may be attributed to the difference in language/heritage represented by the bilingual participant groups across these prior studies. That is, bilinguals who use Spanish and English, may not differ in brain structure relative to monolinguals in the same way as bilinguals using other language pairs differ from monolinguals. 

Here we compared GMV and CT in Spanish-English bilinguals versus monolinguals and included a second dataset of bilinguals and monolinguals, where the bilinguals used a language pairing of English with another language other than Spanish. We tested for a modulating effect of language/heritage with a factorial design. Using the United States Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development Study made it possible to study early cultural bilinguals (those who from an early age regularly speak a language other than English in their home) for the bilingual groups in each dataset, with all participants living in the same country. Within each dataset the bilingual group matched their monolingual comparison group, and the other bilingual and monolingual groups from the other dataset, on age, sex, socioeconomic status (SES), verbal and nonverbal abilities and pubertal status. We tested whether there is an interaction of Language Experience and Dataset on GMV or CT. The results will provide important information about whether brain structural differences in bilinguals depend on the nature of the bilingual groups’ language/heritage, indicating that bilinguals cannot be treated as one and the same.4

Methods

Four groups of participants were drawn from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development Study: early cultural bilinguals of Spanish and English (n=62), and matched monolinguals of English (n=61), together representing Dataset 1; and early cultural bilinguals of other languages and English (n=63), and matched monolinguals of English (n=62), together representing Dataset 2. For Bilingual Groups, we included those who reported on a survey that they spoke the other language with their family 50% or more of the time. Dataset 1 included Spanish-English bilinguals, and Dataset 2, included bilinguals of other languages and English. The other languages were: Dutch (4), Finnish (1), French (8), German (17), Hungarian (2), Italian (8), Polish (4), Portuguese (12), or Turkish (7). For the Monolingual Groups, we included those who reported not speaking another language, and whose parents reported their native language to be English. Propensity matching equated the bilingual with the monolinguals groups on age in months, sex, SES (income), verbal and nonverbal abilities, and pubertal status. The Spanish-English Bilingual Group and their Monolingual Group in Dataset 1 were of Hispanic heritage, whereas the Bilingual Group that used other languages and English and their Monolingual Group in Dataset 2 were of mixed ethnicities, but within each dataset the two groups were matched on their ethnicity. Dataset 1 and 2 therefore differed from each other in terms of language and heritage. 

Whole-brain T1-weighted magnetic resonance images (MRI) were acquired on 3T scanners with a 1x1x1 mm3 voxel resolution. Details on acquisition protocols can be found at https://abcdstudy.org/images/Protocol_Imaging_Sequences.pdf. MRI data were processed using voxel-based morphometry in SPM12 (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/) and vertex-based morphometry in CAT12 (https://neuro-jena.github.io/cat/). A 2x2 ANOVA was used to test for a main effect of Language Experience (Bilingual Groups vs. Monolingual Groups), main effect of Dataset (Dataset 1: vs. Dataset 2), and the interaction of Language Experience and Dataset. Age in months, sex, SES, nonverbal reasoning, site and scanner type were entered as covariates of no interest to account for any variance. Analyses used a voxel-wise threshold of p < .005 (uncorrected) and cluster-level threshold of p < .05 (FDR-corrected). Following the factorial analysis, we used MarsBaR toolbox in SPM12 to create masks of the clusters that showed a significant interaction and extracted the data for each and submitted these to a post hoc t-tests (p < .05) to determine the direction. Our main research question was addressed with the interaction results; for context, we first report on the main effect of Language Experience.

Results

For gray matter volume, the ANOVA revealed a main effect of Language Experience. The Bilingual Groups (combined across both datasets) had more GMV than the Monolingual Groups (combined across both datasets) in the left gyrus rectus (BA 11), extending to right gyrus rectus (BA 25) and to right superior orbitofrontal gyrus (BA 11). 

Turning to the main research question, three regions demonstrated a Language Experience × Dataset interaction: (1) the left postcentral gyrus (BA 1), extending into precuneus (BA 7) and superior parietal lobule (BA 7); (2) the right superior occipital gyrus (BA 19), extending into cuneus (BA 10); and (3) the right superior frontal gyrus (BA 6), extending into precentral (BA 6) and superior frontal (BA 6) gyri. Post hoc t-tests conducted on these three regions revealed that in the left postcentral gyrus, the Bilingual Group in Dataset 2 had less GMV than their Monolingual Group, while this relationship was the opposite in Dataset 1. In the right superior occipital gyrus, the Bilingual Group in Dataset 1 had more GMV than their Monolingual Group, while this relationship was the opposite in Dataset 2. In the right superior frontal gyrus, there were no regions that differed between the Bilingual and Monolingual Groups for either dataset on the post hoc t-test. 

For cortical thickness, the ANOVA revealed a main effect of Language Experience, with the Bilingual Groups (combined across both datasets) showing greater CT than the Monolingual Groups (combined across both datasets) in the left insula (BA 13). 

However, the Language Experience x Dataset interaction analysis, addressing the main research question, yielded no significant results for cortical thickness. 

Conclusion

We found bilinguals differed from monolinguals for gray matter volume and for cortical thickness, consistent with the idea that a dual-language experience brings about changes in brain structure.  The prior literature on GMV and CT comparing bilinguals with monolinguals is mixed. Specifically, a meta-analysis on GMV studies reported more GMV in left cerebellum in bilinguals than monolinguals (but only two of the 10 studies in the meta-analysis contributed to this finding).1 For vertex-wise studies of CT, one study reported bilinguals to have greater CT in the left inferior frontal gyrus and less CT in the right inferior frontal gyrus2, while another reported no differences.3 

Importantly, the presence of an interaction effect on GMV indicates that the language/heritage of the bilingual group plays a role in shaping neuroanatomical outcomes of the dual-language experience, but this observation was not made for CT, where there was no interaction of Language Experience and Dataset. While these findings for GMV could be used to explain the lack of convergence in the literature noted by the meta-analysis of GMV studies in bilinguals,1 our findings of no interaction for CT do not support the idea that language/heritage of the bilingual group plays a role in the cortical thickness differences identified when these groups are compared to monolinguals. As such, our study illustrates the importance of including multiple neuroanatomical measures, as the conclusions drawn from one specific metric may be contrary to those drawn from another. Given our mixed findings, one cannot exclude the possibility that the patterns of difference between bilinguals and monolinguals may depend on the language/heritage of the bilingual group.
