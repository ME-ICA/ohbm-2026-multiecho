Introduction

How does development of visuospatial attention result from local computations in the visual cortex? The human visual system contains what is referred to as the “dorsal visual stream”, a collection of brain regions that process the visual image on the retina and play a key role in our ability to deploy visuospatial attention. However, the structural and functional properties of dorsal stream development across childhood have never been mapped, perhaps due to the difficulty of running attention-based experiments on young children. Overcoming this hurdle, we created two novel, child-friendly videogames designed to help us understand how visuospatial computations develop in the dorsal stream, comprised of visual regions known to control attention allocation in space. Because we will be measuring from the visual system, we quantify population receptive fields (pRF),1 the portion of visual space to which a population of neurons within a voxel will respond to a visual stimulus. Modeling each pRF as a gaussian circle, we can ask two separate questions regarding pRFs: (1) How do static pRF properties of the dorsal visual stream change from childhood to adulthood? (2) Does a pRF’s ability to dynamically change size and position with the deployment of visual attention undergo protracted maturation during childhood? Based on prior work in the human ventral stream,2  we hypothesize that pRFs will undergo protracted development into adulthood and that children will additionally show less spatially dynamic pRFs during the deployment of visual attention. Lastly, we ask how the development of visual pRFs in the dorsal stream scaffold the prolonged maturation of visuospatial attention in children.3

Methods

N=30 children (ages 6 to 12, 17 females) and N=30 adults (ages 22 to 37, 13 females) completed both structural and functional MRI using a 3T Siemens Prisma scanner. All participants underwent T1 and T2-weighted structural imaging (0.8mm3 isotropic voxels) to reconstruct the cortical surface for surface-based analyses. All participants underwent functional MRI (voxel size 2.4mm3 isotropic, TR=2s, simultaneous multi-band acceleration factor = 2) in two separate sessions, each containing a video game-based pRF mapping experiment. In the first game called “Cartoonotopy”, participants complete a “sweeping bar” retinotopic mapping experiment in which they fixate at the center of the screen as a colorful cartoon-filled sliding bar sweeps across the visual field, similar to previous work,1 but participants attend the sweeping bar and monitor it for the occasional appearance of a target pattern. Each voxel’s time-series is modeled with a 2-D gaussian pRF with fit parameters of center position (X,Y coordinates), size (sigma), and an exponent to characterize compressive spatial summation.4 In the second game, “gopheRField”,  participants fixate while attending to a 5x5 grid of points, playing a game with two distinct conditions. The first condition (peripheral condition) requires them to monitor a cued position (gopher hole) for the brief appearance of a target stimulus (gopher) amidst visual noise. The second condition (central attention) requires them to monitor the fixation point (an apple) for the brief appearance of a target (dancing worm). Through a generalized linear model, we fit a beta weight for the response of a voxel to the appearance of a gopher at every position, and then fit a gaussian pRF to this 5x5 grid of beta weights. This allows us to quantify how each voxel’s receptive field dynamically shifts in response to the allocation of visuospatial attention to the periphery (i.e., comparing pRF parameters from central condition pRFs and peripheral condition pRFs). We define visual field map borders using Cartoonotopy retinotopic mapping data, defining maps from primary visual cortex V1 along the dorsal visual stream up to visual field map IPS-5.5 We examine pRF properties within each functionally-defined visual field map. All participant also completed a behavioral task outside of the scanner designed to quantify their visuospatial bias using a digital computer-based task that allows us to measure bias free from effects of handedness.

Results

Using our data from the ‘Cartoonotopy’ experiment, we find that coverage of visual space in IPS regions of the dorsal visual stream significantly increase from childhood to adulthood (p<0.003), while the visual field coverage of primary visual cortex is comparable between children and adults as observed in prior work. Interestingly, we find that the right hemisphere’s coverage is significantly more ipsilateral in childhood, but in adults, the laterality of right and left hemifield coverage is balanced across the hemispheres. Developmentally, there is a significant increase in the coverage of left visual space as measured by pRF density. The increase in coverage of the left hemifield is a result of both an increase in right hemisphere contralateral and left hemifield ipsilateral coverage. Interestingly, through an analysis of variance, we found that laterality biases in visual field coverage of dorsal visual stream field maps (IPS-0 to IPS-5) and age significantly explain the variability in behavioral measures of visuospatial attention biases (p<0.005).

Using the gopheRField pRF mapping experiment, we ask how pRF dynamics changes from childhood into adulthood. We have validated the use of gopheRField experiment in two ways. First, we established that the gopheRField game is a behavioral measure sensitive to visuospatial processing by reproducing two visual field trends previously established in adult literature. We found that (1) the mean reaction time across participants significantly increases as distance from the center of the visual field increases, replicating the “eccentricity effect” (p < 1e-4) and (2) that the mean reaction time is significantly greater in the upper compared to lower visual field, replicating the “vertical meridian asymmetry effect” (p < 0.005). These analyses served to validate the use of gopheRField to study visuo-spatial dynamics.

Using fMRI data from the ‘GopheRField’ experiment, we find that the allocation of attention to the periphery significantly increases pRF  eccentricity (distance from the center of the visual field) for both children (IPS0-3; p<0.001) and adults (IPS0-5; p<0.001), particularly in higher-order regions of the dorsal stream that modulate receptive field properties and their dynamics. Interestingly, we found that the higher-order regions in adults (IPS 1-5; p<0.003), but not children, increased in size with the allocation of attention to the periphery. Thus, while both children and adults are able to dynamically shift pRF size and eccentricity with the allocation of attention, adults are significantly better at it. This supports previous literature that suggests that visuospatial attention undergoes significant development as children get older.

Conclusion

These data provide the first observation of receptive field development in the human dorsal visual stream, demonstrating protracted and behaviorally-relevant development of static and dynamic properties of pRFs. We found a significant increase in static pRF coverage in areas IPS0-5 from traditional retinotopic mapping data, but not in earlier areas of the visual stream, such as V1. This pRF data shows developmental change in overall hemifield laterality from childhood to adulthood and significantly related to behavioral measures of visuospatial bias.  Lastly, attention-driven dynamic shifts in pRF coverage are significantly greater in adults than children. This developmental trajectory of the dorsal visual stream has also been implicated in the acquisition of skills that heavily rely on visuospatial attention, like reading. By studying the development of the attention network, we will provide useful benchmark data for future studies in children with ADHD and dyslexia, offering translational insight as to why there is a high comorbidity between the two disorders.
