Introduction

The sensorimotor-association (S-A) axis is a low dimensional embedding of functional cortical organization1. It is also conceptualized as a spatiotemporal axis of brain development, describing the heterochronous maturation of functional networks following the sensory-to-association hierarchy2. During adolescence, the S-A axis becomes the macroscale gradient explaining the most variance in functional connectivity (FC), thus marking a shift from child- to adult-like patterns of cortical organization3. To characterize the development of the S-A axis, prior work has mainly identified changes in network segregation as a function of chronological age4, typically focusing on group-level age differences3. Until recently, the role of puberty as a contributor to functional cortical development was largely overlooked5, despite the known effects of steroid hormones on brain organization and sex differences in pubertal onset, which underscores that models relying on age alone provide an incomplete account of developmental change and potential related sex differences. Here, we provide a unified, sex-sensitive framework to understand multilevel effects relating to the refinement of the S-A axis during adolescence. We disentangle age and pubertal contributions to a combination of novel features capturing S-A axis maturation, leveraging inter-individual differences and a system-level approach.

Methods

Longitudinal data from three timepoints of the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) study was used, specifically including baseline, 2-year and 4-year follow-ups (Nobservations = 6323, mean age: 11.01 ± 1.65 years, 3220 females; from 4919 unique subjects). For each subject, we used diffusion map embedding to reduce the dimensionality of FC matrices and yield ten low dimensional gradients of brain organization (G1-G10), ordered by their explained variance (mean group-level gradients computed by timepoint are shown in Fig. 1A-B). We then derived two primary measures of developmental cortical organization at the individual level: First, a measure of similarity to the adult S-A axis, defined as the Spearman correlation coefficient between each subject’s S-A axis and an adult S-A axis template1 (Fig. 1C). Second, a “gradient flip” binary measure, capturing shifts in the relative variance explained by the S-A axis (namely whether S-A patterns are reflected in G1 or another gradient) which marks the transition from child- to adult-like patterns of cortical organization (Fig. 1D). To understand system-level changes associated with these developmental measures, we identified network centroids for each of the 7 Yeo networks by computing the median of loadings across regions belonging to each network, representing the position of networks on the S-A axis (Fig. 1E) We then computed an S-A axis expansion measure by taking the sum of the squared values of all 7 network centroids (Fig. 1F). We used Big Additive Models (BAMs) to test for non-linear associations between our measures of developmental cortical organization, age and pubertal stage, defined as a continuous mean score quantifying the development of physical pubertal characteristics, based on the Pubertal Development Scale parent report (the distribution of pubertal stage score by sex is shown in Fig. 1G). Our models accounted for fixed effects of sex and total surface area, and random nested effects of subject, family and site. The relative contributions of age and puberty were assessed by including both variables as covariates.

Results

Across all timepoints, group-level S-A axis patterns were expressed in G2 (Fig. 1A). However, a larger and increasing proportion of subjects showed S-A axis patterns in G1 across timepoints, indicating that a predominant occurrence of the gradient flip at the individual level may not be apparent in the group average (Fig. 1D). Comparably, similarity to adult S-A axis decreased across timepoints at the mean group level, whilst increasing at the individual level (Fig. 1C). Our main analyses revealed age and puberty effects on the similarity to the adult S-A axis (Fig. 2A) and puberty effects (but no age effects) on the probability of the gradient flip occurring (Fig. 2B). Secondary analyses revealed age and puberty effects on S-A axis expansion (Fig. 2C) and S-A axis expansion effects on both similarity to adult S-A axis and the probability of the gradient flip occurring (Fig. 2D). Overall, females showed slightly greater similarity to the adult S-A axis and S-A expansion, indicating more advanced S-A axis development relative to males, but there were no statistically significant interactions of age or puberty effects by sex in explaining these effects.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates developmental, sex-independent effects relating to functional cortical organization during adolescence by disentangling the relative contributions of age and puberty on different features of S-A axis maturation. By leveraging individual differences in S-A axis expansion whilst controlling for age and puberty, we further provide a system-level explanation of S-A axis differentiation. Specifically, the refinement of the S-A axis appears to reflect patterns of changes in network topography and connectivity profiles, whereby sensory networks become more sensory-like and association networks more association-like. Our findings altogether highlight inter-individual differences in brain maturation and sex differences in pubertal timing, underscoring the importance of looking beyond group-averages to understand the mechanisms underpinning brain development.
