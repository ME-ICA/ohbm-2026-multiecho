Introduction

Children with neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs, used interchangeably with neurodiverse in this abstract; e.g., autism, intellectual disability) represent the largest subpopulation of youth with disabilities.1,2 Many of these children exhibit behaviours of concern (BoC; e.g., aggression, self-injury).3 BoC utilize a disproportionate amount of health resources and are associated with reduced quality of life for the child and their caregivers.3 While there is a critical need for an improved understanding of BoC amongst neurodiverse children, paradoxically these behaviours result in exclusion from health research. As a result, these children remain broadly understudied, causing significant health inequity and harm for them and their families. 

Advanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can measure structural and functional properties of the human brain with high spatial resolution. As MRI requires participants to lie still for minutes in a small space, it is challenging to image children with NDDs and bBoC without sedation. Traditional research methods to collect these data are rarely designed for the complex needs of children with NDDs and BoC, underscoring the need for inclusive protocols that will bridge the gap between research and clinical settings. Additionally, these patients typically meet the exclusion criteria of many imaging studies due to neurodevelopmental and behavioural complexity; consequently, not many research groups have attempted to image them. As a result, despite the prevalence, impacts on individuals and society, and substantial costs of these behaviours, little is known about neural correlates in brain imaging in these patients, causing significant health inequity and harm for them and their families. In collaboration with people with lived experience, this project aimed to develop an inclusive protocol for imaging children with NDDs who have BoC without sedation; and test it in a small patient cohort to show feasibility.

Methods

The imaging protocol without sedation was developed from existing standard protocols4,5, literature review, clinical expertise, lived experience, and iterative feedback from participating families. Following caregiver consent, pre-visit surveys and phone interviews were conducted to obtain child demographics (age, NDD and other diagnoses, BoC, etc) and evaluate potential barriers and facilitators to the scanning protocol. Flexible appointment booking including evening and weekends was offered. Preparation materials including resources adapted to the comprehension of the child and including visual tools such as a social story were shared. Children practiced first in a mock scanner, and if successful were scheduled to move forward with the actual scanning procedure. For both the mock and real scan, preferred items (e.g., stuffed animal) and resources (e.g., USB with preferred videos) were on hand. The MRI sequence was matched to a local existing pediatric protocol to allow for group comparisons. Following the mock and real scans, caregivers and children (if able) provided brief feedback to help iteratively improve the protocol.

Results

Ten participants were enrolled in the study. The average age was 11 years (range 6-15 years) and the majority were male (9/10). All genders aligned with biologic sex. The majority self-identified as white (n=6/10), with one participant each identifying as Indigenous, Indigenous-Latino, East Asian, and mixed background (n=4/10). Most participants had more than one NDD diagnosis (n=6/10). The most common diagnoses were attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD; n=8/10), autism (n=4/10), specific learning disorder (n=3/10), intellectual disability (n=2/10), and communication disorder (n=2/10). All participants had BoC with an average of 11 +/-5 behaviours per participant. The most common behaviours fell into categories of aggressive/destructive behaviours, non-cooperative behaviours, and self-injurious behaviours.

Nine participants successfully completed the mock scan; one participant had previously had an MRI for other reasons and would not enter the mock scanner. Of the nine who completed the MRI brain, eight were successful. One patient, who was non-speaking, became agitated during the scan and got out of the scanner. A subsequent patient noted that the headset was uncomfortably tight. This was resolved after this scan and subsequently no further complaints about headset-related pain were reported. The first two participants reported that the sound was much louder in the actual MRI scan than in the mock scan. The volume was adjusted for subsequent mock scans.

Overall caregivers reported positive experiences in this study. One caregiver provided the following feedback: “The pre-visit materials are very well-prepared, and they helped my son a lot in order to attend all the visits.” Another shared: “We had a very positive experience in this study and are happy to be contacted for future studies.”

Conclusion

This study achieved its aim of developing an inclusive protocol for imaging children with NDDs who have BoC without sedation in a cohort of 10 patients, 9 of whom were able to complete the mock MRI scan and move to the real scan, and 8 of whom were able to complete the MRI brain protocol. Future work will use advanced deep learning methods to analyze how brain regions differ from the normal variation in children with NDDs and BoC, matched to existing control cohorts to identify group-wise and individual level differences. 

This study provides a blueprint for transforming research accessibility through the development of an adaptive research protocol for brain MRI. Moving from exclusion to meaningful inclusion is critical to advance research accessibility and ensure findings translate into clinical care.
