Introduction

The hippocampus is a critical imaging biomarker for neurodegenerative diseases and brain aging, and its volumetric assessment from MRI is widely used to quantify cerebral atrophy. In research settings, hippocampal segmentation is typically performed on high-resolution 3D T1-weighted images, or on combinations of T1- and T2-weighted sequences. However, such structural acquisitions are frequently unavailable in routine clinical practice. In Japan in particular, time-of-flight MR angiography (TOF-MRA) is commonly acquired during medical check-ups and vascular screening, while dedicated structural imaging may be omitted.

Although TOF-MRA is not designed for morphometric analysis, recent advances in convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have demonstrated that anatomical structures can be reliably localized even in modalities with limited tissue contrast and elevated noise. We previously introduced hippodeep-MRA, a CNN-based hippocampal segmentation model trained on paired MRA and T1-weighted data, enabling hippocampal volume estimation directly from MRA acquisitions. While initial evaluations demonstrated feasibility and longitudinal sensitivity, the robustness of hippocampal volume estimates across small, heterogeneous cohorts, imaging protocols, and clinical populations has not yet been systematically characterized.

The present study focuses on a multi-cohort, small-sample evaluation of hippocampal volume estimates derived from TOF-MRA. Rather than emphasizing large-scale longitudinal modeling, we investigate practical questions relevant to real-world deployment: outlier prevalence, test–retest reliability, agreement between MRA- and T1-based measurements, and the relative contributions of inter-cohort and within-cohort variability.

Methods

Dataset overview

In this evaluation, the dataset comprises 1,079 MRI scans from 605 unique subjects, aggregated from six independent cohorts encompassing both clinical and research contexts. Subjects may have repeated acquisitions, either within the same contrast or across contrasts (TOF-MRA and 3D T1-weighted MRI). All scans were processed independently without spatial co-registration across timepoints.

Tohoku University Hospital (TUH).
 This institutional clinical cohort includes 81 subjects, predominantly elderly patients undergoing routine or diagnostic imaging. Age information was available for 59 subjects (mean 79.7 ± 8.8 years), and sex information for 78 subjects (27 males, 51 females). This cohort reflects real-world hospital imaging with heterogeneous indications and protocols.

Royal Brisbane cohort (openneuro ds005096).
 This dataset was obtained through Metro North Hospital and Health Service and the Royal Brisbane and Women’s Hospital. It comprises 63 subjects with TOF-MRA scans and expert clinical annotations of intracranial aneurysms, including interval surveillance imaging for a subset of patients. Age information was available for all subjects (mean 56.8 ± 13.3 years), while sex information was not encoded in the present table.

Midnight Scanning Club (MSC; openneuro ds000224).
 The MSC dataset includes 10 healthy young adults with extensive repeated imaging across multiple modalities, including MRA. All subjects had available age information (mean 29.1 ± 3.3 years). This cohort provides high-quality repeated measures under controlled conditions.

TopCoW 2024 subset.
 The cohort consists of 111 subjects recruited for cerebrovascular disease research, including ischemic stroke, transient ischemic attack, and related conditions. Imaging was performed on Siemens scanners (1.5T and 3T) using TOF-MRA protocols. While demographic information is described in the original dataset documentation, age and sex were not available in the present dataframe.

Lausanne TOF-MRA. (openneuro ds003949). This open dataset includes 284 subjects released for aneurysm detection research. Demographic characteristics are reported in the original publication but were not encoded in the current table. ICBM-MRA. This cohort includes 56 subjects with TOF-MRA acquisitions but no demographic metadata in the present dataset.

Overall, demographic information was sparse and cohort-dependent, reflecting realistic constraints of retrospective and multi-source clinical imaging. Consequently, demographic adjustment was not applied in the primary analyses.

Segmentation model

Hippocampal segmentation was performed using hippodeep-MRA, a CNN-based model that we previously introduced, and that was recently integrated into Hippodeep, available https://github.com/bthyreau/hippodeep_pytorch

Briefly, the model was trained using paired TOF-MRA and 3D T1-weighted images, with training labels derived from Hippodeep segmentations on the corresponding T1 images. Training data comprised approximately 3,300 paired scans drawn from institutional and public datasets, including the JPSC-AD cohort, an independent Tohoku University Hospital sample, and the IXI dataset. All training data were selected to avoid restrictive licensing terms, ensuring permissive redistribution and reuse.

In the present study, additional third-party datasets with heterogeneous licensing conditions were included only for evaluation, not for training.

Statistical analyses

Repeated measures were identified at the subject level, both irrespective of contrast and within MRA alone. Agreement between MRA- and T1-derived volumes was assessed using paired within-subject differences. Left and right hippocampal volumes were summed to obtain total hippocampal volume. All scans were processed independently. ICC and Levene tests were used for variance analysis. All analyses were performed in Python using pandas and statsmodels.

Results

Example of application of hippodeep-mra on various sequences is shown on Figure 1.

The variance structure was analyzed using linear mixed-effects models with cohort-specific random intercepts, estimated via restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Variance homogeneity across cohorts was further assessed using the Brown–Forsythe (median-centered Levene) test.

Variance homogeneity testing rejected the null hypothesis of equal variance across cohorts (Brown–Forsythe test, p < 10⁻⁸). Mixed-effects modeling showed that inter-cohort variance accounted for approximately half of the total variance in hippocampal volume (ICC ≈ 0.50), with within-cohort variance of comparable magnitude. No cohort exhibited pathological variance inflation or collapse.


 

Outliers. 

Outliers were identified independently for left and right hippocampal volumes using Tukey’s rule (x ∈ /[Q1−1.5 * IQR, Q3+1.5 * IQR], with IQR=Q3−Q1, the interquartile range ). A scan was flagged if either hemisphere exceeded this criterion. Outliers were identified using Tukey’s 1.5×IQR rule applied separately to left and right hippocampal volumes.

Out of 1,079 scans, 32 (3.0%) were flagged as extreme values in at least one hemisphere: (hippoL: 18 scans, hippoR: 20 scans). This outlier rate is consistent with expected segmentation failures in heterogeneous, multi-site clinical data, as well as MRA who simply did not have enough coverage.

Inter-cohort vs within-cohort variability

Mixed-effects modeling showed that inter-cohort variance accounted for approximately half of the total variance in hippocampal volume (ICC = 0.51), with within-cohort variance of comparable magnitude. No cohort exhibited pathological variance inflation or collapse.

And athough variance homogeneity was statistically rejected (Levene W= 14.3, p<10e-8), variance magnitudes were comparable across cohorts, supporting the use of mixed-effects modeling rather than global normalization.

Conclusion

This multi-cohort evaluation demonstrates that CNN-based hippocampal segmentation from TOF-MRA is robust to real-world heterogeneity in acquisition protocols, clinical populations, and metadata completeness. Outlier rates were low, test–retest candidates were identifiable even in small cohorts, and modality-dependent biases between MRA and 3D T1 were systematic and stable. While absolute hippocampal volumes differed substantially across cohorts, variance structure and within-subject consistency support the use of cohort-specific intercepts rather than global normalization.

These findings reinforce the practical utility of MRA-based hippocampal volumetry for opportunistic and longitudinal analyses in clinical settings where conventional structural MRI is unavailable.
