Introduction

Cognition emerges from coordinated dynamics across anatomically and functionally distinct brain structures. The cortex and hippocampus provide a canonical example, with each structure supporting its characteristic rhythms shaped by its intrinsic geometry and local circuit motifs. However, adaptive behavior depends on the coordinated dynamics between these two structures. Despite extensive empirical work, we lack a principled framework that explains how cortico-hippocampal interactions arise from the physical structure and circuit motifs of these structures and how they transition from healthy coordination to pathological synchrony.

Neural field theory (NFT) offers a natural way to bridge this gap by treating population activity as continuous fields evolving over native geometries of the cortex and the hippocampus. In our work, we formally incorporate the geometry of these two structures in the NFT framework and extend NFT to incorporate interactions between multiple spatially extended structures. Here, we develop a geometry-aware neural field framework that embeds cortex and hippocampus in their native geometries and couples them through a spatially structured, biologically informed mapping between the two surfaces. We then explore how coupling between two geometry-defined dynamical systems reshapes their rhythms, organizes cross-structure synchrony, and drives transitions toward seizure-like dynamics observed in human epilepsy.

Methods

We modeled the cortex and hippocampus activity as separate, continuous neural fields defined on their respective anatomical surfaces. Each structure forms a biophysically grounded feedback loop with subcortical structures. A corticothalamic loop generated the cortical alpha rhythms and a hippocampo-septal loop generated hippocampal theta rhythms. Neural activity evolved according to damped wave equations with physiologically informed synaptic gains, delays, and propagation ranges.

To enable interactions between these two spatially extended systems, we introduced a geometry-informed coupling scheme. Cortical and hippocampal surfaces were mapped into a shared two-dimensional domain using quasi-conformal mapping, preserving local neighborhood structure and anterior–posterior organization. Bidirectional coupling was implemented via spatially localized, exponentially decaying kernels in this shared domain, allowing nearby regions on one surface to preferentially interact with nearby regions on the other.

Numerical integration was performed using geometric eigenmode decomposition, yielding a low-dimensional yet spatially faithful representation of large-scale dynamics. Coupling strength and excitability were systematically varied to characterize healthy regimes and transitions into pathological activity. Model outputs were validated with intracranial EEG recordings from patients with mesial temporal lobe epilepsy.

Results

When simulated in isolation, the corticothalamic and hippocampo-septal systems exhibited robust, physiologically realistic rhythms: alpha-band activity in cortex and theta-band activity in hippocampus. Each of these rhythms is organized by structure-specific spatial eigenmodes. During healthy cognition, eigenmodes corresponding to different spatial scales remained weakly correlated, consistent with non-pathological dynamics.

Introducing cortico-hippocampal coupling selectively reshaped activity in both structures. Increasing coupling sharpened spectral peaks and systematically shifted dominant frequencies upward. Spatially, the coupling induced topographically precise synchrony: cortical regions preferentially synchronized with their corresponding hippocampal regions rather than producing global coherence.

Excessive increase in cortico-hippocampal coupling and hippocampal excitability in the cortico-hippocampal system drives a transition to seizure-like dynamics. These transitions were marked by large-amplitude oscillations, spectral chirps, and a collapse of multiscale independence into widespread synchrony. The hippocampus acted as a focal amplifier, concentrating instabilities that propagated to cortex through the coupled geometry. Empirically constrained simulations reproduced key spectral and synchrony features of human hippocampal-onset seizures observed in intracranial recordings (Figure 2).

Conclusion

Our results suggest a general principle: large-scale brain dynamics are constrained and enabled by the interaction of multiple neural manifolds, each with its own intrinsic geometry and dynamical repertoire. Understanding cognition and disease, therefore, requires models that treat brain structures as interacting dynamical surfaces. The geometry-aware neural field framework developed here offers a principled step in this direction, providing a foundation for unifying structure, dynamics, and pathology across distributed brain systems.
