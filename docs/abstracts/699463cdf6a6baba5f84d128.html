Introduction

Individuals with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) often face social interaction challenges. Hyperscanning approaches, which simultaneously measure brain activity from multiple participants, allow researchers to quantify the degree to which neural activity aligns among interacting individuals. This provides a promising neurobiological framework for understanding social coordination, offering insights into the mechanism that support social engagement and cooperative behavior. Given the differences in social communication behaviors that children with ASD often exhibit relative to their typically developing (TD) peers, this raises the question of whether interpersonal neural synchrony (INS) with their parents also diverges from TD patterns1. Prior work suggests that ASD may be characterized by atypical neural activity during social interaction. However, patterns of INS between child-parent dyads remain insufficiently understood, especially in real-world cooperative contexts.

The present study uses simultaneous EEG recordings of ASD and TD child-parent dyads to characterize INS patterns during a cooperative musical task, which provides a naturalistic, nonverbal cooperation condition to scaffold ASD children’s engagement. It also integrates a metric of behavioral interaction quality with measures of neural activity, allowing for the measurement of differences between ASD and TD dyads in: (1) interaction quality, (2) degree of INS, and (3) spatial patterns of INS. We hypothesized less synchronized behavior and neural activity in ASD child-parent dyads relative to TD controls.

Methods

Participants 

Child-parent dyads were recruited from Albemarle County, Virginia, as part of a larger study on social interaction in ASD. The final analytic sample consisted of 44 child-parent dyads: 16 with a child diagnosed with ASD (Mage = 9.4 years, SD = 1.80; 13 male child participants) and 28 TD dyads (Mage = 8.13 years, SD = 1.50; 17 male child participants). All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and hearing. ASD diagnoses were confirmed through clinical records as well as validation with standardized assessments. None of the parent participants had an ASD diagnosis.

Experiment

Participants completed a series of naturalistic tasks in a clinic testing room, as shown in Figure 1. The present analysis incorporates two music-related tasks: a musical patterns (MP) task and a musical free play (MF) task. In MP, one member of the dyad played a 3-note pattern of their choosing on a 5-bar wooden xylophone; the other participant repeated the pattern, and then both played it simultaneously. The dyad member initiating the pattern alternated. In MF, dyads were instructed to play instruments together without clear rules. Both tasks were approximately 5 minutes long. 

EEG data was recorded simultaneously from each member of the child-parent dyad using Cognionics Quick-20m dry-wireless headsets (19 channels plus reference and ground) throughout the experiment session. Behavioral attunement (BA), an observation-based measure of interaction quality, was derived post-hoc through qualitative evaluation videos from 3 perspectives taken during experiment sessions. BA captures the dyad’s behavioral coordination (e.g., eye contact, reciprocity). Videos were coded by two trained raters blinded to ASD/TD group membership.

INS, referring to the coupling of neural activity across interacting individuals over time, was calculated from child-parent hyperscanning data and served as a neural indicator of interaction quality1. We characterized global (scalp-wide mean) INS, local (channel-specific) INS, and the topographic profiles of INS across the scalp.

Data Processing

INS was calculated from EEG data using windowed cross-lagged correlation2, which evaluates the moment-to-moment correlation between two EEG signals. Consistent with the approach of prior hyperscanning studies3, EEG data from each dyad were segmented into overlapping windows. For each window, correlations were computed for the corresponding channel pairs across participants, allowing for temporal lag. The single peak correlation within each window was extracted; these were then aggregated to create two dyad-level INS measures: Global INS (mean INS; the average of peak correlations across windows and channels) and local INS (max INS; the highest peak correlation of one channel over time, representing the strongest regional neural coupling). Channel-specific INS values were retained to characterize spatial topography.

Statistical Analysis

Group differences between ASD and TD dyads in behavioral and neural synchrony were tested using mixed-measures ANOVAs, with group (ASD vs. TD) as a between-subjects factor and task condition (MP vs. MF) as a within-subjects factor. Dependent variables included BA, global INS, and local INS. For spatial pattern analyses, multivariate ANOVA was applied to channel-specific INS to evaluate overall differences in topography. Univariate analyses identified electrodes that differed significantly in INS.

Results

Behavioral Attunement 

Mixed ANOVA showed that BA differed significantly between groups (ASD vs. TD), F(1, 44) = 18.43, p < 0.01. TD child–parent dyads had higher BA scores than ASD dyads (t = 4.39, p < 0.01), indicating higher-quality social coordination. Neither a significant main effect of task (p=0.54) nor interaction (p=0.87) was found, indicating that TD dyads had significantly higher behavioral attunement relative to ASD dyads during both musical patterns and free-play tasks.  

Global and Local INS

Global INS significantly differed between ASD and TD dyads, F(1, 44) = 4.33, p = 0.04 in mixed ANOVA. The post-hoc test revealed a significantly higher global INS in ASD dyads than in TD dyads, t = -2.21, p = 0.03, indicating higher average similarity in the EEG signal. Neither a significant main effect of task (p=0.07) nor interaction (p=0.48) was found. These results indicate that ASD dyads exhibited higher neural similarity, contradicting our hypotheses. In analyses of local INS, there were no significant differences between group or task conditions or interaction effects.

Spatial Patterns Analysis

            Multivariate ANOVA of channel-specific INS revealed robust differences in the spatial distribution of INS between ASD and TD dyads. A significant main effect of group was found, F(20, 68) = 3.24, p < 0.01, indicating that ASD and TD dyads exhibit distinct topographic profiles of INS across the scalp. Neither the main effect of task (p = 0.98) nor the interaction (p = 0.99) reached significance. Univariate analyses revealed that channels located at temporal (T3, T4, P7, P8), parietal (C3, C4, Cz, Pz), and occipital (O1) regions demonstrated higher INS in ASD dyads relative to TD dyads. As shown in Figure 2, topographical maps further revealed that ASD dyads had more posteriorly distributed INS, whereas TD dyads showed more dynamic patterns, with higher synchrony localized in prefrontal regions.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates that autistic and typically developing child-parent dyads engage in cooperation with differential behavioral and neural profiles. Behaviorally, ASD child-parent dyads exhibited significantly lower behavioral attunement, reflecting less coordinated social engagement during both patterned and free musical interactions. However, contrary to our hypotheses, ASD dyads exhibited higher global INS, suggesting greater overall neural similarity. This elevated global synchrony may reflect a more consistent, but less dynamic, INS profile. 

Topographic analyses showed differences across dyads in INS localization, providing more spatial specificity to the global INS finding. ASD dyads exhibited higher posterior INS, primarily in temporal and parietal regions. In contrast, TD dyads exhibited more dynamic INS. Higher INS in posterior regions in ASD could reflect a greater reliance on sensorimotor neural circuitry and less effortful control during cooperation.

Together, these findings provide evidence for increased neural synchrony between children with ASD and their parents relative to TD peers, despite decreased behavioral attunement. Given that behavioral attunement measures are based on behaviors known to reflect social connection in typically developing individuals, future work should aim to characterize the behaviors in ASD that may align with this heightened neural synchrony. The results of this work could be informative for interventions that target difficulties in social connection in the ASD population.
