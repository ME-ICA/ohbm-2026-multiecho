Introduction

The motivation to exert effort is fundamental to a successful, healthy life and is reduced in neurological and psychiatric disorders, where it is linked to symptoms such as persistent fatigue. Despite its clinical relevance, the mechanisms through which fatigue impacts the willingness to exert effort remain poorly understood.

Effort-based decision-making studies show that rewards are subjectively devalued as a function of effort costs^1 and that such decisions fluctuate on a moment-to-moment basis due to fatigue. Brief periods of high-effort exertion can rapidly increase fatigue and reduce motivation to exert effort, with recovery occurring over seconds.^2,3

A critical feature of everyday decision-making is often overlooked: decisions about effort are frequently made in advance, concerning actions that will be executed later. Here, we manipulated moment-to-moment levels of fatigue by requiring participants to exert either a high or low level of force prior to making decisions about whether they would be willing to exert different levels of effort for reward at the end of the task. This allowed us to whether transient fatigue reduces willingness to choose future effortful actions.

To investigate neural mechanisms underlying these effects, we focused on spinal fMRI. The spinal cord is the sole pathway through which signals related to muscular exertion and fatigue reach the brain. Spinal fMRI remains technically challenging and has only recently been used to investigate cognitive processes. For example, simultaneous brain–spinal cord fMRI revealed learning-related plasticity and functional interactions between spinal segments and cortical networks during motor sequence learning, demonstrating that spinal signals can reflect more than motor execution.^4 We hypothesized that spinal cord signals during decision-making would be modulated by recent physical effort, providing a test of whether spinal activity reflects prior exertion during decisions about future effort.

Methods

Study 1: Behavioural study

To test whether execution of physical effort immediately prior to decisions influences people’s willingness to exert effort for reward, thirty-one healthy young adults (mean age = 24.71 years, SD = 5.23) participated in a behavioural decision-making task. Written informed consent was obtained prior to participation. Participants received monetary compensation for their time (£9), with the opportunity to earn an additional performance-dependent bonus payment (maximum £3).

Participants completed a physical effort–based decision-making task in which they made 128 decisions regarding their willingness to exert effort in the future in exchange for credits (translated into bonus payment). Physical effort was operationalised as force exerted on a handheld dynamometer. Each trial sequence began with a squeeze at either low (easy = 10%) or high (hard = 48%) force relative to the participant’s maximum voluntary contraction (MVC), designed to manipulate momentary fatigue, followed by two decisions. On each decision, participants chose between a rest option (1 credit) and a work option with variable effort requirements (49%, 56%, 63%, or 70% MVC) and variable reward magnitudes (2, 4, 6, or 8 credits). Critically, participants were instructed that they would not execute their choices during the task; instead, 16 choices were selected at random and executed at the end of the experiment to determine bonus payment.

To assess whether recently exerted effort influenced willingness to exert effort in the future, we compared the proportion of rest versus work choices across effort and reward levels using the non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

Study 2: Spinal fMRI - proof-of-principle study

The second study aimed to investigate the neural mechanisms underlying the influence of preceding physical effort on subsequent effort-based decisions, focusing on spinal cord fMRI signals. This proof-of-principle study was designed to test whether spinal cord activity during decision-making is modulated by prior physical exertion, in the absence of concurrent motor execution.

One healthy participant (female, 23 years old, right-handed) performed the same task as in the behavioural study while undergoing MRI. Choices were indicated via eye movements to minimise motor involvement during the decision period.

Total scanning time was approximately 2 hours and included a T1-weighted anatomical scan, a T2*-weighted scan, shimming procedures to optimise signal-to-noise ratio in the spinal cord, and 33 minutes of task-based fMRI acquisition. MRI data were preprocessed using the Spinal Cord Toolbox (SCT). First-level (single-subject) analyses were conducted in MATLAB using an ROI-based general linear model approach (see Figure 1 for the processing pipeline).

Both studies were approved by the University of Birmingham ethics committee (ERN 20-1897AP12).

Results

Study 1: Behavioural study

Behavioural results showed that participants were able to successfully execute both the high and low squeezes on the majority of first attempts (>95%). To examine whether the fatigue manipulation influenced willingness to exert effort for reward at a later point in time, we compared the proportion of rest versus work choices across effort and reward levels using a Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Results showed that following a hard squeeze, participants were more likely to choose the rest option compared to following an easy squeeze (median 29% vs. 24%, p = .003), despite the fact that decisions concerned only potential effort to be exerted at the end of the experiment and did not require exertion immediately after the decision.

Study 2: Spinal fMRI

In the main body of this study, we demonstrate that cervical spinal cord fMRI signal (C4–C6) during the decision period is modulated by the difficulty of the preceding squeeze. In a single-subject, ROI-based GLM analysis, the Hard > Easy contrast revealed relative increases in signal across most ROIs. The strongest effect was observed at C5 (β = 0.20, t = 1.80), with smaller positive effects present in the combined C4–C6 ROI, C6, and both left and right hemicord ROIs. These findings indicate relatively higher spinal cord signal during decision-making following a hard compared to an easy squeeze. In contrast, the C4 ROI showed a small negative Hard > Easy effect, suggesting a relatively stronger signal reduction during decisions following hard squeezes at this level.

The task main effect (decision-making versus baseline) was negative across most ROIs, with particularly pronounced decreases observed in C6 (β = −0.55, t = −3.94) and the left hemicord (β = −0.38, t = −2.72). This pattern indicates an overall reduction in spinal cord signal during the decision period relative to baseline, consistent with task-related modulation rather than net activation.

Conclusion

In everyday life, decisions about effort and reward often concern actions that will be executed in the future. Here, we show that rewards are devalued by effort not only as a function of the effort required to obtain them, but also as a function of effort recently exerted. In addition, we provide evidence that spinal cord signals are modulated by previously executed effort during subsequent decision-making.

Behaviourally, we found that following exertion of high effort, participants were more likely to choose a rest option than a work option, even when the work would be performed only later in the task. Effort-based decision-making is often treated as a relatively stable individual trait that varies between people and may be impaired in neurological and psychiatric disorders. However, recent work has highlighted rapid, moment-to-moment fluctuations in motivation related to fatigue.^2,3 The present findings extend this view by demonstrating that recent effort does not only influence immediate willingness to exert effort, but also reduces commitment to future effortful actions.

We further show that this task design enables modulation of effort-related spinal cord signals during decision-making in the absence of motor execution. In a single participant, decision-related spinal fMRI signals were suppressed relative to baseline, with decisions following hard effort showing reduced suppression compared to those following easy effort. This pattern suggests that recent physical exertion influences spinal cord activity during decisions about future effort, even when no action is being performed. Although spinal cord fMRI has traditionally focused on sensory and motor processes, recent methodological work highlights its suitability for studying cognitive and motivational influences via descending modulation.^5 Our proof-of-principle findings extend this framework by demonstrating effort-dependent modulation of spinal signals during decisions about future actions.

Together, these findings provide converging behavioural and spinal fMRI evidence that recent physical effort modulates decisions about future effort, highlighting a potential role for the spinal cord in linking transient fatigue states to motivational processes.
