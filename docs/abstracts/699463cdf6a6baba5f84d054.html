Introduction

Fatigue affects up to 75% of patients with multiple sclerosis (MS) and is consistently ranked as one of the most impactful and disabling symptoms of MS. In turn, MS-related fatigue is associated with an enormous socioeconomic burden due to reduced work capacity and increased unemployment levels. Despite the impact that fatigue has on society and on individual lives, underlying mechanisms of fatigue remain unclear and there are currently no effective treatments. Targeted neuromodulation interventions have shown promising, but variable, preliminary results. Establishing empirically supported testable stimulation targets is therefore crucial for the implementation of non-invasive brain stimulation as a treatment for fatigue. 

Preliminary findings point towards changes in functional and structural brain networks underlying fatigue in MS. However, a robust and well-validated network for MS fatigue, along with the underlying pathological substrate, remains to be elucidated. The aims of this study were therefore to 1) identify a robust and reproducible brain-network associated with fatigue in MS using lesion network mapping1 and 2) to explore novel cortical targets for brain stimulation therapy of fatigue in MS.

Methods

We analyzed retrospective brain MRI scans from 1,666 MS patients (mean±SD age 42.69±11.37 years, median (range) EDSS 2.0 (0.0–8.5), median (range) disease duration 9.0 (0.0–46.0) years, n (%) female 1146 (68.8%)), collected from 12 European MS centers part of the Magnetic Resonance Imaging in MS (MAGNIMS) study group. Fatigue was assessed using established questionnaires and scores were harmonized across different scales. We applied lesion-network mapping, a novel approach to mapping neurological and psychiatric symptoms to brain networks,2 to explore the functional brain networks related to fatigue. MS lesions were automatically segmented using LST-AI3 and individual lesion masks were transformed into common 2 mm MNI152 space. The brain regions functionally connected to each lesion mask were determined using a normative resting state functional MRI connectome from healthy control participants (n=1,000) obtained from the Brain Genomics Superstruct Project.4 For this analysis we performed seed-based functional connectivity between each individual lesion location and all other voxels in the brain. Voxel-wise one-sample T-tests were used to generate a single “T-map” representing the functional connectivity of each individual lesion mask, referred to as a lesion network. Associations between self-reported fatigue severity and individual lesion networks were identified through voxel-wise general linear models (GLMs) and permutation tests (Figure 1). The network was validated through split-half cross-validation and through robustness to leave-one-cohort-out and leave-one-fatigue scale-out analyses and correcting for confounding factors using spatial correlations and permutation testing. Spatial overlap with canonical resting state brain networks was assessed using the Network Correspondence Toolbox.5 

Follow-up clinical and MRI data were available from 396 patients, which were used to identify lesion network patterns associated with fatigue progression. For this we identified new lesioned tissue between baseline and follow-up by subtracting the two cross-sectionally segmented lesion masks in MNI152 space and thresholding to lesions > 5 voxels to avoid spurious subtraction errors. The resulting masks only containing newly developed lesioned tissue, were used as seeds in the same normative resting state functional connectivity analysis as the cross-sectional data. Associations with change in fatigue severity were assessed with voxel-wise GLMs and permutation tests. Network hubs derived from the fatigue network were entered as seeds in the normative functional connectome to identify potential cortical targets for neuromodulatory interventions. Candidate targets were determined by assessing spatial similarity (Pearson’s correlation) between the connectivity of the network hub and the full fatigue network. 

Results

515/1,666 (31%) of patients suffered from severe fatigue defined by the rescaled FSMC cutoff (≥63 on the FSMC and ≥53.75 rescaled) and 701 (42%) were at least moderately fatigued defined by cutoffs established for each individual scale. Functional lesion network mapping revealed a large-scale brain network associated with self-reported fatigue severity in MS. The network had peaks in the bilateral occipital lobes, frontal poles, middle temporal gyri, inferior frontal gyri, supramarginal gyri, angular gyri, left hippocampus and right thalamus (Figure 2). The network showed significant overlap with canonical visual, default mode, frontal parietal and control networks (Dice scores=0.16-0.78, P<0.05). Validation tests showed that the network was reproducible (split-half spatial-r=0.89, P<0.001), robust to leaving out individual centers and fatigue scales (spatial-r=0.94-0.99, P<0.001) and when controlling for confounding (spatial-r=0.64-0.98, P<0.05). In patients with follow-up data available (median follow-up time 2 (0.62-10.71) years) the functional connectivity pattern of newly developed lesions which was associated with more severe fatigue at follow-up was spatially similar to the full fatigue network (spatial-r=0.72, P=0.014). Network mapping of peak-coordinates from the fatigue network identified several potential targets for future neuromodulatory interventions most promising were the left lateral frontal pole (r=-0.66) and the temporo-occipital part of the left middle temporal gyrus (r=-0.7).

Conclusion

Lesions associated with fatigue in MS are functionally connected to a shared and reproducible brain network involving hub regions of the visual, fronto-parietal and default mode networks. These networks have been implicated as essential components for integrating perception, attention and consciousness. Several hubs in this fatigue network showed a functional connectivity pattern which greatly overlapped with the full fatigue network. These hubs included the left lateral frontal pole and the middle temporal gyrus, providing data-driven testable cortical targets for future neuromodulatory interventions.
