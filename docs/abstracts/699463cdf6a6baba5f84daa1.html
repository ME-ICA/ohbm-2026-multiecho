Introduction

Brain Age, an MRI-derived measure of brain health, is widely used in neurodegenerative research to identify at-risk individuals1. The Brain Age gap (the difference between Brain Age and chronological age) indicates whether a person’s brain is aging faster or slower than the rest of their age cohort. Brain Age has also been applied to child development studies; because childhood and adolescence involve rapid, widespread brain changes, identifying children who differ from peers in Brain Age can act as an early marker of neurodevelopmental or psychological illness2. While Brain Age may be the most well known example, forms of these gap scores or difference scores have been used to identify similar concepts such as cognitive reserve. In this study, we extend the Brain Age framework to model the difference between expected and observed psychopathology in the Adolescent Brain and Child Development (ABCD) cohort in order to quantify resilience.

Specifically, we investigate the statistical and computational challenges prevalent in gap score analyses. Because gap scores represent model residuals capturing unexplained variance in the target variable (e.g., age in brain age, and psychopathology for our study), it is often difficult to disentangle the gap score from the target variable. Brain Age literature has proposed methods to reduce statistical collinearity between the gap and the target variable to improve gap score interpretability3. We evaluate these approaches in our conceptualization of a psychopathology gap score, which we have termed the Resilience/Susceptibility Gap (RS-Gap).

Here, RS-Gap is calculated by leveraging residuals from a predictive model based on Social Determinants of Health, potent predictors of psychopathology4. Inspired by Brain Age work and the fact that most mental health problems emerge in adolescence, this approach aims to clarify individual differences in vulnerability to improve etiology, risk identification, and targeted prevention.

Methods

Participants. The Adolescent Brain and Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study is a 21-site longitudinal study of child/adolescent brain and behavioral development that recruited 11,875 children at 9-10 years of age and has followed them until young adulthood. Our analytic sample was composed of 9,329 participants with nonmissing information on the measures described below.
  
Measures. Social Determinants of Health (SDoH). Variables (n=211) were grouped into 4 broad domains: 1) prenatal environment (n=46), built environment (n=28), immediate environment (n=101), and adverse experiences (e.g., traumatic life events, n=36) and used to predict psychopathology. Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL).  Psychopathology was defined using the Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL Total Problems score), a 113-item questionnaire on which caregivers rated specific problems in the past six months.

Resilience/Susceptibility Gap. Machine Learning. A gradient-boosted tree regression model was trained on SDoH features to predict CBCL Total Problems score using the XGBoost and scikit-learn Python packages. Leave-One-Site-Out Cross-Validation accounted for the multi-site structure of ABCD and family dependencies. Nested 5-fold cross-validation within a grid search pipeline was used to generate the best possible parameters for the regression algorithm. The model generated a predicted CBCL score on the held-out testing site (n = 278-934 per cross-validation fold) based on input features. Gap calculation. Reported CBCL scores were subtracted from predicted scores to determine an RS-Gap for each individual, resulting in a paradigm in which "resilient" individuals (observed behavioral problems lower than prediction) had positive RS-Gaps, while "susceptible" individuals (higher observed problems than prediction) had negative RS-Gaps (Fig. 1). 

Outcomes. To validate the resulting RS-Gap scores (defined based on year-2 data only; 2YFU), we tested associations between the RS-Gap and longitudinal outcomes (defined based on year-3 data; 3YFU). To avoid potential circularity resulting from autocorrelation, we did not include the 3YFU CBCL score as an outcome. Instead, primary outcomes were defined as diagnoses and corresponding symptom items taken from the Kiddie Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia assessment. Secondary psychopathology-associated outcomes included school performance and behavior, peer groups/bullying, and use of mental health or substance use services from KSADS reports. 
Longitudinal validation. Association analyses. All analyses were conducted using linear mixed-effects models (lme4 R [version 4.2.1] package) with random intercepts for site and family membership. Fixed effects covariates included sex, age, and CBCL Total Problems score (2YFU). Much like the BrainAge literature where chronological age is included as a covariate in models wherein BrainAge gap is predicting outcomes, 2YFU CBCL score (the target prediction variable) was included as a covariate. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was used to adjust for multiple comparisons across outcomes across 28 variables. Correction methods for collinearity. In order to mitigate the collinearity between RS-Gap and CBCL score, a common statistical issue with gap scores, multiple correction methods were tested. The most common correction method from the brain age literature involves fitting a linear relationship between the gap score and true score, then calculating a corrected prediction by subtracting the offset from the initial prediction.

Results

Model Performance. SDoH features successfully predicted CBCL Total Problems in our XGBoost model (average R2 = 0.25) with comparable fit across sites. The correlation between predicted and observed CBCL score was r = 0.51. 

Model Validation. Attempts at validating RS-Gap longitudinally using association analyses with 3YFU psychopathology-related outcomes revealed high collinearity between RS-Gap and CBCL Total Problems Score at 2YFU, (r = -0.84, Fig. 2). Because CBCL score was included as a covariate when testing for associations of the gap score (in line with Brain Age best practices), this relationship confounded the results of the LME model and introduced collider bias that flipped associations (Fig. 2B). Specifically, when accounting for CBCL score, “resilient” (RS-Gap > 0) individuals showed higher likelihood of poor academic outcomes such as dropping grades or detentions/suspensions. Resilience was also associated with a lower likelihood of having a best friend or regular friend group, as well as higher risk across the board for suicidality measures, self-harm behaviors, and Conduct Disorder diagnoses/symptoms.

Statistical corrections for collinearity. Although the correction approach removed collinearity, it did not address the flipped associations in validation analyses (Fig. 2C). A closer look at the data revealed that participants classed as most susceptible (i.e. bottom third for RS-Gap) were driving the collinearity between the gap score and the target variable (CBCL). Removing this group fully removed collinearity between RS-Gap and actual CBCL score, and successfully prevented sign flipping of validation analyses when including CBCL as a covariate without the need for further correction (Fig. 2D).

Conclusion

Social Determinants of Health can be leveraged to predict mental health problems in children. 

However, the collinearity between the RS-Gap and measured CBCL score introduces interpretability challenges that must be addressed.

Our findings revealed flipped associations between the RS-Gap and longitudinal mental health outcomes, with resilient individuals performing poorer over time than susceptible individuals. This unexpected association may reflect processes such as stress inoculation or weathering, where repeated exposure to adversity can enhance resilience in the short-term while accelerating overall development of psychopathology5. This implies that child mental health trajectories and effects of SDoH may not be linear. On the other hand, the flipped associations may be largely artificial resulting from the collinearity with the target variable and resulting collider bias. Indeed, our findings that validation analyses followed the expected directionality when removing participants with extreme susceptibility point to this interpretation. Notably, most prediction models perform best within the middle quartile of the distribution, where variance is lowest and trajectories are most normative. However, the individuals that fall outside of this range are often the most clinically interesting. Future analyses will involve identifying associations with structural and functional MRI data to more clearly determine the implications of these adverse results.

These findings have important implications for Brain Age studies. Brain Age research has long grappled with the same statistical tension: gap scores are residuals, and residual‑target collinearity can distort associations, inflate interpretability claims, and produce misleading inferences about accelerated or delayed aging. Our results reinforce concerns raised in the Brain Age literature that gap‑based metrics may conflate true individual differences with statistical artifacts, particularly at distributional extremes. They also highlight the need for careful validation, sensitivity analyses, and transparent reporting of residual‑target relationships when interpreting any gap score—whether indexing brain aging or psychopathology resilience.

As such, further work is needed to robustly leverage and validate gap score analyses to quantify individual differences in psychopathology risk and resilience. If successful, these approaches could prove valuable for identifying children at elevated risk and informing targeted interventions, while also strengthening methodological standards for Brain Age and other difference score frameworks.
