Introduction

The brainstem remains one of the least comprehensively mapped regions of the human central nervous system, owing to its dense microstructural organization, intricate connectivity, and the significant technical challenges associated with imaging and segmenting its diverse tissue components. As part of the Human Brainstem Project within BRAIN CONNECTS, efforts are underway to generate a multiscale, multimodal atlas that integrates data from magnetic resonance imaging, Hierarchical Phase-Contrast Tomography (HiP-CT), polarization-sensitive optical coherence tomography (PSOCT) and light-sheet fluorescence microscopy (LSFM), providing complementary views of brainstem architecture. Among these modalities, PSOCT offers the unique ability to visualize myelinated fiber pathways through their polarization signatures with minimal distortion. Traditional PSOCT techniques, however, are limited to estimating only the in-plane orientation of fibers, restricting accurate reconstruction of complex three-dimensional trajectories. To address this limitation, we developed a 3D axis PSOCT approach (3DA-PSOCT) capable of resolving both in-plane and through-plane fiber orientations, thereby enabling recovery of the complete 3D orientation field. Here we present the application of 3DA-PSOCT to postmortem human brainstem tissue, demonstrating its ability to capture fine-scale myeloarchitecture across large tissue volumes even in regions of densely packed fibers, providing a crucial bridge between macroscopic MRI-based tractography and microscopic histological measurements1.  

Methods

To achieve volumetric reconstruction of brainstem fiber architecture at microscopic resolution, we employed a custom serial-sectioning 3DA-PSOCT system developed in our laboratory using the Telesto platform (Thorlabs Inc.) as shown in Figure 1. Conventional PSOCT provides measurements of birefringence and optic axis orientation but is limited to in-plane orientation estimation. To recover the full 3D fiber orientation, we modified the imaging geometry by mounting the microscope head onto a high-precision rotational stage that allows acquisition of the same tissue region at two controlled illumination angles. For each blockface optical slice, the system collects data at a normal angle (0° relative to the tissue surface normal) and at an oblique angle of 15° tilt. Each measurement captures the projection of the local optic axis onto the imaging plane from a different illumination direction. By jointly processing these angle-dependent measurements, we computationally reconstruct the complete 3D fiber orientation vector, enabling us to resolve both in-plane and through-plane components of the myeloarchitecture. 

To optimize signal quality, all samples were index-matched using either EasyIndex (LifeCanvas Technologies) or glycerol, which reduces scattering and permits deeper light penetration through fixed human brainstem tissue. This preparation enhances the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the PSOCT data without compromising the polarization information. The brainstems were also embedded in agarose to maintain their geometry and facilitate their sectioning. During imaging, the tissue block was immersed in mineral oil to maintain the refractive-index environment, further stabilizing penetration depth and contrast. These optical conditions collectively improve polarization sensitivity and increase the accuracy and robustness of fiber-orientation estimates across large, heterogeneous volumes. 

For whole-brainstem mapping, we integrated the 3DA-PSOCT system with motorized XYZ translation stages and a vibratome to implement an automated serial sectioning workflow2. Following acquisition of each dual-angle optical slice across the full blockface, the vibratome removed a physical section of 300 µm. The newly exposed surface was then repositioned under the imaging objective, and the two-angle PSOCT acquisition was repeated. This iterative imaging-and-sectioning process was continued until the entire tissue block had been imaged, ensuring consistent sampling throughout the specimen with minimal distortion since imaging is performed prior to any sectioning. All system components—including stage control, angle switching, vibratome, and data acquisition—are coordinated through custom software developed in-house, enabling fully automated operation and reliable alignment across thousands of volumetric optical slices. This pipeline provides high-resolution, orientation-resolved imaging over centimeter-scale tissue volumes, facilitating comprehensive reconstruction of the human brainstem’s 3D microstructural organization.

Results

We applied our dual-illumination, serial-sectioning 3DA-PSOCT system to postmortem human brainstems and successfully reconstructed the full three-dimensional optic-axis orientation of myelinated fibers throughout the tissue. Figure 2 illustrates a representative dataset acquired at the level of the medulla oblongata from a 76-year-old neurologically healthy male donor.  

The recovered true birefringence map (Fig. 2a) clearly delineates gray-matter nuclei from surrounding white-matter tracts: structures such as the inferior olivary nucleus (ION), the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus (10N), and the hypoglossal nucleus (12N) - see segmentation in Fig. 2f - exhibit lower birefringence and appear darker than adjacent fiber pathways, including the pyramids (py) and the inferior cerebellar peduncle (icp). Normal-incidence orientation measurements (Fig. 2b) and tilted-incidence measurements (Fig. 2c) were combined to yield the full 3D fiber orientation field shown in Fig. 2d. 

Qualitative comparison with diffusion MRI (dMRI Fig. 2e) highlights strong agreement in major fiber systems as well as key differences driven by resolution. The amiculum (ami), the fiber system wrapping around the ION, exhibits a predominantly left–right orientation (red) in both modalities, and the pyramids demonstrate a robust through-plane orientation consistent with their inferior–superior trajectory (blue). However, the markedly higher spatial resolution of 3DA-PSOCT (10 µm) enables detection of smaller fiber bundles that remain unresolved at the 300 µm resolution of dMRI. A notable example is the medial lemniscus (ml): while dMRI primarily detects its dominant through-plane component (blue), 3DA-PSOCT additionally reveals fine in-plane bundles running left to right (red), demonstrating the ability of our technique to expose microstructural heterogeneity masked at coarser scales. Overall, 3DA-PSOCT provides a richer depiction of brainstem myeloarchitecture, with detailed contrast in both birefringence and local orientation. 

Using the reconstructed full orientation field, we performed tractography at the microscale to explore the continuity and geometry of fiber pathways within selected regions (Fig. 2h). Four subregions were examined. Region 1, corresponding to the amiculum, forms a sheet-like structure that wraps tightly around the ION, exhibiting complex folding patterns that are difficult to capture using lower-resolution modalities. Regions 2 and 4, corresponding to the pyramids (py) and the solitary tract (sol), respectively, show coherent and predominantly through-plane trajectories consistent with their known anatomical course. In contrast, Region 3, the spinal trigeminal tract (sp5), exhibits a more heterogeneous organization with multiple smaller bundles diverging and converging across depth. These tractography results demonstrate that 3DA-PSOCT enables detailed reconstruction of fiber pathways at a scale approaching individual fascicles, revealing structural complexity inaccessible to dMRI-based tractography. 

Together, these findings show that dual-angle 3DA-PSOCT can recover fine-scale orientation features, differentiate nuclei from tracts via intrinsic birefringence, and generate microscale tractography across large tissue volumes. This establishes 3DA-PSOCT as a powerful modality for high-resolution mapping of human brainstem architecture.

Conclusion

The serial sectioning 3DA-PSOCT system our lab has developed has shown its ability to recover the full orientation of the fibers in the brainstem at the microscale.  

Future work will focus on improving the accuracy, scalability, and cross-modal interpretability of 3DA-PSOCT for mapping human brainstem architecture. First, although the current dual-angle acquisition provides sufficient information to reconstruct full 3D fiber orientations, additional illumination angles will allow for more robust estimation in regions with highly complex or crossing fiber configurations. Expanding the system to acquire three or more incident angles will reduce degeneracies in the orientation solutions, improve sensitivity to subtle variations in optic axis direction, and increase stability in low-signal regions.  

Second, we aim to transition from mechanically tilting the microscope head to implementing optical beam tilting through the imaging lens. The mechanical approach, while effective, imposes constraints on speed, stability, and long-term robustness when imaging large blockface volumes. Optical tilting using controlled beam steering - through galvanometric mirrors - would enable rapid switching between angles without moving the imaging hardware. This modification would greatly improve throughput, reduce vibration-induced alignment errors, and allow for finer, more flexible control over illumination geometry. Such an upgrade would also facilitate integration of additional angles while maintaining consistent spatial registration across slices and reducing cumulative mechanical wear. 

Third, we plan to establish a quantitative multiscale comparison of fiber orientations obtained from diffusion MRI, 3DA-PSOCT, and LSFM. Each modality provides complementary information at different spatial resolutions and contrasts. A rigorous comparison requires accurate cross-modal registration of all datasets, which is particularly challenging given the large size of human brainstem tissue blocks, differences in distortions introduced by clearing or embedding, and the disparate resolutions of the techniques. Developing and validating a robust registration pipeline will enable voxelwise and tract-level comparisons, providing new opportunities to assess the biological sources of diffusion signals, evaluate the fidelity of tractography, and link microscale and mesoscale imaging readouts. 

Together, these future developments aim to transform 3DA-PSOCT into a faster, more accurate, and more integrative tool for reconstructing human neuroanatomy. By combining expanded polarization sampling, improved optical engineering, and multiscale registration, we aim to build a comprehensive framework that unifies structural information across modalities and advances our understanding of brainstem connectivity with unprecedented detail.
