Introduction

Adolescence is a period of social transition. During this time, relationships with others - especially peers - become particularly salient.1 These social transitions are accompanied by maturational changes in brain function that may enable individuals to adapt to their unique (social) environments.2 These maturational changes unfold hierarchically across the cortex along along an axis spanning from sensorimotor to association cortex (S-A axis).3 Developmental changes in functional connectivity closely follow this axis, refining the cortical hierarchy into adulthood and allowing for environmental adaptation.4 Despite the importance of social environments during adolescence, little is known about how social relationships shape these functional trajectories. Given that association areas are critically implicated in social processing and demonstrate extended developmental plasticity,3 we hypothesize that adolescents’ social relationship quality is associated with variation in the developmental trajectories of functional connectivity. Here, we investigate how relationship quality relates to age-dependent changes in functional connectivity across the cortex.

Methods

We used cross-sectional behavioral and neuroimaging data from the Human Connectome Project Development for participants aged 8-21 (N = 464, mean age: 14.8 ±  3.9 years, 243 females, Fig. 1A). Resting-state fMRI data were post-processed with XCP-D and parcellated using the HCP multimodal atlas (360 parcels) (Fig. 1B). Functional connectivity (FC) strength for each cortical region was computed as the average Pearson correlation between that region's time series and those of all other regions (Fig. 1C). We analyzed NIH Toolbox Social Relationship Scales measures (emotional support, friendship, loneliness, perceived hostility, perceived rejection) as self-report indices of key aspects of social relationships.5 Exploratory factor analysis combined these five measures and identified latent dimensions, with the first factor representing overall social relationship quality (Fig. 1D). Subject-level factor scores were computed for each participant. To examine potential nonlinear age-related changes in FC metrics and their associations with the social relationship score, generalized additive models (GAM) were fitted (Fig. 1E). For each cortical parcel, FC strength was modeled with age and social relationship score as smooth terms and sex and in-scanner motion as covariates. Age and social effects were quantified as the change in adjusted R² between the full and reduced models that excluded the covariate of interest. Significance was assessed via ANOVA comparing these models, and multiple comparisons were controlled for using false discovery rate correction (q < 0.05). Spatial patterns of developmental and social effects were correlated with sensorimotor-to-association axis ranks3 using Spearman’s rank correlations with a spatial rotation-based significance test to assess their alignment with the cortical hierarchy.

Results

Consistent with prior work4, we found that FC strength increased in sensorimotor regions, while association cortices showed age-related decreases (Fig 2A). Stratifying this relationship according to intrinsic functional network communities showed consistent findings (Fig. 2B). Similarly, the systematic spatial variation in the effect estimates showed high alignment with the canonical sensorimotor-to-association axis (r = −0.77, pspin < 0.001; Fig. 2C). Social relationship quality showed more targeted parcel-wise associations to FC strength, showing the strongest associations with FC strength in frontoparietal and temporal regions when controlling for age, sex, and motion (Fig. 2D). The unthresholded effect size map significantly co-varied with the S-A axis in the opposite direction to age, showing decreased FC strength in sensorimotor areas and increased strength in association regions (Figure 2F; r = 0.48, pspin < 0.001).  When analyzing subscale-specific effects, only perceived hostility showed significant, yet weaker, associations with FC strength (Fig. 2G). This suggests that the latent social relationship quality measure captures the strongest brain-behavior pattern, which individual subscales fail to detect (Fig. 2H). Model improvements with inclusion of the social relationship quality factor were most prominent in frontoparietal and somatosensory networks (Fig. 2I). 

Conclusion

Our study examined functional cortical development during adolescence by exploring the effects of age and social relationship quality on functional maturation. Age-related changes in FC strength closely followed the established S-A axis, with FC strength increasing in sensorimotor and decreasing in association regions. In contrast, significant associations between age and social relationship quality were localized to fronto-temporal subregions and macroscale co-variation with the S-A axis operated in the opposite direction to age effects. Importantly, a latent social relationship quality factor revealed distributed brain-behavior associations that individual subdomains failed to capture, suggesting that aggregate social measures are suited to examine developmental variation in functional connectivity. As adolescent brain development is fundamentally embedded in social contexts, these findings may inform future work examining the pathways through which social relationships affect functional brain architecture during this sensitive period.
