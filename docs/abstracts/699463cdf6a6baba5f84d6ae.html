Introduction

Isolated REM sleep behavior disorder (iRBD) is considered a potent early marker of synucleinopathies, such as Parkinson’s disease. Studies mapping brain circuits in rodents have shown that the hypothalamus plays a central role in regulating sleep and wakefulness, containing both sleep-promoting and wake-promoting neurons that act as 'switches' for sleep-wake transitions1. However, few studies account for the hypothalamus when investigating sleep-related disorders in humans. In this exploratory study, we combined the use of high-resolution quantitative 3D-T1 MRI sequences with a super-resolute atlas of hypothalamic nuclei2 to uncover macro and microstructure variations of HT nuclei, and their association with the disease duration in a well-characterized cohort of iRBD participants. 

 

Methods

We included 17 controls (age: 68.47 ± 7.61 [46-78]) and 17 iRBD participants (age: 67.71 ± 7.89 [50-77]; duration: 70.24 months ± 45.23 [15-156]) meeting the diagnostic criteria for iRBD with polysomnographic confirmation. Subjects were scanned with a 7T MR scanner (TERRA, Siemens) using a (1TX/32RX) Head coil (NOVA). Anatomical data was obtained using a 3D-MP2RAGE sequence (TR=5.000ms/TE=3ms/TI1=900ms/TI2=2.750ms, 256 slices, 0.6mm isotropic resolution, TA= 10min12s). Unbiased T1 maps and T1-w-UNIDEN images were generated after B1+ inhomogeneity correction. T1-weighted volumes, as well as the Neudorfer template/atlas (0.5mm isotropic voxel)2, were cropped in a similar manner, before an optimal spatial registration using the antsRegistration procedure was performed (Figure 1). Volumes and T1 values of hypothalamic nuclei were extracted using Python/Nibabel. The volumes of hypothalamic nuclei were normalized by intracranial volume (ICV) prior to group comparisons. The Mann-Whitney U rank test (two-sided, Python/scipy) was performed to evaluate the modulation of macro and microstructure of individual hypothalamic nuclei by iRBD. Partial correlations (spearman rank Python/scipy) were performed to study the associations between hypothalamic nuclei metrics and disease duration (age corrected).    

Results

No significant differences in age (U=151.0; puncorr= p=0.8358), ICV (U=164.0; puncorr= 0.5128) or whole hypothalamus volume (U=158.00; puncorr= 0.9243) were observed between the two groups. Furthermore, no lateral differences were found for the right (U=161.00; puncorr=1) and the left hypothalamus volumes (U=145; puncorr= 0.6122). Relative to controls, iRBD participants showed lower T1 values in the right suprachiasmatic nucleus (SC) (U=215; puncorr= 0.0159), the left dorsal periventricular hypothalamus (DPEH) (U=212; puncorr= 0.021), as well as the left medial preoptic nucleus (MPO) (U=206; puncorr= 0.0356) (Figure 2). However, iRBD participants showed higher volume only of the right MPO (U=75; puncorr= 0.0175) (Figure 2). Partial correlation accounting for age showed a significant negative association between the disease duration and the T1 value of the left DPEH (ρ=-0.54; puncorr= 0.026). 

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that structural changes in hypothalamic sleep-regulating regions may contribute to iRBD and serve as early biomarkers of its underlying pathology. By extending insights from rodent studies to humans, our research highlights the hypothalamus' role in sleep homeostasis and underscores the need for early biomarkers, as most iRBD patients develop motor symptoms of synucleinopathies within ten years.
