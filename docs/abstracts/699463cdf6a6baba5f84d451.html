Introduction

Functional brain networks dynamically reconfigure between rest and task states to support cognitive performance. Greater stability and segregation of these networks are associated with better cognition1. However, aging is linked to reduced segregation and diminished stability across rest and task states, which may contribute to cognitive decline2,3. Although dopamine has been implicated as a key modulator of network dynamics, most evidence comes from indirect measures, and few studies have used direct PET imaging to examine how dopamine synthesis capacity relates to these changes. This gap limits our understanding of the neurochemical mechanisms underlying age-related alterations in network organization.

            In this study, we combined fMRI-based measures of network stability and segregation with PET imaging of dopamine synthesis capacity to test whether dopaminergic function supports network reconfiguration in younger and older adults. We further explored how tau pathology in aging and preclinical Alzheimer’s disease influences these dynamics, given emerging evidence demonstrating associations between tau pathology and functional network dynamics. By linking dopamine and tau to network stability and segregation, we aim to clarify competing influences on brain network organization and implications for cognitive aging.

Methods

This study used data from the Berkeley Aging Cohort Study (n = 30 younger adults; n = 50 older adults). Participants completed a session of fMRI scanning that included a pre-task resting state scan (5 minutes), four task-based scans, and a post task resting state scan (5 minutes). This was followed by a subsequent memory test to measure episodic memory of stimuli presented during the task. 

Select participants (n = 14 younger adults; n = 41 older adults) also completed a [18F]fluoro-l-m-tyrosine (FMT) PET scan to measure dopamine synthesis capacity. Dopamine synthesis capacity was defined as the mean [18F]FMT Ki(calculated via Patlak plot) within the ventral striatum of each participant. Select older adult participants also completed an [18F]fluortaucipir (FTP) PET scan to measure tau burden (n = 41) and a [11C] Pittsburgh Compound B (PiB) PET scan to measure amyloid load (n = 45). Tau burden was defined as the mean [18F]FTP SUVR within a meta-region of interest composed of medial temporal regions affected in early AD.  [11C]PiB positivity was defined as a DVR within cortical gray matter greater than (1.065). Using the [11C]PiB scans, older adults were grouped as either normal aging (n = 30) or preclinical AD (n = 15). 

For each participant, functional connectivity matrices were extracted during rest and task using the Schaefer-200 atlas4. System stability was calculated as the mean Z-transformed Pearson correlation between rest- and task-state connectivity matrices2. System segregation during rest and task states was calculated as the mean within-network connectivity minus the mean between-network connectivity, divided by the mean within-network connectivity3. Measures of system stability and segregation were compared across participant groups using independent t-tests. Linear regression was used to test relationships between system stability/integration with memory, dopamine synthesis capacity, and tau burden, while accounting for age, sex, and years of education. These relationships were further tested at the level of specific Yeo-7 brain networks (e.g. default, cognitive control, etc.) using partial spearman correlation.

Results

At the whole-brain level, older adults in both the normal aging and preclinical AD groups showed significantly lower system stability (b = -0.08, p = .03) and system segregation during both rest (b = -0.16, p < 0.001) and task (b = -0.08, p = 0.02) compared to younger adults (Figure 1a-c). Across groups, increased system stability was significantly associated with better subsequent memory (b = 3.49, p = .03). Moreover, system segregation during task (b = 4.30, p = .01), but not at rest (b = 1.07, p = .62) was significantly associated with subsequent memory (Figure 1d-f). 

When considering dopaminergic mechanisms of network reconfiguration, we observed a significant interaction between dopamine synthesis capacity and group on system stability (b = -29.8, p = .03). Specifically, we found that dopamine synthesis capacity in younger adults was associated with increased rest-to-task stability within association networks (dorsal attention: rho = 2.57, q = .04; ventral attention: rho = 3.53, q = .04; cognitive control: rho = 2.80, q = .04; default: rho = 2.97, q = .04), but dopamine synthesis capacity was not significantly related to rest-to-task stability in either older adult group. In normal aging, significant relationships between dopamine synthesis capacity and within-network stability were observed in the visual (rho = 2.98, p = .007, q = .05) and default networks (rho = 2.54, p = .02, q = .07). In preclinical AD, we did not observe a significant relationship between dopamine synthesis capacity and within-network stability. Moreover, none of the groups showed a relationship between dopamine synthesis capacity and between-network stability. 

The lack of an association between dopamine synthesis capacity and network stability in the context of preclinical AD prompted us to explore whether tau burden may be associated with a dysregulated network. We found that in the preclinical AD group, increased tau burden was associated with significant decreases in system segregation when transitioning from rest to task states (beta = -0.62, p = .01). This was true across all brain networks individually (visual: rho = 4.34, q < .01; somatomotor: rho = 3.31, q = .02; dorsal attention: rho = 3.19, q = .02; ventral attention: rho = 2.34, q = .047; limbic: rho = 2.30, q = .047; cognitive control: rho = 5.06, q < .01 ; default: rho  = 2.68, q = .04).

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that dopamine synthesis capacity and tau burden exert distinct and competing influences on brain network dynamics in aging. Greater rest-to-task stability and segregation were associated with better cognitive performance, yet both declined with age, consistent with prior work2,3,5. Importantly, dopamine synthesis capacity supported network stability in younger adults, particularly within associative networks, and this relationship was partially preserved in normal aging. However, in preclinical Alzheimer’s disease this dopaminergic influence was absent, and higher tau burden predicted widespread reductions in segregation during task engagement, suggesting tau-driven network integration may contribute to system instability.

Together, these results highlight that dopaminergic function promotes network stability, while tau pathology disrupts segregation, offering a mechanistic framework for understanding cognitive decline in aging and preclinical AD. These findings underscore the need to consider both neurochemical and pathological factors when modeling brain network reconfiguration and its role in cognitive health.
