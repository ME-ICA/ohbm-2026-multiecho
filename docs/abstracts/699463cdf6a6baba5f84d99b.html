Introduction

Introduction: Brain-age prediction models are increasingly used as sensitive imaging-derived markers of neurodegeneration and adverse brain health. However, brain-age is inherently non-specific, as various spatially distributed age-related and neuropathologic processes may converge to a similarly “older” brain-age.1 As a result, elevated brain-age alone provides limited insight into the underlying biological mechanisms responsible for the differences potentially identified by the model. Interrogating the anatomical features that contribute to predicted brain-age is therefore critical for interpreting differences across clinical populations. This study examined group differences in predicted brain-age associated with cognitive status and the regional image features contributing to these differences in a large, prospective aging cohort.

Methods

Methods: A slice-wise deep learning brain-age model (DeepBrainAge2) was applied to structural magnetic resonance images (MRI) from 1,966 participants in the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) study.3 Images were affine-transformed to a common template, skull-stripped, and intensity normalized; the predicted brain-age for each participant defined as the median of all slice-wise predictions. Adjudicated cognitive status (cognitively normal, CN; mild cognitive impairment, MCI; dementia) was defined using cognitive test data and informant interviews. Group differences in predicted brain-age across cognitive status were evaluated with linear models, adjusting for sex, and a linear and quadratic term for chronological age.

To characterize the regional imaging features contributing to predicted brain-age across cognitive status, saliency maps were generated for each participant, reflecting voxel-wise contributions to model predictions. First, the SmoothGrad salience technique4 was applied to map voxel-wise saliency for each participant. Resulting maps were normalized to an MRI template optimized for adults greater than 60 years of age5 and downsampled to 2mm isotropic to limit computational load. Finally, voxel-wise linear models compared salience between MCI/dementia and cognitively normal participants while adjusting for age and sex. Statistical inference was performed using permutation-based family-wise error (FWE) correction for the peak statistic; voxels with FWE-corrected p-value < 0.05 are reported.

Results

Results: Overall, the 1,966 included participants were a mean age of 77.7 years, 60.0% were female, 28.3% were self-reported Black race, 60.0% were cognitively normal, 34.3% had MCI and 5.7% had dementia. Predicted brain-age exhibited a monotonic increase across cognitive status, with a significantly increased predicted brain-age in MCI (β = 1.25 years, 95% CI = [0.93 - 1.56]) and a larger increase in participants with dementia (β = 3.13 years, 95% CI = [2.49 - 3.78]) relative to cognitively normal participants (Figure 1A). Saliency differences associated with cognitive status were identified in ventricular and periventricular regions adjacent to medial temporal, striatal, and brainstem structures, including areas near the temporal horn of the lateral ventricles, posterior parahippocampal cortex, fornix, caudate, and brainstem (Figure 1B).

Conclusion

Conclusion: Predicted brain-age increased in a graded manner across cognitive status, with progressively higher estimates in cognitively normal, mild cognitive impairment, and dementia groups after adjusting for chronological age and sex. The consistency of this monotonic pattern in a large, population-based cohort highlights the sensitivity of image-based brain-age prediction for detecting differences associated with cognitive status and neurodegenerative burden.

In this cohort, saliency differences associated with cognitive status localized to ventricular and periventricular regions adjacent to medial temporal and limbic structures, rather than being diffusely distributed across cortex. This localization is consistent with established patterns of neurodegeneration in dementia and suggests that saliency mapping can help distinguish disease-associated structural features from those reflecting normative aging alone. By revealing regionally specific contributors to elevated brain-age estimates, the saliency framework enhances the clinical interpretability and specificity of deep learning-based brain-age models and suggests a distinct imaging signature of cognitive impairment within an aging population. 

At the same time, the prominence of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)-adjacent regions highlights an important limitation of image-based brain-age models, namely their tendency to emphasize high-contrast features related to atrophy and ventricular expansion. Saliency mapping therefore serves a dual role: it enhances interpretability by identifying disease-relevant anatomical patterns, while also exposing model biases that may obscure tissue-specific changes. Given this predisposition of image-based models, saliency-based findings should be complemented by traditional morphometric analyses to confirm whether observed effects reflect underlying tissue loss, CSF expansion, or other related structural changes. Together, these findings demonstrate that saliency-based interpretation is essential for translating brain-age models from sensitive population-level markers into clinically meaningful tools for understanding and identifying neurodegenerative disease.
