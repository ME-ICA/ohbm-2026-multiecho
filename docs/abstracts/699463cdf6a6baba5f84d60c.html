Introduction

Temporal lobe epilepsy with hippocampal sclerosis (TLE-HS) is increasingly characterized not merely as a focal disorder, but as a systemic condition involving widespread cortical and subcortical atrophies that resemble accelerated biological aging.1 However, the pathophysiological substrates propagating this focal pathology to accelerated global senescence remain to be elucidated. The glymphatic system, a macroscopic waste clearance pathway, has emerged as a key driver of neurodegeneration.2 Recent longitudinal evidence indicates that glymphatic dysfunction (indexed by diffusion tensor image analysis along the perivascular space [DTI-ALPS]) precedes and predicts subsequent atrophies in Alzheimer’s disease, mediating the link between pathology and clinical progression.2 We hypothesized that this clearance failure acts as a shared mechanism driving the structure-pathology coupling in epilepsy. This study investigates whether glymphatic impairment mediates the association between pathological burden and accelerated global brain aging in TLE-HS, and further examines how disease chronicity modulates this pathological pathway to identify critical temporal windows for intervention.

Methods

82 participants underwent T1w and DTI scans (HCs, n=41; TLE-HS, n=41). To minimize laterality-related heterogeneity, only patients with left-sided hippocampal sclerosis were included. The DTI-ALPS index was calculated using a validated FSL-based pipeline (FSL v6.0.7) (Figure 1A),3 following the established method for assessing glymphatic function.4 Residual ipsilateral (left) hippocampal volume was segmented from T1-weighted images using FreeSurfer (v7.3.2) and served as an imaging marker for disease burden. Global atrophies was quantified as the Brain Predicted Age Difference (BrainAGE) (Figure 1B), Specifically, structural T1w images were preprocessed using the VBM-DARTEL pipeline, and BrainAGE was estimated via a Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) model, with final scores adjusted using linear bias correction to remove age-dependent bias.5 Statistical analyses included group comparisons of the ALPS index and Pearson correlations to assess associations between glymphatic function, hippocampal volume, and BrainAGE. Finally, a moderated mediation model was employed to investigate whether the ALPS index mediates the impact of focal hippocampal pathology on global brain aging, with disease duration included to moderate the pathway from hippocampal volume to glymphatic dysfunction.

Results

Patients with TLE-HS exhibited a significant reduction in the global ALPS index compared to HCs (Figure 2A). Laterally, the ipsilateral (left) ALPS index was significantly lower in patients (p < 0.05; Figure 2B), whereas the contralateral (right) ALPS showed a non-significant decreasing trend (p > 0.05; Figure 2C). Importantly, TLE-HS patients exhibited significantly higher BrainAGE (p < 0.01; Figure 2D), reflecting an accelerated biological aging process beyond chronological age expectations.

Correlation analyses revealed a robust negative correlation between the ALPS index and BrainAGE (r = -0.485, p = 0.001; Figure 2E), indicating that impaired glymphatic clearance is associated with accelerated biological aging. Conversely, a significant positive correlation was observed between the ALPS index and residual ipsilateral hippocampal volume (r = 0.181, p = 0.018; Figure 2F), suggesting that severer pathological burden (smaller volume) is linked to greater reduction of glymphatic activity., Notably, the ALPS index significantly mediated the association between hippocampal volume and BrainAGE (indirect effect: β = -0.145, 95% CI [-0.335, -0.013]; Figure 2G) , and the direct pathway between pathological burden and BrainAGE became non-significant (β = -0.246, p = 0.103) after controlling for glymphatic function, suggesting a full mediation effect.

Furthermore, moderated mediation analysis indicated that disease duration significantly modulated the pathway from hippocampal volume to the ALPS index (interaction β = -0.352, p = 0.005; Figure 2H). Crucially, the index of moderated mediation was statistically significant (index = 0.139, 95% CI [0.005, 0.384]), confirming that the indirect effect of hippocampal volume on BrainAGE via the ALPS index varies as a function of disease duration. Conditional process analysis further identified a critical temporal window: the mediating role of glymphatic dysfunction was significant only in patients with short (16 percentile) to moderate (50 percentile) disease duration, but decoupled in those with long duration (84 percentile)ththth (Figure 2I). This suggests that the coupling between focal pathology and glymphatic failure is predominantly active during the earlier and middle phases of the disease course.

Conclusion

Our findings provide in vivo evidence that glymphatic dysfunction may serve as a candidate pathophysiological mediator linking focal hippocampal sclerosis to global accelerated brain aging. Crucially, this mediation is most prominent during early-to-moderate disease stages, identifying a critical temporal window where focal pathology actively drives systemic clearance failure before the relationship decouples in chronic stages. These results support the hypothesis that epilepsy shares common neurobiological mechanisms with neurodegenerative diseases and suggest that therapeutic interventions targeting the glymphatic system may be most effective when administered early to mitigate progressive brain senescence.
