Introduction

Understanding the interplay between the structural and functional organization of the brain has been always a challenging task. While non-invasive techniques such as MRI offer a peek into a functioning brain, the low-resolution nature of the images limits the ability to resolve fine-grained structures. Conversely, histological techniques offer higher spatial details. Nevertheless, the two-dimensional nature of those techniques constraint the ability to capture the spatial complexity of biological tissues. 

Advances in microscopy, particularly light-sheet fluorescence microscopy (LSFM) and tissue-clearing techniques opened a new vista into imaging the whole brain in unprecedented details. LSFM, commonly implemented using single-plane illumination microscopy (SPIM), enables imaging of whole samples with an exceptional resolution while preserving the 3D context of the sample1. However, the sheer volume of data generated by such techniques is often daunting.

In the current work, we present fully automated pipelines, namely SPIMprep and SPIMquant (Fig. 1) that process LSFM data, integrate it with outputs from standard MRI processing workflows such as RABIES2, and map results into standard spaces such as the common coordinate framework v3 (CCFv3) space3.

α-synuclein is a ubiquitous protein, and its gene (SNCA) has been causally linked to Parkinson’s disease. Under normal circumstances, α-synuclein is predominantly soluble; however, in these disorders, the protein undergoes misfolding and aggregation rendering it toxic to the cells.

We showcase our pipelines using a multimodal LSFM/MRI dataset acquired from M83 male mice overexpressing the human A53T mutation in SNCA gene and injected with either α-synuclein preformed fibrils (PFFs) to enhance α-synuclein aggregation or with PBS to serve as controls. We investigate how misfolded α-synuclein as detected by LSFM relates to alterations in functional connectivity (FC) and to volumetric changes in different brain structures. 

Methods

At 6 months of age, 10 transgenic hemizygous M83 male mice4 received an intracerebral injection of either 2.5 μl of PFFs (12.5 μg from 5 mg/ml PFFs) or 2.5 μl of sterile PBS in the dorsal striatum of the right hemisphere. Eight weeks after the injection, the animals underwent a second surgery to implant a headpost on the skull to enable awake MRI scanning.

 

After 1-week recovery period, the animals went through habituation training in a mock scanner mimicking the environment inside the MRI scanner. The training was conducted over a period of 9 consecutive days with progressively increasing restraint duration and noise level.

Awake in vivo MR scanning sessions were performed on a 9.4T Bruker animal scanner. T2*-weighted images for fMRI were acquired with TE/TR of 12/1500 ms, flip angle of 60°, and a resolution of 300 x 300 x 500 µm3. A T2-weighted anatomical image was acquired in the same space using a resolution of 150 x 150 x 500 µm3. An additional GRE3D anatomical image was acquired with 150 x 150 x 150 µm3 resolution for volumetric analysis. 

The animals were monitored throughout the scan using an MR compatible camera. Video recordings were synchronized with the fMRI scanning. Videos were recorded at 20 frames per second using a custom script written with Python-opencv.

Following the MRI scans, the animals were transcardially perfused with ice-cold PBS with 10 U/ml heparin followed by 4% of ice-cold PFA. The extracted brains were post-fixed in 4% PFA at 4 °C for 24 hrs, then washed with PBS multiple times and stored in PBS solution with 0.02% sodium azide. The brain samples were then shipped to Lifecanvas Technologies for tissue clearing and LSFM imaging. The brain tissues were preserved using the SHIELD method and SmartClear was used for clearing. The brains were immunolabelled for phosphorylated α-synuclein (ps129) and propidium iodide (PI) was used for nuclear staining. The samples were imaged using SmartSPIM LSFM with a resolution of 1.8 x 1.8 x 4 µm3.

All fMRI data was preprocessed using RABIES workflow. The preprocessing steps included motion correction, coregisteration to anatomical images, brain masking, transforming to a common space template, nuisance regression, spatial smoothing, and high-pass filtering.

FC was computed as correlations between pairs of anatomical regions based on CCFv3 parcellations.

GRE3D images were cropped, padded, N4 bias-field corrected, and registered to a study-based template5. The modulated images were then compared for voxel-wise morphological changes between groups.

The left and right paws were tagged using DeepLabCut and the x, y coordinates of their movements were extracted. The first principal component of the x, y timeseries was used to extract peaks and duration of movement. Those peaks were later used as regressors for GLM fitting on the preprocessed resting-state data.

The TIFF image stacks for both channels were converted to ome-zarr format and organized in BIDS-compliant directory structure using SPIMprep (https://github.com/khanlab/SPIMprep). SPIMquant (https://github.com/khanlab/SPIMquant) was then used for masking and bias-field correction for the PI image. The corrected PI images were then registered to the high-resolution anatomical image of the CCFv3. The average field fraction was calculated for the α-synuclein images and the PI-to-CCFv3 transformations were applied to bring the α-synuclein images to the CCFv3 space. Regional field fractions were quantified for each region of the CCFv3 parcellations.

Transformations between RABIES anatomical space and CCFv3 anatomical image were calculated and applied to align all the α-synuclein images with the processed functional images.

Additionally, the individual preprocessed PI images were registered to their respective modulated 3D images, and the transformations were used to move the α-synuclein images to the anatomical space to make inferences about the relationship between misfolded α-synuclein and volumetric changes (Fig. 1).

Results

In this study, we present an integrated and fully automated workflow that combines LSFM with functional and structural MRI (Fig. 1). By bringing LSFM images to functional/structural MRI space, the workflow enables direct investigation of how cellular-level pathology relates to mesoscale brain structure and function. The pipeline takes raw images from different microscope channels, cures them, transforms them to standard space. When structural and/or functional MRI data is available for the same brain, the workflow further aligns the LSFM images to the space of the MRI images allowing joint multimodal analyses.

To demonstrate the capabilities of our workflow, we acquired structural and functional images from awake M83 mice injected with either PFFs or PBS. Following the scans, the animals were cleared, labelled for misfolded synuclein and nuclear stain, and imaged using LSFM.

 

Misfolded α-synuclein spreads caudally:

Labelled LSFM ps129 images show a widespread accumulation of misfolded α-synuclein (Fig. 2). The pathology starts in the right dorsal striatum where the PFFs were injected and subsequently spreads rostrally and caudally. Notably, the concentration of misfolded synuclein appears to be higher in the posterior regions, including the pallidum, the thalamus, the hypothalamus, the midbrain, and the hindbrain. In contrast, α-synuclein burden was comparatively modest in the cerebellum as well as in more anterior regions such as the olfactory regions, the isocortex, and the hippocampus as reflected by lower average field fraction.

 

Synuclenopathy disrupts FC:

Synuclenopathy leads to widespread disruption of FC between different brain networks. Bilateral correlations in nodes with higher synuclenopathy showed the most pronounced disruption, particularly the thalamus, the hypothalamus, and the hindbrain. Within the same hemisphere, nodes with elevated levels of ps129, showed reduced correlation strength with each other such as those between the pallidum and the hindbrain.

Long-range connections were also affected. Connectivity between the hippocampus and contralateral regions with high burden of misfolded synuclein such as the palladium, the hypothalamus, and the hindbrain drops significantly compared to those of PBS-injected animals.

Synuclenopathy leads to bimodal volumetric changes:

Relationship between volumetric changes and concentration of synuclenopathy seems to follow a bimodal distribution where modest doses of misfolded α-synuclein can lead to expansion in structures like the cerebellum and the hippocampus. Inversely, elevated doses can lead to shrinkage especially in more posterior structures such as the thalamus and the midbrain as the synuclenopathy spreads caudally to the spinal cord.

Reduced somatomotor cortex activation precedes motor symptoms:

Despite limited spread of misfolded α-synuclein to the somatomotor/somatosensory, spontaneous paw movement showed reduced activation in PFFs-injected animals relative to controls. The spread of synuclenopathy caudally to brainstem and further to the spinal cord can lead to disruptions in ascending sensory pathways which potentially affects cortex excitability

This diminished activation precedes any motor symptoms and can be harnessed to function as an early biomarker.

Conclusion

In summary, we present a fully automated approach that integrates the cellular-level details of LSFM microscopy with the mesoscale-level details of functional and structural MRI. Bringing all these data to the same common space allows joint inferences of how cellular events can affect brain networks synchrony and structure. We showcase our pipeline with a dataset from a mouse model of synuclenopathy. We demonstrate how the spread of misfolded α-synuclein leads to disruption of synchrony between networks, changes the volumes of brain structures, and affects the normal function of somatomotor cortex.
