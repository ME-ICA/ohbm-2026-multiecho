Introduction

Recent theoretical developments in naturalistic reinforcement learning^1 highlight that human behavior in real-world contexts emerges from continuous, dynamic, and context-dependent interactions. Traditional laboratory paradigms rarely capture this complexity, limiting their ability to characterize real-world decision making. Naturalistic behavioral paradigms address this limitation by providing a framework for characterizing latent decision processes as they unfold over time. Within this framework, reinforcement learning models can recover latent goals, underlying reward functions, and internal states as they evolve during task performance.

Recent work further demonstrates that combining real-time behavioral tasks with deep neural network-based inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) reveals latent reward processes, with associated neural signatures of decision making identified using fMRI.^2 Building on this approach, we developed a real-time pedestrian behavior paradigm, the crosswalk task, in which participants navigate a player to cross roads as quickly as possible while evading approaching vehicles. This task is designed to capture continuous, moment-to-moment decisions under dynamic environmental constraints.

By applying IRL to each participant’s state–action trajectories from the crosswalk task, we explore whether individualized reward functions can be learned to capture preferences for specific actions under varying environmental conditions. The resulting reward representations provide a compact description of moment-to-moment risky decision strategies in dynamic environments. Building on this representation, we investigate how individual differences, including anxiety and impulsivity, may be reflected in reward-based decision policies.

Together, this work introduces the crosswalk task as a naturalistic reinforcement learning paradigm for probing latent decision processes in real time that underlie variability in human behavior.

Methods

Fifty-two healthy adults (29 females) aged 19 to 43 years participated in the study. Each participant completed self-report questionnaires assessing anxiety (STAI) and impulsivity (BIS), and performed the crosswalk task (Figure 1a). The task included a brief practice period followed by two 20-minute sessions separated by a 5-minute rest.

The goal of the crosswalk task is to reach the end of the map as fast as possible. If the player reaches the end of the map within the 30 second time limit, bonus score is given based on the amount of time left (Clear). Failure to reach the finish line within the time limit doesn’t result in any penalty (Time over). If the player is hit by a car on the road, the episode ends immediately with penalty (Run over). After an episode ends from one of the three results, next episode starts with different road settings after 5 second pause.

Participants can control the player at every time step using the keyboard, choosing one of five possible actions (up, down, left, right, or do nothing) based on the current state of the environment. The map consists of roads and no-car areas, with roads varying in length (2 or 4 tiles long) and car compositions. Cars differed in size, speed, and color. All the cars on the same road had the same color, and the penalty from getting run over by a car corresponds to the car color (red = –1000, yellow = –500, green = –100). A crosswalk can randomly appear for each road, and standing in or in front of the crosswalk makes the cars pause, allowing safe road crossing without the risk of getting run over.

To infer the reward functions underlying each participant’s behavior, we applied adversarial inverse reinforcement learning (AIRL^3; Figure 1b) to each participant’s state–action trajectories, training one AIRL model per participant for a total of 52 reward networks. Because the crosswalk task was implemented using the Gymnasium library,^4 all the behavior data was recorded as sequences of states and actions at each time step. For data augmentation, a mirrored left–right version of each trajectory was added, resulting in approximately 80 minutes of trajectory data per participant. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) was used as the generator algorithm within the AIRL framework to train the policies. All models shared the same hyperparameters, differing only in total training timesteps. Using the resulting reward networks, we examined how IRL-derived reward signals under specific conditions related to participants’ self-reported anxiety and impulsivity.

After identifying several IRL-derived metrics that showed meaningful associations with self-report measures, we examined which combinations of these features most effectively predicted individual differences in self-reported anxiety and impulsivity. For each construct, we applied the LASSO (least absolute shrinkage and selection operator)^5 regression, which minimizes the residual sum of squares under an L1-norm constraint on the coefficients. This constraint shrinks coefficients toward zero and drives some exactly to zero, thereby performing automatic variable selection while stabilizing estimation. The goal of this analysis was to compare the relative informativeness of IRL-based behavioral metrics and overall task performance measures as indicators of individual differences in anxiety and impulsivity.

Results

Several performance measures from the highway task were significantly correlated with anxiety. Overall, higher anxiety led to lower average score (r=-0.298, p<0.05). This can be explained first by reduced bonus scores from decreased clear ratio (r=–0.275, p<0.05), indicating that higher anxiety led to failure to reach the end of the map within the time limit. Higher anxiety was also associated with the increased probability of getting run over by red cars (r=0.284, p<0.05), which carry the highest penalty of –1000. Success ratio of crossing roads differed based on the condition of the roads.

Road-crossing success showed a context-dependent relationship with anxiety. When crosswalks were visible, higher anxiety was associated with reduced success on long and high-penalty roads, with the strongest effect when both factors were present (r=-0.322, p<0.05). In contrast, under non-visible crosswalk conditions, higher anxiety was linked to increased success on long roads with red cars (r=0.354, p<0.05). Thus, five performance measures were chosen as indicators of anxiety: average score, clear ratio, run over ratio (high penalty), road crossing success ratio (crosswalk visible, crosswalk not visible).

Analysis of the IRL-derived rewards revealed additional insights into context-specific decision making. By mapping the inferred reward function onto each participant’s state–action trajectory, we found that overall mean IRL rewards begin to decline sharply when only 10 seconds remain and the end of the map is not visible. This aligns with the in-task cue that the remaining-time display turns red at the 10-second mark.

It’s notable that individuals with higher anxiety showed even lower IRL rewards around the 10 second mark (Figure 2a). When looking at 0~10 seconds left time bin, negative correlations between anxiety and IRL rewards are consistently found in safe conditions (no car area, crosswalk visible, short road), but not in dangerous conditions (Figure 2b). By combining all the safe conditions, we get stronger negative correlation (Figure 2c), which is defined as the under 10 seconds left metric.

As the goal of the task is to reach the top of the map, notable individual differences were found in IRL rewards regarding the move up action. While player was crossing the road, higher anxiety was positively correlated with moving up early when there the tail of the car was in front of the player instead of waiting for it to completely pass the player (r=-0.436, p<0.01). This trend becomes stronger as the possible penalty of being run over increases (Figure 2d), with the high penaly condition showing the strongest correlation (r=-0.449, p<0.001). Evaluating the last action taken before getting run over showed that high anxiety was positively correlated with moving up before death (r=0.320, p<0.05), but not with other actions. When a car is approaching at the player, high anxiety was also positively correlated with moving up to evade (r=0.318, p<0.05). Thus, we get three more IRL reward based metrics: move up at car tail, move up before death, move up to evade cars.

Based on the derived metrics, LASSO was applied using three subsets of features. Using only the performance-based features predicted STAI scores with a moderate correlation (r=0.32), wheras combining all the features together led to moderate-to-strong correlation (r=0.44). Using only the IRL reward–based features showed even stronger predictive power (r=0.51; Figure 2e), with all four features retained as significant predictors in the model (Figure 2f). This indicates that the IRL-derived metrics captured most of the predictive signal, and that incorporating performance-based features offered limited additional improvement.

None of the identified metrics were associated with impulsivity or predicted BIS scores (performance-only: r=0.13; IRL-only: r=−0.38; combined: r=−0.02), indicating that these metrics are specific to anxiety rather than impulsivity.

Conclusion

This study introduces the crosswalk task as a naturalistic, reinforcement learning–based paradigm for analyzing real-time human decision making under dynamic and uncertain conditions. By embedding continuous action selection within a structured yet ecologically meaningful environment, the task captures moment-to-moment trade-offs between speed, safety, and risk in a way that is difficult to achieve with traditional trial-based laboratory paradigms. Importantly, the task is designed as a modular environment in which key dimensions such as time pressure, environmental risk, and the availability of supportive cues can be independently manipulated, allowing flexible experimental control within a unified task structure.

Across multiple levels of analysis, the crosswalk task proved sensitive to individual differences in anxiety. Overall performance measures revealed context-dependent changes in behavior as a function of anxiety, while IRL uncovered latent reward dynamics and action preferences that were not apparent from task-level outcomes alone. In particular, IRL-derived reward representations captured how anxious individuals differentially value time pressure, safety cues, and risky action opportunities, and these reward-based metrics predicted self-reported anxiety more strongly than performance measures. In contrast, neither performance- nor IRL-derived metrics showed reliable associations with impulsivity, indicating a dissociation between anxiety-related valuation processes and impulsivity as a distinct construct. This pattern may suggest that the inferred reward representations specifically reflect anxiety-related decision processes rather than general response tendencies or action vigor, and we are currently investigating further if we can also measure impulsivity using the task.

Beyond its behavioral utility, the crosswalk task offers a flexible platform for systems and cognitive neuroscience. The task is continuous, time-resolved, and formally grounded in a reinforcement learning framework, making it well suited for combination with neuroimaging methods such as fMRI, EEG, or MEG. Trial-by-trial and state-by-state reward signals inferred through IRL can be aligned with neural activity to examine how value representations, threat sensitivity, and goal-directed control evolve over time in the human brain. This enables a direct mapping between experimentally controlled environmental states, inferred computational variables, and neural dynamics, providing a concrete bridge between behavior, computation, and brain-level mechanisms.

Taken together, the crosswalk task demonstrates how naturalistic behavioral paradigms, when paired with modern reinforcement learning approaches, can bridge the gap between real-world decision making and computational neuroscience. By providing a principled way to infer latent reward processes during continuous behavior, this framework opens new avenues for studying the neural mechanisms of anxiety and other affective traits. More broadly, the crosswalk task offers a reusable and scalable tool for future neuroimaging and translational research, aligning with ongoing efforts in neuroscience to study cognition and affect in settings that more closely approximate real-world behavior.
