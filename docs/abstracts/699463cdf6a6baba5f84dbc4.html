Introduction

Functional activation across the whole brain spontaneously fluctuates during rest. These dynamic patterns are a key characteristic of the brain’s functional organisation and relate to behaviour, cognitive function, and psychiatric disease. Physical activity, including exercise and sleep, has substantial effects on the brain on multiple levels, including changes in neurotransmitters, proteins, and brain volume ^1. High levels of physical activity correlate with structural and functional differences across the brain. Over the lifespan, a more physically active lifestyle may have protective effects on cardiovascular and neurodegenerative disease and is associated with better cognitive performance and improved mental health. Importantly, physical activity also affects blood flow through lowering blood pressure, increasing blood volume and oxygen, and lowering blood and plasma viscosity, all of which affect BOLD signal. Although physical activity and brain function are so intricately linked, it has not been considered that physical activity may in fact drive brain dynamics. In this case, wrist-worn physical activity sensors could be used to predict an individual brain’s dynamic functional organisation. The recent advent of biobanks allows linking patterns of physical activity with neuroimaging data in large, heterogeneous samples of participants. Rather than summarising physical activity or relying on self-reports, we here model the specific temporal patterns of physical activity and sleep from week-long accelerometer recordings.

Methods

We here use free-living physical activity and resting-state functional MRI recordings from 22,000 volunteers (ages 40-69 at recruitment) of the UK Biobank. Physical activity was measured from Axivity AX3 tri-axial wrist-worn (dominant) accelerometers for a period of 7 days (24 hours per day) at a sampling frequency of 100 Hz with a dynamic range of +- 8g. Devices were calibrated and data preprocessed as described in Doherty et al., 2017 ^2. Briefly, preprocessing included flagging invalid values, transformation into vector magnitudes, machine noise removal, and gravity removal. Non-wear time was detected as >60 minutes consecutive stationary episodes, and imputed for early preprocessing, and used as mask for the final model. Relevant time and frequency information was then extracted for each non-overlapping 30-second window. These feature vectors were used as input to a Hidden Markov Model (HMM) to classify each window into four activity classes: sedentary, light activity, moderate-vigorous activity, and sleep. This resulted in a categorical activity timeseries of 4 activities x 20,160 timepoints (30 second epochs over 7 days) per subject.

Acquisition and preprocessing of fMRI data are described in detail in Miller et al., 2016 ^3. Briefly, fMRI data were acquired on 3T Siemens Skyra scanners at a TR of 735 ms for a total of 490 timepoints. Preprocessing included minimal spatial preprocessing followed by temporal preprocessing (ICA-FIX cleanup). Voxel-level fMRI data were transformed into surface space and parcellated using dual regression on the 50-dimensional group-ICA surface components from the Human Connectome Project (HCP; ^4, ^5). We then split the data into training and test set. We fit an HMM with a Gaussian observation model to the parcellated rsfMRI data of the training set to estimate four functional states. The trained model was applied to the test set and state fractional occupancies (FO, the proportion of the timeseries occupied by each state) were extracted for all subjects in training and test set.

We then trained a 1D convolutional neural network (CNN, ResNet) to predict FO of the functional brain states from the individual physical activity timeseries.

Results

Four brain states were identified from the rsfMRI data resembling canonical resting-state functional networks: lateral visual, dorsal attention, default-mode (DMN) and frontoparietal networks. Our activity CNN could predict FO of these four brain states from physical activity timeseries at good accuracy both in- and out-of-sample. We then used gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM) to interrogate the trained CNN and explain the learned link between physical activities and brain states. Grad-CAM revealed that, while time periods of mainly sedentary activity were most relevant for predicting lateral visual, dorsal attention, and frontoparietal networks, periods of sleep were most relevant for predicting DMN occurrence. This indicates that there is a distinct connection between different types of physical activity and the observed patterns of brain function. Other activity classes (light and moderate-vigorous activity) were less relevant for predicting brain states, likely because they are heavily underrepresented in the dataset. Both light and moderate-vigorous activity were mostly relevant for predicting the dorsal attention network.

Conclusion

Physical activity and sleep are intricately linked with brain function. We here showed that the temporal patterns of free-living physical activity as derived from accelerometer recordings can be used to predict the occurrence of functional brain states at rest. Importantly, while the prediction of most brain states was linked to sedentary behaviour, the most relevant predictors for the DMN were periods of sleep. This reflects the close link between the DMN and sleep quality, and it suggests a potential biological relevance for the patterns identified by the network. Future interventional studies may investigate more closely how temporal structure in physical activity modulates resting-state brain dynamics and its downstream effects on brain health.
