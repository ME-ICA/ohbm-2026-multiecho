Introduction

Fear conditioning paradigms provide a framework for studying aversive learning and its neural substrates in humans. Functional MRI studies have consistently implicated a distributed network of regions largely overlapping with the salience network in the acquisition of conditioned fear responses. Hence, fear conditioning has been proposed as a psychobiological model of anxiety vulnerability, although evidence linking laboratory-based fear learning to real-world anxiety remains mixed.

A limitation of previous research is the predominant focus on fear acquisition as a static phenomenon, with less attention to the temporal dynamics of learning and to the flexibility required to update threat–safety associations. Fear reversal learning addresses this gap by probing the inhibition of previously learned fear responses and the formation of new safety representations. Neurobiologically, reversal learning has been associated with increased engagement of prefrontal regions (i.e, vmPFC), which plays a central role in safety learning. Alterations in these processes have been implicated in anxiety disorders.

In parallel, anxiety is increasingly conceptualized as a dynamic construct, characterized by fluctuations, abrupt symptom increases, and temporal persistence. Ecological Momentary Assessment (EMA) enables these dimensions to be quantified in daily life, offering a bridge between experimental paradigms and real-world emotional functioning. Moreover, accumulating evidence suggests that sex differences may modulate fear learning and anxiety-related neural circuitry, yet such effects remain underexplored.

We combined fMRI measures of fear conditioning and reversal with six months of EMA-derived anxiety dynamics in a large sample of healthy adults. By examining early and late learning phases and adopting sex-sensitive analyses, we aimed to identify neural markers of threat and safety learning that predict individual differences in the temporal dynamics of anxiety in daily life.

Methods

Participants and Recruitment.

Participants were recruited from a larger pool (N=840) screened using the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI-T) to ensure a stratified distribution across anxiety quartiles. The final sample consisted of 173 healthy adults (age 18–36). Following a dimensional approach to anxiety, individuals with current Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD) or Social Anxiety Disorder (SAD) were not excluded.

Experimental Task.

Participants underwent a neuroimaging assessment including a delay differential fear conditioning and reversal task. The conditioned stimuli (CSs) consisted of pictures of blue and yellow spheres presented for 2 seconds. The unconditioned stimulus (US) was a brief (250 ms) electric shock delivered to the left hand, calibrated individually to be unpleasant but not painful. The protocol included three phases:

  • Habituation: Presentation of CSs without US.
  • Acquisition (Conditioning): One sphere (CS+) was paired with the US (33% reinforcement rate), while the other (CS-) was never paired.
  • Reversal: The reinforcement contingencies were switched without signaling; the previous CS+ became the new CS- (safe), and the previous CS- became the new CS+ (paired with US).

Skin Conductance Responses (SCR) were recorded simultaneously to validate physiological arousal.

MRI Acquisition and Pre-processing.

Imaging data were acquired on a 3T Phillips Ingenia scanner with a 32-channel head coil. Functional sequences used single-shot gradient-echo EPI (TR=2000 ms, TE=25 ms, voxel size 3x3x3 mm). A high-resolution T1-weighted anatomical scan was acquired for registration. Data were pre-processed using fMRIPrep 1.4.1 13^1, involving motion correction, susceptibility distortion correction, and co-registration to the T1w reference. Functional data were normalized to MNI152NLin2009cAsym space and smoothed with a 6mm FWHM isotropic Gaussian kernel.

Longitudinal and Psychometric Assessment.

Following the fMRI session, participants entered a 6-month Ecological Momentary Assessment (EMA) period. A custom smartphone app^2 triggered three pseudorandomized notifications daily (morning, afternoon, evening). At each prompt, participants rated symptoms of anxious apprehension, muscle tension, worry, and avoidance on 0–100 Visual Analog Scales (VAS).

To capture distinct components of emotional dynamics, four composite indices were derived from the self-reported metrics: Average Score; Probability of Acute Change (PAC), defined as the probability of an emotional rating exceeding the 90th percentile of the total distribution; Emotional Variability (operationalized as Mean Successive Squared Differences, MSSD) , reflecting the instability of affect; and Emotional Inertia. Inertia was calculated using the first-order (lag-1) autocorrelation of repeated EMA measures. These variables were computed for the first month, the sixth month, and the entire six-month follow-up period.

Neuroimaging Analysis and Brain-Behavior Correlations.

Neuroimaging data for both the conditioning and reversal tasks were analyzed by segmenting the experimental paradigms into early (trials 1–8) and late (trials 9–16) phases. The analytical strategy focused on identifying differential activations through between-phase comparisons, as well as characterizing common neural patterns sustained across all phases of the experiment. Furthermore, Region of Interest (ROI) analyses were conducted to quantify the specific degree of activation in key structures implicated in anxiety circuitry, such as the amygdala and the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC), within each of the four experimental phases. Finally, correlation analyses were performed to examine the associations between neural activation in these regions and the computed EMA dynamic metrics (Average Score, PAC, Emotional Variability, and Inertia), thereby linking laboratory-based neural markers of fear learning with ecological dimensions of anxiety.

Results

Neural Correlates of Conditioning and Reversal.

The analysis of 173 healthy participants (N=87 women, N=86 men) revealed a robust neural signature of aversive learning (p < 0.05 FWE-corrected voxel level). A conjunction analysis across all four experimental phases (early/late conditioning and early/late reversal) identified a core network that was consistently recruited regardless of the learning stage. This common activation pattern comprised the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC), bilateral anterior insulae (AIC), and extensive subcortical clusters including the thalamus and striatum, reflecting a sustained engagement of the salience network throughout the task. ^3,4

Beyond this common core, phase-specific contrasts unveiled distinct temporal dynamics. During the early phase of fear acquisition, we observed heightened activity in regions partially overlapping with the posterior Default Mode Network (DMN), specifically the posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) and angular gyri, as well as the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC). Region of Interest (ROI) analyses indicated that these areas exhibited a sharp habituation profile, with activity dropping significantly as contingencies were consolidated. Conversely, the early phase of reversal learning—where participants must inhibit the previously learned fear response—was characterized by the specific recruitment of the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC). Crucially, during this early reversal phase, we found a significant positive correlation (r=0.30 p < .001) between vmPFC activation and amygdala reactivity, suggesting a functional coupling between prefrontal regulatory regions and subcortical affect-generating nodes during the updating of threat contingencies.

Brain-Behavior Associations.

Integration of neuroimaging data with longitudinal Ecological Momentary Assessment (EMA) metrics—specifically the Probability of Acute Change (PAC), Emotional Variability, and Inertia —revealed distinct, sex-specific biomarkers of anxiety dynamics.

In men, the probability of experiencing acute anxiety peaks (PAC) during the first month of follow-up was positively associated with amygdala activation during the early conditioning phase (r = 0.34 ; p < 0.01). This suggests that in males, the initial subcortical reactivity to threat cues is a predictor of real-world emotional instability.

In contrast, anxiety dynamics in women were linked to the neural correlates of safety learning and sensory processing. Specifically, higher vmPFC activation during early reversal was predictive of higher PAC scores at both 1-month (r = 0.31 ; p < 0.05) and 6-month follow-ups (r = 0.30 ; p < 0.01). Furthermore, women exhibited a significant association between right thalamus activation during early conditioning and higher emotional inertia (autoregressive persistence of emotional states) over the six-month period (r = 0.36 ; p < 0.01). These findings highlight a dissociation where anxiety vulnerability is indexed by threat acquisition circuitry in men, but by safety learning and sensory integration circuitry in women.

Conclusion

Our findings provide large-sample evidence that while fear learning and reversal are anchored by a stable, core salience network, successful adaptation to changing threat contingencies requires the phase-specific engagement of distinct neural systems. Specifically, the recruitment of regions overlapping with the posterior Default Mode Network during early conditioning , followed by the engagement of the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) during early reversal, suggests a dynamic transition from internal processing to active regulatory control and safety learning.

Crucially, the observed brain–anxiety associations reveal a significant sex-specific dissociation in the neural predictors of real-world emotional dynamics. We identify that anxiety vulnerability is indexed by initial threat reactivity (amygdala activation) in men, whereas in women, it is tied to the efficacy of safety learning and regulatory circuitry (vmPFC activation). This suggests that fear conditioning paradigms capture meaningful, biologically distinct phenotypes of inter-individual variability that would be obscured in mixed-sex analyses.

Collectively, these results support the utility of fMRI fear learning paradigms as a robust experimental framework for probing the neural mechanisms of anxiety. However, they also underscore the necessity of adopting longitudinal, multidimensional, and sex-sensitive approaches to fully establish the translational value of these neural markers in predicting daily-life symptomatology.
