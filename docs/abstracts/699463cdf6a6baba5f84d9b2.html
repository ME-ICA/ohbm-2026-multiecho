Introduction

Aberrant neural responses to drug-associated cues are a hallmark of addictive disorders. Significant evidence has shown that incentive salience, negative emotionality, and cognitive control are impaired in addiction and recovery 1. fMRI drug cue reactivity (FDCR) paradigms have been widely used to probe these mechanisms, yet their application as mechanistic biomarkers in addiction treatment remains limited. However, FDCR is still pending acceptance by the FDA’s Biomarker Qualification Program (BQP) and has not received official FDA approval. Methodological variability, a lack of replications, and differences in data about reliability and validity have limited the application of FDCR2. Moreover, the selection of regions of interest (ROIs) is a critical issue that significantly affects the results of FDCR. In standard ROIs, it is often assumed that only the intensity of the BOLD signal or certain previous research studies define the analysis, potentially impairing replicability. In this work, we introduce a methodological framework to select ROIs using both previous knowledge and the reliability of signals with the aim of maximizing FDCR analysis and improving replicability. This work focuses on standardized, reliable, and innovative techniques that seek to achieve a FDCR recognized as a valid biomarker with the capability to identify biological neural alterations in recovery from addiction.

Methods

An fMRI drug cue reactivity task designed with standard picture blocks with cues from opioid or meth pictures. Participants is recruited from 88 men with methamphetamine use disorder (MUD) (n=66, age= 35.86 ± 8.47), and with opioid use disorder (OUD) (n=22, age = 34.4 ± 6.26) who underwent two fMRI scans, two weeks apart. The participants admitted to a residential abstinence-based treatment center (12&12 center) in Tulsa, Oklahoma, and were abstinent in the residential program or its aftercare transitional living programs. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before the scans and the study was approved by the Western IRB (WIRB Protocol #20171742).

Functional data analysis was performed in AFNI for both Meth and Opioid users. fMRI preprocessing included three pre-steady-state images, despiking, slice-time correction, realignment, transformation to MNI space, and 4 mm Gaussian full-width-at-half-maximum (FWHM) smoothing, regressing out the first three polynomial terms, and six motion parameters. TRs are censored by excessive motion, defined as the Euclidean norm derived from six motion parameters that are greater than 0.3.

First-level fixed effects analysis was performed on individual imaging data using a general linear model (GLM). For each participant, a contrast image of drug > neutral was constructed and a voxel-wise whole-brain analysis was used, and reported with P < 0.001. For each MUD participant, the Brainnetome atlas (BNA) parcellation was applied, with a total of 246 cortical and subcortical regions, and the average beta weight was estimated for all subregions of BNA. The significance level was adjusted for multiple comparisons using FDR correction (α = 0.002). 

The two-way mixed-effects model Intraclass Correlation Coefficients ICC(3,k) was performed in an ROI based analysis for the average signal within each BNA region. The voxel-wise ICCs using 3dLME to fit linear mixed effects models which included only the intercept and a random effect for subject. This partitions the variance into within and between subject components and allows the ICC to be calculated as the proportion of total variance that is between subjects.

For the ROI selection algorithm, fourteen brain areas were identified based on a recent systematic review of the literature3, which demonstrated that fMRI drug cue reactivity robustly engages neural circuits underlying incentive salience, negative emotionality, and cognitive control, including reward/punishment, habit, salience, memory, self-directed, and executive networks during drug cue exposure. Within each area, regions were ranked separately according to mean signal magnitude and ICC(3,k). The sum of these two ranks (sum-rank) were then computed, and regions with the highest sum-rank within each area were selected as the final ROIs.

Pearson correlations were calculated between the absolute signal magnitude of the selected ROIs and behavioral and craving-related measures, like pre and post scan Visual Analog Scale(VAS), Meth Withdrawal Scale (MWS), Meth Craving Scale (MCS), Desire for Drug Qouestionnaire (DDQ), Meth Vas Craving Score (MVCS),  Meth Cue-Reactivity Screening (MCRS), as well as physiological measures including heart rate and respiration.

Results

In the whole brain analysis, the drug > neutral contrast is significant in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (VMPFC) and inferior frontal gyri (IFG), middle temporal gyri (MTG), amygdala, striatum, insula and secondary visual processing area, and the neutral > drug is significant in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), and inferior parietal lobule (IPL).

In an ROI based reliability analysis among 22 OUDs, 96 ROIs out of 246 BNA regions had point estimates for ICCs over 0.5, with 39 areas between 0.6 and 0.7 and 12 areas over 0.7, which included bilateral dorsal IFG (left: 0.73, right: 0.72), right caudal IFG (0.85), bilateral rostral IFG (left: 0.80, right: 0.80), right caudal MTG (0.86), right anterior superior temporal sulcus (0.74), right rostral lingual gyrus (0.74), left middle occipital gyrus (0.75), right lateral occipital cortex (0.70), right lateral superior occipital gyrus (0.72). The ROIs with ICC less than 0.3 were observed mainly in the superior frontal gyri, superior temporal gyri, parahippocampal gyri, postcentral gyri, insular gyri, limbic, amygdala, basal ganglia, and thalamus.

Based on different cognitive functions, we limit our testing space for the main aims of the study by selecting from (123 in each left and right sides of the brain), then 14 brain areas based on the literature are selected. The number of BNA regions in each area ranged from 4 to 28. Within the frontal cortex, we have areas (A1) DLPFC (28 regions), (A2) IFG (12 regions), and (A3) VMPFC (12 regions). Within the temporal cortex, we have (A4) MTG (22 regions) and (A5) Parahippocampal gyrus (PHG) (12 regions). Within the parietal cortex, we have (A6) IPL (12 regions) and (A7) Precuneus (8 regions). The insular cortex (12 regions) is selected as A8. Within the cingulate cortex (limbic lobe) we have 8 regions for (A9) ACC. Posterior Cingulate Cortex (PCC) contains 6 regions for A10. Within the subcortical areas, we have selected (A11) Amygdala (4 regions), (A12) Hippocampus (4 regions), (A13) ventral striatum (6 regions) and (A14) dorsal striatum (4 regions).

The winning ROI in the ACC area exhibiting the highest combined ranking in the average absolute signal and reliability. 

Pearson correlation analyses between the final selected ROIs and behavioral and physiological measures indicates that the left ITG within Area 4 showed a significant positive FDR-corrected correlation with reaction time (r=0.43, P=0.003). In contrast, the right DLPFC from Area 1 (r=−0.35, P=0.03) and the right IFG from Area 2 (β =−0.39, P=0.01) exhibited significant negative FDR-corrected associations with respiratory amplitude during drug-cue blocks.

Conclusion

Measuring brain activity using task-based fMRI has been one of the key areas of interest for the creation of biomarkers. However, there are many studies that report high activation in brain regions but low reliability in these regions4. Low reliability measurements cannot be used as biomarkers since these cannot predict the outcome. A biomarker designed to predict the future risks of a particular patient should provide stable values. A patient cannot obtain a high score on one examination and a low score on another. That is why the detection of meaningful and highly reliable values is crucial for the creation of biomarkers. Therefore, region selection strategies that consider reliability and signal intensity have a stronger chance of qualifying FDCR as a biomarker.

In this study, a data-driven framework for selecting ROIs with an emphasis on areas with both large mean signal response and good reliability was developed. Using this framework, the average signal values were determined using 66 MUDs, whereas the signal reliability was determined using test-retest analysis of fMRI data from an independent group of 22 OUDs who were subjected to two separate scanning sessions. Using established data from the addiction literature and the neural network for addiction, the entire brain was parceled into 14 major functional areas. Each area was then parcelled into sub-areas using the BNA atlas. Within each area, both the absolute strength of signal as well as the corresponding strength of signal reliability were determined, and areas with the biggest combined strength were selected as the winning areas for the ROIs. This is basically aimed at improving the strength of FDCR-derived indices by considering both strength and reliability.

Regions within the VMPFC, MTG, and amygdala showed the highest average absolute signal. The VMPFC is a major hub for emotional, social, and self-related information processing and serves as a region for connecting large-scale networks that influence emotional processing, decision-making, self-perception, and social cognition in general. The amygdala is a core component of the limbic system, and a major center for processing and controlling emotions, linking emotions to other brain functions such as memory, learning, and other senses. The MTG becomes active during auditory and visual emotional recognition5.

Regions in the DLPFC, IFG, MTG, insula, and ventral striatum (vStr) exhibited the highest levels of reliability. The DLPFC is involved in executive functions, working memory, and cognitive control, such as planning, inhibition, and reasoning. The right IFG plays a central role in response inhibition. The insula integrates bodily state information into higher-order cognitive and emotional processes and has been implicated in conscious urges, such as drug craving. Similar to the amygdala, the vStr is a primary area for emotion regulation, and, in particular, is involved when individuals are responding to rewarding stimuli, and shows heightened activity when individuals are presented with both rewarding and aversive experiences. As expected, the regions selected through the combination of signal and reliability are involved in cognitive control, motivational salience, and negative emotional circuits.

Finally, the selected region in the ACC shows the highest average absolute signal and reliability, suggesting that the ACC functions as a central hub within addiction-related neural networks, and that investigating functional alterations in this region may contribute to validating FDCR as a reliable biomarker, particularly given established links between ACC activity and relapse.
