Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD), bipolar disorder (BD), and schizophrenia spectrum disorder (SSD) show residual overlap in clinical symptoms, yet the extent to which they share common versus diagnosis-specific neurobiological alterations remains unclear. Identifying functional connectivity (FC) patterns that cut across diagnostic boundaries—and linking them to dimensional clinical phenotypes spanning early-life adversity, personality, and symptom domains—may clarify shared pathophysiological mechanisms and inform dimensional treatment strategies.

Methods

Resting-state fMRI data were analyzed from 2,323 participants (1,127 healthy controls (HC), 821 MDD, 183 BD, 192 SSD) from the FOR2107 cohort following stringent quality control. FC was computed between 52 independent components derived from group ICA, yielding 1,326 edges.

Step 1 – Identification of transdiagnostic FC alterations:
General linear models with Freedman–Lane permutation testing and Westfall–Young step-down correction (FWER < 0.05; 5,000 permutations) were used to identify FC differences between patients and HC. Leave-one-diagnosis-out (LODO) analyses classified significant edges as transdiagnostic (shared across all three disorders), shared between two diagnoses (BD+SSD, MDD+BD, MDD+SSD), or diagnosis-specific.

Step 2 – Brain–behavior integration:
In 1,151 patients, sparse generalized canonical correlation analysis (SGCCA) was used to identify multivariate associations between the significant FC edges (n = 94) and 88 clinical and behavioral variables spanning 10 domains: childhood adversity, socioeconomic factors, personality traits, physical health, substance use, and five symptom dimensions (anxiety, depressive, general psychopathology, manic, psychotic). Sparsity parameters and component number were optimized via grid search.

Results

Transdiagnostic FC patterns:
Among the 94 significant edges, 67 (71%) were shared between BD and SSD but not MDD, 8 (9%) were transdiagnostic across all three disorders, however, in pooling all patients compared to healthy controls 36 edges were significantly different, and 8 (9%) were SSD-specific, with the remainder showing other shared patterns. These results indicate extensive overlap between BD and SSD, while MDD exhibited a more distinct FC profile (Fig 1).

In overlapping analyses using Fisher’s exact test showed that Component 2 was significantly enriched for FC edges shared between MDD and BD (OR = 12.4, p = 0.03), whereas SSD-specific edges showed the highest weights in Components 3 and 5 (Fig 2, 3, 4).

Conclusion

BD and SSD share the majority of transdiagnostic FC alterations, whereas MDD demonstrates a comparatively distinct connectivity profile. Multivariate integration revealed five interpretable brain–behavior dimensions linking specific FC patterns to symptom severity, personality traits, and functional outcomes. The differential mapping of shared versus diagnosis-specific FC alterations onto distinct clinical phenotypes supports dimensional models of psychopathology while preserving meaningful diagnostic distinctions.
