Introduction

Mental imagery refers to the ability to picture something in your ‘minds-eye’ in the absence of an external visual stimulus. While early studies focused on the role of visual cortex regions in supporting neural reinstatement of ‘perception-like’ activity during imagery ^1, recent research has instead emphasised the role of larger networks spanning visual, parietal, and frontal regions in the generation of visual images ^2. 

In participants with typical imagery (assessed using the vividness of visual imagery questionnaire, VVIQ), we previously found that regions in the ventral temporal (fusiform face area, FFA, and parahippocampal place area, PPA) and medial parietal cortex (Places1/2 and People 1/2) had category selective activation during imagery of familiar people and places ^3. These regions are therefore active during the generation of visual imagery, and respond differently depending on the stimulus category. However, are these regions critical to the phenomenological experience of visual imagery, or are they supporting other related processes such as memory retrieval or semantic knowledge? In the current work, we compared the brain activity in Control participants to those with a self-reported lack of visual imagery; while actively generating a visual image may seem easy to some, these individuals report a lack of visual imagery in a phenomenon known as ‘Aphantasia’. We can therefore ask how brain activity differs between individuals with self-reported imagery, or lack thereof. Previous fMRI studies have identified multiple group differences between Aphantasics and Controls, including changes in connectivity between visual and frontal regions during imagery ^4. Here we extend previous work by estimating the BOLD timeseries across groups in a broad network of regions including visual cortex, category selective cortex, and subcortex (Figure 1b), and relating this activity to group differences in connectivity.

Methods

Task: Aphantasic and Control participants completed a cued imagery task during functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), where they were asked to think about familiar people and place stimuli (Figure 1a). Aphantasics were screened using the Vividness of Visual Imagery Questionnaire (VVIQ) and had a lower average score (mean = 17.5, range = 16-24) than Controls (mean = 58.6, range = 38-75).  

ROIs: Participants also completed a scene/face localiser that was used to define subject specific ROIs including anterior and posterior FFA and PPA. Population receptive field mapping scans were used to define early visual cortex ROIs. Other ROI definitions were taken from previous work ^5. 

 Analysis: First, we used finite impulse response modelling (FIR) to estimate the average BOLD responses during imagery across our ROIs (using a TENT basis function in AFNI). The advantage of this method is that we can estimate the actual hemodynamic responses, rather than assuming that they follow the canonical hemodynamic response function (HRF). To quantify differences in the timeseries across groups, we entered the beta values in each ROI at each timepoint into repeated measures ANOVAs with Group (Aphantasic or Control) entered as a between-subject factor. Within-subject factors included Timepoint (2,4,6,8, and 10s), Category (People or Place Imagery), and Hemisphere (Left or Right). In addition, we identified category selectivity in each ROI by comparing the average BOLD response for people versus place imagery (paired t-tests). A significant difference indicates a higher BOLD activation for one category compared to the other (* in Figure 2). We also quantified the sign of the BOLD response for each category (one sample t-tests). Values significantly above zero indicate increased activation overall compared to baseline, whereas values below indicate a reduced activation (+/- in Figure 2). 

Next, we calculated functional connectivity across the network by computing the pairwise correlations between the fMRI timeseries in each ROI. We then compared the connectivity between groups for within category connectivity (i.e. between people selective regions), and across category (i.e. between people selective regions and place selective regions). We also identified individual connections that were significantly different between groups. 

Results

FIR: We found significant category selectivity in BOLD responses for both groups during imagery (Figure 2). This was demonstrated in two different ways across ROIs: 1) a significant response relative to baseline for one category only, which could be positive (e.g. Places2) or negative (e.g. pPPA in Controls), or 2) significant responses to both categories with opposite polarity (e.g. aPPA). Other ROIs had significantly positive responses to both categories, and subsequently had no difference between categories (e.g. V2d in Aphantasics).

In visual cortex ROIs, both groups had significant category selective activity with greater activity during place imagery, particularly in the ventral visual stream (Aphantasics: V1, V2v, V3v; Controls: V1, V3v). Both groups also demonstrated the expected category selectivity across most of the medial parietal (Places1/2, People1), ventral temporal (aPPA, pPPA), and place memory ROIs (l/vPMA, FEF), with greater activity for the preferred category. Unexpectedly, however, Aphantasics had significant activation for both people and place imagery in FFA1 and FFA2, and therefore lacked category selectivity in these regions. This contrasts with Control participants who had greater activity for people in these face-selective regions, as expected.

When comparing the activity through time, we found significant interactions between Group and Timepoint, with most group effects present towards the end of the imagery period (8-10s). In visual cortex regions, this was primarily driven by differences in the post-stimulus dip, which was often negative in Controls but remained close to baseline in Aphantasics (for example, see V1 in Figure 2). We also found significant interactions between Group and Hemisphere, generally driven by increased BOLD responses in the left hemisphere in Aphantasics (not plotted here). 

Connectivity: While both groups had a similar structure of connectivity across ROIs, Aphantasics generally had a more correlated network than controls, possibly driven by differences in the pattern of category selectivity across groups. In particular, activity in the medial parietal region People1 was more correlated with the network in Aphantasics. In Controls, Places1 and FEF were more connected with other place selective regions compared to Aphantasics, and FFA2 was more connected to other people selective regions. As a result, connectivity between all pairs of people and place selective regions was significantly greater overall in Aphantasics compared to Controls.

Conclusion

Overall, the BOLD responses of Aphantasics and Controls during visual imagery were largely similar across regions known to be involved in imagery and memory related processes. However, there were a few notable exceptions. First, Aphantasics had no category selectivity in FFA1 and FFA2, which responded equally to the imagery of both people and places. This contrast with Controls, where category selective responses during imagery have been repeatedly shown. Our current results cannot tell us exactly how a lack of category selectivity in FFA1/2 relates to the experience of Aphantasia, but it could reflect a lack of segregation in the imagery network or reduced inhibition/selection. This was also demonstrated in our connectivity analysis, where the Aphantasic network was more correlated overall than in Controls and had more between category connectivity. FFA2 lies close to the so called ‘fusiform imagery node’, which is often implicated for its role in visual imagery ^2, and is a key region for further study in Aphantasia. 

Aphantasics also demonstrated a reduced post-stimulus undershoot compared to Controls, particularly in early visual cortex. While the exact cause of this undershoot is currently unclear, it may also indicate a group difference in the balance of excitation and inhibition in the imagery network. This finding requires further investigation to establish the conditions under which the undershoot is present/absent in Aphantasics and Controls, and how these relate to the experience of visual imagery and other behavioural measures.
