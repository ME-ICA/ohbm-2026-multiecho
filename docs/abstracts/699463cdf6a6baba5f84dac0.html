Introduction

Human cortical growth and folding accelerate between mid‑gestation and term, yet widely used surface atlases are typically provided at discrete time points. The developing Human Connectome Project (dHCP) provides weekly neonatal templates and high‑quality reconstructed white matter surfaces¹, but the growing interest in fetal imaging motivates an age-continuous, phenotypically‑conditioned, topologically accurate representation. Classical surface processing and spherical registration frameworks (including FreeSurfer) provide vertex‑wise correspondence but are not designed to generate templates continuously in age.² Spectral representations of surfaces based on the Laplace–Beltrami operator provide a compact, correspondence‑preserving parameterisation of shape.³ Recent learning‑based surface methods demonstrate the value of enforcing fixed topology across individuals via template deformation.⁴ Furthermore, conditional template learning can link phenotype information to atlases.⁵ Inspired by these previous developments, here we introduce ADA (Age‑Adaptive Template), a continuous spatiotemporal generative model of the grey-white matter interface that provides anatomically faithful, vertex‑wise correspondent cortical meshes for any gestational age (GA) between 24 and 45 weeks.

Methods

We used 1,121 qualifying dHCP sessions (foetal and neonatal) with reconstructed white matter surfaces spanning GA 24–45 weeks.¹ Subject surfaces were brought into dense vertex‑wise correspondence by spherical registration to a study‑specific template (seeded by the dHCP 40‑week atlas) using FreeSurfer.² Surfaces were resampled to a canonical icosahedral mesh with fixed topology. On the canonical mesh we computed a Laplace–Beltrami operator (LBO) eigenbasis using cotangent discretisation and a lumped mass matrix, solving Lφ=λMφ for the first K=1,300 modes.³ A phenotype‑conditioned multilayer perceptron (MLP; two 64‑unit rectified linear unit layers) maps GA onto spectral coefficients (K×3), which decode to 3‑dimensional vertex coordinates via the fixed eigenbasis. Predicted shapes are centred and normalised to a unit root mean square (RMS) radius; a separate linear scale head predicts log‑scale from GA to recover physical size. Training used vertex‑wise L2 loss with regularisers that encourage template centrality and penalise self‑intersection, and a curriculum progressively increased active eigenmodes (from 20 up to 1,300) during optimization to ensure maximum compression in the representation space.

Results

ADA generated smooth, intersection-free cortical surfaces with identical topology and vertex-wise correspondence across the full GA range. Figure 1A illustrates left‑hemisphere white-matter surfaces at 28, 36 and 44 weeks, showing progressive gyrification and regional expansion that qualitatively match the corresponding dHCP weekly templates. Figure 1B quantifies global growth: across all 1,121 dHCP sessions, subject RMS radius increase monotonically with GA. This is closely tracked by the ADA scale prediction, although the empirical trajectory shows a non‑linear trend that is not fully captured by the current linear scale head.

To test whether ADA templates are at least as ‘central’ as discrete atlases, we compared per‑subject template residuals against (i) an ADA‑generated template at each subject’s GA and (ii) an age‑matched dHCP template obtained by linear interpolation between vertex‑wise correspondent weekly dHCP templates. Surface distances were computed after centroid alignment (translation only) using Chamfer distance. Across the 100 held‑out sessions, residual distributions for the interpolated dHCP template and ADA were similar (Figure 1C, paired t-test: p=0.0545), indicating that ADA recovers an unbiased population‑average trajectory. Importantly, centrality gains for the ADA templates emerged at early fetal ages where the dHCP does not provide templates. The interpolated‑atlas residual increased sharply below 28 weeks, while ADA maintained low errors, improving centrality by up to ~2.5 mm (Figure 1D).

Conclusion

We introduced ADA, a continuous, age‑adaptive cortical surface atlas that factorises shape and global scale and uses a fixed LBO spectral decoder to enforce a sparse representation of cortical folding. ADA enables on‑demand template generation for any GA, including fetal ages not covered by existing weekly dHCP atlases, while matching the centrality of conventional discrete templates on held‑out data. This representation provides a compact prior for perinatal surface reconstruction, simulation and downstream surface‑based analysis, and can be extended by (i) using non‑linear scale modelling and (ii) conditioning on richer phenotype vectors (e.g., sex and clinical variables).
