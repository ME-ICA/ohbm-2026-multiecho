Introduction

Understanding how anatomical connectivity shapes large-scale neural dynamics is a central goal of neuroscience. While resting-state effective connectivity models infer directed interactions between brain regions, most assume that endogenous neural fluctuations share identical spectral properties across the brain.1 This assumption is difficult to reconcile with empirical findings showing systematic regional variation in intrinsic timescales and scale-free spectral properties, which covary with anatomical embedding and cortical hierarchy.2,3

Here, we introduce chromatic dynamic causal modelling (cDCM), a structurally informed extension of spectral DCM in which a region’s structural valency (degree) modulates the spectral “colour” of its endogenous fluctuations (Fig. 1). By embedding this mechanism upstream of directed inter-regional coupling, cDCM offers a principled framework for linking anatomical embedding to intrinsic neural timescales while preserving the interpretability and scalability of spectral DCM.

Methods

Chromatic DCM extends spectral DCM for resting-state fMRI by allowing region-specific power-law models of endogenous neural fluctuations.4 Structural connectivity estimates are used to compute normalized structural valency for each region, which is linearly mapped to the spectral exponent governing the power spectral density of intrinsic fluctuations. This yields heterogeneous intrinsic timescales that are independent of directed effective connectivity and hemodynamic parameters.

Parameter identifiability and robustness were assessed using extensive Monte Carlo simulations spanning network size, signal-to-noise ratio, and observation noise structure. To establish mechanistic plausibility, we simulated the dynamics of networks of coupled Hopf oscillators operating near criticality and examined whether structural valency predicted the spectral exponents of simulated activity. Finally, cDCM was applied to empirical resting-state fMRI and structural connectivity data across multiple scales, including cortical and subcortical networks in humans and homologous circuits in macaques, marmosets, and mice, using diffusion MRI–derived and tract-tracing–derived connectomes where available.

Results

Simulations demonstrated that chromatic DCM reliably recovers both effective connectivity and valency–spectrum mapping parameters across network sizes and noise conditions, with accuracy remaining stable as model dimensionality increased. Compared with standard spectral DCM, chromatic DCM consistently yielded lower parameter recovery error, particularly when endogenous or observation noise exhibited strong temporal autocorrelation.

Hopf oscillator simulations revealed that a linear relationship between structural valency and spectral exponent emerges naturally in weakly coupled networks operating near a critical regime, where metastability is maximal. Applied to empirical data, chromatic DCM revealed systematic differences in the valency–spectrum mapping across cortical and subcortical networks, consistent with known hierarchical organization of intrinsic timescales. Across species, the slope of the valency–spectrum relationship was conserved within homologous circuits, despite species-specific differences in baseline spectral properties (Fig. 2). Whole-brain analyses in macaques further showed that modelling spectral exponents as a function of afferent connectivity yielded higher model evidence than alternative definitions of valency.

Conclusion

Chromatic DCM provides a mechanistically grounded extension of resting-state effective connectivity modelling by explicitly linking anatomical embedding to the spectral architecture of endogenous neural fluctuations. By relaxing the assumption of region-invariant intrinsic dynamics, the model improves parameter recovery and yields more biophysically plausible estimates of effective connectivity without sacrificing computational tractability.

These findings suggest that structural valency constrains intrinsic timescales upstream of inter-regional communication, offering a complementary perspective to approaches that incorporate structure solely via priors on directed coupling.5 Chromatic DCM opens new avenues for multimodal integration and provides a flexible framework for investigating how structural organization shapes neural dynamics in health and disease.
