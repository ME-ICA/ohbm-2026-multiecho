Introduction

Human creativity is a complex cognitive phenomenon with promising signatures in large-scale brain dynamics.1 Resting-state neural dynamics have emerged as a correlate of individual differences in creativity, yet the field lacks tools capable of capturing their fine-grained spatial and temporal structure. In prior work2 (in review), we identified a neural subgroup marked by global functional network hypoconnectivity and increased modularity in resting-state EEG (rsEEG), who scored systematically higher across diverse behavioral creativity measures. 

Microstate analysis provides a powerful lens for summarizing moment-to-moment brain activity into recurring topographical patterns reflecting transient large-scale neural network engagement. However, conventional methods rely on Global Field Power (GFP) peaks and hard label assignments, restricting their ability to represent the complexity and variability of underlying neural dynamics.3 To address these limitations, we adapted a probabilistic generative model on the hypersphere, the von Mises–Fisher (vMF) mixture model.4 The vMF distribution, which is the directional analogue of the Gaussian distribution on a hypersphere, yields richer subject-level microstate parameters and EEG sample-level posterior state probabilities for a more detailed characterization of neural dynamics. Here, we make the first deployment of this novel microstate analysis method to model dynamical-systems features of rsEEG linked to human creativity. 

Given our prior work2 indicated higher creativity (HC) individuals display reduced global functional coupling together with stronger functional specialization, we predicted that these static network differences would extend to the fast dynamical properties of rsEEG. Specifically, we hypothesized that HC individuals would show a less constrained topographical state space, reflected in a more diffuse attractor structure, and reduced temporal structure in intrinsic EEG activity.

Methods

We recruited 26 healthy undergraduate students from the University of British Columbia (mean age = 21.13 years, SD = 1.15; 13 women). Participants completed four behavioral measures of creativity indexing divergent thinking, convergent thinking, creative self-efficacy, and real-world creative tasks. We also collected five minutes of eyes-closed resting-state EEG (rsEEG) using a 64-channel EGI HydroCel Geodesic Sensor Net. EEG preprocessing was conducted in EEGLAB (v2023.1) and included average re-referencing, downsampling to 250 Hz, notch filtering at 60 Hz, band-pass filtering (0.5–50 Hz), and artifact removal via Independent Component Analysis (ICA). In prior work2, we identified two distinct neural subgroups based on a graph-theoretical analysis of the subject's functional connectivity profiles, corresponding to higher (HC) and lower (LC) creative profiles across our behavioral measures. In the present investigation, we analyzed the rsEEG signals of the HC (n = 15, 53% male, mean age = 21) and LC (n = 11, 46% male, mean age = 21) subgroups previously identified using a novel methodological framework being developed in our lab for EEG microstate analysis.

EEG microstates were identified using a vMF (von Mises–Fisher) mixture model framework, which models multichannel EEG topographies as a continuous trajectory evolving through a probabilistic state-space defined across the surface of the unit hypersphere. This provides richer parameters beyond the discrete partitioning offered by a k-means approach. The group-level microstate structure was estimated by fitting a 5-component global vMF mixture model on the entire L2-normalized time series pooled across all subjects. We then fine-tuned the global model on each participant’s data during backfitting, while holding the five group-level component centroids fixed. This vMF backfitting procedure yields posterior state probabilities for each microstate at every time point, along with subject-specific parameters describing each microstate (α: mixture weight; κ: concentration). Transition states were defined as time points in which no microstate equaled or exceeded a posterior probability of 0.9, indicating moments where the signal did not robustly match a canonical microstate configuration. Unlike traditional microstate analysis—which typically assigns a hard label to every time point based on maximum spatial correlation—our approach leverages probabilistic state assignment, enabling subject-specific model parameters along with quantification of microstate uncertainty. 

In parallel with the microstate analysis, we applied Recurrence Quantification Analysis (RQA) directly to the continuous EEG time series to capture long-range, nonlinear temporal structure that is not accessible through discrete microstate labels. Prior to RQA, data were downsampled to 50 Hz to reduce computational load while preserving the slow-scale fluctuations relevant for recurrence structure. Recurrence matrices were constructed using a 5% threshold, and standard RQA metrics were computed, including determinism (DET), laminarity (LAM), and trapping time (TT). DET reflects the proportion of recurrent points forming diagonal line structures, indexing the predictability and regularity of temporal evolution in the signal. LAM quantifies the prevalence of vertical structures, indicating how often the system dwells in quasi-stable states. TT, the average length of these vertical structures, reflects the duration for which the system remains in these temporarily persistent neural configurations. Together, these metrics provide a complementary, temporally focused characterization of neural system dynamics beyond the spatial structure captured by microstates. When paired with the spatial insights from vMF microstates, RQA adds a complementary temporal perspective, offering a fuller dynamical-systems view of resting-state brain activity within subgroups.

Results

vMF mixture model metrics: The HC group displayed lower mean kappa values across microstates (less concentration around the microstate templates) (t(17.33) = 1.48, p = .16, d = 0.62). However, this difference was not statistically significant, consistent with a medium-sized effect despite limited power. Subjects in the LC group, on the other hand, displayed numerically higher kappa values and therefore more stereotyped spatial patterns aligned with the global templates. Moreover, the HC group exhibited slightly greater mean normalized entropy (t(17.22) = −0.77, p = .45, d = 0.32), computed from final microstate probability distributions per time point per subject. The HC group also exhibited a slightly greater fraction of time spent in transition states (t(17.19) = −0.79, p = .44, d = 0.32) and mean duration of these transition episodes (t(19.21) = −0.89, p = .38, d = 0.36). 

RQA metrics: Determinism (t(21.88 )= –2.20, p = .04, d = 0.87), laminarity (t(16.55) = –2.82, p = .01, d = 1.15), and trapping time (t(22.32) = –2.51, p = .02, d = 0.95) all showed significantly higher values in the high-creativity group. 

Connecting microstate and RQA results: Transition duration was significantly correlated with both determinism (r(24) = .70, p < .001) and laminarity (r(24) = .65, p < .001).

Conclusion

Our probabilistic microstate framework revealed subtle but meaningful differences in resting-state dynamics between the HC and LC groups that would have been overlooked by a traditional k-means framework. As predicted, the HC group demonstrated greater spatial flexibility in neural topographies, reflected by weaker concentrations around vMF centroids and greater uncertainty in microstate expression. Based on this increased spatial flexibility—and prior theories linking creativity to exploratory, more chaotic neural dynamics—we had expected the HC group to exhibit less temporal organization in their EEG, as quantified by RQA. However, contrary to our expectations, this spatial flexibility was accompanied by more temporally structured activity, as indexed by RQA metrics. Although the HC brain appears intrinsically more capable of flexibly organizing large-scale cortical activity and exploring a broader state space, it simultaneously exhibits a more orderly pattern in how these states unfold over time: transitioning between states in a more sequential manner and showing a greater tendency to dwell within a state once entered. Notably, this pattern of efficient state space exploration echoes our prior functional connectivity findings, which suggested a neural architecture capable of increased reconfigurability while still maintaining strong modular network organization, characteristic of dynamical systems that maintain a balance between exploration and structure. 

A key result supporting this interpretation was that the HC group exhibited a greater mean duration of “transition episodes” in between assigned microstate labels. These transition episodes correlated with RQA indicators of temporal structure, connecting our spatial and temporal findings. Importantly, this association held across both HC and LC participants, suggesting a general property of microstate–dynamical coupling. This result suggests that transition episodes are not periods of random or unstable neural drift, but coordinated trajectories through intermediate neural configurations that unfold in a temporally structured manner. Although these transitions constitute less than 10% of the signal—even at our strict posterior probability threshold of 0.9—they jointly support both the increased spatial diversity and temporal organization seen in the high-creativity group, indicating a potential dynamical marker warranting further investigation.
