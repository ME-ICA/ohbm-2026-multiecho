Introduction

Social cognition enables us to represent and predict others’ actions, intentions and mental states. While prior work distinguishes self from others, implicating medial prefrontal and posterior cingulate cortices in self-referential processing1 and a broader mentalizing network for understanding others2, recent studies highlight that social knowledge can also be encoded continuously within multidimensional social spaces. For example, attributes of others such as power and affiliation have been shown to be metrically encoded in a continuous manner, enabling efficient social inference3. From this perspective, social distance-from-the-self may also constitute a core organizing dimension of social cognition.

Understanding an individual’s closeness in social standing relative to self is vital, as this distance affects decisions for resource allocation, physical safety and opportunities that serve various adaptive advantages. Nevertheless, investigation of such dimensional representation has limited to specific social attributes defining relational metrics between different individuals, leaving unclear how the brain represents social distance with the self as the central point of reference. 

In this study, we aimed to address this gap by applying a multivariate predictive modelling framework to derive a robust whole-brain neural signature of social distance-from-the-self across three levels (Self, Friend, Other). In addition to validating this signature in an independent dataset with diverse cultural backgrounds, we assessed its broader relevance within a social-cognitive framework by examining its generalisability and specificity in distinct social cognition and working memory paradigms. This comprehensive approach moves beyond a simple self-other dichotomy to characterize a continuous neural code for social distance-from-the-self, which may provide a novel framework for understanding both normal and dysfunctional processes along this critical dimension of human cognition.

Methods

A group of 57 healthy, right-handed Mandarin-speaking participants (mean age = 23.96 ± 2.14 years old, 40/17 female/male ratio) completed a Social Distance Task during fMRI scanning. Participants viewed a series of images containing everyday objects (e.g. vase, screwdriver, football) and were cued to imagine either themselves (Self), their best friend (Friend), or a well-known Chinese TV celebrity (Other) interacting with them. The design consisted of three blocks (28 s) of eight image trials (3.5 s) presented for each of the three conditions. Three subjects were excluded due to incomplete data acquisition.

Data were collected using HCP-compliant high-quality neuroimaging protocols. In addition to T1w MPRAGE and T2w SPACE anatomical MRI data, two runs of resting state fMRI data (AP/PA) and a single run of task fMRI data (PA) was collected on a 3T Magnetom Prisma MRI scanner using a 2D MB-GRE EPI sequence (2.0-mm isotropic resolution, TR = 800 ms, TE = 37 ms, flip angle = 52°, bandwidth = 2,290 Hz per pixel, multiband acceleration factor = 8, 480 volumes). A pair of associated spin echo images with AP/PA phase encoding polarity and matching geometry were also acquired for post-hoc distortion correction of neuroimaging data. Preprocessing followed the HCP pipelines, including ICA+FIX denoising and MSMAll areal-feature-based cortical surface registration. 

With the aim of uncovering a robust whole-brain signature that is predictive of social distance-from-the-self, we employed Thresholded Partial Least Squares (T-PLS) and constructed a whole-brain neural predictive model for the three social distance levels in our task (Self, Friend, Other). Cortical vertices and subcortical voxel features with significant contributions to the overall neural model that optimally predicts distance-from-the-self were identified through bootstrap resampling (100 iterations) with z-statistics and FDR correction (q < 0.05). The resulting neural feature weights were Haufe-transformed to a whole-brain activation map to aid model interpretation.

A comprehensive set of validation procedures were employed to ensure the robustness of the neural signature, including the assessment of predictive accuracy, generalizability, and specificity. Predictive accuracy was evaluated via nested leave-one-subject-out cross-validation, quantified using Pearson’s correlation (r), mean squared error (MSE), and the coefficient of determination (R²). Model generalizability was assessed via performance on an independent fMRI dataset that used the same social distance task paradigm, but the TV celebrity exchanged with ‘Queen Elizabeth II’ (n=25). In order to evaluate cross-paradigm generalizability and specificity, we further assessed the prediction performance of our neural signature within independent fMRI paradigms in the HCP-YA dataset (n=100 unrelated subjects), encompassing both social and working memory tasks (Fig 1). Finally, a network correspondence analysis was employed to identify associated large-scale brain networks 4, and a NeuroSynth-based meta-analytic decoding was used to delineate distinct cognitive terms associated with the identified neural signature.

Results

Using a Social Distance Task, we identified a robust whole-brain neural signature that captured varying social distance-from-the-self (Fig. 2: r = 0.80). In particular, greater social distances were associated with a bilateral network, encompassing regions in the prefrontal cortex including the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, and the inferior frontal sulcus; the temporal lobe, including the temporal parietal junction (TPJ), superior temporal sulcus and middle temporal gyrus; and the parietal lobe, including the posterior gyrus, superior parietal cortex and posterior cingulate cortex (Fig. 2). Smaller distance, on the other hand, was linked to inferior frontal gyrus, posterior occipital sulcus and area PF complex. When tested using a LOSO (leave-one-subject-out) approach, the sensitivity of the model illustrated high performance in predicting distance-from-the-self (individual-level r = 0.80 ± 0.04 (mean ± standard error [SE]), mean squared error (MSE) = 0.69 ± 0.02 SE, coefficient of determination (R2) = 0.26 ± 0.04 SE; group-level r = 0.51, MSE = 0.70, R2 = 0.26).

Focusing on the identified neural pattern’s predictive accuracy, we conducted additional evaluations within an independent fMRI dataset collected using the same social task paradigm. In this external dataset, our model further demonstrated high predictive performance in distinguishing self, friend and other conditions (Fig 2, between self and friend, accuracy: 88%, CI: 75%-100%, AUC: 0.99; between friend and other, accuracy: 88%, CI: 73%-100%, AUC: 0.94, between self and other: accuracy: 100%, CI: 100%-100%, AUC: 1.00).

We additionally assessed the model’s cross-paradigm generalizability and specificity in an independent fMRI dataset (HCP), which contained both social and cognitive demand (working memory) tasks. The results illustrated that the neural response of the identified distance-from-self signature successfully predicted the social condition when compared to the random condition (accuracy: 90%, CI: 84-96%, AUC: 0.96), supporting the domain-generality of the neural signature to other types of social cognition paradigms. In contrast the neural signature of distance-from-self did not distinguish 0-back and 2-back conditions in the working memory task (accuracy: 21%, CI: 13%-29%, AUC: 0.15), suggesting that the neural signature is specific for social cognition and does not reflect cognitive demands.

Finally, the meta-analytic decoding of the identified neural signature using the NeuroSynth database revealed strong associations. While greater distance-from-self linked to terms such as social cognition, intention, mood and empathy, suggesting its involvement in core social operations, smaller distance-from-self was associated with terms such as naming, speech production, verbal fluency and psychosis, highlighting self-referential processing. The neural signature was also compared with multiple established brain network parcellations. The positive signature significantly overlapped with the default mode, frontoparietal and language networks, whereas the negative component was associated with visual, cingulo-opercular and dorsal attention networks. 

Conclusion

In this study, we identified a replicable whole-brain neural signature that continuously predicts social distance-from-the-self. This signature demonstrates high predictive accuracy, successfully generalizes to an independent cultural cohort and across social cognition tasks while remaining specific to social, rather than cognitive-demand processes. Its meta-analytic associations with higher-order social and affective processes and overlap with the Default Mode Network (DMN) suggest that distance-from-self may function as a core dimension within a broader cognitive-map-like architecture supporting social inference. Collectively, this brain-based model of a continuous social distance representation may provide a powerful framework for studying normative social cognition and for characterizing transdiagnostic impairments in conditions such as autism, mood disorders and schizophrenia.
