Introduction

Autism spectrum disorders (ASD) are a group of neurodevelopmental disorders characterized by persistent deficits in social interaction and communication, as well as restricted and repetitive patterns of behavior (RRB). Numerous structural and functional neuroimaging studies have reported atypical brain patterns in ASD, yet the findings remain highly heterogeneous. One important source of inconsistency is that many participants with ASD are assessed during sensitive and critical periods of brain maturation, when small perturbations in neurobiological parameters may have disproportionately large effects on the whole network. 

Relatively few studies have examined ASD from an explicitly development‑sensitive perspective, asking which brain regions or connections are most susceptible to atypical neurobiological changes over development. Despite that, most current approaches, such as normative modeling, tend to examine brain regions or connections in isolation. However, converging evidence from network neuroscience indicates that brain regions do not mature in isolation but rather in a temporally and spatially coordinated fashion. This suggests that ASD‑related “miswiring” is better understood at the level of coordinated network patterns.

Recently, a new theoretical framework based on stiff–sloppy modes has been proposed as a promising way to link neurobiological parameters to large‑scale brain dynamics patterns1. In this framework, high‑dimensional parameter space is decomposed into orthogonal directions, each representing a coherent pattern of covariation across brain network parameters, including inter-regional coupling and regional excitability. Stiff modes correspond to directions in which small changes in network parameters induce large changes in network dynamics. By identifying these sensitive developmental directions, the stiff–sloppy approach offers a principled way to probe which aspects of the connectome are most vulnerable to atypical neurodevelopment in ASD.

Methods

Participants and imaging data
The data used in this study were drawn from the ABIDE I and ABIDE II datasets2. We used a composite 116‐region parcellation comprising 100 cortical regions from the scale‑100 Schaefer functional atlas and 16 subcortical regions. Given prior evidence highlighting subcortical contributions to ASD, we explicitly included subcortical structures. 

After applying standard exclusion criteria (e.g., excessive head motion, missing behavioral data, or poor image quality) and restricting the sample to sites with a repetition time (TR) of 2 s to reduce inter‑site acquisition heterogeneity, 455 individuals with ASD and 442 typically developing controls (TDC) between 5 and 25 years of old remained for the analysis.

Pairwise maximum entropy modeling
For each individual fMRI time series, we fit a pMEM that captures the distribution of binarized regional activation patterns3. The model is parameterized by regional “excitability” terms and pairwise couplings , which together define the energy of each binary configuration. We also fit separate group‑level pMEMs for ASD and TDC. 

Stiff–sloppy eigenspace of parameter sensitivity
 To identify combinations of parameters that most strongly affect model behavior in each group, we applied the stiff–sloppy framework separately to the ASD and TDC group models. We computed the Fisher information matrix of the group‑level pMEM to characterize coordinated parameter variations that are most development-relevant, we derived a low-dimensional set of sensitivity-mode features from each group model and summarized each individual’s deviation in this reduced representation. These features provide compact, mode-based descriptors of development-linked network changes without relying on edge-wise testing.

Alignment of individual developmental deviation and nested cross‑validation
 To identify developmental trajectories within the stiff–sloppy eigenspace, we used the eigenvector projections as features to predict chronological age separately in the ASD and TDC groups. Age prediction was carried out using repeated train–test splitting to clearly separate model optimization from evaluation. For each group, we randomly split the full sample into a training set (70%) and a held‑out test set (30%). All model selection and hyper‑parameter tuning were performed exclusively within the training set using an elastic‑net regression model. To assess robustness to sampling variability, this full procedure was repeated 100 times with different random seeds. For each group, we report the mean and distribution of test‑set correlations between predicted and true age of subjects across repeats as an estimate of generalization performance, and we assess the stability of the learned patterns (features) by correlating the final sensitivity vectors across repeats. 

Calculation of brain age gap and association with psychopathology in ASD

The brain age gap (BAG) was defined as the difference between normalized brain‑predicted age and normalized chronological age. Clinical characterization in the ASD group included measures spanning four domains: IQ‑related indices, restricted and repetitive behaviors, social affect, and overall autism severity.

Results

Atypical developmental sensitivity pattern in ASD 

We identified a developmental sensitivity pattern that tracked age in each group, yielding a sensitivity-weighted network of development-related connections. Cross‑validated correlations between predicted and chronological age were robust in the training samples (ASD: r = 0.7733 ± 0.0445; TDC: r = 0.6943 ± 0.0807) and remained moderate in the test samples (ASD: r = 0.5027 ± 0.0630; TDC: r = 0.4854 ± 0.0641) (Figure 1A). The value of the weights represented the development rate of the coupling: positive (negative) values mean the couplings will be strengthened (weakened) during development. The resulting sensitivity features supported accurate brain‑age prediction in both groups (Figure 1B). 

To identify the core network underlying the development, we systematically thresholded the sensitivity matrices and examined the correlation between the resulting projections and age as a function of edge‑wise threshold percentile (Figure 1C). As network size decreased, the age–correlation curve was generally lower in TDC than in ASD, indicating that ASD development is supported by a more restricted and topologically fragmented set of age‑sensitive connections, whereas typical development recruits a broader, system‑wide network of connections.

Disrupted Modularization and Aberrant Cortico‑Subcortical Gating in ASD

We further mapped the sensitivity values into functional modules to examine the topological organization of developmental changes (Figure 1D). In TDCs, developmental sensitivity was strongly modularized: within‑network sensitivity was particularly high in the Somatomotor network, Dorsal Attention network (DAN), and the Ventral Attention network. This pattern suggests that typical development is characterized by selective refinement of sensory and attentional systems, consistent with progressive specialization of perception–action integration and the maturation of top‑down attentional control.

In contrast, the ASD group lacked this characteristic modularization. The sensitivity between DAN and other networks was significantly diminished. Crucially, ASD group exhibited a specific, anomalous pattern of high sensitivity in the connections between subcortical regions and the Default Mode network (DMN). This over-integration suggests an altered gating mechanism between subcortical systems and the DMN in autism, potentially disrupting the balance between internal self-referential processing and external environmental monitoring.

Brain Age Gap Links Atypical Development to Symptom Severity

Finally, we examined the behavioral relevance of these developmental deviations using the brain‑age model–derived brain age gap (BAG). BAG was defined as predicted brain age minus chronological age, such that negative values (BAG < 0) indicate delayed brain age (predicted brain age < real age) and positive values (BAG > 0) indicate accelerated brain age (predicted brain age > real age). Treating BAG as a continuous measure, we observed that more accelerated brain age was associated with lower autism symptom severity: BAG showed negative associations with ADOS_GOTHAM_RRB scores (left; r = -0.143, p = 0.0191, n = 270) and with ADOS_GOTHAM_SEVERITY scores (right; r = -0.129, p = 0.0313, n = 278) (Figure 1E).

Conclusion

Recent critiques of Brain-Wide Association Studies (BWAS) have highlighted a reproducibility crisis, noting that traditional mass-univariate approaches often require excessively large sample sizes to detect small effect sizes reliably4. These challenges arise largely from treating brain connections as isolated features without considering the integrity of the whole network. Our study addresses this by adopting a "stiff-sloppy" analysis. Instead of testing millions of edges independently, which invites multiple comparison issues and noise accumulation, we focus on "stiff modes"—the coordinated directions in parameter space that are most sensitive to the system's statistical structure. By projecting high-dimensional data onto these low-dimensional, biologically informative manifolds, our approach inherently reduces noise and enhances statistical power. This suggests that thoughtful model building, which prioritizes the geometry of brain dynamics over brute-force feature extraction, can yield robust brain-behavior associations even within medium-sized cohorts like ABIDE.

While predictive frameworks such as Connectome-based Predictive Modeling (CPM) have successfully linked brain connectivity to behavioral traits, they often focus on maximizing predictive accuracy at the expense of mechanistic interpretability. Our "developmental sensitivity" framework bridges this gap. It does not merely provide a predictive score; it offers a loading-based ranking of edges and nodes that reveals how brain regions coordinate to support maturation. For instance, our finding of the "stiff" mode highlights specific patterns of integration and segregation—such as the critical cortico-subcortical gating—that must be fine-tuned for typical development. This distinguishes our method from pure prediction: we identify the sensitivity network as the underlying "control knobs" of the brain, showing which specific parameter combinations must change together to drive the emergence of adult-like brain dynamics.

It is important to acknowledge that our findings are based on cross-sectional data from the ABIDE repository. As noted in many previous studies on ASD development, cross-sectional analyses provide snapshots of age-related differences but cannot capture the intra-individual trajectories of true development. However, our results provide a precise, hypothesis-driven foundation for future work. The "sensitive modes" identified here serve as candidate biomarkers that can be specifically tracked in longitudinal samples, from early childhood through adolescence, to validate whether the failure to engage these specific stiff modes predicts long-term clinical outcomes.
