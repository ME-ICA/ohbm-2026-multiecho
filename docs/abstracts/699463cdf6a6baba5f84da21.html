Introduction

The hippocampus plays a critical role in memory and learning and undergoes one of the longest developmental trajectories of any brain structure1. Yet paediatric Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) datasets rarely provide sufficient spatial resolution to reliably delineate hippocampal subfields2. Histological data of healthy developing hippocampi are also limited, leaving gaps in the understanding of how these subfields mature and support emerging cognitive function. Consequently, models of hippocampal development, and interpretations of neurodevelopmental conditions such as ADHD and childhood epilepsy, continue to rely on adult-derived assumptions. This reliance is problematic, as although adult-like hippocampal features are visible at birth, ongoing synaptogenesis and myelination extend into early adulthood and continue to impact subfield volumes meaning automated methods of hippocampal delineation often produce inaccurate volume measures when applied to child data1,2. Further, it is unclear whether manual hippocampal segmentation protocols developed for adults accurately delineate the paediatric hippocampus. To address these limitations in studying the developing hippocampus, we validated and refined MRI hippocampal segmentation protocols for paediatric research. Using manual delineation, we characterised how the developing hippocampal subfields manifest in MRI data.

Methods

High-resolution (0.5-mm isotropic) paired T1- and T2-weighted whole-brain MRI data from children aged 9-16 were acquired using a Philips Ingenia CX 3T MRI scanner using methods described in Wright et al3. For each participant, both left and right hippocampi were manually delineated in the coronal plane working from anterior to posterior. The delineation followed a combined protocol that integrated the methodology of Dalton et al4 with the Hippocampal Subfields Group (HSG) harmonised body protocol5. These approaches were selected as they rely on anatomical landmarks visible in high-resolution MRI such as the stratum radiatum laconosum moleculare (SRLM) together with geometric rules derived from cytoarchitectonic subfield organisation to permit the delineation of hippocampal head, body, and tail regions.

Results

The use of high-resolution sub-millimetre data permitted reliable delineation of hippocampal subfields in paediatric MRI. The paediatric hippocampi exhibited a largely adult-like morphology, permitting the application of adult-derived segmentation rules in cases where the SRLM band was clearly visible (Fig 1). Landmark-based delineation, that is relying on features and anatomical boundaries of the hippocampus visible in the scan, captured meaningful individual variability, including differences in hippocampal head digitations and the emergence of the dentate gyrus at the head–body transition. By combining recently published protocols, the segmentation protocol incorporates current best practice for hippocampal head, body, and tail segmentation in 3T MRI data and demonstrates applicability to paediatric datasets. These refinements support accurate and developmentally appropriate structural and volumetric assessments.

Conclusion

This research outlines a combined hippocampal subfield delineation protocol for high-resolution paediatric in-vivo MRI. As MRI resolution improves, key internal hippocampal features such as the SRLM become discernible, enabling anatomically grounded segmentation throughout development. By validating and refining segmentation methods specifically for the developing hippocampus, this work establishes a foundation for age-appropriate analyses across childhood and adolescence, including studies of hippocampal maturation, memory development, and neurodevelopmental disorders. The protocol supports investigation of fine-grained developmental effects, such as subfield-specific volumetric and thickness changes. Moreover, characterisation of the healthy paediatric hippocampus provides ground truth for comparison in clinical populations.
