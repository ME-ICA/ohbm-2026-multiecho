Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the leading cause of dementia and is characterized by progressive neurodegeneration driven most directly by tau pathology. While amyloid-β (Aβ) marks the earliest stage of AD, regional tau burden is more tightly linked to neuronal loss and clinical decline. Yet among individuals with comparable tau levels, structural MRI shows heterogeneity: some exhibit preserved cortex despite high tau, whereas others show disproportionately severe atrophy, potentially reflecting vulnerability or mixed pathologies.1 Capturing this tau–neurodegeneration mismatch (T-N mismatch) has become an important axis of variability for understanding biological susceptibility and resilience in AD.

T-N mismatch has been previously quantified using residuals from a linear regression of structure on tau, where negative residuals indicate disproportionate atrophy and positive residuals indicate relative preservation.2 Although useful, linear models estimate only the mean association and rely on assumptions of linearity, homoscedasticity, and Gaussian residuals, limiting how well residuals capture the full tau-conditioned distribution—particularly in the tails, where vulnerability and resilience are most apparent and where outliers can distort classifications.

Generalized Additive Models for Location, Scale, and Shape (GAMLSS)3 offer a more flexible alternative by allowing tau to influence not only expected structure but also variance and distributional shape. This enables percentile-based estimates of where an individual falls within the tau-conditioned distribution and provides a more stable and interpretable definition of T-N mismatch. 

Here, we compare a traditional robust linear regression (RLM) approach to a tau-centric GAMLSS framework for quantifying T-N mismatch. We evaluate whether GAMLSS reveals distributional patterns and vulnerability signatures that may be masked in mean-only models and whether these patterns align with known biological heterogeneity in AD.

Methods

Participants were retrospectively selected from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) study. We selected amyloid-positive individuals who had a structural T1w-MRI and tau-PET scan within 365 days of each other. 

T1w-MRI scans were processed using the Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs)4 cortical thickness pipeline to generate whole-brain thickness maps and compute mean cortical thickness. Tau PET scans were quantified using Tau-MaX, a Gaussian mixture modeling–based approach that captures both the extent and magnitude of tau signal.5 Global Tau-MaX was used as the tau index for the present analyses. 

Statistical analysis: To model the association between tau burden (T) and neurodegeneration (N), we fit two parallel models with identical predictors

  1. Robust linear regression (RLM)

We fit a robust linear regression using Huber M-estimation in the form: Structure ~ Tau + Sex. Age was not included as a covariate in order to allow age-related effects to contribute to tau–neurodegeneration (T-N) mismatch, enabling evaluation of age as a potential factor underlying vulnerability or resilience rather than adjusting it away. Residuals from these models served as T-N mismatch scores, representing whether an individual exhibited more or less atrophy than expected for their tau burden. To facilitate comparison with the GAMLSS-derived scores, individuals were categorized into 1) Vulnerable: residuals < mean − 0.44 SD, 2) Canonical: residuals within ±0.44 SD, 3) Resilient: residuals > mean + 0.44 SD. These thresholds approximately map onto the 33rd and 66th percentiles of the conditional distribution

  1. GAMLSS: Tau-Centric Distributional Modeling

Generalized Additive Models for Location, Scale, and Shape (GAMLSS) were fit using the same predictors: Structure ~ Tau + Sex, with a Box–Cox Cole–Green (BCCG) distribution. Age was likewise excluded to permit age-related variability to contribute to T-N mismatch rather than being regressed out. GAMLSS estimates the location, scale, and shape of the conditional distribution, allowing tau to influence not only the expected structural value but also variance and distributional form. For each participant, we derived tau-referenced deviation scores representing their position within the tau-conditional distribution, referred to as T-N mismatch in this project. Individuals were assigned to 1) Vulnerable: percentile < 33, 2) Canonical: 33 < percentile < 66, 3) Resilient: percentile > 66 groups. These classifications are directly comparable to the RLM groups.

Outlier evaluation: During initial model fitting, several extreme thickness values were identified as segmentation-related outliers. To evaluate their impact, all analyses were performed twice—once with outliers included and again after outlier removal. For each scenario, we compared (i) inter-method concordance between RLM and GAMLSS classifications and (ii) intra-method stability when comparing outlier-included and outlier-excluded classifications.

Cross-Method Comparison and Validation: We quantified agreement between RLM and GAMLSS group assignments and identified mismatched categories (e.g., GAMLSS-vulnerable / RLM-canonical [VC], and GAMLSS-resilient / RLM-canonical [RC]). To assess the biological relevance of these groups, we examined associations with age, clinical status using CDR-Sum of Boxes (SB), longitudinal CDR-SB decline using linear mixed-effects models, and the entorhinal cortex-to-parahippocampal cortex (ERC/PHC) thickness ratio, a medial temporal metric associated with LATE-related vulnerability. Analyses focused on determining whether GAMLSS or RLM produced classifications that more closely aligned with structural and clinical markers of neurodegenerative susceptibility and resilience, and whether outlier-driven instability differentially affected the two modeling approaches.

Results

Across amyloid-positive ADNI participants (n = 351), global tau PET showed a moderate negative association with mean cortical thickness (ρ = -0.31). This tau–structure relationship appeared highly similar under both approaches (Figure 1A): RLM showed the expected pattern in which higher tau levels were associated with lower cortical thickness, whereas GAMLSS reflected the same broad relationship but with a smoother, more biologically plausible trajectory. When participants were grouped into resilient, canonical, and vulnerable categories based on T–N mismatch (or deviation) scores from each method, both approaches yielded comparable clinical gradients (Figure 1B). In both models, vulnerable individuals tended to be older, exhibited higher CDR-SB scores, showed lower ERC/PHC ratios, and demonstrated steeper longitudinal clinical progression, whereas resilient individuals showed preserved structural and cognitive profiles. Thus, at a broad level, both modeling strategies recapitulated the expected ordering of biological vulnerability.

Despite these similarities, the two modeling approaches did not classify participants identically. Cross-method comparisons (Figure 2A, left) revealed substantial discordance when outlier cases were included. These discrepancies prompted examination of the influence of outliers on classification. After removing outlier cases, inter-method agreement increased markedly (Figure 2A, right), indicating that extreme observations disproportionately contributed to disagreement between RLM and GAMLSS. Further evaluation demonstrated that RLM was considerably more sensitive to outliers than GAMLSS. In intra-method comparisons between outlier-included and outlier-excluded models (Figure 2B), GAMLSS classifications were largely stable, whereas RLM classifications shifted noticeably, with many individuals moving between canonical, resilient, and vulnerable categories. This instability in RLM T-N mismatch scores suggests that outliers distort the residual distribution, leading to inconsistent classification of participants with otherwise typical tau–structure relationships.

Assessment of the discordant groups (Figure 2C) provided additional insight into the nature of these differences. Participants labeled vulnerable by GAMLSS but canonical by RLM (VC) exhibited clear signatures suggestive of greater vulnerability: they were older, had higher CDR-SB, and showed lower ERC/PHC ratios. Their longitudinal CDR-SB trajectories were also nearly identical to those of the GAMLSS-defined vulnerable (VV) group. In contrast, individuals labeled resilient by GAMLSS but canonical by RLM (RC) were closer in age to the resilient (RR) group. This divergence between the models suggests that GAMLSS retained coherent biological stratification even in the presence of outliers, whereas RLM tended to mislabel both vulnerable and resilient individuals as canonical when influenced by extreme values.

Together, these results show that although RLM and GAMLSS yield similar tau–thickness associations and similar large-scale clinical gradients, their classification behavior diverges meaningfully in the presence of outliers. GAMLSS demonstrates greater robustness to extreme observations, preserving biologically coherent distinctions in vulnerability and resilience that RLM is prone to obscure.

Conclusion

Although RLM and GAMLSS showed similar tau–thickness associations and produced comparable broad clinical gradients, their T-N mismatch classifications diverged in the presence of outliers. RLM mismatch scores were substantially influenced by extreme values, leading to instability in group assignment and misclassification of individuals whose structural and clinical profiles otherwise reflected clear vulnerability or resilience. In contrast, GAMLSS classifications remained stable across outlier-included and outlier-excluded models, demonstrating greater distributional robustness.

Evaluation of discordant groups reinforced this distinction. Participants classified as vulnerable by GAMLSS but canonical by RLM consistently exhibited biological features of heightened susceptibility, including older age, higher CDR-SB, lower ERC/PHC ratios, and steeper longitudinal decline, closely aligning with the GAMLSS-defined vulnerable group. Conversely, individuals classified as resilient by GAMLSS but canonical by RLM aligned with preserved structural and clinical profiles. These findings indicate that GAMLSS better preserves biologically coherent distinctions in vulnerability and resilience, whereas RLM tends to collapse extremes toward the canonical category when influenced by outliers.

Given that large neuroimaging datasets inevitably include segmentation artifacts and measurement outliers, modeling approaches must remain robust to such deviations to avoid cascading misclassification errors. The increased stability of GAMLSS suggests that it offers a more reliable framework for deriving T-N mismatch scores and identifying individuals whose neurodegeneration diverges from tau-predicted expectations. More broadly, tau-centric mismatch frameworks—including T-clinical (tau vs. cognitive decline) and T-microstructure (tau vs. diffusion-derived neurite density or free-water metrics)—can be used to probe biologically relevant processes such as differential vulnerability, resilience, and potential co-pathologies. These mismatch metrics can help isolate individuals whose neurodegeneration is disproportionately severe (or mild) for their tau burden, a phenotype linked to resilience, accelerated vulnerability, and potential co-pathologies such as LATE. By providing a stable deviation signal, GAMLSS strengthens downstream applications that rely on these mismatch approaches, enabling more accurate stratification and deeper insight into heterogeneity across the Alzheimer’s disease continuum.
