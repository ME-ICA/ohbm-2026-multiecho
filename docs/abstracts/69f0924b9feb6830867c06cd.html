Introduction

The brain exhibits a spatially distributed and fluid network of complex interactions, yet analysis of this landscape is typically constrained to methods that provide a static, non-overlapping modular decomposition¹. Bridging this gap to efficiently capture and interpret this overlapping community structure in the brain is vital to advance our knowledge of how the brain integrates information across distributed networks. Amidst a growing repertoire of overlapping community detection algorithms (OCDAs) that allow soft partitions, only a handful of studies have explored overlapping brain network decompositions, generally using a manually selected algorithm. This means that brain-imaging studies across diverse and important applications may rely on sub-optimal, subjective choices that could obscure the most relevant overlapping modular representation for a given structural and/or functional connectome. 

In the present work, we overcome this major limitation by introducing a novel systematic approach to uniquely tailor the optimal OCDA (and its parameters) to the human structural connectome2. Specifically, we evaluate 22 OCDA and parameter combinations on an ensemble of simulated benchmark networks designed to recapitulate key properties of the connectome. We identify the optimal OCDA based on how well it recovers the ground-truth overlapping community structure across benchmark networks, suggesting that it is best positioned to infer overlapping modular architecture in the structural connectome. This optimal OCDA is then applied to the original empirical connectome to characterize the inferred overlapping community structure and pose new biological hypotheses that cannot be adequately evaluated using traditional non-overlapping partition algorithms.

Critically, this approach is generalizable across structural and functional connectomes, providing an accessible and interpretable framework for characterizing overlapping brain architecture across modalities.

Methods

We propose a data-driven approach to identify the top-performing OCDA in the structural connectome of the human cortex, which in turn enables quantitative analysis and biological interpretation of the inferred (potentially overlapping) networks and constituent regions. We systematically evaluate five OCDAs and underlying parameter settings, yielding a total of 22 OCDA—parameter combinations. These algorithms encompass clique percolation, nonnegative matrix factorization (NNMF), speaker-listener label propagation algorithm (SLPA), order statistics local optimization method (OSLOM), and Infomap.

As shown in Fig. 1A, the structural connectome was derived from diffusion-weighted imaging collected from N=973 Human Connectome Project participants3. Data were preprocessed using the HCP Diffusion pipeline, along with MRtrix3 and the FMRIB Software Library. Tractography was performed using second-order integration over fiber orientation distributions (iFOD2) and refined using Anatomically Constrained Tractography (ACT) and Spherically Informed Filtering of Tractograms (SIFT-2). The cortex was parcellated with the 360-region HCP-MMP1 atlas, and the 180 right-hemisphere regions were retained for downstream analysis. The representative group-level right-hemisphere connectome was obtained using a consensus-based thresholding approach, retaining only the edges that ranked in the lowest 15% for coefficient of variation (CV) in strength across the entire cohort (N = 973).

The first step in the OCDA selection process is to generate an ensemble of simulated benchmark networks that mimic community structure properties of this group-averaged connectome, including degree distribution properties that can be directly measured or inferred from power-law model fitting (Fig. 1B). We use the stochastic network generation algorithm of Lancichinetti and Fortunato (2009)4; importantly, each resulting (weighted) network in the ensemble has a corresponding overlapping community structure, providing a ground truth for comparison with each evaluated OCDA. Second, as shown in Fig. 1C, we apply each OCDA—parameter combination to each simulated network in the ensemble and assess how well the ground-truth overlapping structure was inferred—here quantified using extended normalized mutual information. Algorithms that exhibit strong overall performance on the benchmark ensemble (defined as high ENMI values) are judged to most accurately uncover overlapping community structure among networks resembling our empirical structural network, allowing us to select a top candidate OCDA for further exploration.

Upon identifying the top OCDA, as shown in Fig. 1D, we investigated the spatial distribution and connectivity projection profiles of the resulting overlapping communities and their constituent nodes, with a particular focus on the regions assigned to two or more communities (termed ‘overlapping nodes’). In particular, we investigated various aspects of how the overlapping community structure relates to the putative functional hierarchy in the cortex as captured by the principal gradients (PGs) of functional connectivity, provided in the neuromaps library. We additionally computed nodal metrics of network integration (participation coefficient and within-network z-scored degree) to characterize the functional role(s) that overlapping nodes may fulfill in their communities. To clarify whether the top-performing OCDA offered additional and unique information beyond that of a non-overlapping partition, we compared all results obtained with the Louvain community detection method (n.b., the most frequently used community detection method in network neuroscience)

Results

We applied this systematic algorithm-selection framework to identify the OCDA best suited to the right-hemisphere structural connectome derived from N=973 HCP participants. Using an ensemble of connectome-like synthetic benchmark networks, we found that the OSLOM algorithm with P=0.3 consistently yielded the highest ENMI across simulated benchmark networks, outperforming the other OCDA variants and a non-overlapping Louvain partition baseline. 

Given its demonstrated ability to recover ground-truth overlapping structure, we selected OSLOM (with P=0.3) for subsequent analysis of the empirical group-consensus connectome. We identified a robust overlapping modular decomposition comprised of seven structural communities across the right-hemisphere cortex. As depicted in Fig. 2A, these communities mapped to: (1) dorsomedial cortex, (2) limbic network, (3) visual network, (4) ventral attention/visual-stream, (5) somatomotor, (6) insula, and (7) frontal pole. Notably, the OSLOM-derived structural modules exhibited substantial spatial and membership overlap with canonical resting-state functional networks, reinforcing the correspondence between structural and functional architecture.

A key advantage of overlapping community detection is the ability to identify regions that bridge multiple macroscale systems. OSLOM detected 15 overlapping cortical regions spanning two (or three, in the case of the retrosplenial cortex) structural modules, as highlighted in Fig. 2A. These fifteen regions indeed exhibited dense structural connections to the two (or three) modules to which OSLOM assigned them (Fig. 2B), and they also exhibited dense intra-hemispheric connections with each other (Fig. 2C)—consistent with the idea that overlapping regions mediate information exchange among segregated brain networks. Plotting the overlapping regions on the right-hemisphere cortical surface in Fig. 2D showed an even distribution along both medial—lateral and anterior—posterior axes.

Based on the seminal work from Mesulam in 19985 and mounting evidence that ‘integrative hubs’ contribute to multiple functional domains, we hypothesized that overlapping nodes would sit higher in the putative functional hierarchy of the cortex. As shown in Fig. 2E, overlapping regions were distributed across the visual—transmodal axis in the first and second principal gradient of functional connectivity axes (PG1 and PG2, respectively), with a greater concentration toward higher PG1 values—supporting the idea that regions bridging two or more structural communities sit higher in the functional hierarchy of the cortex. However, the variance in PG1 values suggests that the role of a given overlapping region depends upon the communities it bridges, alongside other functional and structural properties, from cytoarchitecture to neurotransmitter receptor densities. 

Having demonstrated that OSLOM generated biologically plausible intra-hemispheric structural networks bridged by overlapping nodes, we finally turned to examine the additional information this OCDA offers beyond that of the conventional non-overlapping partition with the Louvain algorithm. As plotted in Fig. 2F, for the fifteen nodes that OSLOM assigned to multiple communities, the mean PG1 values significantly correlated with the participation coefficient from OSLOM (Spearman’s ρ = 0.65, P = 0.01) but not that of the Louvain partition (ρ = 0.32, P = 0.24). This suggests that the overlapping versus non-overlapping decompositions yield different conclusions for the hypothesis that the participation coefficient increases along the cortical functional hierarchy toward the transmodal associative end. This comparison of metrics derived from OSLOM versus Louvain partitions underscores the utility of flexible overlapping decompositions for characterizing the resulting structural networks and derived nodal statistics, which are widely interpreted across the network neuroscience literature.

Conclusion

Collectively, we introduce a novel and powerful framework for systematically analyzing community architecture in multimodal brain networks that overcomes major limitations of current methodological practices. This approach demonstrates the importance of empirically tailoring OCDAs to the particular network being analyzed (e.g., right-hemisphere structural connectome), rather than relying on overly general statements about the relative performance of one algorithm over another. The comprehensive methodology to select from a range of OCDAs, provided in open-source code for wide accessibility, and the biological insights themselves imbue a new perspective on network structure that is highly relevant to researchers across the intersection of brain structure, function, and network topology. 

After identifying OSLOM (with P=0.3) as the top-performing OCDA variant across our ensemble of synthetic connectome-like benchmark networks, we applied this algorithm to the empirical right-hemisphere structural connectome, inferring seven structural communities that were bridged by 15 integrative overlapping nodes. This overlapping modular decomposition offered novel insights into the structure of human (intra-hemispheric) cortical connectivity, especially by identifying overlapping regions that spanned multiple biologically plausible modules and sat higher in the putative functional hierarchy of the cortex. Furthermore, the OSLOM decomposition yielded graph-theory measures (e.g., participation coefficient) that better distinguished these overlapping regions and more closely aligned to an independent measure of cortical organization (PG1) than a comparable traditional non-overlapping partition algorithm (Louvain), supporting the hypothesized hierarchical variation of nodal participation in the network. 

The differing patterns in functional connectivity PG1 values among the 15 overlapping nodes suggest that these (possible) cross-network structural bridges serve a wide spectrum of functions, which likely arise from a confluence of factors that include cytoarchitecture, dynamic brain state, receptor densities, and functional properties of the underlying macroscale brain systems. Given this apparent functional heterogeneity, future investigation is warranted to more fully characterize how the nature of overlapping regions relates to their own intrinsic structural, functional, and/or cytoarchitectonic properties, as well as those of the network communities they span.

In applying our systematic framework to the human cortical right-hemisphere connectome, our results highlight the importance of uncovering meaningful overlapping network partitions that better capture how the brain may balance functional integration and segregation on a static backbone of structural connectivity.
