Introduction

The midbrain is a critical hub for motor control, sensory processing and autonomic regulation, relying on a well-organized vascular network to support oxygen delivery and metabolic demands. While there has been progress towards mapping cortical vasculature, subcortical microvascular architecture remains undercharacterized because human data are scarce and difficult to obtain at a level of detail that depicts these intricate vessels. Here we segmented the midbrain vasculature from a T2*-weighted post-mortem MRI scan acquired at 50 µm isotropic resolution1. We quantified vascular characteristics within specific midbrain regions related to auditory processing – the inferior and superior colliculi and periaqueductal grey (PAG).

Methods

Data pre-processing and vessel segmentation: 

The raw T2*-weighted post-mortem midbrain volume was pre-processed using ANTs’ N4 bias field correction2 employing a dense 3 mm B-spline grid. This configuration removes contrast variations across fibres and subcortical tissue, while preserving higher-frequency vesselness features, hence enhancing segmentation consistency. Vessel segmentation was performed using the updated VesselBoost pipeline3, incorporating intensity-based augmentation to handle T2* contrast variability. The deep-learning model was pretrained on 14 time-of-flight images from the StudyForrest project4 and fine-tuned on a 300-slice slab from the post-mortem midbrain. The fine-tuned model was subsequently applied to the whole volume to reconstruct the complete vascular map (Figure 1(a)).

Orientation analysis:

For region specific vasculature analysis, we registered a manual atlas to the T2* image to label the inferior/superior colliculi and PAG. Orientations were estimated from the principal eigenvector of the 3D structure tensor computed on the segmented vasculature (Figure 1(b)). To relate orientations to anatomy, signed distance fields were generated; angles between each vessel direction vector and the unit gradient of the respective distance field were calculated to determine whether vessels were oriented towards the cerebral aqueduct. Angle distribution was analysed by regions. 

Feature analyses:

We further extracted the vessel graph from the whole-specimen segmentation to conduct graph-based analysis of the colliculi vasculature, including the number of segments, segment length, radius, tortuosity and node degree. To minimise noise artefacts, the shortest and thinnest 1% of vessel segments were excluded from statistical analysis. We quantified vascular density within the inferior and superior colliculi and calculated the number of vessel segments spanning between the colliculi and PAG regions.

Results

Regional differences in vascular density were observed across the midbrain: the superior colliculus showed a higher mean vascular fraction (5.4% of tissue volume) than the inferior colliculus (4.4%), while PAG exhibited the highest density (6.0%). Relative to the surface of the cerebral aqueduct, vessels within the PAG did not show a preferred orientation, whereas vessels within the superior and, to a lesser degree, inferior colliculi showed a preferred angle between  (Figure 2(a)). Most vessel segments had a length between 100 μm and 400 μm (most common: 200 – 300 μm) and a radius between 50 μm and 120 μm (most common: 85 – 100 μm). Vessel segments were straight (most common curveness < 1.1) and with predominantly endpoints and bifurcations observed (Figure 2(b)).

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the feasibility of quantifying vascular characteristics within midbrain tissues using whole-specimen T2*-weighted MRI segmentation. While Duvernoy et al. reported a three-fold higher vascular density in the inferior colliculus than in the superior colliculus5, our results instead showed a 20% reduction of vascular fraction in the inferior colliculus. This discrepancy likely reflects the limited ability of the 50 µm T2* voxels to capture the many 5–10 µm capillaries present histologically. We also observed that the inferior colliculus may contain proportionally more small-scale vessels, and that vessels in both colliculi display a consistent preferred orientation. These attributes are relevant for interpreting BOLD fMRI, whose amplitude depends on the angle between the vessel and the main magnetic field. A key limitation is our inability to distinguish arteries from veins, leading to combined vascular metrics. Overall, the presented framework provides a basis for extending vessel-level geometric and connectivity analyses to whole-specimen or in-vivo datasets and supporting future investigations of subcortical vascular organization and neurovascular coupling.
