Introduction

Although the pathophysiology of schizophrenia remains unclear, the Bayesian brain hypothesis has attracted attention as a possible unified framework for explaining it.1,2 The Bayesian brain hypothesis posits that human perception and cognition are achieved through updating internal models that reflect the external environment, based on prior predictions and prediction errors derived from sensory inputs. Within this framework, schizophrenia is thought to involve impaired detection of prediction errors due to reduced precision of both prior predictions and sensory inputs, resulting in the inability to appropriately update internal models. This dysfunction is considered to give rise to symptoms such as hallucinations and delusions. However, empirical measurement of prediction errors in schizophrenia has been limited, and thus the Bayesian brain hypothesis of schizophrenia remains insufficiently verified. Previous psychological studies have shown that when recognizing masked faces, people tend to predict the hidden parts of the face using an average face representation.3,4 Based on this, we hypothesized that when viewing the same face without a mask, prediction errors would occur as deviations from the predicted average. Therefore, we aimed to test the Bayesian brain hypothesis of schizophrenia by measuring prediction errors arising from masked-face recognition.

Methods

Eighteen patients with schizophrenia (9 male, 9 female; mean age = 45.0 years, SD = 11.9) and eighteen healthy controls (5 male, 13 female; mean age = 38.6 years, SD = 11.3) participated in the study. All participants provided written informed consent after receiving a detailed study explanation. This study was conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki guidelines. To measure prediction errors, we presented masked face images followed by the same faces with the mask removed, and recorded the corresponding brain activity. Figure 1 shows the experimental procedure. Two types of facial stimuli were used: (1) individual, unmodified faces, and (2) average faces created by morphing ten randomly selected faces. We hypothesized that prediction errors would occur for individual faces, but not for average faces, because the deviation from prediction would be smaller in the latter. Additionally, to assess normal face recognition responses, we presented unmasked faces first followed by masked faces, and measured brain activity during this sequence as well. 

Brain activity was recorded using a whole-head MEG system (RICOH160-1; RICOH Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) with 160-channel axial gradiometers in a magnetically shielded room at Kumagaya General Hospital. Participants lay supine and were instructed to remain awake and relaxed. The gradiometer coils (15 mm diameter, 50 mm height, 23 mm separation) sampled data at 2,000 Hz with an online 500-Hz low-pass filter. For co-registration, five fiducial markers (nasion, bilateral 10 mm anterior to tragus, and bilateral preauricular points) were digitized. Recording began immediately after closing the shielded room door to ensure alertness, with continuous monitoring by trained technicians.

Continuous MEG signals were first denoised using the dual-signal subspace projection (DSSP) algorithm in RICOH MEG Analysis. DSSP is functionally comparable to the temporally extended signal space separation algorithm. Further processing used MNE-Python. Line noise artifacts at 48–52, 98–102, 148–152, 198–202, 248–252, and 298–302 Hz were notch-filtered. Signal-space projection was applied to remove heartbeat and eye movement artifacts.

To minimize edge effects, epochs were extracted from -2500 to 2500 ms relative to face image onset. Baseline correction utilized the -200 to 0 ms pre-stimulus interval. Epochs with artifacts >4000 fT were excluded. Remaining epochs were averaged to obtain event-related fields (ERFs), bandpass filtered (1–25 Hz), and converted to Z-scores using the -200 to 0 ms baseline. A spatiotemporal cluster test compared waveforms between mask-removal and face-recognition conditions.

For source estimation, structural T1-weighted MRI scans (1-mm slice thickness) were acquired using a Discovery MR750w Expert 3.0T scanner and reconstructed using FreeSurfer. Data epoching and averaging followed the sensor-level analysis procedure. Source estimation was conducted on averaged ERFs using MNE-Python. Forward head modeling used a single-compartment boundary-element model defined by the inner skull surface. Source current distribution was estimated via a noise-normalized dynamic statistical parametric mapping method, with noise covariance computed from empty room recordings. A depth-weighting parameter (depth = 0.8) was applied to reduce bias toward superficial currents. Source time series were morphed onto the FreeSurfer "fsaverage" template. To reduce computational costs for the cluster test, the "fsaverage" template with 1,284 vertices was adopted.

The source time courses were bandpass filtered between 1 and 25 Hz and converted to Z-scores using the -200 to 0 ms baseline period. Finally, the spatiotemporal cluster test was conducted to compare the waveforms between the mask-removal and face-recognition conditions.

Results

Figure 2 (A, B, C, D) shows the results of the sensor-level analysis. For each group and face type, we compared the face-recognition and mask-removal conditions using a spatiotemporal cluster test. The orange-highlighted areas in the ERFs indicate the spatial and temporal windows, with the cluster-corrected p<0.05. In the HC group, a significant difference was observed between 234 and 511 ms over the eight sensors in the left temporal region (indicated in white) when viewing individual faces in the mask-removal condition compared with the face-recognition condition. No significant clusters were observed under other conditions. Therefore, we present the ERFs and mean F-map during this time window from the same eight sensors that showed significant responses in the individual face condition of the HC group. Figure 2 (E) shows the results of the source-level analysis. For each group and face type, we compared the neural responses between the face-recognition and mask-removal conditions using a spatiotemporal cluster test. The orange-highlighted areas indicate the spatial and temporal windows, with cluster-corrected p<0.05. In the HC group, a significant response was observed at 207–408 ms in the vertices centered on the left insula (outlined by the blue line) when viewing individual faces after mask removal. No significant clusters were observed under other conditions. Therefore, we present the ERFs and mean F-map during this time window from the same vertices that showed a significant response in the individual face condition of the HC group.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we measured the prediction error elicited by mask removal from faces using MEG and examined the Bayesian brain hypothesis in patients with SZ. HCs exhibited prediction errors in the left insular cortex when the mask was removed from individual faces, even without repeated stimuli, indicating a high prediction precision in masked face recognition. Patients with SZ show deficits in detecting prediction errors, suggesting a decrease in the precision of prior predictions and sensory input, as postulated by the Bayesian brain hypothesis.
