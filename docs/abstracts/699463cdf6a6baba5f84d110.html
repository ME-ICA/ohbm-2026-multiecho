Introduction

Sleep is a fundamental biological process, both in terms of the time spent sleeping and its implications for physical and mental health. Sleep research has revealed distinct physiological patterns in neuronal firing, metabolism and haemodynamics as the brain descend from wakefulness to sleep. To investigate these processes, various modalities have been employed, including EEG, fMRI and PET.1 While early PET studies with the radioligand [18F]Fluorodeoxyglucose ([18F]FDG) only provided static information and required several scanning sessions to compare conditions, more recently, functional PET (fPET) has been used to investigate metabolic changes dynamically within a single scanning session during task performance 1 as well as during sleep.2 With the aim to further elucidate alterations in glucose metabolism during sleep, multimodal fPET-EEG data were acquired in healthy volunteers, thereby providing complementary information on arousal states and dynamics of glucose metabolism across wakefulness and sleep.

Methods

Twenty healthy volunteers were measured on a long axial field of view (FOV) Biograph Vision Quadra PET/CT Scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Germany), with simultaneous EEG using a 32-channel cap (Brain Products GmbH, Germany). Subjects were neither sleep deprived nor were they medicated to induce sleep. An ultra-low dose (ULD) CT was acquired prior to PET start and reconstructed with an extended Hounsfield unit scale, to fully cover the range of values in the EEG electrodes. Scatter and attenuation correction were performed using the ULD CT. Subjects were placed with their head centered in the FOV for optimal PET sensitivity and PET data were reconstructed to 1 s frames using a maximum ring difference of 322. EEG and fPET recording started simultaneous with application of [18F]FDG. The radioligand was applied using a bolus + constant infusion protocol (5.47 MBq/kg) over a total period of 90 minutes. After a 20-minute baseline acquisition, subjects were instructed to close their eyes. EEG was recorded using a sampling rate of 5 kHz, later downsampled to 256 Hz for further processing. Sleep stages N1-N3 were manually annotated according to the guidelines by the American Academy of Sleep Medicine. Within a separate scanning session, a structural T1-weighted image was acquired with a 3 T MAGNETOM Prisma MRI scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Germany). Standard preprocessing was applied to fPET data, including correction for head movement and transformation to MNI standard space via a structural MRI scan, followed by smoothing with a 3D Gaussian filter (8 mm FWHM). Two data sets that were missing the corresponding structural MRI scan were normalized using a PET template that was derived from normalized mean images of the remaining subjects. Percent signal change (%SC) in glucose metabolism between rest and sleep was quantified using the functional PET toolbox 3, combining sleeping stages N1-N3 into one regressor. Indiviudal %SC in clusters showing significant changes (one-sample t-test, p < 0.05, family-wise error (FWE) corrected cluster level, following p < 0.001 uncorrected voxel level) was extracted and correlated with total time spent in various sleep stages.

Results

After preprocessing, 18 (mean age 25.1 ± 5.2 years; 12 females) of the 20 datasets were identified as suitable for analysis. Sleep staging based on EEG data revealed a large spread in the distribution of sleep stages. All subjects fell asleep during the measurement and spent a median of 17.5 minutes (IQR = 15.0) in N1. N2 was identified in 10 subjects (median = 18.2 minutes, IQR = 22.5), while only 2 subjects reached N3. A widespread reduction in glucose metabolism was found in the voxel-wise fPET analysis (Figure 1). Effects were most prominent in the thalamus (-22.1 ± 11.1%) from the wake state) and regions of the default mode (-9.8 ± 8.3%), salience (-8.9, ± 7.5%) and frontoparietal networks (-11.8 ± 8.4%). Pearson correlation of cluster-wise %SC and time spent in N2 revealed a strong negative association (p < 0.05, Bonferroni corrected, r = -0.87). No such association was found for N1 or other regions. N3 occurred too sparsely to include in further analyses.

Conclusion

In this work, we present stable and widespread reductions in glucose metabolism during NREM sleep in non-sleep-deprived, healthy individuals, achieved without pharmacological sleep induction. Strikingly, all subjects fell asleep for extended periods of time. This may indicate a potential caveat in investigations of prolonged resting state, especially with eyes closed. The most pronounced reductions were observed in the thalamus, where correlations with N2 duration suggest sleep-phase-dependent effects on glucose metabolism. These findings are in line with early [18F]FDG PET studies, reporting progressive metabolic decrease from N1 to N3.4 Likewise, a recent multimodal sleep study showed  metabolic  changes in the thalamus during sleep.2 The thalamus is centrally involved in sensorimotor integration and the generation of sleep spindles5, further supporting its sensitivity to NREM-sleep dynamics. These robust findings highlight the feasibility of combined fPET-EEG sleep studies without the sleep deprivation or medication. Future work should incorporate extended recording durations to capture sufficient N3 and REM sleep for comprehensive analysis
