Introduction

Predictive machine-learning (ML) models built from neuroimaging and clinical biomarkers hold promise for supporting early diagnosis and prognosis in Alzheimer’s disease (AD). However, most clinical ML studies in AD prioritise maximising classification accuracy, even though analytic flexibility in preprocessing, feature construction, and model selection can produce substantial variability in results, leading to poor reproducibility. Prior “many-analysts’’ studies [1] demonstrate that small differences in analytic choices often yield inconsistent or overly confident conclusions, a concern amplified in neuroimaging where sample sizes are relatively modest, and regional measures vary with processing pipelines [3]. In this work, we shift the emphasis from performance maximisation alone to understanding data- and ML model-driven instability: we systematically examine variability across resampling runs, diverse ML architectures (including stacked ensembles and the AutoML-Multiverse framework), and multiple imaging, clinical, and multimodal feature sets. We aimed to quantify uncertainty in predictive performance, determine how strongly conclusions depend on modelling decisions, and highlight the risks of relying on single-pipeline analyses in small-to-moderate AD datasets, and to assess whether such analytic sensitivity generalises across independent cohorts. 

Methods

We implemented the AutoML-Multiverse framework that defines a latent space of analytic pipelines, constructed from ~20,000 preprocessing and modelling combinations [2, 3]. Unlike traditional AutoML approaches that define pipeline similarity based on accuracy alone, our method organises pipelines according to the similarity of their patient-level predictions, providing more information to the latent ‘pipeline space’ (i.e., the multiverse). Bayesian optimisation is then used to efficiently traverse this latent space, identify high-performing regions, and construct data-driven ensembles that integrate complementary information across pipelines (see Figure 1 for a schematic overview). 

Our primary aim was to characterise how sensitive a clinically relevant ML task in AD (stable mild cognitive impairment (sMCI) vs. progressive MCI (pMCI)), is to analytic choices (e.g., pipeline selection, data modality, repeated resampling) and to assess whether these patterns are consistent across independent cohorts. To evaluate this modelling sensitivity, we analysed performance across 100 independent resampling runs, three feature configurations (imaging-only, clinical-only, and multimodal), and two datasets (ADNI and NACC). AutoML-Multiverse results were compared against nine individual ML models and a stacked ensemble baseline. 

Structural MRI volumes were derived from T1-weighted scans, head size-corrected, and standard-scaled; clinical variables included age, sex, and cognition measured by MMSE/MoCA tests. Sample sizes were n=369 (224 sMCI, 145 pMCI) for ADNI and n=225 (106 sMCI, 119 pMCI) for NACC. In ADNI, mean age was 70.4 ± 7.0 years in both sMCI and pMCI, with similar sex distributions (sMCI: 60.5% male; pMCI: 59.1% male) and high cognitive scores (MMSE median [IQR]: 28.6 [1.0] vs 28.2 [2.0]). In NACC, participants were slightly older (sMCI: 72.7 ± 7.8; pMCI: 74.3 ± 8.8 years), sex distributions were balanced (sMCI: 56.6% male; pMCI: 52.1% male), and MoCA scores were lower and more variable in pMCI (median [IQR]: 23.1 [3.0] vs 21.7 [4.0]). 

Results

Across 100 resampling runs (Figure 2), balanced accuracy on the unseen test set showed substantial variability across models, modalities, and cohorts, indicating that sMCI–pMCI prognosis is highly sensitive to analytic choices. In ADNI, AutoML achieved the highest mean accuracy across imaging-only (0.695 ± 0.059), clinical-only (0.543 ± 0.042), and multimodal (0.677 ± 0.058) settings, outperforming nine individual models and the stacked ensemble. In NACC, AutoML remained among the strongest performers in imaging-only (0.696 ± 0.083) and clinical-only (0.677 ± 0.078) tasks. In the NACC multimodal setting, XGBoost achieved the highest mean balanced accuracy (0.644 ± 0.098), with AutoML performing comparably (0.640 ± 0.085). 

Performance trends differed markedly across cohorts: imaging-only and multimodal pipelines ranked highest in ADNI, whereas imaging-only and clinical-only performed best in NACC, indicating limited cross-cohort generalisability of modality-specific conclusions. Notably, multimodal performance did not consistently exceed imaging-only accuracy, reflecting the fact that adding low-signal or cohort-specific clinical features can increase model variance and reduce generalisability in small-to-moderate datasets. AutoML constructed ensembles in only 20–42% of runs, and no single pipeline was consistently selected as the optimal choice across different train-test splits, modalities, or datasets; further evidence of substantial pipeline-induced variability. 

Conclusion

Our findings show that predictive ML models for Alzheimer’s disease, such as sMCI–pMCI classification, can be highly sensitive to modelling decisions, data modality, and cohort characteristics, underlining the risks of interpreting single-pipeline analyses as definitive. The AutoML-multiverse framework offers a principled way to characterise model- and sampling-driven uncertainty, potentially revealing when seemingly similar pipelines yield meaningfully different patient-level predictions. By providing an instability-aware evaluation strategy that generalises across cohorts, this work outlines a practical path toward more reliable and transparent machine-learning studies in neurodegenerative disease research.
