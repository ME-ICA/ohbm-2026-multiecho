Introduction

Introduction

Predictive coding theory suggests that the brain functions as a proactive inference machine, where higher cortical regions (e.g., frontal) generate predictions about upcoming sensory inputs and transmit them to lower regions (e.g., occipital) for comparison with the actual inputs. Matches between expected and actual inputs result in attenuated neural responses (Expectation Suppression), while mismatches amplify activity as prediction errors (Surprise Enhancement) that feed forward to higher regions for further processing.1 However, the dynamic transmission of top-down predictions and bottom-up errors in the human brain remains poorly understood. In particular, it is unclear how the brain differentially responds to predictable and unexpected stimuli, and how frequency-specific neural oscillations contribute to the information exchange across brain regions. Some studies reported reduced responses to predicted stimuli,2 while others found enhanced ERP amplitudes,3 challenging the simple suppression account. It was reported that alpha and beta rhythms may encode top-down predictions,4 while gamma oscillations may carry bottom-up errors. However, direct evidence in humans for frequency-specific propagation of these signals remains sparse.

To address these gaps, we conducted a visual mismatch experiment using three stimulus sequences: predictable (Pre), unpredictable (Unp), and prediction error (PE). EEG was employed to capture neural activity across the whole brain and time course. Event-related potential (ERP) and time-frequency analysis (TFA) assessed both the strength and spectral features of neural responses. By comparing the amplitude of components and oscillatory power across conditions, this study uncovered the mechanisms by which predictive and error signals were transmitted in the human cortex. The findings provided critical insights into the neural basis of predictive coding, advancing our understanding of human perception and cognition.

Methods

Participants

Forty-two healthy right-handed participants with normal or corrected vision took part in this study. All participants gave written consent, in accordance with the Ethics Committee of the Klinikum Rechts der Isar, and were compensated for their participation.

Stimuli and Procedure

Participants completed a three-day training phase, viewing two 10-minute blocks per day containing Pre and Unp image sequences. Each sequence began with a 1.1 s fixation cross, followed by five images presented consecutively (800 ms each). In the Pre condition, image order was fixed, allowing stable predictions to form; in the Unp condition, images were randomly shuffled. The PE sequence was not shown during training. In the test session, participants viewed three 10-minute blocks (with 1-minute breaks), where Pre, Unp, and PE sequences appeared in randomized order. PE sequences were derived from the predictable sequences but contained 1-3 substituted images in positions 2-5, thereby violating expectations. To maintain attention, 10% of trials included an inverted image as a catch trial. Participants responded as quickly and accurately as possible and received reaction-time feedback after each block. 

EEG Recording and Preprocessing

EEG was recorded with a 65-channel EEG system, and the sample rate was 1000Hz. The data was re-referenced to the average reference and down-sampled to 500Hz. A 0.25-0.75Hz transition band was set for the high-pass filter to remove low-frequency interference. The power-line interference of 50, 100, and 150Hz was removed. The bad channels were rejected and interpolated using the superfast spherical interpolation method. After that, the data was referenced to the average again to get a more robust re-reference. Independent Components Analysis (ICA) was carried out to remove artifacts. The components were automatically classified and rejected if the probability of a non-brain source was over 0.8.

Event-Related Potential Analysis

Preprocessed data were epoched from -200 to 800 ms relative to stimulus onset. For each subject, trials for stimuli 2-5 were averaged within each condition. The -200 to 0 ms interval served as the baseline, and a 30 Hz low-pass filter was applied to remove high-frequency and line noise.

Pairwise comparisons among the three conditions (PE vs Pre, Pre vs Unp, PE vs Unp) were conducted using a whole-brain spatiotemporal cluster-based permutation test (channels × time) in FieldTrip.5 The test was two-tailed with 1000 randomizations. Cluster formation was based on a channel-level threshold of 0.05 and required at least two neighboring channels. Cluster significance was assessed at α = 0.025. Based on the statistical patterns, frontal (F1, Fz, F2, FC1, FCz, FC2), central (C1, Cz, C2, CP1, CPz, CP2), and occipital (PO7, PO3, POz, PO4, PO8, O1, Oz, O2) regions were defined, and grand-averaged ERPs across subjects were performed for these ROIs.

Time-Frequency Analysis

Epoched EEG data were decomposed into their time-frequency representation using FieldTrip. Time-frequency power was estimated using a multitaper convolution method with a Hanning taper. Frequencies of interest ranged from 8-40 Hz in 1Hz steps (𝛼: 8-12 Hz, 𝛽:13-30Hz, 𝛾: 31-40 Hz). A fixed analysis window of 0.2 s was applied at all frequencies. Baseline corrected with respect to the -100 to 0 ms pre-stimulus interval using a decibel transform (dB). 

Cluster-based permutation test (time × frequency) was conducted on the time-frequency power within each ROI. Cluster correction was applied across the time-frequency dimension, with clusters formed at a sample-level threshold of p < 0.05. A two-tailed test was used, with the cluster-level significance threshold set to α = 0.025. For each comparison (PE vs Pre; Unp vs Pre; PE vs Unp), 1000 random permutations were executed.

Results

Results

Whole-Brain Spatiotemporal ERP Analysis Results

The ERPs for three conditions across three ROIs are presented in Figure 2A. Within the typical time window of mismatch response (~200ms), a negative component was observed in the occipital region, while a positive component appeared in the frontal region. Additionally, a later negative component in the frontal and central regions was identified between 300 and 400 ms.

The results of pairwise comparisons are shown in Figure 2B. When comparing the Pre and Unp conditions, around 200 ms, a significant negative cluster (p = 0.005) was found in the occipital region, which indicated that the N2 component in Pre condition was more negative than Unp condition, and a significant positive cluster (p = 0.002) was found in the frontal region which indicated that the P2 component in Pre condition was more positive than Unp condition. Moreover, a significant positive cluster (p = 0.002) was found in the frontal-central region around 300-400 ms, which indicated that the N300/400 component in the Unp condition was more negative than the Pre condition (see Figure 2B, upper 1st plot). When comparing the PE and Pre conditions, a significant negative cluster (p = 0.001) was found in the frontal-central region around 300-400 ms, which indicated that the N300/400 component in the PE condition was more negative than the Pre condition (see Figure 2B, upper 2nd plot). There was no significant difference between PE and Unp conditions across whole electrodes and ROIs. The topological brain map of the difference in pairwise comparison is shown in Figure 2B, lower panel.

Time-Frequency Analysis Results

As shown in Figure 2C, power was averaged for each combination of three ROIs and three frequency bands, yielding nine plots in total. Each row represents an ROI, and each column indicates a frequency band. Between 100-200 ms, the alpha band power increases in three ROIs (Figure 2C left, 1st column), while there is a decrease around 300 ms in the beta bands of three ROIs (Figure 2C left, 2nd column). In the gamma band, an increased power was found in the central region around 300 ms. The pairwise permutation test for three conditions across each electrode in the specific ROI is performed, corrected by the cluster of time-frequency domain, and a significant positive cluster was found in CP1 electrode (see Figure 2C right).In the central CP1 electrode, the power of the Unp and PE conditions is significantly stronger than that of the Pre condition in the gamma band (see Figure 2C right, upper). A positive cluster is observed in the time-frequency domain when comparing Unp and Pre (p = 0.006, see Figure 2C right, upper middle), as well as when comparing PE and Pre (p = 0.02, see Figure 2C right, upper right). Meanwhile, there is no significant difference between the PE and Unp conditions. Conclusion

Conclusion

The larger occipital ERP amplitude observed in the Pre condition around 200 ms reflected that the visual cortex was more sensitive to predictable input, and the sensory representation was sharpened. In the later period, the ERP amplitudes were larger in PE and Unp compared to Pre in frontal and central regions, suggesting a differentiated processing of novel information to expected information. An increase in power within three ROIs in the alpha band (8-12 Hz) between 100-200 ms was observed, which suggested the top-down prediction related to alpha band. A stronger gamma-band power was observed between 300-400 ms when comparing PE and Unp conditions to Pre, indicating that the propagation and processing of the bottom-up error signal may be associated with gamma oscillations. The ERP and TFA results for PE and Unp were not significant at any spatial, temporal, or frequency points. This suggested that the processing mechanisms behind these two conditions were similar, as both conditions related to error processing and were similarly novelty driven.
