Introduction

The menopausal transition presents a psychoneuroendocrine phase of marked hormonal fluctuations accompanied by structural and functional brain changes.1 This period is increasingly recognized as a window of vulnerability for mental health, during which emotional functions, including emotion regulation (ER), are affected.2,3 First evidence links menopausal symptoms to greater ER difficulties and affective burden3 and task-based ER fMRI studies suggest altered recruitment of cognitive control regions across menopausal stages.4 However, intrinsic neural dynamics of ER during this transition remain unknown. Using spectral dynamic causal modeling (spDCM), the present study examines for the first time how menopausal status modulates resting-state effective connectivity within an ER-related network.5 Additionally, we test whether network dynamics predict individual ER difficulties across pre-, peri-, and postmenopausal women.

Methods

We analyzed psychometric, hormone, and resting-state fMRI data from 76 healthy women (PREmenopausal: n = 32; PERImenopausal:  n = 19; POSTmenopausal: n = 25; age 35-59). Menopause status was defined by menstrual history and confirmed via serum FSH levels (POST > PERI > PRE, p < .001). PRE were naturally cycling and scanned during early follicular phase; PERI showed irregular cycles and menopausal symptoms; POST reported >12 months amenorrhea. Self-reported ER ability was assessed with the Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale-16 (DERS-16). Resting-state data (10 min) were acquired at a 3T MRI scanner and preprocessed with fMRIPrep 24.1.1. A first-level general linear model was estimated for each participant, including six motion parameters and white matter/CSF signals as nuisance regressors. Time series for each region of interest within a meta-analytically predefined ER network5 were then extracted as the principal eigenvariate from a 6-mm sphere centered on peak MNI coordinates. Effective connectivity was estimated using spectral dynamic causal modeling (spDCM). For each participant, a fully connected DCM including all ROIs was specified, and Bayesian model inversion was performed using variational Laplace estimation to fit the cross-spectral densities. Group-level effects of menopausal status were assessed using hierarchical parametric empirical Bayes (PEB). Pairwise group contrasts (PRE > PERI, PRE > POST, PERI > POST) were evaluated using Bayesian model reduction and averaging within this framework. Within-group PEB models tested associations between effective connectivity and DERS-16 scores. Parameters with posterior probability >.95 were considered robust.For interpretability, only connections exceeding an absolute connectivity strength of ≥|.10| Hz (and ≥|.01| Hz for DERS-16 associations) are reported. Finally, leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) assessed whether effective connectivity could predict DERS-16 scores within each group.

Results

The three groups (PRE, PERI, POST) were comparable across most sociodemographic and psychometric variables, and no significant differences were observed in self-reported emotion-regulation difficulties (DERS-16; p = .229). 

Second-level PEB analyses revealed strong evidence for group differences in ER network effective connectivity across menopausal groups (see Figure 1). The PRE > POST contrast (Figure 1B) showed opposing connectivity pattern, with PRE showing greater excitatory output connections from bilateral inferior frontal gyri (IFG) and the left medial temporal gyrus (LMTG) to frontal, temporal, and parietal regions. Reduced inhibitory connections in PRE relative to POST originated from medial frontal, superior temporal and parietal sources and targeted all three lobes of the network. The PRE > PERI contrast (Figure 1A) revealed the largest number of differences. Here, frontal source regions (LIFG, RIFG, RMFG) projected to all other lobes with a mixed pattern of enhanced and reduced excitatory and inhibitory influences. Notably, the left medial frontal gyrus (LMeFG) emerged as a selective target hub, receiving predominantly excitatory inputs from regions across the network. The PERI > POST contrast (Figure 1C) showed fewer differences overall.  Reduced inhibitory outputs from frontal and parietal regions were found for PERI compared to POST. The LMTG emerged as the dominant source region, showing enhanced connectivity for PERI: inhibitory projections to left frontal and temporal regions (LIFG, LSTG) and excitatory projections to right frontal and parietal targets (RMFG, RSMG). Across contrasts, PRE differed most strongly from the other groups in frontal connectivity, while the LMTG and LMeFG contributed to specific group comparisons rather than showing uniform effects across the menopausal stages.

Within-group PEB analyses identified group-specific associations between effective connectivity and DERS-16 scores, with patterns differing markedly across menopausal stages. PRE showed a balanced mix of positive and negative associations, predominantly involving inhibitory connections across the network; PERI displayed fewer and mainly negative associations, which were mostly confined to inhibitory connections targeting frontal regions; and POST exhibited the largest number of connection associations, most of which were positive alongside a more balanced excitatory/inhibitory connectivity profile. Across groups, the right medial frontal gyrus (RMFG) emerged as a key source hub for POST, sending excitatory influences to temporal and parietal regions and inhibitory influences to frontal regions. The LIFG emerged as a target hub across groups, receiving excitatory and inhibitory inputs from the network and showing a group-dependent association profile with the DERS-16 score. 

Results of the LOOCV revealed distinct predictive connectivity profiles across groups. In PRE, DERS-16 scores were predicted by three pathways, including frontal-frontal (excitatory and inhibitory) and temporal-frontal (inhibitory) connections. PERI likewise showed three predictive connections, all confined within the frontal lobe. In POST, only a single inhibitory connection (RMFG to LMeFG) negatively predicted DERS-16 score, whereas in PRE and PERI this predictive pathway ran in the opposite direction (LMeFG to RMFG) and showed a positive association with DERS-16.

Conclusion

Our results suggest that resting-state effective connectivity within an ER network varies across menopausal transition, with perimenopausal women showing unique connectivity patterns that reveal network changes not captured when comparing only pre- and postmenopausal groups. While PRE showed predominantly enhanced frontal-driven outputs, PERI and POST increasingly relied on medial frontal and temporal pathways. The extensive differences in the PRE vs PERI contrast point to perimenopause as a phase of heightened neural variability1,4 accompanied by the emergence of transition-sensitive hubs such as the LMeFG and LMTG. These distinct neural connectivity architectures occur despite comparable self-reported ER abilities, suggesting that similar ER outcomes may be supported by different neural mechanisms across the menopausal transition.4 Notably, spDCM revealed a reversed predictive pathway in POST, highlighting directional changes that conventional functional connectivity approaches would not detect. While cross-sectional, these findings point toward a potential reorganization of ER network dynamics across menopausal stages and underscore the value of studying perimenopausal samples to better characterize neural mechanisms relevant to affective resilience and vulnerability in hormonal transition periods.
