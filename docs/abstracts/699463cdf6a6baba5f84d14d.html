Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is heterogeneous, encompassing diverse symptoms and comorbidities. Studies using resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) have consistently linked MDD to changes in functional connectivity (FC). Mapping brain-symptom associations with multivariate approaches and clustering subjects based on co-occurring brain activity patterns is a clinically useful approach to understand the impact of brain dynamics heterogeneity on symptom presentations. Pioneering studies have used symptom-specific fMRI-FC networks and revealed biological depression phenotypes corresponding to distinct symptom profiles and responses to brain stimulation interventions.1-2

Electro- and magnetoencephalography (EEG/MEG) directly measure neuronal population activity with millisecond precision. This activity is characterized by rhythmic oscillations, which are fundamental for neuronal computations and provide millisecond-resolution temporal clocking mechanisms. Transiently coupled large-scale oscillatory networks have been found to be essential for dynamic routing of information in healthy brain functions, and, conversely, aberrant oscillatory dynamics are characteristic to many brain disorders. In depression, local oscillatory activity is aberrant in several frequency bands. Despite the promise of revealing dynamic oscillatory coupling directly linkable to underlying neural mechanisms via M/EEG recordings, no studies so far have leveraged oscillation-based FC data to identify putative depression phenotypes by clustering patients according to their co-occurring oscillatory and synchronization patterns.

To address this knowledge gap, we collected symptom data using 10 self-report questionnaires, resting-state MEG, and MRI data from 263 patients with unipolar depression and from 75 healthy controls. We computed multivariate brain-symptom associations and leveraged the latent components to identify depression phenotypes with clustering algorithms. 

Methods

DATA DESCRIPTION

Study participants consisted of the volunteers with N=263 patients with MDD (163 females, 87 males, and 13 labeled as “other”) and 75 healthy control participants (29 females, 45 males, and 1 labeled as “other”). All subjects gave the informed consent form. 

All participants were required to complete 10 following self-report symptom scales. These scales include Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) measuring depressive severity, Quick Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology 16-item (QIDS) evaluating depressive symptom severity, Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item (GAD) Scale assessing anxiety symptoms, Ruminative Responses Scale Short-version (RRS) evaluating ruminative thought, Sheehan Disability Scale (SDS) evaluating functional impairment, Brief Experiential Avoidance Questionnaire (BEAQ) measuring experiential avoidance, PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL) assessing post-traumatic stress disorder symptoms, Alcohol, Smoking, and Substance Involvement Screening Test Lite (ASSIST) assessing substance abuse, Positive Valence Systems Scale (PVSS) examining positive affect and reward processing, and WHO-5 Well-Being Index (WHO) measuring subjective well-being.

Fifteen minutes eyes-open resting-state MEG data were recorded with a 306-channel MEG system at the BioMag Laboratory or at MEG Core in Finland. T1-weighted anatomical MRI scans were obtained with a 3-tesla whole-body MRI scanner at AMI Centre, Aalto University. 

DATA ANALYSIS 

Temporal signal-space separation and Maxwell filtering were used to suppress extra-cranial noise and compensate for head motion in the raw MEG data. Independent component analysis was used to extract ocular and heartbeat artefacts. Freesurfer software was used for MRI volumetric segmentation, surface reconstruction, flattening, and cortical parcellation. MNE-Python was used for MEG-MRI coregistration and preparation of the forward and inverse operator for the source modelling of the MEG data. Noise-covariance matrices were computed from data filtered to 151–249 Hz. Source-space vertices were collapsed into 200 parcels of the Schaefer-atlas using fidelity optimized parcellations. Broadband parcel time series were filtered into narrowband time series with 32 Morlet wavelets with center frequencies spanning from 2.1 to 59.3 Hz in log-linear space. 

Oscillation based FC was then computed for each narrow-band frequency and between all cortical parcels with two metrics: phase synchrony (PS) and amplitude correlations (AC), with weighted phase lag index (wPLI) and orthogonalized correlation coefficient (oCC), respectively. 

To identify brain-symptom associations, we applied Partial Least Squares Correlation (PLSC).3 PLSC aims to extract latent components which can maximize the common information shared between two datasets by projecting data into a low-dimensional space. Age and sex effects were first regressed out, and the cross-covariance of symptoms and node strength for AC and PS were computed separately.  Statistical significance of the latent components was obtained using a permutation test by randomly shuffling the order of samples 1,000 times and building the null distributions of singular values. To test the stability of loadings, we created 1,000 bootstrap samples by sampling with replacement and estimated the standard errors for each element’s loading of each latent. 

We then used the latent components to identify depression phenotypes, i.e., clusters of patients with similar patterns, with the Leiden clustering algorithm, which is designed to identify non-overlapping communities from a network.4 A key feature of the Leiden method is the resolution parameter that determines the scale of community detection, yielding a balance between finer, smaller clusters and coarser, larger ones. This adaptability enables the clustering granularity to align with the structural complexity of the data.

Results

BRAIN-SYMPTOM ASSOCIATIONS OF OSCILLATION-BASED CONNECTIVITY

Applying the multivariate approach PLSC, we obtained two significant latent components. One component was derived from AC (p=0.0085) and another from PS (p=0.0452).The AC-derived latent component was associated with symptom dimensions including rumination (RRS), anhedonia (PVSS), post-traumatic stress disorder (PCL), substance abuse (ASSIST), and well-being (WHO) (Fig. 1 a). In contrast, the PS-derived latent component captured the overall severity of depressive symptoms as measured with PHQ-9 and QIDS, along with the other 8 symptom scales (Fig. 1 a). 

For the AC-derived latent component, the key contributions emerged in theta (4–8 Hz) and beta (25–28 Hz) bands (Fig. 1 b). In contrast, for the PS-derived latent component, the key contributions emerged in the alpha band (11–16 Hz) and the beta band (22–26 Hz) (Fig. 1 b). These findings suggest that each latent component represents features from distinct frequency bands, reflecting neural underpinnings of brain-symptom associations in a frequency- and symptom-specific manner.

PHENOTYPE IDENTIFICATION AND CHARACTERIZATION

Following the identification of brain-symptom associations, we applied the Leiden clustering method to the two significant latent components to categorize MDD into five phenotypes (Fig. 1 c). 

All five depression phenotypes demonstrated distinct spectral profiles differing from the HC group for both PS and AC in a frequency-specific manner (Fig. 1 d). Cluster 1 exhibited alpha-to-beta-band hyperconnectivity in AC and PS, with a wider frequency band for AC (7–20 Hz) than PS (7–9 Hz and 18–22 Hz). Similarly, cluster 5 exhibited overall robust hyperconnectivity both in AC (2–60 Hz) and PS (6–30 Hz). Clusters 2–4 exhibited, in contrast, widespread hypoconnectivity in a frequency-specific manner. Cluster 2 exhibited hypoconnectivity, particularly in AC at the theta-alpha band (5–10 Hz) and in PS within the alpha band (8–15 Hz), cluster 3 over a wide frequency range in AC (2–60 Hz) and in PS (6–30 Hz), and cluster 4 in AC within the delta-theta range (2–8 Hz) but hyperconnectivity in PS in the beta-gamma band (25–40 Hz). Interestingly, graph strength in the alpha band peaked at different frequencies across clusters for AC, specifically at 9, 11, 11, 10, and 8 Hz for clusters 1–5, respectively, whereas PS showed much more consistent peak frequencies, with peaks at 10, 10, 11, 10, and 10 Hz for clusters 1–5 (Fig. 1 d). 

We then tested whether symptom profiles of one phenotype differed significantly (bootstrapping, N=1,000, p<0.05) from the rest of the cohort (Fig. 1 e). All symptom scores were z-scored across the whole cohort, so that the zero circle represented the average scores across patients after normalization. The values outside the zero-circle indicated more severe symptoms, while values below 0 indicated less severe symptoms. Cluster 1, associated with hyperconnectivity, exhibited more severe symptoms for most questionnaires, namely QIDS, GAD, RRS, SDS, PCL, and PVSS. Cluster 2, exhibiting hypoconnectivity in both AC and PS, demonstrated generally less severe symptomatology compared to the other depression phenotypes except for RRS and BEAQ. Cluster 3, with more robust hypoconnectivity, had larger PCL and BEAQ scores, while RRS and ASSIST scores were smaller. Cluster 4, with AC hypoconnectivity, was characterized by higher PHQ-9, ASSIST, and WHO scores. Cluster 5, with the highest hyperconnectivity in AC and PS, was characterized by more severe ASSIST but smaller PCL scores. Overall, these data established that the identified MEG oscillation phenotypes were characterized by different symptom profiles, representing different dimensions of depression symptoms. 

Conclusion

Here, we demonstrate for the first time that individually source-reconstructed MEG data and oscillation-based connectomes can identify biological depression phenotypes. With this approach, we identified five stable depression phenotypes showing neurophysiological and symptomatologic heterogeneity. 

The different phenotypes exhibited frequency-specific hyperconnectivity or hypoconnectivity compared to the healthy controls. Such increases and decreases in oscillation-based connectivity indicate separate pathophysiological mechanisms linked to depression across the affected population. The presence of opposing phenotypes in depression might explain the discrepancies in the previous findings of both increased and decreased alpha connectivity as well as the recent negative findings of the meta-analysis of fMRI data. Oscillations are one of the key mechanisms linking cellular and molecular deficiencies to the whole-brain dynamics and behavior and may thus explain the complex relationship between treatment modalities in depression. Our results of altered large-scale oscillation-based FC are in line with findings of inhibitory deficiencies, specifically in GABAergic STT-interneurons in cellular models of depression, which suggest that inhibitory dysfunctions may contribute to network-level aberrancies that cause symptoms at the behavioral level. 

Although biological phenotypes are meaningful per se for understanding the pathophysiological basis of heterogeneity in depressive symptoms, the critical question is whether the phenotypes are clinically relevant to understand the heterogeneity of depressive symptoms. Our results show that the five MEG connectivity phenotypes exhibited statistically different symptom profiles. This suggests that while these symptom characteristics may both relate to hyperconnectivity, they emerge via different biological mechanisms. 

Our findings demonstrate the presence of MEG-based FC in defining neural depression phenotypes with unique characteristics. Given that oscillations are among the key mediators between cellular neurophysiology and behavior, these phenotypes are likely to reflect underlying cellular and molecular deficiencies in depression. Collectively, our findings suggest that MEG connectivity phenotypes derived in this study have a great potential to serve as novel mechanistic biomarkers for depression paving the way for personalized medicine applications.
