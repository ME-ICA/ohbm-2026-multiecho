Introduction

Inhibitory control deficits are a core feature of obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD) and have been linked to dysfunction of the supplementary motor complex. Within this system, the pre-supplementary motor area (pre-SMA) and SMA proper play a key role in suppressing prepotent responses and selecting context-appropriate alternatives, not only for motor actions but also for higher-order cognitive operations such as language. 

Neuroimaging and familial studies indicate that altered pre-SMA activation during response inhibition is among the most consistent findings in OCD and may represent a vulnerability endophenotype.^1 However, most evidence comes from motor inhibition paradigms (e.g., Go/Nogo and Stop-Signal tasks), which mainly capture compulsive aspects of the disorder. By contrast, the present study investigated semantic inhibition in individuals with elevated OCD traits using the Hayling Sentence Completion Task.^2 This task allows for a direct examination of how the SMA/pre-SMA complex supports the suppression of dominant verbal responses. Based on this framework, we conducted a region-of-interest (ROI) analyses focusing on SMA and pre-SMA activity during Hayling task performance in individuals with high OCD traits and matched controls.

Methods

Forty right-handed native Spanish undergraduates (20 moderate-to-severe-OCD-trait, 20 controls), free of psychiatric diagnosis or psychotropic medication, were selected from an online screening of 963 students using the Yale–Brown Obsessive–Compulsive Scale. OCD and control groups were defined dimensionally according to Y-BOCS scores (24–31 vs 8–10) and matched for age and sex. 

During fMRI acquisition, participants performed an auditory version of the Hayling Sentence Completion Task. After each incomplete sentence (e.g., For the barbecue, he put the meat in the…), three visually presented response options (e.g., Grill / Plate / Valley) accompanied by a cue indicating task condition:  a green frame instructed participants to choose the most appropriate completion (automatic condition), whereas a red frame required selection of the least semantically related word (inhibition condition). Responses were made using a three-button response pad, with the right hand. 

Images were acquired on a 3T GE Signa scanner with a T2*-weighted EPI sequence (TR = 2000 ms, TE = 22 ms, 36 slices) and a high-resolution T1 scan. Preprocessing and statistical analyses were conducted in SPM12. A trial-wise GLM modelled automatic and inhibition events, and the resulting contrast images were then entered into a random-effects second-level analysis implementing a two-way mixed ANOVA. Region-of-interest analyses (using MarsBaR) focused on left SMA and left pre-SMA (figure 1), anatomically defined with the JuBrain Anatomy Toolbox, to test main effects of condition and group, as well as Group × Condition interactions.

Results

ROI analyses in the left SMA/pre-SMA complex revealed a robust main effect of condition in pre-SMA, with greater activation during inhibition than automatic trials (Inhibition > Automatic: t = 7.61, p_FWE < .001), whereas SMA proper showed no significant task effect (t = −1.20, p_FWE = .99). No main effects of group were observed in either ROI (all p_FWE > .08), although SMA tended to respond more strongly in controls than in the high-OCD-trait group (Control > OCD-trait: t = 1.74, p_FWE = .08). Crucially, a significant Group × Condition interaction emerged in pre-SMA (t = 2.56, p_FWE = .012), whereas no such interaction was observed in SMA (p_FWE = .64). This interaction reflected a reduced inhibition-related increase in BOLD signal (Inhibition − Automatic) within pre-SMA in individuals with high-OCD trait scores compared to controls, suggesting an altered modulation of this region during semantic inhibition in the Hayling task.

Conclusion

These findings support a functional dissociation within the SMA complex, with pre-SMA—but not SMA—showing robust engagement during semantic inhibition in the Hayling task. The reduced inhibition-related modulation of pre-SMA observed in participants with elevated OCD traits suggests a subtle inefficiency in recruiting this region to suppress prepotent verbal responses. This pattern points to pre-SMA dysfunction as a potential neural correlate of semantic inhibitory difficulties in OCD and highlights the utility of the Hayling task for probing non-motor aspects of inhibitory control in this population. Notably, whereas de Wit et al. reported greater pre-SMA activation in OCD patients (and their unaffected siblings) than in controls during motor response inhibition,^1 we observed the opposite pattern for semantic inhibition, suggesting that pre-SMA abnormalities in OCD may depend on the specific domain and demands of the inhibitory task (motor vs. semantic).
