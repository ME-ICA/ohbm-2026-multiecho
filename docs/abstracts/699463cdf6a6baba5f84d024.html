Introduction

1. Introduction

Students frequently carry robust alternative conceptions from childhood into higher education, making science learning particularly difficult and resistant to traditional instruction. Many scientific ideas are counterintuitive, abstract, or situated at higher hierarchical levels that require the integration of multiple subordinate concepts (She, 2002; She & Liao, 2010; Shih, et al., 2025). Consequently, conceptual change is a central challenge in science education, requiring learners to transform naïve conceptions into coherent, scientifically accepted knowledge. According to the Dual Situated Learning Model (DSLM), meaningful conceptual change occurs when cognitive conflict is deliberately induced and supported by the provision of new mental sets that reorganize relevant ideas into coherent explanatory frameworks (She, 2002, 2004; She et al., 2025).  

Although the behavioral mechanisms of conceptual change are well documented, the underlying brain mechanisms remain largely unexplored. Prior work suggests that prefrontal regions (MFG, SFG, IFG) support executive control, conflict monitoring, and higher-order reasoning, while the hippocampus underpin episodic/semantic memory integration and contextual reinstatement (Briggs et al., 2021; Duff et al., 2020; She, et al., 2023). Temporal regions such as the superior temporal gyrus and middle temporal gyrus subserve semantic processing and link new learning with prior knowledge (Gagnepain et al., 2017).

To address this gap, the present fMRI study examined whether prefrontal, hippocampal, and temporal regions support scientific conceptual change under low- versus high-difficulty conditions. Specifically, we investigated how task difficulty and conceptual change success (correct vs. incorrect explanations) differentially engage BOLD activation and modulate functional connectivity among these regions across the four DSLM-aligned stages of learning: prediction, experiment-video viewing, thinking, and response.

Methods

2. Methods

Forty-eight undergraduate and graduate science and engineering majors (32 men, 16 women; mean age = 21.5 ± 1.49 years) participated. All reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no neurological or psychiatric history. All procedures were approved by the Research Ethics Committee of National Chiao Tung University (NCTU-REC-108-124E); written informed consent was obtained and participants were compensated.

A panel of 10 experts in science and science education developed 39 scientific conceptual change items. Task difficulty was defined by the number of new mental sets required to construct a more scientific understanding (She, 2002, 2004), yielding 20 low-difficulty and 19 high-difficulty items. Each item comprised four stages: (1) question and prediction (12 s), (2) experiment video viewing (12 s), (3) thinking (6 s), and (4) response (12 s), with jittered blanks (2–4 s) and a brief non-science baseline question. Tasks were administered in three runs of 13 items (≈12 min 46 s per run). Response was recorded using an MR-compatible audio system (Fig.1).

fMRI data were acquired with an event-related, long-trial design. Stage-wise general linear models contrasted (a) low vs. high task difficulty, (b) correct vs. incorrect predictions, and (c) correct vs. incorrect response. Significant clusters were identified at p < 0.05 (FWE-corrected). Peak coordinates are reported at p < 0.001 uncorrected (≥10 voxels), with FWE-surviving clusters marked by asterisks.

Results

3. Results

Behavioral outcomes. Prediction accuracy for low-difficulty items (70%) was substantially higher than for high-difficulty items (46%), consistent with expert difficulty classification. Explanation scores, indexing the completeness and accuracy of understanding, averaged 23.29 ± 6.16 (low) versus 14.36 ± 6.27 (high), indicating more successful conceptual change for low-difficulty tasks and reach significant difference level (F = 46.48, p < 0.001).

Whole-brain activation. Consistent with our hypotheses, the hippocampus (HIP) showed greater activation during the video viewing stage for low vs. high task difficulty (FWE p < 0.05). Two HIP clusters were also more active for correct vs. incorrect predictions during video viewing (FWE p < 0.01), and an additional HIP cluster emerged during the prediction stage for correct vs. incorrect response (FWE p < 0.001). Prefrontal regions exhibited stage-specific effects: during video viewing, two middle frontal gyrus (MFG) clusters differentiated correct vs. incorrect predictions (FWE p < 0.05). During prediction, two superior medial frontal gyrus (SMFG) clusters (FWE p < 0.01 and p < 0.001) and one superior frontal gyrus (SFG) cluster (FWE p < 0.05) showed greater activation for correct vs. incorrect response. During response, one inferior frontal gyrus (IFG) cluster was more active for correct vs. incorrect explanations (FWE p < 0.05).

In addition, the superior temporal gyrus (STG) exhibited greater activation for correct vs. incorrect response in the prediction stage (FWE p < 0.01), with two clusters in the video stage (FWE p < 0.001) and one cluster in the response stage (FWE p < 0.01). Under low task difficulty, STG also showed increased activation for correct vs. incorrect response, including one video-stage cluster (FWE p < 0.01). The middle temporal gyrus (MTG) was significantly activated in the prediction stage when contrasting high vs. low task difficulty (FWE p < 0.01).

Functional connectivity. Functional connectivity analyses revealed that during correct explanations—reflecting successful conceptual change—there was robust connectivity among the prefrontal, hippocampal, and temporal regions (Fig. 2). In contrast, incorrect explanations were associated with markedly weaker connectivity across these same areas. These results indicate that stronger, more coordinated interactions among these regions are essential for successful conceptual change, compared with instances in which conceptual change fails (Fig. 2).

Conclusion

4. Conclusion

This study demonstrates that scientific conceptual change engages a distributed neural network spanning prefrontal control regions, the hippocampus, and temporal semantic areas. Greater hippocampal activation under low task difficulty and correct explanations (successful conceptual change) suggests that the hippocampus plays a key role in integrating new mental sets with prior knowledge and updating conceptual associations. Stage-dependent recruitment of the MFG, SMFG, SFG, and IFG indicates that prefrontal regions support conflict monitoring, strategic control, and higher-order reasoning across prediction, evidence evaluation, and explanation. Moreover, heightened STG and MTG activation during successful conceptual change underscores the importance of temporal semantic processing in stabilizing revised scientific conceptions.

The functional connectivity results indicate that stronger prefrontal–hippocampal coupling, along with enhanced interactions between these regions and the temporal cortex, underpins successful scientific conceptual change compared with unsuccessful conceptual change. By linking DSLM instructional stages to stage-specific neural activation patterns, this study advances a neurocognitive account of how the prefrontal, hippocampal, and temporal regions work together to support both weak and radical restructuring of scientific concepts.
