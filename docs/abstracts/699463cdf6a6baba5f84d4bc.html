Introduction

As the human cortex develops, distinct laminar organization emerges, with myelin and iron levels varying across layers and regions to support neural communication, metabolism, and specialized functions. At birth, myelin and iron levels are low, but both rise substantially with age. Flechsig proposed that cortical regions that myelinate earlier undergo faster functional maturation^1. Hallgren and Sourander showed regional difference in the rate of iron accumulation, with rapid increase in the first two decades followed by slower growth^2. These findings imply that myelination and iron accumulation follow different time courses. While myelin builds up within the first years of life, iron accumulation lasts decades. 

Yet, these foundational works were based on post-mortem qualitative evaluations, in limited number of brain regions. Therefore, the differential time courses of myelin and iron have not been evaluated quantitatively across the entire brain and across cortical layers.

Multiparametric mapping (MPM) of quantitative MRI (qMRI) enables non-invasive microstructural measurements^3. The longitudinal relaxation (R1) is mostly sensitive to myelin, while the effective transverse relaxation (R2*) is associated with myelination and tissue iron content. The macromolecular tissue volume (MTV) reflects the non-water tissue content and serves as an additional myelin-related marker^4. In adults, laminar organization has been probed by sampling MPM values from deep white matter to the pial surface. However, such laminar, multiparametric characterization has not been applied to infants or directly compared with adults. Thus, the rates of myelination and iron accumulation in infancy and their relation to adult values across cortical depths and functional systems remain largely unexplored. 

Here, we compare infant and adult laminar MPM profiles. We identify depth-dependent microstructural changes and distinct myelin and iron trajectories across cortical layers and regions.

Methods

Healthy infants (N=15, 9-16 months old) were scanned on a Siemens 3T Prisma MRI scanner during natural sleep (1 mm isotropic resolution). Adult participants (N=10, 23-38 years old) were scanned with the same scanner and protocol.

 MRI data were acquired using an MPM protocol, which included 3D multi-echo gradient-echo (mGRE) scans with T1- (FA=21°), PD- (FA=6°), and MT-weighting (FA=6°). All scans used TR=24 ms, eight equidistant echoes (TE=2.4–18.4 ms), reduced to five echoes for the MT-weighted acquisition, a bandwidth of 488 Hz/Px, a 256-mm FOV, and 2×2 CAIPI acceleration. For B1+ mapping, we acquired SESTE images. MP2RAGE data were collected as well (flip angles=4°,5°, inversion times=700,2500 ms).

R2*, magnetization transfer saturation (MTsat), and R1 maps were computed from the mGRE scans using the hMRI toolbox^3. Additional R1 maps were estimated from the MP2RAGE scans, which were registered to the mGRE space and corrected for B1+ inhomogeneities. 

Proton density (PD) maps were obtained with the mrQ toolbox^4. PD was subsequently calibrated to water content (WC) by normalizing PD values to those in the ventricles, assumed to contain 100% water. Ventricular calibration excluded the choroid plexus using k-means clustering. This was particularly important in infants, where the choroid plexus occupies a larger proportion of ventricular volume. MTV, representing the non-water fraction, was then calculated as 100% – WC. In the present analysis, we report results for R2*, MTV and MP2RAGE-derived R1 (Fig. 1a). 

Variable flip-angle R1 and MTsat maps showed comparable developmental patterns and are not reported here. Cortical surfaces were reconstructed in FreeSurfer using the MP2RAGE contrast (Fig. 1b).

Age-related trajectories of R1 and R2* (R1,2*) were modeled using an exponential growth function, which captures an increase that gradually plateaus with age^2:

R1,2* = a ∗ (1 - e -age/τ)  + c 

The parameter c represents the value at birth, a captures lifespan change, and a + c reflects the asymptotic level in advanced age. The parameter τ denotes the characteristic time constant describing how rapidly the curve approaches this plateau. Larger values of τ correspond to slower accumulation rates.

Model fitting was performed using a Bayesian nonlinear regression framework, estimating the posterior distributions of the model parameters. Priors were defined for each parameter based on physiological constraints: prior distribution of τ was modeled with a truncated normal distribution (10 ± 10 years, constrained to 0–40). Parameters a and c were assigned flat priors within ranges of 0–3 [1/sec] for R1 and 0–50 [1/sec] for R2*. Markov Chain Monte Carlo sampling (PyMC) was used to obtain full posterior distributions and predictive intervals. 

We fitted the model across participants for 34 left-hemisphere cortical regions covering the entire hemisphere. For each ROI, model fitting was carried out independently at six depth levels: deep white matter (DWM; 140% of cortical depth into WM), superficial white matter (SWM; 40% into WM), the cortical boundary between gray and white matter (WM-GM, 0%), superficial cortex (SCTX, 20% of cortical depth), mid-cortical layer (Mid-CTX, 50) and deep cortex (DCTX, 80%). Fitting the exponential growth model showed that it captured the age-related trajectories well (median RMSE=2.5% across all ROIs, depths and qMRI parameters).

Post-mortem measurements (Fig. 1c) from the literature were used for validation: tissue water content (scaled to tissue content) and cholesterol measurements in the forebrain were taken from Dobbing et al. (131 humans, 10 weeks–7 years plus adult averages)^5. We fitted an exponential model with the plateau (a+c) fixed to adult values and compared these results to MTV and R1 in whole white matter. For iron, we used prefrontal cortex values from Hallgren & Sourander (98 humans, 0–100 years)^2, whose fitted exponential parameters we compared to R2* in the whole cortex.

Results

We acquired qMRI maps for infants and adults (Fig. 1a) to assess lifespan trajectories of myelin and iron across cortical depths and regions. First, we validated that our metrics captures expected whole brain distributions, laminar patterns and known developmental trends.

We evaluated the feasibility of laminar analysis by sampling qMRI values across cortical depths, SWM and DWM (Fig. 1b). In adults, depth profiles showed the expected pattern, with higher R1 and R2* values in white matter than in cortex, in line with known stronger myelination in the white matter. Infants’ profiles differed markedly from adults, with large effect sizes (Cohen’s d > 2), reflecting known higher myelination and iron content of the adult brain. Therefore, our MPM framework robustly captures laminar differences and developmental changes in cortical microstructure.

Next, we evaluated whether our multiparametric lifespan dataset captures the known differential developmental dynamics of myelin, iron, and tissue content (Fig. 1c). To do this, we compared exponential models fitted to our qMRI parameters with post-mortem histological measures of cholesterol, iron, and non-water content reported in the literature^2,5. Importantly, the time-constant of the exponential model has units of time, regardless of the modeled parameter, enabling direct comparison across measurements with different physical units. Histology showed that the time constant for non-water content, i.e. tissue fraction, was the shortest (1.4 years), indicating rapid early increases. Cholesterol, reflecting lipid-rich myelin content, also exhibited a short time constant (2.1 years). In contrast, cortical iron showed a much longer time constant (13.2 years), consistent with its slow accumulation across development. We then examined whether MTV, R1, and R2* replicate these distinct temporal dynamics. Non-water content measured with MRI (MTV) has comparable units to its histological counterpart (% tissue fraction), and overlaying the two revealed strong agreement, with a rapid rise and similar plateau value. Likewise, R1 closely matched the cholesterol trajectory, both demonstrating fast increases, whereas R2* paralleled cortical iron, showing a slower rise. 

Together, these results indicate that our dataset, despite a modest sample size, reliably captures biologically meaningful laminar patterns and distinct developmental rates of myelin, tissue content, and iron. 

We next leveraged our dataset to examine whether developmental rates of myelin and iron vary across cortical layers and regions, and whether the slow versus fast dynamics of iron and myelin are global or tissue-specific. We focused on R1 and R2*, for which Bayesian model fitting was more robust (Fig. 2a). Developmental time constants varied across cortical depths and between superficial and deep white matter (Fig. 2b), with the shortest observed in superficial white matter. Within the cortex, we found an increase of the R1 time constant with cortical depth (69% of posterior in the DCTX are larger than SCTX). R2* consistently exhibited longer time constants than R1, by an average of 4.6 years (73% of posterior samples across ROIs and depths) (Fig. 2c). Comparing time constants with plateau values across cortical ROIs revealed that, for both R1 and R2*, regions with longer time constants also reached higher plateau values (Fig. 2d).

Spatial analysis showed that time constants for R1 and R2* varied across cortical regions and depths, with distinct patterns for each parameter (Fig. 2e). Precentral cortices exhibited longer time constants, whereas frontal, occipital and postcentral somatosensory areas matured more rapidly. Finally, correlations of time constants between R1 and R2* across ROIs were low (median R²=0.13 across all posterior distributions). The largest differences were observed in frontal, temporal, and parietal cortices, highlighting parameter-specific microstructural developmental patterns. 

Conclusion

Pioneering post-mortem studies suggested rapid myelination and slow iron accumulation across the lifespan. However, these findings were limited in scope: myelin studies rarely included adult comparisons, while iron studies were restricted to a few brain regions. Critically, previous work did not allow direct comparison of myelin and iron temporal dynamics across cortical layers and regions, leaving a significant gap in our understanding of the coordinated maturation of these two fundamental tissue components.

Here, we use in vivo multiparametric qMRI to characterize, for the first time, distinct maturational patterns of myelin and iron across the cortex and at different laminar depths. Using an exponential growth model, whose time-constant parameter is expressed in time units, we could directly compare developmental rates across parameters with different units. This approach allowed us to quantitatively assess whether the fast dynamics of myelin and the slow dynamics of iron are global or vary across cortical layers and regions.

We first demonstrate the feasibility of multiparametric mapping and depth-wise profiling in the infant cortex, revealing clear laminar differences compared to adults. Despite a limited sample size, our dataset reliably reproduces known histological developmental patterns, with early-life developmental changes in myelination captured by R1 and MTV and slower iron accumulation across lifespan captured by R2*. As MTV measures from MRI and from histology provide comparable values, we could compare model parameters beyond the estimated time constant. The agreement of both the time constant and the plateau values across modalities confirms that our MRI-based models capture the full lifespan profile.

To address uncertainties arising from the modest sample size, we adopted a Bayesian nonlinear regression framework. This approach enabled estimation of posterior distributions for the fitted parameters, providing an assessment of parameter variability and confidence. Our analyses showed that R2* consistently exhibits longer time constants than R1, confirming slower iron accumulation relative to myelination. Interestingly, time constants varied with cortical depth: the shortest values were observed in the SWM, indicating rapid myelination and iron accumulation. For R1, time constants increased progressively toward deeper cortical layers, reflecting an inside-out developmental pattern in which deeper cortex matures over a longer period than superficial layers. 

Across regions, longer developmental rates correlate with higher asymptotic values, suggesting that regions developing over extended periods reach higher final levels of myelin or iron. The spatial distributions of R1 and R2* time constants showed low correlation, implying for largely independent dynamics of myelin- and iron-related development across the brain.

Several limitations should be noted. Our study relied on a relatively small sample with a limited age range, and the 1-mm acquisition resolution introduces sensitivity to partial volume effects. R1 and R2* were used as proxies for myelin and iron, but both metrics are influenced by multiple tissue properties. The time constant estimates for R1 are shorter than R2*, but are still longer compared to the time course of developmental myelination previously reported, thus reflecting potential contribution from iron accumulation. Importantly, we replicated R1 findings with MTsat, a myelin-sensitive qMRI parameter, confirming the robustness of our myelin-related findings (data not shown). The multiparametric nature of our dataset offers potential to further disentangle myelin and iron trajectories.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates laminar- and region-specific developmental trajectories of myelin and iron captured in-vivo with qMRI. Our approach lays the foundation for future studies to examine the interplay between microstructural maturation and functional specialization across the human cortex.
