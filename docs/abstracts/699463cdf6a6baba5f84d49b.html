Introduction

Human cognition reflects a balance between stable traits and rapid, moment-to-moment fluctuations, which together shape behavioural performance. From a neuroimaging perspective, fMRI studies have linked temporal variability in both BOLD activity and network-level interactions to transient and more gradual changes in task performance.1,2 However, fMRI is temporally constrained by the haemodynamic response and by the averaging required to obtain reliable network-level estimates. EEG and MEG offer millisecond-level resolution to capture fast neural dynamics. Time-domain analyses, including event-related potentials and microstate dynamics, reveal rapid fluctuations in task-related cognitive processing.3,4 Complementing temporally resolved activity, neural oscillations and rhythmic patterns across canonical frequency bands further underpin cognitive function.5 Yet, most studies on the time evolution of cognitive processes focus on either temporal or spectral features in isolation, rather than their joint contribution.

Methods

In this study, we examined whether network dynamics, jointly resolved in time and frequency, capture how cognitive processes unfold over multiple timescales. Healthy adults (N=37) completed a computerised version of the Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT) consisting of 128 trials, while whole-head MEG was recorded. We modelled the resulting MEG data using the time-delay embedded Dynamic Network Modes (TDE-DyNeMo) model, which expresses brain activity as a smoothly time-varying mixture of spatially defined networks, evolving at millisecond resolution and grounded in both spectral power and functional connectivity. To ensure reproducibility, we performed 30 DyNeMo runs (three sets of 10) using the same hyperparameters, and verified that both network spatial maps (via Pearson correlation) and time courses (via percentage of variance explained) were highly consistent across the run with the lowest final training loss in each set. We next applied two post hoc analyses at the group level to characterise slow session-wide trends and trial-level fluctuations in network engagement. First, slow trends were extracted by computing a summary metric (e.g., mean network activation) within consecutive, task-informed DyNeMo segments and assessing its evolution throughout the paradigm. Second, trial-level fluctuations were analysed by relating (via Pearson correlation) network activity during each trial to its reaction time (RT), enabling the identification of networks whose engagement co-varied with trial-by-trial behavioural performance.

Results

Behaviourally, reaction times (RTs) decreased significantly over the course of the task, with 87% of participants showing negative linear slopes (p = 9 x 10-6, one-sample t-test). Slow trends analysis (Figure 1a) revealed a gradual decrease in engagement of the task-relevant frontoparietal network (p = 0.006, linear fit across segments) and a concurrent, smaller increase in a non-task-relevant left temporo-parietal network (p = 0.03). At the trial-level, group-level DyNeMo activity showed significant associations with RTs in the two task-relevant cognitive networks (Figure 1b). Specifically, higher prefrontal engagement predicted faster responses (r = −0.33, p = 8 x 10-4), with the opposite association for the frontoparietal network (r = 0.31, p = 1 x 10-3).

Conclusion

Our results demonstrate that prefrontal and frontoparietal networks exhibit distinct temporal dynamics during cognitive processing, with slow session-wide trends and trial-level fluctuations relating to behaviour. The applied TDE-DyNeMo framework, which jointly models temporal and spectral properties, also enables a post hoc spatio-spectral characterisation of these networks, revealing increased theta-band power (prefrontal) and broadband oscillatory suppression (frontoparietal). This provides additional insight into their functional engagement over time compared to previous studies that considered temporal or spectral features separately. In the future, this framework could support more fine-grained temporal investigations of fluctuations in large-scale network activity.
