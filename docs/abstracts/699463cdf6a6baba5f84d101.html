Introduction

Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) probes causal structure–function links by inducing E-fields in the cortex and observing elicited effects, such as  motor evoked potentials (MEPs). Conventional motor mapping identifies  hotspots on the scalp from MEP amplitudes; recent “E-field informed’’ mapping instead regresses voxel-wise E-fields against MEPs to obtain spatially precise cortical muscle maps.^1 Current protocols typically sample coil positions/orientations randomly, yielding many near-duplicate E-fields in the region of interest (ROI). Repeating similar patterns wastes pulses, reduces information gain, and prolongs sessions^2—undesirable given subject fatigue, clinical time limits, and repositioning demands. These constraints motivate actively designing stimulation sequences that maximise information about the E-field–MEP mapping. 

We aim to identify coil placements whose induced E-fields are maximally linearly independent within the ROI, thereby ensuring that each stimulation explores a genuinely new portion of the E-field manifold. This perspective aligns naturally with long-standing ideas in Optimal Experimental Design: when measurements are costly, one aims to select stimuli that most effectively reduce uncertainty in a high-dimensional parameter space.^3^4 Determinant-maximising criteria serve this purpose well: they enlarge the volume spanned by the sampled E-fields and, in turn, sharpen the confidence bounds in the voxel-wise regression. To further disentangle subtle pattern differences that low-rank structure tends to obscure, we also examine Gaussian-kernel embeddings as an alternative similarity measure. In combination, these strategies yield a coherent determinant-based framework for designing TMS stimulation sequences and reveal the regimes under which prospective selection substantially outperforms random sampling.

Methods

We constructed subject-specific finite-element head models and simulated a dense library of TMS-induced E-fields using realistic conductivities (437 scalp positions at 2.5 mm spacing x 12 tangential orientations separated by 15 degrees). For each subject, we defined a cortical ROI around the primary motor cortex and sampled the magnitude of the E-field at V vertices on the cortical surface. For each candidate coil position and orientation c = 1, ..., N, this yielded an E-field vector ec in RV. Stacking all candidates results in an E-field matrix: E in RN x V, where each row corresponds to one stimulation placement and each column corresponds to one cortical location in the ROI. This matrix compactly encodes the forward mapping from discrete coil settings to spatial E-field patterns.

Our design objective is to choose a subset of k rows of E that will actually be used in the experiment. Conceptually, this is a standard design-of-experiments problem: each selected placement is an "experiment" that contributes information to a voxel-wise E-field–MEP regression model. In linear regression, the covariance of the estimated parameters is inversely proportional to the information matrix XXT. D-optimal design maximises det(XXT), thereby minimising the volume of the confidence ellipsoid and yielding statistically efficient estimates. By treating the selected E-field patterns as the design matrix for our regression, we adopt the same principle: we seek subsets of rows ES whose Gram matrix ESEST has a large determinant. Equivalently, we explicitly favour stimulation sets that span a large volume in E-field space, i.e., that probe many linearly independent directions in the ROI, so that the E-field–MEP mapping is tightly constrained.

To approximate D-optimal subsets without combinatorial search which is an NP-hard problem, we use a greedy row-selection algorithm on E, which greedily maximises the volume of the selected submatrix and controls the condition number of the associated information matrix using QR decomposition. Operationally, we iteratively apply QR with pivoting to E and at each step select the row (coil placement) whose residual has the largest norm, i.e., the E-field pattern that is least explained by the span of previously chosen patterns. So, each newly selected row adds a nearly orthogonal direction in the relevant feature space, expanding the spanned volume and improving reconstruction or inference quality. In our context, this ensures that every additional pulse contributes genuinely new information rather than re-sampling already well-characterised E-field directions.

At the same time, the linear D-optimal formulation inherits an important structural limitation from the E-field matrix itself. Because the coil can only move within a restricted workspace and the induced fields are governed by Maxwell’s equations and tissue conductivities, E is typically very low rank: after a moderate number of selections, many candidate E-fields lie close to the span of those already chosen. Beyond this point, further rows add redundancy rather than information.

To better handle this low-rank structure and to resolve finer differences between candidate E-fields, we additionally consider a kernelised extension of the same determinant-based design principle. Instead of working with raw inner products <ei, ej>, we define a Radial Basis Function (RBF, or Gaussian) kernel based similarity: Kij = exp( - ||ei - ej||2 / (2τ)) , while τ is typically set using the median of pairwise squared distances. The kernel implicitly maps each E-field pattern into a high-dimensional feature space in which subtle spatial differences become more pronounced. We again maximise det(.), but now with respect to kernel-based similarities. In practice, this encourages coil placements that are well separated under the RBF metric and can alleviate degeneracies caused by the low rank of E.

Results

Across subjects, the determinant-based greedy strategy yielded E-field sets that were markedly less redundant than those obtained by random sampling when evaluated on the selected E-field submatrix. At each greedy iteration, we tracked two diagnostics: mean absolute row-correlation (stimulus-wise redundancy) and mean absolute column-correlation (voxel-wise separability), indexing the diversity of successive stimulations and the distinguishability of ROI vertices under the induced fields. Relative to random selection, the determinant-based procedure drove down both measures early in the sequence. Together, these reductions indicate that early selections are both more diverse and more informative for discriminating cortical locations.

This improvement, however, was not unbounded. With the original (linear) algorithm, column-correlation continued to decrease only over an initial regime and typically ceased improving after roughly 30 selected stimulations. Beyond this point, it often rose again, in some cases surpassing the level observed under random sampling. Importantly, this inflection coincided with the numerical effective rank of the full E-field matrix, which lay between 26 and 30 across the four subjects. This alignment suggests that once the dominant linear subspace of feasible E-fields is saturated, further stimulations fail to add independent directions and may reintroduce voxel-level redundancy.

Introducing an RBF kernel to define similarity between E-field patterns altered this trajectory. The kernelised design attenuated the tendency of column-correlation to plateau and rebound in the low-rank regime. This behaviour is consistent with the kernel operating in an implicitly higher-dimensional feature space, preserving separability even as the linear representation approaches rank deficiency. The gain, however, was not without cost. Compared with the linear determinant criterion, the RBF variant generally achieved a smaller reduction in stimulus-wise redundancy, reflecting its emphasis on dispersion under a pattern-similarity geometry rather than strict linear (cosine or inner-product) orthogonality.

Then, we conducted virtual mapping sessions using four individual cortical templates, three hotspot locations per subject (crown, wall, fundus), and ten independent MEP realizations per target. Each session unfolded stimulus by stimulus: following every virtual pulse, the voxel-wise regression pipeline updated both the cortical map and the hotspot estimate. Performance was quantified by two metrics: (i) the geodesic distance (GD) between the estimated and true hotspot on the cortical surface, and (ii) the normalized root mean square deviation (NRMSD) between the recovered cortical map and a one-hot reference (hotspot = 1, all other vertices = 0), capturing how rapidly a focal representation emerges.

Determinant-based sequences converged more rapidly than random sampling for both GD and NRMSD. The early portions of the NRMSD curves were especially steep, underscoring that informed selection accelerates recovery of the overall map shape, not merely the peak location. We quantified convergence speed via the area under the curve (AUC) over the first 50 stimulations, before near-saturation. When pooling across all targets, the linear determinant design consistently achieved lower AUCs and, in most cases, reduced variability across MEP realizations relative to random sampling. The RBF kernel variant further improved convergence, yielding the lowest AUCs—most prominently for NRMSD—and eliminating the late-stage NRMSD “rebound” occasionally observed with the linear design. This stabilization mirrors the matrix-level diagnostics, with the RBF kernel mitigating low-rank-induced loss of voxel-wise separability and preserving map-shape recovery as sequences grow. The same qualitative ordering held when analyses were stratified by anatomical target, indicating that the benefit is not restricted to a single folding context.

Conclusion

This work advances a principled framework for the prospective design of TMS coil sequences in E-field–informed motor mapping. The practical impetus is straightforward yet consequential: within a discretised coil-pose space, uninformed exploration tends to generate highly correlated E-field patterns within the ROI. Such redundancy inflates the number of pulses required to stabilise voxel-wise E-field–MEP estimates. By contrast, the determinant-based selection criterion draws directly on classical results from regression design. By favouring subsets of E-field patterns that span a large volume in feature space, it explicitly promotes stimulations that are maximally informative and that reduce uncertainty in voxel-wise models.

Across four subject-specific templates, three distinct targets (crown, wall, fundus), and independent MEP realizations, determinant-based sequences outperformed random sampling in both hotspot localisation (geodesic distance) and cortical-map recovery (NRMSD relative to a focal one-hot reference). Crucially, this behavioural advantage is mirrored by mechanistic signatures in the selected E-field submatrices themselves. Early in the sequence, determinant-based selection simultaneously suppresses stimulus-wise redundancy (mean absolute row-correlation) and enhances voxel-wise separability (mean absolute column-correlation).

A central insight emerging from these analyses is that voxel-wise separability has an intrinsic ceiling. Under a linear kernel, column-correlation often stops improving after approximately 30 selections and may increase thereafter, closely tracking the numerical effective rank of the full E-field matrix (26–30 across subjects). This observation grounds a concrete failure mode of mapping performance in a biophysically induced low-rank constraint: once the dominant linear subspace of feasible E-fields is saturated, further selections can reintroduce voxel-level dependencies and manifest as late-stage degradation in map-shape recovery, such as the observed NRMSD rebound.

Extending the framework with a Gaussian (RBF) kernel alleviates this saturation. By redefining diversity in terms of E-field pattern similarity within an implicitly high-dimensional feature space, the kernelised design reduces the tendency for voxel-wise separability to stall or degrade. In practice, this translates into faster convergence and lower AUCs—most clearly for NRMSD—and prevents the late-stage rebound characteristic of the linear criterion. The accompanying trade-off is interpretable: relative to the linear determinant design, the RBF variant generally produces a smaller gain in stimulus-wise decorrelation, reflecting its optimisation of spread under a similarity-based geometry rather than strict linear orthogonality.

Several limitations are still held. All results are derived from replay simulations under a simplified generative assumption in which MEPs are driven predominantly by the E-field at a single hotspot via a sigmoidal input–output relationship; real motor representations may be spatially extended or multi-focal. In addition, the chosen NRMSD metric emphasises recovery of a focal map and may underweight more distributed representations.

Taken together, determinant-based sequence design offers a statistically transparent and practically implementable route to improving sample efficiency, complementing recent geometric dispersion approaches such as farthest point sampling^5, which emphasizes spatial coverage in E-field pattern space, it does not explicitly optimize statistical efficiency. At the same time, our work exposes how low-rank E-field structure constrains voxel-wise identifiability and motivates kernelised extensions. Future work will test these ideas on empirical TMS datasets, explore richer generative and fitting models for distributed motor representations, and incorporate operational constraints—such as coil movement costs and session time budgets—into prospective sequence optimisation.
