Introduction

Social attachment represents an essential evolutionary feature to promote survival and well-being.^1 However, comprehensive brain models of social attachment in humans are lacking, and it remains unclear whether social attachment and other rewarding experiences involve similar mechanisms.

Methods

Across eight functional magnetic resonance imaging studies (n = 420), we used predictive modelling to identify neurofunctional representations of social attachment with romantic partners and friends in real life and generalized the neural signature towards the digital world (i.e. social media), as well as evaluated its specificity towards non-social rewarding experiences including taste reward and monetary reward.

Results

We found that recall of positive autobiographical romantic experiences with partners and pleasant experiences with friends engaged the ventral striatum and medial prefrontal reward hub (p < 0.05, FWE corrected). Multivariate predictive analyses established an accurate whole-brain signature of romantic love significantly predicted reward processing on social media (Accuracy = 80%, p < 0.001), yet not to pleasant tastes processing. Social attachment was largely encoded in medial prefrontal cortex and ventral striatum, while taste reward and monetary reward were encoded mainly in dorsal striatum. All brain models predicted positive affect rather than general arousal in independent data.^2 Importantly, the brain model for romantic love accurately predicted (a) neurofunctional drug cue reactivity in cannabis users but not in non-using controls (Accuracy = 77%, p < 0.001), indicating an overlapping neural basis between drug addiction and love, and (b) higher reactivity towards the picture of the romantic partner following oxytocin but not placebo administration (Accuracy = 80%, p < 0.001), indicating a specific role of oxytocin in partner bonding.

Conclusion

Together, these findings offer an integrated account of how social attachment and non-social rewarding experiences are constructed in the brain.
