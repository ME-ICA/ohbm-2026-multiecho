Introduction

Attractor dynamics are a hallmark of complex systems, including the brain (Khona & Fiete, 2022; Englert et al. 2025). Free energy minimizing attractor networks provide a formal route to understand how Bayesian inference in complex multi- and metastable networks emerges from the first principles of self-organization (Spisak & Friston, 2025). In this framework, stable attractor states can be interpreted as learned priors, while ongoing activity implements Bayesian belief updating via local message passing and stochastic sampling dynamics. A prediction of the ensuing framework is “self-orthogonalization”: even when exposed to correlated sensory input, the network’s attractors tend to become approximately orthogonal (approaching a so-called Kanter–Sompolinsky projector-network organization in which attractors align with the eigenmodes of the coupling matrix). Self-orthogonalization effectively disentangles the latent causes of observable sensory input. This behaviour is an emergent property of predictive coding-based, free energy minimizing plasticity, which furnishes a characteristic “signature” that can be quantified in empirical brain networks. At the macroscale, resting-state fMRI offers two metrics needed to test these predictions: (i) population-level dynamics or flow and (ii) functional connectivity as an empirical proxy for coupling relevant to activity flow. We translate the free-energy attractor-network formalism to whole-brain fMRI by treating the resting-state functional connectome as the coupling matrix of a functional connectivity–based attractor neural network (fcANN). Here, we test two theory-derived signatures in empirical data: (1) eigenmode–attractor alignment, and (2) approximate attractor orthogonality. Finally, we discuss the neuroscientific relevance of the spatial patterns of the ensuing attractor states and provide a data-driven illustration of their embedding in a low dimensional energy landscape manifold.

Methods

Theory 

The derivation of our framework (Spisak & Friston, 2025) starts from a general formulation of random dynamical systems and a universal partitioning based upon causal influences and ensuing conditional dependencies. This separates internal (µ) from external (η) states via the ”Markov blanket” comprising sensory (s) and active (a) states, so that: η ⊥µ | s, a. Maintaining this partition - for extended time periods - requires internal states to infer external causes via free energy minimization: µ ∝−∇µF. It can be shown that, if the internal state µ comprises nested interacting elements or “particles” (where one particle’s internal state σi can be another’s external state), the system’s dynamics can give rise to complex attractor network structures. When parametrizing network elements with a Continuous Bernoulli kernel, free energy minimization - from the point of view of a single node σi, given all other nodes σ\i - yields a stochastic generalized variant of the standard Hopfield update rule Eq[σi] = L(bi + ∑ j̸=i Jijσ j), where L is the Langevin function and Jij is the coupling strength (Spisak & Friston, 2025). 

Being a generalized case of a Boltzmann machine, the network implements Markov Chain Monte Carlo sampling from the global posterior distribution P(σ|s, J), given the prior represented by attractors and sensory input s. This reveals a recursive structure: local Bayesian inference at the node level gives rise to collective macro-scale Bayesian inference at the network level (which itself is also a VFE-minimizing system): c.f., (Palacios et al., 2020; Thestrup Waade et al., 2025). This allows multiple levels of description: an important aspect of scale-invariant brain dynamics with “coarse-grained” measurements (like fMRI): see (Friston et al., 2021) for further discussion. Minimizing VFE with respect to couplings Jij yields a learning rule that contrasts observed vs. predicted correlations: ∆Jij ∝ σiσ j−L(bi +∑k̸=i Jik σk)σj. It can be shown both analytically and numerically (Fig. 1D, (Spisak & Friston, 2025)) that this learning rule intrinsically drives attractor states towards orthogonality, which optimizes mutual information, compression, memory capacity and computational robustness: e.g., (Kanter & Sompolinsky, 1987; Friston et al., 2024; Schmidhuber, 2010; Wallace and Dowe, 1999).

A Model for Large-Scale Brain Dynamics

We adopted the above framework as a model for large-scale brain dynamics as measured by fMRI (fcANN), with the nodes σi representing activity in brain regions and coupling weights J reconstructed as the negative inverse covariance matrix of the activation timeseries (Englert et al., 2025, Fig. 1 A-C). This corresponds to the undirected effective connectivity under symmetrical coupling; i.e., Jij = Jji, see (Friston et al., 2014) for details. Theory suggests that fMRI may provide sufficient time resolution to capture the slow processes associated with macro-scale computations (Friston et al., 2014). Indeed, there is robust empirical evidence in the literature that large-scale brain dynamics (”activity flow”) align with the derived inference rule (Cole, 2024). Further, there is initial evidence indicating that: connectome-based attractor networks exhibit efficient and rich attractor dynamics and attractor dynamics can capture perceptual states, like pain modulation (Englert et al., 2024).

Here, we analysed three resting-state cohorts (discovery n=41; replication n=48 and n=29) to map attractor states of the functional connectome and to assess attractor orthogonality conditions. We obtained regional timeseries with standard techniques, initialized an fcANN with functional connectivity (partial correlation) and ran 100,000 random network relaxations to enumerate attractors. Eigenmode–attractor alignment was tested against phase-randomized surrogate time series; convergence was contrasted to symmetry-preserving weight permutations.

Results

The resulting attractors were found to be approximately orthogonal, and the leading eigenvectors of the coupling matrix J were strongly aligned with attractors (EV1–EV6: r=0.85, 0.83, 0.82, 0.80, 0.68, 0.39; all p<0.001). Deterministic relaxation converged substantially faster for the empirical connectome than with an appropriate null model (median 383 vs 3543.5 iterations at beta=0.04, see Englert et al. 2025 for details), indicating that connectome topology supports an efficient descent to free-energy minima1. 

In terms of the spatial patterns of the attractors, we found that they closely resemble canonical intrinsic brain networks or functional connectivity modes: e.g., (Fox et al., 2005). The spatial patterns associated with first pair of attractors show a close correspondence to two commonly described complementary brain systems, that have been previously found in anatomical, functional, developmental, and evolutionary hierarchies, as well as gene expression, metabolism, and blood flow. A common interpretation of these two patterns is that they represent (i) an “intrinsic” system for higher-level internal context, commonly referred to as the default mode network and (ii) an anti-correlated “extrinsic” system linked to the immediate sensory environment, showing similarities to the recently described “action mode network” (Dosenbach et al., 2025). The second pair of attractors - spanning an approximately orthogonal axis - resemble patterns commonly associated with perception–action cycles (Fuster, 2004), and are commonly described as a gradient across sensorimotor modalities (Huntenburg et al., 2018), recruiting regions associated with active (e.g. motor cortices) and perceptual inference (e.g., visual areas).

1 In committing to a functional connectivity proxy, we precluded directed (i.e., asymmetric) coupling. This is a severe approximation because it precludes non-dissipative, solenoidal dynamics that breaks detailed balance, and underwrites nonequilibrium steady-states. In other words, we restrict ourselves to the dissipative components of self-organization: i.e.,  purely dissipative structures (Friston, 2019; Nicolis and Prigogine, 1977; Prigogine, 1978).

Conclusion

These results connect the formal theory of free-energy–minimizing neural networks to empirical whole-brain fMRI: a connectome-parameterized attractor network recovers stable attractors whose geometry matches key predictions of self-orthogonalizing (projector-like) architectures. Importantly, while the fcANN analysis used only resting-state statistics to set couplings, it still showed eigenmode-attractor alignment and near-orthogonality beyond stringent nulls, suggesting that macroscale functional organization is well described by an attractor basis that is constrained by connectivity. This view reframes canonical “resting-state networks” as the priors of a generative model and recurring substates as trajectories shaped by an underlying free-energy landscape. Future work can leverage this framework to test mechanistic hypotheses about how stimuli, plasticity, development, or pathology reshape the attractor basis.
