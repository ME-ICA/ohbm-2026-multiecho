Introduction

Accurate modeling of the hemodynamic response function (HRF) is central to the analysis of task-based fMRI data, as it links latent neural activity to the observed BOLD signal. Despite its importance, most pipelines rely on a limited set of temporal basis functions to model the HRF, which cannot always capture meaningful variability in HRF shape across subjects, brain regions, and experimental conditions. This misspecification can bias activation estimates, reduce statistical sensitivity, and limit the interpretability of neurophysiological differences.

Flexible alternatives such as finite impulse response (FIR) models allow data-driven HRF estimation, but typically trade flexibility for stability. In particular, subject-specific voxelwise estimation can yield high-variance HRF estimates, especially at low and moderate signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), and typically treats voxels as independent, non-spatial units. As a result, they may fail to leverage shared spatial structure across voxels and generally do not account for relationships between individual characteristics (e.g., age, sex, cognitive ability) and HRF shape.

To address these limitations, we propose a machine-learning framework for HRF estimation that combines demographic covariates with spatial regularization. HRFs are modeled as nonlinear functions of learned subject-level embeddings that incorporate individual covariates, allowing inter-individual variation in HRF to be captured explicitly. These subject embeddings are mapped to voxel- and condition-specific HRFs using a neural network, while spatial information is incorporated through Laplacian-based regularization that promotes spatial coherence while preserving voxel-level specificity. Together, this framework yields physiologically plausible,  interpretable HRF estimates that are robust at the single-subject level and capture meaningful subject- and condition-specific temporal variation in task-based fMRI data.

Methods

We propose a subject-specific machine-learning framework for estimating HRFs in task-based fMRI that integrates nonlinear modeling with spatial regularization to better capture variability in neurovascular coupling across individuals and brain regions. HRF estimation is embedded within a convolutional neural network (CNN) framework that models the forward relationship between task stimuli and the observed BOLD signal.

For subject i and voxel v, the observed BOLD time series yv,i is modeled as a sum of convolutions betwee condition-specific stimulus regressors and voxel-, subject-, and condition-specific HRFs, with temporally correlated noise: 

yv,i = ∑Nshv,i,s + εv,i,   εv,i ~ AR(p), 

where Ns denotes the convolution design matrix for condition s and hv,i,s represents the HRF associated with subject i, voxel v, and condition s. Within the CNN, this convolutional structure is treated as a differentiable forward model, allowing HRFs to be learned directly from the data via backpropagation. This formulation permits temporal features such as response onset, time-to-peak, width, and post-stimulus undershoot to vary across cortical locations, subjects, and experimental conditions.

To capture inter-individual differences in hemodynamic timing and morphology, HRFs are parameterized as nonlinear functions of subject-level embeddings. For each subject i, we construct a low-dimensional embedding gi that combines a trainable subject identifier with demographic and individual covariates, including categorical attributes (e.g., sex, handedness) and standardized continuous measures (e.g., age, body mass index). This embedding summarizes subject-specific physiological and anatomical characteristics that may influence vascular dynamics and BOLD response shape.

Voxel- and condition-specific HRFs are generated from the subject embedding via a neural network component of the CNN. For each voxel v and condition s, we define 

hv,i,s = fv,s(gi;θv,s) 

where fv,s is implemented as a multilayer perceptron with ReLU activation. This provides a flexible, nonparametric mapping from subject-level covariates to voxel- and condition-specific HRF shapes, enabling the model to learn nonlinear relationships between individual characteristics and hemodynamic responses while sharing information across voxels and conditions.

To stabilize voxelwise HRF estimation and reflect the spatial organization of cortical vasculature and functional architecture, we incorporate spatial regularization through a graph Laplacian constructed from voxelwise or surface-based adjacency. A Laplacian penalty promotes smooth variation of HRF shapes across neighboring voxels at the subject level, encouraging spatially coherent hemodynamic fields while preserving genuine regional differences in response timing and morphology.

Model parameters are estimated by minimizing a penalized mean squared error objective that combines data fidelity and spatial smoothness. The loss function is optimized jointly across subjects, voxels, and conditions, yielding spatially coherent and physiologically interpretable HRF estimates while preserving voxel- and condition-specific temporal features. Overall, this framework enables stable subject-level HRF estimation that captures meaningful nonlinear relationships between individual characteristics and neurovascular responses in task-based fMRI data.

Results

A. Simulation studies. 

We first evaluated the proposed CNN-based framework using simulations to assess HRF recovery and activation detection under spatially heterogeneous hemodynamics. A two-dimensional slice (50 X 50 voxels) was partitioned into four spatially contiguous regions, each assigned a distinct HRF ranging from canonical-like to strongly non-canonical shapes with delayed or early peaks and large undershoots. Smooth voxel-level variation in HRF timing and width was introduced within each region, and event-related stimulus sequences were generated for multiple task conditions. BOLD time series were simulated via convolution with HRFs and added AR(1) noise.

The proposed CNN-based model achieved the most accurate HRF recovery, yielding estimation error by 50-75% relative to the canonical GLM and by 20-30% compared with smooth FIR (sFIR), with the largest gains observed in regions with delayed peaks and strong undershoots. Improvements were strongest in regions exhibiting delayed or non-canonical HRFs, where canonical assumptions performed poorly. The CNN model consistently recovered smooth spatial fields of HRF features such as time-to-peak and width, closely matching ground truth patterns.

In terms of activation detection, the proposed method achieved 10-15% lower false negative rates than canonical HRF models and 25-40% lower false positive rates than sFIR, reflecting a more favorable bias-variance trade-off. Compared to Gaussian kernel smoothing baselines, the Laplacian-based regularization preserved sharper regional boundaries while maintaining spatial coherence, resulting in more stable activation maps and higher overall detection accuracy.

B. Application to HCP motor-task data.

We next applied the proposed method to task-based fMRI data from the Human Connectome Project Young Adult (HCP-YA) cohort, focusing on the Motor task, which includes cue, left and right hand, left and right foot, and tongue movement conditions. Voxel-wise HRFs were estimated for each subject and condition using our framework and compared against canonical HRF and sFIR-based approaches. To avoid confounding due to family structure, we restricted the analysis to 416 genetically unrelated subjects.

The proposed CNN-based model, both with and without spatial regularization, produced smooth and physiologically plausible HRF fields throughout the cortex, exhibiting clear spatial organization and substantially reducing voxel-wise noise compared to competing approaches. While the sFIR method yielded reasonable estimates of the average HRF shape, it showed greater variability across voxels, whereas the proposed framework achieved a more favorable bias–variance trade-off, resulting in markedly improved stability.

Estimated HRFs revealed systematic subject-level variability in both timing and morphology, as well as condition-specific temporal differences across motor regions that were attenuated or obscured under canonical HRF assumptions. Visualization of fitted mean HRFs across methods highlighted the stability of the CNN and CNN+Spatial models, while cortical surface maps of estimated activation amplitudes demonstrated that the CNN approach substantially reduced salt-and-pepper–like spatial noise. Incorporating spatial regularization further enhanced coherence across neighboring voxels without oversmoothing functionally distinct areas, yielding clear, interpretable activation patterns consistent with known motor system organization.

Overall, these results demonstrate that integrating nonlinear subject-specific HRF modeling with spatial regularization substantially improves HRF estimation accuracy, activation detection, and interpretability in both controlled simulations and real task-fMRI data.

Conclusion

Accurate estimation of the HRF is critical for valid and interpretable task-based fMRI analysis, yet remains challenging due to substantial variability across subjects, brain regions, and experimental conditions. In this work, we introduced a subject-specific machine-learning framework for HRF estimation that integrates nonlinear modeling with spatial regularization, addressing key limitations of canonical and voxelwise HRF estimation approaches.

By modeling HRFs as nonlinear functions of learned subject-level embeddings that incorporate demographic and individual covariates, the proposed framework captures systematic inter-individual variability in hemodynamic timing and shape that is not accessible to traditional models. The use of neural networks enables flexible, data-driven HRF estimation without restricting responses to a fixed parametric family, while shared subject embeddings allow information to be borrowed across voxels and conditions. Spatial regularization via a graph Laplacian further stabilizes estimation by enforcing coherence across neighboring voxels, reducing variance and suppressing physiologically implausible oscillations without oversmoothing genuine regional differences.

Simulation studies demonstrated that the proposed method substantially improves HRF recovery and activation detection, particularly in settings with heterogeneous and non-canonical HRF shapes. Compared to canonical GLM and sFIR, the spatially regularized framework achieved lower estimation error, improved sensitivity and specificity, and more stable spatial patterns of HRF features. Application to Human Connectome Project Young Adult motor-task data further showed that the method yields smooth, interpretable HRF fields across the cortex, reveals systematic subject-level variation, and captures condition-specific temporal differences that are attenuated or obscured under canonical assumptions.

Together, these results highlight the value of integrating subject-level covariates, nonlinear modeling, and spatial regularization for HRF estimation in task-based fMRI. The proposed framework provides a flexible and interpretable approach for single-subject analysis, with potential extensions to group-level inference, longitudinal designs, and studies of individual differences in neurovascular coupling.
