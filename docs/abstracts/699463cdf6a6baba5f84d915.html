Introduction

Understanding how sensory experience shapes brain development is a central challenge in neuroscience, bridging the gap between genetically encoded constraints and experience-dependent plasticity. While intrinsic structural and functional features of cortical organization are evolutionarily conserved, sensory experience significantly shapes the maturation and specialization of neural circuits. Congenital sensory deprivation offers a unique model to disentangle these factors; however, it remains unclear how intrinsic constraints and sensory experience jointly shape the brain’s large-scale functional architecture.

Traditionally, research on the sensory-deprived brain has relied on region-specific connectivity analyses, which fail to capture the brain’s global "functional geometry"—the low-dimensional gradients that organize cortical regions along continuous functional axes. Specifically, while a principal gradient extending from sensorimotor to transmodal cortices characterizes typical development, it is unknown whether this hierarchy persists without sensory input or how strictly intrinsic axes constrain plasticity.

Here, we address these questions by combining naturalistic movie fMRI and functional gradient mapping in congenitally blind, congenitally deaf, and typically developed individuals. Using a naturalistic paradigm to probe ecologically valid hierarchies, we investigate: (i) whether the principal functional hierarchy remains stable under deprivation; (ii) if modality-specific gradients are altered in the absence of sensory input; and (iii) whether experience-dependent reorganization follows these intrinsic organizational axes.

Methods

Participants and Stimuli. We analyzed fMRI data from 47 participants: 30 typically developed (TD) controls and two rare groups of congenitally sensory-deprived individuals (9 congenitally blind, 8 congenitally deaf)1. The TD participants were divided into three groups (N=10 each) and presented with a naturalistic movie (101 Dalmatians) in one of three sensory formats: Audiovisual (AV), Audio-only (A), or Visual-only (V). Congenitally blind participants were presented with the Audio-only version (including audio description), while congenitally deaf participants viewed the Visual-only version.

Data Acquisition and Preprocessing. Functional MRI data were acquired on a 3T Philips Ingenia scanner using a gradient-echo EPI sequence (TR=2000 ms, voxel size =3×3×3 mm) . Preprocessing followed standard pipelines, including spike removal, slice-timing correction, realignment, spatial smoothing (6mm FWHM), and percent signal normalization. Nuisance regression was applied to remove motion parameters and spikes, followed by bandpass filtering (0.008–0.08 Hz) . Data were registered to MNI-152 space and parcellated using the multimodal Glasser 360-region atlas.

Functional Gradients Analysis. To map the brain's functional geometry, we computed functional connectivity (FC) matrices (Pearson correlation) for each participant. We applied diffusion map embedding to the thresholded (10% density) FC matrices using the BrainSpace toolbox (parameter α=0.5) to extract low-dimensional cortical gradients2. Gradients were aligned via Procrustes rotation to the average gradient of the TD-AV group. We quantified the "functional geometry" using the gradient range (difference between maximum and minimum eigenvector values), serving as a proxy for functional differentiation/heterogeneity along specific axes.

Functional Reorganization and Feature Mapping. We employed the Network-Based Statistic (NBS) to identify whole-brain connectivity differences between groups (F≥9, p<0.05), utilizing a composite null hypothesis test to isolate effects specific to congenital deprivation rather than transient sensory absence. To test if reorganization follows intrinsic hierarchies, we correlated the spatial maps of FC alterations (NBS scores) with the intrinsic sensory gradients, assessing significance via spin-test permutation to correct for spatial autocorrelation. Finally, to determine the functional relevance of reorganized regions, we correlated regional BOLD time courses with annotated stimulus features—ranging from low-level visual/auditory features (e.g., motion energy, spectrograms) to mid-level speech attributes.

Results

Our analysis of the low-dimensional embedding space demonstrated that the macroscale functional architecture of the cortex is robust to congenital sensory deprivation. The principal functional gradient (G1), which spans from unimodal sensory regions to transmodal association cortices, was consistently preserved across all groups. Quantitative comparison of the gradient range—a proxy for functional heterogeneity—confirmed no significant differences in the principal gradient between sensory-deprived individuals and the audiovisual control group (Blind vs. AV: T=−1.93, p=0.07, Hedges' g=−0.83; Deaf vs. AV: T=0.27, p=0.79, Hedges' g=−0.14). This indicates that the primary scaffold of cortical organization is established independently of modality-specific sensory experience.

In contrast to the stability of the primary axis, we observed significant, modality-specific alterations in the geometry of secondary functional gradients. Congenital deprivation led to a "geometric contraction"—a significant reduction in the range of the gradient corresponding to the deprived modality. Specifically, congenitally blind individuals exhibited a marked reduction in the range of the visual-anchored gradient (G2) compared to the audiovisual control group (T=4.17, p<0.001, Hedges' g=−1.78, BF10 =37.27) and the deaf group (T=−3.62, p=0.002). Similarly, congenitally deaf individuals showed a specific contraction in the auditory-anchored gradient (G3) relative to controls (T=−5.6, p<0.001, Hedges' g=−2.31, BF10 =400.79). These large effect sizes and the lack of effect in the temporary deprived populations suggest that sensory deprivation reduces the functional differentiation of the deprived system from the rest of the brain.

Network-Based Statistic (NBS) analysis revealed structured cross-modal reorganization. In blind participants, we observed significantly increased functional connectivity between deprived extrastriate visual areas (e.g., MT+ complex, dorsal stream) and higher-order regions in the dorsolateral and inferior frontal cortices (overlapping with Frontoparietal and Default Mode Networks). In deaf participants, reorganization was characterized by increased coupling between extrastriate visual areas and auditory association cortices (e.g., Superior Temporal Sulcus, areas A4 and A5).

Crucially, we tested whether this plasticity aligns with the brain’s intrinsic functional geometry. We found significant positive correlations between the spatial maps of functional reorganization and the pre-existing sensory gradients. For blind individuals, the pattern of connectivity changes correlated with the visual gradient (Spearman ρ=0.42, pnull <0.001). For deaf individuals, reorganization correlated with the auditory gradient (Spearman ρ=0.36, pnull =0.003). This demonstrates that compensatory plasticity is not random but unfolds along intrinsic functional axes.

Finally, correlation analysis between regional BOLD time courses and annotated movie features confirmed the functional relevance of these reorganized networks. In congenitally deaf participants, auditory association cortices (e.g., Left/Right A5) showed significantly stronger correlations with visual stimulus features compared to controls (e.g., Left A5: T=5.246, p=0.028, FDR corrected across all atlas regions). Conversely, in blind participants, deprived visual areas (e.g., Left MT, Right V4t) exhibited stronger synchronization with speech-related features (e.g., Right V4t: T=6.615, p=0.001, FDR corrected across all atlas regions). These findings confirm that intrinsic gradients channel specific sensory computations—speech processing to visual cortex in the blind, and visual processing to auditory cortex in the deaf.

Conclusion

How do sensory experience and innate organisational principles jointly shape the macroscale functional architecture of the cortex? Here, leveraging naturalistic stimulation and the framework of functional geometry, we compare audiovisual, visual-only, and auditory-only conditions in congenitally blind, congenitally deaf, and neurotypically developed individuals. We demonstrate that congenital sensory deprivation does not disrupt the overall hierarchical organization of cortical functional gradients. Extending recent observations, our data indicate that the primary gradient retains its canonical resting-state configuration—spanning from unimodal sensory to transmodal regions—independent of sensory experience3. This robust preservation aligns with evolutionary and developmental evidence suggesting an intrinsic organisational scaffold4.

However, the impact of congenital sensory deprivation is detected in the brain’s intrinsic functional geometry. Sensory-deprived cortices exhibit reduced functional specialization specifically along the gradient dimension associated with the deprived modality. This reduced specialization facilitates postnatal, experience-dependent modelling. Notably, such reorganization follows a geometric principle: it unfolds along the intrinsic sensory gradients themselves, with perceptual information from spared unimodal or transmodal regions being systematically rerouted to sensory-deprived areas.

Our gradient-based findings are complemented by finer-grained connectivity analyses. In congenitally blind individuals, we observe increased connectivity between extrastriate areas and key hubs of the frontoparietal and default mode networks. This increased visual-to-frontal integration occurs alongside a reduction in intra-network connectivity within the visual system itself, suggesting a ‘redistribution’ of functional resources toward higher-order transmodal regions. A complementary pattern emerges in congenitally deaf individuals, who exhibited strengthened connectivity between the visual network and auditory association cortices.

By testing the relationship between the spatial distribution of functional changes and the underlying sensory hierarchy, we showed that this reorganization follows the pre-existing sensory hierarchies. This implies that the brain’s intrinsic functional architecture actively ‘chaperons’ plastic reorganization along inherent functional geometry. Consequently, the altered connectivity patterns provide compelling support for the idea that functional reorganization unfolds along meaningful computational and anatomical trajectories.

Our data also reveal a distinctive pattern of functional recruitment. The occipital cortex’s extensive connections and central hierarchical positioning confer exceptional versatility, facilitating significant functional repurposing in blind individuals. In contrast, the auditory cortex demonstrates relatively constrained reorganizational potential, relying primarily on strengthening pre-existing heteromodal connections.

In the light of our results, we propose that the brain's intrinsic functional geometry governs plastic cross-modal reorganization, providing a unified model explaining both stability and flexibility in sensory-deprived brains. This perspective aligns with evidence that even primary sensory areas are embedded in a richly interconnected architecture supporting early multisensory integration5. Unimodal cortices exhibit the capacity to integrate cross-modal input that becomes functionally dominant when the canonical sensory input is absent. Altogether, these results underscore that the development of cortical architecture emerges from a dynamic interplay between intrinsic, genetically driven organizational frameworks and extrinsic, experience-dependent shaping forces
