Introduction

Neurodegenerative disorders are the leading cause of dementia, affecting approximately 0.73% of the global population in 2019 and projected to double by 2050. Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and Parkinson's disease (PD) are the two most common neurodegenerative disorders worldwide, comprising 60–80% and 3.6% of dementia cases, respectively.1 The burden of dementia is rising fastest in low-income countries like Colombia, driven by increased life expectancy.1 

Age-related gray matter volume (GMV) loss is well established and accelerates in neurodegenerative conditions, underscoring the need for early detection. MRI-based normative models quantify individual deviations from typical age-related brain patterns, such as GMV, using large structural MRI datasets and morphometric analyses. These models fall into two categories: reference models, tailored to specific populations, and standard models, derived from healthy individuals to represent typical aging.2 Most existing models aim to be “standard,” yet their reliance on predominantly European-descendant cohorts limits their generalizability, making them more suitable as reference models for those populations. Furthermore, current pipelines often require several hours of processing, restricting clinical applicability.

To address these limitations, we propose developing a normative reference model for the Colombian population using streamlined morphometric pipelines that can process data in under one hour. This approach will enable rapid identification of atypical aging patterns associated with neurodegenerative diseases, promoting more equitable and timely diagnoses. 

Methods

We developed a normative modeling framework based on three methodological reviews2,3,4 and optimized the pipeline to maximize classification performance between healthy controls (HC) and neurodegenerative disease (ND) patients in a Colombian cohort. Specifically, we evaluated: i) two feature-based approaches highlighted in the reviews, voxel-based morphometry (VBM) and brain morphometry; ii) common feature representations used in the literatures, including normative deviation scores, principal components (PCA), and brain-age difference (chronological age minus predicted age); and iii) normative deviation scores derived from either a single reference model or multiple models stratified by age ranges. 

We retrospectively assembled a training dataset of 2,110 HCs aged 18–80 years (mean ± SD: 46.78 ± 16.63 y; 1,158 females) and a testing dataset comprising 549 HCs (44.07 ± 17.41; y 271 females), 109 AD (71.90 ± 7.37 y; 64 females), and 140 PD patients (68.45 ± 8.81 y; 55 females). All datasets were selected and inspected by a Neuroradiologist based on radiological reports in a cross-sectional study approved by the institutional ethics committee. Anatomical T1-weighted MRI scans were preprocessed using FSL (version 5), including cropping, bias-field correction, brain extraction and registration to the MNI space. Both brain morphometry and VBM analyses were implemented using FSL due to its efficiency and fast processing times, ensuring suitability for integration into clinical workflows. 

For brain morphometry, preprocessed images were segmented into gray matter (GM), white matter (WM), cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and seven subcortical structures: hippocampus, amygdala, nucleus accumbens, caudate, pallidum, putamen, and thalamus. Then, their volumes were calculated and normalized to intracranial volume (ICV). For VBM, a study-specific GM template was created by averaging training-set images and enforcing left–right symmetry via x-axis flipping. Individual GM maps were non-linearly registered to this template, modulated to adjust for local expansions/contractions, and smoothed with an isotropic Gaussian kernel (FWHM = 4 mm3). 

The normative deviation score (Znd ) was computed as: 

Znd=(Y−mean(Yreference) )/sqrt(σ2reference)

where Y is the individual feature value,  and Yreference and σreference are the mean and standard deviation from the training data. For VBM, the reference was a voxel-wise map derived from the template cohort. Age-stratified models were defined by sex-specific breakpoints in the slope of the linear relationship between normalized brain volume (brain2ICV) and age. Breakpoints were identified using a sliding window approach (minimum 20 years, incremented by 1 year), with critical ages defined where consecutive windows showed significantly different slopes. 

Normative deviation scores were used as predictors in brain-age regression models. We compared a linear model with L1 regularization (Lasso) and a non-linear gradient boosting model (XGBoost). Hyperparameters were optimized via grid search with cross-validation, minimizing negative mean squared error. For Lasso, the key parameter was regularization strength (α); for XGBoost, we tuned tree count, depth, learning rate, and sampling ratios. The best-performing model (minimum MAE, maximum R2) was used to compute delta age (chronological age minus predicted brain age). 

Finally, classification of AD and PD versus HC was performed using a Support Vector Classifier (SVC) with L2 normalization, regularization strength set to 1, and a squared hinge loss. A calibrated, stratified 5-fold cross-validation was used to preserve group balance. Performance was evaluated using the area under the curve (AUC) and F1-score.

Results

We identified six breakpoints in the aging trajectory: ages 22, 34, 44, 61, 72, and 76 for females, and ages 27, 32, 44, 60, 68, and 74 for males (Fig. 1A). These breakpoints mark the upper limits of our age ranges for the stratified models. For instance, the first age range for females is [18, 22] and for males, it is [18, 27].

We found that the XGBoost regression model stratified by age on the VBM features achieved the best performance in predicting chronological age (Table 1). XGBoost had a higher R2 and lower MAE across both feature sets than Lasso. For VBM, XGBoost reached R² = 0.84, MAE = 5.95 ± 3.99 years (p < 0.0001), while for brain morphometry it achieved R² = 0.63, MAE = 8.25 ± 6.64 years (p < 0.0001). Moreover, stratifying models by age ranges significantly improved the performance using VBM features, the R² increased to 0.86 and MAE decreased to 4.66 ± 3.44 years (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 1B). In contrast, the performance decrease slightly using brain morphometry features (R² = 0.63, MAE = 8.67 ± 6.01 years, p < 0.0001).

We found that delta-age derived from VBM features yielded the best classification performance between HC and ND patients, regardless of the number of references used (Table 2). The model using delta-age as input consistently outperformed those based on original features (Znd) or their PCA-reduced versions, achieving the highest combination of AUC, accuracy, and F1-score. For AD vs HC, results were AUC = 0.91 ± 0.04, accuracy = 0.85, and F1 = 0.87. For PD vs HC, results were AUC = 0.87 ± 0.05, accuracy = 0.82, and F1 = 0.85 (Fig. 1C). Stratifying models by age ranges improved the performance slightly. For AD vs HC, results were AUC = 0.92 ± 0.02, accuracy = 0.85, and F1 = 0.88. For PD vs HC, results were AUC = 0.88 ± 0.02, accuracy = 0.84, and F1 = 0.87.

In contrast, brain morphometry features showed substantially poorer classification performance between HC and ND subjects (Table 3). For AD vs HC, the original features performed best (AUC = 0.84 ± 0.02; accuracy = 0.87; F1 = 0.52), while for PD vs HC, delta-age yielded the highest performance (AUC = 0.69 ± 0.04; accuracy = 0.64; F1 = 0.56). Stratifying models by age ranges slightly reduced performance. For AD vs HC, performance decreased slightly using the original features (AUC = 0.83 ± 0.02; accuracy = 0.87; F1 = 0.51), whereas for PD vs HC, performance dropped notably using delta-age (AUC = 0.65 ± 0.03; accuracy = 0.57; F1 = 0.57). 

Conclusion

We developed a population-specific normative reference model for Colombia that enables the rapid and accurate detection of atypical aging patterns associated with the two most common neurodegenerative diseases. The model is designed to integrate easily into clinical workflows and performs at a level comparable to leading methods. 

Our analysis pipeline revealed distinct age-related breakpoints across the lifespan, including critical inflection points previously reported in molecular studies,5 demonstrating that the simple brain2ICV metric can effectively capture aging trajectories. Consistent with one of the referenced methodological reviews 3, VBM outperformed traditional brain morphometry in predicting chronological age and classifying ND patients from HC. This was achievable with straightforward machine learning models suitable for clinical deployment. Finally, while multiple age-stratified references improved age prediction, they offered only marginal gains in disease classification.
