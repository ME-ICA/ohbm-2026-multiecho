Introduction

Cerebral palsy (CP) affects approximately 1–2 per 1,000 live births and is associated with persistent motor impairments resulting from early brain injury.1 In Europe alone, CP accounts for more than 95,000 years lived with disability (YLDs) each year among children under five years of age. A range of evidence-based rehabilitation approaches has been shown to improve upper limb function, including goal-directed and task-specific training and bimanual intensive training.2 However, the structural mechanisms underlying therapeutic effects remain poorly understood.

Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) from magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain enables the examination of white matter (WM) microstructure and structural connectivity, allowing the characterization of network-level changes over time. Further, advanced multi-compartment models - such as neurite orientation dispersion and density imaging (NODDI) - can quantify characteristics related to neurite density and organization.3 Given the vulnerability of WM pathways in CP, such imaging approaches may be promising for the investigation of potential rehabilitation-induced structural alterations.

Against this background, we examined a cohort of children and adolescents with unilateral CP or acquired hemiparesis undergoing a novel rehabilitation program (“Magic Moves”) to investigate whether longitudinal changes in WM microstructure and structural connectivity occur following the intervention.  

Methods

Ten participants 10.7 ± 1.7 years (age range 6–17 years) with unilateral CP or acquired hemiparesis were enrolled in a prospective longitudinal design and underwent a two-week intensive rehabilitation program comprising 45 hours of occupational training, coordination and fine motor skill training through learning magic tricks, bimanual activities, and repetitive neuromuscular magnetic stimulation or functional electric stimulation (“Magic Moves”). Brain MRI was acquired at three time points: pre-intervention (10-20 days before, T0), post-intervention (one week after, T1), and 3 months post-intervention (T2) (Fig. 1). 

Brain MRI was acquired on a 3-Tesla scanner (Philips Ingenia Elition X, software version 5.7.1) using a 32-channel head coil. Among other sequences, structural 3D T1-weighted images (1 mm isotropic, TR = 9 ms, TE = 4 ms) and DWI (2 mm isotropic, TR = 5900ms, TE = 91ms, multi-shell, 120 diffusion directions, b-values ranging from 0 to 2500 s/mm²) were obtained.

Preprocessing was performed with MRtrix3 (3.0.4) and FSL (6.0.7.17) and included denoising, Gibbs-ringing removal, motion correction, and eddy-current correction, followed by coregistration to the T1-weighted images. Brains with right-hemispheric lesions (2/10 subjects) were flipped along the mid-sagittal plane to homogenize lesion lateralization and enable group-level analyses. All brains were subsequently registered to MNI152 standard space. Microstructural parameter maps were generated using the NODDI Matlab toolbox (R2023b).3 Whole-brain tractograms were generated using constrained spherical deconvolution (CSD) with 10 million streamlines and SIFT2 weighting, from which structural connectivity matrices were derived (Fig. 1).4

Voxel-wise statistical analysis of NODDI maps was performed in SPM12 using a flexible factorial design modelling the main effect of the timepoint (T0, T1, T2). Network-based statistics (NBS) were applied to detect longitudinal connectivity changes across time points.5 Significance thresholds were set at p<0.05 (peak-level and cluster-level family-wise error-corrected [FWE] for voxel-wise analyses and FWE-corrected within NBS [t-threshold 2.6, 5000 permutations]).

Results

All ten participants were included in the longitudinal analyses. Analysis of NODDI maps revealed significant longitudinal microstructural changes. In the supplementary motor area (SMA), a statistically significant increase in the intracellular volume fraction (FICVF) was observed in both hemispheres comparing T0 to T2. In a cluster within the lesioned hemispheres, mean FICVF increased from 0.38 at T0 to 0.42 at T2 (peak-level FWE-corrected p = 0.016, cluster-level FWE-corrected p = 0.377). In the contralateral hemisphere, mean FICVF increased from 0.43 at T0 to 0.47 at T2 (peak-level FWE-corrected p = 0.057, cluster-level FWE-corrected p = 0.003) (Fig. 2). Other NODDI parameters showed no statistically significant clusters.

Network-based statistics identified a significant subnetwork comprising three non-lesioned-hemispheric nodes connected by two edges. This subnetwork showed increased structural connectivity in the non-lesioned hemisphere when directly comparing T0 with T2, specifically along the inferior temporal gyrus – posterior cingulate cortex (test statistic = 2.63) and the middle temporal gyrus – posterior cingulate cortex (test statistic = 2.69) connections (Fig. 2).

Conclusion

In this longitudinal study, voxel-wise NODDI analysis across three scanning timepoints (T0, T1, T2) revealed a significant increase in FICVF within the SMA at the 3-months follow-up examinations in both the lesioned and non-lesioned hemispheres. Structural connectivity analysis, performed for the T0–T2 comparison, identified a significant subnetwork in the non-lesioned hemispheres comprising the inferior temporal gyrus–posterior cingulate cortex and middle temporal gyrus–posterior cingulate cortex connections. Together, these results may demonstrate measurable microstructural and network-level changes over the course of rehabilitation.

The increase in FICVF within the SMA at T2 in both hemispheres suggests post-intervention microstructural remodeling in motor-related regions. As FICVF reflects properties of the intracellular compartment, such as neurite density or axonal and dendritic volume, these changes may indicate alterations in the underlying neural architecture. The absence of significant effects immediately after the intervention (T1), together with the emergence of changes only at 3-months follow-up examinations (T2), further suggests that such microstructural adaptations require time to consolidate, underscoring the importance of extended follow-up intervals when assessing therapy-related plasticity. Of note, the SMA is a highly plausible brain area in which occupational effects may lead to changes, which may be due to its central role in controlling movement coordination without being the area of last execution. Additionally, the SMA is known for its capability for plasticity, even in adults in whom a lesioned SMA may over time be compensated for by the healthy hemisphere. Moreover, the network-based findings may indicate intervention-associated reorganization involving temporal regions and the posterior cingulate cortex, highlighting structural changes outside classical motor areas. These additional regions may also be relevant for understanding how broader structural networks adapt over the course of rehabilitation.

Together, these findings may indicate that intensive rehabilitation (“Magic Moves”) could be accompanied by improvements in WM integrity and network organization in young individuals with unilateral CP or acquired hemiparesis. Detecting therapy-associated alterations in both a motor-related region (i.e., SMA) and a temporo–cingulate subnetwork might underscore the capacity of advanced diffusion MRI to localize where structural remodeling may occur. Longitudinal imaging approaches can help clarify how different brain systems respond to intensive rehabilitation and may ultimately inform the refinement of targeted, mechanism-based interventions in pediatric neurorehabilitation. Further studies with larger cohorts and control groups are needed to confirm these findings and to determine their relevance for functional outcomes.
