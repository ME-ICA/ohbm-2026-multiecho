Introduction

TThe human brain structural network represents an economical complex system that optimizes the trade-off between wiring costs and the topological efficiency. To minimize the wiring cost and metablic expenditure, the brain predominantly forms short-range structural connections. Despite this bias, it also maintains a small number of long-range structural connections whose functional values may justify their substantial cost. However, the functional role of these connections and their spatial arrangement in human brain remain largely unknown. Here, we investigated how long-range structural connections and their specific spatial arrangement modulated network communication, including efficiency of multiple communication strategies, network diversity and communication stability. Furthermore, we delineated the adaptive value of long-range connections by characterizing their associations with behavior and underlying gene expression patterns1.

Methods

We analyzed diffusion MRI (dMRI) from 339 unrelated healthy adults of the Human Connectome Project (s900 release, https://www.humanconnectome.org/). Beginning with the minimally preprocessed data, we used the MRtrix3 toolbox (https://www.mrtrix.org/) to further preprocess dMRI data, including bias correction, modeling of white matter fibers via constrained spherical deconvolution, intensity normalization, probabilistic fiber tracking and filtering. We then used the Human Brainnetome Atlas with 246 regions (http://atlas.brainnetome.org/) to define nodes and reconstruct the connection weight with streamline length. To gain the group-level connectome, we used the consistency-based thresholding method, where the top 30% of connections with the smallest coefficient of variation (CV) in connection weight among individuals were selected and averaged across participants (Fig 1A). We defined the connections corresponding to the top 25% of streamline length in the group-averaged matrix as long-range structural connections. To examine the effect of long-range connections on brain network communication, we created two types of surrogate networks, 1000 networks for each (Fig 1A): (1) All long-range connections were randomly rewired such that their lengths fell below the 75th percentile of streamline lengths in the group-level connectome (called “reduced” network); (2) The spatial arrangement of long-range connections was randomly disrupted while preserving their length distribution and the weight-length relationship (called “spatial” network). 

   We investigated the impact of long-range connections on three aspects of communication measurements (Fig 1B): (1) Communication efficiency across three strategies2: global efficiency, navigation efficiency and diffusion efficiency; (2) Network diversity, one minus the mean cosine similarity between connectivity profiles of two regions connected by long-range connections in the empirical brain network and spatial surrogates, or the shortened long-range connections in the reduced surrogates. Higher diversity indicates greater ability to integrate specialized information sources3; (3) Communication stability: search information/path transitivity of shortest-path and navigation-path, connection degeneracy. Low search information means the path has a high success rate of information transmission and less noise, and high path transitivity means the path allow information that diverges from the main pathway to flow back toward it4. Finally, connection degeneracy was calculated as the mean cosine similarity among the connectivity profiles of a region's long-range neighbors, with this metric averaged across all brain areas. High degeneracy suggests that if a part of long-range connections of area i is damaged, i will still receive similar inputs and deliver similar output through the rest long-range connections5.We used the Brain Connectivity Toolbox (https://sites.google.com/site/bctnet/) to compute the above metrics and used permutation test to examine the difference between real network and surrogates. 

   To examine the relationship between the distribution of long-range connections and human behaviors, we performed a meta-analysis using Neurosynth database (https://www.neurosynth.org/) (Fig 1C). Regions were sorted according to the number of long-range connections they owned and then binned into 20 intervals with 5% increments. Each interval was associated with 24 representative behavioral terms. Finally, we used partial least squares regression to explore the relationship between the gene expression profile (from Allen Human Brain Atlas) and the spatial distribution of long-range connections (Fig 1D). We searched for Gene Ontology (GO) terms that enriched at the top of the weight (up-regulated) ranked list of genes in the PLS1 components using GOrilla (https://cbl-gorilla.cs.technion.ac.il/). Subsequently, we used the rrvgo (http://bioconductor.org/packages/rrvgo/) to reduce the redundancy of GO terms.

Results

By comparing the reduced surrogates with the real network, we found that long-range structural connections could enhance the communication efficiency of all the three communication strategies (Fig 2A). Long-range connections exerted a greater impact on enhancing network diversity than shorten ones in reduced surrogates (Fig 2B). Regarding communication stability (Fig 2C), long-range connections decreased the search information and increased the path transitivity of navigation-paths, yet provided no significant contribution on the communication stability of shortest-paths. Long-range connections also had higher degeneracy than rewired ones in reduced surrogates.

   Regarding spatial arrangement, we found that the specific distribution of long-range connections specifically facilitated the efficiency of navigation strategy (Fig 2A), and it led to lower contribution of long-range connections to network diversity (Fig 2B). In terms of communication stability (Fig 2C), the specific distribution of long-range connections could increase the path transitivity for both shortest-paths and navigation-paths, as well as the connection degeneracy of long-range connections. However, the specific spatial arrangement of long-range connections conferred no advantage in reducing the search information of shortest-paths and navigation-paths. 

   Behavior meta-analysis indicated that brain areas with a higher number of long-range connections were more related to some primary functions (e.g., motor) and reward-based decision making (Fig 2D), suggesting the potential role of long-range connections in adaptation and evolution. Additionally, we found that the first component (PLS1) explained 28.93% of the variance in the alterations in the number of long-range connections of each brain region (1000 permutations, p < 0.001), and there was a strong correlation between PLS1 and long-range connections (r = 0.538, p < 0.001). The top-related genes in the transcriptional profiles were enriched in several biological process GO terms, including positive regulation of signal transduction, cell communication and response to stimulus, and cellular component terms including vesicle (p < 0.05, FDR corrected) (Fig 2E&F). No significant enrichment in molecular function was found. It also supported the significance of long-range connections in brain communication.

Conclusion

In this study, we provided comprehensive evidence on the role of long-range structural connections in human brain network. We found that long-range connections could significantly enhance communication efficiency, diversity and stability, while exerting negligible contribution to the communication stability of shortest-paths. Furthermore, the specific spatial distribution of long connections also had impact on certain aspects of network communication (e.g., improved the navigation efficiency), but may be sub-optimal. Behaviorally, long-range connections were more related to primary (e.g. motor) and reward-based behaviors, and were associated with gene expression and biological functions related to brain communication. Overall, our findings suggest that long-range connections serve as a specialized, genetically-regulated architectural feature designed to support the communication values and human behaviors.
