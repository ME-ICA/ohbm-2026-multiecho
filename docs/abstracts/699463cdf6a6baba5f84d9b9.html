Introduction

Current treatment strategies for schizophrenia rely largely on the clinician’s expertise and often involve testing multiple medications before finding an effective one. This trial-and-error process exposes patients to unnecessary side effects and may undermine the therapeutic relationship. Although many drugs with distinct mechanisms of action exist, choosing which neurobiological systems to target remains challenging because the pathophysiology of schizophrenia is still not fully understood.

This challenge reflects the disorder’s complex neurobiology. The long-standing dopaminergic hypothesis positions dysregulated dopamine signaling as a central contributor to symptoms.^1 More recent perspectives suggest that structural abnormalities and neurotransmitter imbalances jointly drive this dysregulation.^2,3 In particular, reduced cortical glutamatergic activity—possibly linked to cortical atypies—may disturb the excitation–inhibition balance. Dopaminergic mechanisms may initially compensate for this imbalance but eventually fail, leading to the altered dopamine function characteristic of schizophrenia. This view highlights the importance of incorporating region-specific neurochemical systems into models of disease mechanisms. However, despite recent advancements, the link between the underlying biology and neuroimaging markers, as well as their usefulness for guiding individualized clinical decisions, remains limited. Bridging this gap requires translational approaches that integrate biophysical models with multimodal neuroimaging data.^4

The goal of this work is to create and validate a personalized connectome-based network model—termed the Virtual Brain Twin (VBT)—designed to support clinicians in selecting the most appropriate treatment for each patient.

Methods

Virtual Brain Twins (VBTs) represent each patient’s brain as a network of interconnected nodes, where local node dynamics are modeled using a mean-field framework that incorporates dopaminergic, glutamatergic, and GABAergic neuromodulatory processes.^5 We hypothesize that the multiscale causal and signaling pathways of the dopaminergic system remain coherent when embedded in a network context, allowing dopamine to modulate cortical inhibitory and excitatory dynamics and compensate for early reductions in cortical excitability. If this is correct, then individualized connectome-based network models, formulated within a weak-coupling regime, should predict patient-specific changes in brain activity under variations in dopaminergic tone. 

Dopaminergic modulation is implemented through Michaelis–Menten kinetics and Hill-type dose–response functions, which capture the nonlinear relationship between dopamine concentration and receptor occupancy. Nodes (neural masses) are connected according to each patient’s diffusion-weighted MRI connectome and interact through excitatory, inhibitory, and dopaminergic pathways. Structural imaging features—such as gray matter volume and D1 receptor density derived from MRI and PET—are used to parameterize the model for each individual. Using these personalized VBTs, we simulate resting-state BOLD activity and extract key features including functional connectivity, dynamic fluidity, and regional ALFF. By systematically varying dopaminergic tone and cortical drive parameters, we construct a parameter manifold that formalizes dopamine’s causal influence on large-scale brain dynamics.

A simulation-based inference algorithm then recovers the parameters for individual patient data, which is hypothesized to predict pathways to restore the individual’s healthy brain activity.

Results

Firstly, we constructed Virtual Brain Twins (VBTs) for 100 healthy subjects from the Human Connectome Project and compared their simulated activity with empirical fMRI data. The synthetic signals closely reproduced key properties of the empirical recordings. To validate that this correspondence arises from the network structure of the model, we examined the correlation between simulated and empirical regional ALFF values. This correlation vanished when excitatory connectivity was set to zero or when the connectome was randomly shuffled, demonstrating that realistic network architecture is necessary for reproducing empirical activity patterns.

Secondly, in a pilot dataset also comprising schizophrenic patients, the model revealed a subject-specific pathological regime distinguishable from the healthy state. Simulated pharmacological interventions within the virtual twins produced individualized medication effect trajectories, which aligned retrospectively with known treatment outcomes. These results suggest that the framework can differentiate patient-specific responses and may serve as a basis for personalized therapeutic prediction.

Conclusion

In this work, we provided new insights into the neurobiological mechanisms underlying this complex disorder and informed more targeted therapeutic strategies.
We successfully constructed Virtual Brain Twins (VBTs) for 100 healthy subjects and demonstrated that these individualized models can reproduce brain activity patterns that closely match empirical data. Using this framework, we investigated how dopamine shapes cortical excitability within a whole-brain network context.
We then used this modeling strategy to establish the foundation for future clinical applications. If the hypothesized predictive effects survive cohort-level testing, it provides an avenue to perform systematic neuroimaging and Virtual Brain Twin–guided medicine.
