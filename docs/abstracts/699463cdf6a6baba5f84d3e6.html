Introduction

Comparative neuroimaging is essential for shedding light on the neural foundations of human language. Key to the human language network is the arcuate fascicle (AF), a white-matter pathway connecting inferior frontal and temporal regions, notably Broca’s and Wernicke’s areas (Friederici & Becker, 2025). The human AF is typically left-lateralized and exhibits two major temporal terminations, in the superior temporal gyrus (STG) and the middle temporal gyrus (MTG). The AF–MTG connection, together with its leftward lateralization, was long considered uniquely human and closely associated with language. Comparative studies in non-human primates described an AF-like tract primarily terminating in the STG and lacking robust left lateralization (e.g., Eichert et al., 2019; Roumazeilles et al., 2020; Sierpowska et al., 2022; Friederici & Becker, 2025). Recently, we challenged this view by demonstrating a weak, left-lateralized AF–MTG connection in both captive and wild chimpanzees (Becker et al., 2025). Here, we broaden this comparative approach to all great apes. Using ultra high-resolution MRI tractography, we compare the strength of AF–STG and AF–MTG connections across humans, bonobos, chimpanzees, gorillas, and orangutans. This analysis allows us to identify when key patterns of fronto-temporal connectivity evolved among great apes.

Methods

We reconstructed the AF using probabilistic tractography in all five great ape species.  For this, we collected 12 post-mortem ape brains from European zoos and African sanctuaries as part of the large, ethically sustainable Evolution of Brain Connectivity (EBC) project (three brains per species: bonobo, chimpanzee, orangutan, gorilla), all of which died naturally and 3 in vivo brains from human participants. All brains were extracted within a post-mortem interval (PMI) of 9 ± 7 h and immersion-fixed in formalin. Tissue integrity was consistently high, as confirmed by MRI and histological screening.

Non-human ape scanning and analysis        
 Ultra-high-resolution diffusion MRI was acquired on a dedicated small-bore Bruker Biospec 94/30 (9.4 T; Paravision 6.0.1) MRI scanner equipped with 300 mT/m gradients. A 3D segmented spin-echo EPI sequence (500 µm isotropic; b = 5000 s/mm², 56 directions; 3 b0; TE = 58.9 ms; TR = 1000 ms; matrix 240×192×144; segmentation factor 32; one reverse-phase b0) yielded data of exceptional quality (Eichner et al. 2024). Total acquisition time was ~90 h per brain.

Preprocessing
 Data were processed using a pipeline optimized for post-mortem dMRI, including signal debiasing, denoising, 3D Gibbs ringing removal, field-drift, distortion, motion, eddy-current correction, as well as ball-and-sticks crossing fiber modelling (FSL v6.0; MRtrix3 v3.0.2). Structural images were segmented into grey and white matter using multi-contrast fuzzy c-means clustering.

ROI definition for tractography of the AF     
 Frontal and temporal ROIs were defined on the JUNA chimpanzee template as previously described (Becker et al. 2025). Briefly, a parietal white-matter seed ROI was placed in the AF core and two waypoint masks were positioned in the frontal lobe and either the posterior MTG or the posterior STG. The temporal ROIs included only the lateral two-thirds of the gyrus, in order to limit false-positive connections that did not reach the respective gyrus. The frontal mask covered connections to the IFG and MFG. The template brain was non-linearly registered to each brain using ANTs SyN with multi-contrast input (all FLASH scans), five resolution levels, and optimized parameters (gradientStep 0.3; updateFieldVarianceInVoxelSpace 7.5). The resulting deformation fields were then applied to warp the ROIs onto the individual brains.

Probabilistic tractography 
 Using FSL, 20,000 streamlines per voxel were seeded in the parietal ROI with the default tracking parameters and a white-matter mask. Streamlines crossing both, the temporal and frontal ROIs were retained to reconstruct the long AFSTG and AF-MTG segments. Connectivity values (i.e. the number of streamlines) and visitation maps were normalized by seed size and scaled to a reference seed size of 1000 voxels. Connections with combined bilateral values of less than 50 were deemed non-robust.

Visualization
 Tractography visitation maps were log-transformed, registered to template space, and averaged. Group AF-STG and AF-MTG maps were thresholded at ≥10 streamlines per voxel for visualization.

Human probabilistic tractography  
 The same tractography parameters and ROI definitions (in MNI space) were applied to human data for cross-species comparison. High-resolution diffusion MRI data were acquired from three healthy adults (1.3 mm isotropic; b = 1000 s/mm²; 60 directions; TE 75 ms; TR 6 s; CMRR-SMS 2; GRAPPA 2; 3 repetitions; 2 reverse-phase b0s) on a Siemens 3T Prisma with a 32-channel coil. Preprocessing included motion, eddy-current, and distortion correction, tensor fitting, and estimation of fiber-crossing models (FSL v6.0). Tractography was run in individual space using the white-matter mask with the same procedures used for the great apes. The maps were intensity normalized, log-transformed, and warped to MNI space for group averaging.

Results

Across all five great ape species, every hemisphere showed arcuate fascicle (AF) terminations in both the superior and middle temporal gyri (STG, MTG). Figure 1 shows the MTG connection across apes.

To quantify lateralization, the Asymmetry Quotient (AQ) was calculated from the connectivity values (nb. of streamlines) AQ = (R – L) / [(R + L) × 0.5]. MTG connectivity was consistently left-lateralized in all great apes, including humans (one-sample t-test: t = –5.35, p < 0.001). Humans showed a mean MTG AQ of –1.18 (SD = 0.30), and non-human apes showed a similar leftward bias (mean AQ = –1.20). STG connectivity, in contrast, did not exhibit reliable left lateralization.

An independent t-test showed no significant difference in MTG lateralization between humans and non-human apes (t = 0.04, p = 0.97), indicating that MTG asymmetry is conserved across the species studied.

Despite this shared lateralization pattern, species differed substantially in connection strength with humans showing the strongest MTG terminations bilaterally.

Conclusion

We propose that the MTG termination of the AF did not emerge de novo in the human lineage. Instead, our findings suggest that the human configuration evolved through strengthening an existing MTG connection of the AF, which dates back at least 12-16 million years ago, when our last common ancestor with orangutans lived. This pre-existing pathway may have provided an important neural scaffold for the evolution of human language. 

Future studies will investigate the evolutionary function of the AF-MTG in great apes using behavioral data acquired from the same apes during their lifetime. Of particular interest would be whether such behaviors could be precursors to syntax and semantics, as is the case for the language faculty in humans.
