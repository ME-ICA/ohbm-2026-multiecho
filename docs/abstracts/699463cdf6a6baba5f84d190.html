Introduction

Social norms are shared expectations that guide appropriate behavior within social groups and play a crucial role in maintaining social cohesion and stability. Although people generally conform to social norms, violations occur regularly in everyday life and are particularly prominent in neuropsychiatric disorders involving frontal and temporal lobe dysfunction. Among these conditions, behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia (bvFTD) represents a compelling clinical model for investigating the neural and cognitive foundations of social norm compliance and transgression. Patients with bvFTD frequently exhibit inappropriate social behavior, raising fundamental questions about how neurodegeneration disrupts the computational mechanisms that normally support social behavior. Despite progress in neuroscience, the mechanisms underlying social norm compliance remain poorly understood. Here, we propose a neurocomputational framework to characterize social norm transgression in bvFTD patients.

Methods

We built on decision theory to model social normative decisions as cost-benefit trade-offs between self-interest (i.e., the benefit gained from violating a social norm) and various social costs that discourage norm violations. Based on previous research, we identified three key social costs. First, individuals may adhere to norms voluntarily through the internalized moral values (moral-based norm compliance).1 Second, reputation concerns may deter people from breaking norms when an audience is present (reputation-based norm compliance). 2 Third, norm adherence may be motivated by the desire to avoid social punishments (sanction-based norm compliance). 3

To capture the contribution of these factors, we designed a “social norm violation task” in which participants decide whether to comply with or transgress a social norm to obtain a reward, under conditions that varied the presence or absence of an audience and the presence or absence of a potential sanction. We additionally assessed the subjective value participants assigned to norms, rewards, audiences, and sanctions, using rating tasks. Because social normative decisions likely depends on broader social-cognitive abilities, we also measured participants’ social semantic and theory of mind (TOM) abilities, using dedicated controls tasks. 

Three groups completed the tasks: patients with bvFTD (N = 12), patients with Alzheimer’s disease (N = 20), and healthy controls (N = 21). Participants’ behavior was characterized by fitting a computational model that modelled the probability P of transgressing a norms as a softmax function of the net value of the transgression (i.e., benefits minus the costs): 

Net Value = 𝑘R * Reward - (𝑘N * Norm + 𝑘A* Audience + 𝑘S * Sanction)

P(violation) = softmax(Beta * Net Value)

The free parameters 𝑘R, 𝑘N, 𝑘A, and 𝑘S quantify each participant’s sensitivity to rewards, norms, audience, and sanctions, respectively, while the parameter beta captures choice stochasticity.

Finally, we used model-based lesion-mapping to link individual differences in computational parameters to regional neurodegeneration. Neurodegeneration was assessed using 18FDG-PET to measure hypometabolism and T1-weighted MRI to estimate cortical thickness. We leveraged heterogeneity in patients’ atrophy patterns to identify brain regions in which neurodegeneration was associated with specific computational alteration.

Results

We observed increased social norm transgressions in bvFTD patients compared to AD patients, with a trend toward higher levels compared to healthy controls (figure 1a). This higher transgression rate in bvFTD did not result from distortions in the subjective valuation of personal benefits or social costs, as ratings were comparable across groups. Instead, it reflected a distortion in how self-interest and social costs are weighted in decision-making. Computational modelling of choices in the social norm violation task (figure 1b) indicated that the increased social norms violations in bvFTD patients were best explained by diminished weighting of internalized social norms (kN) and audience-related costs (kA), rather than by increased weighting of rewards or decreased weighting of sanctions.

Model-based lesion mapping will further allow us to identify brain regions in which neurodegeneration produces specific computational alterations. Based on previous research, we hypothesize that damage to the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) and ventral striatum (VS) may be associated with reduced sensitivity to self-interest and internalized social norms; that anterior insula damage may produce a general decrease in sensitivity to social costs; that temporoparietal junction (TPJ) degeneration may selectively impair mental states attribution and reputation processing in the presence of an audience; that right lateral prefrontal cortex (rlPFC) damage may reduce sanction-based norm compliance; and that social normative decisions rely on semantic knowledge of social concepts, which may be compromised by degeneration of the superior anterior temporal lobe (ATL).

Conclusion

In this study, we investigated social normative decision-making in bvFTD patients using a combined behavioral and neurocomputational approach that conceptualizes such decisions as cost-benefit trade-offs between personal interest and social costs. We show that bvFTD patients exhibit reduced sensitivity to internalized social norms and to the presence of an audience during social decision-making, resulting in increased norm transgressions relative to control groups. Model-based lesion-mapping further enables us to link these computational alterations to neurodegeneration in specific brain regions, as indexed by cortical thickness and glucose hypometabolism. More broadly, integrating clinical model such as bvFTD with computational phenotyping and lesion-based mapping provides a promising avenue for elucidating the neurocomputational bases of social norm compliance and transgression in both healthy and pathological brains.
