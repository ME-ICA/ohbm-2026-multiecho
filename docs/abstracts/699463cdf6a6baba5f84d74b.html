Introduction

The human lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) is a small and anatomically complex structure that is difficult to delineate and segment. Although the LGN has always been central to visual neuroscience, only recent advances in high-resolution structural MRI have made its precise anatomical segmentation possible. Such detailed characterization is important for several reasons. Measuring LGN volume helps in the study of visual disorders such as glaucoma, where early changes may appear in subcortical structures. Identifying its exact location and shape is also crucial for the design of visual prostheses aimed at restoring sight. In addition, precise anatomical delineation helps separate LGN fMRI activity from responses in nearby nuclei such as the ventral pulvinar, since functional images are often acquired with insufficient resolution to distinguish these structures.


Anatomical segmentations of the LGN often rely on manual delineation. These require substantial expertise, can be time-consuming for large datasets, and naturally exhibit some variability across repeated segmentations. Recent automatic approaches provide a useful alternative, especially for researchers without expertise in segmenting the thalamus. However, the ability of such methods to recover LGN size, location and contour has not yet been tested systematically. To address this question, we evaluated six methods using several quantitative metrics and compared their segmentations against manually drawn LGN delineations from an expert researcher.1 Our results show that although one method performs among the best in several metrics, no single approach excels across all anatomical dimensions. Instead, several algorithms have distinct strengths for particular measurements. This suggests that LGN segmentation should be treated as a task-specific choice, guided by the feature (volume, location, or shape) of interest.

Methods

We used a dataset of 27 MP2RAGE T1-weighted unified (UNI) images of healthy subjects (mean age 25.9 ± 3.8 years, 15 female) reconstructed at a resolution of 0.4 mm isotropic.2 On this data, we applied several state-of-the-art segmentation methods, namely FreeSurfer’s current probabilistic atlas of the human thalamic nuclei, the NextBrain atlas, DeepThalamus and HIPS-THOMAS. For a strictly alignment-based segmentation approach, we registered LGNs from the BigBrain onto the individual anatomy. Lastly, we performed manual segmentations in the subject's native space (manual novice segmentation). Using the EvaluateSegmentation software,3 we quantified the different segmentations to the gold-standard as a ground-truth: LGN segmentations manually drawn by an expert.1 For evaluating the quality of the segmentations we utilized four metrics, the Hausdorff distance, the balanced average Hausdorff distance,4 the volumetric similarity and the Euclidean distance between the centre of masses. These metrics allow us to assess three different aspects of segmentations, namely contour, volume and location.

Results

Without any manual intervention, all methods produced LGN segmentations near the expected anatomical location. However, the resulting segmentations differed markedly across methods, and substantial variability was also evident across subjects. Panel C shows variability concerning the contour in the first row and differences in volume estimations of the segmentation methods in the last row.

Figure 2 quantifies the variation across methods based on different evaluation metrics. For instance, HIPS-THOMAS, DeepThalamus and the manual novice segmentation performed the best concerning contour, based on the Hausdorff distance. BigBrain and HIPS-THOMAS achieved the highest score in estimating the volume. The manual novice segmentation, HIPS-THOMAS and DeepThalamus were best at estimating the location of the centre of the LGN.

Conclusion

No single algorithm consistently outperformed the others across all aspects of LGN segmentation, indicating that the choice of method should be guided by the specific feature of interest. Based on our evaluations, we recommend HIPS-THOMAS for contour accuracy, BigBrain or HIPS-THOMAS for volume estimation, and DeepThalamus or HIPS-THOMAS for anatomical localization. For applications requiring reliable performance across multiple dimensions, HIPS-THOMAS provides the most balanced overall results. Several automated approaches performed comparably to our novice manual segmentation, and in some cases exceeded it. These findings suggest that both manual and well-validated automated methods can be suitable for studies of visual disorders, for delineating fMRI responses in the thalamus, and for informing the design of LGN-targeted visual prostheses.
