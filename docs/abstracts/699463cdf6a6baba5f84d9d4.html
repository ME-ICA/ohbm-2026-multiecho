Introduction

Human bodies and brains show substantial morphological variation across individuals reflecting both genetic and environmental factors. Emerging studies suggest that specific aspects of body variation like height or body mass index (BMI) are associated with variation in regional brain anatomy. However, individual aspects of body stature and shape are known to show strong covariation with one another - suggesting value in using principal components of body shape for analysis of brain variation rather than piecemeal use of  isolated body metrics. Moreover, it remains unclear if: (i) body and brain anatomy remain associated with one another after accounting for the strong skeletal scaling that links body and cranial size, and (ii) observed relationships between body and brain size reflect primarily genetic or environmental factors. Addressing these issues would clarify basic questions regarding the coordinated development of our bodies and brains, and also provide an important framework for understanding the co-occurring somatic and neuroanatomical alterations that occur in diverse clinical settings. 

Methods

Brain structural MRI data from the UK Biobank (UKB1) (N ~29000 after QC2) were processed with FreeSurfer (V6) to estimate global (total tissue volume -TTV, cerebral volume - CV, gray matter volume - totGMV, white matter volume - totWMV, total surface area - totSA and cortical thickness -meanCT), 12 subcortical volumes (SC, left and right averaged) and regional (HCP parcellation, 180 regions, left and right hemisphere averaged) cortical thickness (CT), surface area (SA) and gray matter volume (GMV). UKB individuals with sMRI data were also scored for the first 3 principal components of body shape variation as derived by Ried et al.3, from variation in  height, weight, BMI, waist and hip circumference and waist to hip ratio (WHR) in an independent sample of 82,355 people. PC1 (henceforth “A1”) captures variations in adiposity , while PC2 and PC3 (henceforth “H1” and “H2”) capture independent components of height variation (high H1 score - tall with small WHR; high H2 scores - tall with low WHR and narrow hips). We first mapped phenotypic correlations (Rp) of A1, H1 and H2 with global and regional brain anatomy after adjustment of both body PCs and neuroanatomical measures for  age, age2, sex, scan center, age x sex and age2 x sex.  Next, all 558 neuroanatomical measures were genetically characterized using genome-wide association analyses of 16 million SNPs2 with PLINK. GWAS results were used to 1) estimate genetic correlations (Rg) between body PCs (using GWAS results of Ried et al.) and neuroanatomical traits pairs using LDSC4 and to 2) find gene-level associations using MAGMA5 (using a 10kbp window on both sides of each gene).  Genes associated with variation in both body PCs and brain anatomy  were submitted to FUMA (https://fuma.ctglab.nl/) for wikipathways and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analyses. All phenotypic and genetic analysis were conducted both with and without  adjustment of neuroanatomical measures for intracranial volume (ICV). All reported p-values were corrected for multiple-testing using the FDR method within each analysis - i.e, global Rg, global Rp, all subcortical Rg, subcortical Rp, 180 regional CT, 180 regional SA, 180 regional GMV- Rp and Rg. FDR p-values for enrichment are reported directly from FUMA.

Results

Global: H1 & H2 showed small to moderate phenotypic correlations (Rp's) with most global brain traits  (TTV, CV, totGMV, totWMV, totSA, Fig 1A), which decreased after ICV regression, suggesting mediation by head size (Fig 1A). A1 had negligible Rp with global brain traits before ICV regression, but showed weakly positive correlations after ICV regression (Rp ~ 0.09-0.13). None of the 3 body PCs were phenotypically correlated with mean CT. A1 showed no significant Rg’s globally without ICV regression, but had weakly positive and significant Rg's (Rg ~ 0.09-0.13) with most global brain traits post-ICV regression. For H1 and H2, the patterning of body-brain genetic correlations for global brain traits (Rg) largely echoed that for Rp (Fig. 1B), except that  Rg’s were consistently higher than Rp’s.  

Subcortical: A1 showed weak negative Rp (all |Rp| < 0.1) across multiple subcortical volumes which became weakly positive (Rp< 0.1) after ICV regression. H1 showed the strongest Rp with subcortical volumes (max Rp ~ 0.19 for  cerebellar GMV) which were reduced after ICV regression (FIG. 1C) - again suggesting head size mediation as seen for global volumes. H2 showed weaker subcortical Rp than H1 (all Rp < 0.07), which also became negligible post-ICV regression.   The strongest genetic correlations (Fig. 1D) were seen between cerebellar GMV and H1 (Rg ~0.26). H2 showed strongest Rg with cerebellar GMV also (Rg ~ 0.12). All Rg between body PCs and subcortical volumes  were reduced with ICV regression (Fig. 1D). A1 did not show significant Rg with any SC. 

Regional Cortical: Of the three body PCs, H1 showed the strongest Rp’s and Rg’s with regional GMV (Fig. 1C and Fig 1D). There was significant heterogeneity in these relationships across the cortex. On average, Rg’s were greater than Rp’s (paired t.test p < 2e-16).  Even after ICV regression, significant positive Rg's remained between H1 and parts of the cingulate,  temporal, and insular cortices, although Rp became negligible. Rp between regional GMV and A1 and H2 were very low (although significant, Rp < 0.05) both with and without ICV regression (Fig. 1C). Regional GMV showed no significant Rg with A1 and positive but weaker (than H1) Rg with H2 (FIg. 1D) which were mostly made insignificant with ICV regression. Separate analysis of regional SA and CT revealed that observed relationships between body PCs and regional cortical GMV were mostly driven by SA.

MAGMA: Shared gene-level associations for A1 and global brain traits included HMGA2 and ATP2A1 without ICV regression and LIN7C and BDNF after ICV regression. 18 genes (including HMGA2) overlapped between H1 and global brain traits (either with or without ICV regression). The set of genes overlapping between H1 and all global brain traits post-ICV regression showed Reactome enrichment for "glutathione synthesis and recycling" (p = 3.3e-2, FDR corrected). The gene set overlapping between A1 and all subcortical volumes before ICV regression (Fig. 1E) showed enrichment for three wikipathways (in FUMA): "16p112_distal_deletion_syndrome" (FDR p=1e-8), "arylamin metabolism" (FDR p = 2.9e-3) and "sulfation bio-transformation ratio" (FDR p = 1.1e-2). When looking at the fraction of gene-level overlap between each subcortical volume and the H1, (Fig. 1E) the strongest result was for caudate with an overlap of 16% and that this specific gene set drives the wikipathway enrichment mentioned above. At the regional cortical scale, localized overlapping gene sets were seen for all three body PCs and GMV both with and without ICV regression (Fig. 1E) . Investigation in regional SA and CT showed many brain regions with overlapping genes with the three body PCs. Enrichment analysis of all overlapping genes for regional GMV, SA and CT with the different body PCs resulted in enrichment of the 16p11.2 deletion wikipathway  for both SA and A1 (no ICV regression, FDR p = 4.5e-10)  and for ICV-regressed CT and A1 (FDR p = 2.2e-3). 

Conclusion

Although the link between body size and brain size has been known for long, the phenotypic and genetic nature of these links across different brain phenotypes at different scales is not well understood. In this work we used three independent dimensions of body size (adiposity and two components with dominant loading of height but opposite loadings for WHR) to explore these questions and displayd the heterogeneity in both phenotypic and genetic similarities between brain traits and these body traits. We showed evidence of genetic correlation between body dimensions and brain traits being stronger than phenotypic correlations, which is suggestive of shared environmental influences affecting the brain and the body differentially. We also presented how body dimensions play a minimal role in predicting cortical thickness, which supports previous knowledge of the genetic distinctiveness of cortical thickness compared to area or volume measures. Both phenotypic and genetic correlations between body PCs and brain traits were reduced when the brain traits were adjusted for ICV, indicating that these relationships are in part mediated by head size. However, several relationships between body PCs and brain traits - especially through the lens of genetic correlation existed above and beyond ICV effects. Lastly, we showed that the genes which significantly affected the body and the brain can help us understand the biological processes which may be involved - we saw enrichment of arylamin metabolism, sulfation bio-transformation ratio, glutathione synthesis and recycling. Perhaps most interestingly, we saw how the fractional genetic overlap of multiple brain traits with adiposity (A1) was enriched for 16p11.2 distal deletion syndrome. In particular, this enrichment in gene-based overlap between subcortical volumes and A1 was driven by caudate (Fig. 1E), which is a structure known to be associated with food intake and hence obesity, which is a well-known trait in 16p11.2 deletion.
