Introduction

Neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson’s disease (PD) are known to be highly heterogeneous. Recent work in disease progression modelling includes promising methods such as the Disease Progression Modeling and Stratification (DPMoSt) model1, the Subtype and Stage Inference model2, and the Disease Course Mapping model3. These models perform best when large samples of longitudinal data are available. However, acquiring such samples remains a challenge for most institutions. Pooling data from smaller longitudinal studies can help overcome sample size issues, but this requires the datasets to be shareable. Many clinical datasets cannot be easily shared due to privacy constraints. These difficulties can be bypassed by using federated learning methods, which allow for the decentralized fitting of statistical or machine learning models.

We developed a federated version of a disease progression model based on DPMoSt1 and tested it on synthetic data and a simulated federated scenario from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) dataset. We varied the heterogeneity of the datasets between simulated sites and we explored the behaviour of the federated model under different heterogeneity conditions.

Methods

Code developed for this project is available at https://github.com/neurodatascience/fl-prog. 

Disease progression model

We model each biomarker as a logistic function with steepness and midpoint parameters. In addition, we add a subject-specific translation (“time-shift”) parameter which represents the patient’s disease duration on an absolute disease time scale. Finally, we include additive Gaussian noise with a variance parameter.

Synthetic data simulation

We randomly sampled cross-sectional and longitudinal (up to 3 visits) biomarker data from logistic functions for 50 subjects for each of 3 sites (150 subjects total). We reset each subject’s timepoint values so that their first sample always has a timepoint of 0 – mimicking the fact that their true disease progression state is unknown. Fig. 1a shows an example of each of our three simulation conditions:

  • Independent and identically distributed (IID) case: data for all sites are sampled from the same distribution.
  • 50% overlapping timepoints: simulating different sites having patients at different but overlapping stages of the disease.
  • 0% overlapping timepoints: simulating different sites having patients at completely different stages of the disease.

Each condition was simulated 20 times.

Simulated federated setting on ADNI data

We simulated a federated scenario by randomly assigning individuals from the ADNI dataset in 5 different cohorts. The resulting federated cohorts were composed of 340, 502, 422, 455 and 280 participants, for a total of 1999 individuals. Individual magnetic resonance images (MRIs) from the ADNI dataset were processed with FreeSurfer 7, and we collected longitudinal regional volumes for the following regions of interest: parahippocampal and entorhinal areas, middletemporal and inferior parietal gyri, and precuneus. 

Federated model fitting

For both the Synthetic and ADNI scenarios, we implemented the model in PyTorch and used the Fed-BioMed framework4 to fit it in a decentralized way using the Federated Average algorithm5. Fed-BioMed uses a server-client architecture where each dataset is associated with a different client. Models are fit on local data by each client independently, then fitted parameters are sent back to the server to be averaged. The global (averaged) model parameters are then sent back to clients to initialize local models for further training. The process repeats until model convergence.

Results

Synthetic data

Fig. 1b compares model fits between all three conditions across two setups: centralized (data all pooled together) and federated. Overall, the centralized model performs similarly across all conditions, while the federated model performs worse as data becomes more heterogeneous between sites. However, individual model fits (Fig. 1c) seem to show that, in non-IID conditions, the weaker fit seems to be due to a dilation of the time axis, suggesting that the federated model still captures relative biomarker progression trends. 

ADNI federated

Fig. 2 shows the estimated progression curves and resulting estimated time-shift for all the individuals across the 5 cohorts. The estimated progression shows increased acceleration for subcortical regions and middle temporal gyrus, followed by moderate progression for precuneus and inferior parietal regions. The time-shift distribution shows larger time-shift values for individuals with Alzheimer’s patients (AD) as compared to healthy control (CN) and subjects with early mild cognitive impairment (EMCI) and late MCI (LMCI). Post-hoc analysis revealed a strong correlation of the estimated federated time-shifts with respect to their centralized counterpart (R = 0.73, p<0.05), and a significant correlation of the time-shift with respect to clinical scores ADAS13 (R = 0.18, p<0.05) and MMSE (R = 0.17 p<0.05).

Conclusion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first demonstration of a federated disease progression model. Using simulated data, we show that our model’s estimated parameters are similar to ground truth values in the IID case, and that there are no significant differences between the federated setup and the centralized (mega-analysis) setup. In the non-IID cases, the model seems to still be able to capture biomarker trends. Using real MRI data from the ADNI dataset, we find estimated participant-level time-shift values that track with participant disease status and correlates significantly with clinical scores.
