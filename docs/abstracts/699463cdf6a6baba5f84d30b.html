Introduction

Neural correlates of spontaneous thought have typically been studied using random-onset experience sampling during functional neuroimaging.1 While this approach has yielded substantial insights into the brain basis of spontaneous thinking, random-onset sampling may lead to imprecise temporal estimates of brain activity relative to the arising of mind-wandering events. To address this limitation, we have developed a method termed real-time fMRI-triggered experience sampling (rt-fMRI-ES), in which experience sampling probes (trials) are triggered based on real-time estimates of increased blood-oxygenation-level-dependent (BOLD) activity during a resting state. In our pre-registered study, rt-fMRI-ES targeted increased BOLD activity in two typically-anticorrelated regions: (1) the dorsal anterior insular cortex (daIC), which is theorized to underlie physiological arousal,2 and (2) the posteromedial cortex (PMC), a region of the default mode network which is known to activate during stimulus-independent thought (or mind-wandering).3 In an effort to establish proof-of-concept for the rt-fMRI-ES method, we tested the following hypotheses: (H1) Ratings of subjective arousal that are time-locked to trials triggered by the daIC will be higher than arousal ratings time-locked to trials not triggered by the daIC; and (H2) Ratings of externally-focused attention that are time-locked to trials triggered by the PMC will be lower than external-attention ratings time-locked to trials not triggered by the PMC.

Methods

Sixty healthy adults (24 male, 36 female) each underwent a single neuroimaging session that consisted of six 8-minute runs of rt-fMRI-ES. Neuroimaging data were acquired using a Siemens 3-Tesla MAGNETOM Prisma MRI scanner using a 64-channel head coil. During rt-fMRI-ES, participants were instructed to focus on a fixation crosshair and were told that experience sampling prompts consisting of nine rating scales of 1-to-9 would appear at variable time intervals (Figure 1B). Experience sampling trials were presented based on transient increased BOLD activation of the run’s target region (daIC or PMC) (Figure 1A), or based on lack of target activation detected after 50 seconds (“time-out” trials). The target region of interest alternated every other run, and the target region order was counterbalanced across subjects. Ongoing estimates of BOLD activity of both the target and non-target regions were measured using the Open Neurofeedback Training (OpenNFT) software.4  The rt-fMRI-ES triggering algorithm was configured to detect increased target region BOLD activity based on a pre-defined set of rules: (1) There was a consecutive BOLD percent-signal-chance (%SC) increase across three volumes in the target region; (2) at the current volume, the BOLD %SC of the target region was greater than the run’s baseline value; (3) Criteria 1 and 2 were not met in the non-target region, (4) At least 50 seconds have elapsed since the last trial was triggered.

Each pre-registered hypothesis was tested using a linear mixed-effects model. To test H1, ratings of arousal were modeled as a function of trial type, which was included in the model as a categorical fixed effect with two levels: non-daIC-triggered and daIC-triggered. The slope contrasting trial type, the model intercept, and covariances between fixed effects were included as random effects grouped by subject in the model. To test H2, ratings of external-attention were similarly modeled as a function of trial type included in the model as a categorical fixed effect with two levels: non-PMC-triggered and PMC-triggered.  Random effects grouped by subject were likewise included in the model.

Results

After excluding subjects and trials from the linear-mixed effects model testing H1 due to head motion and data quality issues (n=42; 1243 total trials), the model showed there was no significant effect of triggering trials based on increased daIC activity on arousal ratings (Figure 2A), β = –0.02, SE = 0.11, df = 39.7, t = –0.21, p = .84. Similar subject and trial exclusion procedures were performed for the linear-mixed effects model testing H2, yielding an inclusion of 1429 total trials across 49 subjects. We found evidence in support of our second hypothesis, as PMC-triggered trials were significantly associated with lower external-attention ratings compared to trials that were not triggered by the PMC (Figure 2B), β = –0.30, SE = 0.12, df = 45, t = –2.50, p = .016.

Conclusion

Our results provide proof-of-concept validation of the rt-fMRI-ES method by demonstrating its ability to capture momentary decreases in externally focused attention based on real-time detection of activity in the PMC. This may provide initial support for the rt-fMRI-ES method over traditional random-onset experience sampling measures, specifically for aspects of spontaneous thought related to internally oriented attention. Lack of evidence for our first hypothesis could be explained by a discordance between the form of arousal assessed by our experience sampling measure (psychological, highly-salient thought) and selection of the daIC, a region that may be more relevant to physiological markers of arousal. Clinically relevant applications of the rt-fMRI-ES method, such as targeting maladaptive forms of thought, have the potential to pinpoint the neural bases of hallmark cognitive patterns in psychopathology.
