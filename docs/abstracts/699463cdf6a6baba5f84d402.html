Introduction

Cerebellar ataxias are a heterogeneous group of disorders primarily affecting the cerebellum, characterized by impaired balance and coordination in the absence of muscle weakness. Cerebellar ataxias can be categorized into non-progressive congenital forms that remain stable over time, such as Joubert syndrome (JS), and hereditary or metabolic slowly progressive (SP) cerebellar ataxias, which are marked by gradual cerebellar atrophy and progressive worsening of ataxia over years or decades. Clinically, SP is more severe than JS in terms of symptoms and disability, possibly due to the impossibility to generate effective compensation linked to the continuous action of the etiopathogenetic causes1.

Despite remarkable progress on the molecular, cellular, and microcircuit physiology of the cerebellum, very little is known about the network mechanisms that lead to cerebellar ataxia. To date, literature has focused mainly on the qualitative evaluation of MRI, since these pathologies have a clear distinctive sign: the molar tooth for JS and generalized cerebellar atrophy for SP.

Since motor dysfunction is the main clinical manifestation in subjects with cerebellar ataxias, we focused on the somatomotor network (SMN). In addition, we analysed the ventral attention network (VAN) because of the increasingly recognized role of the cerebellum in attention switching and network reconfiguration2. This work bridges that gap by integrating volumetric analysis, graph theory, and brain dynamic simulations to uncover structural, topological and dynamic alterations between JS and SP ataxias compared to healthy controls (HC).

Methods

Subjects:16 patients were recruited at Istituto Neurologico “Carlo Besta”: 8 JS (3 females, 21±6 years) and 8 SP (3 females, 20±5 years) and 11 HC (5 females, 21±2 years). 

MRI acquisition: Protocol included (3T Philips Achieva): 

  • 3DT1-weighted imaging (FFE sequence, TE/TR/TI=3.8/8.2/855ms, 1×1×1mm³);
  • Diffusion-weighted imaging (SE-EPI, TE/TR=85/8400ms, 7/32/32 directions per b-value, b=0/1000/2000s/mm², 2.5×2.5×2.5mm³);
  • Resting-state fMRI (GE-EPI, TE/TR=30/2400ms, 200 volumes, 3×3×3mm³).

Clinical/neuropsychological assessment: a comprehensive battery of clinical/neuropsychological assessment was performed on a subset of 19 subjects. Several cognitive domains were evaluated testing intelligence, visuomotor, executive, attentive, and emotional/social functions. 

Data preparation: after preprocessing, an ad-hoc atlas of 146 brain regions was created (cortical: Schaefer, subcortical: FIRST, cerebellar: CerebNet and SUIT), with each region assigned to one of seven functional networks. Analyses focused on SMN and VAN, given their relevance to motor and cognitive functions in ataxia. 

Structural and functional connectivity matrices: Structural (SC) and functional connectivity (FC) matrices were computed for SMN and VAN. SC was estimated via anatomically constrained tractography using multi-shell spherical deconvolution and 30 million streamlines. FC was derived from BOLD signals correlations. Two SC matrices (weight-SC: streamline count; length-SC: streamline length) and two FC matrices (static-FC: average BOLD correlation; dynamic-FC: time-varying BOLD correlation) were generated per network. 

Network analysis: For each network the total normalized volume was computed from 3DT1. Graph theory metrics, including indices of topology (mean edge weight), centrality (density, strength, betweenness centrality and core nodes), integration (global efficiency), and segregation (clustering coefficient), were extracted from weight-SC and static-FC matrices to assess network topology3. Volumes and graph theory metrics were also extracted specifically for cerebellar nodes of each network. 

Neural dynamics were simulated using The Virtual Brain (TVB) framework4, applying the Wong-Wang neural-mass model to each brain region5. Subject-specific SC matrices were used as input, and four key parameters (global coupling, inhibitory feedback, excitatory input, and recurrent excitation) were optimized by comparing simulated static-FC and dynamic-FC with empirical data, estimating the excitation/inhibition network-specific balance. Fig1 shows an overview of how volumetric measures, graph theory indices, and TVB parameters (MRI-derived parameters) were extracted. 

Statistical analysis and machine learning: MRI-derived parameters from SMN and VAN were statistically compared between groups (p<0.05). Only MRI-derived parameters that were significantly different in at least one network were considered for the following analyses. 

To evaluate whether MRI-derived parameters can predict clinical/neuropsychological scores, a stepwise linear regression for each score was performed, selecting those with adjusted-coefficient of determination (R²-adj)>0.6, and normalized root mean square error (NRMSE)<median. 

To assess whether selected combinations of MRI-derived parameters could identify biologically relevant groups within each network, the correspondence between unsupervised clustering and clinical group membership was explored. Following principal component analysis (PCA), the first two principal components were retained for clustering using k-means with the gap statistic method. 

Results

Volumetric alterations: Considering SMN and VAN, group-specific differences emerged, mainly due to the overall involved cerebellar regions. In SMN, SP patients exhibited a reduced overall volume compared to HC (p=0.008). When considering solely the cerebellar regions within the SMN, SP patients exhibited reduced volume compared both to HC and JS patients. In VAN, both JS (p=0.03) and SP patients (p=0.003) showed a reduced overall volume compared to HC. When focusing solely on cerebellar regions within the VAN, SP patients showed smaller volumes compared to both HC and JS (p<0.0001), whereas no significant difference was observed between JS and HC. Therefore, network volume reduction in SMN and VAN was more extensive in SP than JS and was almost entirely explained by changes occurring in the cerebellum. 

Topological alterations: We performed graph theory analysis to quantify changes in network topological organization. Graph theory analyses revealed group differences in SC-weighted matrices for both the SMN and VAN, and in FC matrices for the VAN only. 

In SMN, SP patients showed reduced mean betweenness centrality in SC-weighted matrices compared to both HC (p=0.034) and JS patients (p=0.018), meaning that SMN regions in SP were structurally less central. The same result was confirmed when considering only cerebellar nodes belonging to SMN. 

In VAN, both JS and SP patients showed altered structural organization compared to HC. Specifically, JS patients showed reduced mean edge weight (p=0.0004), strength (p=0.0004), and global efficiency compared to HC (p=0.0008), reflecting a widespread weakening of structural connections. On the other hand, SP patients showed reduced density (p=0.038), indicating that the number of structural connections was reduced but the remaining connections maintained their strength. In addition, both JS (p=0.0008) and SP patients (p<0.008) showed reduced clustering coefficient in the SC-weighted matrices, indicating a loss of network structural segregation. Similar results were observed when considering only the cerebellar nodes in VAN. Indeed, both JS (p=0.007) and SP patients (p=0.0005) showed a reduced clustering coefficient and not only JS (p=0.0009) but also SP patients (p=0.00003) showed reduced strength compared to HC. 

Despite structural alterations in VAN, JS patients showed an increased number of functional core nodes (derived from FC matrix) compared to HC (p=0.01). When considering only the cerebellar nodes in VAN, SP patients showed a decrease in the number of functional core nodes compared to JS (p=0.049). 

Network dynamics alterations: Analysis of TVB parameters revealed group difference in both SMN and VAN networks. In SMN, SP patients showed higher Ji values compared to JS patients (p=0.02), while JS patients showed an increase in G compared to HC (p=0.01) in VAN. 

Statistical analysis and machine learning: Regression analyses identified MRI-derived network parameters that reliably predict clinical and neuropsychological scores. The best performing regression models are summarized in a heatmap (Fig.2A). Notably, two regression models within SMN and fourteen within VAN demonstrated strong predictive power (R2-adj > 0.6 and NRMSE < median). 

PCA was applied to MRI-derived parameters to reduce dimensionality and identify major patterns of variance. PCA was followed by k-means clustering to identify biologically meaningful subgroups. In SMN, the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) explained a total of 62.2% of the variance. In VAN, the first two principal components explained a total of 66.7% of the variance (Fig2B). 

Conclusion

This study provides the first network-level comparison between JS and SP cerebellar ataxias, combining volumetric analysis, graph theory metrics, and brain dynamic simulations. Our results reveal distinct patterns in JS and SP compared to HC, which could explain different clinical profiles. MRI-derived parameters predicted motor and cognitive scores and distinguished the two different forms of cerebellar ataxias.

Somatomotor network alterations underlie motor deficits in slowly progressive ataxia: SP patients showed widespread structural degeneration in the SMN, including reduced volumes in cerebellar regions and decreased structural betweenness centrality. These alterations indicate reduced information transfer across the network and were associated with more severe motor impairment (SARA scores). The increased local inhibitory feedback (Ji) suggests a compensatory mechanism that may help stabilize neural activity despite atrophy. In line with these findings, unsupervised clustering revealed that SP patients share a defined pattern in SMN despite the patient’s heterogeneity. 

Ventral attention network reorganization in Joubert Syndrome: In JS patients, in addition to volume reductions in the VAN, widespread alterations were observed in the graph theory metrics of the structural connectivity. Despite these structural alterations, JS patients showed an increase in the number of functional core nodes compared to HC. This suggests that several VAN regions assume a more central role within the network. In addition, JS patients showed increased global excitatory coupling, indicating hyperconnectivity and hypersynchrony at the network level. These changes may be explained by a compensatory process in which key regions assume a more central role to preserve cognition and attention, reflecting neuroplastic responses aimed at compensating for structural deficits. This compensatory mechanism, observed only in JS subjects, may reflect the greater developmental plasticity associated with the congenital nature of JS, which could support more effective functional reorganization. Consistent with the pronounced alterations observed in JS, unsupervised clustering revealed a well-defined pattern in VAN, with 100% of JS subjects falling into the same cluster together with only one HC subject and three SP patients.

Our findings demonstrate that JS and SP cerebellar ataxias involve distinct network-level mechanisms: SP is primarily associated with SMN degeneration, while JS shows VAN reorganization. MRI-derived parameters reliably predict clinical scores and distinguish patient groups, offering new insights into cerebellar network dysfunction and adaptation.
