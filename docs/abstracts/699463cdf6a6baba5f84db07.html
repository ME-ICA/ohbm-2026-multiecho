Introduction

Schizophrenia is a complex disorder encompassing various symptom clusters such as positive and negative symptoms. While neuroimaging and computational mechanistic investigations have improved our understanding of positive symptoms1, negative symptoms remain poorly understood. Blunted affect, the absence of emotional expression via one’s face or voice, is a core symptom of the negative symptom cluster. A recent hypothesis about negative symptoms proposes that blunted affect may be a consequence of underestimating one’s own ability to influence other people’s affective reactions to one’s own affect2. This hypothesis can be operationalized and tested within an active inference or predictive coding framework, which assumes that agents learn about their environment based on prediction errors (PEs). That is, agents hold a model of the world and strive for that model to be as accurate as possible: If their predictions about sensory inputs coming from the world turn out to be wrong (i.e. the experience a PE), agents update their internal model or change the environment with actions. We took a dimensional perspective of negative symptoms to investigate how social-affective inference is related to diminished affective expressivity in the general population. We additionally tested how PE-updating may change as a function of symptom related distress.

Methods

We invited people from the general population scoring on opposite ends of the constricted affect dimension of the Schizotypal Personality Questionnaire (SPQ3). Participants also filled out the Community Assessment of Psychic [sic!] experiences (CAPE 424), which is used to assess psychotic symptoms in the general population and their clinical relevance, including symptom-related distress. Participants completed two novel trial-by-trial PE-learning tasks: (1) The SAP (Social Affective Prediction) task which operationalizes social-affective PE (saPE)-learning, and (2) the SAPC (SAP Control) task that captures PE-learning in a different context. In the SAP task, participants view one of three different faces each with different inherent probabilities of smiling (p=0.9, p=0.6, or p=0.2) on 120 trials. Participants were instructed that some of the faces were more likely to smile back than others. Participants indicated their prediction by actively smiling at the face or staying neutral, displaying no specific emotion and confirming that prediction with a button press. The sequence of outcomes (whether a face smiles or not) were predetermined and the same for all participants (Figure 1).

The control task (SAPC) used the same predetermined outcome sequence, but instead of predicting smiles, participants predicted whether or not an egg will spoil. We optimized the predetermined outcome sequence of both tasks for parameter recoverability, by simulating a generative model that captures trial-by-trial PE learning, the Hierarchical Gaussian Filter (HGF 5). The HGF captures how uncertainty influences perception and hierarchical PE updating according to predictive coding principles. Participants performed the tasks in the MRI scanner. Cardiac, respiratory, and pupil data were acquired in conjunction with behavioural and MR data.
 Full multimodal data was acquired from 44 participants. Here we present the analysis of the behavioural modeling and physiological data. 

Results

First, we fit a categorical Generalized HGF to the behavioural data of both tasks and analysed the effects of group and tasks on the model’s learning parameters using Bayesian repeated measures ANCOVAs. We estimated a coupling parameter  that describes whether participants used environmental changes during the task to inform their predictions about the different faces (or eggs) in the task. For the SAP task, we found anecdotal evidence (BF10=2.1) for a difference between high and low CA participants (across tasks) for this parameter . High CA participants exhibited lower values on the parameter, indicating that they used the changes of their environment (smiliness of the faces) less compared to low CA participants. Moreover, we found that being more distressed by one’s negative symptoms modulated  values (BF10=7.8) in the SAP task: This suggests that symptom-related distress may result in individuals making less use of the smiliness of their environment when making inferences about receiving smiles from people. 

We also analysed heartrate (HR) responses to (non-model based) PEs in the SAP and the SAPC task and found that high CA participants exhibited lower HRs in response to PEs compared to low CA participants (BF10=5.2) overall. However, there seems to be an absence of evidence for the task*group interaction of HR responses to overall non-model-based task PEs (BF01=3.4) in comparison to the task and group main effects. This suggests that high compared to low CA participants may exhibit slower recovery after anticipatory HR slowing after seeing an anticipated outcome.

Conclusion

These findings suggest that social-affective inference is less flexible in individuals who report distress with their own negative symptoms and that constricted affect is related to different physiological responses to PEs in general. This highlights the role of symptom-related distress in saPE learning and possibly clinical trajectories.

Further, joint modeling of fMRI, behavior and physiology will be pre-registered, building on these first-pass behavioural findings.
