Introduction

Perception is fundamentally predictive: rather than passively encoding input, the brain generates internal models that anticipate sensory events and compare expectations with actual signals. Predictive coding formalizes this framework, proposing that higher-order areas generate top-down predictions while lower-level areas propagate residual mismatches, or prediction errors, to update these models[1]. Despite its theoretical prominence, direct empirical evidence at the human whole-brain level remains limited, largely due to the lack of macroscale models that explicitly separate prediction from error signals in a biologically plausible manner. For instance, most neuroimaging studies rely on static paradigms capturing isolated prediction violations rather than the continuous, hierarchical interplay described by theory.

Here, we address this gap by leveraging PredNet[2], a deep recurrent neural network with architecturally explicit modules for Prediction (Â) and Error (E). To empirically adjudicate between competing formulations, we compared two architectures: (1) the original error-propagation model, where errors ascend the hierarchy to update high-level states, and (2) a modified CNN-like model, where convolved input frames ascend the hierarchy. By combining these models with voxel-wise encoding on naturalistic fMRI data, we aimed to map cortical signatures of predictions versus errors and determine which architectural principle best explains human brain organization.

Methods

To test hierarchical predictive coding in the human brain, we combined biologically inspired deep recurrent networks with naturalistic fMRI. We used two variants of PredNet, a 4-layer network with separate Prediction (Â) and Error (E) units. Both models were pre-trained on the naturalistic KITTI dataset[3], which captures continuous motion statistics from self-driving car videos, and fine-tuned on the movie Titanic to learn longer temporal dependencies. The models were then tested on Forrest Gump[4], which also served as the stimulus for the StudyForrest fMRI dataset (N = 15). Time-resolved features from Prediction and Error units across layers (L1–L4) were aligned with voxel-wise BOLD signals. Voxel-wise ridge regression quantified (1) beta weights, reflecting the direction and magnitude of model-driven modulation, and (2) encoding performance, indexing the information content captured by each feature set.

Results

We first visualized PredNet’s temporal predictions. At short horizons (40 ms), predicted frames closely matched fine visual details, while longer horizons (1–2 s) preserved semantic gist but blurred high-frequency features. Error signals increased sharply at scene transitions, showing how the network detects surprising events in continuous input (Fig. 2A). These results confirm PredNet captures temporal dependencies and produces interpretable predictions and errors before neural alignment. 

Building on this, we mapped Prediction (Â) and Error (E) signals across the cortex to test hierarchical predictive coding. A clear dissociation emerged between the two streams. Prediction signals showed a hierarchy-dependent inversion: at Layer 1, predictions positively correlated with activity across visual and higher-order networks, whereas at Layer 4 they exhibited strong negative beta weights in early visual cortex (V1) despite high encoding performance, consistent with “explaining away,” while frontal and parietal source regions remained positively engaged (Fig. 2B). In higher-order regions such as the DMN, activity was stronger at lower layers but diminished at higher layers, reflecting top-down prediction: successful high-level predictions reduce residual errors, leading to suppression at higher layers, while unpredicted information persists at lower layers.

In contrast, Error signals acted as brain-wide “teaching signals,” showing uniformly positive beta weights across the cortex from Layer 1 to 4, consistent with global broadcasting of surprise to support rapid updating. Critically, this organization was architecture-dependent. In the Modified CNN-like model, where inputs rather than errors ascend the hierarchy, the “explaining away” signature vanished: Layer 4 no longer suppressed V1 and instead showed nonspecific excitation (Fig. 2C). These results demonstrate that hierarchical depth alone is insufficient; explicit error propagation is required for the brain’s top-down suppressive mechanism.

Conclusion

This study provides the first direct visualization of hierarchical prediction and error signals in the human brain during naturalistic vision. By leveraging deep generative networks with architecturally explicit modules, we bridge computational theory with biological measurement in ways that conventional paradigms cannot achieve. Our findings suggest that the brain implements error-propagation predictive coding—not merely hierarchical feature extraction. The architectural specificity of the explaining away signature, combined with the unexpected brain-wide distribution of error signals, refines our understanding of how prediction and error computations are organized in cortex. More broadly, this work demonstrates that manipulable deep generative models can serve as tools for probing neural computation—varying architecture, timescale, and information flow to isolate principles and test their biological validity. This framework offers a path toward mechanistic understanding of how the brain constructs perception from continuous sensory experience.
