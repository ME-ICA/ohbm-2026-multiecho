Introduction

Structural connectivity derived from diffusion MRI provides a quantitative description of the brain’s anatomical organization. Network measures based on fractional anisotropy (FA) and streamline count (SC) are widely used in developmental and clinical neuroimaging and are often treated as stable biomarkers. However, their interpretability critically depends on test–retest reliability, as insufficient reliability can obscure true neurobiological signal and compromise downstream inference.

Previous studies have shown that the reliability of structural connectivity is heterogeneous and sensitive to analytic choices, particularly thresholding strategies. Thresholding is commonly applied to remove weak connections, yet it can substantially alter network topology and may differentially affect reliability across metrics, hemispheres, and brain systems. Most prior work evaluates reliability at a single scale, leaving its behavior across thresholds insufficiently characterized.

Persistent homology (PH) has emerged as a topological framework for capturing multiscale features of brain networks, such as backbone structure and cycles, that are not well described by conventional graph metrics. Despite its promise, the reliability of PH-based structural connectivity measures remains largely unexplored. In this study, we evaluate test–retest reliability of FA- and SC-based structural connectivity across thresholds, analytic scales, and functional brain networks in a developmental cohort, aiming to identify robust representations of brain structural organization.

We evaluate test–retest reliability of structural connectivity in the Human Connectome Project Development cohort. We examine FA- and SC-based network measures across hemispheres and thresholds, assess the reliability of PH-based topological features, and quantify consistency of structural connectivity among twelve canonical functional networks..

Methods

Data were obtained from the Human Connectome Project Development (HCP-D) cohort, including participants with repeated diffusion MRI scans. Structural connectivity matrices were constructed using diffusion-derived measures of fractional anisotropy (FA) and streamline count (SC). Brain regions of interest (ROIs) were defined using a standard cortical parcellation and were further grouped into twelve canonical functional networks. Analyses were conducted separately for the left and right hemispheres as well as for the whole brain.

For network-based structural connectivity, FA- and SC-weighted adjacency matrices were generated for each subject and scan session. A series of proportional thresholds was applied to each matrix to retain varying levels of connection density, allowing assessment of how test–retest reliability changes as weaker connections are removed. For each thresholded network, graph-theoretic metrics including network density, clustering coefficient, and global efficiency were computed.

Test–retest reliability of network metrics was quantified using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) based on a two-way random-effects model with absolute agreement and single-measure definition. ICC values were calculated separately for each hemisphere and across thresholds to characterize reliability patterns under different analytic conditions.

Persistent homology (PH) was applied to both SC- and FA-based structural connectivity matrices to capture multiscale topological properties of brain networks. From the resulting persistence diagrams, three summary features were extracted: backbone strength, backbone dispersion, and cycle strength. Test–retest reliability of these PH-based features was assessed using ICC with 95% confidence intervals.

For functional network–level analyses, pairwise structural connectivity among the twelve functional networks was evaluated. For SC-based connectivity matrices, overall test–retest consistency was quantified using the image intraclass correlation coefficient (I2C2), providing a global measure of matrix-level reliability for each hemisphere. For FA-based connectivity, where missing values were prevalent, I2C2 was not applicable. Instead, zero-valued connections were treated as missing, lower-triangular ROI–ROI edges were extracted and stacked across subjects, and edge-wise ICC values were computed after excluding missing data. This procedure was repeated across coverage thresholds ranging from 0.4 to 0.9 to examine threshold-dependent reliability patterns within and between functional networks.

Results

Network-based analyses revealed distinct test–retest reliability patterns across connectivity modalities, metrics, and thresholds. For FA-based structural connectivity, intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) varied systematically with thresholding and hemisphere. Network density and global efficiency exhibited consistently high ICC values across thresholds, whereas clustering coefficient showed greater sensitivity to thresholding, with reliability decreasing as thresholds increased. Similar patterns were observed across left, right, and whole-brain analyses, with minor hemispheric differences.

For SC-based networks, density and efficiency demonstrated high and stable reliability across thresholds, while clustering coefficient showed a pronounced decline in ICC as weaker connections were removed. This effect was consistent across hemispheres and was more pronounced at higher thresholds, indicating that clustering metrics are particularly sensitive to thresholding decisions in streamline-based networks.

Persistent homology (PH)–based features demonstrated moderate to high test–retest reliability for both SC- and FA-derived connectivity. For SC-based PH measures, backbone dispersion showed the highest reliability (ICC = 0.924, 95% CI [0.911, 0.935]), followed by backbone strength (ICC = 0.770, 95% CI [0.736, 0.800]) and cycle strength (ICC = 0.708, 95% CI [0.667, 0.745]). For FA-based PH measures, backbone strength exhibited high reliability (ICC = 0.924, 95% CI [0.847, 0.955]), while backbone dispersion and cycle strength showed moderate reliability.

At the functional network level, SC-based connectivity among twelve canonical functional networks demonstrated excellent overall test–retest consistency. The image intraclass correlation coefficient (I2C2) was 0.9292 for the whole brain, with similarly high values for the left (0.9310) and right (0.9275) hemispheres. Most network pairs exhibited high reliability, although connections involving the somatomotor and orbitofrontal-affective networks showed comparatively lower consistency.

For FA-based functional network connectivity, edge-wise ICC analyses revealed increasing reliability with higher coverage thresholds for both within-network and between-network connections. However, certain between-network connections, particularly those involving the orbitofrontal-affective and somatomotor networks, were eliminated at higher thresholds, resulting in missing values and indicating consistently weak or unstable connections across sessions. Together, these results highlight modality- and scale-dependent reliability patterns in structural connectivity and demonstrate the utility of multiscale and network-level analyses.

Conclusion

This study presents a comprehensive and multilevel evaluation of test–retest reliability in structural brain connectivity using diffusion MRI data from a developmental cohort. By jointly examining conventional network-based metrics, persistent homology–based topological features, and functional network–level connectivity, we demonstrate that reliability is a structured and non-uniform property of the structural connectome, rather than a fixed characteristic of the data.

Across network-based analyses, reliability patterns differed substantially by metric and modality. Network density and global efficiency consistently exhibited high stability across hemispheres and thresholds, indicating that global summaries of connectivity are relatively robust to analytic variation. In contrast, clustering-related measures showed pronounced sensitivity to thresholding, particularly in streamline-based networks, with reliability decreasing as weaker connections were progressively removed. These findings highlight that commonly reported graph-theoretic metrics can differ markedly in reproducibility even when derived from identical underlying data, emphasizing the need for metric-specific reliability assessment.

Persistent homology–based features demonstrated moderate to high test–retest reliability across both FA- and SC-derived connectivity, supporting their utility as multiscale descriptors of structural organization. Notably, backbone-related features were more reliable than cycle-based measures, suggesting that core network structure is more reproducible across sessions than higher-order cyclic topology. This distinction provides insight into which topological properties are most stable and informative in developmental structural connectomes.

At the functional network level, SC-based structural connectivity among twelve canonical brain systems exhibited excellent overall reproducibility across hemispheres, indicating that large-scale system-level organization is highly stable. However, specific network pairs, particularly those involving somatomotor and orbitofrontal-affective systems, consistently showed lower reliability or were eliminated under stricter thresholding. These patterns suggest the presence of connections that are both biologically weak and methodologically vulnerable, and caution against overinterpretation of such edges in developmental studies.

Together, these results underscore the importance of explicitly incorporating reliability considerations into the construction and interpretation of structural connectomes. By systematically characterizing how reliability varies with connectivity modality, analytic scale, and thresholding strategy, this work provides practical guidance for selecting robust connectivity representations and supports the integration of topological and network-level approaches for reproducible and interpretable neuroimaging research.
