Introduction

Hemispheric specialization is a core principle of human brain organization1, yet the extent to which lateralization in one cognitive domain constrains the organization of others remains debated. Two dominant accounts offer competing predictions: the causal hypothesis2, in which dominance for one function induces complementary dominance in another through developmental or computational pressures3; and the statistical hypothesis4, which posits that each function lateralizes independently, with cross-domain correlations emerging only probabilistically at the population level. Disentangling these views requires multimodal, multidomain assessment of hemispheric organization within individuals.

Methods

We combined task-based and resting-state fMRI in 287 participants from the BIL&GIN cohort to test whether language lateralization phenotype (typical left-dominant vs. atypical right-dominant) predicts hemispheric asymmetries in visuospatial attention and numerical cognition. Network-level asymmetry was quantified using two validated domain-specific atlases: ALANs5 (Atlas for the Lateralized visuospatial Attention Networks, Fig. 1a) for line bisection judgments and LUCA (Lateralized Underpinnings of Comparison and Arithmetic, Fig. 1b) for arithmetic calculation and numerical interval comparison. Weighted BOLD asymmetry indices were extracted for 11 lateralized networks and modeled using linear regression controlling for demographics, manual preference, intracranial volume, and response hand. We further assessed whether language phenotype influences intrinsic connectivity (degree centrality asymmetry, degree centrality sum, and homotopic connectivity) within the same networks.

Results

Language phenotype significantly affected task-evoked asymmetry in three ALANs networks (Fig. 2, Top Row). In the parieto-frontal network (pFDR=0.008, η2p=0.031), typical individuals showed stronger rightward asymmetry (μ=1.73, CI95%=[1.38, 2.08]) than atypical individuals (μ=0.72, CI95%= [0.01, 1.43]). The temporo-frontal network showed a robust effect (pFDR=0.0005, η2p=0.054), with typical individuals right-lateralized (μ=0.93, CI95%= [0.76, 1.10]) and atypical individuals bilateral (μ=0.26, CI95%=[-0.09, 0.61]). The visual network displayed a weaker but significant effect (pFDR=0.02, η2p=0.021). In all significant ALANs network, atypical individuals did not show leftward reversal but reduced asymmetry.

In numerical cognition, phenotype significantly influenced four LUCA networks (Fig. 2, Middle and Bottom Rows). The calculation fronto-intraparietal network showed a strong effect (pFDR=0.0002, η2p=0.068), with typical individuals showing leftward asymmetry (μ=0.31, CI = [0.24, 0.37]) and atypical individuals bilateral again (μ=0.05, CI = [-0.08, 0.18]). Significant effects also emerged in the visuomotor, subcortical calculation, and comparison fronto-intraparietal networks (all pFDR=0.04), again reflecting reduced, but not inverted, asymmetry in atypical individuals. No handedness effects or interactions were found.

In contrast to task fMRI, resting-state analyses showed no significant phenotype effects across any ALANs or LUCA networks (all pFDR greater than 0.05, η2p less than or equal to 0.017). Degree centrality asymmetry, degree centrality sum, and homotopic connectivity showed overlapping distributions between typical and atypical groups.

Conclusion

Our results challenge a global reciprocal model in which atypical language dominance yields mirror-reversed organization. Instead, they support a graded coupling account: lateralization magnitudes covary across domains, whereas asymmetry direction and intrinsic connectivity remain largely independent. Typical language dominance is associated with amplified attentional and numerical asymmetries, whereas atypical dominance reflects reduced, not reversed, asymmetry. The absence of resting-state effects suggests cross-domain relationships emerge during active processing rather than from intrinsic architecture, supporting a multidomain framework in which hemispheric specialization arises from partially independent, domain-specific asymmetry processes.
