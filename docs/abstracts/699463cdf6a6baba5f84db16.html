Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) presents significant heterogeneity in both its clinical manifestations and underlying neurobiology. This variability challenges the effectiveness of one-size-fits-all treatment approaches, as current diagnostic frameworks, which rely solely on symptoms, fail to capture the varied biological mechanisms driving the disorder. Although alterations in cortical thickness (CT) have been widely reported in MDD, inconsistent findings likely indicate the presence of distinct neurobiological subtypes within the same diagnostic category1. Converging evidence suggests that inflammation, early adverse events, and metabolic dysfunctions may define unique MDD subtypes, potentially linked to unique brain profiles2. These pathophysiological mechanisms are believed to be mediated by specific neurotransmitter systems, with changes in dopaminergic signaling influencing immuno-metabolic, homeostatic, and mood dysregulations3. However, the replicability and generalizability of brain-based stratification approaches is still open to question, particularly across independent clinical populations. Addressing these gaps, we applied a data-driven clustering approach to investigate whether brain-based clusters of depressed patients in a large community sample can be replicated internally and externally in an independent clinical cohort. The identified clusters were then characterized for clinical, inflammatory, and neurotransmitter receptor signatures to advance precision psychiatry.

Methods

We investigated brain-based subtypes in 1,531 individuals with MDD from the UK Biobank (discovery cohort), with external validation in 144 currently depressed MDD inpatients from IRCCS Ospedale San Raffaele (HSR) (validation cohort). In the UK Biobank cohort, MDD was defined as meeting at least one of the following criteria: i) International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10 codes for MDD (F32-F33); ii) at least one diagnostic code for a depressive disorder in primary care records. To ensure participants were experiencing a depressive episode at the time of scanning, we selected individuals who scored > 0 on the "Frequency of depressed mood in the last two weeks" item from the Recent Depressive Symptoms scale (RDS-4). Regarding HSR cohort, severity of depression was assessed through the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS). While brain-derived measures were already extracted in the UK Biobank, the T1-weighted images of HSR cohort were preprocessed following the UK Biobank pipeline to ensure cross-cohort comparability, and regional CT values were extracted from 62 regions of interest based on the Desikan-Killiany-Tourville atlas using FreeSurfer. We applied a stability-based relative clustering validation approach (NeuReval Python package, https://github.com/fede-colombo/NeuReval) in the UK Biobank cohort using a 55-45 training-validation split, employing K-means as clustering algorithm and support vector machine (SVM) as classifier4. Within the training set (N=842), we implemented a 100×2 repeated cross-validation, adjusting for age, sex, age-by-sex interactions, and age squared as confounds within the cross-validation scheme. SVM hyperparameters were optimized via grid search. This process was repeated for 2-4 clusters, selecting the solution with minimum normalized stability across cross-validation iterations. Statistical significance was assessed through 10,000 Monte Carlo simulations using sigclust R package. The optimal clustering solution from the training set was applied to the UK Biobank test set (N=689), with the trained SVM predicting cluster labels in this set. Linear models and Chi-square tests were used to profile clusters in terms of neuroimaging, socio-demographic variables, organic comorbidities, clinically relevant characteristics (e.g., severity of depression, exposure to childhood trauma through the Childhood Trauma Screener [CTS-5], and treatment resistance), and markers of systemic inflammation, with standardized effect sizes (Cohen's d) computed. For neuroimaging, multiple comparisons were adjusted with False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction. For each set, we characterized molecular signatures by fitting multiple linear regression models to predict cluster-specific CT effect sizes (Cohen's d) from the spatial distribution of 19 neurotransmitter receptors and transporters obtained from positron emission tomography (PET) imaging studies, accessed via the neuromaps toolbox. The models were cross-validated with a distance-dependent method and statistical significance was assessed through spin tests (10,000 repetitions). In addition, dominance analysis was performed to assess the contribution of each input variable to each model5. Finally, we tested external generalizability by applying the trained and optimized stratification model to the HSR cohort (gold-standard quantitative validation), similarly characterizing clusters for neuroimaging, clinical, socio-demographic, inflammatory, and molecular signatures (Figure 1).

Results

Using the UK Biobank training set, the stability-based relative clustering validation identified a 2-cluster solution as optimal, achieving 87.5% cross-validation accuracy (p<0.001). Cluster 1 (N=386) patients exhibited widespread cortical thinning compared to Cluster 2 (N=456) (pFDR<0.001, d=0.48-1.65) and were characterized by significantly higher depression severity (RDS-4) (Tenseness: p=0.005; Unenthusiasm: p=0.022) and emotional abuse (p=0.018), elevated monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR) (p=0.02), and increased prevalence of diabetes (p=0.03) (Figure 2a, d). This model demonstrated high generalization performance in the UK Biobank test set (accuracy=96.5%, p<0.001; Cluster 1: N=279, Cluster 2: N=410), replicating a cluster distinguished by reduced CT (pFDR<0.001, d=0.38-1.60), greater depression severity (p=0.018), childhood trauma exposure (Emotional neglect: p=0.008; Sexual abuse: p=0.017), systemic inflammation index (SII) (p=0.009) and platelets-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) (p=0.030), and diabetes comorbidity (p=0.003) (Figure 2b, d). Dominance analysis revealed that dopamine transporter (DAT) density was the strongest contributor to cluster-specific cortical patterns in both sets (Training set: R2adj= 0.73, pspin <0.001; Test set: R2adj= 0.76, pspin <0.001) (Figure 2e). External validation in the HSR cohort replicated the 2-cluster structure with 80.6% accuracy (p=0.002; Cluster 1: N=116, Cluster 2: N=28). One cluster was characterized by lower CT (pFDR<0.001, d=0.32-3.03), older age (p=0.018), earlier age of onset (p=0.038), and a trend toward significance for treatment resistance (p=0.05) (Figure 2c, d). Notably, these clusters exhibited a distinct molecular signature, with metabotropic glutamate receptor 5 (mGluR5) emerging as the primary contributor to clusters’ cortical profiles (R2adj= 0.70, pspin <0.001) (Figure 2e).

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that a data-driven, brain-based stratification approach can identify MDD subtypes with partially replicable features across independent cohorts. This stratification unveiled a severe subtype characterized by widespread cortical thinning, alongside a milder subtype with relatively preserved cortical integrity. These subtypes replicated with high accuracy in the UK Biobank test set (96.5%), whereas the gold-standard quantitative validation in the HSR cohort showed moderate generalization (80.6%), revealing meaningful population-specific differences. While the cortical thinning pattern replicated structurally across cohorts, the associated clinical-biological profiles diverged substantially. In the UK Biobank community sample, cortical thinning co-occurred with elevated inflammation, childhood trauma, and metabolic comorbidity, consistently validated in the internal test set. In the HSR inpatient cohort, which comprised severely ill, treatment-seeking individuals, cortical thinning associated with treatment resistance, earlier onset, and older age, without an immuno-metabolic signature. To explore the neurobiological bases of these population-specific differences, we examined associations between cluster-specific cortical patterns and neurotransmitter receptors and transporters distributions. Distinct molecular signatures emerged: dopamine transporter (DAT) explained cortical patterns in UK Biobank, while metabotropic glutamate receptor 5 (mGluR5) dominated in HSR. This suggests that cortical thinning in a subtype of MDD patients may arise from distinct etiological pathways shaped by population characteristics, offering a potential explanation for divergent clinical profiles and only moderate cross-cohort generalization despite structural replication. This framework has important implications for precision psychiatry, suggesting that stratification models with strong within-cohort validation may not generalize straightforwardly to external populations. The moderate cross-cohort generalization accuracy (80.6%) combined with divergent clinical and molecular profiles highlights that factor like illness severity, treatment exposure, and sampling characteristics substantially influence subtype manifestation. In conclusion, this study provides a reproducible framework for parsing MDD heterogeneity, emphasizing that reliable stratification models for precision psychiatry must consider population-specific factors and integrate multilevel information to enable targeted interventions tailored to individual clinical contexts.
