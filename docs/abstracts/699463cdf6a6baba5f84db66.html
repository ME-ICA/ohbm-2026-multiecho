Introduction

The hallmark amyloid and tau pathologies of Alzheimer's disease (AD) can accumulate for decades before cognitive decline emerges. However, subsequent cognitive trajectories are heterogeneous with some individuals showing remarkable resilience despite substantial pathological load1. This resilience has been linked to large-scale network features2, yet the neurobiological mechanisms underpinning these protective network configurations remain unclear.

The locus coeruleus noradrenergic (LC-NA) system provides widespread neuromodulatory control over the brain's large-scale network architecture, particularly the frontoparietal and attention networks which play pivotal roles in top-down cognitive control and bottom-up reorienting3. Accordingly, lower LC functional connectivity (LC-FC) to these networks is linked to greater distractibility and worse memory in older individuals. Beyond these broader cognitive roles, accumulating evidence directly links the LC-NA system to resilience. Individuals with elevated AD pathology yet higher LC integrity, novelty-related activity, metabolism, and catecholamine synthesis capacity show relative preservation of cognition. Animal models support a causal role, with selective loss of noradrenaline exacerbating cognitive deficits that are partly rescued by LC-activation or noradrenergic replacement. Thus, variability in the LC-NA system confers resilience or risk to pathology-related cognitive decline. This variability may also interact with established risk factors, with females and ε4+ carriers facing heightened vulnerability to cognitive decline4. 

Here, we examined whether multi-scale LC-FC is associated with cognitive trajectories in 393 cognitively unimpaired amyloid-positive individuals. We hypothesized that greater LC-FC, particularly to frontoparietal control and attentional networks, would be associated with more stable cognitive trajectories, especially in individuals with greater vulnerability to amyloid-related decline.

Methods

We analyzed data from 393 cognitively unimpaired, amyloid-positive (Aβ+; centiloid ≥19) individuals aged 65-85 from the A4 study5. All participants had longitudinal Preclinical Alzheimer Cognitive Composite (PACC) data over a mean follow-up of 5.5±2.5 years (mean 14.3±6.0 visits). PACC is a continuous measure weighted towards episodic memory and designed to be sensitive to cognitive decline in preclinical AD. 

Aβ pathology was assessed using 18F-Florbetapir PET performed during pre-randomization screening. We utilized the mean standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) across a neocortical composite region with a whole cerebellar reference as previously described. SUVR’s were converted to the centiloid scale.

3D T1-weighted structural and resting-state fMRI scans were acquired at baseline. Functional images underwent slice-time correction, motion correction, and spatial normalization to 2mm MNI152 space using ANTs non-linear registration (which demonstrates reduced brainstem registration error compared to alternatives). We applied ICA-AROMA for motion artifact removal and regressed nuisance signals (white matter, CSF, fourth ventricle, and motion parameters). Fourth ventricle signal removal is crucial for accurate LC connectivity estimates. High-pass temporal filtering (0.009 Hz) was applied without spatial smoothing to preserve signal specificity within the small LC region.

Brain regions were defined using the Schaefer-Tian 232 atlas (200 cortical, 32 subcortical regions). The LC was defined using a validated post-mortem template, dilated to preserve its anatomical shape when resampling to 2mm resolution. We extracted baseline LC-FC at three scales: (i) global LC-FC (average connectivity across all regions), (ii) network LC-FC (average connectivity to each Yeo17 network), and (iii) parcelwise LC-FC (connectivity to each parcel). This approach captures both the LC's widespread broadcast neuromodulation (global) and its specific connectivity patterns with critical networks (network/parcel).

NeuroCombat harmonization removed inter-scanner variability whilst preserving biological variability across 22 scanner sites. Age, sex, education, APOE status, Aβ, and PACC subcomponents were preserved during harmonization.

Linear mixed-effects models examined associations between multi-scale LC-FC and cognitive trajectories within Aβ+ individuals. All models included random intercepts and slopes for time, with natural cubic splines (2 degrees of freedom) to capture non-linear temporal changes in PACC. Core covariates included education, cumulative treatment dose, framewise displacement, plus age and sex interacted with time.

We used likelihood ratio tests comparing nested models to test whether: (1) LC-FC moderates cognitive trajectories (LC-FC⨯time), (2) LC-FC moderates the relationship between Aβ and cognitive trajectories (LC-FC⨯Aβ⨯time), (3) effects differ by sex (LC-FC⨯sex⨯time), (4) effects differ by ε4 status (LC-FC⨯ε4⨯time), and (5) effects differ by sex within ε4 carriers (LC-FC⨯sex⨯ε4×time). False discovery rate correction controlled for multiple comparisons across spatial units within models. Network and parcelwise models are displayed using -log10(p) values signed based on the directionality of the second spline term which captures the latter years (past the median timepoint) of the cognitive trajectories and is thus more sensitive to accelerating cognitive decline. 

We tested robustness across different parcellation granularities (400-parcel, Yeo-7 networks), examined tissue-independence by controlling for cortical thickness and weighted degree, and confirmed LC-specificity using an anatomically-shifted control mask in the anterior pons.

Results

Data were available for 393 Aβ+ participants (age 71.6±4.7 years; 59.8% female; education 16.7±2.9 years; 58.0% APOE ε4+) with longitudinal PACC data over 5.5±2.5 years (13.4±6.7 visits).

Higher global LC-FC was associated with attenuated PACC decline (χ²=8.65, df=2, p=0.013), though this didn't survive FDR correction (pFDR=0.208; Fig.2a). At the network level, this was driven by stronger LC connectivity to frontoparietal control (ContA: χ²=22.72, pFDR<0.001) and somatomotor (SomMotB: χ²=13.73, pFDR=0.033) networks. Across parcels, 20/232 regions showed significant effects (pFDR<0.05), spanning frontoparietal, attentional, somatomotor, and insular regions. Critically, higher global LC-FC was most protective against cognitive decline in individuals with greater amyloid burden (χ²=20.39, df=2, pFDR=0.001; Fig.2b). This effect was strongest in frontoparietal control (ContA: χ²=24.42, pFDR<0.001), dorsal attention (DorsAttnA: χ²=15.01, pFDR=0.012), ventral attention (SalVentAttnA: χ²=13.15, pFDR=0.018), somatomotor (SomMotA: χ²=11.87, pFDR=0.028), and temporoparietal (TempPar: χ²=14.07, pFDR=0.014) networks. At the parcelwise level, 28/232 regions spanning lateral and medial frontal, temporal, parietal, and somatomotor cortices showed significant effects (pFDR<0.05).

These effects were further modified by sex and ε4 status. Higher LC-FC was associated with less cognitive decline in ε4+ carriers (global: χ²=14.20, pFDR=0.018; Fig.2d), with strongest effects in control (ContC: χ²=13.54, pFDR=0.018) and visual (VisCent: χ²=13.78, pFDR=0.018; VisPeri: χ²=20.87, pFDR=0.002) networks, with 23/232 significant parcels. This effect remained significant when controlling for Aβ. While we observed a trend toward LC-FC being more protective in females (χ²=6.16, p=0.046; Fig. 2c), this didn't survive FDR correction. However, when combining these risk factors, LC-mediated resilience was most pronounced in female ε4+ carriers (global: χ²=22.08, pFDR=0.037; Fig.2e), particularly within visual networks (VisPeri: χ²=26.60, pFDR=0.011), with 9/232 significant parcels. 

 

Results were robust across different network partitions (Yeo-7) and parcellation granularities (400-parcel). Controlling for local cortical thickness and weighted degree had minimal impact on our results, with only a few (6/28) regions losing significance in the LC-FC⨯Aβ⨯time model. The anatomically-shifted control mask revealed no significant global or network effects for any model. At the parcelwise level, a small number of scattered regions showed significant effects; however, these were minimal and showed zero spatial overlap with our primary LC findings. Notably, for our principal finding (the interaction between LC-FC and amyloid burden on cognitive decline) the shifted control analysis yielded no significant parcels.

Conclusion

These findings demonstrate that individuals with preclinical AD who have stronger LC functional connectivity exhibit resilience against cognitive decline, with this protection most pronounced in those at greatest risk. This provides an upstream neuromodulatory basis for prior work linking intrinsic cortical network connectivity to cognitive resilience2 and suggests that the LC's beneficial effects are conferred through its modulation of distributed brain networks subserving adaptive goal-directed behavior.

The resilience associated with LC-FC was not uniform across our cohort. LC-FC's protective effect was strongest in individuals with high amyloid burden, fulfilling the definition of a functional cognitive reserve mechanism1: the LC modulates the deleterious relationship between pathology and cognition. We also observed similar, albeit weaker, effects for ε4 status and sex, with the most pronounced protection in female ε4+ carriers. This subgroup faces the highest vulnerability4, potentially due to greater dysfunction of cortical networks. Consequently, they may be disproportionately reliant on the LC to recruit compensatory resources as cortical systems falter. Individual differences in LC system robustness may stem from upstream environmental factors. Higher educational and occupational attainment has been linked to greater LC integrity, whilst early life stress can induce lasting LC dysregulation. Together, these findings frame the LC as a crucial nexus where lifelong environmental exposures and genetic risk factors converge to shape an individual's capacity for cognitive resilience.

Beyond its global effects, LC-mediated resilience was driven by connectivity to networks critical for adaptive behavior: frontoparietal control, attentional, and somatomotor networks. This provides a neuromodulatory basis for prior findings linking cortical network connectivity to resilience and aligns with foundational theories of LC function. The LC regulates neural gain, selectively amplifying salient representations and allowing flexible reconfiguration of brain states to meet cognitive demands3. Even during rest, LC-FC captures individual differences in the capacity to modulate networks and maintain their functionality despite AD pathology. In ε4+ carriers, LC-FC resilience additionally involved visual networks. Given that brief phasic-like LC stimulation preferentially activates lower-order sensory-motor regions, higher occipital LC-FC in resilient ε4+ carriers may reflect preserved phasic-dominant firing modes.

The multifactorial nature of AD suggests combinatorial intervention strategies may be necessary to optimally modify disease progression. Our findings indicate that an individual's endogenous LC-FC could serve as a marker to identify high-risk amyloid-positive individuals who may benefit most from therapeutic interventions targeting the LC-NA system. Treatments that act partially on this system, such as atomoxetine and transcutaneous vagal nerve stimulation, have shown promise in patients with mild cognitive impairment. Our work suggests that noradrenergic interventions in the preclinical stages, alongside other therapies or alone, may bolster resilience by maintaining network functionality to preserve cognition in high-risk individuals.

More broadly, these findings highlight the importance of considering the brain's neuromodulatory systems in preclinical AD. By shifting focus from cortical regions to the ascending systems that regulate them, we open new avenues for both understanding and intervening in the earliest stages of disease. The observation that protective effects were most pronounced in those at greatest risk suggests precision targeting of interventions may offer the most effective path forward for maintaining cognitive function in preclinical Alzheimer's disease.
