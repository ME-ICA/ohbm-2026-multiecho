Introduction

Neuronal avalanches provide an event-based description of brain activity, in which scale-free bursts of activation propagate across distributed networks and complement conventional oscillatory connectivity measures.¹ These cascades have been observed in invasive preparations and in human magnetoencephalography (MEG), where they reveal reproducible spatiotemporal motifs at rest.² Because they naturally capture fast, aperiodic fluctuations, avalanches are emerging as mechanistically grounded markers of large-scale network organisation and as candidate features for brain–computer interfaces (BCIs).³, Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a prototypical neurodegenerative disease with prominent network-level disruption, in which alterations of fast cortical dynamics and avalanche spreading have been reported.⁴ Sensitive, non-invasive biomarkers capable of tracking such reorganisation at the single-subject level are needed for monitoring disease progression and for adaptive BCIs.³,

Avalanche-based connectivity is typically quantified using the Avalanche Transition Matrix (ATM), which counts time-lagged co-activations between regions but does not explicitly encode transition probabilities and treats all events equally, regardless of duration. This may increase susceptibility to brief, noise-dominated activations and limit suitability for real-time applications. Building on recent work that demonstrates the stability and behavioural relevance of avalanche fingerprints in humans,³, we introduce the Weighted-Stochastic Avalanche Transition Matrix (ws-ATM), a Markov-based reformulation that embeds avalanche propagation into a probabilistic framework while accounting for activation and avalanche duration. We hypothesised that ws-ATM would provide a more stable and sample-efficient characterisation of whole-brain dynamics than the original ATM and would enhance the detection of ALS-related network alterations that align with clinical impairment.

Methods

We analysed resting-state MEG recordings from 39 patients with clinically definite or probable ALS and 39 age- and education-matched healthy controls. Participants underwent two eyes-closed runs (3.5 min each) acquired with a 154-channel whole-head system. Data were band-pass filtered (0.5–48 Hz), denoised using principal component analysis to suppress environmental artefacts, and cleaned with independent component analysis to remove electrocardiographic and ocular contributions. After artefact rejection, runs were concatenated to obtain, for each subject, 122.8 s of continuous MEG. Sensor-level data were co-registered to individual structural MRI when available, or to a standard template otherwise, and source reconstructed with a linearly constrained minimum variance beamformer using a realistic volume-conduction model. Time series were extracted from 78 cortical regions of interest defined by a modified Automated Anatomical Labeling atlas, excluding cerebellar and subcortical structures because of limited MEG sensitivity to deep generators.

Neuronal avalanches were identified by z-scoring each regional signal and thresholding supra-threshold excursions. An avalanche was defined as a contiguous sequence of time bins in which at least one region exceeded the threshold, bounded by bins with no supra-threshold activity. Based on empirical deviations of the signal distribution from Gaussian noise and anatomical constraints on interhemispheric conduction delays, we fixed the avalanche threshold at 4 standard deviations and imposed a minimum duration, thereby discarding ultra-brief events likely dominated by artefacts. This procedure yielded, for each subject, a set of binary spatiotemporal patterns encoding avalanche onset, spread and termination.

For each avalanche, we computed both the conventional ATM and the proposed ws-ATM. In the original formulation, the ATM entry Aᵢⱼ reflects the frequency with which region j is active at the subsequent time bin given that region i was active at the current bin; avalanche-specific matrices are averaged to obtain a subject-level ATM. In ws-ATM, we reinterpret avalanche evolution as a Markov process: time-shifted co-activations between regions are accumulated while explicitly tracking the number of consecutive co-active bins, such that longer co-activations contribute proportionally more than short ones. For each starting region, outgoing counts are normalised to sum to one, producing a row-stochastic transition matrix that encodes both the strength and persistence of propagation pathways. A ws-ATM is computed per avalanche and then combined into a single subject-level representation via length-weighted averaging, assigning larger weights to longer avalanches.

We first assessed the robustness and convergence properties of ws-ATM versus ATM in healthy controls by progressively removing avalanches from the time series, recomputing subject-level matrices at each removal step, and quantifying the deviation from the full-data matrix with an L1 edge-wise distance. Group-level trajectories and between-method differences were compared using paired t-tests with false-discovery-rate correction. The same procedure was repeated in ALS and with avalanches removed at random positions to test robustness. We then evaluated clinical relevance by performing, for each matrix formulation, edge-wise group comparisons between ALS and controls (two-sample t-tests) and correlating edge weights in ALS with ALS Functional Rating Scale–Revised scores using Spearman’s rank correlation. Finally, for each edge, we examined the concordance between the sign of ALS–control differences and the direction of clinical correlations, and we projected edge-level results onto regions by counting, for each node, the number of incident significant edges, yielding a nodal summary of disease-related reorganisation.

Results

Avalanche truncation analyses revealed marked differences in stability between ws-ATM and the conventional ATM. In healthy controls, ws-ATM converged rapidly: as avalanches were progressively removed, L1 distances from the full-length matrix increased slowly and almost linearly, remaining modest even when roughly half of all events were discarded. Inter-subject variability of ws-ATM entries also remained low across truncation levels, indicating that the essential propagation structure is captured with comparatively few avalanches. By contrast, ATM exhibited steeper error growth, wider dispersion across subjects and delayed convergence, particularly at intermediate truncation levels, demonstrating a stronger dependence on total avalanche count and temporal ordering. These patterns were reproduced when avalanches were removed at random positions and in ALS patients, underscoring the generality of ws-ATM’s improved robustness.

From clinical point of view, using ATM, we identified a relatively small set of edges with uncorrected differences between ALS and controls, predominantly involving temporal–occipital links and dominated by increased transition probabilities in ALS. With ws-ATM, the number of significant edges increased several-fold, revealing a distributed pattern of intra- and inter-lobe alterations that prominently engaged frontal and motor cortices. Both strengthened and weakened transitions were observed: hyperconnected edges likely reflect pathological over-synchronisation or compensatory recruitment, whereas reduced transitions point to functional segregation or loss of integration. This coexistence of hyper- and hypo-connectivity is consistent with previous reports of flexible but vulnerable dynamics in ALS and other network disorders.⁴

Clinical correlation analyses highlighted the added value of the weighted-stochastic formulation. For ATM, significant associations between edge weights and ALS Functional Rating Scale–Revised scores were sparse and mostly negative, suggesting that stronger transitions tend to accompany greater disability. Ws-ATM yielded a richer pattern of both positive and negative correlations and a substantially larger overlap between edges that differed across groups and those that covaried with clinical status. When we focused on “concordant” edges, in which the direction of ALS–control differences matched the sign of clinical correlations, ws-ATM outperformed ATM by a wide margin. Concordant edges clustered around frontal and motor hubs, including precentral and superior frontal regions implicated in both motor and cognitive manifestations of ALS, and overlapped with regions previously shown to display altered avalanche spreading and flexible dynamics.⁴

Conclusion

In this work, we introduce the Weighted-Stochastic Avalanche Transition Matrix as a principled extension of avalanche-based connectivity that embeds neuronal propagation into a Markov-chain framework and explicitly incorporates activation and avalanche duration. By normalising outgoing transitions to obtain a row-stochastic matrix and weighting longer co-activations more heavily, ws-ATM provides a compact summary of both the strength and persistence of propagation pathways, directly grounded in the neuronal avalanche framework established in invasive and non-invasive recordings.¹,² Our convergence analyses show that ws-ATM stabilises rapidly with comparatively few avalanches, remains robust when large portions of the data are removed and exhibits reduced inter-subject variability relative to the conventional ATM, indicating improved sample efficiency and resilience to artefact rejection—key properties for clinical and real-time applications.

Applied to ALS, ws-ATM substantially amplified disease-related signatures of altered fast, aperiodic dynamics and preferentially highlighted edges and nodes whose direction of change was consistent with clinical impairment. The framework revealed a spatially distributed, frontal-centred pattern in which pathological hyperconnectivity and functional segregation coexist and strengthened the link between disrupted avalanche propagation and ALS Functional Rating Scale–Revised scores by enriching for edges that both differentiated patients from controls and covaried with disability. Together with prior work showing that avalanche dynamics constrain and are constrained by structural connectivity and behaviour,¹–⁵ these findings support ws-ATM as a clinically informative window onto the evolving reconfiguration of cortical networks in ALS and as a flexible feature space for patient stratification and progression monitoring.

Beyond ALS, the weighted-stochastic formulation is agnostic to disease aetiology and can readily be ported to other neurological and psychiatric conditions in which fast, aperiodic brain dynamics are disrupted.³,⁴ Its computational efficiency, event-based nature and compatibility with streaming implementations make ws-ATM an attractive candidate for integration into adaptive BCIs, where neuronal avalanches can be estimated on-the-fly to drive closed-loop control and guide individualised training.³,⁵ At the same time, important limitations remain: we restricted our analysis to cortical sources because of MEG’s reduced sensitivity to deep generators; recordings were cross-sectional; and only the resting state was examined. Future work should integrate subcortical and cerebellar regions via multimodal imaging, extend ws-ATM to EEG to improve accessibility, and validate its performance in longitudinal cohorts, task-based paradigms and neuromodulatory interventions such as transcranial magnetic stimulation or deep brain stimulation. Building on the present results and prior demonstrations of avalanche-based markers in clinical and BCI contexts,³–⁵ these developments will be crucial to establish ws-ATM as a general, translational framework for tracking neuronal avalanche dynamics and their clinical consequences.
