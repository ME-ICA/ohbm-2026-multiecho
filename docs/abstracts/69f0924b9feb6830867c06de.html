Introduction

Understanding the neural basis of social interaction requires methods that can capture brain activity from multiple individuals engaged in naturalistic behavior. While hyperscanning fMRI has enabled simultaneous imaging of interacting humans, it is restricted mainly to visual interaction and equivalent whole-brain approaches in animal models remain limited. Most rodent social interaction studies of brain activity rely on EEG or calcium imaging techniques,1,2 which provide high temporal resolution but are restricted to cortical surfaces or small fields of view (FOV). As a result, current methods cannot fully capture distributed network dynamics that underlie complex social behaviors.

Zero echo time (ZTE) fMRI offers a promising solution for bridging this gap. With a local transmit RF-coil, ZTE is sensitive to blood flow/volume changes during brain activation. Its close-to-zero acquisition delay and high bandwidths minimize susceptibility artifacts and sensitivity to body movement, and gradual gradient steps reduce acoustic noise, enabling stable imaging in awake and behaving animals.3,4 These features make ZTE ideally suited for social interaction paradigms, where naturally occurring behaviors with subtle movements are essential components of the experimental design. By preserving artifact-free brain coverage and reducing motion-related signal changes, ZTE fMRI allows investigation of whole-brain activity associated with diverse social behaviors that cannot be reliably measured with traditional EPI-based sequences.

Currently, literature lacks studies examining whole-brain activity during social interaction in relation to specific behavioral events. To our knowledge, one recent preprint has reported dual-mouse fMRI during social engagement,5 but its primary focus was not on characterizing behavior-linked neural responses. Thus, the aim of our study was to pilot ZTE-fMRI studies focused on behavioral events during social interaction with awake mice.

Methods

Experimental procedures were accepted by National Animal Experiment Board (license ESAVI-2023-19961) and conducted in accordance with the European Commission Directive 2010/63/EU guidelines. A total of 4 adult male C57BL/6J mice (JAX) were used. The mice were paired in different conspecific combinations, resulting in five experiments. Prior to experiments, a PTFE screw implant was fixed on top of the skull with dental cement. After 1-week of recovery time, animals were habituated to awake imaging using a 3D-printed mock MRI holder with a custom head-fixation system. During habituation and scanning, mice stood head fixed but otherwise freely on a slightly slippery glass surface without body restraint; only plastic sidewalls limiting lateral sliding (Figure 1). The glass surface was warmed using circulating water. For placement into the holder, mice were briefly anesthetized with 3-5 % isoflurane.

A progressive 15-day habituation protocol was used, gradually introducing mice to handling, scanner noise, and head fixation. On day 16, a second mouse was placed opposite the first to acclimate animals to social presence prior to dual-mouse imaging. MRI was performed on a 9.4 T Agilent system using two custom-made surface loop coils (Neos Biotec) for transmit/receive. For the second coil, RF shielding loop was incorporated to reduce coil coupling. Mice were scanned alone (on separate sessions) or during social interaction, positioned so that head centers were 4.5 cm apart. Behaviors were recorded with two MRI-compatible video cameras (MRC Systems).

After preparation, magnetization-transfer–weighted MB-SWIFT high resolution anatomical images were acquired, followed by ZTE functional imaging. We used an in-house made variant of silent ZTE sequence with gradual gradient changes and spoke order optimized for silent scanning: flip angle 2°, repetition time per volume 1.2 s, 2151 spokes/volume, bandwidth 125/500 kHz, 0.27 x 0.27 x 0.55 anisotropic resolution, matrix 128³, FOV 35 x 35 x 70 mm, with total acquisition time of 25 min. Both animals’ brains were covered with a single FOV. Two RF amplifier units were used for simultaneous signal transmission, and signals from the two coils were acquired at the same time.

Functional data were reconstructed and the images from both coils were summed together. Each image from all animals was registered to a reference high resolution brain acquired from the same study. Twelve behaviors were identified and annotated from video: whisking, sniffing, stepping (left/right/both), escape attempt, bruxing, blinking, freezing, eye movements, twitching, and grooming. Behavioral epochs were modeled as boxcar regressors, and fMRI data were analyzed with a general linear model (SPM 12). Consecutively, parameter estimates showing relationship between the regressors and fMRI time series were calculated, and statistical maps were generated for the individual behavior (individual whisking) and shared behavior (shared whisking). A comparison map contrasting shared to individual responses was also created. T-contrast maps were generated, and voxels exceeding an uncorrected voxel-wise threshold of p < 0.01 were displayed.

Results

The most frequently observed behaviors were whisking, sniffing, bruxing and different kinds of movements. The statistical maps in Figure 2 show the brain correlates of whisking, which was the most common behavior that induced a response in the observing mouse (i.e. initiating social interaction). In the shared whisking and comparison maps, more posterior and deep regions (medial frontal cortex, septum, insular cortex, amygdala, and paraventricular thalamus) were activated in comparison to individual whisking maps, where mostly frontal cortex (including secondary motor and prelimbic cortex) and olfactory bulb activation was seen. 

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that ZTE fMRI is a feasible and robust method for investigating social interaction related brain activity in behaving mice. Our in-house made MRI holder together with the ZTE sequence enabled detection of meaningful fMRI responses

Socially driven responses were observed across multiple behavioral categories, with the strongest effects associated with whisking. This finding is consistent with whisking being a primary exploratory and communicative behavior in rodents thus prevalent for close-range interactions.

Individual whisking events activated brain regions that were mainly localized in the anterior and lateral frontal cortex together with olfactory areas (Figure 2 A). In the absence of coupling to the conspecific’s behavior, these activations may be related to exploratory whisking and olfactory-driven processes rather than social reactivity. When mice were whisking during social interaction, activity within the medial frontal cortex (Figure 2 B) was consistent with previous EEG and calcium-imaging findings, which reported increased synchrony among medial frontal cortex neurons during social engagement.1,2 This supports the interpretation that medial frontal cortical dynamics play a key role in attentional processes that facilitate monitoring and responding to social cues.

Beyond frontal cortical regions, significant activity increases were also observed in the septum, insular cortex, amygdala, and paraventricular thalamus in response to whisking during social interaction (Figure 2 B and C). The involvement of the insular cortex is consistent with earlier findings⁵ and underscores its role in interoceptive, emotional, and social–affective processing. Engagement of the septum, amygdala, and paraventricular thalamus—novel observations in the present study—suggests contributions from limbic and thalamic circuits involved in emotional salience, motivational state, and arousal during social interaction.

Overall, these results indicate that ZTE-fMRI offers a promising avenue for studying naturalistic social behavior in small animals, enabling the detection of neurobehavioral coupling across distributed cortical and subcortical networks.
