Introduction

Attentional habits are implicitly and gradually learned through repeated exposure to regularities, automatically deployed, and resistant to extinction.1 These attentional habits are distinct from oculomotor habits,2 and from endogenous and exogenous covert attention, where goals and salience, respectively, drive spatial orienting. The neural mechanisms support these experience-driven, yet involuntarily deployed, spatial biases on attention are unclear. Both exogenous and endogenous covert attention modulate sensory processing in the visual cortex3 and recruit overlapping regions within dorsal (FEF, IPS) and ventral (TPJ) networks.4 Attentional biases driven by reward-based selection history engage the striatum in attention to posterior parietal regions.5 However, the neural circuits that mediate covert spatial attention via other forms of selection history (i.e. learned probabilities and statistical regularities), particularly when it becomes habitual, is far less understood. One such way in which an attentional habit may materialize, is through a sequence of selective attention steps (analogous to a motor habit). Learning motor habits, as well as implicit and sequential learning recruits the striatum,6–8 suggesting that experience-driven attentional habits, in the form of sequence-based covert spatial attentional biases, may similarly engage striatal circuits, in addition to known attentional orienting networks. Here, we probed how attention can be biased automatically using previously implicitly learned sequential regularities. Specifically, we ask whether sequential spatial regularities recruit brain regions associated with habit-like processing in addition to previously associated attention network regions.

Methods

Participants completed a three-session experimental study, consisting of two behavioral training sessions (sessions 1-2) and one fMRI testing session (Session 3), using an attentional serial reaction time (attSRT) task (Fig 1). Prolonged exposure to a repeating sequence during the two pre-scan training sessions facilitated automaticity of the attentional bias, which is critical for an attentional habit. On each trial of the attSRT, a Gabor target appeared briefly at one of four ordinal locations, and participants reported its tilt (clockwise or counterclockwise) as quickly as possible after the Gabor target appeared. Blocks alternated between learned sequences, where target locations followed a repeating spatial sequence across trials; and novel sequences, where locations were randomized. Novel sequences were further separated into two conditions: novel-cued and novel-uncued. For the novel-cued condition, a central cue (black line radiating from fixation toward the future target location) was flashed during the initial fixation. Participants were not informed of the repeating sequence in the learned condition. Each 2-s trial consisted of a pre-stimulus fixation (950ms), stimulus presentation (250ms), mask (600ms), feedback (200ms). In the novel-cued condition, the cue was flashed for 250ms during the fixation interval (e.g., 200ms fixation, 250ms cue, 700ms fixation). During training sessions, a gaze-contingent display required fixation on a center crosshair throughout each trial. During session three, eye movement data confirmed fixation throughout the task; and no feedback will be provided. The task used a blocked design, with each block consisting of 16 trials from a single condition. Sessions 2 and 3 each contained 10 runs, with each run (~5min) consisting of 8 task blocks (each duration 32s; 2:1:1 ratio of learned, novel-cued, and novel-uncued) and 3 fixation blocks (each duration 14s; first, middle, and last block of each run). Session 1 consisted of 8 runs, each with 12 task blocks (4:1:1 ratio between learned, novel-cued, and novel-uncued). Block order within runs was counterbalanced across participants.

Functional imaging was acquired with a 3T Siemens Trio scanner. T2-weighted images were be acquired within each volume (TR=1000ms; TE=28ms; field-of-view=210mm; multiband factor=4; voxel size=2.5x2.5x2.5mm3 voxel). High-resolution whole-brain T1-weighted structural scans were acquired for each participant (voxel size=1x1x1mm3; TR=1000ms; TE=29ms). fMRI data during the attSRT task was collected across 10 runs (each 298s). Data were processed with Freesurfer 7.4.1.

Functional images co-registered to participant’s high-resolution structural scan. To capture variance in BOLD signal across conditions, a general linear model (GLM) was created for each subject (across all runs), with regressors for each task condition. Analyses focused on the contrast of learned vs. novel-uncued trials to isolate neural activity associated with implicitly learned attentional biases. The resulting subject-level beta maps were entered into one-sample t-test to identify consistent patterns of activation across subjects. Behavioral covariates reflecting attentional benefits (reaction time and accuracy: learned – novel-uncued), averaged across runs within each subject, were included. Regions of interest (ROIs) were defined in each participant’s native space.

Results

All participants successfully completed behavioral training, having a mean accuracy above the pre-defined threshold, and exhibited performance benefits from the attentional habit. To examine how behavioral performance related to task-related neural activity, group-level analyses were conducted using each participant’s accuracy benefit (learned – novel-uncued) as a covariate in the GLM. We observed greater bilateral putamen activity for the learned sequence compared to the novel-uncued sequence, specifically within the executive control subregion of the putamen.

Conclusion

Activity in the putamen reflects the deployment of covert attentional habits guided by implicitly learned sequence regularities, compared to a top-down (endogenously) attentional control strategy. Importantly, this automatic use of implicitly learned sequential spatial regularities recruits the putamen independent from eye movements, and is not driven by motor actions. This suggests striatal circuits supporting attentional habits, distinct from both involuntary salience-driven and volitionally controlled attention.
