Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a prevalent and disabling psychiatric condition characterized by persistent low mood, anhedonia, cognitive dysfunction, and somatic symptoms. It is a leading cause of disability worldwide and exhibits high heterogeneity in both the clinical presentation and treatment outcomes. Neurobiological models conceptualize MDD as a disorder of distributed brain networks implicated in emotion regulation and cognitive control, including the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) ^1.

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) to the DLPFC is an established, non-invasive treatment for MDD, particularly in patients who fail to respond to first-line interventions. Despite its efficacy at the group level, individual response to rTMS remains highly variable with about one-third of the population not responding to treatment. This highlights the need for reliable predictors of treatment outcome.

Electroencephalography (EEG) provides a direct, non-invasive measure of brain activity and has been explored as a biomarker for predicting rTMS response. Prior work has reported group-level associations between baseline EEG features such as frontal alpha asymmetry/spectral power and rTMS outcome ^2 ^3. However, these findings have rarely been validated at the individual level using deep-learning approaches. In addition, variables such as age, baseline depression severity, sleep patterns, and personality traits may also influence treatment response. These variables are often readily available to clinicians and may therefore be more easily integrated into clinical prediction models. 

This study investigated whether baseline EEG characteristics and clinical/sociodemographic variables (clinical data) can predict individual rTMS treatment response in MDD. In a sample of 119 individuals undergoing rTMS treatment for MDD, we compared the performance of a deep learning model for the prediction of rTMS outcome using both clinical and EEG data. 

Methods

Sample

Data were obtained from the single-site open-access TDBRAIN study provided by the Brainclinics Foundation ^4. 119 participants (60 female, mean age: 43.0±13.17 years) with a primary diagnosis of MDD undergoing rTMS treatment were included. Each participant received a minimum of 10 rTMS sessions targeting the DLPFC (10 Hz stimulation at left DLPFC or 1 HZ stimulation at right DLPFC).

Clinical data

Clinical data consisted of 1) demographic data on age, sex, and education level 2) depression symptom severity measured with the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) 3) drug use 4) sleep quality 5) personality features measured with the 60-item NEO-FFI questionnaire on a 5-point Likert scale and 6) cognitive performance scores on the auditory oddball task.

EEG data

EEG data were recorded using a 26-channel montage based on the 10-10 international system with a sampling rate of 500 Hz and a low-pass filter of 100 Hz. Resting-state EEG data were recorded for two minutes with both eyes open and eyes closed conditions. 

Data were preprocessed with an in-house automated preprocessing pipeline in Matlab (version 2022b) based on EEGLAB. Noisy or flat channels were removed based on excessive amplitude differences. Signals were then re-referenced to an average montage and downsampled to 250 Hz. An FIR filter was applied (1-45 Hz) and independent component analysis (ICA) was used to remove eyeblink artifacts. The automatic artifact removal (AAR) toolbox was used to remove large amplitude and muscle artifacts.

Calculated features for the EEG data included the per-channel power spectral density of conventional frequency bands (Delta, Theta, Alpha, Beta, and Gamma), individual and central alpha peak frequency, cordance (averaged per condition and frequency band for four electrode lobe locations), frontal alpha asymmetry measures between right (F4) and left (F3) frontal hemispheres, and interhemispheric alpha asymmetry.

Deep learning

We predicted rTMS outcome (defined as a 50% reduction in BDI score) using a Multi-Layer Perceptron Classifier (MLP) and 5-fold cross-validation. For each training instance, we scaled the data and then performed a grid search with 5-fold inner cross-validation to find optimal hyperparameters for the MLP (2 hidden layers; layer size: 10-256 ; layer activation: Hyperbolic Tangent Function/Rectified Linear Unit, loss optimizer: Adaptive Movement Estimation/Stochastic Gradient Descent; learning rate: 0.0001-0.05; learning rate type: constant/adaptive). Best performing hyperparameters were then applied to the outer test set and evaluated by the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity. Model performance was calculated as the average AUC, sensitivity, and specificity over the 5 folds. 

Results

A total of 78 individuals (66%) met criteria for response to rTMS treatment. Contrary to expectations, objective markers of brain function assessed by EEG could not predict rTMS outcome above chance level for individual patients (average AUC: 0.50, sensitivity: 0.73, specificity: 0.29). In contrast, the model trained on clinical data achieved a moderate performance in the prediction of rTMS outcome (average AUC: 0.67, sensitivity 0.69, specificity 0.56). Feature importance analyses revealed that a variety of personality traits such as high extraversion and varying scores on agreeableness and conscientiousness, high sleep duration, higher age, and higher depression severity were associated with response predictions by the model (see figure 1).

More results, including additional EEG features and model parameters are expected to follow.

Conclusion

In this study, we investigated the potential of using EEG in the prediction of rTMS response in MDD, and compared its performance to that of using readily available clinical information. Given that rTMS directly targets cortical functioning, we hypothesized that EEG features would provide biologically relevant information for rTMS outcome. Unexpectedly, our results showed that commonly used resting-state EEG features did not generalize well to reliable individual-level prediction, performing no better than chance.

These findings challenge the assumption that baseline EEG markers, despite prior group-level associations with rTMS outcome, translate well into clinically useful predictors. In contrast, clinical and sociodemographic variables showed moderate predictive utility (AUC=0.67). These measures may reflect more stable trait-like factors and behavioral context relevant to the response to rTMS. 

Some caution is warranted in the interpretation of these findings. For example, the summary measures derived from the EEG signal in this study may not contain relevant information in the temporal domain. The use of EEG timeseries with deep learning may therefore provide more accurate predictions for rTMS response. Additionally, more complex models with different parameters may provide better performances than the current 2-layer model. In the coming time, we will work on integrating EEG time series and more advanced deep learning model and compare the performance with clinical data and the combination with EEG.
