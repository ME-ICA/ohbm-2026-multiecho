Introduction

Fabry disease (FD) is an X-linked genetic disorder caused by pathogenic GLA variants that impair the lysosomal enzyme α-galactosidase A (α-Gal A). This leads to systemic accumulation of substrates, primarily globotriaosylceramide within endothelial and vascular smooth muscle cells. This may manifest in dysfunction of several organ systems, including the heart, kidneys, and brain, with early strokes being a common clinical presentation.1

The glymphatic system is a brain-wide perivascular network in which cerebrospinal fluid enters along periarterial spaces, diffuses into the parenchyma, and exits along perivenous pathways to facilitate interstitial solute clearance. The Diffusion Tensor Image Analysis ALong the Perivascular Space (DTI-ALPS) is a method used as a surrogate marker of glymphatic dysfunction. It employs the calculation of diffusion in regions of interest next to the top of the lateral ventricles, in the areas of the projection and association pathways. In these regions, glymphatic flow is thought to run through perivascular spaces oriented perpendicularly to both the ventricles and the adjacent fiber tracts. Using the ratio of diffusion in the supposed direction of the glymphatic flow and the directions perpendicular to both the direction of the glymphatic flow and the white matter fibers, the ALPS-index is calculated.2

We found no previous studies reporting on glymphatic function in FD.

Methods

The study included 66 FD patients [26 males with an age of 46.2 ± 11.4 years (mean ± standard deviation), and 40 females with an age of 45.3 ± 15.5] with genetically confirmed pathogenic GLA variant, recruited from a specialized Fabry center in Charles University in Prague. All patients underwent a 3T MRI imaging that included structural T1 MPRAGE and FLAIR images, and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) with 30 noncolinear directions (b = 1100 s/mm2) and five b = 0 s/mm2 images. The DTI images were corrected for bias, Gibbs ringing artifacts, susceptibility-induced distortions, head motion, and eddy currents using SYNB0-DISCO, FSL, and MRtrix3.

To identify the lesions of the (presumed) vascular origin in FD, we followed the Standards for Reporting Vascular Changes on Neuroimaging 2 (STRIVE-2) guidelines. To segment the lesions, we used the HyperMapp3r tool with subsequent heavy manual segmentation revisions. The brain volume used as a covariate in later analyses was acquired using a CAT12 segmentation pipeline.

The regions of interest used in the DTI-ALPS method were automatically segmented based on a previously established approach.3 Briefly, the MNI152-space atlas masks of the corticospinal tract and superior longitudinal fasciculus were restricted to the top of the lateral ventricles and subsequently transformed into each subject’s diffusion space. These masks were then used for the ALPS-index calculation. To exclude the possibility of white matter pathologies directly influencing the ALPS indices, we also subtracted the mask of lesional changes from the regions of interest used in ALPS-index calculations (Figure 1).

We used two-tailed partial correlation with age, sex, and brain volume as covariates to ascertain any relationship between the vascular lesion load and ALPS indices calculated using the total and lesion-less regions of interest.  Due to identifying outliers in the lesion volumes segmented, we performed additional sensitivity analysis with their exclusion.

Results

We found a significant correlation between the lesion volumes and the ALPS indices calculated using both the lesion-less masks [p = 0.002, r(61) = -0.384] and original masks [p = 0.002, r(61) = -0.381].

The mean lesion volume was 5916 mm³ [range 122-73628 mm³, standard deviation (SD) ± 13216 mm³].

Based on lesion volumes, we identified 6 outliers with a mean lesion volume of 42992 mm³ (range 15949-73628 mm³, SD ± 21665 mm³), resulting in 62 patients with a mean lesion volume of 2328 mm³ (range 122-9736 mm³, SD ± 2253). Sensitivity analysis showed that the correlations remained significant for ALPS indices calculated using both the lesion-less masks [p < 0.001, r(55) = -0.458] (Figure 2) and original masks [p < 0.001, r(55) = -0.464].

Conclusion

Our results show changes in ALPS indices that are associated with lesion load of presumed vascular origin in FD.

The DTI-ALPS method is commonly used as a surrogate marker for glymphatic dysfunction. Although a study in cerebral small vessel disease patients found a correlation between glymphatic function as measured by intrathecal gadolinium injection and the DTI-ALPS method, its interpretation remains controversial. In essence, the ALPS-index reflects the ratio of diffusion of water molecules perpendicular to the lateral ventricles (a direction in which the glymphatic flow is theorized to flow in this area) to the “background” diffusion of water molecules in directions perpendicular to both the glymphatic flow and white matter tracts. However, it remains to be seen whether white matter degeneration could independently affect ALPS indices in a manner separate from glymphatic dysfunction. We aimed to minimize such variables by excluding areas with visible white matter pathology from the regions of interest used in the DTI-ALPS analysis.

Several other diseases are associated with the decrease in ALPS indices, including Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease, normal pressure hydrocephalus, and small vessel disease. A bi-directional relationship has been shown in Parkinson’s disease, where the accumulation of pathological α-synuclein causes glymphatic dysfunction, and vice versa, a glymphatic dysfunction causes increased α-synuclein accumulation.4 Moreover, the noradrenaline-mediated slow vasomotion has been shown as a major driver of glymphatic clearance5, thus implying a possibility of small vessel pathology affecting the glymphatic function.

We theorize three possible pathways of glymphatic dysfunction in FD. Firstly, glymphatic dysfunction may arise secondary to the FD associated small vessel disease, with pathological vascular changes leading to impaired vasomotion which was shown to be an important driver of glymphatic function. Secondly, in a similar vein to previous findings in neurodegenerative diseases, substrate accumulation within brain tissue may directly contribute to dysfunction of the glymphatic system. Lastly, FD vascular pathology may affect the noradrenergic innervation by damaging the locus coeruleus or its efferent pathways, thus also impacting vascular vasomotion.

In conclusion, these results could imply a degree of glymphatic dysfunction in Fabry disease that is associated with its lesion load.
