Introduction

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) can be particularly challenging for young children, as it requires them to remain still for an extended period while lying inside a loud and enclosed scanner. Many children experience anxiety or fear when faced with the large, unfamiliar machine and the noises it produces [1]. Movement during scanning often leads to motion artefacts, reducing image quality and sometimes necessitating scan repetition or sedation [2]. To address these challenges, we developed the MRI Space Mission. This is a child-centred, stepwise paediatric MRI training protocol. Previous studies have shown that playful preparation can reduce anxiety and sedation rates in paediatric MRI [3,4]. Our activities are structured to gradually build familiarity and confidence, helping children practise staying still and understand what to expect during the scan. This protocol aims to improve scan success rates by familiarising children with the MRI environment in a playful way and reducing procedure-related anxiety. 

Methods

The paediatric MRI training protocol was implemented across two studies involving 40 children aged 5–7 years (Study 1) and 40 children aged 7–9 years (Study 2) from kindergarten and primary school (Fig. 2-Table 1). High-resolution T1w images (TR/TE = 2000/2.26, 1 x 1 x 1 mm3), functional images (TR/TE=1500/30, 3 x 3 x 3 mm3) and diffusion images (DSI, TR/TE = 5000/91 ms, 2× 2 × 2 mm³, 129 directions, max b-val = 3000 s/mm²) were acquired using a 3T Siemens Prisma scanner equipped with a 32-channel head coil. We collected T1, T2, and DSI scans for both studies, resting-state fMRI for Study 2, and task fMRI for Study 1. Sequences were repeated when necessary, and to minimise movement and fatigue while ensuring children’s best performance. The total scan time was capped at 1 hour. To enhance engagement, our training incorporated a space-themed storyline, immersing children in the MRI space mission to be trained as space cadets with an animated companion, AstroCat. Through this imaginative framing, children explored the science of MRI and learned about the scanning procedures in a gamified way. The protocol included 8 mini-activities, such as reading a picture book about AstroCat’s MRI spaceship flight, watching short animated videos and MRI images, changing into spacesuit (medical gown), and participating in hands-on games (e.g., practise fMRI tasks, play tunnel statue game, and mock scanner training that simulated the MRI experience) [5]. Each activity was represented by a planet from Earth to Neptune, and the actual MRI scan corresponded to a mystery planet (Fig. 1). Children collected a sticker upon finishing each activity to paste in their MRI Space Passport. During the scan, children engaged in games or videos that aligned with our storyline of travelling to a mystery planet (Fig. 2-Table 2). After completing the scan, they received a mission completion certificate with a screenshot of their brain image. All children and parents completed emotional and attitudinal questionnaires before and after the sessions. MRI data quality was inspected at the console, and data preprocessing was performed using SPM12 (www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm). For functional data, bad slices and volumes with motion artifacts were detected and corrected using ARTrepair (https://www.nitrc.org/projects/art_repair/). A run was deemed unusable if more than 25% of its volumes exceeded the 0.6-mm motion threshold. Only data meeting these quality criteria were counted as successful. 

Results

Out of 80 participants, 97.5% of children aged 5–7 years (Study 1) and 100% of those aged 7–9 years (Study 2) were willing to complete the MRI scan. Only one 7-year-old child declined to enter the MRI room after training. Across both studies, T1 and T2 scans were successfully acquired for most participants (>60% in Study 1 and >70% in Study 2). Success rates were slightly lower for the task fMRI in Study 1 (62.5%) and resting-state fMRI in Study 2 (50%), and lowest for the DSI scans (<45%) (Fig. 2-Table 2). Children reported having a lot of fun during the MRI sessions and gave high ratings to activities that made them feel excited about the MRI scan (Fig. 2-Table 1). Among 7–9-year-olds, the Astronaut Certificate and Brain Photo received the highest ratings (4.35/5). For 5–7-year-olds, the Space Passport and Planet Stickers were rated the highest (4.47/5). Parents provided positive feedback, appreciating the preparation and support offered by the research team. 

Conclusion

Across both studies, the 98.75% attendance rate and the high activity ratings indicated that our MRI training protocol effectively increased both children’s and parents’ willingness to participate in an MRI study. Although scan success rates were higher in older children, younger participants were able to complete most sequences with high-quality data. The lower success rates for the DSI scans in both studies were due to long scan duration and the 1-hour cap of the session. Overall, our completion rates were higher than those reported in previous studies, and our sequence-level success rates were comparable to or better than prior paediatric MRI work that implemented child-friendly preparation [2,3,5]. In conclusion, our space-themed MRI training protocol is feasible and effective for children. The protocol’s structured activities, combined with preparation and reward, can effectively reduce anxiety and movement during scanning, making paediatric MRI research more practical and reliable. However, the results suggest that there is still room to further reduce head motion and improve children’s ability to maintain still with additional measures in future protocols.
