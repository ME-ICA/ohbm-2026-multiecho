Introduction

Superficial white matter (SWM) bundles, including short U-fibers, form dense cortico-cortical circuits that support local information integration. Yet their study remains limited due to high anatomical variability and the difficulty of reliably identifying short, cortico-cortical association pathways1. 

Current classification strategies, based on bundle length or gyrus-to-gyrus connectivity, remain imprecise and strongly affected by inter-individual variability in cortical folding.

Sulci, in contrast, provide stable and developmentally grounded landmarks that strongly constrain the trajectories of short association fibers2. Despite this relevance, no systematic sulcus-based framework exists to describe or classify associative bundles.

Here, we introduce a new tractography-based method that classifies associative white matter bundles according to the sulci they cross. By quantifying sulcus-bundle interactions at the streamline level, this tool provides a reproducible and anatomically pertinent framework to describe short-range connectivity. We demonstrate the approach using the human white-matter atlas described in Herlin et al. 20233, illustrating how sulcal trajectories naturally organize association fibers into interpretable categories.

Methods

Pipeline overview - We developed a sulcus-informed classification tool combining (1) automatic sulcal extraction from cortical surfaces using the BrainVISA/Morphologist toolbox4, (2) whole-brain deterministic tractography and (3) streamline-to-sulcus intersection testing to derive a sulcal “crossing profile” for each bundle (see fig. 1). These two last steps can be found within the Ginkgo toolbox5.

Bundles, template and sulci extraction - We used the SWM bundle atlas from Herlin et al. 20233 as a demonstration dataset. This atlas provides a comprehensive set of short association bundles reconstructed in the MNI (ICBM 2009c nonlinear asymmetric) template. Streamlines were already grouped into anatomically defined bundles, allowing us to directly evaluate sulcus–bundle interactions without subject-level variability. For this study, all sulci of the MNI template were identified and segmented by the BrainVISA/Morphologist pipeline4 and included: 62 sulci in the left hemisphere and 57 in the right hemisphere. The method identifies folds from the cortical surface mesh, reconstructing each sulcus as a labeled 3D structure.

Bundle-sulcus interaction mapping - Streamlines from each bundle of the atlas were resampled to ensure equidistant points along their trajectory.    

For each point of the resampled streamline, nearby vertices of each sulcal mesh were identified, taking into account the appropriate linear transform between the anatomy and the diffusion space. Candidate sulci were evaluated for both proximity and alignment : the distance between fiber points and sulcal vertices, the angle between the local fiber direction and the sulcal orientation (estimated from the principal direction of the sulcal mesh), and the location of the fiber-sulcus interaction relative to the sulcus' gravity center were computed. 

Each streamline’s contribution to a given sulcus was then weighted using two Tukey apodization functions : one applied along the streamline length to down-weight extremities, and one applied to optimize locally orthogonal fiber-sulcus configurations.

For each bundle, the algorithm aggregates these weighted contributions across all streamlines to produce a sulcal crossing histogram, where each entry corresponds to the final interaction weight with a specific sulcus. These histograms form the per-bundle sulcal affinity profiles, which are stored as individual files and assembled into a global bundle-to-sulci affinity matrix. 

This output provides, for each bundle, a quantitative description of which sulci it crosses and with what relative weight. Concretely, for each bundle, the algorithm produces a JSON file summarizing its sulcal crossing profile, i.e. the list of sulci it intersects, and the corresponding weighted affinities.

Results

For each bundle of the short association fiber atlas registered to the MNI template, the tool allowed us to obtain: (1) the number of sulci crossed, (2) the identity of each crossed sulcus, and (3) a sulcal affinity score corresponding to the proportion of streamlines intersecting each sulcus.

In addition to the affinity tables, the tool outputs sulcal surface masks, bundle index tables, and spreadsheet-style summaries listing all bundle-sulcus pairs. Bundles were then grouped according to the number of crossed sulci (e.g., 1-sulcus, 2-sulci, 3-sulci categories) and their exact sulcal trajectory. Importantly, the tool also performs the reverse mapping: for any given sulcus, it returns the full list of bundles intersecting it, enabling future analyses that are either bundle-centric or sulcus-centric. 

In the current example, bundles from both hemispheres exhibited similar distributions in the number of crossed sulci (see Fig. 2.). In the left hemisphere, the majority of short association bundles crossed either one (n = 561) or two sulci (n = 520). Higher-order trajectories were progressively less frequent, with 295 bundles crossing three sulci, 132 crossing four, 65 crossing five, and 36 crossing more than six sulci. The right hemisphere showed a comparable pattern, with slightly larger counts in most categories: 664 bundles crossed one sulcus, 723 crossed two, 391 crossed three, 179 crossed four, 62 crossed five, and 54 crossed more than six sulci.

Despite these differences in raw counts, the overall proportions of bundles per sulcus-count category were highly conserved between hemispheres (see Fig. 2). Both sides displayed a similar decrease from simple (1–2 sulci) to complex (>4 sulci) trajectories. Indeed, we computed the proportion of bundles in each category relative to the hemisphere’s total bundle count. In the left hemisphere (total = 1,609 bundles), 34.9% crossed one sulcus, 32.3% crossed two, 18.3% crossed three, 8.2% crossed four, 4.0% crossed five, and 2.2% crossed more than six. In the right hemisphere (total = 2,073 bundles), the corresponding proportions were 32.0%, 34.9%, 18.9%, 8.6%, 3.0%, and 2.6%. Thus, both hemispheres showed a consistent exponential-like decline in frequency as the number of crossed sulci increased, with only minimal hemispheric asymmetry in proportional distribution.

Conclusion

We introduce the first tractography-based tool that classifies associative white-matter bundles according to the sulci they cross. Applying the tool to the short-association bundle atlas of Herlin et al. (2023)3 produced the first sulcus-based ontology of short white-matter bundles in humans. This ontology links each bundle to a specific sequence of sulci, enabling classification not only by position or geometry but by sulcal anchoring, an anatomical principle previously unavailable for short-range association fibers.

This approach reveals a clear sulcal organization of superficial bundles, improves anatomical interpretability, and offers a reproducible foundation for constructing a white-matter ontology grounded in cortical folding. This sulcus-based classification opens new avenues for studying development, inter-individual variability, pathology, and cross-species comparisons.
