Introduction

The rapid adoption of Artificial Intelligence-powered conversational agents (AICA) such as ChatGPT has fundamentally transformed human-computer interaction. The development has provided unprecedented capabilities that extend beyond mere information retrieval and allow complex interactions and socio-emotional simulation 1,2. Unlike traditional search engines or social media, AICAs can function as dynamic and real-time cognitive and empathic agents, capable of facilitating task execution and providing quasi-social companionship 3,4. While initial studies examined the cognitive-behavioral implications of interactions with AICAs the impact of the technological transformation on the architecture of the human brain remains completely unknown. To this end, the present study capitalized on structural neuroimaging in combination with computational anatomy analyses to elucidate the neurostructural correlates of AICA usage in a comparably large cohort of healthy young adults.

Methods

A total of 222 participants were included in the analysis. Following structural magnetic resonance imaging (sMRI) acquisition, they completed questionnaires regarding the frequency and motivation of Generative AI (GAI, namely AICA) usage (primarily encompassing general GAI use, functional GAI use, and socio-emotional GAI use). SMRI data were then processed with standard pre-processing pipeline in the CAT12 toolbox before being utilized for regional and network morphology analyses. An 8-mm full width at half maximum Gaussian filter was applied for smoothing.

Regional morphology analysis: 1) voxel-based morphology (VBM) identified linear relationships between gray matter volume (GMV) and GAI use; 2) deformation-based morphology (DBM) determined linear relationships between brain deformation features and GAI use. Statistical significance was evaluated at a voxel-wise threshold of p < 0.001 (uncorrected) with cluster-level FWE correction at p < 0.05. Behavioral decoding of VBM and DBM results was performed with the Brainstat toolbox and Neurosynth database.

Network morphology analysis included the following steps. 1) Network construction: an individualized morphological similarity network (MSN) was constructed using the AAL90 atlas and Jensen-Shannon divergence-based similarity. 2) Graph theory analysis: global and node properties of the MSN were computed using the Gretna toolbox. Pearson correlation analysis determined associations between graph theory metrics and GAI use. FDR correction at p < 0.05 was applied to control for multiple comparisons. 3) Connectome-based predictive modeling (CPM) was employed to predict GAI use from MSN connectivity. Only edges significantly associated with GAI use (p < 0.05) were retained for predictive modeling. Robust estimates were obtained through 100 repetitions of 5-fold cross-validation and p value was determined via 5000 permutations. Gender, age, and total intracranial volume were controlled as covariates throughout all analyses.

Results

VBM analysis showed significant positive correlations between frequency of general GAI use and GMV in the left superior frontal gyrus (SFG) and calcarine cortex (Fig. 1A). The frequency of socio-emotional GAI use was significantly negatively correlated with GMV in a cluster extending from the left superior temporal gyrus to the amygdala (AMYG; Fig. 1B). DBM analysis further revealed a significant positive correlation between general GAI use and deformation in the left SFG (Fig. 1C). Behavioral decoding indicated that brain regions with GMV changes associated with general GAI use primarily involved executive function, self processing, and decision-making; regions linked to socio-emotional GAI use were mainly associated with emotional processing and social processing; regions with deformation changes related to general GAI use were predominantly associated with motivation, reward, and decision-making.

Graph theory analysis revealed that more frequent general GAI use was correlated with higher clustering coefficient and local efficiency in the hippocampus (HIP), while more frequent socio-emotional GAI use was correlated with lower clustering coefficient in the cuneus (Fig. 2A). CPM analysis showed that an MSN significantly predicted general GAI use (Fig. 2B). Such an MSN predominantly comprised a positive network centered on hubs including the SFG, visual processing regions, AMYG, and HIP-related regions, along with a negative network formed by three additional connectomes (Fig. 2C).

Conclusion

This study provides the first empirical evidence elucidating neuroanatomical correlates of GAI usage. We observed that general GAI usage is related to the structural morphology of prefrontal regions involved in executive function and self-reflection with a multivariate analysis additionally indicating associations with a widespread network encompassing frontal, limbic and visual processing regions. Conversely, specific socio-emotional GAI usage maps more narrowly onto substrates implicated in socio-affective and visual processing, including superior temporal and amygdalar systems. Collectively, these findings enrich the burgeoning field of human-AI interaction, laying a critical foundation for addressing two pivotal questions: 1) what specific neuroanatomical profiles predispose individuals to frequent GAI engagement and 2) whether and how GAI is actively sculpting the architecture of our cognitive brain and social brain.
