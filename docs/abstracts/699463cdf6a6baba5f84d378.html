Introduction

The autonomic nervous system (ANS) which regulates an extensive range of bodily processes from control of blood pressure and heart rate to bladder and bowel function, is one of the primary systems afflicted by accumulation of α-synuclein in Parkinson’s disease (PD). As such, autonomic failure (AF) is a pervasive feature of PD driving a significant portion of disability and morbidity, and manifesting up to 20-years before diagnosis1. One of the most common presentations of AF is neurogenic orthostatic hypotension (nOH), a sustained drop in blood pressure upon standing that may lead to syncope and have significant consequences like falls, fractures and death. Peripheral cardiac noradrenergic denervation contributes to nOH, and animal studies as well as post-mortem human studies have revealed damage to medullary nuclei that control blood pressure and others in the cardiovascular control circuit. However, our understanding of in-vivo dysfunction of these nuclei is still poor due to challenges in imaging the small nuclei of the brainstem. Nonetheless, it is crucial to measure in-vivo dysfunction given that α-synuclein impacts neuronal function long before it leads to neuronal loss and atrophy2. Very recently, technical developments demonstrated that functional connectivity can be successfully measured in the brainstem-cortex axis3.

Here, we investigated effective connectivity via dynamic causal modelling4 across a central ANS network in early PD patients and aged-matched healthy individuals with the goal of understanding how central dysfunction contributes to nOH. Our findings show that increased drops in blood pressure upon standing in PD are linked to stronger medullary self-inhibition and altered outgoing projections from medullary regions to others in the central ANS. These were not found in healthy individuals with comparable blood pressure drops and may explain how α-synuclein afflicts central ANS function in-vivo, later translating into known post-mortem findings.

Methods

Population and tests

This work took baseline data from 81 individuals with PD and 65 aged-matched healthy controls (HC) from the ongoing “Quantitative MRI for Anatomical Phenotyping in Parkinson’s Disease” project (qMAP-PD) who had resting state fMRI data available. All participants completed a medical and neurological examination with a movement disorder neurologist, as well as autonomic questionnaires (SCOPA-AUT, NMSQ) and the 3-minute standing test: individuals lie down for 5-minutes, have their blood pressure measured, then stand-up and have their blood pressure measured every minute for 3 minutes. Differences in questionnaires scores were assessed with independent sample t-tests corrected for multiple comparisons.

Imaging and preprocessing

Individuals underwent a multimodal MRI session including ~6 minutes of resting-state fMRI. Image acquisitions were performed at the Functional Imaging Laboratory in Queen Square (London, UK), on a Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma 3T scanner, using a 64-channel head-and-neck coil. fMRI used echo-planar sequences (EPI, TR = 1.18s, TE = 32.0ms, with a flip-angle of 64°, multi-band acceleration factor = 6), with an in-plane resolution of 2.0 x 2.0 mm (104 x 90) and a slice thickness of 2.0mm (no gaps, 78 slices, FOV = 208mm, slices were aligned to the bicommisural plan with the acquisition volume aligned to cover the entire brain hemispheres and brainstem).

Functional images were processed using SPM12 functions: slice-time acquisition correction, realignment and simultaneous correction of field inhomogeneities, an intermediate step identified outliers with framewise displacement exceeding 0.9mm and recomputed the mean without those volumes as well as interpolated those volumes, segmentation and co-registration of anatomical to functional images, normalisation to MNI space (same resolution), and liberal spatial smoothing was used given our focus on the brainstem (FWHM = 4mm). Data was then denoised using ICA-based automatic removal of artefacts (AROMA) optimised for movement noise, and we regressed out five principal components from a brainstem CSF mask.

Central Autonomic Network

We investigated central ANS dysfunction across a brainstem-cortical network with eight regions-of-interest (ROI), based on anatomical and functional atlases, that was divided into four types of ROIs. Medullary: rostro-ventrolateral medulla (RVLM) responsible for blood pressure regulation, and nucleus of solitary tract (NST) a major integrator of autonomic afferent signalling; Relay: medial parabrachial (MPB) and periaqueductal grey (PAG); Hypothalamus; and Cortical: central medial nucleus of the amygdala (CeA), anterior insular cortex (AIC), and subgenual anterior cingulate cortex (sgACC). To improve data quality and signal-to-noise ratio of 3T data we averaged bilateral ROIs and used spherical atlas-informed approximations of the RVLM, NTS and MPB.

Dynamic Causal Modelling

Spectral Dynamic Causal Modelling 4 was used to infer effective connectivity between the ROIs per individual. After optimizing these, we used Parametric Empirical Bayes 5 to investigate the relationship between the group-level neuronal parameters and several covariates: laying to 3-min standing difference in systolic blood pressure, the same change for diastolic blood pressure, age, and a binary-variable controlling for use blood pressure medications. Finally, we investigated the role of intra-connectivity within types of ROIs, the role of IN-connectivity into types of ROIs (i.e. how important the influence of other regions on this type is), and the role of OUT-connectivity from a type of ROIs to others (i.e. the importance of this type of ROIs influence on others). This was done by combining reduced models of these hypotheses (Figure 1), which led to 662 unique combinations and a factorial model space of 6622 to assess commonalities and differences. Bayesian model comparison assessed the evidence for each model, and each family of factor-levels.

Results

Population and test scores

The 81 individuals afflicted by PD had the diagnosis for an average of 1.2 ± 0.67 years and were on average 59.55 ± 9.38 years old. HC were on average 61.45 ± 8.84 years old. Both groups showed preserved cognitive scores in the MoCA (PD: 26.96 ± 2.35; HC: 27.04 ± 1.99). Neurological testing showed an average score of 18.89 ± 8.68 for the motor assessment (UPDRS-III) in the PD group and 1.12 ± 1.55 for the HC group (p < 0.001), with a Hoeh & Yahr score of 1.27 ± 0.5 for the former and zero for the latter. With regards to autonomic function, the PD group presented with higher dysfunction according to the total score of the SCOPA-AUT (PD: 10.96 ± 6.33; HC: 7.13 ± 6.14; p < 0.05) and to the cardiovascular sub-score (PD: 0.65 ± 0.80; HC: 0.23 ± 0.43; p < 0.05). 19 out of the 81 individuals with PD (23.46%) and 14 out of the 65 HC (21.54%) used blood pressure medication, which was included as a covariate in subsequent analyses.

Model performance 

The average variance explained by the individual dynamic causal models was 89.17 ± 2.28% (range: 83.18% to 95.30%), which confirmed a good-fit between the estimated and real cross-spectral densities.

Changes in central ANS effective connectivity with drops in blood pressure

We used Parametric Empirical Bayes to estimate group-level effects across covariates and neural connections, and Bayesian Model Reduction to investigate specific hypotheses on the most relevant types of neural connections. Specifically, these were intra-connectivity, in-connectivity (influence of others on the region-type), and out-connectivity (influence of the region-type on others).

Across all unique reduced models, the highest joint posterior probability explaining commonalities and differences was 34.50%, which associated blood pressure drops during the standing task with, intra-connectivity in relay regions, outwards connections from medullary regions and the hypothalamus, and inwards connections into the relay and cortical regions. The seemingly low explanatory power must be considered in the context of the elevated number of models which lead to evidence dissolution (e.g. the random probability is ), nonetheless it meant there was no single best explanation for the data. Hence, we investigated the role of specific levels of the factors of interest by pooling all models which shared the same level of a factor, in practice ignoring the variability across other factors. The pooled probability was 68.19% for intra-connectivity within relay regions, 87.15% for out-connections from the medulla and hypothalamus, and 49.17% for in-connections into relay and cortical regions, which highlighted that connections departing from the medulla and hypothalamus had a high explanatory power regardless of what happened in other factors. To confirm these findings, we conducted an automated parameter search across all possible combinations of ON or OFF connections (i.e. hypothesis free) and obtained an average of each connection value weighed by their respective model probability. The final parameters, with a 99% threshold probability of being present different than 0, were in-line with the most likely model from the group-factor analyses but included one connection outside the hypotheses-space (as well as self-connections never tested in the hypotheses space; Figure 2A).

We repeated the same analyses for the HC group. In this group, drops in blood pressure during the standing task are associated with a pooled probability of 95.42% for intra-connectivity within cortical regions, 38.79% for out-connections from relay, cortical regions and hypothalamus, and 60.54% in-connections into relay, cortical regions and the hypothalamus. An automated parameter search (Figure 2B) revealed findings in agreement with the hypotheses-based analysis but additionally included one connection from the hypothalamus to the NST.

Conclusion

We investigated how effective connectivity in the brainstem-cortex ANS axis contributed to drops in blood pressure during a standing task in individuals recently diagnosed with PD. Our findings reveal that at rest, individuals with PD show changes in how medullary regions and the hypothalamus project to other regions, particularly to relay nuclei in the pons and midbrain, and to the cortex. Moreover, we also identified a predisposition of the RVLM for self-inhibition, in patients only. These findings are compatible with known pathophysiology of the disease and may predispose individuals with PD to poorer central ANS reactivity when an orthostatic stimulus is present, leading to higher drops in blood pressure.

A core feature of PD is accumulation of misfolded α-synuclein in the medulla oblongata, a finding that is present at the earliest detectable disease stage. Various medullary centres are particularly afflicted by this, and in general, efferent pathways tend to be more afflicted. One of the main afflicted medullary nuclei is the RVLM, which is the main centre for sympathetic outflow to increase blood pressure via action on peripheral and coronary vessels as well as cardiac muscles. Damage to this area is known to impair the capacity to up-regulate blood pressure, in animal models, and similar mechanisms are believed to be present in humans. Our findings indicate that stronger self-inhibition of the RVLM is one of the key connections explaining the neural data of individuals with PD who experience stronger blood pressure drops during the standing task. This is in-line with known features of disease progression and physiology of blood pressure regulation, as self-inhibition may result from α-synuclein-mediated damage. Interestingly, healthy controls showed the opposite effect, presenting less self-inhibition of the RVLM. Additionally, the PD model was dominated by bottom-up connectivity and identified outgoing excitatory connections from the RVLM to the medial parabrachial (a relay nuclei) and the centromedial nucleus of the amygdala. These may not be directly linked to RVLM-activity, but rather to increased state signalling to higher-order regions due this region’s decreased ability to regulate blood pressure. Finally, we also included the NST in medullary regions. The NST is the main integrator of information from baroreceptors, cardiac receptors, amongst others relaying the state of the cardiovascular system, and despite being part of afferent pathways it is amongst the earliest afflicted nuclei (even if not to the same extent as efferent ones). The NST plays a key role in the blood regulation circuit. When blood pressure drops, excitatory input to the caudal ventrolateral medulla decreases, which releases the constant inhibitory input of the latter on the RVLM and allows efferent flow to up-regulate blood pressure. Our model identified increased excitatory input from the NST to the RVLM, at rest, in relation to drops in blood pressure, which may seem paradoxical but could represent part of the abnormal baseline susceptibility of the central ANS in these patients, impairing an appropriate reaction to orthostatic challenges.

Despite some limitations with respect to the resolution of the functional images which forced the approximation of some ROIs, our investigation into in-vivo function of the brainstem-cortical axis in PD shed light into important dysfunctional mechanisms which had not yet been studied beyond animal models or hypothesised from post-mortem human work. This work provides both evidence that fMRI can be reliably used to study the brainstem and is a strong proof-of-concept for further higher resolution functional neuroimaging studies into this matter. This is especially important as describing these mechanisms in-vivo in patients with established autonomic failure may provide useful progression markers and identify those in the prodromal period who may be at more risk of developing autonomic dysfunction.
