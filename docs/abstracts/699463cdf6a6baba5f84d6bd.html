Introduction

Coordinate-based meta-analyses (CBMAs) aim to quantitatively synthesize neuroimaging findings associated with a topic of interest by identifying spatial convergence of reported peak coordinates across individual studies1,2. Conventional activation likelihood estimation (ALE), the most commonly used CBMA method, tests this convergence against a null hypothesis of uniform spatial distribution of effects3. However, a non-uniform “reporting baseline” exists in the neuroimaging literature, i.e., certain brain regions are consistently more likely to be reported as significant. This occurs irrespective of the topic, potentially biasing meta-analytic findings4,5. Specific (co)activation likelihood estimation (SALE) has been proposed as an approach to account for such bias, but has been primarily limited to meta-analytical connectivity mapping studies5. Furthermore, the original algorithm lacks a robust correction for multiple comparisons, largely due to high computational costs. Here, after characterizing the spatial pattern of the reporting baseline, we propose a more rigorous, graphics processing unit (GPU)-accelerated SALE approach. This novel approach explicitly accounts for the regional differences of reporting likelihood and extends the original SALE algorithm5 by applying cluster-level family-wise error correction (cFWE). We demonstrate the utility and higher specificity of the cFWE-SALE relative to conventional ALE by performing meta-analyses on 47 behavioral domains using coordinates data of studies include in the BrainMap dataset.

Methods

We characterized the spatial heterogeneity of reporting likelihood across original studies and meta-analyses using two approaches. First, across original task-based fMRI and PET studies included in BrainMap (7,849 experiments), we convolved all reported coordinates with Gaussian kernels and aggregated them to generate a map, hereafter referred to as the reporting baseline map. Second, across meta-analyses, we performed an umbrella meta-analysis on convergent cluster coordinates, automatically extracted via pubget from 250 ALE studies available in PubMed Central, and quantified their unthresholded convergence.

To understand what factors may contribute to the reporting baseline, we evaluated its spatial association with the following maps: (i) a task-generic map obtained by contrasting various tasks against rest using subject-level data from a multi-domain task battery, (ii) cerebral blood flow, (iii) the sensorimotor-association axis, and (iv) canonical resting-state networks.

We then developed a refined cFWE-SALE procedure that accounts for the reporting baseline while correcting for multiple comparisons. In this approach, across all experiments within a topic, we first compute an observed ALE score map using the standard approach, which quantifies uncorrected and unthresholded convergence of foci across the brain. Next, null ALE maps are generated by sampling random foci weighted by the reporting baseline map, and voxel-wise null distributions are constructed to obtain observed and null p-value maps. These p-value maps are then thresholded to extract observed cluster masses and a null distribution of maximum cluster masses, which are then used to derive pcFWE, thresholded at < 0.05 (Fig. 1). We implemented the meta-analyses on GPUs (https://github.com/amnsbr/nimare-gpu), reducing runtime for each analysis from hours to minutes. We applied cFWE-SALE and standard cFWE-ALE to 47 BrainMap behavioral domains. Between-domain similarity was quantified using Pearson and Dice correlations and was compared between the two methods using paired T-tests.

Results

The reporting baseline across original studies peaked in association areas such as the insula, anterior cingulate cortex, and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (Fig. 2A). A similar (r = 0.89, pvariogram < 0.001) pattern of convergence was observed in the umbrella ALE meta-analysis across 250 ALE studies (Fig. 2B). 

The reporting baseline map showed significant spatial associations with task-generic contrast and cerebral blood flow maps, but not with the sensorimotor-association axis (Fig. 2C). Across canonical resting-state networks, the reporting baseline was significantly higher in the salience network (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, the reporting baseline was highly similar between fMRI and PET studies (r = 0.77, pvariogram < 0.001), suggesting the baseline was not purely related to modality-specific confounds. 

Between-topic correlation of unthresholded maps and Dice similarity of thresholded maps was significantly higher for ALE (r: 0.36±0.18; dice: 0.18±0.16) than for SALE (r: 0.02±0.17; dice: 0.02±0.09), indicating higher behavioral specificity of SALE meta-analytic maps (Fig. 2E). Example maps for “Action” and “Language” behavioral domains are shown in Fig. 2F, illustrating higher functional specificity of convergent regions identified by cFWE-SALE.

Conclusion

Our findings highlight that the likelihood of reporting significant effects in the neuroimaging literature is spatially heterogeneous across brain regions. This systematic heterogeneity likely reflects a combination of methodological confounds, biases in research practices, and intrinsic biological factors that together influence the likelihood of observing or reporting effects (activations) in specific brain regions. Importantly, this non-uniform reporting baseline of original studies ultimately propagates to meta-analytic ALE studies, which test against a null hypothesis of uniformly distributed effects. We introduced cFWE-SALE, a meta-analytic algorithm that takes the reporting baseline into account, and by doing so, leads to higher functional specificity of meta-analytic findings. Future work is needed to assess the utility of cFWE-SALE across additional domains and imaging modalities, and further explore the sources and implications of the reporting biases in neuroimaging research.
