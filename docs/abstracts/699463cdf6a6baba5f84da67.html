Introduction

A unified sense of self emerges from the integration of bodily and cognitive processes. While the “bodily self” relies on pre-reflective multisensory and sensorimotor signals, the “cognitive self” relies on higher-order cognitive processes that allow individuals to reflect on and attribute mental contents to themselves, such as episodic and autobiographical memory1. A key feature shared by both domains is self-location, defined as the subjective experience of situating oneself in space and/or time. Bodily self-location refers to the experience of occupying a position in physical space, typically within the boundaries of the body. In contrast, cognitive self-location refers to the capacity to mentally project the self across subjective time, enabling the recollection of past events or the imagination of future ones through autonoetic consciousness and mental time travel2. Neuroimaging and clinical studies suggest that bodily and cognitive aspects of the self rely on partially overlapping neural substrates, notably involving the temporo-parietal junction, posterior parietal cortex, and hippocampal networks. However, despite growing evidence that bodily and cognitive aspects of the self interact and share a common network3 , the relationship between bodily self-location and cognitive self-location remains poorly understood. The present study addresses this gap by systematically integrating causal evidence from focal brain lesions, intracranial electrical stimulation in patients with neurological disorders, and correlative evidence from functional neuroimaging studies in healthy participants. 

Methods

We conducted a systematic review of the literature in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines to identify studies reporting alterations of self-location induced by electrical brain stimulation (EBS), focal brain lesions, or experimental manipulations in healthy participants. Included studies comprised: (i) patients in whom disturbances of self-location were elicited by intracranial EBS (stereoelectroencephalography (SEEG), electrocorticography (ECoG), direct cortical stimulation during awake brain surgery, or deep brain stimulation (DBS)); (ii) patients with incidental focal brain lesions associated with disturbances of self-location; and (iii) healthy participants in which self-location was experimentally manipulated.

Inclusion criteria. For bodily self-location, we included phenomena involving illusory displacement or changes in perceived self-location: 1) out-of-body experiences, in which the self is perceived as located outside the physical body; 2) heautoscopy, characterized by seeing a visual double accompanied by unstable self-location 3) illusory self-motion (e.g., sensations of whole-body translation, falling, or floating). In healthy participants, we included fMRI or PET studies in which bodily self-location was experimentally manipulated, most often through full-body illusion paradigms. For cognitive self-location, we included illusory experiences of self-projection in time and space, with a specific focus on projection into the past accompanied by a subjective sense of re-experiencing the event, particularly when induced by EBS. In lesion studies, we carefully distinguished between patients who retained factual knowledge of past events and those who specifically lacked the capacity for subjective re-experiencing. Only cases with explicit descriptions of impaired autonoetic consciousness, capacity to mentally travel into the past, were included in order to dissociate cognitive self-location deficits from general episodic memory impairments. In healthy participants, we included fMRI or PET studies employing experimental paradigms that manipulated self-location in time, such as mental time travel tasks.

Data extraction. For each publication, we extracted general study information (authors, year, region) and participant details (sample size, number of cases, diagnosis, lesion characteristics). For EBS studies, we extracted detailed methodological information, including stimulation modality (SEEG, ECoG, or during awake surgery), targeted brain regions, and stimulation parameters. For each stimulation site that evoked a self-location disturbance, anatomical information was recorded (site of stimulation, stereotactic coordinates when available). Only EBS studies reporting MNI or Talairach coordinates, or providing sufficient anatomical detail for reliable estimation, were included; all coordinates were converted to MNI space. 

In lesion studies, we extracted lesion etiology and anatomical location for cases presenting altered self-location. Lesion locations were reconstructed by manually tracing published lesion images onto a standard MNI template using 3D Slicer. Only lesion studies providing sufficiently detailed neuroanatomical images to allow reliable reconstruction were included in the lesion-based analyses.

For functional neuroimaging studies, all reported activation peaks and detailed task descriptions were extracted.

Functional connectivity analysis. To investigate networks underlying bodily and cognitive self-location, we assessed resting-state functional connectivity for all EBS sites, lesions, and fMRI peaks using a unified approach. Whole-brain seed-based connectivity maps were generated for each anatomical location using a normative connectome from 1,000 healthy adults4. A sensitivity analysis identified voxels consistently connected across anatomical locations (threshold: t ≥ 7), enabling the identification of common and modality-specific networks while ensuring comparability across EBS, lesion, and fMRI data.

Results

Literature review. Of the 5,175 articles screened, 13 EBS studies reporting alterations in bodily self-location were included, along with 10 studies describing focal brain lesions affecting bodily self-location and 3 fMRI studies in healthy participants. For cognitive self-location, 7 EBS studies providing stereotactic coordinates reported stimulation-induced alterations in cognitive aspects of the self, including mental time travel and autonoetic consciousness. In addition, 5 lesion studies and 12 fMRI studies in healthy participants investigating cognitive self-location were included.

Functional connectivity analysis. Our preliminary results focus only on electrical brain stimulation and fMRI studies. 

Electrical Brain Stimulation. Sensitivity analyses of resting-state functional connectivity revealed distinct but partially overlapping networks for stimulation sites evoking bodily versus cognitive self-location. Bodily self-location–evoking sites were strongly connected to sensorimotor regions with 80% showing functional connectivity to the middle cingulate cortex, primary somatosensory and motor cortices (postcentral and precentral gyri), and cerebellum, consistent with the integration of multisensory and motor signals. In contrast, cognitive self-location–evoking sites were functionally connected in 85% of cases to a distributed network including the precuneus, hippocampus, amygdala, superior frontal gyrus, inferior parietal lobule, and both posterior and anterior cingulate cortices. Taken together, these results indicate overlapping connectivity for bodily and cognitive self-location within the cingulate cortex and posterior parietal regions, including the precuneus.

fMRI. Sensitivity analyses of fMRI studies manipulating bodily self-location showed that 88% of peak activation sites were functionally connected to the precuneus and parahippocampal gyrus, highlighting the role of integrative sensorimotor and spatial processing regions in anchoring the self in space. For cognitive self-location across paradigms, 90% of peak activation sites were functionally connected to the posterior cingulate cortex, precuneus, superior frontal gyrus, and precentral gyrus, indicating that cognitive self-location engages both core default mode network nodes and sensorimotor regions. Taken together, these results reveal that bodily and cognitive self-location share overlapping connectivity primarily within the precuneus. 

Conclusion

By focusing on self-location, our study provides insight into the common neural substrates of the bodily and cognitive self. Our results provide converging evidence that, although conceptually distinct, bodily and cognitive self-location rely on partially overlapping neural networks. Bodily self-location appears to anchor the self in the present moment through sensorimotor and interoceptive signals, whereas cognitive self-location enables the self to disengage from the immediate here-and-now and project across subjective time, engaging medial temporal and fronto-parietal networks. The convergence of both forms of self-location in the posterior cingulate cortex and, notably, the precuneus suggests that these regions function as integrative hubs, supporting self-reference processing across spatial and temporal domains. The next step will be to extend this analysis to focal brain lesions.
