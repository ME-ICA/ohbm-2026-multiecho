Introduction

With continued scientific advancements, increased life expectancy has become a reality in many countries and with this, a critical rise in dementia cases has been observed. Models using structural Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) to predict the “brain age” have gained traction as potential biomarkers for neurodegeneration. Despite the paradigm's promise, there is a marked scarcity of brain age research originating from the Global South. This lack of representation is crucial because unique sociodemographic variables, such as inequality and education, significantly impact brain health and dementia profiles in such countries. Therefore, this work aims to contribute to this under-explored area by investigating the clinical potential of the brain age paradigm as a neurodegeneration biomarker within a specific Brazilian clinical dementia cohort, and to evaluate the influence of associated sociodemographic factors on brain age. 

Methods

The data analyzed consisted of a cross-sectional sub-sample from the IDOR Alzheimer's cohort, originally recruited between 2011 and 2018 for the study of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) as described in previous work1. Inclusion criteria required participants to be 45 years or older and have 8 or more years of education. The sample was categorized into: Cognitively Normal (CN, N=78), Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI, N=29), Alzheimer’s Disease (AD, N=36), and non-Alzheimer dementias (non-AD, N=18). 

T1-weighted images were processed using FreeSurfer v.7.2.0. Segmentation of regions-of-interest (ROIs) was based on the Desikan-Killiany atlas. Input features for the brain age model initially included cortical thickness, volume, and area of 68 ROIs, volume of 37 subcortical regions (segmented via the FreeSurfer pipeline), and the estimated Total Intracranial Volume (eTIV). 

A linear regression XGboost model was selected for brain age prediction. All initial metrics (242 variables) were standardized before application. The model was optimized using Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE), where feature subgroups were tested in increments of 5 variables, selecting the subset that produced the lowest Root-Mean Squared Error (RMSE). Leave-one-out cross-validation was used to maximize training and test opportunities and ensure result stability. 

A post-hoc correction proposed by Cole and colleagues2 was applied to the predicted age to deal with intrinsic systematic age bias. The Brain Age Gap (BAG), defined as the difference between the corrected predicted age and chronological age, was the primary outcome measure. To assess the impact of sociodemographic and clinical factors on the BAG, a multivariate linear model was performed, including sex, diagnosis, age, education, and socioeconomic status as predictor variables, alongside an interaction term between age and diagnosis. Differences between diagnostic groups were evaluated using estimated marginal means and corrected for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni correction. All statistical analyses were conducted using R v.4.5.1. 

Results

The brain age model with 242 variables performed with an R-squared of 0.31, Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of 4.77 years, and RMSE of 6.25 years. Recursive Feature Elimination selected an optimized model with 15 variables that yielded the lowest RMSE of 5.28 years (R-squared= 0.51; MAE = 4.1 years). After applying the post-hoc correction, the model performance metrics changed to R-squared = 0.51, MAE = 5.87 years, and RMSE = 7.29 years. These key variables included volume or thickness measurements from temporal (e.g., left superior temporal gyrus, right fusiform gyrus), frontal (e.g., left lateral orbito frontal cortex, left pars orbitalis), and subcortical regions (e.g., right hippocampus, right globus pallidus). 

The multivariate linear model analyzing the corrected BAG demonstrated that the BAG of the AD group (p < 0.001) and the non-AD group (p < 0.001) were significantly different compared to the CN group. Crucially, the socioeconomic variables of education, socioeconomic status and sex were not significant in explaining the corrected BAG, although education (p = 0.059) and male sex (p = 0.055) approached the margin of significance  

The AD group exhibited the highest estimated marginal mean BAG (9.4 years), followed by the non-AD group (8.9 years), the MCI group (2.8 years), and the CN group (-0.18 years). Conclusion

This work successfully applied a brain age prediction model to investigate neurodegeneration in a cross-sectional sample from a Brazilian dementia cohort, filling a notable gap in the literature regarding brain age analysis in the context of the Global South. The final model, using 15 predictor variables derived from structural MRI, demonstrated performance metrics aligned with current international literature. The primary finding validates the effectiveness of the corrected Brain Age Gap (BAG) in distinguishing between different clinical diagnostic groups. Regarding socio-environmental influences, the study found no significant interaction or influence of education, socioeconomic status, or sex on the corrected BAG. This outcome is inconclusive to confirm the previous findings in the literature that highlights the importance of high education and low inequality in neuroprotection, especially in the Latin American context. This non-significant finding is likely influenced by the restricted educational range criteria (8+ years of education) and the comparatively small sample size used for training (N=78 CN subjects), suggesting that a larger, more socio-economically diverse sample is necessary to accurately assess the impact of these variables in the Brazilian context.
