Introduction

Temporal Interference (TI) stimulation is a relatively recent brain stimulation modality, which has the potential of reaching deep brain targets non-invasively. In essence it is a variation of transcranial alternating current stimulation (tACS), where two electrical signals at slightly different kHz frequencies are applied at two different electrode pairs respectively. The basic assumption behind TI stimulation is that the neurons react mostly to the envelope of the total signal rather than to its high frequency components. Although the principle of TI for stimulating neural tissue was first proposed in the 1950s, the ability of TI to stimulate deep brain targets in mice was first demonstrated in 2017 by Grossman et al.1 Since then, several research groups have embraced this method and explored its potential to stimulate the human brain for various diagnostic and therapeutic applications.2 

In this work we perform a systematic in-silico exploration of the potential of TI to stimulate brain tissue with sub-centimeter resolution in a representative head model. The stimulation parameters that determine which brain areas will be directly excited by TI are the locations of the electrodes and the applied current ratio. By performing an unbiased and random sampling of the aforementioned parameter space, we simulate the TI field distribution for each parameter combination and record only those patterns that are mono-focal and of sub-centimeter size. The final result is a map of voxels in a typical brain which highlights which areas can be stimulated with sub-centimeter resolution. For the dataset and excitation threshold that we chose it can be shown that at least 20% of gray and white matter can be reached non-invasively and focally with TI.

Methods

The basic TI configuration consists of two pairs of electrodes placed on the scalp of the subject, where each pair is fed by an alternating current (AC) signal with its own frequency in the kHz range (1kHz-10kHz). The difference f1-f2 of the two frequencies f1, f2 is below 100 Hz. In order to calculate the electric field produced by an arbitrary pair of electrodes we used the SimNIBS open source software3, which uses the quasi-static approximation of Maxwell’s equations. The dataset that was used for the simulation (T1 and T2 structural MRI images) is the “ernie” example subject provided by SimNIBS itself. The values of tissue conductivities were assigned according to the Gabriel et al.4 database.

After calculating the electric fields corresponding to the two electrode pairs and frequencies, a novel averaging technique was used to calculate the mean TI stimulation amplitude. Since the main stimulation effect is due to the projection of the electric field along the main neuronal orientation, the TI modulation depth for each possible orientation was calculated at each voxel, and averaged based on the assumption of uniform distribution of orientations. The complete simulation pipeline can be seen in fig. 1.

In order to calculate the electric field produced by any possible electrode configuration on the scalp, we uniformly sampled the allowed placement positions on the example subject’s scalp with Ne=188 samples. For each of these electrode positions we used a common reference electrode to form a pair and compute the induced electric field. This way, the field of an arbitrary pair of electrodes can be computed as the difference of the fields they produce with respect to the common reference. There are Nd=17578 possible electrode pairs derived from the initial sampling of the scalp, which gives rise to more than 154 million possible TI configurations. An unbiased random subsample of these configurations was taken into account, and for each configuration the TI modulation depth (“TI pattern”) was calculated for a range of current ratios between the two electrode pairs. For each current ratio the TI pattern was transformed into a binary voxel map, according to whether the TI modulation exceeded or not a relative threshold (70% of the maximum value). If for any current ratio the binary voxel map turned out to be monofocal (i.e. a single, connected voxel cluster) with volume less or equal than 1 cm3, we marked the voxels belonging to this optimal cluster as “reachable”. This way, for each TI combination of our random sample, a subset of voxels in the brain were marked as “excited” or “reachable” by TI (if said TI combination can produce at least a single monofocal cluster of volume ≤1 cm3). The set of all these voxels is the so-called “feasible” or “reachable” sub-set of the brain that can be focally stimulated.

To sum up, the pipeline for computing the feasibility map of our example subject for TI stimulation comprises the following steps:

1. Uniform sampling of the portion of the scalp surface where electrode placement is allowed.

2. Calculation of the stimulated electric field for each electrode pair with the help of SimNIBS.

3. Random unbiased selection of pairs of dipoles from all possible TI combinations.

4. Calculation and threshold-clustering of the TI pattern for the chosen pairs of electrodes for different current ratios.

5. If a monofocal sub-centimeter cluster exists for any of the tested current ratios, marking of the cluster’s voxels as reachable.

6. Collection of all marked voxels into a comprehensive “reachability” map of the subject’s brain.

Results

In total we sampled randomly 624 272 TI configurations out of 154 484 253 possible configurations (the latter corresponding to a scalp discretization of 188 possible electrode locations). This unbiased sample size corresponds to approximately 0.4% of the global parameter space. After computing the TI patterns for each of the sampled configurations, the percentage of excitable brain tissue (gray and white matter) that can be stimulated monofocally with sub-cm resolution was found to be at 20%. In detail:

> 16.00 % of gray matter

> 24.99 % of white matter

can be stimulated monofocally with sub-cm resolution. This percentage is expected to be only a lower bound, since the volume of stimulated tissue is increasing as the sample size increases, albeit with a continuously dropping rate (i.e. the increase of covered volume appeared to be saturating as the exploration of the parameter space progressed).

However, when we computed the percentage of gray matter tissue that can be reached in the same manner in some typical sub-cortical regions, like the hippocampus, amygdala and thalamus, the results were much lower. In particular:

~0.26 % of hippocampus

~0.95 % of amydgala

~0.53 % of thalamus

can be stimulated monofocally with sub-cm resolution.

The computed feasibility map along with the aforementioned subcortical structures and the T1-MRI of the example subject can be seen in fig. 2.

The above results prompted us to investigate the reasons why these sub-cortical regions are much more difficult to reach with TI than cortical regions. A closer look at the electric field pattern in the vicinity of these regions showed that there is a noticeable drop in electric field intensity when crossing the boundaries of these regions. A correlation of the field pattern with the tissue segmentation revealed that this drop happens when passing from white to gray matter. This observation is consistent with the boundary conditions of the electric field in conductive matter, which state that the ratio of the normal field components on either side of the tissue boundary is inversely proportional to the ratio of the respective conductivities. Since white matter has a lower conductivity than gray matter, the field is expected to drop when passing from white to gray matter. Thus, a biophysical explanation for the difficulty in stimulating the aforementioned subcortical regions is that they are partially in contact with white matter, which attenuates the electric field arriving from their exterior.

Conclusion

We have explored in-silico the ability of TI to stimulate brain regions focally with sub-cm resolution by using a publicly available example subject dataset and the open source simulator SimNIBS in combination with in-house developed code for the calculation of the TI modulation depth and the systematic sweep of the TI parameter space. This study reveals both the potential and the limitations of this stimulation method.

In particular, it was found that TI can stimulate at least 20% of the excitable brain tissue focally and with sub-cm resolution (~16% of gray matter and ~25% of white matter), when the threshold of stimulation is placed at 70% of the maximum achieved stimulation value. On the other hand, basic sub-cortical structures like the hippocampus, amygdala and thalamus can barely be reached focally with such high resolution. One important reason for this fact is that these structures are partially surrounded by white matter, which causes a local drop in electric field intensity.

Only a small proportion of the global parameter space of possible TI configurations has been explored, and the existence of extremely fine-tuned TI combinations that can stimulate aforesaid sub-cortical structures cannot be ruled out at this point. However, the above results hint at the need for novel non-invasive techniques that can reach deeper sub-cortical tissues while remaining focal. One such technique, based on the TI principle, is the multipolar TI (mTI), which uses more than two pairs of frequencies in order to increase the focality of the stimulation. This method has been proposed relatively recently5 and shows promising results as far as improving focality is concerned. A systematic exploration and optimization of mTI in the context of non-invasive, deep, focal stimulation is currently underway by the authors and will be presented in future work.
