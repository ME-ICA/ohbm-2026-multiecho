Introduction

Poor sleep quality impairs neural efficiency and cognitive performance ^1^2. This is seen during aging which is accompanied by changes in sleep patterns that influence physical and cognitive health^3^4. While subjective assessments of sleep quality (e.g., Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, PSQI) capture behavioral and experiential aspects of sleep, waking EEG provides objective assessment of brain function. Brain complexity, for example, is proposed as a sensitive marker of alteration in neurocognitive function with ageing. This multiscale index reflects the balance between neural integration and segregation. Compared to linear analyses, nonlinear approaches, such as this, are better able to characterize spatiotemporal properties of dynamic brain activity^5. Few studies have examined how subjective sleep quality influences EEG-derived measures of wake brain complexity. Here, we apply machine-learning classifiers to nonlinear complexity features—including connectedness, self-similarity, and entropy—to determine whether these metrics distinguish healthy older adults with good versus poor sleep, potentially identifying neural markers linked to everyday cognitive function.

Methods

Resting-state EEG was collected from 32 healthy older adults (aged 60+), categorized by PSQI into 9 good sleepers and 25 poor sleepers. EEG recordings (128 channels, 5 minutes, eyes closed) were preprocessed, normalized, and decomposed using the discrete wavelet transform to extract delta (1–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), and alpha (8–13 Hz) frequency bands. Ten nonlinear complexity metrics—approximate entropy, correlation dimension, detrended fluctuation analysis, energy, Higuchi fractal dimension, Hurst exponent, Katz fractal dimension, Boltzmann–Gibbs entropy, Lyapunov exponent, and Shannon entropy—were computed from 5-second epochs. Six statistical descriptors were extracted for each metric (mean, standard deviation, 95th percentile, variance, median, and kurtosis) yielding 180 features per subject per channel. Feature selection was performed using Mann–Whitney U tests with Bonferroni correction across all channels and features, retaining only statistically significant metrics for training. Eighteen machine-learning classifiers were trained and evaluated using leave-one-out cross-validation. Classification performance was assessed using precision, recall, specificity, accuracy, F1-score, and the area under the curve (AUC), providing a comprehensive evaluation of predictive performance.

Results

Nonlinear EEG complexity features enabled robust classification of sleep quality in healthy older adults. In the delta sub-band, k-nearest neighbors (KNN) achieved the best performance at channel C11 (right central-parietal) with a precision of 1.00 and an AUC of 0.95. In the theta sub-band, the stochastic gradient descent classifier (SGDCV) excelled at channel C15 (right frontal; precision = 0.95; AUC = 0.91). For the alpha sub-band, quadratic discriminant analysis (QDA) performed best at channel C32 (left frontal; precision = 0.95; AUC = 0.91). Considering all sub-bands together, SGDCV at channel A9 (left parietal) achieved high classification performance (precision = 0.95; AUC = 0.91), highlighting its potential value as a global marker of sleep-related neural complexity. These findings indicate that slow-frequency complexity in frontal, prefrontal, and parietal regions is sufficient to distinguish healthy older adults with good versus poor sleep quality (Figure 1).

Conclusion

Nonlinear EEG complexity, combined with machine-learning classifiers, precisely distinguishes healthy older adults with good versus poor sleep. Frontal and prefrontal regions (C11, C15, C32) showed strong discrimination across delta, theta, and alpha bands, suggesting that slow-frequency complexity reflects neural mechanisms supporting memory consolidation, executive function, and attentional control. Global analysis across all sub-bands highlighted channel A9 in the left parietal region (SGDCV: precision = 0.95; AUC = 0.91) as a robust marker of sleep quality, emphasizing the contribution of integrated neural networks to sleep-dependent brain dynamics. Collectively, these results demonstrate that healthy older adults with poor sleep exhibit distinctive patterns of neural complexity across multiple slow-frequency bands, which may reflect reduced efficiency in integrating neural information during wakefulness. Biologically, this could be related to altered coordination of excitatory and inhibitory neuronal populations or reduced synchrony of slow oscillations. The methodology—combining high-density EEG, multiscale nonlinear metrics, and machine-learning-based classifiers—provides a framework to identify spatially and spectrally specific neural signatures of sleep quality. This approach could inform interventions to improve sleep and cognitive resilience in aging and serves as an effective tool for characterizing how sleep shapes waking brain dynamics.
