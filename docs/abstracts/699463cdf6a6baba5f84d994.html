Introduction

Humans can infer the motor intention underlying an observed action within milliseconds, which is a fundamental skill for social cognition and effective social interaction. The Action Observation Network (AON) provides the anatomical substrate for this ability, a core neural system that maps others’ actions onto the observer’s motor repertoire [1]. While the AON is known for its adaptability through prior sensorimotor experience, it remains unclear whether this plasticity extends to support functional compensation following brain damage. 

Methods

Using magnetoencephalography (MEG), we examined the spectro-temporal dynamics underlying action comprehension in healthy individuals and in patients with brain tumors infiltrating the left inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), a core hub of the AON, before and after resection surgery. Participants performed an action observation task involving images of incomplete, ongoing actions embedded in naturalistic contexts that either facilitated or interfered with action recognition. Using an A2FC design, they were required to infer the overarching motor goal of observed actions while considering both the movement kinematics and the contextual information in which actions were embedded [2,3].

Results

In healthy participants, action comprehension elicited left-lateralized mu rhythm (8–14 Hz) desynchronization across a fronto-parietal network and ventral object-recognition regions, with stronger power decreases for incongruent relative to congruent actions. In contrast, patients exhibited a post-surgical rightward shift in mu activity, characterized by increased power in contralateral homologous regions, particularly under high-demand incongruent conditions. This right-lateralized mu activity, absent in healthy controls, was associated with improved post-surgical behavioral performance in most patients.

Conclusion

Overall, these findings highlight the AON’s dynamic capacity for neuroplastic reorganization in the face of brain damage, and identify mu rhythms as sensitive markers of compensatory plasticity supporting social cognition.
