Introduction

The perinatal period is a critical window of vulnerability for structural brain network development, during which disruptions can lead to a range of neurodevelopmental problems. Modelling patterns of cortical development during this period can provide insight into the long-term effects of perinatal injury at different post-menstrual ages, and enable the development of targeted interventions. Morphometric INverse Divergence (MIND) networks capture the structural similarity between pairs of cortical regions based on the Kulback-Leibler divergence between their vertex-level feature distributions, and have been shown to provide insight into structural brain changes across the lifespan.1,2 Whilst existing models of perinatal cortical development typically capture absolute structural changes such as gyrification, tracking age-related changes in MIND network edges captures the synchronisation or coupling of development across different cortical areas. Such coordinated maturation is informative because it reflects how distributed regions are organised into integrated developmental programmes, providing a systems-level description of cortical development beyond absolute regional change. Here, we tracked the maturation of neonatal MIND network edges across the perinatal period, to derive spatiotemporal maps of synchronised structural development. We then tested whether neonatal MIND networks could predict neurodevelopmental outcomes at 18-months, and interpreted key behaviour-predictive edges in the context of their spatiotemporal emergence.

Methods

Data Processing and MIND Network Construction

T2-weighted MRI scans from n = 775 neonates from the developing Human Connectome Project3 (scanned 27 to 43 weeks post-menstrual age, PMA) were processed through a pipeline of cortical surface reconstruction, feature extraction and regional parcellation according to the Surface-based Melbourne Children’s Regional Infant Brain (M-CRIB-S) atlas.4 We constructed MIND networks for each neonate from five vertex-level cortical surface features: cortical thickness, mean curvature, sulcal depth, surface area and volume, with each MIND network edge value capturing the multi-feature structural similarity between each pair of cortical regions. 

MIND Network Trajectory Clustering

We tracked the synchronisation of development of each MIND network edge across post-menstrual age. We fitted splines to their age trajectories, filtering for edge trajectories with significant spline fit (p < 0.05), then characterised and clustered edges by the time taken to reach 5, 25, 50, 75 and 95% of their peak maturation. We characterised each cluster according to its developmental timing, brain location, and overlap with Von Economo cytoarchitectonic classes, see Figure 1. 

Behavioural Outcome Prediction

Bayley III scores for motor, cognition, and language development, and Quantitative Checklist for Autism in Toddlers (Q-CHAT) were obtained at 18-months follow up for n = 620 neonates. We trained a connectome-based predictive model to predict these behavioural scores from MIND networks at birth age.5 Predictive performance was assessed according to correlation between predicted and actual scores. We mapped key behaviour-predictive edges onto the synchronised development clusters to identify the key timing of their development.

Results

Timing Clusters Relate to Cytoarchitectonic Maturation

We found that a 7-cluster solution was optimal, where edges in cluster 1 matured early, reaching 50% of their peak maturation at 31 weeks PMA, whilst higher clusters matured later, e.g. edges in cluster 7 reached 50% of their peak maturation at 40 weeks PMA. Mapping clusters onto Von Economo classes, we found them to represent the following: cluster 1: primary sensory development and insular integration with the rest of the brain, already emerging by 27 weeks PMA; cluster 2: primary motor integration with the rest of the brain, particularly with pre-established primary sensory networks, from 28 weeks PMA; cluster 3: insular development from 29 weeks PMA; cluster 4: secondary sensory development and integration from 30 weeks PMA; cluster 5: integration between association regions and the pre-established primary sensory network throughout the perinatal period; cluster 6: limbic development and association region integration, from 32 weeks PMA; cluster 7: association 2 development, from 32 weeks PMA, continuing beyond full term (Figure 1a). Newly developing networks tended to integrate with pre-established networks: primary motor networks integrated with existing primary sensory networks in the early perinatal period, and association areas integrated with all existing networks later in the perinatal period. 

MIND Networks Predict Behavioural Development

MIND networks at birth significantly predicted all four behavioural outcomes at 18-months (motor: r = 0.194, p < 0.001; cognition: r = 0.212, p < 0.001; language: r = 0.180, p < 0.001; Q-CHAT: r = 0.130, p = 0.002), see Figure 2a. These results improved on the predictive ability of birth age: (motor: r = 0.155, p < 0.001; cognition: r = 0.166, p < 0.001; language: r = 0.115, p < 0.001; Q-CHAT: r = 0.089, p = 0.033). 

By mapping behaviour-predictive edges back to our previously defined developmental clusters, we found that edges that significantly predicted motor development emerged earliest and were most likely to be found in developmental cluster 1, edges that predicted cognition emerged next and were most likely to be found in developmental clusters 1 and 6 (primary sensory and association regions), whilst edges that predicted language and Q-CHAT scores emerged later and were most likely to be found in developmental clusters 5, 6 and 7 (association regions), see Figure 2b.

Conclusion

Our results establish a spatiotemporal map of synchronised structural development during the perinatal period. MIND networks at birth also significantly predicted later neurodevelopmental outcomes including motor, cognition and language skills and the Q-CHAT. Isolating behaviour-predictive MIND network edges, we were able to use the synchronised developmental trajectory clusters to interpret the timing of their emergence during the perinatal period, with motor-predictive edges the first to emerge, followed by cognition, language and Q-CHAT-predictive edges.  

Overall, tracking synchronised structural development can inform our spatiotemporal understanding of perinatal development, and provide fresh insights into how individual differences in behavioural outcomes emerge.
