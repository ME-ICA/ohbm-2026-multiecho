Introduction

Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) is a well-established technique in systems neuroscience, used to non-invasively modulate cortical activity and assess both local excitability and large-scale brain dynamics. When combined with EEG, the stimulation elicits TMS-evoked potentials (TEPs), which reflect the brain’s response to stimulation with millisecond precision. These signals result from both local activation and propagated activity but are influenced by several factors. TEPs are typically analyzed by averaging trials and identifying peaks (e.g., N15, P30, N45, P60, N100), used as markers of excitability and inhibition. However, recent studies show that TEP features depend on stimulation intensity, coil orientation, the direction of the induced current and even minimal changes in coil position.1 Furthermore, the phase and power of ongoing alpha oscillations at the time of stimulation, as well as attentional and cognitive states, significantly affect the resulting potentials.2 These findings indicate that TEPs arise from complex interactions between stimulation parameters and the dynamic physiological state of the brain. To account for such complexity, precise control over stimulation is essential. Multi-locus TMS (mTMS) systems provide a key advancement by enabling electronic switching between stimulation targets without moving the coil. The five-coil mTMS device used in this study allows millimeter-level steering of stimulation, ensuring high spatial precision and timing accuracy.3,4 We combined mTMS with EEG to stimulate six adjacent sites in the sensorimotor cortex (M1 and S1, 5 mm apart). Using machine learning, we tested whether TEPs contain sufficient temporal information to accurately classify stimulation sites. We hypothesize that even with few trials per condition, the rich temporal dynamics of TEPs allow reliable decoding, supporting the development of real-time, closed-loop TMS–EEG protocols.

Methods

Eight healthy participants (four females, four males, aged 22–42 years) were recruited at Aalto University. All participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and with approval from the Ethics Committee of the Hospital District of Helsinki and Uusimaa (No. HUS/1198/2016). Before the mTMS–EEG session, each participant underwent structural, functional and diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to support individualized targeting and coregistration. The InVesalius software was used for neuronavigation with individual MRIs. The experimental session began with a 10-minute resting-state EEG recording divided into 5 minutes without muscle activation and 5 minutes with light pre-activation of the target muscle. During the mTMS–EEG experiment, a five-coil mTMS transducer was positioned over the left sensorimotor cortex. Within this region, we defined six stimulation sites arranged at 5-mm spacing. In the primary motor cortex (M1), the motor hotspot for the abductor pollicis brevis (APB) was identified using electromyography (EMG) and is referred to as M10. The resting motor threshold (RMT) was determined at the hotspot as the minimum intensity that elicited motor evoked potentials exceeding 50 μV peak-to-peak in at least five of ten trials. Two additional motor stimulation targets were then placed 5 mm lateral (M1–5) and 5 mm medial (M1+5) to the hotspot position. In the primary somatosensory cortex (S1) across the central sulcus, three homologous sites were defined using the measured M1–S1 separation, yielding S1–5, S10, S1+5. The induced electric field at each target was oriented approximately perpendicular to the central sulcus, with an anteriorly directed field for M1 and a 180° flipped, posteriorly directed field for S1 (Figure 1). Each site was stimulated with 150 single monophasic pulses delivered in pseudorandom order, for a total of 900 trials per participant across the six targets. The stimulation intensity was set to 110% of the individual RMT. The inter-stimulus interval was jittered between 3.7 and 4.2 s to reduce anticipation and habituation. EEG was concurrently recorded using a 64-channel cap and a NeurOne amplifier at 24-bit resolution and 5-kHz sampling (Bittium Biosignals Ltd., Finland). Preprocessing was performed in MATLAB R2023b (The MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). Continuous data were epoched from –1800 to +1800 ms around each TMS pulse. Trial rejection removed approximately 10% of trials due to eye blinks or muscle activity. To further minimize artifacts, the source-estimate-utilizing noise-discarding (SOUND) algorithm and signal-space-projection source-informed reconstruction (SSP–SIR) algorithms were applied. Cleaned data were then downsampled to 1 kHz. For TEP analysis, post-stimulus intervals from 30 to 1050 ms were extracted. Statistical analyses included repeated-measures ANOVA to identify latency-specific differences across sites. For machine learning, features consisted of voltage amplitudes across electrodes in sliding temporal windows. Linear support vector machines were trained in a one-versus-one scheme to classify stimulation sites, with cross-validation performed at the participant level. To assess robustness, the full analysis was repeated while progressively reducing the number of trials per site (130, 97, 65, 32, 13, 7 per site). 

Results

Figure 2A shows TEP time courses and latency-specific topographies at 50, 100, 200 and 300 ms for each stimulation site. Average TEPs recorded under-the-coil channels (C1, C3, CP1, and CP3) showed a significant main effect among targets in both M1 and S1 at multiple latencies. In particular, at 50 ms, TEPs significantly differed in both M1 (Repeated-Measures ANOVA, F2,14 = 37.23, p < 0.001) and S1 (rm-ANOVA, F2,14 = 52.39, p < 0.001), while other differences were found at 100 ms (rm-ANOVA, M1: F2,14 = 4.29, p < 0.01; S1: F2,14 = 6.52, p < 0.01) and 300 ms (rm-ANOVA, M1: F2,14 = 4.10, p < 0.05; S1: F2,14 = 3.10, p < 0.05). Temporal decoding analysis was used to predict the stimulation site from evoked activity. Specifically, a one-vs-one linear Support Vector Machine (SVM) was trained at each time point to distinguish sites in M1 (M1−5, M10, M1+5) and S1 (S1−5, S10, S1+5) separately. Decoding analyses showed that stimulation sites could be significantly predicted in the first 600 ms for M1 (Cluster-Based Permutation Test; CBPT, n = 500; p < 0.05) and in the first 670 ms for S1 (CBPT, n = 500; p < 0.05) using all trials (130, balanced among subjects and stimulation areas) (Figure 2B-C). Further analysis examined how the number of trials affects decoding accuracy, particularly relevant for online experiments. Reducing the number of trials from 130 (100%) to 97 (75%), 65 (50%), 32 (25%), 13 (10%), and 7 (6%) showed that with 100%, 75%, and 50% of trials, stimulation sites could be significantly predicted within the first 600 ms for M1 and 670 ms for S1 (CBPT, n = 500; p < 0.05). However, with only 6% of trials, significant classification persisted up to 180 ms for M1 and 200 ms for S1 (CBPT, n = 500; p < 0.05). To investigate the neurophysiological basis of stimulation site classification, we identified the most predictive EEG channels using the approach by Haufe et al.^5 For sake of simplicity, we focused on timepoints selected based on latencies identified in a classical TEP analysis (Figure 2A). In M1, distinguishing M10 from M1−5/M1+5 at 50 and 100ms primarily involved under-the-coil channels, while at 200 and 300 ms, occipital and frontal channels became engaged. Frontal channels distinguished M1−5 from M1+5 at early latencies, whereas occipital channels contributed at later timepoints. In S1, S10 was distinguishable from S1−5 using under-the-coil channels across all latencies, while S1−5 vs. S1+5 followed a similar pattern except at 100ms, where occipital channels became important. The differentiation between S10 and S1+5 showed a more complex temporal pattern involving under-the-coil, frontal, and occipital channels at different latencies (Figure 2D-E).

Conclusion

Our findings show that the temporal evolution of TEPs elicited with five-coil mTMS contains enough information to reliably distinguish stimulation sites separated by only 5 mm in M1 and S1. Site-specific signatures emerged from about 50 ms and extended up to 600–670 ms with full trial counts, while early components remained discriminative even with very few repetitions. This indicates that both local responses and later network dynamics encode precise spatial information, and that early temporal structure is particularly robust. The ability to decode stimulation site identity from short protocols suggests that temporally informed machine-learning approaches can make TMS–EEG more efficient and potentially suitable for rapid target selection, neuromodulation monitoring and closed-loop applications. Although validation across different populations, sessions and preprocessing pipelines is still required, this work provides a clear proof of principle that exploiting the temporal dimension of TEPs enhances the spatial specificity and practical feasibility of mTMS–EEG paradigms.
