Introduction

Epigenetic clocks derived from DNA methylation provide quantitative estimates of biological aging and have been linked to morbidity, mortality, and neurodegenerative risk. However, the neuroanatomical correlates of epigenetic aging remain incompletely characterized, particularly at the voxel level and across different clock formulations. Moreover, most existing neuroimaging studies of biological aging rely on cohorts from high-income, predominantly European-ancestry populations, limiting the generalizability of findings. Understanding how epigenetic aging relates to brain structure in underrepresented populations is critical for advancing equitable models of brain aging and neurodegeneration.

Methods

Structural T1-weighted MRI and peripheral blood DNA methylation data were analyzed from 215 participants recruited in Colombia, representing an underrepresented Latin American population and encompassing individuals with Alzheimer’s disease (AD; n = 100), frontotemporal dementia (FTD; n = 39), and cognitively normal controls (CN; n = 76). All participants underwent standardized clinical and neuropsychological assessment. MRI data were acquired using harmonized 3D T1-weighted protocols and processed using voxel-based morphometry (VBM) implemented in CAT12 within SPM12. Preprocessing included bias-field correction, tissue segmentation, spatial normalization to MNI space using DARTEL, modulation to preserve volumetric information, and spatial smoothing with an 8-mm full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel.

DNA methylation was measured from peripheral blood samples and used to compute multiple epigenetic clocks, capturing distinct dimensions of biological aging. These included canonical clocks (Horvath, Hannum), phenotypic and mortality-related clocks (PhenoAge, GrimAge), functional clocks (DunedinPACE, IntrinClock), and system-specific clocks reflecting metabolic, immune, and inflammatory aging processes. For each clock, epigenetic age acceleration was calculated as the residual or difference between epigenetic age and chronological age, providing an age-independent index of biological aging.

Voxel-wise multiple linear regression models were estimated to assess associations between gray matter volume and either raw epigenetic age estimates or epigenetic age acceleration indices. All models included sex, diagnostic group (patient vs. control), and total intracranial volume (TIV) as covariates; chronological age was additionally included when modeling raw epigenetic age. Directional contrasts tested both negative and positive associations between epigenetic aging metrics and regional gray matter volume. To directly compare epigenetic and chronological aging effects, models including both predictors were also evaluated.

Statistical inference was performed at the voxel level using an initial threshold of p < 0.001 (uncorrected), with false discovery rate (FDR) and family-wise error (FWE) corrections applied to control for multiple comparisons. Results were visualized using whole-brain glassbrain and cluster-level overlays.

Additionally, resting-state functional MRI data are being analyzed in a subsample to extend the investigation to functional connectomics. Ongoing analyses include preprocessing with established pipelines and the construction of whole-brain functional connectivity matrices, followed by graph-theoretical characterization of network topology. This multimodal approach is intended to evaluate whether epigenetic aging signatures observed in gray matter structure are paralleled by alterations in intrinsic functional networks.

Results

Across multiple epigenetic clocks, higher epigenetic age was consistently associated with lower regional gray matter volume, with convergent effects observed in the anterior insula, orbitofrontal cortex, medial temporal regions (including parahippocampal and fusiform gyri), and posterior cingulate cortex. Functional and phenotypic clocks—particularly DunedinPACE and IntrinClock—showed the most robust and spatially extensive associations, surviving false discovery rate correction. Canonical clocks (Horvath, Hannum, PhenoAge) demonstrated similar but more spatially restricted patterns.

Direct comparisons between epigenetic and chronological age revealed that epigenetic age explained additional variance in gray matter volume beyond chronological age, especially in fronto-insular and anterior temporal regions. No regions showed stronger associations with chronological age after correction for multiple comparisons.

Analyses using epigenetic age acceleration replicated and strengthened these findings. Greater biological age acceleration was associated with reduced gray matter volume in overlapping fronto-insular, medial temporal, and cingulate regions. These effects were generally stronger in patients with Alzheimer’s disease and frontotemporal dementia compared with cognitively normal controls, indicating an interaction between accelerated biological aging and neurodegenerative vulnerability.

Overlay analyses across clocks revealed substantial cross-clock convergence, with approximately 60% of clocks implicating anterior insular and orbitofrontal regions, and a smaller but consistent overlap in medial temporal and posterior cingulate cortices. Several system-specific clocks (e.g., liver- and OMIC-based metrics) did not show significant associations after correction.

Resting-state functional connectivity analyses in a subsample are currently ongoing and will assess whether these structural aging signatures are paralleled by alterations in large-scale functional network organization.

Conclusion

These findings demonstrate that peripheral DNA methylation–based measures of biological aging are robustly linked to region-specific patterns of gray matter loss, particularly within fronto-insular, medial temporal, and cingulate networks implicated in aging and neurodegeneration. Epigenetic age and age acceleration explained variance in brain structure beyond chronological age, underscoring their relevance as markers of biological brain aging. Importantly, this work provides neuroimaging evidence from an underrepresented Latin American population, addressing a critical gap in human brain mapping research. Ongoing functional connectomic analyses will further clarify whether these structural aging signatures extend to large-scale network organization, supporting a multimodal framework for studying brain aging across diverse populations.
