Introduction

Research funders worldwide have implemented diverse policies to increase data sharing and transparency among their grantees, including data management plan requirements, open access mandates, and data availability statement policies. However, there has been limited ability to systematically compare the effectiveness of these approaches across funders at scale. Understanding which funding agencies have higher rates of data sharing—and how these rates have changed over time—could provide valuable insights into which policies and incentive structures most effectively promote open science practices.

The PubMed Central Open Access (PMCOA) collection provides an opportunity to address this gap. With over 6.7 million full-text publications available for computational analysis, PMCOA enables large-scale text mining to identify data sharing statements across the biomedical literature. By combining tools for automated detection of open data availability statements (OddPub, Reidel et al. 2025) with tools for extraction of funding acknowledgments (rtransparent, Serghiou et al. 2021), we can estimate data sharing rates stratified by funding agency.

In this project, we developed an analytical pipeline to compare the relative frequency of data sharing between major funders of biomedical science. We processed the complete PMCOA collection using validated text mining tools to identify publications containing open data statements, extracted funder names using named entity recognition, and calculated year-wise data sharing percentages for major funding agencies across 15 years (2010–2024). Results are available through an interactive online dashboard at https://opensciencemetrics.org.

Methods

Data Source and Preprocessing

We downloaded 6.7 million articles with commercial and non-commercial licensed content in XML format from the June 2025 baseline of the PubMed Central Open Access (PMCOA) collection. Publications were processed using rtransparent, an R package for extracting transparency indicators from biomedical literature including conflict of interest disclosures, funding statements, trial registration, and data/code availability. Non-research article types (reviews, errata, commentaries, editorials, letters, and corrections) were excluded based on article-type metadata, leaving 5.5 million research articles for analysis.

Open Data Detection

Research articles were processed with OddPub version 7.2.3, a validated tool for detecting open data and open code availability statements in scientific publications. OddPub uses pattern matching and natural language processing to identify statements indicating that data has been made publicly available, distinguishing these from generic data availability statements that do not indicate actual sharing.

The current version of OddPub (v7.2.3) provides more conservative and accurate estimates compared to earlier versions, having been validated against manual annotation in recent studies. This version identified ~375k research articles with open data statements, representing approximately 6.8% of the research article corpus.

Funder Identification

Funder names were extracted from the funding acknowledgment sections of open-data research articles using Named Entity Recognition (NER).  The distribution of the funders followed a power law distribution. 125 top funders were identified and validated against funder entries in the OpenAlex database (Priem, J., Piwowar, H., & Orr, R., 2022)

Rate Calculation

For each identified funder, we counted the total number of research publications both with and without open data across publication years 2010 to 2024. Year-wise percentages of publications with open data were calculated by dividing the count of open-data publications by total publications for each funder-year combination.

Data Availability and Reproducibility

All code used for this analysis is available under CC0 license on GitHub (https://github.com/nimh-dsst/osm). Results are also accessible via an interactive online dashboard at https://opensciencemetrics.org, allowing users to explore data sharing trends by funder, country, and year.

Results

Our analysis identified substantial variation in data sharing rates across major biomedical research funders. Among 47 funders with sufficient publication volume for reliable estimates, data sharing rates ranged from 6.0% to 24.6% over the 2010–2024 period.

Top Performers

The Howard Hughes Medical Institute (HHMI) demonstrated the highest data sharing rate at 24.6% (2,503 of 10,189 research publications). Other high-performing funders included the United States Department of Agriculture (21.6%), India's Department of Biotechnology (19.5%), the Czech Science Foundation (18.7%), and the U.S. Department of Energy (17.9%).

European funders showed strong performance, with France's Agence Nationale de la Recherche at 17.5%, Germany's Max Planck Society at 17.4%, and France's Centre National de la Recherche Scientifique at 17.3%. The UK's Wellcome Trust achieved 14.4% with the largest absolute number among top performers (9,873 open-data publications from 68,683 total).

Major Funders by Volume

Among the largest funders by publication volume, the U.S. National Institutes of Health (NIH) showed 13.4% data sharing (57,220 of 426,267 publications), while China's National Natural Science Foundation showed 9.1% (38,274 of 420,824 publications). The European Commission achieved 13.0% (25,542 of 196,678), and UK Research and Innovation reached 12.1% (21,694 of 179,659).

Temporal Trends

Time-series analysis revealed generally increasing data sharing rates across most funders from 2010 to 2024, with acceleration visible after 2018 coinciding with increased adoption of data sharing policies. However, the rate of increase varied substantially between funders.

Conclusion

Our large-scale analysis of 5.5 million research publications demonstrates that data sharing rates vary consistently and substantially by funder, ranging from 6% to nearly 25%. This variation persists across geographic regions and funding agency types, suggesting that funder-specific policies and practices meaningfully influence researcher behavior regarding data sharing.

The current version of OddPub (v7.2.3) provides a lower and more accurate estimate of data sharing rates in biomedical literature than earlier versions and previous reports (6.8% vs. approximately 15% in earlier studies; Serghiou et al. 2021). The lower estimate reflects improvements and changes in the OddPub algorithm, especially with respect to statements that data will be made available upon request. However, recent mandates requiring data availability statements may have paradoxically decreased the sensitivity of automated detection tools. As more journals require standardized statements regardless of actual data sharing, distinguishing meaningful commitments to share data from boilerplate compliance language becomes more challenging.

Our findings have practical implications for research policy. Funders can use these comparative data to benchmark their grantees' data sharing practices against peer organizations and to evaluate the effectiveness of their open science policies. The substantial variation we observe suggests that some policy approaches may be more effective than others at incentivizing actual data sharing behavior.

The interactive dashboard at https://opensciencemetrics.org enables funders, researchers, and policymakers to explore these trends dynamically. Future work will expand the analysis to include open code sharing rates, examine specific policy interventions, and incorporate additional publication sources beyond PMCOA.

All analysis code and data are openly available (DOI: https://github.com/nimh-dsst/osm), enabling replication and extension of this work.
