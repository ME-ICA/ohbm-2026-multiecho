Introduction

Auditory working memory (AWM) is critical for the temporary maintenance of sensory information, particularly in real-world communication contexts. Despite extensive research on visual working memory, the encoding of auditory features in working memory and their neural representation have remained less explored. Previous work has suggested that sounds are stored in working memory as objects that include both task-relevant and task-irrelevant features1. An fMRI study showed that when both task-relevant features of the same object, namely pitch and location, had to be memorized, they were represented concurrently in working memory and were decodable from frontal and parietal cortex in addition to early auditory cortex2. However, it remains unclear whether both task-relevant and -irrelevant features are equally represented during the maintenance period and whether task relevance modulates their neural encoding into working memory. Here, we investigated whether we can decode both task-relevant and task-irrelevant features of an auditory object from magnetoencephalography (MEG) signals during an AWM task.

Methods

Following a behavioral screening session (inclusion criterion: >60% accuracy on voice identity non-match trials with location match), thirty participants (22 female, 8 male; age 19-30 years, M = 23.47, SD = 3.16) were included in the MEG experiment to perform a delayed match-to-sample task. Stimuli were spoken syllables that differed both in perceived spatial location and voice identity (i.e., specific combinations of timbre and pitch). Participants were instructed to memorize voice identity (the relevant feature), while location (the irrelevant feature) could be ignored. The task required a match non-match decision about a probe sound whose irrelevant feature value could either be identical to or differ from the memorized stimulus (see Figure 1). We acquired data continuously using a whole-head CTF MEG system with 275 axial gradiometers at a sampling rate of 1200 Hz. We performed time-resolved MVPA using linear support vector machine (SVM) to decode voice identity and spatial location from MEG sensor data. Classification was performed separately for each feature and for each period of interest to discriminate between higher versus lower-pitch voices and between left versus right-lateralized sounds using 5-fold cross-validation. We used a sliding time window approach, where each time window covered 400 ms of each channel and trial and was shifted by 10 ms steps for each iteration. We tested decoding performance against chance level and compared encoding strength between features using cluster-based permutation tests (10,000 permutations per test, cluster significance p < 0.05, family-wise error corrected). For behavioral analysis, accuracy and reaction times for correct trials were analyzed in separate 2x2 repeated-measures analyses of variance (ANOVA) with the factors Location Variation (match versus nonmatch) and Voice Identity Variation (match versus nonmatch).

Results

Behavioral analyses revealed a significant main effect of location change on accuracy (F(1,29) = 14.02, p < 0.001) as well as a significant interaction (F(1,29) = 11.48, p = 0.002) demonstrating that voice identity matches were detected more accurately and faster when the irrelevant location matched the probe as well, whereas for voice identity non-match trials, there was no effect of the irrelevant feature. Multivariate pattern analyses revealed that both task-relevant and -irrelevant features were decodable during the encoding phase (see Figure 2). The task-relevant voice identity (S1(M±SD) = 60.9% ± 1.4, S2((M±SD) = 61.3% ± 1.2) yielded higher classification performance compared to location (S1(M±SD) = 53.8% ± 1.2, S2((M±SD) = 52.7% ± 1.3). Cluster-based permutation tests on the difference time courses confirmed that voice identity decoding significantly exceeded spatial location decoding from approximately 260 ms to 1000 ms post-stimulus onset (relative to each stimulus) for both S1 and S2 encoding periods (cluster-corrected p < 0.001). In contrast, we have not found robust decoding of either feature during the delay phase.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the present behavioral findings support our previous observation of an effect of irrelevant feature changes on memory performance, suggesting that location information is encoded and memorized alongside voice identity. Our MEG analyses showed that both relevant and irrelevant features could be decoded during and immediately after the presentation of the sample stimuli. Here, task relevance seemed to modulate the decodability of the auditory features, with a better decoding performance for the task-relevant feature sound identity. However, neither the relevant nor the irrelevant feature could be decoded reliably during the maintenance phase with the present analysis approach based on evoked magnetic fields. Attempts are ongoing to assess whether stimulus-specific information during the delay period might be decodable with different, e.g., spectral signal-based, analysis methods.
