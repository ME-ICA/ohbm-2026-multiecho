Introduction

Across neuroimaging studies, it is well established that considerable variability exists both between and within individuals. This may in part be due to the diverse signaling properties of cortical inputs, some of which are known from animal studies to be widespread and variable in nature. Recent computational modeling work suggests that subtle cortical fluctuations that are linked to Nonspecific thalamocortical projections (NTPs) may have the function of maximizing information integration1. This subset of thalmocortical neurons are known to respond to changes in timing of somatosensory stimulation2, and they are known to regulate cortical arousal and attention by activating widespread areas of the cortex3. Furthermore, it has been shown that the circuit formed between the NTPs and the cortex is responsible for producing consciousness4. However, to date NTPs have hardly been studied in humans.

In contrast with NTPs, Core thalamocortical neurons are known to synapse with extremely high fidelity upon a specific and topologically compact cortical region. A highly studied circuit for the understanding of brain function uses median nerve stimulation (MNS) of the somatosensory pathway, which produces characteristic and focal responses at specific latencies through a Core nucleus of the thalamus to the cortex at about 20 (N20) and 22 ms (P22), resulting in 2 dipole sources within the hand area of S1 and M1, respectively. These inputs are thought to result in cortico-cortical responses as of 35 ms.

In order to understand the electrophysiological properties of NTPs in humans, the objectives of the current study are: (i) to non-invasively replicate previous findings that found distributed responses to MNS across the cortex using direct electrodes in patients5; (ii) to characterize these distributed sources as either NTPs, Core thalamocortical projections, or cortico-cortical based on their signaling properties.

Methods

Twelve healthy adults underwent MNS during magnetoencephalographic (MEG) recording. The experimental session was performed in 2 blocks, each lasting about 5-minutes, for a total of up to 1037 trials per block. Trials were subdivided according to block number (Block 1 or 2), as well as whether the current trial was an increase (INC), a decrease (DEC), or no change (NoC) in the interstimulus interval (ISI).

An Event-Related Beamformer (as implemented in the BrainWave MATLAB toolbox6, https://github.com/cheynelab) was used to localize the sources of somatosensory-evoked fields (SEFs) within the first 50 ms. The variance in these SEFs in space and time (i.e. source localization and peak latency) were quantified across the experimental blocks and trial types. The features were used in semi-supervised learning as follows. Four 4 machine learning models (Random Forest, Support Vector Machine, Neural Network and Naïve Bayes) were trained on the variance characteristics of several SEFs based on known origins: (i) sources within the secondary somatosensory cortex were used as labeled examples of Nonspecific thalamocortical sources (based on known anatomy); (ii) the N20 and the P22 were used as examples of Core thalamocortical SEFs; (iii) the m35 was used as an example of cortico-cortical SEFs. The model with the highest accuracy was then used to estimate the origins of the remaining SEFs. To determine the accuracy of the prediction, a Bayesian mixed effects model was used to compare labeled vs labeled + predicted SEFs on the features extracted above.

Results

Across all twelve subjects, 202 SEFs were found (mean = 15.8 per subject; range = 8-20; Figure 1). Of the 58 labeled SEFs, there was a main effect of ISI on spatial variance (p < 0.001, median R2 = 0.347), and a main effect of Block on spatial (p < 0.001, median R2 = 0.353) and temporal variance (p < 0.001, median R2 = 0.106). Pairwise comparisons revealed increases for NTP over Core and/or cortico-cortical SEFs for spatial variance due to ISI (all probability of direction (pd) > 95), temporal variance due to ISI (NTP> cortico-cortical, pd = 77), spatial variance due to Block (all pd > 88), and temporal variance due to Block (all pd > 84; cortico-cortical > Core, pd = 99.9). Only the features with main effects were used to train the machine learning models. Of the four models trained, Neural Network had the highest accuracy (0.829), and was used for estimating the population of the remaining (unlabeled) SEFs. Of the 202 SEFs, only 171 were found in all conditions, which were used for the prediction. The results were: NTP = 22 labeled and 42 predicted, Core = 24 labeled and 33 predicted, and cortico-cortical = 12 labeled and 38 predicted SEFs. A comparison of median R2 values revealed similar values across labeled and labeled + predicted SEFs.

Conclusion

Our analyses support the hypothesis that NTPs can be observed non-invasively in healthy adults. Furthermore, we observed that NTPs are variable in space and time in response to changes in timing of somatosensory stimulation, and also throughout the duration of the experiment. Lastly, we observed that the timing cortico-cortical inputs are similarly variable throughout the duration of the experiment. These findings help to explain that variance within subjects is repeatable and measurable: it’s a feature of NTP and cortico-cortical inputs, not a bug.
