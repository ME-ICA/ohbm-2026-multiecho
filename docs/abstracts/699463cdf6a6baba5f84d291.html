Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder and the leading cause of dementia worldwide, primarily affecting memory, language, cognition, and even emotional regulation.ˆ1 The pathological processes underlying AD begin long before clinical symptoms become apparent. Consequently, early and accurate diagnosis is essential but remains challenging due to the complex and multifactorial nature of the disease.ˆ2 Neuroimaging biomarkers are crucial in characterizing the temporal progression of AD pathology.ˆ1 MRI-derived morphometric (MO), microstructural (MS), and graph-theoretical (GT) metrics capture unique insights into neurodegeneration. Individually, each of these metrics has demonstrated utility for AD diagnosis and staging.ˆ3-5 For instance, structural atrophy patterns often represent early signatures of neurodegeneration, whereas network-level disruptions assessed through connectivity-based metrics offer complementary evidence of altered connectivity in AD.ˆ3 Despite these advances, it remains unclear whether combining an increasing number of heterogeneous imaging features improves classification and prediction across tasks, or if specific feature types are more beneficial for particular clinical tasks, such as distinguishing different stages of impairment, identifying early disease-related changes, or predicting disease progression. To address these gaps, this study systematically examines how integrating MO, MS, and GT metrics influences machine learning (ML)-based AD diagnosis and disease progression. Furthermore, we incorporate eXplainable AI approaches to enhance understanding of the decision-making processes in ML frameworks and support clinical translation.

Methods

Data were obtained from ADNI (https://adni.loni.usc.edu/), including T1w-sMRI and dMRI from 380 participants (220 cognitively normal [CN], 115 mild cognitive impairment [MCI], 45 Alzheimer’s dementia [ADD]). Three disease stage classification (DSC) tasks were evaluated (CN vs. MCI, CN vs. ADD, MCI vs. ADD), along with one longitudinal cognitive decline prediction (LCDP) task. The LCDP analysis used a subset of 155 subjects (88 CN, 47 MCI, 20 ADD), where annualized MMSE change (ΔMMSE) was computed as score difference divided by time in years (mean follow-up = 6.6 years). T1w-sMRI scans underwent FreeSurfer v7.4.1 (https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/) recon-all processing, including standard preprocessing, surface reconstruction, and extraction of cortical and subcortical features based on the 84-region Desikan–Killiany (DK) atlas. dMRI data underwent MRtrix3-based preprocessing (https://www.mrtrix.org/), FSL/ANTs corrections, tensor estimation, iFOD2 tractography with SIFT2 filtering, and T1-aligned connectome construction for extracting structural connectivity features.

For each subject, graph-theoretical (GT) features (n=589) were derived from diffusion-based connectivity matrices, including 574 node-level metrics (82 DK regions × seven measures: clustering, degree, eigenvector, closeness, betweenness, strength, PageRank) and 15 global network measures capturing topology and integration properties. Morphometric (MO) features (n=26) comprised mean cortical thickness across 10 cortical regions per hemisphere (including inferior and middle temporal, superior frontal, temporal pole, and parietal, supramarginal, precuneus, superior temporal, inferior parietal, and rostral middle frontal regions), volumes of 14 subcortical structures (including hippocampus, amygdala, thalamus, caudate, putamen, pallidum, and nucleus accumbens each from both hemispheres), and total CSF and brain parenchymal volume (BPV). Microstructural (MS) features (n=5) included whole-brain MD and FA, left and right hippocampal MD, and mean FA from the TBSS skeleton. Amyloid status (A–/A+) was incorporated as an additional feature across all experiments to capture amyloid-related pathophysiology. In total, each subject’s feature vector comprised 621 measures (26 MO + 5 MS + 589 GT + amyloid).

We developed a systematic ML framework to investigate how the incremental inclusion of MS and GT features to MO impacts interpretability and predictive accuracy. We employed seven classifiers and regression models with different learning behaviors, such as linear, tree-based, kernel, and ensemble methods for DSC and LCDP tasks, respectively. We incorporated a nested cross-validation (NCV) module into the training pipeline. The NCV module consisted of a 3-fold inner loop for hyperparameter optimization and a 5-fold outer loop for unbiased assessment of model performance. The ensemble estimator was constructed by selecting the top three performing base estimators. Additionally, we employed the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) technique to determine feature significance for better interpretability and clinical translation. Results

The results showed that the ensemble classifier outperformed base classifiers across all DSC tasks. For CN vs ADD, the highest performance (86.2±8.8% BACC, 93.2±3.2% accuracy, 93.0±3.3% F1-score, 93.7±4.5% rocAUC) was achieved using a combination of MO+MS+GT feature sets. However, the MO features alone exhibited comparable performance (85.2±8.7% BACC), whereas the MS features alone showed the weakest performance (63.3±10.6% BACC). In the MCI vs ADD task, MO features yielded the best results (74.9±6.5% BACC, 79.5±5.4% AUC), while the integration of MS and GT features was not effective in improving predictive performance, GT features alone yielding the lowest performance (58.9±9.3% BACC). In the CN vs. MCI task, MO features remained the strongest unimodal predictor (62.4±6.1% BACC), while MS yielded the lowest performance (55.2±4.4% BACC). In the LCDP task (ΔMMSE), the best prediction was achieved using MO features with SVR, producing the lowest MAE (0.371±0.10) and highest R² (0.279±0.20). The second-best performance was obtained with MO+MS (0.381±0.10 MAE), indicating marginal improvement by incorporating additional features. Explainability analyses using SHAP consistently identified amyloid status, CSF, and key MO features, particularly hippocampal volume, temporal pole, inferior temporal thickness, amygdala, and accumbens, as the most influential across tasks. In addition, the GT features such as node strength, PageRank, clustering, and betweenness in temporal-parietal areas specifically contributed to the CN vs. ADD classification. Conclusion

This study shows that MRI-derived morphometric features are effective for AD staging and predicting cognitive decline. Features from T1w-sMRI alone provide strong and clinically meaningful predictions, while multimodal data provide added value primarily in specific diagnostic cases. The use of XAI improves transparency and supports clinical interpretation. Clinically, the findings of the study highlight T1w-sMRI as a practical, cost-effective biomarker for disease assessment.
