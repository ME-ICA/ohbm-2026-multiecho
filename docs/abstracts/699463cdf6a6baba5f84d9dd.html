Introduction

Anesthesia, administered in over 300 million major surgeries annually worldwide, remains a cornerstone of modern medicine despite incomplete understanding of its neural mechanisms. Previous studies indicate that loss of consciousness via anesthesia involves alterations of functional connectivity (FC) between brain regions and networks. Yet it is unclear how associated mechanisms map differentially across different anesthesia agents. Perl et al1 employing modelling for data augmentation and subsequent deep learning for dimensionality reduction were able to determine a mapping representing states of consciousness in a low-dimensional space indicating few central features segregating these states. 

We aim at defining robust patterns characteristic of the awake and the anesthetized states. We analyze a resting-state fMRI data set with 3 levels of sevoflurane anesthesia, including burst-suppression, and imaging of the awake states pre- and post-anesthesia with the aim of extending the analysis strategy to data acquired with different anesthetic agents. Sparse coding allows for specifying a parsimonious representation of the FC matrices thereby highlighting its most important constituents. By suppressing weak and noisy contributions to the FC matrix, sparse coding may yield connectivity patterns that align with known anatomical pathways or theorized consciousness networks (e.g., fronto‑parietal, thalamo‑cortical), improving sensitivity to condition‑specific disruptions.

We conduct within‑subject‑comparison to reveal whether anesthesia preferentially eliminates or enhances specific sparse-coded FC states.

Methods

Fifteen subjects underwent resting state functional imaging with a gradual sevoflurane anesthesia protocol. The scanning protocol consisted of five sessions. Session 1 measured the awake state. Session 2 measured deep anesthesia with a burst suppression pattern. Sessions 3 and 4 measured emergences with moderate and light anesthesia levels, respectively. Session 5 measured full recovery to an awake state. Every subject in this study underwent an identical scanning procedure across all levels of consciousness. Preprocessed four-dimensional volumes of BOLD signals were obtained. Functional whole brain data were parcellated based on the Schaefer 100 region of interest atlas. Time series were obtained for each subject and time point. Functional connectivity was measured using Pearson correlation matrices computed for all region-region pairs in each session and individual. The upper triangular part of these matrices, not including self connections, was extracted to create a vector of 4,950 region-region connectivity measurements with respect to each individual's connectivity configuration in a given processing session.  The dimensionality reduction procedure of choice used in this study was Dictionary Learning with Sparse Coding. Unsupervised dictionary learning decomposes a given matrix into two: a dictionary and a sparse representation of the matrix against this dictionary . The algorithm used in this study was set to discover a dictionary of ten atoms with L1 regularization. The algorithm ran for a maximum of 5,000 iterations in parallel with processors available. The learned dictionary described the commonality in connectivity motifs across different consciousness states, and the sparse representation described expression levels of these motifs in each experimental session and subject. The accuracy of this representation was measured using normalized Frobenius distance error. To facilitate visualization, low-dimensional sparse representations allowed the application of principal component analysis, projecting both the original connectivity features and sparse representation coefficients into two-dimensional spaces for qualitative evaluation of separability among sessions. To quantify similarities among sessions in the sparse representation space, average coefficient vectors were calculated for all fifteen subjects across each level of consciousness, resulting in five characteristic patterns for wakefulness, deep anesthesia, and two intermediate levels. Pairwise similarities among these averaged representations were calculated with respect to five different measures: cosine similarity (angular relationships), Pearson and Spearman correlations (linear and monotonic relationships), inverse Euclidean distance, and generalized Jaccard Index (magnitude-normalized similarities). A parameter sweep analysis with respect to dictionary size and consequently with respect to the discriminability of states of consciousness was performed. To analyze these effects, silhouette scores were calculated in two-dimensional PCA plots of sparse representations with varying numbers of components from five to fifty, where preanesthesia and post recovery phases were considered under a common awake state such that a total of four states of consciousness were obtained. A higher silhouette score corresponds to a clearer separation among clusters in the learned feature space, which shows that dictionary size affects a model’s ability to model consciousness variance rather than idiosyncratic variance.

Results

The dictionary learning algorithm successfully decomposed functional connectivity patterns into ten sparse components with a small reconstruction error, suggesting that a small set of connectivity templates can be used to describe different levels of consciousness adequately. Inspection of the coefficient matrix highlighted a very interesting structure: samples with a given level of anesthesia were very similar in their sparse activation profiles, with a very strong correspondence between pre-anesthesia and post-recovery wakefulness. The two states of wakefulness, which were very far in time because of heavy exposure to anesthetics, had nearly identical coefficient profiles for all dictionary atoms. The learned dictionary preserved the hierarchical connectivity patterns over various scales. Some atoms were related to network-level interactions among multiple canonical systems in a distributed fashion, while others were connected to more localized connectivity among regions that were anatomically close. The sparse representation of the coefficient matrix implied a preference of different states of consciousness for different sets of these patterns, which indicated a systematic modulation of these connectivity patterns by anesthesia.

PCA performed on connectivity vectors indicated a moderate level of clustering, with a partly overlapping two-dimensional cloud for each state of consciousness. However, PCA performed on coefficient vectors indicated a marked improvement in separation among different groups. This is an indication that dictionary learning is better at decoupling the relevant variance in consciousness than linear dimensionality reduction when performed on connectivity matrices. Quantitative similarity analysis among the mean session representations demonstrated systematic correspondence to the progression of anesthetic depth. For all five similarity measures: cosine, Pearson product moment, Spearman rank order, Euclidean distance, and Jaccard index, consistent patterns emerged with peak similarity between consecutive states of consciousness and a gradual decline with progressive separation in the anesthesia levels. Deep anesthesia had the least similarity with both awake states and a terminal point in the dimension of consciousness. Moderately and lightly anesthetized sessions were placed in an intermediate level with similarity profiles lying in between deep anesthesia and complete arousal.

Most importantly, pre anesthesia wakefulness and post recovery awareness under these mathematical models showed a very high degree of similarity among them, which in many cases exceeded 0.75 in correlation and tended towards being identical. This shows that the basic connectivity topology which underlies awareness is preserved and can be recovered under very deep transients imposed by anesthetics. The analogous behavior of these similarities under different mathematical models confirms our belief in them not being artifacts. The silhouette analysis performed on varying dictionary sizes shows an opposing relationship concerning the distinctiveness of the state of consciousness based on the number of components. The maximum separation was attained using a dictionary of 5-10 atoms with a silhouette score of approximately 0.5. As the number of components increased beyond 15, the accuracy of cluster formation continued to worsen, becoming negative for components above 35. Lower dimensional dictionaries tended to represent large-scale network patterns with systematic variations based on levels of consciousness, successfully identifying global states about collective subjects for comparable depths of anesthesia. Higher-dimensional maps emphasized increasingly more specific connectivity information based on individual subjects and dynamic differences.

Conclusion

This work shows that sparse coding can be used to describe different levels of consciousness based on patterns of how the brain is connected. Successful separation of whole-brain correlation matrices into a simple set of understandable parts shows that specific connectivity patterns, rather than network-wide changes, are involved in changes in consciousness caused by anesthesia. Perhaps most impressively, these pre and post recovery wakefulness correlations show such a high degree of overlap with regards to their sparse descriptions, indicating that these structures are very stable and strong in support of conscious processing. The linear correlation of intersession similarity with anesthetic depth progression confirms that sevoflurane takes a continuous trajectory through a neurobiological space rather than making a transition from a definite state to another distinct state. Several different similarity measures all concurred in ranking subjects in exactly the same manner as objective measures of awareness, thus corroborating that representations based on sparse connectivity were indeed informative indicators of awareness. Low-dimensional reconstructions lack anesthetic depth information but have a good grasp of network-level information common to subjects in a similar state of consciousness, thus identifying common signatures linked with anesthetic depth but without individual information. Higher-dimensional reconstructions have a nearly perfect recreation of the data based on intricate connectivity information not common in subjects, thus hiding relevant information about consciousness in subject-specific variance. This dimensionality trade off has important theoretical implications. First, it implies that consciousness is related to synchronized activity over a set of brain-wide networks on a scale of coarse temporal resolution. The dictionary learning technique used in this work aims at learning reusable connectivity motifs, which can be used in a flexible manner among observations, thus providing information on both shared aspects and individual variability in a single model. A comparison of dictionary atoms obtained from different methods of consciousness manipulation may distinguish which connectivity patterns are characteristic of a particular anesthetic and which are common to all. Linking such dynamics of sparse coefficients with electrophysiology and performance in a behavioral task will make these neural computations more understandable mechanistically. Additionally, in light of the systematic relationship observed between sparse representation and levels of consciousness, these findings make dictionary learning a promising candidate for understanding neural architectures of awareness and providing a way to quantify levels of consciousness based on network connectivity.
