Introduction

The neural basis of visual hallucinations (VH) remains elusive (1-3). They may occur in ophthalmological and neurological disorders(1), including dementia and Parkinson’s disease (PD, with or without dementia). It remains a mystery whether a unifying mechanism underlies visual hallucinations or if disease-dependent causes dominate. The fact that visual deprivation or visual loss can give rise to hallucinosis is well recognized but mechanisms linking deafferentation to hallucinations is unknown. Why hallucinations emerge in retinal and cortical disorders of visual awareness and their relation with plasticity remain intriguing questions.

For example, Charles Bonnet syndrome (CBS) is characterized by VH in ocular disorders, in particular in the context of age-related macular degeneration. It has been suggested that chronic low level deafferentation leads to increased cortical excitability in higher level regions, but the role of early visual and imagery related regions in the parietal cortex remain unknown. Based on bottom-up models of deafferentation, we hypothesized that parietal imagery networks (4) and early visual areas would be relevant in the amplification of neural responses to periodic visual patterns, usually perceived as hallucinations in CBS.

Methods

Task-related and retinotopy functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) data were collected from 77 adult participants.  Study groups: Patients with age-related macular degeneration (AMD) with hallucinations (n=13). Control groups: Patients with AMD without visual hallucinations (n = 32) and matched healthy participants (n=32). Before the start of the study, participants underwent neuropsychiatric screening.

Neuroimaging protocol: fMRI Imaging was performed using a 3T MRI scanner (MAGNETOM Prisma, Siemens Medical Solutions) with a 64-channel head-coil and EPI sequences (2x2x2 mm, TR 2000 ms, TE 30 ms, FA 90°). Visual stimulus consisted of periodic geometric figures which often elicit or mimic hallucinatory patterns, known to be causal triggers of hallucinatory patterns. Each session began with a 7-minute resting-state functional scan, followed by a visual stimulation paradigm consisting of two 10-minute runs. This was followed by a 10-minute T1-weighted structural scan and a 10-minute retinotopy mapping block. A population receptive field (pRF) model was estimated from BOLD responses to the simultaneous bars moving stimuli by using a model-driven approach.  In the visual block design paradigm, participants were exposed to periodic circular empty-field flicker (Ganzflicker) stimuli intended to induce elementary visual imagery similar to those reported in hallucinatory states. We analyzed data with group based random effects General Linear Models, with FDR correction at 0.05 level.

The study was conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki after approval by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine of the University of Coimbra. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Results

Patients with visual loss showed the expected reduction in activation in primary visual cortex. Notably, larger stimulus-related activation was observed in VH patients vs AMD without VH in early areas involved in visual processing, in particular peripheral regions of the primary visual cortex (V1) and parietal regions involved in visual imagery. 

Regarding retinotopy and population receptive field organization both clinical groups showed markedly and significantly increased population receptive fields as compared to controls. Remarkably, pRFs were significantly larger in the visual periphery in the VH group, as compared to the AMD group without VH, in similar V1 peripheral regions where the VH group showed higher stimulus related activation.

Finally, we were able to longitudinally follow a patient with AMD who subsequently developed visual hallucinations and found increased periodic pattern related V1 and parietal activation, providing a within subject demonstration of the main group effects. 

Conclusion

These findings provide evidence for a joint role of parietal imagery networks, involved in visual imagery, and primary visual areas (V1) in the amplification of neural responses to periodic visual patterns, in patients with retinal degeneration and visual hallucinations. They also suggest an intriguing relation with retinotopic reorganization. In sum, our results suggest a role for V1-parietal networks in the amplification of visual content related to hallucinatory experiences.
