Introduction

Neurological and psychiatric disorders show strong sex differences across the lifespan, yet the neural basis of these differences remains underexplored. Sex influences the brain dynamically, as hormonal changes across development, adulthood, and aging shape both structural and functional connectivity. Although prior studies have identified sex differences at specific ages, no work has comprehensively shown how sex differences in functional and structural connectomes evolve continuously from childhood to old age.

Brain connectomes are high-dimensional and highly sensitive to preprocessing choices, yielding unstable results. To address these issues, we modified and retrained the recently developed AI model called the Krakencoder1—a multimodal connectome-fusion tool that learns a low-dimensional representation capturing shared information across many structural and functional connectivity “flavors”- to better encode sex-specific information. Using this model alongside a large, harmonized dataset of 1,286 individuals aged 8–100 years from the Human Connectome Project enables more robust modeling of sex-related brain–behavior patterns. We demonstrate the improved performance of our so-called “sex-fine-tuned” Krakencoder by comparing it to a baseline ensemble model trained on raw connectomes.

Our study provides the first lifespan-wide characterization of sex differences in brain networks. By building sex-classification models and interpreting their network-level importance across age, we reveal how functional and structural connectivity diverge between males and females throughout development and aging. These findings offer new insights into the timing and organization of sex-biased brain differences with implications for understanding, predicting, and treating neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative disorders.

Methods

We analyzed data from 1,286 healthy individuals (643 female, ages 8–100) drawn from HCP-Development, HCP-Aging, and three HCP disease-control cohorts (young adults). All MRI data, including resting-state fMRI and diffusion MRI, were acquired using harmonized HCP-Lifespan protocols and processed using the same connectome extraction pipelines. For each participant, 15 connectivity flavors were generated across three atlases (fs86, shen268, coco439): 9 functional (FCcorr, FCgsr, FCpcorr for each atlas) and 6 structural (deterministic sdstream and probabilistic ifod2act for each atlas).

To study how sex-related patterns develop with age, we divided individuals into ten age bins with approximately equal numbers of samples. Within each bin, three sex-classification models were constructed: (1) logistic ridge regression applied to the latent space of the original Krakencoder,  (2) logistic ridge regression applied to a sex fine-tuned Krakencoder, and (3) an ensemble logistic regression model trained directly on raw connectomes. Each approach was applied in three data settings, using: all 15 connectome flavors (Fusion), 9 FCs only (Fusion FC), or 6 SCs only (Fusion SC).

The Krakencoder is a multimodal connectome-fusion autoencoder that maps each connectivity flavor into a shared 128-dimensional latent space; latent vectors across flavors and modalities can be averaged to create fusion representations (fusion FC, fusion SC, fusion). We fine-tuned the Krakencoder using 448 lifespan subjects (fine-tuning set), optimizing reconstruction and subject identifiability, while adding a sex classification arm from each flavor’s latent representation. For each age bin, we randomly selected 30 subjects (15 female, 15 male) from the rest of 838 subjects’ data not used in fine-tuning the Krakencoder (so-called sex classification dataset), so that each bin had an equal number of test samples. For the training set, we combined the rest of the unused sex classification dataset samples within that bin with subjects from the 448 fine-tuning set of the same age range, and then downsampled to 30 training subjects (15 female, 15 male) in each bin. This sub-sampling was repeated 100 times.

To identify which brain networks are informative of sex, we conducted a network inclusion sensitivity analysis using the sex-fine-tuned Krakencoder. We focused on the 7 functional Yeo networks2, including visual (VIS), somatomotor (SMN), dorsal attention (DAN), ventral attention (VAN), limbic (LIM), control (CON), default mode (DMN), along with Subcortex (SUB) and Cerebellum (CBL). For a given target network or network pair, all other edges were replaced with that age bin’s training population mean (so-called masked data) and sex classification models retrains with this masked data. Sex classification accuracy on the model with the masked data was compared to accuracy of the model trained on the original (un-masked) data. Smaller drops in accuracy indicate that the retained connections contain more sex-relevant information.

For the ensemble models using raw connectomes, logistic ridge regressions were trained separately for each connectivity flavor, and predictions were combined by majority vote. For interpretability, ridge coefficients were transformed using the Haufe transformation3, which yields feature weights that more directly reflect underlying sex-related signal in the presence of strong collinearity. Coefficients were standardized to enable comparison across models with different regularization strengths.

Results

Figure 1a illustrates sex classification accuracy across the lifespan. Among all three models, the Sex Fine-tuned Krakencoder achieves the highest performance (acc≈0.80–0.95), outperforming both the original Krakencoder and the baseline ensemble model trained on the raw data (acc≈0.60-0.75). The sex fine-tuned Krakencoder significantly outperformed the raw connectome ensemble model across all age bins, demonstrating the advantage of fine-tuned latent-space representations.

In Fusion SC and Fusion FC, accuracy is lowest in childhood (ages 8–11), indicating the smallest sex differentiation in connectomes. Sex differences then become more distinct during adolescence, peaking in young–mid adulthood (especially ages 22–36 for the fine-tuned model). FC-only models (FusionFC) had decreased sex classification with aging while SC-only models (FusionSC) showed increasing accuracy in middle to late adulthood (36–80). This suggests that connectomes show a continued divergence of SC and convergence of FC, respectively, throughout mid to late adulthood.

Sensitivity analyses (Figure 1b) reveal distinct sex patterns of FCs (top panel) and SCs (middle panel) across the lifespan. In particular, DMN FC had the smallest accuracy drops across all ages, indicating strong and persistent sex associations. Other higher-order networks (VAN, CON) are also informative of sex, with importance peaking in midlife. As people age, cerebellar FC becomes increasingly divergent between males and females. In SC, lower-order networks (SUB, CBL) along with the DMN were most important for classifying sex, while CON shows decreasing sex-association with age and SMN displayed an inverted-U pattern with highest importance at young and old ages. Fusion FC+SC largely reflected combined effects (Figure 1b, bottom panel), with DMN remaining the strongest contributor of sex-differences and CBL becoming increasingly important for classifying sex with older age. Network-pair importance analyses (Figure 1c) shows that FC within/between higher-order networks, particularly DMN–DMN, DMN–CON, DMN–LIM, DMN–VAN, are sex-informative across almost the entire lifespan. SC between regions of the cerebellum (CBL-CBL) and between subcortical regions (SUB-SUB) demonstrated highest importance for sex classification.

Figure 2 shows the connectome feature directionality derived from standardized Haufe-transformed coefficients from the raw connectome ensemble model. We again see that, generally speaking, FC in higher order networks (Figure 2a) and SC in lower order networks (Figure 2b) are more important for classifying sex. Females exhibit stronger FC in higher-order networks, especially within regions of the DMN across nearly all ages (Figure 2a Top). Males exhibit stronger FC within CON and across several higher-order network pairs (DMN–VAN, DMN–DAN, CON–VAN; Figure 2a, bottom). Connections with CBL (CBL-DAN, CBL-SMN) in FC become increasingly associated with female sex as people age, while males showed increasing cross-hemispheric FC from left to right cerebellum. Overall, SC within different regions of SUB, CBL and VIS networks were associated with male and female sex. Females specifically had stronger across-hemisphere  CBL and SMN SC while stronger SC within same hemisphere networks was associated with male sex. These findings align the Krakencoder feature importance results and underscore robust, network-specific, and age-dependent sex differences in FC and SC across the human lifespan.

Conclusion

Our study provides the first map of sex differences in brain multi-modal connectivity across the lifespan, from childhood to old age. We used our new Krakencoder tool to uncover more robust sex differences in brain networks than can be found using raw connectome data. Our findings highlight complementary aspects of structural and functional brain organization across the lifespan. We found that connectome differences are smallest during childhood, with FC differences being most distinct in midlife (22-45) and diminishing with continued aging, while SC differences peak in later adulthood (55–65) and continue to diverge into older age. In FC, sex-specific effects are dynamic and primarily involve higher-order networks, with females having stronger within-DMN FC, and males exhibiting stronger FC in CON and between left and right cerebellum. Unlike FC, sex differences in SC are more consistent across the lifespan and are concentrated in lower-order subcortical, cerebellar and sensorimotor networks. Males appeared to have stronger within-network and within-hemisphere SC while females had stronger SCs across networks and hemispheres. Together, these complementary profiles highlight how FC may reflect more dynamic sex differentiation in response to biological and environmental factors, while network-level SC sex differences are more consistent across the lifespan, but do continue to diverge between the sexes as individuals age. This complementary division underscores how sex-specific trajectories in brain organization manifest differently in functional versus structural domains across ages. Knowing how sex can differently impact connectomes across the lifespan is critical if we are to better understand the mapping between brain and behavior.
