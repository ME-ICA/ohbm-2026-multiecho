Introduction

Understanding how individual differences in cognition relate to the microstructure of the human cortex remains a central challenge in neuroscience. While prior work has focused largely on cortical thickness, surface area, or white-matter connectivity, grey-matter microstructure has received comparatively less attention despite its direct link to cytoarchitecture and cellular organization. Advances in diffusion MRI now allow the characterization of microstructural properties within the cortex, offering new opportunities to probe structure–function relationships at a biologically meaningful scale. Among these metrics, the Return To Origin Probability (RTOP) provides a sensitive measure of microscopic diffusion restrictions, reflecting features such as cellular density and complexity. However, its capacity to predict diverse cognitive abilities across healthy individuals remains largely unknown. Here, using the Human Connectome Project dataset and a comprehensive set of cortical parcellations, we systematically evaluate the predictive value of RTOP-derived microstructure for a wide range of cognitive domains. We further examine how spatial resolution and sample size shape predictive accuracy, providing new insights into the limits and potential of grey-matter microstructure for behavioral prediction.

Methods

We evaluated how cortical grey-matter microstructure predicts cognitive performance using Return To Origin Probability (RTOP), a diffusion-based descriptor sensitive to cellularity and microstructural complexity. RTOP maps were processed in a surface-based framework using the HCP 32k fsLR space. All subjects from the Human Connectome Project with complete diffusion surfaces and cognitive scores were retained (N=884). Data were split into a training set (N=796) and a held-out test set (N=88), stratified by gender.

RTOP surfaces were resampled to fsLR via barycentric interpolation and parcellated using Schaefer functional atlases ranging from 100 to 1000 parcels. For each parcellation, we extracted parcel-wise mean RTOP values, resulting in feature sets matched across hemispheres. Cognitive outcomes consisted of 58 behavioral measures. Age and gender were treated as confounds and removed using a multi-output linear residualization model applied within the training data.

Prediction models were implemented using Kernel Ridge Regression (linear kernel), embedded in a preprocessing pipeline that applied parcel-wise mean-centring followed by standardization. Hyperparameters were selected via an inner 5-fold cross-validation grid search. Performance was quantified as Pearson's correlation (ρ) between predicted and observed cognitive scores in the held-out test set.

Two analyses were performed. (1) Parcellation Optimisation: We compared predictive accuracy across all Schaefer resolutions to assess the effect of spatial granularity on structure–cognition mapping. (2) Learning-Curve Analysis: Using the 800-parcel resolution, we evaluated model vs. data-limitation by repeatedly subsampling training fractions (0.15–1.00) across 1000 bootstrap iterations within 10-fold cross-validation. This procedure quantified how prediction accuracy scales with sample size and whether performance saturates due to limited data or insufficient model expressiveness.

All analyses used a fully nested cross-validation design to prevent information leakage and ensure unbiased evaluation.

Results

Figure 1 presents the predictive performance across all cognitive variables for which at least one model achieved a correlation of ρ ≥ 0.15. Prediction accuracy did not systematically vary with the spatial granularity of the Schaefer atlas: no resolution (100–1000 parcels) consistently outperformed the others. Instead, the results show that certain cognitive abilities are intrinsically more predictable from RTOP-derived grey-matter microstructure, with the strongest effects observed for Delay Discounting and Vocabulary, both reaching correlations of approximately ρ ≈ 0.27 in the held-out test set.

Figures 2 shows learning curves computed for three data-driven task clusters obtained via PCA with Varimax rotation.1 Across clusters (figures 2.a, 2.b and 2.c), prediction accuracy improved monotonically, with the exception of Instrument and Emotional support, as additional subjects were included in the training set. The Cognition cluster exhibited the highest overall performance, peaking at ρ = 0.287 for Vocabulary, and consistently outperforming the Psychosocial and Emotion clusters. Within the Emotion cluster, the best performance reached ρ = 0.217 for Emotion Recognition Total, reflecting correct answers on the Penn Emotion Recognition Test. Interestingly, prediction of individual emotion subcategories (Anger, Fear, Neutral, Sadness) was poor (ρ< 0.1)

The learning curves further revealed a characteristic saturation pattern: gains in predictive accuracy slowed markedly beyond ~600 training subjects, indicating that performance is no longer constrained by dataset size but by model expressiveness. These results suggest that while RTOP captures microstructural features relevant for several cognitive functions, more flexible, non-linear models may be required to unlock additional predictive signal.

Conclusion

Our study aimed to quantify the extent to which cognitive abilities can be predicted from cortical grey-matter microstructure as estimated by RTOP. While prior research has shown that microstructural diffusion measures carry behavioral information, these investigations typically focused on restricted brain regions or white-matter features. For example, Menon et al. reported correlations of ρ ≈ 0.19 when predicting selected behavioural scores from insular RTOP,2 and Ooi et al. found that diffusion-based white-matter metrics can achieve ρ < 0.30 for cognition-relevant components.1 However, the predictive utility of cortical RTOP for a broad set of cognitive traits at the level of individual measures has remained largely unexplored.

Our first objective was to evaluate the predictive value of RTOP-derived cortical microstructure across a comprehensive set of cognitive outcomes. The results in Figure 1 show that certain tasks, particularly Delay Discounting and Vocabulary, can be predicted with correlations up to ρ ≈ 0.27, situating RTOP as a relevant microstructural measure of structure–behavior associations regarding previous studies. These findings demonstrate that RTOP captures biologically meaningful microstructural features relevant to higher-order cognitive functions.

A second objective was to determine whether predictive performance is constrained by dataset size or model capacity. Learning-curve analyses (Figures 2) revealed monotonic improvements in accuracy as training set size increased, most notably within the Cognition cluster. However, performance gains plateaued beyond approximately 600 subjects, suggesting that the current linear Kernel Ridge Regression models are primarily model-limited rather than data-limited. This pattern indicates that more expressive or non-linear models may better exploit the rich microstructural information encoded by RTOP, potentially enabling stronger predictions of individual differences in cognitive abilities.

Taken together, these results highlight the promise of cortical RTOP as a biologically grounded marker for cognitive variability, while also pointing toward the need for advanced modeling strategies to fully realize its predictive potential.
