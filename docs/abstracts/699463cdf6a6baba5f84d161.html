Introduction

The brain’s intrinsic resting-state networks (RSNs) organization is suggested foundational to supporting mental processes. However, emerging evidence indicates that the intrinsic functional connectivity (FC) underlying behavior frequently transcends the boundaries of these networks, challenging the assumption that mental processes rely primarily on specialized, segregated systems. While previous studies have highlighted the importance of network integration (i.e., enhanced cross-network communication) for cognitive performance during tasks1, findings at rest are more nuanced2 and remain limited to few cognitive processes. Consequently, it remains uncertain how the modular organization of RSNs relates to the offline maintenance of a comprehensive range of cognitive, but also socio-emotional, and personality traits. If meaningful behavioral variation is better captured by connectivity patterns that extend beyond RSN boundaries, this would challenge the functional relevance of a modular resting-state architecture. This study therefore tests whether these diverse domains align more strongly with intrinsic FC structured by RSNs or with a more distributed, cross-network organization.

Methods

We analyzed 779 young adults from the HCP dataset (ages 22–37), using 58 behavioral measures across cognitive, socio-emotional, and personality domains. Resting-state fMRI data were preprocessed and parcellated into 448 brain regions matched to 7 cortical3 and 1 non-cortical RSNs. FC was estimated and used to predict behavioral scores via kernel ridge regression with nested split-half cross-validation repeated with resampling (n=200 total estimates)4, controlling for confounds (gender, age, total intracranial volume). Significance was assessed by comparing the distribution of prediction errors (standardized RMSE) to a permuted null distribution (n=200) (paired-t-tests, false discovery rate correction)4. For each significantly predicted behavioral measure, a Haufe transform5 was computed. This transform provided insight into the contribution of specific inter-regional FC that were most effective in the prediction. An exploratory factor analysis (EFA) on the Haufe transforms identified latent connectivity-behavior (CB) patterns. Network integration (average between-RSN CB) and segregation (average within-RSN CB) metrics were computed to assess relationships between brain organization and behavior (Fig 1). Significance of network metric changes was assessed post-hoc based on their reliability across prediction repetitions (95% confidence interval). Network specialization was operationalized as the joint presence of a reliable increase in segregation and a reliable decrease in integration.

Results

Of the 58 behavioral variables, 52 were significantly predicted by FC and analyzed further (Fig 2A). EFA identified three main factors (Fig 2B): Cognition (18 measures), Positive Affect (8 measures), and Negative Affect (10 measures) (Fig 2C). Global network specialization (increased segregation and decreased integration) was linked to better scores for the Cognition factor, but not for Positive affect or Negative affect (Fig. 2D). Network specialization was also associated with Cognition for two RSNs (Cognitive flexibility network (CFN), self-awareness network (SAN)) (Fig 2E). Better scores in the Cognition factor were also associated with a reliable decrease in integration for two RSNs (Performance monitoring network (PMN), social and communication network (SCN)), with no associated changes in segregation (Fig 2E). Positive affect was associated with increased segregation for the CFN and decreased integration for the somatomotor network (SMN), while Negative affect was associated with decreased segregation for the CFN and no reliable changes in integration (Fig 2E).

Conclusion

We demonstrate that FC associated with behavior organizes into a latent structure that distinctly separates behavioral measures related to cognition, positive affect, and negative affect. Notably, personality traits do not emerge as a distinct latent FC domain. Overall, our findings indicate a mechanistic difference between the resting-state maintenance of cognition and emotion: cognition relies on network specialization (increased segregation and decreased integration), while affective dimensions do not, suggesting that they are supported by a more flexible, distributed whole-brain architecture beyond the brain’s intrinsic network organization. Furthermore, we highlight a key difference between passive and active cognitive organization: whereas the recruitment of cognitive processes during tasks involves integration between brain systems1, the offline support of these processes is associated with the maintenance of a specialized, segregated network architecture.
