Introduction

The HEALthy Brain and Child Development (HBCD) Study, together with related large-scale initiatives (e.g., The Developing Human Connectome) have enabled unprecedented opportunities to map the emergence and maturation of large-scale functional brain networks1. Yet, mapping the development of overlapping and interconnected brain networks requires new frameworks for comparing functional brain networks across ages. To date, most fMRI pipelines have relied on single subject pre-processing, followed by group-level analyses of derived features (e.g., pair-wise fMRI time series data derived across voxel pairs). Yet, the inherently low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of neonatal resting-state (rs)-fMRI coupled with weak long-range anterior-posterior functional connectivity4 poses challenges for deriving the full neonatal functional connectome.

BrainSync is an orthogonal transform that allows direct comparison of resting fMRI (rfMRI) time-series across subjects2. It overcomes the inherent challenge associated with any rs-fMRI data, namely, that it represents spontaneous brain activity, and thus is not time-locked across participants. The BrainSync method allows the time series data to be rotated such that spontaneous brain activity can be synchronized across different scans from the same subject and also between scans from different subjects. This synchronization results in an approximate matching of the time-series at homologous locations across subjects. We applied BrainSync to the neonatal data from the HBCD R1.0 to derive four preliminary BrainSync rs-fMRI atlases across neonatal weeks.  We then compared brain connectivity matrices across weeks using the BrainSync atlas  compared to the canonical analysis approach (i.e., connectivity matricies first derived on individual subject data and then averaged across the age-groups).  

Methods

Data used in the preparation of this study were obtained from the HEALthy Brain and Child Development (HBCD) Study, release 1.0.1 Minimally preprocessed resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) data were accessed through the NIH Brain Development Cohorts Data Sharing Platform6 and registered onto the grayordinate coordinate system by registration to infant templates.

Participants were grouped into four postnatal age age groups: 0–1 weeks (n = 66; 27M/39F), 2 weeks (n = 69; 33M/36F), 3 weeks (n = 85; 45M/40F), and 4–5 weeks (n = 67; 28M/39F). For each group, an fMRI atlas was created using the GroupBrainSync method and is representative of the collective fMRI dataset of each group.2 Individual fMRI data were then temporally synchronized to the corresponding fMRI atlas using BrainSync,3 a method that applies an orthogonal transformation to spatially aligned time-series data that synchronizes fMRI across subjects. The BrainSync transform maximizes temporal correspondence across subjects without altering signal variance. This procedure enhances sensitivity to shared network structure while accommodating inter-individual variability in neonatal functional organization. In addition, it enables direct comparison of functional time courses across individuals within each age group.

We conducted a series of analyses to assess age-related effects on neonatal functional connectivity using two complementary approaches: (1) a conventional group analysis in which connectivity was computed at the individual-subject level and then averaged within age groups, and (2) a direct analysis performed on age-specific fMRI atlases. Mean time series were extracted from regions of interest (ROIs) defined by the Myers–Labonté parcellation,4 and pairwise functional connectivity was estimated using Pearson correlation between all ROI pairs. Connectivity matrices were restricted to cortical regions with non-zero coverage and analyzed separately for atlas-derived and group-averaged connectivity estimates. To facilitate network-level comparisons, matrices were reordered according to large-scale functional networks simplified from the original 23-network scheme into 15 networks by aggregating smaller systems (Fig. 1a).

To further compare network identification between approaches, we performed seed-based analyses targeting the default mode network (DMN). A composite seed was defined using three ROIs in the inferior precuneus and one in the posterior cingulate cortex. Whole-brain connectivity maps were generated by correlating the seed time series with all cortical vertices. This procedure was applied directly to each fMRI atlas and, separately, to individual subjects with results subsequently averaged within age groups (Fig. 1b). rs-fMRI FC matrices were further analyzed across the four age groups by using the Brain Connectivity Toolbox (https://sites.google.com/site/bctnet/). For each group, correlation matrices were thresholded to a fixed proportional density (10%) based on absolute connection strength while preserving edge sign. Signed modularity was estimated using the Louvain community detection, employing the symmetric treatment of positive and negative weights. A modularity index was computed for each group and matrix type at a fixed resolution parameter (γ = 1.0). (Figure 2b) Community assignments were retained for subsequent node-level analyses. (Figure 2a)

Within-module connectivity strength was quantified at the node level as the sum of signed connections between each node and other nodes assigned to the same community. (Figure 2c) Within-module strength was averaged across nodes belonging to predefined functional networks, defined by the Myers-Labonte Parcellation, and summarized across age groups. Group-level trajectories were visualized using dual-axis plots to compare atlas-based and group-mean estimates, with uncertainty represented as the standard error across nodes. (Figure 2d)

Results

As shown in Figure 1A., we observed a higher-level of intra-network connectivity and more pronounced negative inter-network connectivity in the BrainSync atlases as compared to the matrices derived from single-subject derived group averages. Color scales in fMRI atlases range from correlation coefficients of -0.6 to 0.6 while the group average ranges from -0.2 to 0.2. While within-network integrity and segregation is stronger with fMRI atlases, the overall network structure is preserved.

Atlas-based analyses revealed stronger and more spatially coherent representations of the default mode network (DMN) relative to single-subject derived group-averaged maps, with age-dependent increases in network specificity. As shown in Figure 1B, using a seed in the medial parietal region, we observed positive connectivity to the angular gyrus, the temporal lobe, medial frontal and dorso-lateral frontal regions. These connections strengthened with age, and long-range interactions were more readily detected in atlas-based maps. Overall, our atlases will inherently have higher signal-to-noise ratio SNR facilitating clearer detection of network structure compared to group-level averaging.

Cross-sectional analyses of the atlases across ages revealed systematic developmental changes in modular organization and within-community connectivity between 0-5 weeks of postnatal age (Figure 2). However, community assignment based on the functional connectivity matrices was similar assignments between fMRI atlas-based and single-subject derived group analysis and consistent across ages.

fMRI atlas-based connectivity consistently demonstrates higher modularity and stronger within-community strength compared to single-subject derived group-averaged estimates, suggesting improved sensitivity to network segregation. The modularity index (Figure 2B.) increases with age for both approaches, reflecting maturation of functional network architecture that parallels across the two methods. However, the atlas-based measurements show greater magnitude, indicating clearer delineation of network boundaries.

Region-wise estimates of within-community strength again was generally higher than single-subject derived group estimates. However, when scaled differentially for visual comparison (Figure 2C), differential patterns emerged between analyses among frontal and sensory-motor cortices (Figure 2C.). We observed that atlas-based estimate more strongly captured within-community strength in the dorsolateral prefrontal, the orbitofrontal and medial-prefrontal cortex. In contrast, these regions grew progressively towards the upper range of measures by 5 weeks of age. While this is aligned with previous observations, we also question whether higher inherent BOLD is also playing a role. Since fMRI-atlas have higher SNR, we may be more sensitive to detecting within-community strength in the frontal lobe in comparison to group-level analysis. Therefore, atlas-based methods may better detect emerging long-range connectivity and network specialization.

Network-specific trajectories (Figure 2d) reveal that anterior and posterior DMN networks, alongside the auditory network, exhibit robust age-related increases in within-community strength, consistent with prior evidence of DMN maturation during early development.5 These patterns, although at different scales, are similar across the two methods. On the other hand, motor and visual networks show contrasting patterns across the two methods. However, we do observe that the overall variance is lower using fMRI atlas-based methods than the group-average study.

Conclusion

We provide a novel set of neonatal functional brain atlases based on synchronized rs-fMRI data obtained from infant fMRI data in the first HBCD data release. Unlike prior atlases describing functional brain parcels4,  these atlases provide the first maps of voxelwise functional connectivity by week of neonatal development.

One of the key advantages of using atlas-based connectivity estimates lies in their ability to enhance sensitivity to developmental features. By leveraging higher SNR, these atlases enable detection of subtle patterns of network maturation and long-range connectivity that may be obscured in traditional group-averaging approaches. This improved sensitivity is particularly valuable in the neonatal period, where low SNR often limits interpretability. As shown, these atlases demonstrate that strong intra- and inter-network connectivity is present in the neonatal period, suggesting that the lower connectivity observed in typical group-level analyses may reflect the inherent low SNR signal of rs-fMRI data in this age range. By leveraging BrainSync alignment, these atlases achieve substantially higher SNR, enabling clearer detection of long-range functional circuits and more spatially coherent network patterns.

In addition to improved sensitivity, atlas-based methods offer substantial computational efficiency. Analyses can be performed on a single, high-quality atlas rather than hundreds or thousands of individual datasets, dramatically reducing time, energy, and computational resources. This efficiency makes atlas-based approaches an attractive option for exploratory analyses and hypothesis generation, allowing researchers to identify promising patterns before committing to large-scale individual-level studies.

However, this approach is not without limitations. Because atlas-based connectivity reflects group-level means, it does not capture individual variability, which is critical for understanding heterogeneity in development and for clinical applications. Despite this limitation, atlas-based methods provide a powerful starting point for hypothesis-driven research, offering a practical and resource-efficient strategy for characterizing normative developmental trajectories and informing the design of future studies.

Overall, these findings underscore the advantages of atlas-based approaches for characterizing developmental changes in functional network organization. By leveraging higher SNR and spatial coherence, atlas-based methods provide more accurate estimates of network segregation and connectivity strength, which is critical for understanding normative brain development and identifying early markers of neurodevelopmental disorders.
