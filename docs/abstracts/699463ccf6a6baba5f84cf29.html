Introduction

In stroke, the absence of premorbid MRI prevents assessment of pre-stroke brain morphology and brain reserve, which may be affected by premorbid vascular risk factors or by stroke itself. As a result, clinical evaluations of brain structure and function are as a rule conducted only after the initial stroke event, at a time when stroke-related damage and early compensatory changes may already be present.1 Consequently, post-stroke assessments risk conflating newly acquired deficits with pre-existing vulnerabilities, such as age-related decline, small vessel disease, or neurodegenerative changes.2–5 This complicates efforts to disentangle the true baseline capacity of the brain from the consequences of the stroke itself. A more accurate characterization of premorbid brain health is therefore essential for understanding heterogeneity in stroke outcomes and recovery potential. We hypothesized that anatomical MRI-estimated brain age in the non-stroke hemisphere may serve as a proxy to premorbid brain reserve. Importantly, given that older patients are more susceptible to accumulating neuropathological changes and age-related atrophy, we hypothesized that brain age, rather than chronological age, would provide a markedly better prediction of post-stroke motor outcome and recovery specifically in older individuals.

Methods

We used anatomical MRI to analyse brain age longitudinally in the non-stroke hemisphere in 66 patients with first-ever unilateral stroke. Brain age was calculated using a machine learning model that has been trained and validated in a large group of people.6 Brain age gap (BAG) was calculated as difference to chronological age. Patients were categorized as old or young using median split of age to allow comparison of brain age and sensorimotor outcome in old and young patients. We compared BAG, at 3 weeks and at 6 months post-stroke in the two groups, to evaluate the rate of neurodegeneration between the two groups. Sensorimotor outcome was defined as upper extremity Fugl-Meyer Assessment (FMA-UE) at 6 months and recovery as change relative to early (baseline) impairment. The reflex component of FMA-UE was excluded to assess only pure voluntary movement component. We assessed whether BAG independently predicted motor outcomes beyond established clinical and lesion-related factors in the whole group and the two subgroups.

Results

Brain age in 66 patients with moderate-to-severe initial motor impairment (FMA-UE<45; median age [IQR]: 57 [45–69] years; 25.8% female) showed strong correlations with chronological age at baseline (ρ=0.81) and at follow-up (ρ=0.81). BAG was similar in both groups with no significant longitudinal changes (P>0.1) indicating minimal neurodegeneration in the non-stroke hemisphere. Whole-group multiple regression analyses identified baseline FMA-UE, age, and corticospinal tract injury as significant predictors of outcome and recovery. In older patients (N=34, median age [IQR]: 61 [59–66] years), higher BAG together with baseline motor function predicted poorer motor outcome (β=-0.489, P=0.028) and less recovery (β=-0.011, P=0.021), explaining an additional 4% and 7% of variance, respectively. BAG was not significant in younger patients. Partial regression analysis in the older group showed a negative correlation between BAG and both motor outcome (r=-0.38, P=0.028) and recovery (r=-0.48, P=0.005), while controlling for chronological age, lesion volume, corticospinal tract injury and baseline motor scores. These correlations were not present in younger patients (P>0.84).

Conclusion

Our findings show that BAG of the non-stroke hemisphere predicted motor outcome and recovery in older patients. This proxy for premorbid brain reserve may inform on plasticity potential and be a relevant biomarker for prediction of recovery.
