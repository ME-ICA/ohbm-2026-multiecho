Introduction

The idea that left-handed individuals may possess greater creative potential than right-handed ones has intrigued researchers since the mid-20th century.^1 Some evidence suggests that left-handed people excel in domains such as music, arts, and architecture, although other studies report minimal or no differences.^2 Approximately 20% of left-handed individuals exhibit atypical language organization, either lateralized toward the right hemisphere or bilaterally distributed, which may influence cognitive processes,^3 some of which could be associated with creativity. This neural variability could underlie subtle differences in problem-solving, conceptual thinking, and the capacity to generate original ideas. Creativity itself can be conceptualized in multiple dimensions, including fluency, flexibility, originality, and elaboration, which are commonly assessed using tasks such as the Alternative Uses Task (AUT) developed by Guilford.^4 The AUT provides a systematic measure of divergent thinking, and recent advances using artificial intelligence, such as Ocsai, allow more precise evaluation of originality, opening new avenues for understanding individual differences in creative performance.^5 Together, these findings highlight the intricate interplay between handedness, brain lateralization, and creative abilities, suggesting that left-handedness may confer certain cognitive advantages in some domains while reflecting diverse patterns of neural organization and cognitive processing.

Methods

A total of 115 participants were recruited for the study (96 left-handers, 19 right-handers; 23.85 ± 5.50 years). All completed the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory (EHI), which was used to classify manual preference following standard scoring procedures. Language lateralization was determined using a Laterality Index (LI) obtained during an fMRI verb generation task, calculated with the LI Toolbox for SPM12. Based on EHI and LI values, participants were classified in four groups: right-handers (RH, n = 19), left-handers with typical lateralization (Typ, n = 64), left-handers with bilateral distribution (Atyp-B, n = 13), and left-handers with right lateralization (Atyp-R, n = 19). As expected, groups differed in LI and EHI, with no sex differences observed.

All MRI data were acquired on a 3T GE Signa Architect scanner. Structural T1-weighted volumes and functional EPI images were collected following a standardized protocol. Functional data preprocessing in SPM12 included realignment, motion correction, co-registration, segmentation, normalization to MNI space, and smoothing with a 4-mm FWHM kernel. No participant exceeded 2 mm of translational or rotational movement. The verb generation task contrasted a control condition (reading letter pairs aloud) with an activation condition (producing aloud the first semantically related verb), allowing for estimation of hemispheric dominance within an inferior frontal gyrus mask.

To assess creative performance, participants completed the Alternative Uses Task (AUT). They generated as many alternative uses as possible for three objects within a 3-minute period and selected their three most creative responses. Creativity was quantified using objective and subjective metrics. Objective originality scores were computed with the automated Ocsai system, yielding two indices: standard originality (mean originality across all responses) and top originality (mean originality of self-selected top responses). Subjective scoring included fluency, flexibility, and elaboration, evaluated by two trained judges. Inter-rater reliability was excellent across all dimensions (ICC: 0.91–1.00).

Statistical analyses were carried out in SPSS 28. To test group differences, a series of univariate ANOVAs was conducted with each creativity dimension as a dependent variable. A four-level between-subjects models was implemented. The model evaluated the effect of combined handedness x language lateralization (RH, Typ, Atyp-B, Atyp-R). Spearman correlations examined the relationship between continuous measures (EHI, LI) and creativity. This analytic approach enabled a detailed examination of how manual preference and cerebral language organization relate to divergent thinking across multiple creativity dimensions.

Results

When examining the four groups defined by the combination of manual handedness and language lateralization, significant main effects emerged for flexibility (F(3,111) = 4.18, p = .008) and for both indices of originality (standard originality: F(3,107) = 3.64, p = .015; top originality: F(3,107) 4.21, p = .007)). Post hoc comparisons indicated that RH produced higher flexibility scores than all three left-handed groups (Typ, Atyp-B, Atyp-R). In contrast, for standard originality, the Typ, Atyp-B and Atyp-R groups all showed significantly higher scores than RH, while for top originality, the Typ and Atyp-R groups again outperformed RH. Regarding elaboration, the main effect did not reach significance (F(3,111) = 2.36, p = .075), although marginal comparisons suggested a trend toward higher scores in the Typ and Atyp-R groups relative to the RH group. No significant differences were observed for fluency across groups. Figure 1 provides the graphical representation of these patterns.

The results revealed significant positive correlations between EHI scores and both Standard Originality (ρ =.234, p = .025) and Top Originality (ρ = .256, p = .014) within the left-handed group, indicating that stronger left manual lateralization was associated with greater originality in responses, particularly in the most creative uses generated for each object. No significant relationships were found for the remaining creative dimensions. Additionally, no significant correlations were observed in the right-handed group. We did not find significant correlations between lateralization of language and creative dimensions in neither left-handed nor right-handed participants. 

Conclusion

The overall pattern of findings indicates that the key source of creative variability in this study lies in manual handedness rather than in hemispheric language lateralization. Although the groups were defined by both dimensions, the creative profiles did not map onto differences in language lateralization; instead, they consistently distinguished left-handers from right-handers. 

Across creative dimensions, left-handers showed a tendency toward greater originality, whereas right-handers demonstrated advantages in flexibility. Importantly, these patterns were the same for all left-handers—whether their language was left-lateralized, bilateral, or right-lateralized—and we found no evidence that language dominance was related to creativity in any group. In contrast, within left-handers, the positive association between the degree of left manual preference and originality further supports the view that manual lateralization itself is a relevant contributor to creative thought. The absence of similar associations in right-handers underscores that these relationships are not simply driven by general lateralization strength, but may reflect unique characteristics of the left-handed population.

A key limitation, however, is the relatively small sample of right-handed participants, which may have constrained the detection of more subtle effects or interactions involving this group. Future studies should incorporate larger and more balanced samples to clarify the robustness and generalizability of these handedness-related creative profiles

Taken together, the findings suggest that left-handedness may predispose individuals to a different creative style—one marked by the generation of more novel or unconventional ideas—whereas right-handedness may favour cognitive processes that support flexible switching among categories or approaches. These distinctions are consistent with proposals linking left manual lateralization to more heterogeneous or distributed neural organization, potentially facilitating associative richness, while more typical patterns in right-handers may support structured cognitive control.
