Introduction

Human social behavior is defined by a fundamental tension between the evolutionary drive for self-interest and social necessity of fairness. Inequality aversion (IA) quantifies the behavioral preference for fairness over unequal outcomes, which is a cornerstone of human cooperation1. While evolutionary theories suggest humans biologically favor in-group members, interactions with close partners are governed by strong prior expectations of reciprocity. In-group inequality may represent a severe social prediction error, distinct from neural processing involving strangers. Despite behavioral evidence that social distance modulates fairness, the neurocomputational mechanisms that enable the brain to flexibly regulate fairness across social contexts remain poorly understood. Prior neurocomputational models highlight the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and striatum as central to social valuation. However, it remains unknown how these regions interact to gate selfish vs prosocial behaviors depending on social distance. Emerging evidence suggests the mPFC is organized along intrinsic spatial gradients, yet whether this topography supports the complex computation of IA remains un-investigated.

To address this gap, we combined functional MRI with a prosocial decision-making paradigm to delineate the neural architecture of inequality aversion. Behaviorally, we found that IA is context-dependent: in-group IA imposed high cognitive cost (slower reaction times) due to expectation violations, whereas out-group IA was relatively automatic. This divergence was supported by a rostral-caudal dissociation that in-group IA recruiting rostral regulatory networks (OFC, IFG), while out-group IA engaging caudal attentional systems (Precuneus, IPL). We further revealed a dorsal-to-ventral gradient in the corticostriatal pathway. Finally, we proposed a Corticostriatal Gating Mechanism, where mPFC-striatum coupling downregulates the impact of striatal activity on prosocial behavior. 

Methods

Participants and Experimental Design: We recruited forty-two healthy, right-handed young adults for this study (29 females; age range: 19–26 years; mean age = 21.7 years). Participants completed four functional MRI (fMRI) task runs and one resting-state run. The task runs consisted of a prosocial decision-making paradigm divided into two In-group runs (partnered with a close friend) and two Out-group runs (partnered with a stranger) (Fig. 1A). In each trial, participants chose between a fixed fair allocation (1/1) and a context-dependent unfair alternative. This design created three game types: Prosocial (1/0), Sharing (2/0), and Envy (1/2)2 (Fig. 1B). To isolate the neural signatures of Inequality Aversion (IA), analyses focused on the contrast between Envy (disadvantageous inequality) and Prosocial (control) games. IA behavior was operationalized as the context-specific shift in prosocial choice probability and reaction time (RT) between these conditions.

fMRI Acquisition and GLM Analysis: Whole-brain activation maps were estimated using a General Linear Model (GLM). Regressors modeled the encoding phase for each game type (Envy v.s. Prosocial) and social context (in-group v.s. out-group). IA-related neural activation was identified by contrasting brain activity between Envy and Prosocial trials (Envy > Prosocial).

mPFC Parcellation and ROI analysis: To investigate fine-grained anatomical organization, we employed a connectivity-based parcellation approach. First, a functional mPFC mask was defined as the overlapping area significantly activated across all game types and social groups. This mPFC mask served as the seed for a tractography-based parcellation scheme, which subdivided the mPFC into six clusters (mPFC1–mPFC6) arranged along a dorsal-to-ventral gradient (Fig. 2C). Anatomical Regions of Interest (ROIs) for the striatum were defined using the Brainnetome Atlas3, comprising six bilateral subregions spanning dorsal, ventral, and limbic territories.

Functional Connectivity and Gradient Analysis: We assessed both intrinsic and task-modulated functional connectivity to characterize the mPFC-striatal circuit. Resting-state functional connectivity (FC) of each mPFC subregion was calculated to investigate the intrinsic organization of mPFC. Task-based FC was examined using whole-brain generalized psychophysiological interaction (gPPI)4 models with each mPFC subregion as the seed. IA-related functional coupling was isolated by contrasting the PPI interaction terms for Envy > Prosocial games. Moreover, to uncover the functional gradient within mPFC, connectivity profiles were compared across mPFC subregions using paired t-tests (GRF-corrected, p < 0.05), performed separately for resting-state and task-based data. We specifically extracted mPFC-striatal coupling to quantify the modulation of mPFC-striatal pathway during inequality processing.

Multivariate and Mediation Analyses: To link neural architecture to behavior, we applied Partial Least Squares (PLS) analysis, incorporating mPFC and striatal activation, mPFC-striatal connectivity strength, and behavioral indices (choice and RT) related to IA. Finally, to test the hypothesis of Corticostriatal Gating Mechanism, we conducted a mediation analyses to determine whether mPFC-striatum connectivity statistically mediated the influence of dorsal striatal activity on prosocial behavior and inequality aversion.

Results

Behavioral dissociation of Inequality Aversion: Participants exhibited significantly higher prosocial choice rates toward in-group than out-group in both Prosocial and Envy games (p < 0.001). Crucially, Inequality Aversion (IA) manifested differently across social contexts. In-group IA was characterized by a higher cognitive cost, evidenced by significantly slower reaction times (RT) compared to the relatively automatic Out-group IA (p < 0.05; Fig. 1C). Across participants, this processing cost (IA-related RT) was positively correlated with activity in the right globus pallidus (GP) (r = 0.31, p = 0.05) and ventral mPFC (mPFC2; r = 0.32, p = 0.04), suggesting a specific neural footprint for the cognitive effort required to process inequality (Fig. 1D).

Rostral-caudal dissociation of neural substrates: Whole-brain GLM analyses contrasting Envy versus Prosocial games revealed distinct neural architectures of IA across social distances. In-group IA predominantly engaged rostral regulatory regions, including OFC, IFG, and MFG, consistent with the recruitment of cognitive control to resolve expectation violations (Fig. 2A). In contrast, Out-group IA primarily recruited caudal attentional systems, including precuneus, IPL, and SPL (GRF-corrected, voxel-wise p < 0.05; Fig. 2B). This macroscopic dissociation confirms that expanding the moral circle to strangers relies on a distinct neural computation from maintaining close partners.

Dorsal-to-ventral gradient in mPFC: We revealed six subdivisions of mPFC by using a tractography-based parcellation scheme. We then examined resting-state functional connectivity (FC) across mPFC subregions and revealed a robust dorsal-to-ventral gradient in mPFC (Fig. 2C, left): dorsal mPFC subregions preferentially connected to frontoparietal control networks and the anterior insula, whereas ventral mPFC subregions showed stronger connectivity with limbic and striatal valuation regions. This intrinsic connectivity gradient was consistently observed across both lateral and medial mPFC subdivisions.

Corticostriatal gating mechanism and behavioral regulation: Task-based gPPI analyses of mPFC subdivisions demonstrated that this dorsal-to-ventral gradient of mPFC is functionally recruited during IA-related processing. We observed a robust dorsal-to-ventral gradient in mPFC-PPI connectivity in both social contexts, with spatially distributed effects spanning prefrontal and striatal regions. Notably, the Out-group IA exhibited more extensive PPI effects (Fig. 2E). Specifically, the ventrolateral subdivision (e.g., mPFC2) exhibited significant positive coupling with the nucleus accumbens (NAc), while dorsomedial subdivision (e.g., mPFC3) showed positive coupling with both GP and NAc.   

To link this gradient to behavior, Partial Least Squares (PLS) analysis revealed that a graded contribution of mPFC subregions positively predicted IA-related reaction time in the out-group condition (r = 0.2499; Fig. 2C). Moreover, mPFC1-striatal connectivity positively predicted both in-group IA (r = 0.31) and out-group (r = 0.40), with weights of striatal regions following to a dorsal-to-ventral hierarchy. This finding suggests that inter-individual variability in gradient engagement tracks the processing speed of fairness decisions. 

Finally, to test the hypothesis of Corticostriatal Gating mechanism, we conducted the mediation analyses and found that mPFC-striatal coupling statistically mediated the relationship between striatal activity and prosocial traits. Specifically, ventral mPFC-GP connectivity significantly mediated the association between GP activity during IA and Social Interest (SRA4; indirect effect = -0.26), while dorsal mPFC-NAc connectivity mediated the association between NAc activity and Social Trust (SRA2; indirect effect = 0.17; Fig. 2D). These findings demonstrate a top-down gating mechanism where specific mPFC subregions modulate distinct striatal outputs to regulate social behavior.

Conclusion

The present study delineates the neurocomputational architecture underlying Inequality Aversion (IA). By integrating behavioral modeling with connectivity-based parcellation, connectivity profiles and multivariate analyses, we demonstrate that the human preference for fairness is context-dependent, supported by a hierarchically organized corticostriatal system. We revealed three major findings that extend current models of social decision-making. Behaviorally, we found that IA is context-dependent: inequality involving in-group members imposed a higher cognitive cost (slower reaction times) due to social expectation violations, whereas out-group IA was relatively automatic. This divergence was supported by a rostral-caudal dissociation in neural recruitment, where the cognitively demanding in-group IA recruited rostral regulatory networks (OFC, IFG), while the automatic out-group IA engaged caudal attentional systems (Precuneus, IPL). Crucially, we dissected the neural mechanisms governing this Out-group IA, revealing a functional double dissociation along a dorsal-to-ventral gradient in the mPFC and striatum. The dorsal striatum encoded the magnitude of aversion (driving the choices to reject unfairness), while the ventral mPFC tracked the processing cost (reaction time). Finally, mediation analyses supported a corticostriatal gating mechanism, where mPFC-striatum coupling downregulated the impact of striatal activity to regulate behavior. These findings suggest that the human brain utilizes a hierarchically organized, socially gated neural mechanism to resolve the conflict between social bonds and expectation violations.

First, we address the longstanding debate regarding the cognitive cost of fairness. We show that this cognitive cost is dictated by social expectations: in-group inequality triggers a high-stakes social prediction error that recruits deliberative control resources, whereas out-group fairness relies on a distinct, valuation-based architecture. This distinction is critical for reconciling conflict-based and value-based models of social cognition, suggesting the distinct neural mechanisms of betrayal by a friend v.s. unfairness by a stranger.

Second, we map the structural basis of this regulation to a robust dorsal-to-ventral gradient within the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC). Using tractography-based parcellation, we reveal that this intrinsic gradient segregates the neural computation of IA: dorsal mPFC integrates with frontoparietal control networks to drive the choice to reject unfairness, while ventral mPFC tracks the processing cost (reaction time) and link to valuation systems. This topography provides a structural scaffold for the moral circle, allowing the brain to titrate control resources based on the complexity of the social input.

Finally, we identify a Corticostriatal Gating Mechanism as the engine of prosocial control. Our mediation analyses demonstrate that the mPFC does not merely represent social value but actively gates striatal signals to regulate behavior. Specifically, top-down coupling from dorsal and ventral mPFC downregulates primitive striatal aversion signals, enabling context-appropriate prosocial responses even when they conflict with immediate self-interest.

Taken together, these findings provide a systems-level account of human social decision-making. Our findings suggest that the capacity for social cooperation extends beyond simple heuristics, relying on the brain’s ability to repurpose conserved, gradient-based decision circuits to navigate the complex tension between biological self-interest and the social necessity of fairness.
