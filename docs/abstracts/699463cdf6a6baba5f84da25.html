Introduction

Treatment-resistant depression (TRD) presents a major clinical challenge, with many patients failing to respond to standard therapies. Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) targeting the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) has emerged as an effective alternative, yet its efficacy remains heterogeneous. 1 A key limitation lies in the imprecise localization of stimulation targets. Recent advances highlight the subgenual anterior cingulate cortex (sgACC) and its functional connectivity (FC) with the left DLPFC as a critical circuit for TMS efficacy2,3; however, group-level FC targets derived from healthy cohorts2,3 may not accurately reflect the group-level FC targets derived from healthy cohorts may not accurately reflect the aberrant connectivity profiles in major depressive disorder (MDD). To address this, we developed an MDD big data-guided, individualized TMS targeting algorithm. This novel method integrates robust, group-level statistical maps of sgACC-FC abnormalities from the large-scale DIRECT consortium with individual resting‐state functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (rs‐fMRI) using dual regression to generate precise, patient-specific DLPFC targets. 4 By circumventing the low signal-to-noise ratio in the sgACC region and leveraging the reliability of big data, this algorithm promises to enhance targeting accuracy and therapeutic outcomes. This prospective, open-label clinical trial aims to evaluate the feasibility and preliminary efficacy of applying this personalized targeting protocol within an intensive Stanford Neuromodulation Therapy (SNT) framework5 for TRD. This study incorporates a comprehensive phenotyping battery (including various questionnaires and cognitive assessments) to profile treatment response and investigates brain network mechanisms of this intervention with resting-state fMRI, aiming to advance precision neuromodulation and treatment outcomes for TRD.

Methods

Participants and Intervention

Thirty outpatients with treatment-resistant depression (age 18–41; 9 female) received a 5-day, open-label, individualized intermittent theta-burst stimulation (iTBS) intervention at the Institute of Psychology, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Following the SNT framework5, the iTBS protocol comprised 50 sessions (1,800 pulses/session) delivered in 10 daily sessions over consecutive days at 90% of the resting motor threshold, with 50-minute inter-session intervals (Fig 1A).  

Individualized Targeting and Neuromavigation

Prior to intervention, each participant underwent a baseline MRI scan. An individualized left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex target was generated using a published MDD big-data-guided, individualized TMS targeting algorithm based on a dual-regression approach. Precise and reproducible coil placement was ensured using the the Black Dolphin Navigation Robot (S-50, China), a sub-millimeter smart neuromavigation system, alongside a 3D individualized brain mask (Fig 1A). 

Clinical and Cognitive Assessments

Depressive symptoms were assessed daily during the intervention using the 6-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-6), analyzed via generalized linear mixed models. The primary clinical outcome was the change from baseline to post-intervention on the Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS). Secondary outcome measures included the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17), HAMD-6, Hamilton Anxiety Scale (HAMA), and General Assessment of Functioning (GAF). Participants also completed self-report questionnaires (e.g., PHQ-9, GAD-7, see Fig 1D) and computer-based cognitive tasks (e.g., stop-signal, Wisconsin card sorting, emotional n-back) (see Fig 1B). Response was defined as a ≥50% symptom reduction on these scales; remission was defined as scores ≤10 (MADRS), ≤7 (HAMD-17), or ≤4 (HAMD-6). Pre-post comparisons used paired t-tests or Wilcoxon Rank Sum Tests, depending on normality assessed by the Shapiro-Wilk test. After the post-assessment, participants were followed online for up to 12 months. The current study focuses on the first 4-week follow-ups, a period during which participants were required to maintain a stable antidepressant medication regimen. Longitudinal changes in MADRS and HAMD-17 scores were analyzed with generalized linear mixed models, with p-values Bonferroni-corrected.

Neuroimaging Acquisition and Analysis

MRI protocols included T1, T2, diffusion tensor imaging, resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI), think-aloud fMRI, and rumination-task fMRI (Fig 1C, Fig 1E). Rs-fMRI data from 29 participants (one missing post-intervention scan) were preprocessed using the DPABISurf protocol, with and without global signal regression. Whole-brain functional connectivity (FC) matrices (454 × 454) were constructed using the DPABINet pipeline, incorporating the Schaefer 400 atlas (parcellated into 17 networks grouped into 8 major networks) and the Tian atlas of 54 subcortical areas. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were computed and Fisher’s r-to-z transformed. Pre-post FC changes were analyzed with permutation-based paired t-tests (5000 permutations) within DPABINet, and results were corrected using Network-Based Statistics (NBS). Edges demonstrating the most significant FC change were identified, and their change was correlated with clinical improvement.

Results

Safety and Cognitive Performance

No severe adverse events were reported. Side effects were limited to transient fatigue, mild discomfort at the stimulation site, and facial muscle discomfort during stimulation. Four participants reported increased anxiety during some sessions. All side effects were manageable and resolved spontaneously. Cognitive assessment revealed no negative side effects; instead, performance improved significantly on the stop-signal task (higher accuracy: t(22)=3.44, p<0.05; shorter reaction time: t(22)=-3.14, p<0.01) and the Wisconsin card sorting task (decreased reaction time: t(22)=-2.24, p<0.05), indicating enhanced cognitive inhibition and flexibility.

Clinical Outcomes

Generalized linear mixed-model analysis revealed a significant effect of day on mean HAMD-6 scores (F(5,145)=28.91, p<0.001) and a significant effect of week on both mean MADRS (F(5,137)=16.27, p<0.001) and HAMD-17 scores (F(5,135)=31.00, p<0.001). Scores at all follow-up time points were significantly lower than baseline (Bonferroni-corrected p<0.01). The response rate immediately post-intervention was 23.33% for both MADRS and HAMD-17, increasing to 35.71% (MADRS) and 42.86% (HAMD-17) at weeks 2 and 4. Remission rates showed a similar trajectory, increasing from 6.67% to 17.86% (MADRS) and from 10% to 21.43% (HAMD-17) over four weeks. In the longitudinal intent-to-treat analysis (up to one year), 50% of participants (15/30) met remission criteria and 70% (21/30) met response criteria.

Symptom and Functional Improvement

Significant improvements were observed across most clinical measures from baseline to post-intervention. Depressive symptoms were significantly reduced (QIDS-SR16: t(25)=-6.29, p<0.001, d=-1.23; PHQ-9: t(24)=-15.72, p<0.001, d=-3.14), alongside reduced anxiety (HAMA: t(29)=-6.00, p<0.001, d=-1.09; SAS: t(24)=-3.71, p=0.001, d=-0.75). Participants demonstrated improved daily functioning (GAF: t(29)=4.18, p<0.001, d=0.76) and better work/social adjustment (WSAS: t(20)=-2.99, p=0.007, d=-0.65). They reported less emotional disturbance (POMS_TMD: t(24)=-6.90, p<0.001, d=-1.38), more positive emotions (RESS_HAP: t(22)=2.65, p=0.02, d=0.65; RESS_POS: t(22)=2.20, p=0.04, d=0.46), and reduced cognitive fusion (CFQ: t(24)=-5.69, p<0.001, d=-1.14), experiential avoidance (AAQ-II: Z=-2.48, p=0.01, r=-0.53), and rumination (RRS_RT: t(18)=-4.19, p=0.001, d=-0.96).

Functional Connectivity Changes

Network-based statistic analysis of rs-fMRI data revealed a significant, widespread reduction in whole-brain functional connectivity (FC) post-intervention (Edge p<0.001, Component p<0.05). Prominent reductions were observed between the somatomotor (SMN) and visual networks (VN), the default mode (DMN) and ventral attention networks (VAN), and the DMN and SMN. The most significant FC decrease (t=-5.57, p<0.001) was between the left hippocampal head (limbic network) and the left SomMo3 region (SMN). This reduction correlated positively with clinical improvement on the MADRS (R=0.50, p=0.006). Increased FC between the dorsal attention network (DAN) and VN was also observed.

Conclusion

Conclusion: 

This prospective, open-label clinical trial demonstrates the feasibility, safety, and preliminary efficacy of a novel, big data-guided individualized TMS targeting method applied within an intensive, 5-day SNT protocol for patients with treatment-resistant TRD. By integrating group-level maps of sgACC dysconnectivity from a large MDD cohort with individual rs-fMRI data, our dual regression algorithm generated precise, patient-specific DLPFC targets, circumventing sgACC signal-to-noise limitations. Clinical outcomes showed a significant and sustained reduction in depressive and anxious symptoms, with remarkable longitudinal improvement: response and remission rates continued to increase for weeks post-intervention, ultimately reaching 70% and 50%, respectively, at one-year follow-up. Notably, the intervention also enhanced cognitive control, improved daily functioning, and reduced maladaptive emotional and ruminative processes, underscoring its broad therapeutic impact.

Neuroimaging analyses revealed that successful treatment was associated with a targeted reconfiguration of large-scale brain networks. The most significant change was a widespread reduction in functional connectivity (FC), particularly in pathways implicated in depression pathophysiology: between the somatomotor (SMN) and visual networks (VN), and critically, between the default mode (DMN), ventral attention (VAN), and somatomotor networks. The strongest FC decrease was observed between the left hippocampus (limbic network) and a somatomotor region, and the magnitude of this reduction correlated positively with clinical improvement. This suggests a core mechanism whereby intensive, targeted DLPFC stimulation may act to "uncouple" overactive hippocampal-driven emotional/ruminative circuitry from somatosensory and self-referential (DMN) processing, thereby dampening the somatic and cognitive embodiment of negative affect. Concurrently, increased FC between the dorsal attention network (DAN) and VN suggests a facilitation of adaptive, externally-oriented attentional processes. Together, these findings support the model of effective antidepressant neuromodulation as a "network reset" that restores balance between internally-focused (DMN) and externally-focused (DAN) systems.

The study has several important implications. First, it provides a proof-of-concept for precision targeting in accelerated TMS protocols, suggesting that individualized connectivity-based targets can yield robust and durable clinical outcomes. Second, the observation of a delayed clinical response trajectory, with benefits consolidating over weeks, underscores the need for extended follow-up in TMS trials to fully capture treatment efficacy. Third, the identified FC reduction in the hippocampus-somatomotor circuit emerges as a promising candidate biomarker for treatment response.

However, this study has limitations. Its open-label, non-randomized design and modest sample size preclude definitive efficacy conclusions and may inflate effect size estimates due to placebo and expectation effects. The lack of a sham-control or active comparator group prevents isolation of the specific effect of the individualized targeting from the intensive SNT schedule itself.

Future research must prioritize a large-scale, double-blind, randomized controlled trial (RCT) comparing this individualized targeting approach against standard targeting methods (e.g., the 5-cm rule or group-average FC targets) within the SNT framework. Incorporating a sham-control arm will be essential to establish efficacy. Finally, investigating whether baseline connectivity profiles can predict the delayed response pattern or the specific circuit changes observed could pave the way for truly personalized treatment selection and optimization in TRD.
