Introduction

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are a pillar of today’s computer vision and image processing. Their architecture, mainly based on convolutional and pooling layers, progressively extracts hierarchical patterns from input images. When applied to magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) images, CNNs have demonstrated strong performance in the classification of neurological diseases.1 Despite these advances, combining MRI data with CNN models remains challenging due to the high dimensionality and complexity of brain images, as well as the limited interpretability of CNNs commonly viewed as black boxes. Generally, neuroimaging studies rely on feature-based approaches, such as volumetric or radiomic features fed as input to traditional Machine Learning (ML) algorithms, which offer greater explainability.2 This contrast motivated our study, which compares CNN performance with feature-based ML models for the classification of multiple system atrophy (MSA), a rare atypical parkinsonian disorder, whose symptoms overlap with those of Parkinson’s disease (PD), making precise diagnosis particularly challenging. We therefore used T1-weighted MRI images (T1-MRI), a fundamental non-invasive technique used to examine brain structure and detect abnormalities.

Methods

Our dataset consists of 92 MSA patients age-matched with 92 healthy controls. All images were pre-processed, ensuring spatial normalization in the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) template and skull-stripping. The architecture of our 3D CNN, detailed in previous studies,3 is inspired from AlexNet and VGGNet and we considered our CNN model trained directly on T1-MRI as baseline. Subsequently, we compared its performance with six state-of-the-art ML algorithms —Linear Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest (RF), Logistic Regression (LR), XGBoost, K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), and Decision Tree (DT)— trained on two types of features extracted from the same images : (i) U-Net-Vol, consisting of whole-brain ROI volumes (n=138) computed by AssemblyNet,4 a novel U-Net-based deep learning (DL) pipeline pre-trained for T1-MRI segmentation; and (ii) CNN-Feat, where our 3D CNN was utilized as a feature extractor to establish voxel-based representation, thus mapping each image into a 4608-dimensional feature vector (flatten layer output). Both CNN and ML classifiers were trained using five-fold cross validation repeated ten times, and their performance was evaluated on a hold-out set, representing 20% of the dataset.

Results

Overall, we achieved competitive performance for MSA classification, with 80-95% accuracy for both CNN and most ML classifiers. Our results revealed varying performance across ML algorithms and feature types. RF with U-Net-Vol achieved highest accuracy (95.0%), outperforming our baseline (89.5%). However, SVM and LR performed better with CNN-Feat (93.9%). XGBoost reached better results with volumetric features, whereas KNN and DT demonstrated more limited discriminative ability for both features. Statistical analysis using Kruskal-Wallis followed by a post-hoc Dunn’s test showed significant differences for most pairwise comparisons to our baseline (p − value < 0.05), except for SVM and LR with U-Net-Vol. Furthermore, SHAP values of RF model with U-Net-Vol highlighted the brainstem, cerebellum, and putamen as the most discriminative regions, in line with our previous MSA-related studies.1,2,5 These findings were in line as well with our CNN activation maps showing strong activations in the same regions.

Conclusion

Despite the differences in feature extraction approaches, we found consistent results between the implemented explainability methods. Our findings emphasize a strong relation between the classifier architecture and the type of extracted features, as each ML method leverages distinct aspects of the data. Additionally, our results show that replacing the fully connected block of CNNs with a classifier can improve predictive accuracy. Future work will aim to combine both feature types into a unified multi-level representation of T1-MRI images by integrating quantitative anatomical information with voxel-based patterns. This approach could contribute to better utilization of MRI data, a deeper understanding of underexplored neurodegenerative diseases through the explainability of volumetric features and ultimately improve the development of computer-aided diagnosis tools.
