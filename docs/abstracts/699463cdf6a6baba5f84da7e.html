Introduction

The transition from childhood to adulthood involves significant reorganization of the functional connectome.1,2 Recent work has characterized the macroscale cortical organization in adults using low-dimensional gradients that separate unimodal sensory regions from transmodal association cortex.3,4 However, it remains unclear how the developing brain "grows into" the adult patterns of functional architecture. Here, we investigated the effects of age, state (i.e., movie and rest), and age x state interactions on global gradient measures in children and adolescents. Further, we asked whether these established adult gradients of brain organization scaffold connectome-level developmental trajectories.

Methods

Both movie-watching and resting-state fMRI data used here are from help-seeking children and youth in the Healthy Brain Network dataset.5 We divided participants into a developmental cohort (ages 5-18 years; n = 781, 343 females) and a young adult “template” cohort (ages 18-21 years; n = 26, 12 females). Standard preprocessing was performed using fMRIPrep, and parcellation was done using the Shaefer-1000 atlas.

All analyses were done separately for rest and movie conditions. FC was computed for each individual as the pairwise correlation of parcel time series, and these were averaged for both developmental and young adult cohorts. Reference young adult gradients were computed by applying PCA directly to the adult group FC matrix. We retained the top n gradients required to explain 90% of the variance in the adult FC matrix which was 15 in both rest and movie. We then performed the following analyses:

1. Gradient maturation: We projected individual subjects’ FC matrices into the adult gradient space to recover the same template gradients for each subject. We then computed three metrics: reconstruction error (information lost using adult gradients), variance aligning with adult gradients, and alignment score (Procrustes similarity). We modeled the effects of age, state, and their interaction on each metric and tested for statistical significance using permutation testing (5,000 permutations).

2. FC age effects related to adult gradients: We performed mass-univariate regression on the strength of all functional connectivity edges, modeling age-related effects while controlling for head motion, sex, and site. For each edge, we quantified the variance attributable specifically to age by comparing a full model that included linear and quadratic age terms to a reduced model without age. The resulting difference in explained variance (ΔR²), weighted by the direction of the age effect, was assembled into a signed age effect matrix and projected onto the adult gradient space to assess how age-based FC differences align with the top gradients of adult cortical organization.

Results

Gradient similarity analysis revealed a state dependent functional alignment with age. We found a robust main effect of state on reconstitution error (p < 0.001), where movie-watching significantly reduced error compared to rest regardless of age, suggesting naturalistic stimuli globally align FC variance to the mature gradient architecture better than resting state. We observed significant age × state interactions for variance capture (p = 0.004) and alignment score (p = 0.016) such that these measures showed a significant increase in movie watching but not in resting state.

The FC age effects analysis confirmed that a significant portion of the age-based FC reorganization variance aligned with the top gradients from the young adult cohort. Approximately 60% of the total variance in age-related edge changes aligned with the top 15 adult gradients (p < 0.001). This suggests that the age-based FC differences may be underpinned by a selective strengthening or pruning of connections along the dominant gradients of the adult brain architecture. We are now investigating these findings in a longitudinal sample.

Conclusion

These findings suggest that the brain’s principal functional gradients are present early in development and provide a stable scaffold for maturation. With age, functional organization becomes increasingly aligned with the adult gradient structure, a process that is most clearly revealed during naturalistic processing. Consistent with this, developmental changes in FC at the edge level align with the dominant gradients that define mature cortical organization, reflecting progressive refinement that supports and reinforces an established functional architecture.
