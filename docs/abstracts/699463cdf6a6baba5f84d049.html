Introduction

Event-Related Potentials (ERPs) have been instrumental in investigating the neural processes underlying cognition. While traditional approaches to ERPs focus on average voltages in pre-defined time-windows, there has been a recent shift towards more advanced analysis methods. One method is Multivariate Pattern Analysis (MVPA), which provides a fine-grained spatiotemporal window into neural representations.1 The idea behind MVPA is to predict to which experimental condition an observed EEG recording belongs. By extending this analysis in time, one obtains a Generalization Across Time (GAT) matrix that informs on the temporal consistency and the neural dynamics of the representations.2 Heikel et al.3 applied this method to a semantic mismatch experiment investigating the nature of the N400 and P600 ERP components in language comprehension. They start from a set of possible GAT patterns that they argue follow from possible organizations of neural processes (see Fig. 13), and find evidence for a 'cascading' model of the N400 and P600. We here replicate their methodology on a different semantic mismatch dataset and report conflicting results. Rather than finding evidence for a `cascading’ organization, we find evidence supporting a `latency shifted’ process, according to the possible GAT patterns identified by Heikel et al.3 Investigation of spatial differences in their EEG signals revealed that right anterior electrodes separated conditions over the whole timeframe in the data, rather than showing the canonical transition at centroparietal regions from the negative N400 to the positive P600 (see Fig. 2B3). This suggests that the sustained nature of the signal at these anterior electrodes might underlie their ‘cascading’ result. In a second experiment, we therefore used synthetic data, with and without spatial dissociation, to investigate whether and to what degree this is indeed the case. We find that their `cascading’ result is indeed driven by spatial dissociation.

Methods

The dataset for our first experiment was provided by Aurnhammer et al.4 and consists of EEG recordings in an expectation based language comprehension experiment. Participants were presented with sentences varying in expectancy and lexical association of the target word in four conditions. Condition A was high on both, while condition D was low on both, Condition B featured low lexical association and high expectancy, whereas condition C was high in lexical association and low in expectancy. The data consists of 49 participants (mean age = 23 years; age range 19-29; 6 male) of which 9 were excluded due to excessive artifacts or to technical problems during recording. After a short six sentence practice phase, participants were presented with three blocks of 80 sentences. Rapid serial visual presentation was used to display the words of each sentence for 350ms, followed by a 150ms blank. At the end of each sentence, the participants had to rate semantic plausibility. A total of 120 experimental and 120 filler sentences were presented in an order that minimized confounds of expectancy or priming across trials. EEG was recorded from 26 scalp electrodes distributed according to the 10-20 system. The sampling rate was 500Hz and preprocessing of the data included ocular artifact removal based on EOG recordings, re-referencing to the average of left and right mastoids, band-pass filtering between 0.01 and 30 Hz and baseline correction. Epochs were segmented from -200ms to 1200ms relative to target word onset. The data was preprocessed by the authors of the original paper.

Our methodology for experiment one replicated Heikel et al.3 Data were downsampled to 100Hz, and we focused on conditions A and D, as they show the biggest difference in N400 and P600 amplitude. For each participant, a classifier was trained to tease apart the conditions based on voltages at a single time point. While Heikel et al.3 used a ridge classifier, we used logistic regression on electrodes F3, Fz, F4, C3, Cz, C4, P3, Pz, and P4 to optimize decoding performance. Logistic regression was implemented with an inverse regularization of 0.001, an L2 penalty term, a 'liblinear' solver for optimization, and a limit of 1000 iterations for convergence. Stratified K-fold cross validation using 5 splits was applied with 80% of the data per fold used to train the classifier on one time point, and the remaining 20% to test on all time points. Performance was evaluated on the test set using AUC scores. These scores were accumulated across and averaged over folds and visualized using a GAT matrix depicting the generalized decoding performance, as can be seen in Figure 1. Additionally, the diagonal performance (i.e., classifiers tested only at the time point on which they were trained) and the average performance of all classifiers trained in the N400 (300-500ms) and P600 (600-800ms) time windows were plotted. 

We generated the data for experiment two synthetically, using combinations of sine waves with random gaussian noise. Biphasic signals were constructed to simulate the N400 and P600 responses found by Aurnhammer et al.4 in the experimental condition (D) relative to control (A). The aim was to investigate whether a topographical spatial dissociation is needed for MVPA to indicate a `cascading’ architecture. To this end, we simulated data both with and without incorporating such spatial dissociation (see Fig. 1.2 and 1.3). To simulate spatial dissociation, sustained signals were added to all anterior electrodes, rather than focusing only on right anterior electrodes as in the Heikel et al. data.3 The MVPA pipeline was otherwise the same as in experiment one.

Results

The results for experiment one can be seen in Figure 2.1. The y-axis depicts the time point on which the classifier was trained, while the x-axis depicts the time points it was tested on. One row is therefore the decoding performance of one trained classifier. The GAT matrix for the MVPA analysis executed on the dataset from Aurnhammer et al.4 (see Fig. 2.1) shows a narrow pattern around the diagonal with squares around the N400 and P600 time windows with some degree of overlap. This suggests sustained activity of a neural pattern in these time windows. Interestingly, our results indicate consistent below chance performance for classifiers trained on time points around 400ms and tested on time points between 600ms and 1200ms. This is reflected by the blue bars, indicating that classifiers trained on the N400 time window systematically tend to predict the wrong class and vice versa. Based on the possible GAT structures identified by Heikel et al.3, this would suggest that a similar neural pattern is present across conditions, supporting a 'latency shift’ organization. Indeed, this contrasts with the results of Heikel et al.3 (see Fig. 3A in the original paper3), which supported a ‘cascading’ architecture. Further, comparing our results to the GAT matrix of Heikel et al.3 reveals that the patterns are indeed qualitatively different. The overlap in Figure 2.1 is less pronounced and overall above chance decoding starts at ca. 300ms instead of 150ms already. 

The results for experiment two can be seen in Figure 2.2 and 2.3, depicting the GAT matrices for the two simulated synthetic datasets. Figure 2.2 shows the simulated data with a consistent spatial separation of conditions on the anterior electrodes. A smeared out pattern of above chance decoding around the squares of the N400 and P600 time windows can be seen along with overlap in the decoding pattern between the N400 and P600 windows. Further, no below chance decoding bars can be observed in neither the N400 nor the P600 time window, which is in line with the pattern of a 'cascading' sequence (see Fig. 1C3) as identified by Heikel et al.3 On the other hand, the GAT matrix in Figure 2.3 depicts the simulated data without spatial separation between conditions and shows a different pattern–high within but low across N400 and P600 time window decoding performance, resembling Heikel et al.’s3 `latency shift’ organization. 

In sum, we observed a resemblance between the results from the simulated data with spatial separation and Heikel et al.3, and between the simulated data without spatial separation and Aurnhammer et al.4 (see Fig. 2 & Heikel et al.3), indicating that there seems to be a qualitative difference between the data from Heikel et al.3 and Aurnhammer et al.4 in terms of spatial dissociation.

Conclusion

The GAT matrix of our first experiment (see Fig. 2.1) indicated contradicting results to the GAT matrix from Heikel et al.3, which was argued to show evidence for a 'cascading' organization of the N400 and P600. Instead, our results rather suggest  a 'latency shift' organization, rendering direct conclusions about the nature of the temporal organization problematic. Experiment two revealed more about the link between the neural data and the patterns in the resulting GAT matrices. It seems that there is a many-to-one relationship when going from underlying neural patterns to their expression in GAT matrices. The 'latency shift' pattern that Heikel et al.3 propose (see Fig. 1D3) can be caused by the same underlying neural pattern being shifted in time between conditions. This can cause the classifier to identify the pattern present at later time points as belonging to the opposite condition because it actually indicated the opposite condition during training. As the analysis of the simulated data in experiment two (see Fig. 2.3) shows, the same pattern in the GAT matrix can be caused by a different neural pattern in the input. In the simulated data with no spatial separation, the reason for the below chance decoding is an inversion in the polarity of the neural activity relative to baseline. While Condition D shows enhanced activity in the N400 time window, in terms of a negative deflection, within the P600 time window it shows enhanced activity as a positive deflection. A classifier trained in the N400 time window therefore would associate a more negative deflection in the P600 time window with condition D, while it actually belongs to condition A. Hence, the below chance decoding may be caused by a polarity inversion in neural activity of multiple distinct processes, rather than a temporal simple shift between conditions.

This apparent many-to-one mapping from neural patterns to GAT matrices renders inferences from the GAT matrices about the neural data challenging and non- deterministic, thereby leaving uncertainty about the actual cause of the pattern in the GAT matrix. For our results of experiment one, using the data from Aurnhammer et al.4, this implies that two alternative explanations cannot be distinguished; it could both be due to a negative deflection followed by a positive deflection of potentially multiple processes and to a single latency shifted process between conditions. These explanations cannot be disambiguated considering only MVPA results, while the ERP results actually do reveal two distinct processes, as indexed by the N400 and P600, respectively. Additionally, since experiment two showed the importance of spatial dissociation of conditions in the neural patterns, it is possible that the low spatial resolution of EEG data might affect results and hide the real neural patterns due to smearing of the activity as a result of volume conduction. 

In conclusion, the many-to-one mapping from underlying neural patterns to their expression in GAT matrices, as well as the blurring effect of volume conduction on the exact readings of brain activity with EEG, render the interpretation of MVPA-derived GAT matrices challenging. Hence, while MVPA seems promising as a more fine grained analysis method of EEG data, it suffers from spatial inaccuracy and needs to be interpreted with caution.
