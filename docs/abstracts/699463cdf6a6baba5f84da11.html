Introduction

From early childhood to adolescence, gray matter (GM) and white matter (WM) change following a topological organization. While GM decreases in the prefrontal, striatum, and cuneus/precuneus, WM increases in the brainstem and the cerebellum across youth1. Gray matter reduction is interpreted as a maturational process (pruning), ultimately leading to better performance. For instance, cortical reductions in frontoparietal areas are associated with better working memory performance from childhood to adulthood2. Working memory, inhibition, and flexibility constitute the core executive functions (EF), a set of goal-directed behaviors with a major impact on cognition, daily activities, academic achievement, and mental health3. However, the relationship between EF development and gray/white matter maturation in early school age requires further exploration. The question of reproducibility remains open, and many studies include limited sample size and cross-sectional data4. To address this gap, we leveraged the baseline data from the “Art and Development” study, a randomized controlled trial evaluating the benefits of arts interventions5. These data include 103 children aged 5-9 years who completed T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging and a comprehensive battery of EF tasks. We combine voxel-based morphometry with linear mixed models (LMMs) and mediation analysis to assess age effects on gray/white matter maturation, executive development, and the relationship between age, white matter volume changes, and global EF.

Methods

Psychometric testing. Children performed standardized, gamified EF tasks from which we derived six cognitive measures, including visuospatial working memory (2-back condition accuracy), verbal working memory (Digit Span Backward), cognitive flexibility (DCCS), interference control (Flanker incongruent trial inverted response time), response inhibition (Go/No-go d′), and selective attention (U-Cancellation d′) tasks. A global EF score was computed (mean of 6 cognitive z-scores).

MRI acquisition & preprocessing. High-resolution T1-weighted MRI scans were acquired in 141 children using a 0.8 mm isotropic MPRAGE sequence (TR/TE = 2000/2.49 ms, TI = 900 ms, flip angle = 9°, GRAPPA = 2, FOV = 230×230×166.4 mm; 208 slices). The final sample includes images from 103 children who completed a full behavioral assessment and passed quality control (CAT12 mean weighted image quality ratio = 89.97 ± S.D. = 0.4%). T1-weighted images were segmented, modulated, and smoothed at 6 mm FWHM kernel size using CAT12’s voxel-based morphometry (VBM) standard pipeline (output resolution = 1.5 mm isotropic).

Statistical analyses. Modulated gray/white-matter segments were smoothed with a 6 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel size and entered as input for multiple regression models in SPM12. Analyses were restricted to the corresponding tissue type using an explicit mask derived from the age-appropriate MNI pediatric asymmetric gray/white matter template (4.5–8.5-year-old prepubertal template) thresholded at 0.2. We examined associations between regional gray/white-matter volume and age (mean age = 7.22 ± 0.86, age range = 5.5-8.9 years old), with Socioeconomic Status (SES = (z(parental education)+z(income/household size)/2), Biological Sex, and Total Intracranial Volume (TIV) as covariates. LMM evaluated the effect of Age Group (2 levels: 52 older kids, 51 younger kids; based on age median split of 7.14 years), Category (2-back, Digit Span, DCCS, Flanker Go/No-Go, U-Cancellation), and their interaction on executive function performance (dependent variable: z-score of cognitive measure), controlling for SES, and Biological Sex. Finally, we conducted mediation analyses to investigate whether gray/white matter volumes mediated the association between age and EF, with the significance of the indirect effects evaluated using 5,000 bootstrap resamples. For the latter analysis, GM/WM volumes were extracted from significant clusters associated with the age effect in the multiple regression models (whole-brain analyses) at statistical threshold depicted below in Figure 1.

Results

Age-related brain structural changes. GM volume decreases, and WM increases associated with Age are depicted in Figure 1A. GM volume decrease (p < 0.0005 uncorrected, k=150 voxels) focused in frontal regions: the left medial orbital gyrus and dorsolateral superior frontal gyrus, medial parietal regions: the right cuneus/precuneus, and the thalamus-striatum. Large white matter volume increase (p < 0.05 FWE corrected, k=150 voxels) occurred in the brainstem and the cerebellum. Spatial overlap between GM decreases and WM increases was observed at GM–WM boundaries, particularly in subcortical and infratentorial regions. These results may relate to concurrent cortical thinning and white matter expansion rather than distinct tissue-specific effects.

Age-related change in behavior and white matter mediation effects. The LMM showed a significant main effect of Age Group on EF performance ([F(1, 97.43) = 30.2, p < 0.001], Figure 1B). Test Category, Group * Test Category, Sex, and SES effects were not significant. Mediation analysis (Figure 1C) revealed that Age was positively associated with global EF performance (EF; β = 0.38, p < 0.001) and brainstem–cerebellar white matter volume (β = 0.48, p < 0.001), with a significant indirect effect of age on EF via white matter volume (β = 0.16, p = .008). Importantly, the direct association between age and EF remained significant after accounting for white matter volume (p < .001). An additional model including age-related gray matter volume change neither improved model fit nor showed associations with global EF performance.

Conclusion

While gray matter volume decreased in frontal, parietal, and subcortical regions, white matter volume increased predominantly in brainstem–cerebellar regions in a hundred early school-age children, consistent with known developmental trajectories1. Mediation analyses indicated that age-related white matter expansion partially explained age-related variability in global executive function, while gray matter volume changes were not directly associated with executive function performance. These findings suggest that large-scale brainstem-cerebellum white matter maturation provides a key structural substrate for executive function development at this developmental stage, with gray matter changes likely reflecting concurrent but functionally distant maturational processes. Further analyses will include a population-based template, and seed-based functional connectivity analyses aiming to explore the functional relevance of gray matter pruning for EF development.
