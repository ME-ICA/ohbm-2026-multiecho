Introduction

The human cerebral cortex is organized along continuous functional gradients that capture intrinsic dimensions of large-scale brain organization. The principal functional gradient describes a transition from primary unimodal cortices to transmodal association regions, while a secondary gradient further differentiates visual cortex from somatomotor and auditory regions1. Converging evidence indicates that similar hierarchical axes are expressed across multiple neurobiological modalities, including microstructural properties, functional connectivity patterns, and cortical gene expression, reflecting fundamental organizing principles of the brain2. 

Cortical structure provides the biological substrate that constrains and supports intrinsic functional organization, thereby enabling cognitive processing and behavioral flexibility. Structural features such as cortical thickness exhibit high heritability, and have been consistently linked to individual differences in cognition and behaviors. However, it remains unclear how cortical structure aligns with intrinsic functional gradients at the individual level, how such coupling contributes to behavioral variability, and to what extent these relationships are genetically determined.

In the present study, using Human Connectome Project Young Adult (HCP-YA) and UK Biobank datasets, we investigated how cortical thickness relates to behaviors through its alignment with multiple functional gradients, and further characterized the genetic architecture of cortical thickness underlying these functional gradients.

Methods

Gradient-aligned cortical thickness

We quantified gradient-aligned cortical thickness for each individual. Canonical functional gradient maps were obtained from Margulies et al., derived from functional connectivity data of the HCP S900 cohort. Both group-level gradient maps and individual cortical thickness measures were parcellated using the Schaefer 400-region cortical atlas. For each participant, Pearson correlation coefficients were computed between the vector of regional cortical thickness values and each functional gradient map. These correlation coefficients indexed the degree to which an individual’s cortical thickness distribution aligned with each functional gradient axis and were referred to as gradient-aligned cortical thickness.

Behavioral association analysis in HCP

Associations between gradient-aligned cortical thickness and behavior were examined in the HCP-YA dataset (N = 1,113). Each gradient-aligned cortical thickness measure was correlated with 159 behavioral phenotypes. Partial correlation analyses were performed while controlling for age, sex, and total gray matter volume.

Heritability analysis

Heritability of gradient-aligned cortical thickness was estimated using twin data from the HCP-YA cohort. Drawing on measurements from monozygotic (MZ) and dizygotic (DZ) twin pairs, heritability estimates were obtained using the Sequential Oligogenic Linkage Analysis Routines (SOLAR) Eclipse software package, which applies a variance-components framework to partition phenotypic variance into genetic and environmental components.

Genome-wide association study

The same procedure was applied to the UK Biobank dataset to derive gradient-aligned cortical thickness measures. Genome-wide association analyses were conducted in a subset of 26,655 unrelated White British participants. Following genotype quality control, 7,301,482 variants were retained for analysis. Covariates included age at imaging, age squared, sex, assessment center, genotype measurement batch, and the first ten genetic principal components. Genome-wide statistical significance was defined as P < 5 × 10⁻⁸.

Genetic correlation estimation

We conducted LD-score regression (LDSC) analyses to estimate the genetic correlations between gradient-aligned cortical thickness and outcomes for brain health, including clinical diagnoses (Alzheimer’s disease, stroke, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia), cognition (fluid intelligence, reaction time, pairs matching, trail making test A, trail making test B, numeric memory, symbol digit, general memory performance) and brain structure (whole-brain cortical thickness) using summary statistics of GWAS.

Results

Behavioral associations of gradient-aligned cortical thickness

In the HCP-YA cohort, gradient-aligned cortical thickness exhibited dissociable associations with behavior across primary and secondary gradient dimensions (Fig. 1A). Primary-gradient-aligned cortical thickness (PG-CT) showed its strongest associations with the Cognition and In-Scanner Task Performance domains, with higher alignment consistently related to poorer cognitive performance, suggesting that tighter structure–function coupling along the principal gradient constrains cognitive flexibility. In contrast, Secondary-gradient-aligned cortical thickness (SG-CT) was most strongly associated with the Emotion and Substance Use domains. Specifically, greater SG-CT was negatively associated with negative emotionality, while being positively associated with substance use.

Analyses in the UK Biobank cohort yielded convergent associations of PG-CT with cognitive phenotypes. PG-CT was most strongly associated with fluid intelligence (r = −0.04, p = 2 × 10⁻¹⁸). In contrast, SG-CT showed its strongest associations with processing speed measures, including Symbol Digit Substitution (r = −0.04, p = 1 × 10⁻¹⁷), Trail Making Test B (r = 0.04, p = 2 × 10⁻¹⁵), and Trail Making Test A (r = 0.03, p = 1 × 10⁻¹⁰).

Heritability of gradient-aligned cortical thickness

Twin-based heritability analyses in HCP-YA revealed that both gradient-aligned cortical thickness measures were substantially genetically influenced. The heritability of PG-CT was estimated at 0.64, while SG-CT showed a heritability of 0.70, indicating greater environmental modulation of structure–function alignment along trans-modal hierarchies compared with more genetically constrained sensorymotor axes.

Genetic architecture of gradient-aligned cortical thickness

Genome-wide association analyses identified distinct genetic architectures underlying the two gradient-aligned measures (Fig. 1B). Significant loci for PG-CT mapped to genes including SLC39A8, STXBP6, C15orf54, BANK1, PSMD6, and PRICKLE2, which are implicated in neurodevelopment, cortical structural differentiation, synaptic function, and the regulation of system-level homeostasis. For SG-CT, significant loci mapped to MSH3, CMPK1, OMA1, DAB1, MTX3, FOXD2, DHFR, C15orf54, C16orf95, and MTRNR2L2, which are primarily related to cellular maintenance, metabolic function, mitochondrial function, and DNA repair.

LDSC analyses revealed gradient-specific genetic correlations. PG-CT showed significant negative genetic correlations with fluid intelligence (rg = −0.12, p = 0.03), general memory performance (rg = −0.27, p = 0.04), and whole-brain cortical thickness (rg = −0.31, p = 0.004). In contrast, SG-CT was positively genetically correlated with stroke (rg = 0.35, p = 0.008), negatively correlated with whole-brain cortical thickness (rg = −0.37, p = 0.04), and showed a trend-level association with reaction time (rg = 0.12, p = 0.07).

Conclusion

In this study, we demonstrate that individual differences in cortical thickness aligned with intrinsic functional gradients constitute a genetically informed pathway linking brain structure to behavior. By quantifying gradient-aligned cortical thickness, we show that alignment along the principal and secondary functional gradients exhibits dissociable behavioral profiles that are supported by distinct genetic architectures. Stronger alignment along the principal gradient is associated with lower cognitive performance and is underpinned by genetic influences related to system differentiation and hierarchical information processing, consistent with the notion that trans-modal cortical systems benefit from reduced structural constraint to support flexible and abstract cognition. In contrast, alignment along the secondary gradient preferentially relates to affective regulation, reward-related behaviors, and processing speed, and is supported by genetic mechanisms involved in cellular energy metabolism and structural maintenance, reflecting the organization of sensorimotor and sensory–action transformation pathways that link perceptual processing to action selection and autonomic responses. Together, these findings suggest that functional gradient–aligned cortical structure captures biologically distinct, genetically constrained modes of brain organization that differentially shape cognition, affect, and vulnerability to brain health outcomes.
