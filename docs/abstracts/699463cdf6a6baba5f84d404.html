Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias (ADRD) frequently involve multiple co-occurring neuropathologies such as amyloid-β (Aβ), tau, cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA), TDP-43, Lewy bodies (LB), and hippocampal sclerosis (HPSC). Structural MRI is non-invasive and widely available, yet the neuroanatomical signatures of these pathologies remain difficult to detect, especially when they co-occur. There is great interest in whether deep learning can reliably infer neuropathologies that previously could only be detected post mortem [3,4], but interpretability remains a critical barrier for clinical adoption.  Here, we evaluate a 3D convolutional neural network (3D CNN) trained solely on 3D volumetric T1-weighted (T1w) brain MRI for the prediction of six neuropathological outcomes, and we compare multiple explainable AI (xAI) methods to characterize the spatial features driving model decisions.

Methods

The primary dataset for our experiments consisted of a subset of the National Alzheimer’s Coordinating Center (NACC [1]) database (N=950; age: 76.01 ± 11.86 years, 625 Dementia/204 Cognitively Normal/ 121 Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI), 390F/560M), with rich multimodal data including both in vivo MRI imaging and post mortem neuropathology in the same subjects. MRI scans were bias-corrected, skull-stripped, nonlinearly aligned to a study-specific brain template, resampled to 2-mm isotropic resolution (91×109×91), and intensity-normalized. Separate 3D CNN models comprising four convolutional blocks with instance normalization, ReLU activations, dropout, and 3D pooling were individually trained to predict the presence of Aβ, tau, CAA, TDP-43, LB, and HPSC. Each network functions as an independent binary classifier rather than as part of a joint multi-output model. The network was optimized using Adam (1×10⁻⁴ learning rate with exponential decay), early stopping, and batch size 8. Performance was evaluated using balanced accuracy and F1 score on a held-out test set. To interpret model predictions, we applied a suite of xAI methods: occlusion sensitivity analysis (OSA), Guided Grad-CAM, Shapley value maps, SmoothGrad, Integrated Gradients, and layer-wise relevance propagation (LRP). Each method uses a different mathematical principle to highlight features in the image that were important in making the classification, yield maps for each individual and each pathology. For each pathology, individual saliency maps were computed and averaged across 100 test subjects for positive and negative classes. xAI maps were inspected qualitatively and compared for consistency across methods.

Results

The MRI-only 3D CNN demonstrated strong discriminative ability for several neuropathologies. Performance was highest for Aβ (balanced accuracy = 0.72; F1 score = 0.87) and TDP-43 (balanced accuracy = 0.79; F1 score = 0.68). Moderate performance was observed for tau and CAA, while LB and HPSC showed weaker classification performance, reflecting subtle or spatially heterogeneous signatures in T1w MRI. xAI analyses revealed pathology-specific and biologically plausible spatial patterns. Aβ and tau maps consistently highlighted temporal and parietal regions across SmoothGrad, Integrated Gradients, and LRP, aligning with atrophy patterns known to be related to these pathologies in ADRD. Tau maps showed especially strong medial temporal involvement. For HPSC, all methods prominently emphasized the hippocampus. TDP-43 maps exhibited distributed frontal and temporal contributions, consistent with clinical–pathological studies. OSA yielded coarse but stable patterns, whereas Guided Grad-CAM provided class-specific localization with sharper anatomical boundaries. SHAP and LRP revealed more focal high-magnitude attributions but occasionally showed strong hemispheric lateralization, consistent with known limitations of gradient-based xAI. Across pathologies, xAI methods agreed on broad regional trends but diverged in fine-grained voxel-level details, reflecting method-dependent sensitivities and the hierarchical nature of CNN feature representations.

Conclusion

A 3D CNN trained solely on structural MRI can detect multiple dementia-related neuropathologies with moderate to strong accuracy, particularly for Aβ and TDP-43. Comprehensive xAI analyses reveal that the model relies on anatomically plausible regions for several targets, especially tau and HPSC, supporting the biological validity of MRI-based deep learning approaches. As recently noted by [5] variability across xAI methods highlights ongoing challenges in reliably interpreting deep neural networks in neuroimaging. Salience maps depend on the accuracy of the classifier, and can differ because each method defines feature importance differently. Deep networks also rely on distributed feature combinations that cannot always be localized uniquely to specific spatial regions.
