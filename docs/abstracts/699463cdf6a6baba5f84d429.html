Introduction

Resting-state electrophysiological activity exhibits rich intrinsic structure that is remarkably stable within individuals. This structure can be leveraged to derive neural “fingerprints” that reliably differentiate individuals from brief recordings, reflecting trait-like properties of large-scale brain organization. Such neural fingerprints have been demonstrated primarily in MEG and fMRI, using spectral and network-level features, and have shown robustness across health and disease contexts.^1,3

Extending neural fingerprinting approaches to EEG is particularly appealing given its accessibility and frequent use in cognitive and affective neuroscience. However, it remains less well established whether resting-state EEG spectral fingerprints show comparable within-individual stability and, critically, whether they relate to behaviorally meaningful individual differences. In particular, the relationship between resting-state EEG fingerprints and cognitive emotion regulation, such as reappraisal ability, is largely unexplored.^5

Here, we examine sensor-level resting-state EEG spectral fingerprints in a large, fully acquired cohort (N = 121, healthy participants) with extensive behavioral and neuroimaging characterization. Already when analysing a subset of 10 participants, we observe high fingerprint stability, reliable individual differentiation, and systematic associations with cognitive reappraisal performance. Structural MRI was acquired for all participants and used to compute individualized finite-element head models, enabling principled multiscale analyses linking sensor-level fingerprints to source-resolved neural activity.^4

Methods

The data were drawn from a deeply phenotyped adult cohort (N = 121; age range 18–50 years) comprising healthy participants with complete resting-state EEG, cognitive task performance, questionnaire-based phenotyping, and structural MRI. All participants underwent identical acquisition protocols across multiple sessions, enabling consistent integration of electrophysiological, behavioral, and neuroanatomical measures. The present analysis focuses on a subset of 10 participants (N=10) with complete resting-state EEG and behavioral data to establish fingerprint stability and behavioral associations.

EEG was recorded in an electromagnetically shielded booth using a 64-channel BrainVision system (actiCap) with additional horizontal and vertical EOG channels, sampled at 2500 Hz during eyes-closed resting state (~10 min). The protocol comprised two EEG sessions on separate days (each including two cognitive tasks, resting-state recordings, and questionnaires) and a separate MRI session. One EEG task was a cognitive emotion-regulation task. Here the resting-state EEG data as well as the behavioral data of a cognitive emotion regulation task and selected questionnaires are reported. For details about the full experiment see ^4.

Structural MRI (T1- and T2-weighted; 3 T Siemens Trio) was acquired for each participant and used to compute individualized finite-element head models for EEG forward modeling and source reconstruction (for details see ^4).

EEG data were processed in MATLAB/FieldTrip, downsampled to 250 Hz, band-pass filtered (1–40 Hz), and segmented into 60 non-overlapping 10-s epochs. EOG channels were removed, leaving 60 scalp channels.

For each epoch, sensor-level power spectral density (PSD) was estimated using multitaper FFT with a Hanning taper (2–30 Hz, 2-Hz resolution). Channel-wise PSD values (µV²/Hz) were vectorised across channels × frequencies, yielding 900 features per epoch. Subject-level neural fingerprints were computed by averaging epoch-wise PSD feature vectors. Canonical frequency bands were defined as delta (2–4 Hz), theta (4–7 Hz), alpha (8–12 Hz), and beta (13–30 Hz).

Fingerprint stability was assessed using a split-half approach (30 epochs per half). Within-subject fingerprint stability (WFS) was defined as the Pearson correlation between an individual’s two fingerprints, and between-subject fingerprint similarity (BFS) as the mean off-diagonal correlation across individuals.

Emotion regulation was assessed using a cognitive reappraisal task, in which participants either viewed or reappraised neutral or negative IAPS images, followed by trial-wise affect ratings. Such behavioral indices included reappraisal success and ER slope. Health-related measures comprised Life Events (LE) and General Health Questionnaire (GHQ) scores. Associations were assessed using Pearson correlations.

Results

All 10 participants included in the present analysis contributed the complete set of 60 non-overlapping resting-state epochs (10 s each), corresponding to a total analyzed duration of 600 s (10 min) per subject. Resting-state EEG PSD-based neural fingerprints exhibited pronounced individual specificity and high within-individual reproducibility. Within-subject fingerprint stability (WFS), quantified as the Pearson correlation between split-half fingerprints for each individual, was high across participants, with a mean value of r̄ = 0.881. This value substantially exceeded the mean between-subject fingerprint similarity (BFS), defined as the mean off-diagonal correlation across individuals (r̄ = 0.589), indicating that individual fingerprints were more similar to themselves across independent data segments than to those of other participants.

At the individual level, split-half fingerprint correlations ranged from r = 0.676 to r = 0.968, demonstrating consistently reproducible subject-specific spectral power distributions despite the use of independent subsets of data. These results indicate that sensor-level resting-state EEG PSD features capture stable, trait-like characteristics of intrinsic brain activity at the individual level.

Beyond stability and identifiability, EEG fingerprint power metrics showed systematic associations with behavioral indices of cognitive emotion regulation. Global fingerprint power, defined as the mean PSD across all channels and frequencies, exhibited negative correlations with reappraisal success (r = −0.604, p = 0.085) and ER slope (r = −0.637, p = 0.065), suggesting a tendency for individuals with lower overall resting-state spectral power to display more effective cognitive reappraisal.

Band-specific analyses revealed particularly strong associations between ER slope and power in several frequency bands. ER slope was negatively correlated with delta-band power (r = −0.872, p = 0.002), theta-band power (r = −0.773, p = 0.014), and beta-band power (r = −0.827, p = 0.006), indicating that inter-individual differences in emotion-regulation performance were reflected in distinct components of the resting-state spectral profile. In addition, mean reappraisal ratings for negative stimuli showed a strong negative association with alpha-band power (r = −0.824, p = 0.006), linking subjective emotional experience during reappraisal to intrinsic alpha-band dynamics.

In contrast to these behaviorally specific effects, health-related questionnaire measures showed little evidence of association with EEG fingerprint power. General Health Questionnaire (GHQ) scores were not correlated with global fingerprint power (r = 0.045, p = 0.903) and did not show consistent relationships at the band level. Life Events scores exhibited only moderate negative trends with global fingerprint power (r ≈ −0.32), which did not reach statistical significance, and similarly lacked robust band-specific effects. This pattern suggests a dissociation between resting-state EEG fingerprints and broad self-reported mental-health burden, relative to their stronger associations with task-based measures of cognitive emotion regulation. However, this has to be explored further in the larger cohort.

Conclusion

These findings demonstrate that resting-state EEG contains highly stable, individual-specific spectral neural fingerprints that can be extracted from brief recordings acquired alongside complex behavioral experiments. Using a PSD-based, sensor-level representation, we show that within-individual fingerprint stability substantially exceeds between-individual similarity, indicating that intrinsic EEG spectral profiles are robust and reproducible at the level of individual participants. This pattern of stability and individual differentiation is consistent with prior MEG-based fingerprinting work showing that short segments of intrinsic neurophysiological activity carry sufficient information to reliably distinguish individuals.¹,³ Importantly, our results extend these principles to standard EEG recordings, highlighting their potential for scalable fingerprinting approaches.

Beyond stability, the present findings suggest that EEG neural fingerprints are behaviorally meaningful. Fingerprint power metrics were selectively associated with performance-based indices of cognitive emotion regulation, with particularly strong relationships observed in low- and mid-frequency bands. The involvement of delta, theta, alpha, and beta power points to a distributed spectral signature linking intrinsic brain dynamics to the capacity to regulate emotional responses, potentially reflecting trait-like differences in large-scale neural coordination, cognitive control, and affective processing. Notably, these associations were observed for task-derived measures of reappraisal rather than for broad self-reported mental-health burden.

In contrast, weak or absent associations with GHQ scores and only modest trends with cumulative life stress suggest a dissociation between resting-state EEG fingerprints and general distress or symptom load. This pattern implies that EEG spectral fingerprints may preferentially capture neurophysiological traits supporting cognitive control over emotion, rather than global negative affect or self-reported mental-health status. Such specificity aligns with emerging views that intrinsic brain dynamics encode stable regulatory capacities that are not reducible to overall well-being or psychopathology severity.

The presented sensor-level findings positions resting-state EEG neural fingerprints as a powerful link between intrinsic brain dynamics and individual differences in cognitive emotion regulation, bridging scalable electrophysiological measurements with trait-level behavioral variability. This lays the foundation to deeper explore the resting-state EEG data of the cohort (N = 121) with MRI-informed source-reconstructed neural fingerprint.
