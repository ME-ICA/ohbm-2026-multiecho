Introduction

Focal epilepsy is a common neurological disorder characterized by recurrent, spontaneous seizures originating from a pathological region or network. In patients, seizures tend to spread from an epileptogenic zone (EZ) network to a connected propagation zone (PZ) network1. Both EZ and PZ networks display significant differences in structural connectivity to the rest of the brain2. Curiously, inconsistencies emerge when assessing functional connectivity — where stereotactic electroencephalography (SEEG) and 3T fMRI report increased and decreased functional connectivity respectively at EZ and PZ networks3, 4. 

Discrepancies between studies may reflect methodological differences. Previous MRI investigations have primarily focused on patient-level changes without considering spatially-specific alterations relative to healthy controls. To this end, it remains unclear as to how functional connectivity deficits emerge at pathological epilepsy networks. 

Considering this, we assessed changes in functional connectivity using 7T resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) amongst a cohort of drug-resistant focal epilepsy (DRFE) patients and healthy controls — with specific focus on SEEG-defined EZ and PZ network partitions. 

Methods

We retrospectively included 23 DRFE patients from the Multimodal Whole Brain Imaging in Epilepsy (MOBILE) dataset published by the European Human Brain Project and demographically matched 42 healthy controls. Inclusion criteria included patient SEEG evaluation and the availability of presurgical quality controlled 7T anatomical and functional MRI data with no signs of major brain abnormalities. The MOBILE study received approval from the local ethics committee and all participants provided written informed consent.

MRI data acquisition included B1+ and 0.6 mm3 T1 mapping followed by 1.6 mm3 rs-fMRI. Scans were conducted with either a whole-body 7T MAGNETOM or MAGNETOM TERRA system (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) using a 1H 1Tx/32Rx head coil (Nova Medical, Wilmington, USA). All SEEG recordings were performed as part of clinical patient workup and followed national French guidelines.

T1 images were B1+ corrected, skull-stripped and segmented using FreeSurfer. Cortical and subcortical regions were parcellated using the Virtual Epileptic Patient atlas and served as regions-of-interest (ROIs) for subsequent connectivity analyses. rs-fMRI data was preprocessed using fMRIPrep (v20.2.1) followed by an established nuisance regression protocol. 

Regional epileptogenicity index (EI) values were computed from patient SEEG recordings. ROIs that displayed an EI ≥ 0.4 or 0.1 ≤ EI < 0.4 were assigned EZ and PZ network membership respectively. ROIs with an EI < 0.1 are considered to minimally contribute to the spread of epileptiform discharges and were assigned non-involved (NI) status. ROIs not covered by the SEEG evaluation were labeled as non-explored (NE). 

Functional connectivity was calculated as the squared Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r2) between all VEP region pairs (Figure 1A). For each patient, deviations in connectivity relative to controls were quantified using z-scores and adjusted for age, sex and scanner effects (Figure 1B). Median deviation scores were taken to define inter- and intra-epileptic network connectivity (connection types) across pairs of regions. 

Results

Functional connectivity differences between patients and controls were subtle across all connection types (Figure 2A). Nonetheless, z-score analysis revealed significant variability between networks (F9,111.5 = 8.39, p < 0.0001) — with EZ-EZ, EZ-PZ and PZ-PZ connections displaying functional connectivity closer to that of healthy controls (F1,150.3 = 54.51, p < 0.0001), albeit with increased subject-level variability (Figure 2A). 

On average, functional connectivity between the EZ regions of patients were slightly decreased compared to controls (z-score = -0.24, Figure 1A, 2A). Notably, connectivity was consistently higher within and between EZ and PZ network regions when the thalamus was included in one of the two partitions (F1,27.5 = 13.84, p = 0.0009, Figure 2B). Mean SEEG-derived EI values significantly correlated with functional connectivity z-scores (r = -0.31, p < 0.0001, Figure 2C).

Conclusion

The current study provides evidence that functional connectivity alterations in DRFE patients are spatially heterogeneous and modulated by epileptogenicity. Contrary to prior group-level fMRI studies suggesting widespread hypoconnectivity4, we found functional connectivity involving non-epilepsy networks (NI and NE regions) was slightly elevated amongst patients. In line with previous structural MRI analyses2, functional connectivity between SEEG-defined areas of the EZ and PZ network were relatively preserved with the exception of EZ-EZ connection types. Of particular interest, differences in functional connectivity between connection types appear to be at least partially related to thalamus involvement. The potential role of the thalamus in seizure generation and/or propagation may help explain previous descriptions of thalamic (micro)structural alterations related to focal epilepsy5. 

In conclusion, our rs-fMRI study comparing DRFE patients and healthy controls highlights pathology-related functional connectivity modification in SEEG-defined epilepsy networks. Our findings underscore the importance of individualized network-level analyses and support the integration of multimodal data to better understand and eventually target pathological connectivity in focal epilepsy. Future directions could include the investigation of dynamic or higher-order functional metrics to assess the stability of connectivity profiles and provide insights that may prove useful to clinicians.
