Introduction

Empathic pain allows individuals to understand and share others’ suffering, engaging a distributed network that spans sensory, affective, and socio-cognitive systems1. Beyond its social significance, empathic pain is also subject to modulation by cognitive expectations and neurochemical states. Placebo analgesia represents a powerful example of top-down expectation-driven modulation, capable of reshaping how individuals perceive both personal and observed pain2. Meanwhile, oxytocin has been widely implicated in social bonding and affiliative processing, and recent evidence suggests it can further influence empathic responses and pain modulation3. Yet, how placebo expectations and oxytocin jointly alter the directed neural communication underlying empathic pain remains unclear.

Although prior studies have identified core regions involved in empathy for pain—such as the posterior superior temporal sulcus (pSTS), amygdala, insula, and sensorimotor cortices—most research has focused on regional activation or undirected connectivity, overlooking the hierarchical organization and causal flow of information across networks.

Here, we examined how empathic pain pathways reorganize under three conditions: no analgesia, placebo instructed analgesia, and intranasal oxytocin joint instructed analgesia. Using multivariate pattern–based identification of key nodes together with directed connectivity mapping, we constructed condition-specific empathic pain circuits and characterized their hierarchical flow. This framework allowed us to isolate distinct bottom-up and top-down dynamics associated with expectation-driven analgesia and oxytocin-induced socio-affective modulation. Together, we aimed to elucidate how cognitive beliefs and neurochemical influences independently and interactively relieve empathic pain, tracing their directional flow through key pain-processing brain regions.

Methods

We recruited 116 healthy adults assigned to three groups: no-treatment (n=35; 20.40±1.67 years), placebo (n=37; 20.06±1.82 years), and oxytocin (OXT; n=44; 21.64±2.58 years). A double-blind, between-subjects, placebo-controlled neuropharmacological fMRI design was performed in the current study. Before spray administration, participants were explicitly informed that the upcoming nasal spray was a potent analgesic capable of substantially reducing the perception of pain. Participants then self-administered either intranasal OXT (24 IU), placebo spray, or received no spray. All sprays were delivered in six puffs (three per nostril) and administered 45 minutes before scanning (Figure 1A).

To integrate subjective ratings, treatment condition, and analgesic responsiveness, we constructed an analgesia-weighted model. For each participant, raw pain ratings R (1-5 points, 1-not at all, 5-strongest pain) were adjusted using a treatment-specific intercept shift and an analgesia-dependent gain term, producing an individualized score R’. This formulation separates baseline modulation driven by treatment expectations from sensitivity to analgesic efficacy (Figure 1B).

We used partial least squares regression (PLSR) to predict these adjusted scores from whole-brain voxel wise general linear model (GLM) contrast maps4. The PLSR identified latent components capturing maximal covariance between brain activity and behavioral ratings (Figure 1C), yielding condition-specific predictive spatial patterns. To map directed interactions among the key regions derived from PLSR, we applied lagged partial-correlation directionality analysis (LPC-DA), an advanced method for estimating directionality across fMRI time series5 (Figure 1D). For each region of interest (ROI) pair obtained by PLSR , we computed partial correlations between the present signal of one region and the subsequent signal of another while controlling for autoregression. Directionality was assessed by comparing opposite coefficients across participants with paired t-tests and applying Bonferroni correction (q <0.05). Significant asymmetries identified dominant information-flow pathways, allowing reconstruction of no-treatment, placebo, and OXT-specific analgesia circuits associated with empathic-pain.

Results

Across the three intervention conditions, we first observed a clear behavioral effect: both placebo-instructed analgesia and intranasal oxytocin joint instructed analgesia significantly reduced subjective empathic-pain ratings relative to the no-analgesia group, whereas no reliable difference emerged between the placebo and oxytocin groups. This pattern suggests that expectancy alone produces a robust analgesic shift, with oxytocin providing no additional behavioral enhancement beyond this placebo-driven benefit.

At the neural level, the brain appeared to assemble empathic pain through distinct regulatory modes. PLSR (FDR < 0.05) revealed that the no-analgesia group relied on a compact socio-affective network—pSTS, ventral tegmental area (VTA), amygdala, nucleus accumbens (NAcc), insula, and supplementary motor area (SMA)—capturing the brain’s intrinsic bottom-up construction of empathic pain. The placebo-instructed analgesia group showed a modestly expanded signature, introducing early visual input from the calcarine cortex. This suggests that placebo expectations act partly by adjusting perceptual interpretation before affective hubs are engaged. The intranasal oxytocin joint instructed analgesia group recruited the broadest set of contributors, including parahippocampal, occipital (cuneus, calcarine), and motor-related regions (precentral gyrus, midcingulate cortex (MCC)), indicating that oxytocin encourages a more integrative, context-aware mode of modulation (Figure 2A-C Left).

LPC-DA further illustrated these differences in information flow. In the no-analgesia group, empathic pain followed a primarily bottom-up path—from the pSTS through the VTA, amygdala, NAcc, and insula, and ultimately to the SMA—reflecting a natural ascent from perceptual decoding to affective and motor preparation. In contrast, both analgesic conditions displayed hybrid architectures blending bottom-up and top-down influences. Placebo-instructed analgesia began in the calcarine cortex, passed through the pSTS, and then entered the amygdala–NAcc–insula pathway before reaching the VTA and SMA, consistent with expectancy-driven reinterpretation of sensory cues. Oxytocin produced an even more integrated pattern: information originated in the parahippocampus, moved to the calcarine cortex and pSTS, and then dispersed across amygdala, NAcc, cuneus, precentral gyrus, and insula before converging on the VTA, MCC, and SMA, reflecting the incorporation of contextual memory and higher-order control (Figure 2A-C Right).

Conclusion

Our study shows that both placebo-instructed analgesia and oxytocin-supported analgesia relieve empathic pain by reshaping how the brain routes information, rather than simply reducing activity in pain-related regions. While the no-analgesia state relies on a clean bottom-up construction of empathic pain, the two analgesic interventions introduce distinct combinations of top-down and bottom-up influences. Placebo expectations modify early perceptual interpretation before affective systems engage, whereas oxytocin promotes a more integrative mode that incorporates contextual memory, perceptual detail, and motor readiness.
