Introduction

Somatic symptoms, including regional pain, fatigue, and experiences of bodily dissociation, are highly prevalent in the general population. They contribute to functional impairment and mental health problems and often precede severe mental disorders such as depression.¹,² Despite their clinical relevance, studies that monitor somatic symptoms in everyday life using spatially precise, body-region–specific assessment methods remain scarce. Most previous work relied on categorical symptom checklists or global severity ratings, which lack anatomical specificity, temporal resolution, and a close link to everyday bodily experience. As a result, the neuronal networks associated with somatic symptoms as they occur in daily life remain largely unidentified.

To address this gap, we developed Somascape, a smartphone-based application that enables real-time, spatially precise monitoring of somatic symptoms in everyday life using an interactive 3D body avatar. Combined with functional MRI, this approach allows simultaneous investigation of everyday somatic symptom experience and the neural networks supporting these symptoms in otherwise healthy adults.

The heterogeneity of somatic symptoms across body regions (e.g., legs and feet, upper back and chest, abdomen) led us to hypothesize that their neural representation follows somatotopic organizational principles, with distinct connectivity patterns supporting symptoms in different anatomical regions.³ Alternatively, somatic symptoms may be supported by higher-order brain networks involved in cognitive control, affective processing, and interoceptive evaluation. We tested these competing hypotheses by first mapping activation and functional connectivity during a body-focused attention fMRI task and subsequently profiling somatic symptoms in everyday life using the Somascape app. This longitudinal design allowed us to identify neural patterns that predict future region-specific somatic symptoms in daily life.

Methods

Thirty healthy adults (14 female; age range 18–35 years) were recruited from the University of Tübingen, Germany. Exclusion criteria were mental or neurological disorders, active psychopharmacological and strong analgesic medications and treatment, cognitive impairment (Mini-Mental State Examination score <24), and MRI contraindications. 

The study comprised 2 phases to establish temporal precedence of brain measures over symptoms. Phase 1 included two MRI scanning sessions with structural and functional MRI, and Phase 2 8–10 weeks of somatic symptom assessment using the novel App Somascape . During fMRI scanning, participants completed two functional runs of a body-focused attention task, attending to one of altogether five body categories (arms and hands, legs and feet, abdomen, chest and upper back, head and face). For each category, participants recalled bodily memories with negative (injury, pain, discomfort) or positive (pleasant sensation, comfort) valence in different conditions (see Figure 1).

MRI data were acquired at a 3T Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma scanner with a 32-channel head coil. Structural imaging included T1-weighted (1 mm isotrophic) MPRAGE and T2 partial-volume scans perpendicular to the hippocampal formation (04 * 04 *2 mm); functional data (2 mm isotrophic) were acquired using multiband echo-planar imaging. Preprocessing included skull stripping, motion-, slice-timing-, and field-map-correction, coregistration to T1 images, normalization to a DARTEL group template and MNI152 space , and spatial smoothing (4 mm FWHM). Temporal preprocessing comprised band-pass filtering (0.01–0.1 Hz), nuisance regression of motion parameters (24 regressors), white-matter and csf signals, and physiological noise. Motion outliers with framewise displacement > 0.5 mm were modeled as additional regressors. Whole-brain eigenvector centrality mapping (ECM) was computed from task-based fMRI data to identify influential network hubs based on the first eigenvector of the connectivity matrix.

Participants completed 8 weekly symptom assessments over 8-10 weeks using Somascape, localizing bodily symptoms on a 3D avatar and rating intensity and suffering on 0–100 visual analog scales. Symptoms were assigned to five body categories. From this data, 3 composite scores were derived: (i) The Intensity Score quantified cumulative symptom severity by integrating subjective intensity with spatial extent; for each symptom, intensity ratings were combined with the natural logarithm of the number of selections of the corresponding body region, and summed across body regions and assessment rounds, (ii) The Impairment Score captured functional interference using SSD-12 scores from week 8, retrospectively assessing impairment over the preceding 8–10 weeks across cognitive, affective, and behavioral domains, aggregated into regional and global scores, (iii) The Suffering Score quantified subjective distress, based on week-8 ratings of suffering per body region (0–100), summed into regional and global indices(see Figure 1).

Brain–behavior relationships were examined using partial least squares analysis with singular value decomposition (PLS-SVD) to identify latent variables maximizing covariance between brain measures and behavioral profiles. Statistical significance was assessed using permutation testing (500 iterations), and feature reliability using bootstrap resampling (500 iterations), with bootstrap sampling ratios ≥ |1.96| considered reliable. Individual-level predictive validity was evaluated using partial least squares regression with leave-one-out cross-validation, quantifying correspondence between predicted and observed behavioral scores  4(ECM).

Results

83.3% of the tested participants (n = 24) reported at least one somatic symptom during the testing period, whereas 16.7% (n = 5) reported no somatic symptoms in that time (see Figure 2a). The spatial distribution of reported symptoms showed heterogeneous body involvement, with positive symptoms (red) and negative symptoms (blue) distributed across body regions (see Figure 2a).

PLS-SVD identified ten latent variables (LVs) capturing orthogonal dimensions of shared variance between whole-brain EC patterns during body-focused attention and subsequent somatic symptom profiles. Permutation testing (500 iterations) revealed two statistically significant LVs, together explaining 26.6% of total brain–behavior covariance.

The primary LV (LV2) explained 22.6% of covariance and showed robust statistical significance (p < 0.0001) with a strong brain–behavior correlation (r = 0.977) (see Figure 2b–c). Behavioral loadings revealed a clear somatotopic dissociation between lower extremity and visceral symptoms. Leg and feet symptoms loaded consistently positive across symptom dimensions (Suffering BSR = +1.79; Impairment BSR = +1.64), indicating that higher expression of the LV2 brain pattern predicted elevated lower-extremity symptoms over the 8-week period. In contrast, stomach symptoms loaded uniformly negative (Suffering BSR = −1.81; Intensity BSR = −1.75; Impairment BSR = −1.69), indicating reduced visceral symptoms in individuals expressing the same connectivity pattern. Although individual bootstrap ratios did not exceed the |1.96| threshold, the consistent direction of effects across all symptom dimensions within each body category provides convergent evidence for reliable region-specific somatotopic organization.

Cortical topography for LV2 showed negative EC weights in left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and right superior and middle temporal gyri, and positive weights in the left temporoparietal junction, bilateral posterior parietal cortex, posterior cingulate cortex, and precuneus (see Figure 2c). 

A second latent variable (LV7) explained 4.0% of covariance and reached statistical significance (p = 0.046), with a high brain–behavior correlation (r = 0.984) (Figure 2b–c). Two behavioral features showed reliable contributions: reduced upper back and chest impairment (BSR = −2.08) and reduced leg and foot intensity (BSR = −2.02). Negative loadings indicate that higher LV7 expression was associated with lower symptom severity. Brain weights show negative EC in superior temporal sulcus and posterior parietal regions, with positive weights in medial and lateral prefrontal cortices.

PLSR with leave-one-out cross-validation demonstrates strong group-level model fit (training R² = 0.87) but limited individual-level prediction (cross-validated R² = 0.01 ± 0.02), consistent with the high dimensionality of brain data relative to sample size (129,583 voxels; n = 30). Our next analyses will therefore focus on specific regions to uncover regionally precise brain-behavior relationships with lower dimensionality of the fMRI data.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that whole-brain functional connectivity measured during a body-focused fMRI task significantly predicts the regional distribution of somatic symptoms sampled 8-10 weeks later. This is the first time that an fMRI study demonstrates the neuronal signature of somatic symptoms sampled in everyday life in healthy adults. This has critical implications as understanding the signature of somatic symptoms experienced in everyday life allows us to develop novel interventions that aim at reducing associated suffering and impairments of life quality, as well as preventing the development of more severe mental disorders. 

One latent variable that we identified uncovers that individuals with a specific connectivity configuration during a body-focused attention task subsequently report more symptoms in the leg and feet category but fewer abdomen symptoms, while those with opposite patterns show reverse profiles. This somatotopic dissociation extends previous research on cortical body representations to somatic symptom experience that mostly used resting state data only and no sampling of symptoms in everyday life, suggesting brain processing of bodily attention differentially shapes vulnerability to symptoms in specific anatomical regions. 

Specifically, these regions include the TPJ and PPC, whose involvement aligns with their established roles in body representation, bodily self-consciousness, perspective-taking, and multisensory integration critical for constructing multisensory body representations. Prefrontal regions with negative loadings may reflect executive control in directing bodily attention towards or away bodily pain. Superior temporal regions implicated in processing biological motion and inferring bodily states relate to body reflective functioning. The identification of this network has important implications for understanding individual differences in symptom localization, and may inform region-targeted interventions.

Another latent variable links impairment of the upper back and chest and the intensity of leg and feet symptoms to distinct connectivity patterns, suggesting higher pattern expressions may protect against symptoms and index resilience‑conferring neural configurations warranting investigation in larger samples and clinical populations.

In conclusion, brain connectivity during body-focused task demonstrates somatotopic organization prospectively predicting region-specific somatic symptoms. Multivariate analysis, and ecological assessment provide a framework for understanding neural predictors with translational potential for personalized prevention and intervention.
