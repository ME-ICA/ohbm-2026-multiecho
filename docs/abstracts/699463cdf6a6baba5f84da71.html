Introduction

Clinically characterized by a spectrum of neurological syptoms, including confusion, transient cognitive deficits and altered consciousness, the postictal state is defined as the moment ictal activity terminates and lasts until the brain and behavior return to the patient’s baseline.1 Despite the clear clinical definition of this period, its underlying functional network physiology remains poorly understood. This knowledge gap exists primarily due to the severe logistical challenges associated with immediate post-seizure scaning: while resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) has extensively characterized the interictal brain, acquiring imaging data in the acute aftermath of a seizures a challenging scenario. Consequently, very few studies have succesfuly captured this transient phase.2 Here we present one of the first succesful acquisitions of immediate post-seizure rs-fMRI data in a cohort of patients with focal epilepsy.
To analyze this unique dataset, we employed functional connectome (FC) brain fingerprinting—a novel framework that identifies the individual’s connectivity profile,3 further improved to identify the uniqueness of the fingerprint.4 To our knowledge, this is the first study to apply fingerprint metrics to this specific model of epilepsy, aiming to characterize whether the postictal brain collapses into a homogenous pattern of suppression or reorganizes into a distinguishable, identifiable phenotype.

Methods

We studied 14 adult patients with a diagnosis of focal epilepsy, during long-term video-EEG monitoring for presurgical evaluation in two different centers (Fig. 1A) (University Hospital Goethe Frankfurt am Main, Germany; University Hospital Bonn, Germany). Subjects underwent two rs-fMRI 3 T scans: a postictal scan (the most immediate moment after a clinical seizure) and an interictal scan (>36 hours seizure-free). Data were preprocessed with fMRIPrep and projected to MNI space; motion, CSF, WM, global signal and aCompCor regressors were removed and data were band-pass filtered (0.01–0.08 Hz).
BOLD signals were parcellated and extracted from the Schafer-200 cortical atlas. Following Amico and Goñi fingerprinting framework,4 BOLD time series were split into two halves to compute FC (Fig. 1B) by Pearson correlations between parcels, for each scan and for each half separately. Identifiability matrices for both post- and interictal states were computed to extract fingerprint metrics: Iself which measures how stable is the fingerprint across time, Iothers which measures how similar a subject FC is to others, and the difference of them (Idiff) quantifying how identifiable the FC of a subject is. To characterize FC idiosyncrasy, we computed edge-wise intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) (Fig. 1C), set an edge-uniqueness threshold at ICC > 0.6 and grouped edges by 7 canonical networks.
Postictal-interictal differences in fingerprint metrics, mean ICC values within and between each
resting-state network were tested with paired t-tests and FDR correction (α= 0.05). For the analysis
of reliable edge counts, a Fisher’s exact test was used to compare the proportion of high-ICC edges
between the postictal and interictal states.

Results

Fingerprinting analysis showed no differences in the stability of the fingerprint (Iself) between both
states (t-test = 0.21; pFDR = 0.83), but significant difference in Idiff (t-test = 3.16; pFDR = 0.011),
driven by significant lower Iothers (t-test = -4.192; pFDR = 0.003); this indicates that the postictal
FC fingerprint becomes highly distinctive than the interictal state, due to the less similarity of single
subjects to others. ICC analysis exploring edge idiosyncrasy revealed that 40% of edges exceeded the
uniqueness threshold (ICC > 0.6) in the postictal state, compared to only 34% in the interictal state
(Fisher’s exact p < 0.001). This uniqueness spanned multiple networks, both intra- and inter-network
connections. To stand out, higher ICC was observed within visual (pFDR < 0.001) and frontoparietal

networks (pFDR < 0.001), as well as between visual-dorsal attentional (pFDR < 0.001) and visual-
default mode networks (pFDR < 0.001) connections. Node-level, the largest ICC-absolute strength
increases were observed across visual (pFDR < 0.001), dorsal attentional (pFDR < 0.001), default mode (pFDR < 0.001) and frontoparietal (pFDR < 0.001) networks.

Conclusion

Our findings provide a novel physiological characterization of the postictal brain. Contrary to states of deep unconsciousness like anesthesia,5 which drive networks toward homogeneity, we demonstrate that the postictal state becomes significantly more identifiable than the interictal state. This increase in identifiability is driven by a reduction in the similarity to other subjects (Iothers) while the stability of the fingerprint is preserved, resulting in a highly idiosyncratic and patient-specific network configuration. We interpret this increase in reliability as a potential sign of system stabilization following the ictal event, possibly reflecting a transient state of functional rigidity. Further studies are needed to deeply characterize the individual reconfiguration of the brain in the seizure aftermath.
