Introduction

Parkinson’s Disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disease characterized by loss of dopaminergic neurons and the pathological accumulation of misfolded alpha-synuclein in the brain. While tremor, rigidity and bradykinesia are the hallmark symptoms of PD, non-motor symptoms are highly prevalent and can start years before motor symptoms onset and diagnosis.  A highly prevalent non-motor symptom is gastrointestinal dysfunction, which affects up to 80% of PD patients. This, together with the finding of alpha-synuclein accumulation in enteric tissues, makes the interaction between gut and brain a central topic in the study of PD pathogenesis and progression. Gut bacteria are responsible for the regulation of intestinal and immune functions, and they have a role in the production and degradation of neurotransmitters. Patients with PD exhibit alterations in microbiota, often characterized by the decreased abundance of short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing bacteria, such as Faecalibacterium, which have a neuroprotective role.1 Moreover, the burden of PD is often aggravated by cognitive decline and dementia.  Resting state magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) indirectly measures spontaneous neural activity, and it has been widely used to study functional brain alterations in PD. Regional homogeneity (ReHo) measures local brain activity by evaluating the similarity of the time series of a voxel to that of its neighbours. It can differentiate PD from healthy controls, and it correlates with clinical features.2 As the association between cognition, brain function and gut alterations in PD is still understudied, this study aims to investigate the link between gut microbiota, spontaneous neural activity and cognitive performance in PD. 

Methods

The cohort consists of 67 subjects diagnosed with PD (age 72.8 ± 6.7 years, 26 females), recruited at the Neurological Clinic of the Padova University Hospital.

MRI acquisition and processing
MRI data for all participants were acquired with a 3 Tesla Philips Ingenia scanner equipped with a 32 channel phase-array coil. Structural images included T1-weighted and T2-weighted FLAIR, both with voxel size 1 mm3 isotropic. Functional resting state images (68 axial slices, voxel size 2.5 mm3 isotropic, TR=1430 ms, TE=23 ms, FA=50°, A-P phase encoding direction, FOV=240x240x170 mm, pixel bandwidth 271, MB-factor 4, 640 volumes) were acquired with the subject keeping eyes open.

The established pipeline for MRI preprocessing included slice timing correction, despiking, distortion correction, motion correction, masking, PCA-based suppression of signal components from ventricles and white matter, confound regression and high-pass filtering, and spatial registration to the subject’s anatomical T1-w reference. Regions of interest (ROIs) were defined by registering a cortical atlas with 400 parcels, grouped into 17 functional networks3, to each subject native space.

Resting state brain activity was evaluated through ReHo, which is based on Kendall’s coefficient of concordance and it ranges from 0, meaning no agreement, to 1, meaning perfect agreement. It was computed in AFNI with 3dReHo, considering a neighbourhood of 27 voxels, which allows to include face-, edge-, and node- wise neighbours. For each subject, ReHo values were averaged within aforementioned ROIs.

Microbiota data collection and processing
Stool samples were collected from 56 PD subjects and analysed using 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing. The sequencing protocol included DNA extraction, PCR amplification of the V3-V4 hypervariable regions, purification and Illumina Miseq i100 Plus sequencing. The QIIME suite was used to obtain an abundance table of the taxonomic composition of each sample from the raw sequencing files. The preprocessing steps consisted of primer trimming, denoising of raw reads and taxonomic classification of the identified taxa using a Naïve Bayesian Classifier and the GreenGenes2 database. Using the microbiome R package, summary statistics were derived, including the Bulla evenness index, a measure of how evenly species are distributed. It ranges from 0 to 1, with 1 indicating perfect evenness and values closer to 0 indicating dominance by a few species.

Cognitive evaluation
All participants underwent a battery of neuropsychological tests to assess cognitive functions. Individual tests were used to compute a normalized score for each cognitive domain (attention, visuospatial, memory, executive, social cognition, language). Focusing on the attention domain, the included tests were: Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT), Trail Making Test B–A (TMT-BA) and Composite Shifting Index (CSI), obtained from the Verbal Fluency task; while for the visuospatial domain: Benton Judgment of Line Orientation (Benton JLO), and Rey–Osterrieth Complex Figure Copy (RCFC).

Statistical analysis
Statistical analysis was performed in MATLAB 2024b. A multiple linear regression model was built to relate regional ReHo values to microbial summary statistics, the genus Faecalibacterium, and the z-compound of the six cognitive domains. Age, sex, their interaction, medication status, ROI size and the mean jacobian were included in the model as nuisance regressors. The resulting p-values were corrected for multiple comparisons with Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR). For those regions significantly associated with both microbial measures and cognitive z-compounds, the analysis was further extended to the neuropsychological tests included in the definition of that z-compound. Finally, mediation analysis was carried out to assess whether spontaneous brain activity acts as mediator between gut microbiota and cognitive performance, considering sex, age and medication as covariates.

Results

ReHo showed a significant positive relationship with microbial evenness in temporal and occipital areas, with the lowest p-values expressed in the right extrastriate cortex (p-value=1.4e-05) and right temporal occipital cortex (p-value=9.9e-05), which are part of the visual and dorsal attention networks respectively (Figure 1A). A similar pattern of significant positive results was found for Faecalibacterium, which showed the highest association with brain activity in a region of the right temporal occipital cortex (p-value=1.7e-05).

The score for the attentive domain showed significant positive association with ReHo with a more distributed pattern, involving both hemispheres and multiple brain networks, with the lowest p-values expressed in the left superior parietal lobule (p-value=5.1e-05) and in both left and right lateral prefrontal cortices  (p-values 5.7e-05 and 8.3e-05), which belong to the dorsal attention, control and salience ventral attention networks respectively. Similarly, the region mostly related with the z-compound of the visuospatial domain was located in the left superior parietal lobule (p-value=4.2e-05), as shown in Figure 1A.

Microbial measures and cognitive scores highlighted a partially overlapped pattern of significant associations with ReHo in occipital and temporo-parietal  areas. ReHo was significant in relation to both evenness and attention in two temporo-parietal and three visual network ROIs; while evenness and visuospatial z-compound related to ReHo in one temporo-parietal, one dorsal attention and four visual network ROIs. Spontaneous activity in such ROIs was further correlated with the scores of the individual cognitive tests used to compute the attentive and visuospatial z-compounds. As shown in the tables in Figure 1B:

- For the regions that were associated with the attentive z-compound, we obtained significant correlations between ReHo in RH_TempPar_ST_1 and RH_VisPeri_Striate_2 and fluenci CSI; ReHo in RH_VisCent_ExStr_3, RH_VisCent_ExStr_4 and RH_VisPeri_Striate_2 with TMT-BA. 

- Concerning the visuospatial domain we obtained significant associations between ReHo in LH_TempPar_Temp_1 and Benton JLO; ReHo in RH_DorsAttnA_TempOcc_2, RH_VisCent_Striate and RH_VisPeri_Striate_2 and RCFC;  ReHo in LH_VisPeri_Striate_2 and RH_VisCent_ExStr_1 and both tests of the domain.  

In the mediation model for attention performance, the effect of evenness on fluency CSI was mediated by ReHo in RH_VisPeri_Striate_2, while the effect of evenness on TMT-BA was mediated by ReHo in RH_VisCent_3 and _4. Mediation analysis between evenness and the visuospatial domain showed that the effect of gut microbiota on Benton JLO was mediated by ReHo in the region LH_TempPar_Temp_1, while for RCFC the mediator was brain activity in RH_DorsAttnA_TempOcc_2, RH_VisCent_ExStr_1, RH_VisCent_Striate and RH_VisPeri_Striate_2 (Figure 1C). 

Conclusion

We investigated the local synchronization of spontaneous neural activity in Parkinson’s Disease across the whole cortex, and how it relates to peripheral microbial biomarkers and cognitive outcomes. Spontaneous brain activity in temporal and occipital areas, mainly belonging to the visual and attention networks, correlated with one important summary statistic of microbial composition, i.e., microbial evenness, and one key genus known to impact neurotransmitters synthesis and inflammation in several diseases, namely Faecalibacterium. In addition, ReHo was associated in similar regions with cognitive performance in the attentive and visuospatial domains as well. Additionally, ReHo in those common ROIs correlated with the individual cognitive tests employed to compute the z-compounds. While measures of gut bacteria and cognitive scores are not directly related, they are linked through changes in resting state functional brain activity, quantified with ReHo, as proved by mediation analysis.

Microbial evenness is a measure of how evenly the microbial species are distributed, and it can be considered an indicator of ecosystem stability, with higher values associated with more evenly distributed communities. As they are involved in the synthesis and degeneration of neurotransmitters, changes in the abundances of gut bacteria can have an effect on brain function and structure. Faecalibacterium is a genus responsible for the production of butyrate, which is a SCFA involved in the oxygen consumption of epithelial cells, in the protection of intestinal barrier, and in the reduction of blood-brain-barrier permeability.1 ReHo positively correlated with cognitive scores for the attentive and visuospatial domains. The former had a distributed pattern across all functional brain networks, while the latter involved regions mainly located in the central and peripheral visual networks. Cholinergic degeneration may be involved in altered brain activity in occipital areas and its association with cognitive performance, as Positron Emission Tomography (PET) studies showed a reduction in acetylcholinesterase in PD, with a more widespread pattern  of denervation in PD with dementia. The presence of these posterior functional and metabolic alterations are thought to be a risk factor for a worse cognitive outcome and development of dementia. 4 Additionally, Single Photon Emission Computed Tomography (SPECT) studies revealed that PD is characterized by occipital hypoperfusion, which correlates with impaired visual processing.5 ReHo is a measure of local synchronization known to be coupled with cerebral perfusion. Hence, while we recognize as a limitation the unsolved entanglement between spontaneous brain activity and vascular component in the blood oxygenation level dependent (BOLD) signal, we provide new insights on the usefulness of functional MRI as a key tool to better understand the hemodynamic and neural contributions to functional brain alterations in PD, in particular through advanced techniques such as dynamic causal modelling. Future studies should be focused on highlighting eventual links between the temporo-occipital alterations reported here, and subcortical alterations in thalamic and collicular regions, which might be a key factor in visuospatial and attentive impairment in PD. This will deepen the understanding of the role of resting state brain activity in the cross-talk between the microbiome, the gut and the brain.
