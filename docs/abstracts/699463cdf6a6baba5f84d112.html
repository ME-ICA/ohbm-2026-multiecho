Introduction

Mitochondria are central to cellular energy transformation, neuronal signaling, and brain health (Sagan 1967; Lipmann 1941; Lane & Martin 2010). Dysfunction in mitochondrial bioenergetics affects neurotransmission, cognition, ageing, and neuropsychiatric status (Kann & Kovács 2007; Mattson et al. 2008; Holis et al. 2015), yet in vivo characterisation of mitochondrial biology in the human brain remains limited. We recently produced the first spatial atlas of mitochondrial biology in healthy adults (Mosharov et al. 2025), including a neuroimaging-based approach to predict brain-wide mitochondrial content and enzyme activities. The Mitochondrial Stress, Brain Imaging, and Epigenetics (MiSBIE) study provides a unique ground-truth opportunity to test whether multimodal MRI–derived mitochondrial estimates capture age-related variation, genetic mitochondrial defects, stress-induced systemic mitochondrial responses, and cognitive performance across healthy adults and patients with rare mitochondrial disorders.

Methods

Ten healthy participants (males, 36–54 y) matched to a post-mortem reference brain (make, 54y) were used to build backward linear regression models predicting biochemical mitochondrial features (complex I–IV activities, mitochondrial density [MitoD], tissue respiratory capacity [TRC], mitochondrial respiratory capacity [MRC]) from multimodal MRI (diffusion, structural, fMRI). Specifically, predictive features were extracted across complementary MRI modalities, including diffusion microstructure metrics (AD, FA, RD, StreDensity, ICVF, ISOCF, MD, OD), structural tissue markers (T1w, T2w, T1w/T2w, cortical thickness, GM and WM density), and resting-state functional indices (Max_activity, ReHo, entropy, ALFF, fALFF), ensuring broad coverage of microstructural, anatomical, and neurofunctional variance. Models were then applied to 59 controls and 28 patients with either a single large-scale deletion (n=12) or the m.3243A>G point mutation (without MELAS, n=14; with MELAS n=2). See logical flow in Figure 1. We evaluated age effects, group differences (MANCOVA, age-regressed), and whole-brain voxelwise maps. Relationships were assessed with systemic mitochondrial stress biomarker growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15) sampled at nine timepoints before and after mental stress induction, and with cognitive performance reduced into 10 principal components.

Results

Models accounted for 20–35% of voxelwise and 62–79% of regional mitochondrial variance (Figure 1). Predicted MitoD and TRC showed robust age-related decline (P < .001), whereas MRC remained stable. These age effects exceeded those observed in any individual MRI modality, indicating that mitochondrial phenotypes reflect biologically integrated information. Patients with single large-scale deletions showed significantly higher CII, MitoD, and TRC across grey, white, and mixed tissues, whereas MELAS patients showed lower CII and trends toward reduced MitoD. Age-related effects localised primarily to the orbitofrontal cortex, insula, basal ganglia, and VWFA, with relative preservation of major white-matter tracts. Disease effects partially overlapped, particularly in the orbitofrontal cortex, putamen, optic radiations, and the motor hand-knob region. Systemically, elevated plasma GDF15, particularly during mental stress, was strongly associated with increased MitoD and CII and reduced MRC, consistent with expected changes in cells deficient in mitochondrial protein translation. Cognitively, mitochondrial indices correlated with PC8 (accuracy & error monitoring): lower MitoD and higher MRC were each associated with better performance. Results are summarised in Figure 2.

Conclusion

Multimodal neuroimaging–derived mitochondrial estimates capture biologically relevant variation across ageing, genetic mitochondrial pathology, systemic stress responses, and cognition. Spatially specific alterations in mitochondrial features, particularly in orbitofrontal, basal ganglia, and visuomotor territories, suggest that mitochondrial energy transformation defects influence distributed neural systems, rather than global tissue properties. Associations with the validated energetic stress marker GDF15 provide additional evidence that predicted brain mitochondrial features are sensitive to systemic mitochondrial stress. Cognitive correlations also support functional relevance for neuroenergetic efficiency. Together, these findings establish MitoBrainMap-based mitochondrial neuroimaging as a promising, non-invasive tool for mapping human bioenergetics, characterising rare mitochondrial disorders, and linking cellular metabolism to cognition.
