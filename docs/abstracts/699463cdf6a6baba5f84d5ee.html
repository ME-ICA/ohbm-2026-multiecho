Introduction

During an event that results in a mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI), sudden acceleration and deceleration of the skull causes the brain to rotate and stretch, resulting in shear strain injury and biomechanical deformation1. These forces concentrate at the base of the brain where the cerebrum connects to the spinal cord, marking an apex where nearby white matter and deep midline structures are particularly vulnerable to injury within mTBI2. Hypothalamic and brainstem structures are two such areas positioned at this apex. However, neuroimaging research investigating mTBI-related volumetric changes within these areas is sparse primarily due to difficulty robustly imaging and segmenting the small subnuclei of both structures. Recent advancements in neuroimaging have created an avenue to more precisely quantify volume changes of small deep subcortical structures3,4. We therefore assess whether there are differences in hypothalamic and brainstem subnuclei volumes between mTBI patients and a group of trauma control patients using robust automated segmentation tools.  

Methods

46 mTBI patients and 32 trauma control patients aged 18-60 were recruited as inpatients in Melbourne, Australia. All recruited participants had recently suffered accidental bodily injury (e.g. motor vehicle accident, workplace injury, falls, etc) and were classified into the mTBI group if they had also sustained a head strike and fulfilled the World Health Organisation criteria for definition of mTBI. Participants who sustained their injury in the absence of a head strike were recruited into the TC group. Participants completed an MRI neuroimaging protocol that included collection of T1-weighted scans (MPRAGE) at 6 - 10 weeks following injury. Lateralised hypothalamic subnuclei volumes were derived using a Bayesian sMRI algorithm3 (see Figure 1a), while select brainstem nuclei volumes were derived using a Bayesian algorithm that uses a probabilstic atlas generated from ex vivo brain specimans 4 (see Figure 1b). To investigate volume differences between groups Bayesian linear regressions were fitted, with age, sex, estimated total intracranial volume, and time since injury as covariates.  

Results

Results from the Bayesian linear regressions provided anecdotal evidence to support increased volume for 4 brainstem nuclei for mTBI patients compared to TC. These subnuclei were the left parabrachial complex (BF = 1.243), left and right pontis oralis (left: BF = 1.239, right: BF = 1.539), and median raphe nuclei (BF = 1.18). Otherwise, no evidence for group difference was found for 6 out of the 16 brainstem nuclei (BF =1). Anecdotal level evidence for the null hypothesis was found for 4 out of 16 brain stem nuclei (BF = 1/3 - 1) and substantial evidence for the null hypothesis was found for two brainstem nuclei, specifically, the left and right laterodorsal tegmental area (BF = 0.208 and 0.259 respectively). No evidence of group differences was found for whole hypothalamic volumes and 3 out of 10 hypothalamic subregions (BF = 1). For the remaining 7 out of 10 hypothalamic subregions anecdotal level evidence supporting the null hypothesis was found (BF = 1/3 - 1). 

Conclusion

The findings from this study demonstrate that at approximately 2 months following a mTBI incident, volume in the hypothalamus and brain stem are essentially normal, aside from anecdotal level evidence suggesting increased volume in the left parabrachial complex, left and right pontis oralis and median raphae nuclei of the brain stem. These areas are particularly involved in arousal, initiation, and sleep regulation. Future work is aimed at investigating whether there are atypical hypothalamic and brainstem structural connectivity profiles within mTBI.
