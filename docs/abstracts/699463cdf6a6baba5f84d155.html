Introduction

Relapsing–Remitting Multiple Sclerosis (RRMS) is a chronic neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative disorder affecting large-scale neural systems through progressive demyelination and grey- and white-matter loss. Cortical and deep subcortical structures, particularly the thalamus, show consistent atrophy across MS subtypes^1–3, with thalamic degeneration detectable even in early or preclinical stages. However, grey matter alterations are also often evidenced beyond the thalamus in MS patients, but substantial interindividual variability may challenge the identification of these alterations. Actually, these individual structural alterations can hardly be captured by the traditional case-control group comparison approach. 

Normative modelling^4 here offers a solution by estimating individualized deviation maps relative to a demographically matched healthy reference population. Deep generative approaches such as conditional variational autoencoders (CVAEs)^5 extend region-wise regression models^4, 6 by learning the full multivariate distribution of brain structure and capturing nonlinear, covariate-dependent relationships using large multisite datasets. Accordingly, in RRMS, such models may enable the detection of subtle, subject-level anatomical abnormalities and can provide biomarkers linked to disease expression and progression. Building on prior work in normative modelling^4, 6, we trained a large-scale deep normative model on 8,814 healthy individuals to generate reference distributions for 434 cortical and subcortical grey-matter regions. Applied to a longitudinal RRMS cohort and matched controls, this framework allows us to quantify individual abnormality burden, to identify spatially vulnerable regions, and to examine links between structural deviations, disability, and disease course.

Methods

We developed a deep normative modelling framework using a CVAE trained on a large healthy reference cohort (N = 8,814) drawn from major imaging initiatives including UK Biobank, HCP (Development, Young Adult, Aging), OASIS, and ADNI. All T1-weighted MRI scans were processed with a standardised pipeline yielding 434 cortical and subcortical grey-matter volumes. Age, sex/gender, and dataset were incorporated as one-hot covariates in both the CVAE encoder and decoder, implemented with fully connected layers, batch normalisation, and Exponential Linear Unit (ELU) activations to ensure stability across multisite data.

Model development used a stratified 80/20 train–holdout split and 5-fold cross-validation. All data splits were stratified with respect to age, sex, and dataset. Across folds, predictions showed high fidelity (mean r = 0.9250 ± 0.0028; mean loss = 0.0389 ± 0.0012).

The clinical sample comprised 200 RRMS patients and 50 matched healthy controls, each scanned at baseline and one-year follow-up (12.7 ± 1.6 months). Clinical measures included structural MRI, Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) scores, and disease duration. After training, the CVAE generated normative predictions for each participant, and regional Z-scores were computed using observed minus predicted values scaled by the regional model error estimated from the healthy holdout set.

A global abnormality burden metric was defined as the number of regions showing extreme negative deviation (Z < −1.96), indexing widespread grey-matter reduction. Spatial patterns of abnormality were summarised as prevalence maps showing the proportion of RRMS participants with extreme deviations at baseline and follow-up.

Group differences between RRMS and controls in extreme deviations were tested with negative binomial regression. Associations between abnormality burden, EDSS, and disease duration were evaluated using linear regression, and longitudinal clinical relevance was assessed with linear mixed-effects models incorporating random intercepts. Together, these analyses enabled comprehensive quantification of individualised neuroanatomical deviations in RRMS.

Results

The CVAE demonstrated strong predictive performance on matched healthy controls drawn from previously unseen centres, indicating excellent model generalisability. For these controls, prediction performance reached mean Pearson correlation of r = 0.9313 with a total loss of 0.0376, showing that the learned normative manifold transfers robustly across acquisition sites and reliably reconstructs healthy brain structure.

Using this normative model, individual deviation maps in RRMS revealed a stable spatial pattern of structural abnormalities (Figure 2-A). The thalami showed the highest prevalence of significant negative deviations (24–25% of patients), followed by the putamen, caudate, hippocampal tail, and amygdala (>10%). RRMS participants consistently demonstrated higher abnormality burden than matched controls (Figure 2-B). At baseline, negative binomial regression showed that RRMS participants had 58% more significant deviations than controls (Rate Ratio = 1.58, 95% CI 1.12–2.23, p = 0.009; ~6 vs. ~4 deviations), increasing to 69% at follow-up (Rate Ratio = 1.69, 95% CI 1.20–2.39, p = 0.003).

Cross-sectional analyses indicated that greater abnormality burden was associated with worse baseline disability, with each additional deviation linked to a 0.073-point increase in EDSS. Abnormality burden also correlated with disease duration, with each significant negative deviation corresponding to ~1.7 additional years, suggesting that cumulative structural damage aligns with long-term disease progression.

Longitudinal mixed-effects analyses showed no independent effect of abnormality burden on one-year EDSS change, although estimates were directionally consistent with a modest contribution to later disability. In contrast, abnormality burden showed a small but significant association with longitudinal disease duration trajectories. Together, these findings highlight that the deep normative CVAE yields a stable and clinically informative deviation signature in RRMS, reliably distinguishing patients from controls and capturing meaningful variation related to disability and disease course.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that deep normative modelling with a large-scale CVAE provides a sensitive and clinically informative approach for identifying individualized grey-matter abnormalities in RRMS. Deviation maps revealed a reproducible phenotype centred on subcortical structures—especially the thalami, basal ganglia, and medial temporal regions—highlighting subtle abnormalities in areas such as the hippocampus that traditional case–control analyses often miss due to low or heterogeneous effects^7. This discrepancy suggests that conventional group-level approaches may overlook subtle yet clinically relevant structural abnormalities due to low effect sizes or inconsistent expression across individuals. In contrast, our individualized deviation maps demonstrate sensitivity to these finer-grained alterations, offering a more comprehensive characterisation of MS-related neuroanatomical disruption and its relationship to behavioural phenotype. These findings align with meta-analytic and volumetric MRI evidence^1-3, 8 while offering a finer-grained characterization of MS-related neuroanatomical disruption.

The abnormality burden score showed significant associations with disability and disease duration, extending prior work using regression-based normative models^6. Unlike region-wise linear approaches, our generative framework models the full multivariate and nonlinear structure across 434 regions, producing anatomically coherent and stable patient-level phenotypes. This richer representation improves sensitivity to subtle, spatially coordinated abnormalities that may be overlooked by independent regression models.

Deep normative modelling thus complements and advances existing approaches for brain-abnormality profiling in MS. Given clinical consensus that regional brain atrophy is a prognostic marker^9, our whole-brain abnormality metric provides a concise, individualized indicator of neurodegenerative burden with relevance for both cross-sectional and longitudinal assessment. Although not predictive of short-term EDSS change, its consistent associations with baseline disability and disease course support its potential as a marker of cumulative pathology.

Overall, these results position deep generative normative modelling as a scalable, biologically grounded framework for individualized MS characterization, with applications in early detection, patient stratification, and longitudinal monitoring. Future work incorporating multimodal imaging and additional covariates may further enhance its precision and prognostic value.
