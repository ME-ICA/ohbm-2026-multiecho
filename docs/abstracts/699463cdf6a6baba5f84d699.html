Introduction

Spatial attention exhibits systematic asymmetries that reflect the organization of the cortical attention network. In adults, a leftward bias—often termed pseudoneglect—has been linked to dominant engagement of the right frontoparietal system 1. However, the developmental origins of this asymmetry remain unclear. Previous studies suggest that children do not consistently show adult-like pseudoneglect and may even demonstrate weak or reversed rightward preferences, pointing to a potential reorganization of hemispheric contributions during maturation 2.

Converging evidence shows that preparatory alpha-band modulation (8–12 Hz) provides an early signature of spatial biasing: reductions over one hemisphere signal the release of inhibition and a shift of attentional resources to the contralateral field. Because alpha lateralization mirrors the functional asymmetry of attentional circuits, it offers a direct readout of the developing systems that support pseudoneglect 3. Prior work further suggests that the strength and direction of this preparatory alpha bias predict subsequent attentional selection, linking pre-stimulus dynamics to downstream performance 4. During stimulus processing, this hemispheric organization is echoed in the posterior contralateral N2 (N2pc), which indexes the efficiency of target selection and distractor suppression—operations that strengthen with age as frontoparietal networks consolidate 5.

Given the protracted maturation of right-lateralized attention systems, children may rely on a different hemispheric balance than adults (Fig. 1A). Direct electrophysiological comparisons remain limited, leaving unclear how preparatory alpha and stimulus-evoked dynamics shape emerging spatial biases. Here, we use electroencephalography (EEG) in children and adults performing the same visual search task, quantifying pre-stimulus alpha lateralization and N2pc to determine whether the adult left-lower bias is already present in childhood or emerges later.

Methods

Fifty-one adults from Beijing Normal University and 76 children from local schools participated as paid volunteers. All were right-handed, had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, reported no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Ethics Review Board of Beijing Normal University, with written informed consent obtained from all adults, children, and their parents. Six adults and nine children were excluded due to excessive artifacts, leaving 45 adults (19 females; age range = 19–27 years, mean = 23.00±1.91 years) and 67 children (24 females; age range = 7–13 years, mean = 10.59±1.65 years) for analysis.

Stimuli were presented in a visual search array on a 21-inch gamma-linearized CRT monitor (1920 × 1080 pixels, 60 Hz) with a central fixation cross on a black background. Each array contained 12 items: one circle target and 11 diamond distractors (1.7° × 1.7°, 50% contrast, mean luminance 13.5 cd/m²). Targets appeared equally often in the left (8 or 10 o’clock) or right (2 or 4 o’clock) visual field. Participants maintained fixation and indicated the target location. Trials began with a fixation period of 900–1100 ms, followed by 200 ms stimulus presentation and a blank screen up to 3,000 ms (Fig. 1B). The experiment included eight blocks of 30 trials, and practice blocks ensured ≥85% accuracy.

EEG was recorded continuously from 128 sites using a HydroCel Geodesic Sensor Net (0.01–400 Hz, 1,000 Hz sampling, online Cz reference, impedances <50 kΩ), downsampled to 250 Hz, and processed EEGLAB Toolbox. Signals were band-pass filtered (1–40 Hz), re-referenced to averaged mastoids, and segmented from –600 to 800 ms relative to stimulus onset. Trials with behavioral errors, RT outliers, or artifacts were excluded; ocular artifacts were corrected via independent component analysis (ICA). Following ICA, segments with horizontal eye movements exceeding ±50 μV at F9/10 (200–400 ms) or blink/eye-closure deviations exceeding ±70 μV at F1/2 (0–200 ms) were additionally rejected. Pre-stimulus alpha activity (8–12 Hz) was analyzed from –400 to 0 ms using Morlet wavelet decomposition (3 cycles; 1-Hz steps; 10-ms resolution) in the FieldTrip toolbox.

Neurophysiological asymmetry indices (AI) were quantified using EEG measures. Pre-target alpha lateralization was computed over posterior electrodes (PO7/PO8, PO3/PO4, O1/O2, 66/84,71/76) as the normalized difference between right- and left-hemisphere alpha power, with negative and positive values indicating greater left- and right-hemisphere alpha activity, respectively. The N2pc component was derived by subtracting ipsilateral from contralateral activity at posterior electrodes (P7/P8, PO7/PO8, 58/96, 66/84) relative to target location. For each condition, N2pc amplitude was defined as the mean voltage within a ±10 ms window centered on the peak in the 200–300 ms interval, and latency was defined as the time point of this peak. N2pc AI for both amplitude and latency were calculated as the normalized difference between left- and right-target N2pc, such that positive values indicate smaller contralateral negativity or earlier peaks for right-field targets. Behavioral AI were quantified using the standard deviation of reaction times (RTSD) for targets in the left versus right visual field, normalized by their mean. Negative values indicate more consistent responses to left-field targets, whereas positive values indicate the opposite. (Fig. 1C).

Statistical analyses were conducted in JASP. One-sample t tests assessed whether AI values differed from zero. Mediation analyses examined whether N2pc AI mediated the relationship between pre-target alpha AI and RTSD AI. Standard mediation effects, including direct (c’), indirect (a×b), and total effects (c), were estimated using 5,000 bootstrap samples to obtain 95% confidence interval (CI). The significance level was set at 0.05.

Results

At the behavioral level, spatial asymmetries showed a clear age- and visual-field–dependent dissociation. In the upper visual field, children exhibited a significant positive RTSD AI (t(66) = 2.283, p =0.026), indicating more consistent responses to right-field targets, whereas adults showed no reliable lateralized bias (t(44) = 1.480, p =0.246). In contrast, in the lower visual field, adults exhibited a significant negative RTSD AI (t(44) = –2.437, p =0.019), reflecting more consistent responses to left-field targets, in line with the classic pseudoneglect pattern, while children did not show a comparable asymmetry (t(66) = –0.397, p =0.693) (Fig. 2A). These findings indicate that the adult-like leftward bias emerges selectively in the lower visual field. The absence of such a bias in children, together with a rightward consistency advantage in the upper visual field, suggests that spatial attention in childhood relies on less specialized or alternative control mechanisms.

To link behavior with neural mechanisms, participants were grouped according to the visual-field location of their behavioral bias: adults with a left lower-field (LLF) advantage (n = 25) and children with a right upper-field (RUF) advantage (n = 39). These subgroups were then used to examine corresponding neurophysiological differences.

Pre-target alpha lateralization, indexing preparatory attentional states, revealed a clear dissociation aligned with these behaviorally defined spatial biases. Among adults showing a left lower visual field bias, the pre-target alpha AI was significantly negative (t(24) = –2.164, p =0.041), consistent with dominant right-hemisphere engagement during spatial attention preparation. In contrast, among children showing a right upper visual field bias, the pre-target alpha AI was significantly positive (t(38) = 2.283, p =0.026), indicating a reversed hemispheric bias at the preparatory stage (Fig. 2B). This pattern suggests that the neural architecture supporting preparatory spatial attention differs qualitatively across development.

We next examined target-evoked N2pc during visual search. In adults exhibiting a left lower visual field bias, targets presented in the left lower visual field elicited a negative N2pc latency AI (t(24) = –2.988, p =0.006), indicating more efficient attentional selection for behaviorally preferred left-field locations. In contrast, in children exhibiting a right upper visual field bias, targets presented in the right upper visual field elicited a negative N2pc amplitude AI than targets in the left upper visual field (t(38) = –2.910, p =0.006), suggesting enhanced attentional engagement rather than accelerated selection to right-field targets (Fig. 2C).

Next, we conducted mediation analyses linking pre-target alpha, N2pc, and behavioral asymmetries. In adults, more negative pre-target alpha AI predicted more negative N2pc latency AI and RTSD AI, indicating that stronger right-hemisphere preparatory attention facilitated faster target selection and more consistent responses for left-field targets (indirect effect = 0.098, p = 0.027, 95% CI = [0.010, 0.267]; direct effect = −0.10, p = 0.203, 95% CI = [–0.094, 0.487]; total effect = −0.10, p = 0.033, 95% CI = [0.223, 0.572]). In children, more positive pre-target alpha AI predicted more negative N2pc amplitude AI and more positive RTSD AI, suggesting that left-hemisphere–biased preparatory attention enhanced resource allocation during target processing and supported more consistent responses to right-field targets (indirect effect = 0.080, p = 0.030, 95% CI = [0.006, 0.193]; direct effect = 0.189, p = 0.131, 95% CI = [–0.058, 0.432]; total effect = 0.268, p = 0.022, 95% CI = [0.223, 0.572]) (Fig. 2D).

Conclusion

Our results reveal a clear developmental shift in spatial attention: adults show the expected left-lower visual field bias, reflecting greater behavioral consistency and faster target selection for left-field targets, accompanied by right-hemisphere–dominant preparatory alpha activity and earlier N2pc latency. In contrast, children exhibit a pronounced right-upper visual field tendency, characterized by more consistent responses to right-field targets, left-hemisphere–biased pre-target alpha, and larger N2pc amplitudes, indicating enhanced attentional engagement rather than faster selection. These findings demonstrate that lateralized spatial attention is neither uniform across the visual field nor fully mature in childhood. Instead, they highlight a developmental reorganization of hemispheric asymmetry, in which the adult-like right-hemisphere dominance supporting pseudoneglect emerges gradually and region-specifically, while children rely on alternative, left-hemisphere–biased attentional strategies. Overall, this study provides novel insight into the maturation of neural mechanisms underlying spatial attention and reveals how hemispheric specialization develops across childhood to adulthood.
