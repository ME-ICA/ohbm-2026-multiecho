Introduction

The 11.7T human MRI Iseult system represents the culmination of more than two decades of development in ultra-high-field (UHF) MRI. This system produced its first anatomical images (humans and non-human primates) between 2023 and 2025, marking a major milestone for MRI. The scientific motivation behind Iseult aligns with the broader pursuit of UHF in the MRI community, especially driven by functional MRI (fMRI) and magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS). Functional MRI relies on detecting blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) signal changes of a few percent order of magnitude. Detecting such subtle signal changes becomes increasingly challenging as voxel size decreases (spatial resolution increases) and signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) decreases (due to higher spatial or temporal resolution). UHF systems leverage the promise of an improved signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR), temporal SNR, and functional CNR to compensate for these challenges. Furthermore, UHF enhances BOLD specificity by allowing higher spatial resolution with minimal impact on sensitivity and by boosting extravascular relative to intravascular signal components. However, these benefits come with significant technical challenges. The greater sensitivity to motion requires robust motion correction strategies. B0 inhomogeneities becomes more noticeable, thus specfic shimming protocols need to be implemented. More prominent B1+ inhomogeneties and higher energy deposition (SAR) can be addresse with better RF pulse designs and parallel transmission (pTx). Here, we present the first task-based fMRI results obtained with the Iseult scanner using a well-validated short functional localizer that tests different cognitive domains1, as a proof-of-concept to demonstrate the feasibility and reliability of functional imaging in humans at 11.7T. We used a 3D-EPI sequence that implements prospective motion correction based on servo-navigation2,3 and an 8Rx-31Tx coil.

Methods

Anatomical and functional data acquisition: Data were collected from four healthy volunteers who provided their written and informed consent. The AROMA 8Tx31Rx head coil4 was used, alongside on-the-fly, participant-specific, tailored RF pulses with parallel transmission. Task-based data were acquired using whole-brain T2*‑weighted 3D-EPI sequences with the following parameters: 1.2mm³ spatial resolution, TR=2.12s, TE=19ms, and iPAT=2x3.
A slightly modified version of the functional localizer used in1, in which auditory stimuli were replaced by visual equivalents due to the lack of 11.7T compatible audio equipment, was employed (Figure 1-a). The localizer paradigm run was ~5 minutes, comprising 100 events, including 20 rest periods. The remaining events comprised horizontal checkerboard stimuli (10), vertical checkerboard stimuli (10), mental calculation trials (20), language comprehension trials involving sentence reading (20), and motor trials corresponding to left (10) and right (10) button presses. Orbital servo navigators2 (designated as SNAV in the figures) were incorporated into the 3D-EPI sequences for prospective motion correction. Offline phase stabilization was also performed also using PEERS3. AP and PA phase-encoding acquisitions were collected for distortion correction. A T1‑weighted MP2RAGE anatomical reference was acquired (0.8mm³ resolution, acquisition time=9 minutes). It included FatNav-based motion correction.
Preprocessing: First, tissue segmentation and cortical surface reconstruction of the T1‑weighted scan was performed prior to fMRIPrep5 and after generating a denoised and bias corrected T1‑weighted image using N4BiasFieldCorrection and SynthSeg. The resulting outputs were then fed, along with the bold functional data to a conventional fMRIPrep pipeline including TOPUP distortion and motion correction, anatomo-functional alignment (in the native T1-weighted and MNI space).
Statistical analysis: First-level general linear model (GLM) at the subject level was used to analyse the functional MRI data. It included six task-related regressors, six motion regressors, cosine drift terms (high-pass at 0.01Hz) and a baseline regressor (Figure 1-c). The motion regressors were derived from the servo navigation motion estimates: Since servo navigation provides prospective motion correction, the residual motion captured by fMRIPrep was minimal as observed in Figure 1-b. After z-scoring the size effect maps, activation maps were obtained by thresholding the z-score maps for false positive rate (FPR) at p<0.001. Voxel-wise tSNR maps (computed as the voxel-wise ratio of the mean by the standard-deviation) were generated from the fMRIPrep preprocessed BOLD data after removal of task-related and drift components through regression.
Evaluation: We examined the impact of the servo navigation on residual motion estimates. Temporal SNR was evaluated through its distribution histograms. Task activations for motor and math tasks, and %BOLD changes in motor ROIs were used to assess signal stability, and activation reliability for an example of motor task and higher order cognitive task.

Results

Figure 1-a shows that with servo nagivation and PEERS corrections the residual head motion captured by fMRIPrep is significantly reduced. According to our data, servo navigation effectively counterbalances head motion of at least ~0.5 mm translation (sub-04).

Figure 2-a shows the subject-wise tSNR histograms in the native T1-weighted and MNI spaces.
The tSNR distributions show an average tSNR of around 40-50 with sub-01 having a higher tSNR than the other participants. This participant also moved the least. The results are consistent in the T1-weighted and native spaces.


Figure 2-b(left) shows the percent of BOLD signal change for the motor task based on 10 click-left and 10 click-right trials for sub-01 and sub-02. The %BOLD change shown is the mean computed over an ROI defined as the activated voxels by either click-right (z > 3.21) or click-left (z < -3.21), as shown in the thresholded activation maps in Figure 2-b(right).
Figure 2-b show approximately 5% BOLD change for the motor task. When comparing the mean percent bold change and onsets of the trials indicated by the red and blue vertical lines (respectively for right-click and left-click), this signal appears clean, and the %BOLD peaks align well with the trials. This is evident when two trials of the same type occur close to each other, allowing the observation of two peaks separately (e.g., light red and light blue arrows). When the time gap is too small, only one large peak is observed because the temporal resolution is insufficient (e.g., dark red and dark blue arrows).

Figure 2-c displays the subject-wise activation maps related to the math trials. The coordinates displayed for the activation maps (motor and math-related) represent the same coordinates in the MNI space. These coordinates were then projected back into each subject's native space to make them comparable between volunteers, while remaining on the subject level in the native T1-weighted space. The colobar is the same for the motor and math-related activation maps. Figure 2-b(right) and c show that the activation maps are spatially specific: The shape of the activations closely follows the cortical surface. Remarkably, the regions activated across subjects for the math task are highly consistent, demonstrating strong reliability, especially considering that these activations are derived from only 20 trials.

Conclusion

With robust motion correction strategies, task-based fMRI is reliable at 11.7T. Our experimental design enabled spatially specific and reproducible motor and math activations aligned with cortical anatomy. These results open the way for future studies where the spatio-temporal resolution would be pushed further.
