Introduction

The language pathways of the brain play a critical role in speech and language development in children, especially at a young age. Many studies have evaluated language-related white matter properties to predict language outcomes from early infancy to adolescence. Higher Fractional Anisotropy (FA) and lower Mean Diffusivity (MD) typically reflect more mature white matter organization and are related to enhanced language skills. However, associations vary across functions, tracts, age groups, and populations. The key white matter tracts involved in language development include: The Arcuate Fasciculus (AF), which supports comprehension, naming, language production; the Superior Longitudinal Fasciculus (SLF), involved in phonological processing and language production; the Inferior Longitudinal Fasciculus (ILF), which contributes to reading; the Inferior Fronto-Occipital Fasciculus (IFOF), important for semantic–lexical processing and retrieval; and the Uncinate Fasciculus (UF), which supports lexical retrieval. Despite extensive research linking microstructural properties of these pathways to language abilities, their developmental trajectories are not fully defined. This study aimed to characterize the maturation of these language-related pathways from birth to adolescence in a cross-sectional cohort of typically developing children. The first objective was to examine age-related differences in mean FA and MD across the AF, SLF, ILF, IFOF, and UF. We hypothesized that FA would increase and MD would decrease with age in all tracts, with the most rapid changes expected during infancy. The second objective was to compare mean FA and MD across five age groups to identify potential tract-specific or age-specific differences in developmental patterns. Lastly, we established normative values for FA and MD across these pathways, providing reference standards to guide future experimental research on language development in both normally and abnormally developing children.  

Methods

Participants: 
Participants were drawn from a cohort previously described by Bruckert et al. (2019)1. Infants, children, and adolescents ages 1 day to 17 years who underwent MRI at Lucile Packard Children’s Hospital (Stanford) between 2010 and 2017 were included (n = 1,142). Patients were excluded for brain abnormalities (tumor, hemorrhage, stroke, malformations, hydrocephalus, prior injury, or nonspecific white matter changes; n = 866), clinical history affecting development (prematurity, hearing loss, motor or language delays, neurodevelopmental, genetic, endocrine, neurological, or psychiatric disorders; n = 144), or technical issues in MRI acquisition (n = 19). Participants (n=113) were divided into five age groups of comparable size: infants (0–1 years, n = 25), preschoolers (2–4 years, n = 23), young school-age (5–8 years, n = 19), middle school- age (9–12 years, n = 24), and adolescents (13–17 years, n = 22). 

MRI Acquisition and Preprocessing: 
MRI scans were acquired at 3T GE Discovery MR750 using an 8-channel head coil. High-resolution T1-weighted images (3D SPGR, voxel size 0.4688 × 0.4688 × 1 mm³) and diffusion-weighted images (DTI-EPI, voxel size 0.9375 × 0.9375 × 3 mm³, b = 1,000 s/mm², 25 directions) were collected as part of standard clinical pediatric brain MRI protocol. 

Data were preprocessed using mrDiffusion in MATLAB, including motion correction, registration to T1, resampling to 2 × 2 × 2 mm³ voxels, and adjustment of diffusion gradients. FA and MD maps were computed using least squares fitting. Relative head motion was assessed; five participants were excluded for excessive motion.

White Matter Tract Identification: 
 Tracts were segmented using Automated Fiber Quantification (AFQ, PyAFQ). Deterministic whole-brain tractography was generated, and major pathways were identified using template-based ROIs. Infant-specific templates were used for participants 0–23 months. Tracts were refined, cleaned, resampled to 100 nodes, and FA/MD metrics extracted. Participants with failed tract segmentation were excluded for specific tracts: left AF (n = 2), right AF (n = 2), left IFOF (n = 7), right IFOF (n = 7), left ILF (n = 2), right ILF (n = 1). All were infants.

Statistical Analysis: 
Normality was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test. FA and MD distributions were non-normal, so the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used for group, gender, and hemisphere comparisons.

The relation between age and white matter metrics (FA and MD) was modeled using a logarithmic function of the form y = b₀ + b₁·ln(x). In this model, y represents the estimated FA or MD value, x is age in years, b₀ is the constant term, and b₁ is the regression coefficient for the log-transformed age. For each regression model, regression coefficients, R², and 95% confidence intervals of the means were estimated. 

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to test the effects of sex and age group on mean FA and mean MD within each tract. Non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis to assess differences in white matter metrics across tracts. Effect size was calculated using eta squared (η²). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction to identify which tracts differed significantly. 

The Levene test was performed to assess the homogeneity of variances. Pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Games-Howell post hoc test.

Results

Mean FA and MD values across the sample 

Across all ten language-related tracts, mean FA increased and mean MD decreased with age. No significant differences were found between female and male participants in either mean FA or mean MD values in any tracts across ages. Some combinations of age groups and tracts showed significant differences between males and females, but Two-way ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of age group for all tracts (p < 0.0001), whereas sex and age group interaction were not significant in any tract (p>0.25). Therefore,  mean FA and MD were evaluated across sexes. 

No significant differences were found between the mean FA values in the left and right sides of AF, IFOF, SLF, or UF across ages.  Mean FA was significantly higher on the right ILF than on the left ILF (KS 0.27, p = 0.0006). Mean MD was significantly higher on the left side for the AF (KS= 0.33,  p<0.0001), IFOF (KS= 20, p<0.0001), ILF (0.42, p<0.0001), and SLF (KS = 0.26, p= 0.0007), and higher on the right side for the UF (KS=0.35, p= 0.0197) across ages. These differences remained stable across age groups.

Mean FA increased with age in all tracts following a logarithmic trajectory, showing rapid early maturation that gradually slowed. The regression models significantly fit the logarithmic trajectory and explained 54–69% of the variance in FA (p < 0.001). Mean MD decreased in all tracts following an inverse logarithmic trajectory, with sharper reductions during the early years that gradually leveled off with age, accounting for 55–63% of the variance (p < 0.001). 

Mean FA differed significantly across the ten tracts (Kruskal–Wallis H = 166.02, p < 0.001, η² = 0.143), with post-hoc comparisons showing that the UF bilaterally had consistently lower FA than the other tracts, while the AF, IFOF, ILF, and SLF exhibited comparable values. Similarly, mean MD differed significantly across the tracts (Kruskal–Wallis H = 166.58, p < 0.001, η² = 0.143), with the UF bilaterally showing higher MD than most other tracts, and the ILF showing higher MD than the AF, IFOF, and SLF, which generally had comparable values. 

Intragroup comparison across age groups

Levene’s test indicated that FA variance was largely stable across age groups for the majority of tracts, with the exception of UF-R, ILF-L, and ILF-R, which showed significant differences between groups (p<0.05). In contrast, MD variance differed significantly across all tracts (p< 0.008), with the largest variance observed in the ILF-L and UF-R. 

Post hoc Games-Howell tests revealed that preschoolers had significantly higher mean FA than infants across all tracts. Young school-aged children showed higher FA than preschoolers in all tracts except the UF-L. Middle schoolers presented higher FA than young school-aged children only in the ILF-R and SLF-L. No significant differences were found between adolescents and middle schoolers in any tracts. In addition, five tracts (AF-L, IFOF-L, UF-R, ILF-L, ILF-R) showed no significant FA differences between young school-aged children and adolescents. 

For MD, no significant differences were observed between infants and preschoolers, except marginally lower values in preschoolers for IFOF-L and UF-L. Young school-aged children had lower MD than preschoolers across all tracts. Middle schoolers showed further decreased MD compared to adolescents in all tracts except AF-R, UF-L, and SLF-R. Adolescents did not differ from middle schoolers in any of the tracts and showed no significant difference from young school-aged children in SLF-R, with only marginal decreases in AF-R and UF-L.

Conclusion

White matter microstructure in language-related pathways follows a highly age-dependent maturation pattern from birth through adolescence. Across all ten tracts, FA increased and MD decreased according to logarithmic trajectories, indicating accelerated maturation in the early years that progressively slows in later childhood. Intragroup differences revealed that the greatest changes in FA and MD occur between birth and the early school years, with minimal differences thereafter. By mid-childhood, most of the tracts approach values similar to those of adolescence.

The white matter language tracts studied here showed similar developmental trajectories and overall values, though some tract-specific differences were found. The ILF was the only pathway to exhibit hemispheric differences in FA, with higher values on the right than the left. MD had small but significant asymmetries, with higher values on the left hemisphere for all tracts except the Uncinate, which showed the opposite pattern.  The Uncinate Fasciculus showed lower FA and higher MD than the other pathways, along with a more protracted maturation trajectory, consistent with prior studies2. 

This study provides a robust normative dataset for typically developing children from infancy through adolescence. The developmental trajectories characterized here represent an important foundation for future investigative studies of typical and atypical language development. Notably, the rapid maturation in younger children aligns with clinical observation that language growth is most accelerated and impacted during the early years of life.
