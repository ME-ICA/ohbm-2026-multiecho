Introduction

Auditory verbal hallucinations (AVH) are a hallmark symptom of schizophrenia and are associated with extensive alterations in functional brain networks. Up to 70% of individuals with schizophrenia experience AVH, typically described as hearing voices in the absence of external auditory input1. Increasing evidence suggests that the core pathophysiological mechanisms of schizophrenia may involve dysregulation across multiple neurotransmitter systems. Neurochemical modulation, by regulating neuronal excitability and plasticity, further shapes symptom expression2. Accumulating evidence indicates that functional brain abnormalities of schizophrenia patients with auditory verbal hallucinations (AVH+) are closely linked to multiple neurotransmitter systems, including glutamate (Glu), dopamine, serotonin, and norepinephrine3. However, the specific influence of distinct neurotransmitters and their transporters on functional brain networks remains insufficiently characterized. A particularly notable advancement has been the integration of molecular imaging data from positron emission tomography (PET) and single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) into fMRI analyses4. This approach, termed Receptor-Enriched Analysis of Functional Connectivity by Targets (REACT), provides a framework for bridging micro- and macro-scale systems. Despite being a relatively recent development, REACT has already demonstrated broad applicability, including studies of clinical conditions such as schizophrenia and chronic pain. This study aimed to delineate the relationship between neurotransmitter function and aberrant brain network organization in schizophrenia under auditory stimulation, thereby advancing our understanding of the underlying pathophysiology and informing potential pharmacological interventions.

Methods

All data for this study were sourced from OpenNeuro Dataset ds004302 (https://openneuro.org/datasets/ds004302) which is released under the CC0 license. Data were obtained from a publicly available dataset comprising 46 patients with schizophrenia (23 with frequent AVH and 23 without) and 25 age-, sex-, and IQ-matched healthy controls. Participants underwent fMRI scans during auditory stimulation involving white noise, words, sentences, and reversed speech. 

Images were acquired with a 3T Philips Ingenia scanner (Philips Medical Systems, Best, The Netherlands). Functional data were acquired using a T2-weighted echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence with 341 volumes and the following acquisition parameters: TR = 2000ms, TE = 30ms, flip angle = 70˚, in-plane resolution = 3.5 × 3.5mm, FOV = 238 × 245mm, slice thickness = 3.5mm, inter-slice gap = 0.75mm. Slices (32 per volume) were acquired with an interleaved order parallel to the AC-PC plane. We also acquired a high-resolution anatomical volume with an FFE (Fast Field Echo) sequence for anatomical reference and inspection (TR = 9.90ms; TE = 4.60ms; Flip angle = 8˚; voxel size = 1 × 1mm; slice thickness = 1mm; slice number = 180; FOV = 240mm).

Structural and functional MRI data were preprocessed using fMRIPrep, which implements Nipype-based, standardized workflows. Anatomical preprocessing included: (1) brain extraction, (2) tissue segmentation, (3) spatial normalization to MNI-152 standard space, (4) surface reconstruction with FreeSurfer (version 7.4.1, https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/). Functional preprocessing included: (1) BOLD reference estimation, (2) head-motion estimation, (3) slice-timing correction, (4) susceptibility distortion correction, (5) generation of preprocessed BOLD in native space, (6) EPI–T1w registration, (7) resampling to MNI-152 standard spaces with registration matrix as the same as anatomical image, (8) sampling to FreeSurfer surfaces and registering to fsaverage5 space, (9) nuisance regression with head motion parameters, white matter, cerebrospinal fluid and global signal, and (10) temporal filtering with 0.01-0.1Hz. Then BOLD signals of each ROI were extracted with Schaefer-400 cortical atlas and Tian subcortex atlas, followed by application of REACT to identify neurotransmitter-related brain activity patterns.. 

Neurotransmitter distribution maps for six molecular targets (DAT, 5-HTT, mGluR5, GABAa, NET, and VAChT) were derived from publicly available PET atlases (https://netneurolab.github.io/neuromaps/index.html). The functional networks, influenced by the molecular systems, were analyzed across four auditory tasks (words, sentences, reversed speech, white noise) using a two-step dual regression approach30. First, receptor/transporter density maps were entered as spatial regressors to extract the dominant BOLD time series associated with each molecular system. Both fMRI data and design matrices were demeaned, and analyses were constrained to a binarized gray-matter (GM) mask intersected with voxels that had available molecular signal. In the second step, the subject-specific time series from step one were used as temporal regressors in a multiple regression to generate subject-level, neurotransmitter-enriched functional maps. This step was restricted to a GM mask shared across all participants. Data and design matrices were demeaned; temporal regressors were standardized to unit variance prior to fitting.

We employed PLSC analysis using the myPLS toolbox (https://github.com/danizoeller/myPLS/) to explore disrupted patterns of trans-neurotransmitter receptor density functional network map and corresponding latent variables (LVs) for each participant. This method also facilitated the examination of multivariate correlations between the spatial patterns of functional networks and neurotransmitter receptor densities.

Results

Four PLSC models were examined across auditory conditions (words, sentences, reversed speech, white noise). Reversed speech yielded four significant latent variables (LVs), whereas the other conditions each showed three.

White noise condition. Three significant LVs were identified, all reflecting mixed patterns linking neurotransmitter-enriched functional connectivity with both SZ vs. HC and AVH+ vs. AVH− contrasts. LV1 (48.26% covariance, p=.001) showed widespread cortical involvement (visual, somatomotor, dorsal attention, control, default) and subcortical regions, and was primarily associated with SZ vs. HC, with positive loadings on 5-HTT, VACht, DAT and negative loadings on NET, GABAA, mGluR5. This pattern was also secondarily associated with AVH status via NET. LV2 (15.14%, p=.001) and LV3 (11.15%, p=.003) showed distinct cortical–subcortical patterns and mixed neurotransmitter profiles, each primarily related to SZ vs. HC and reliably associated with AVH status through differential GABAA, mGluR5, 5-HTT, NET, and DAT loadings.

Words condition. Three significant mixed LVs were observed. LV1 (53.32%, p=.001) involved widespread sensory, attention, control networks and striatal regions, and was strongly associated with SZ vs. HC (positive 5-HTT, VACht; negative NET, GABAA, mGluR5), with secondary AVH-related effects mainly involving GABAA and mGluR5. LV2 (18.71%, p=.001) and LV3 (10.15%, p=.006) showed complementary cortical patterns and neurotransmitter profiles, primarily distinguishing SZ from HC and secondarily differentiating AVH status.

Sentences condition. Three significant LVs were identified. LV1 (50.08%, p=.001) showed extensive cortical and striatal involvement and was primarily linked to SZ vs. HC (positive 5-HTT, VACht; negative NET, GABAA, mGluR5), with weaker but reliable AVH associations. LV2 (20.12%, p=.001) and LV3 (9.24%, p=.01) exhibited distinct attention- and somatomotor-related patterns, each showing mixed neurotransmitter loadings primarily associated with disease status and secondarily with AVH status.

Reversed speech condition. Four significant mixed LVs were detected. LV1 (52.45%, p=.001) showed widespread cortical and striatal engagement and was primarily associated with SZ vs. HC, with secondary AVH-related effects. LV2 (15.32%, p=.001) and LV3 (9.34%, p=.007) demonstrated complementary cortical–thalamic patterns, primarily distinguishing SZ from HC with weaker AVH associations. LV4 (8.65%, p=.009) was uniquely characterized by stronger AVH-related effects, while also showing a secondary association with SZ vs. HC.

Correlations between LV score and hallucination severity. Under the words condition, LV1 scores within the NET-enriched functional connectivity exhibited a significant positive association with hallucination severity after false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Under the sentences condition, LV1 scores within the GABAA-enriched functional connectivity were significantly and positively correlated with hallucination severity after correction.

Conclusion

Our study revealed widespread cortical and subcortical abnormalities in SCZ particularly within the somatomotor and cognitive control networks, as well as in subcortical nuclei including the nucleus accumbens, putamen, and caudate across different auditory conditions. These alterations were accompanied by extensive neurotransmitter-enriched network abnormalities. General SCZ-related differences were characterized by reduced nucleus accumbens-enriched network loadings and divergent mGluR5/GABA𝐴 effects. Meaningful auditory stimuli (sentences and words) evoked more consistent patterns of network disruption, whereas reversed speech induced more complex and often opposite alterations. Patients with frequent AVH+ exhibited heightened sensitivity to meaningful input, showing greater disruption within GABA𝐴- and nucleus accumbens-enriched networks. Notably, LV1 scores of these networks under the sentences condition were positively correlated with hallucination severity.

Our findings align with this view: patients performed better under neutral white-noise stimulation than during meaningful auditory tasks, suggesting that reducing extraneous stimulation may partially alleviate CN-related attentional deficits. Impaired CN function likely contributes to excessive allocation of attention toward internal stimuli, hindering patients’ ability to disengage from hallucinations. Pronounced SMN abnormalities may further underlie core clinical symptoms by disrupting sensorimotor integration. In the reversed speech condition, LV4 identified a distinct spatial configuration of neurotransmitter-enriched functional connectivity that differentiated the AVH+ from the AVH− group. This pattern was characterized by pronounced positive and negative loadings within the control and default mode networks, indicating opposing forms of dysregulation in patients with AVH. Such a configuration aligns with the Top-Down and Bottom-Up Predictive Dissonance Hypothesis, which proposes that hallucinations arise when excessively strong internally generated predictions outweigh weakened sensory evidence5. Our study further identifies neurotransmitter-specific contributions to these network abnormalities, particularly involving the NET, GABA𝐴, and mGluR5 systems. These results converge with previous findings showing a reduction of GABAergic inhibitory interneurons in the hippocampus of schizophrenia patients, and elevated norepinephrine (NE) levels in those with prominent positive symptoms. The nucleus accumbens, a key component of the reward network, also exhibits structural and functional alterations in schizophrenia.

By integrating molecular data on neurotransmitter receptors and transporters with fMRI-based functional analyses, this study provides novel insights into how speech-related stimuli modulate neurotransmitter-specific brain activity in schizophrenia. These findings deepen our understanding of the neurochemical foundations of the disorder and offer a theoretical framework for future multi-receptor and multi-drug treatment strategies.
