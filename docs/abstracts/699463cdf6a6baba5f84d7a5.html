Introduction

The locus coeruleus-norepinephrine (LC-NE) system serves as a global modulator of arousal and stress, profoundly influencing executive function and emotional regulation through widespread projections to the forebrain 1,2. Dysregulation of this system is frequently implicated in anxiety disorders; however, the precise circuit mechanisms converting global noradrenergic signals into specific maladaptive behavioral outcomes remain elusive. The medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and thalamus form a reciprocal circuit essential for cognitive control and affective processing2. While LC activation is known to restructure large-scale brain networks, it remains unclear how it selectively disrupts synchrony within this specific thalamocortical loop to drive anxiety-like states. Understanding this mechanism is critical, as the impact of LC output on circuitry and behavior is notoriously variable and context-dependent.

To address this, we investigated the role of the paraventricular thalamus (PVT), a midline nucleus characterized by high network connectivity, as a potential regulator of this process. The PVT acts as an integrative hub for stress and arousal signals and is densely interconnected with the mPFC, positioning it to functionally gate thalamocortical communication. We hypothesized that the PVT functions as a hierarchical intersection that determines how global LC-NE activation reorganizes PrL-mediodorsal thalamus (MD) synchrony to shape anxiety-like behavior. To test this, we employed a multimodal approach combining chemogenetic functional MRI (fMRI), dual-site spectral fiber photometry, and behavioral assays in mice. We sought to determine whether the PVT gates the thalamocortical response to LC modulation, and to causally establish if PVT activity is required to translate these neural dynamics into avoidance behavior.

Methods

We combined chemogenetic fMRI, graph-theoretical network analysis, dual-site calcium photometry, and behavioral testing in mice. For fMRI experiments, mice expressing hM3Dq in Dbh⁺ LC neurons (LC-Gq) and control littermates underwent cerebral blood volume–weighted fMRI (CBV-fMRI) on a 9.4T Bruker system under light isoflurane anesthesia3. Following a 10-min baseline period, clozapine-N-oxide (CNO; 1 mg/kg, i.p.) was administered via catheter, and imaging continued for 30 min post-injection (Fig. 1A). fMRI data were preprocessed using AFNI- and ANTs-based pipelines3, including slice-timing and motion correction, skull stripping, spatial normalization to an EPI template, de-spiking, ICA denoising, nuisance regression (motion and CSF), spatial smoothing, detrending, and high-pass filtering.

Voxelwise functional connectivity (FC) was computed within an anatomically defined medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) mask encompassing infralimbic, prelimbic, and cingulate cortices. Modularity decomposition of FC matrices using Newman’s spectral algorithm identified four reproducible mPFC functional modules (ILm, PrLm, aACCm, pACCm). Module-averaged time courses were used as seeds for thalamic FC analyses. LC-induced circuit changes were quantified as Post-30 minus Baseline FC and compared between groups using cluster-level correction based on spatial autocorrelation estimates.

To assess intra-thalamic network organization, voxelwise degree centrality (DC) was computed within the thalamus at baseline. DC was calculated as the number of supra-threshold positive FC edges (r > 0.25). Relationships between baseline hubness metrics and LC-induced PrLm–MD FC changes were tested using linear mixed-effects models.

For causal circuit interrogation, En1-Cre mice received bidirectional chemogenetic manipulation of the PVT (hM3Dq or hM4Di) while LC neurons were activated or left unmanipulated. Dual-site fiber photometry recorded calcium signals from the prelimbic cortex (PrL) and mediodorsal thalamus (MD). Signals were processed in MATLAB using filtering, bleaching correction, and ΔF/F normalization. Thalamocortical synchrony was quantified using frequency-resolved phase-lag index (PLI) analyses with cluster-based permutation testing. Anxiety-like behavior was assessed using the light–dark box under counterbalanced CNO and vehicle sessions, with behavioral ratios logit-transformed for statistical analyses.

Results

LC–NE activation selectively reorganizes prelimbic–mediodorsal thalamic connectivity. To determine how LC–NE activation alters prefrontal–thalamic communication, we performed chemogenetic CBV-fMRI in mice expressing hM3Dq in Dbh⁺ LC neurons (LC-Gq) and control littermates (Fig. 1A). Modularity analysis of voxelwise functional connectivity within an anatomically defined mPFC mask consistently identified four functional submodules: infralimbic (ILm), prelimbic (PrLm), anterior ACC (aACCm), and posterior ACC (pACCm) (Fig. 1B). Among these modules, only the PrLm exhibited significant LC-dependent changes in thalamic connectivity. Specifically, LC–NE activation selectively reduced PrLm coupling with the MD, whereas connectivity of the other mPFC modules remained unchanged (Fig. 1C). Group-level analysis confirmed a significant decrease in average PrLm–MD functional connectivity in LC-Gq mice relative to controls. 

Baseline PVT hubness predicts LC-induced PrLm–MD decoupling. Given the selective involvement of the MD, we next asked whether intra-thalamic network organization predicts the magnitude of LC-induced circuit reconfiguration. We quantified voxelwise degree centrality (DC) within the thalamus at baseline as a measure of intrinsic hubness. Voxelwise correlation analysis revealed that baseline DC within the PVT significantly predicted the magnitude of PrLm–MD connectivity reduction following LC activation (Fig. 1D). Linear mixed-effects modeling confirmed a robust negative relationship between baseline PVT DC and PrLm–MD FC change (p = 0.001), indicating that animals with higher PVT hubness at rest exhibited stronger LC-induced thalamocortical decoupling. These findings identify the PVT as a candidate integrative hub whose intrinsic network state determines susceptibility of the PrL–MD pathway to LC neuromodulation.

PVT activity is required for LC-induced PrL–MD desynchronization. To test whether the PVT causally gates LC-driven circuit reorganization, we combined chemogenetic manipulation of PVT with dual-site calcium photometry recordings from the PrL and MD (Fig. 2A). LC activation increased PrL calcium activity, and this effect was further enhanced when PVT was concurrently excited (LC-Gq + PVT-Gq), whereas PVT inhibition attenuated LC-driven PrL responses (Fig. 2B). In contrast, MD calcium activity was not directly modulated by LC activation alone. We next examined frequency-specific thalamocortical synchrony. Phase-lag index (PLI) analysis revealed a selective increase in low-frequency PrL–MD coupling (0.22–1.05 Hz) during the Post-30 period relative to Baseline exclusively in the LC-Gq + PVT-Gq condition (Fig. 2C). No significant synchrony changes were observed when LC activation occurred when PVT was inhibited, indicating that PVT activity is required for LC-induced reorganization of thalamocortical phase relationships.

PVT gates the translation of LC-driven circuit changes into anxiety-like behavior. We next assessed whether PVT-dependent circuit reorganization translated into behavioral outcomes using the light–dark box test (Fig. 2D). Analysis of the change in light-zone time ratio revealed a significant LC × PVT interaction (F(1,53) = 8.50, p = 0.005). Reduced light-zone exploration, indicative of increased anxiety-like behavior, was observed selectively in the LC-Gq + PVT-Gq group. Across animals, PrL calcium activity significantly predicted anxiety-like behavior under LC activation, with stronger PrL responses associated with reduced light-zone exploration (p = 0.005; Fig. 2E). Finally, moderated regression analyses demonstrated that this PrL–behavior relationship was shaped by PVT state and low-frequency PrL–MD synchrony. A significant PVT × PLI interaction and a three-way interaction among PrL activity, PVT state, and PLI indicated that PVT-dependent phase-lag coupling determines how LC-driven prefrontal activity is translated into behavior (Fig. 2F).

Conclusion

This study provides convergent systems- and circuit-level evidence that the PVT serves as a critical gate through which global LC–NE activation is transformed into selective thalamocortical reconfiguration and anxiety-like behavior. Although LC output is classically viewed as a diffuse neuromodulatory signal, our results demonstrate that its functional impact on cognition and behavior is neither uniform nor inevitable, but instead depends on the intrinsic network state and causal engagement of specific thalamic hubs.

Using chemogenetic CBV-fMRI, we show that LC activation selectively weakens coupling between the PrL and the MD. This circuit specificity challenges models in which LC–NE broadly suppresses or enhances prefrontal–thalamic interactions and instead supports a reconfiguration framework in which neuromodulatory signals reshape targeted control pathways. Importantly, the magnitude of PrL–MD decoupling was predicted by baseline PVT hubness, indicating that intrinsic thalamic network architecture sets the gain of neuromodulatory effects. These findings position the PVT as a latent regulator whose resting-state connectivity biases the system toward vulnerability or resilience under arousal.

Causal circuit manipulations further established that PVT activity is required for LC-driven thalamocortical reorganization. Dual-site calcium photometry revealed that LC activation alone was insufficient to induce coherent PrL–MD desynchronization; instead, concurrent PVT excitation enabled low-frequency, non-zero phase-lag coupling and amplified prelimbic responses. In contrast, PVT inhibition abolished LC-induced synchrony changes and prevented behavioral expression of anxiety-like avoidance. These results indicate that the PVT does not merely relay arousal signals, but actively configures intra-thalamic and thalamocortical phase relationships that determine how cortical activity propagates to behaviorally relevant outputs.

At the behavioral level, prelimbic activity emerged as a consistent predictor of anxiety-like avoidance, but this relationship was dynamically shaped by PVT state and PrL–MD synchrony. Moderated regression analyses revealed that neither cortical activity nor thalamocortical coupling alone was sufficient to explain behavior; instead, their interaction within a PVT-dependent framework governed behavioral outcomes. This supports a hierarchical intersection model in which LC provides global arousal drive, the PVT gates network-level integration, and the PrL functions as a context-sensitive cortical readout. Together, these findings offer a mechanistic explanation for the variability of LC-related anxiety phenotypes and reconcile how a global neuromodulatory system can yield circuit- and behavior-specific effects. By integrating thalamic hubness, phase-resolved synchrony, and causal manipulation, this work highlights the PVT as a key control node linking neuromodulation to adaptive or maladaptive behavioral states. More broadly, it underscores the importance of thalamic network organization in shaping cortical function and suggests that targeting thalamic gating mechanisms may provide new avenues for intervention in stress- and mood-related disorders4,5.
