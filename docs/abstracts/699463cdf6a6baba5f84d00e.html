Introduction

Schizophrenia is consistently associated with hippocampal structural alterations, yet the fine-grained genetic mechanisms linking the disorder to specific hippocampal subfields remain unclear. Most previous genome-wide studies have focused on total hippocampal volume or bilateral averages, which can obscure localized and lateralized effects that are critical for memory and psychosis-related phenotypes1,2. The hippocampus is anatomically and functionally heterogeneous, and converging evidence suggests that subfield- and hemisphere-specific alterations may differentially relate to schizophrenia risk and symptom dimensions. Leveraging the largest available genome-wide association study (GWAS) datasets for schizophrenia and hippocampal subfield volumes, we performed a comprehensive cross-trait genetic analysis to map global, local, and variant-level overlap between schizophrenia liability and 44 left- and right-hemispheric hippocampal traits. Our aim was to identify shared loci, quantify hemispheric specificity, and highlight biological pathways that may underlie schizophrenia-related hippocampal vulnerability.

Methods

We analyzed GWAS summary statistics for schizophrenia (53,386 cases; 77,258 controls of European ancestry) and 44 hippocampal volumetric traits from the UK Biobank imaging cohort (N = 33,224; FreeSurfer v7 segmentation)3,4. Hippocampal phenotypes included bilateral whole hippocampal volumes, head and body volumes, and 19 subfields per hemisphere, processed using a harmonized pipeline with standard imaging confound adjustment. Global genetic correlations were estimated using linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC) with precomputed LD scores from 1000 Genomes European reference data, excluding the major histocompatibility complex and 8p23.1 regions because of complex LD structure. To enhance discovery of shared variants, we applied pleiotropy-informed conditional and conjunctional false discovery rate (cond/conjFDR) analyses across all schizophrenia–subfield pairs, defining significant SNPs at condFDR < 0.01 and conjFDR < 0.05. Independent loci were obtained through LD-based clumping and merging, identifying lead and candidate SNPs per locus. Local genetic correlations were assessed using LAVA in ~2,500 predefined genomic regions that showed sufficient univariate signal in both traits, with FDR correction for multiple testing. All conjFDR-significant variants were functionally annotated in Functional Mapping and Annotation, including genomic context, Combined Annotation-Dependent Depletion and RegulomeDB scores, chromatin states, and mapping to protein-coding genes via positional, expression quantitative trait loci, and chromatin interaction information. Gene-set enrichment analyses were performed for Gene Ontology (GO) biological processes. Subfield-specific and hemisphere-specific shared loci were quantified by integrating locus membership across left and right traits separately.

Results

LDSC revealed weak global genetic correlations between schizophrenia and hippocampal traits (rg range: –0.078 to 0.049), with several nominally significant negative correlations in left-sided subfields but no robust genome-wide signal, suggesting mixed directional effects across the genome. In contrast, cond/conjFDR analyses demonstrated extensive polygenic overlap. Across all schizophrenia–subfield pairs, we identified 635 subfield-level shared loci, which merged into 171 distinct cross-trait loci after LD-based merging. Shared loci were observed for 43 of the 44 hippocampal traits, with the right parasubiculum as the only exception (Fig. 1). Effect directions at the variant level showed a modest predominance of concordant effects (risk-increasing alleles associated with larger volume; 54.7%), alongside a substantial fraction of discordant effects, consistent with a complex genetic relationship between schizophrenia liability and hippocampal morphology.

Hemispheric analyses highlighted pronounced lateralization. We identified 106 distinct shared loci for left-hemispheric traits and 117 for right-hemispheric traits, with 49 loci specific to the left hemisphere (46.2%) and 61 specific to the right (52.1%). The strongest left-hemispheric cross-trait signal mapped to the MAPT locus on chromosome 17, whereas the strongest right-hemispheric signal mapped to the TMEM219 locus on chromosome 16. Functional annotation of 171 shared loci mapped 17,703 candidate SNPs to 1,065 protein-coding genes. Gene-set enrichment analyses revealed 255 significant GO biological process terms, prominently including nervous system development, regulation of generation of neurons, and neuron differentiation (FDR q < 1×10⁻4). LAVA analyses identified 218 genomic regions with significant local genetic correlations between schizophrenia and hippocampal traits; 29 of the 171 conjFDR loci overlapped LAVA-supported regions, providing orthogonal validation. Several shared loci replicated earlier work on schizophrenia–hippocampus overlap, while six loci were novel for schizophrenia or hippocampal volume.

Conclusion

This large-scale cross-trait imaging genetics study demonstrates that the relationship between schizophrenia and hippocampal structure is driven by fine-grained and partially lateralized genetic effects rather than strong global correlations. By integrating LDSC, cond/conjFDR, and LAVA across 44 hippocampal traits, we delineate 171 shared loci that link schizophrenia liability to subfield-specific and hemisphere-specific variation, with MAPT- and TMEM219-centered loci exemplifying left–right asymmetry. The enrichment of genes involved in nervous system development and neuronal generation supports a neurodevelopmental framework in which genetic risk for schizophrenia converges on hippocampal circuits. These findings refine the genetic architecture connecting psychiatric risk to hippocampal morphology and provide testable hypotheses for future work on symptom dimensions, cognitive phenotypes, and longitudinal trajectories, as well as potential targets for mechanistic and translational studies.
