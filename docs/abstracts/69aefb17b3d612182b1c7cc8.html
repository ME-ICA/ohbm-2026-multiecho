Introduction

Climate change is increasingly recognized as a major determinant of human health, yet its neurobiological implications remain largely unexplored. While prior research has linked atmospheric conditions to physical and mental health outcomes, the potential influence of weather on brain structure has not been systematically investigated. This gap is particularly pressing given projections of increased climatic variability and extreme weather events, which may pose novel risks to neurological health.

Atmospheric variables such as temperature, solar radiation, precipitation, and wind speed can affect the brain through multiple pathways. These include physiological mechanisms like vitamin D synthesis and circadian rhythm regulation, as well as behavioral factors such as physical activity and social engagement. However, integrating large-scale meteorological data with neuroimaging cohorts has posed significant methodological challenges.

In this study, we address this gap by linking monthly averaged atmospheric data from the ERA5 reanalysis dataset to structural MRI data from 30,831 participants in the UK Biobank. We examine associations between weather patterns and brain metrics, including grey and white matter volumes and white matter microstructure. To contextualize the relevance of these associations, we compare them to the effects of polygenic risk scores (PGRS) for Alzheimer’s disease and self-reported mental health measures.

Our findings reveal that ambient weather conditions, particularly lower precipitation and wind speed, and higher solar radiation and pressure, are significantly associated with brain structure, often exceeding the explanatory power of genetic and psychological factors. These results suggest that weather is a measurable environmental determinant of brain structure and highlight the need to incorporate meteorological exposures into models of brain health.

Methods

We analysed structural MRI data from 30,831 participants in the UK Biobank, including 4,294 with follow-up scans. Participants with diagnosed neurological, psychiatric, or circulatory conditions were excluded. T1-weighted and diffusion-weighted images were processed using FreeSurfer and an optimised diffusion MRI pipeline, respectively. Quality control included Euler number thresholds and the YTTRIUM method for diffusion metrics.

Monthly atmospheric data were obtained from the ERA5 reanalysis dataset, averaged over the UK for the month preceding each MRI scan. Variables included 2-metre temperature, 10-metre wind speed, surface pressure, UV radiation, and total precipitation. These were spatially averaged across a broad UK grid to ensure robust regional estimates.

Brain metrics included cortical and white matter volumes, and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) parameters: fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity (MD), radial diffusivity (RD), and axial diffusivity (AD). Regional brain volumes were extracted using the Desikan-Killiany atlas, and white matter tracts were defined using the JHU atlas.

We computed polygenic risk scores (PGRS) for Alzheimer’s disease using LDpred2 and summary statistics from a large GWAS. Self-reported mental health measures included the Recent Depressive Symptoms (RDS-4) score and neuroticism, both validated in the UK Biobank.

Statistical analyses involved linear models predicting brain metrics from weather variables, controlling for age, sex, site, household income, intracranial volume, and surface holes. We compared these models to those including PGRS and self-reported mental health. Likelihood ratio tests assessed whether genetic or psychological variables improved model fit. Longitudinal effects were examined using mixed-effects models with participant as a random factor.

All p-values were FDR-corrected. Standardised beta coefficients were reported for comparability across predictors.

Results

Atmospheric conditions were significantly associated with multiple markers of brain structure. Across the full sample (N = 30,831), favourable weather—characterised by higher temperature, solar radiation, and surface pressure, alongside lower precipitation and wind speed—was linked to increased cortical volume and enhanced white matter integrity. Specifically, these conditions were associated with higher fractional anisotropy (FA) and lower axial, radial, and mean diffusivity, indicating more coherent white matter microstructure.

Effect sizes for atmospheric variables (median ± MAD β = 0.013 ± 0.013) exceeded those of Alzheimer’s disease polygenic risk scores (PGRS; β = 0.006 ± 0.001) and were comparable to self-reported depression and neuroticism (β = 0.013 ± 0.012). The strongest associations were observed between FA and 10-metre wind speed (β = –0.059, 95% CI [–0.068, –0.049], pFDR = 1.78 × 10⁻³⁰), and between FA and surface UV radiation (β = 0.055, 95% CI [0.045, 0.064], pFDR = 4.42 × 10⁻²⁷).

Likelihood ratio tests indicated that adding PGRS or self-reported mental health measures did not significantly improve model fit in most cases. Only 25% of models showed significant improvement (p < 0.05, FDR-corrected), and the additional explained variance was minimal (ΔR² < 0.03%). Neuroticism contributed most to models predicting cortical volume (F = 26.12–27.16, p < 0.05), followed by depression (F = 11.29–11.89, p < 0.05).

Longitudinal analyses in the follow-up subsample (n = 4,294) confirmed these patterns. Total precipitation showed the strongest and most consistent associations over time, negatively correlating with cortical volume (β = –0.051, 95% CI [–0.053, –0.048], pFDR < 2.23 × 10⁻³⁰⁸) and FA (β = –0.048, 95% CI [–0.051, –0.046], pFDR < 2.23 × 10⁻³⁰⁸), and positively with MD and RD.

Region- and tract-level analyses revealed that white matter microstructure was particularly sensitive to atmospheric variation. The inferior longitudinal fasciculus showed the strongest associations (β < 0.08) across multiple weather variables. Additionally, volumes of the amygdala and ventricles were among the most weather-sensitive grey matter regions, particularly in relation to precipitation.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence that ambient atmospheric conditions are associated with human brain structure, with effect sizes that exceed those of the genetic risk for common psychiatric disorders and Alzheimer's and mental health, considering a healthily aging population. These findings suggest that weather is a biologically relevant environmental factor that should be considered in models of brain health and disease risk.

Favourable weather, defined by higher temperature, solar radiation, and pressure, and lower precipitation and wind speed, was consistently associated with greater cortical volume and more coherent white matter integrety. These associations were observed both cross-sectionally and longitudinally, suggesting that atmospheric exposures may influence brain structure over time.

Several mechanisms may underlie these associations. Solar radiation may promote neuroplasticity through vitamin D synthesis and circadian regulation. Atmospheric pressure and precipitation may act indirectly by modulating behaviou, favourable weather supports physical activity, social interaction, and exposure to natural environments, all of which are linked to brain health. Conversely, high-pressure systems may increase air pollution exposure, which has been associated with neuroinflammation and increased dementia risk.

Despite adjusting for key covariates, residual confounding from unmeasured socioeconomic and lifestyle factors remains possible. Differences in urbanicity, healthcare access, occupation, and housing may influence both weather exposure and brain structure. Additionally, the use of regional weather averages may obscure individual-level variability in exposure, such as time spent outdoors or indoor environmental conditions.

The one-month averaging window, while pragmatic, may not capture the most relevant temporal dynamics of weather–brain interactions. Moreover, the UK Biobank imaging sample is not representative of the general population, limiting generalisability. Non-linear effects and thresholds may also exist, particularly in response to extreme or cumulative exposures.

Future research should prioritise longitudinal designs to establish temporal precedence and explore dose-response relationships. Integrating wearable sensors and ecological momentary assessments could improve exposure measurement. Identifying vulnerable populations and modifiable behavioural mediators will be essential for translating these findings into public health interventions.

If causal relationships are confirmed, these results could inform strategies to mitigate the neurological impacts of climate change. Urban planning, building design, and individual-level interventions, such as light therapy or structured activity during adverse weather, may help buffer the brain against environmental stressors.

In summary, our findings establish atmospheric patterns as measurable correlates of brain structure. As climate variability intensifies, understanding the neurobiological sensitivity to weather will be critical for anticipating and addressing future health challenges.
