Introduction

The dynamic complexity of brain activity has been linked to psychiatric symptoms and Electroencephalogram (EEG) studies have reported alterations in multiscale entropy (MSE) across neuropsychiatric conditions. MSE quantifies temporal irregularity by estimating sample entropy (SampEn) over multiple time scales1. Across clinical and pharmacological studies, atypical MSE has been proposed as a signature of altered cortical network dynamics, potentially reflecting reduced inhibitory interneuron activity and consequent shift of excitation–inhibition balance2, 3. MSE thus provides a quantitative framework to probe neurophysiological mechanisms relevant to neurodevelopmental disorders.

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is characterized by difficulties in reciprocal social communication and restricted, repetitive behaviors, and many individuals also show atypical sensory processing . Prior work has reported reduced SampEn at longer temporal scales in ASD relative to typically developed (TD) controls, but how such complexity differences map onto symptom domains remains unclear. Moreover, most studies have examined resting-state EEG in children4, and evidence linking EEG complexity to socio-emotional processes—particularly challenging for individuals with ASD—remains limited. Beyond a single tightly-controlled task study5, ecologically valid paradigms are scarce. 

Here we asked whether EEG complexity alterations in ASD (1) generalize to adulthood, (2) relate to ASD-related trait variation, and (3) during naturalistic free viewing of emotional and non-emotional movies capture variability relevant to social information processing.

Methods

We analyzed EEG data from 34 adults with ASD (8 females; age: mean = 38.07 years, SD = 9.94, range = 21–61) and 29 TDadults (4 females; age: mean = 33.52 years, SD = 12.62, range = 20–62). All ASD participants were outpatients at Showa Medical University Karasuyama Hospital and provided written informed consent. Participants completed the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule, Second Edition (ADOS-2)6 and the self-reported Adult/Adolescent Sensory Profile (AASP)7. 

EEG was recorded during free-viewing of two short movie clips: a non-emotional condition (Inscapes), non-narrative animation developed for neuroimaging research and composed of slowly evolving abstract shapes and colors8, and an emotional condition (Partly Cloudy, Pixar), a short film shown to robustly engage social networks and pain-related networks during viewing9, 10. 

Signals were acquired with an EEGO™ mylab amplifier (ANT Neuro, Berlin, Germany) using a 64-channel Waveguard™ cap positioned at equidistant scalp locations; the ground electrode was placed at the frontal midline anterior to the central electrodes, and data were referenced to electrode 5Z. Data were sampled at 1,000 Hz and preprocessed with channel interpolation, anti-alias filtering and downsampling to 200 Hz, notch and high-pass filtering, common-average referencing, ASR-assisted artifact suppression, ICA-based component rejection, and current source density (CSD) transformation. SampEn was then computed for each channel at each scale1, 3 from continuous 60-s time series using identical time windows across participants.

Results

Group comparisons using a nonparametric, cluster-based permutation tests identified one significant cluster at the fine-scale and one at the coarse-scale in both the non-emotional and emotional movie conditions. At the fine scale (time series sampled at 200–100 Hz), individuals with ASD exhibited significantly higher SampEn than TD controls. A significant coarse-scale cluster was also observed in the non-emotional movie (time series sampled at 12.5–6.67 Hz) condition and in the emotional movie condition (20–6.67 Hz), indicating lower SampEn in ASD relative to TD controls. Spatially, significant effects were predominantly confined to occipital regions during the non-emotional movies, whereas during the emotional movies—particularly at the coarse-scale—the cluster extended over a broader set of scalp regions. 

To examine relationships with ASD-relevant traits in the combined cohort (ASD and TD participants pooled), we performed nonparametric, cluster-based permutation tests of Spearman correlations. In both movie conditions, we identified a significant positive cluster-wise association between ADOS-2 Communication scores and SampEn at the fine scale (non-emotional movie: 200–66.7 Hz; emotional movie: 200–100 Hz). We also observed, in both conditions, a significant positive cluster-wise association between AASP Sensation Seeking scores and SampEn at the middle scale (non-emotional movie: 28.57–13.33 Hz; emotional movie: 20–15.38 Hz). Clusters showing significant symptom associations were predominantly localized over parietal–occipital regions; however, the cluster associated with ADOS-2 Communication during the emotional movie condition extended more prominently into the right hemisphere.

Conclusion

We observed reduced complexity at longer time scales alongside increased complexity at shorter time scales in adults with ASD. The former pattern is consistent with prior studies of resting-state EEG in children with ASD and EEG acquired under controlled experimental conditions in adults with ASD, whereas the latter constitutes a novel finding. Assessment scores related to communication and sensory traits were differentially associated with complexity across time scales. Although ASD is a heterogeneous phenotype comprising multiple symptom domains, MSE may provide a useful tool for probing its neurophysiological substrates. By analyzing EEG during naturalistic free viewing under two stimulus contexts, we further examined context-dependent modulation of neural complexity. Group differences and correlations with communication-related traits were more widespread in the emotional context than in the non-emotional context. These results suggest that neural complexity indexes physiological mechanisms relevant to atypical social and affective information processing in ASD.
