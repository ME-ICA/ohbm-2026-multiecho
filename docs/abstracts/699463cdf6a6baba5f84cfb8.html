Introduction

Accurate localization of interictal epileptic spikes is crucial for presurgical evaluation in refractory focal epilepsy. However, reconstructing deep epileptic sources from high-density electroencephalography (HD-EEG) remains challenging due to signal attenuation by volume conduction and the intrinsic ill-posed nature of the inverse problem. The EEG forward problem was addressed using a realistic head model constructed from imaging data and solved with a recently developed finite element method (FEM) local-subtraction approach.1 For the EEG inverse problem, especially when deep sources are involved, scanning and depth-weighted current density reconstruction (CDR) methods are commonly applied. Among them, the Unit Noise Gain (UNG) beamformer provides robust deep-source estimates through spatial filtering. Depth bias inherent in Minimum Norm Estimation can be reduced using the standardization in Standardized Low-Resolution Brain Electromagnetic Tomography (sLORETA), while Dynamic Statistical Parametric Mapping (dSPM) similarly compensates for depth-related effects through biological noise weighting. More recently, the sLORETA standardization framework was integrated with Kalman filter to reduce depth bias, yielding the so-called standardized Kalman filter (SKF).2 As a dynamic inverse method, SKF exploits the temporal information in HD-EEG for tracking the source dynamics with high spatiotemporal resolution. 

Here, we report the first-time application of SKF for accurate localization and dynamic characterization of a deep epileptic source originating from the amygdalo-hippocampal complex. We then addressed the spatiotemporal tracking capabilities of SKF for mapping the propagation of epileptic activity within deep structures. Stereo-EEG served as the gold standard for validating this new reconstruction approach. Finally, we compare SKF with current standard approaches to assess performance gains for localizing deep sources in focal refractory epilepsy.

Methods

The patient is a 16-year-old right-handed adolescent with refractory focal epilepsy that began at age 13. A 64-channel video-EEG was recorded for 72 hours. One type of interictal epileptic spikes (IES), which shared the morphological similarity with the epileptic aura, was identified under temporal electrodes (F7, FT7, T7, TP7) (Fig. 1(f)). The same spike morphology was later confirmed in stereo-EEG, performed using 14 intracerebral electrodes comprising 128 contacts, targeting the left mesial temporal, insular, and orbitofrontal regions. In stereo-EEG, IES involved left amygdala (A’ contact), left temporal pole (TP’ contact), followed —after a delay— by the recruitment of the left hippocampus (TB’, HT’ and GPH’ contact) (Fig. 1(g)). To enhance the description of such dynamics from the amygdala to the hippocampus, the projection of the sEEG potential and moving dipole scans were performed in Fig. 1(h, i), serving as references for comparison with HD-EEG source reconstructions.

Patient T1-weighted MRI and CT images were co-registred using FSL FLIRT. Head segmentation used FreeSurfer for brain tissue and threshold-based segmentation for the skull (Fig. 1(a, b, c)). A tetrahedral FE mesh of the the six-compartment head model —scalp, skull compacta, skull spongiosa, cerebrospinal fluid, gray matter, and white matter— was constructed from the label map using the CGAL script of the SimNIBs’ charm pipeline. Then the source position grid was placed uniformly inside of the cortex (Fig. 1(d, e)). We computed the EEG lead field L with the transfer matrix approach in combination with the recently developed local subtraction approach, which provides highly accurate simulations while remaining computationally efficient by using DUNEuro.2,3

For the inverse solution, we applied SKF, marking its first use in clinical epileptic source localization. SKF is a new spatiotemporal inverse CDR method specifically developed for this purpose. SFK uses the basic random-walk model, comprising prediction and update steps, as in a recursive Kalman filter. Subsequently, a sLORETA-type standardization is applied to weight the current distribution estimates at each time point, to eliminate depth bias.2 We compared SKF with standard inverse methods known to be effective for deep source imaging, including the instantaneous approaches sLORETA and dSPM, as well as UNG beamforming. Considering that the deviation of the forward model introduces noise-like variability in the residual y real EEG - L * x current density, we applied the prior measurement signal-to-noise ratio (PM-SNR) technique to better balance physiologically plausible activity against the total noise of the model,4 which was set to 6 dB in the study. All estimations were computed by using Zeffiro Interface.5

For each inverse method, the parcellation function in Zeffiro Interface was used to perform statistical analysis of reconstruction strength in selected parcellations based on the DKT-atlas map. Boxplots were used to quantify the source reconstruction strength around the spike peak. Additional time-series curves were used to show the reconstruction strength of specific parcellations over the time course of epileptic activity.

Results

By interpolating the head model and the MRI, we visualized how the inverse reconstructions were distributed across the different methods (Fig. 2(a, b)). Beyond visualization, we performed statistical analysis of reconstructed source strengths across different parcellations or tissues in the left hemisphere using boxplots. Based on the boxplot statistics (Fig. 2(c)), applying a threshold of 0.6 to the normalized reconstructed values effectively highlights the regions with the strongest activation.

The reconstructions of HD-EEG IES obtained with all inverse methods indicate the involvement of the left amygdala and hippocampus (Fig. 2). sLORETA and SKF produced highly concordant spatial patterns, both highlighting the left amygdala (LH36) and hippocampus (LH35) with extensions to the left entorhinal cortex (LH05), the temporal pole (LH32), and the parahippocampal gyrus (LH15). However, boxplot analysis reveals that SKF yields stronger reconstruction values than sLORETA. dSPM also identified the amygdala and hippocampus but displayed its strongest activity in the isthmus of the cingulate gyrus (LH09), likely reflecting depth-related estimation bias. The UNG-beamforming results focus on the left hippocampus and amygdala, with a slight diffusion extending to the entorhinal cortex and parahippocampal gyrus. When comparing the reconstruction results with the active contacts located in the temporal pole (TP’), amygdala (A’), and hippocampus (TB’, HT’ and GPH’) during the E2/EA sEEG events, both sLORETA and SKF reconstructions substantially cover the areas associated with these active contacts. UNG-beamforming accurately estimates activity in the amygdala and hippocampus. However, due to its focalizing characteristics, activities slightly farther away, near the temporal pole, are not reconstructed.

The dynamics between the left amygdala and the left hippocampus as revealed during the sEEG IES events was considered. The tracking potential of the different inverse methods were assessed by analyzing the time-series curves of the rising slope of the IES (Fig. 2(d)). Since (1) the hippocampus is several times larger than the amygdala and (2) the signals recorded by HT’/GPH’ sEEG contacts in the left hippocampus do not exactly match the characteristics of TB’ signal, selecting a smaller region of interest (ROI) for the reconstruction intensity over time was more appropriate. We therefore set 5-mm-diameter spherical ROIs centered at A’1 and TB’2 positions to evaluate the reconstruction dynamics. SKF clearly separates the dynamics between the amygdala and the hippocampus, reproducing the same directionality as in the sEEG IES events. This dynamic is highlighted by arrows showing a delay of approximately 4 ms between the rising slope of the amygdala and that of the hippocampus (Fig. 2(d)). This pattern holds true at the peak, with a similar delay of about 4 ms: the amygdala reaches its maximum value first, before declining. The maximum amplitude in the amygdala was lower than that in the hippocampus. None of the other methods was able to capture the dynamic from the left amygdala to the left hippocampus. When examining the curve peaks, both sLORETA and dSPM display essentially synchronized patterns, while the UNG-beamforming showed a reversed timing relationship, with hippocampal activation preceding of the amygdala.

Conclusion

In this study, we report the first evaluation of the SKF method, based on local subtraction FEM forward modeling, for localizing and tracking the deep and dynamic epileptogenic source in an epileptic patient. SKF reliably reconstructed epileptic activities within the left amygdalo-hippocampal complex, in close concordance with the irritative zone identified by sEEG. Compared with conventional approaches, SKF showed superior robustness to noise and high accuracy in localizing deep epileptogenic sources, while also preserving their spatial extent and configuration. 

When comparing the peak-time source reconstructions obtained from different inverse methods, the statistical parameter mapping (SPM) methods, including sLORETA, SKF, and dSPM, provided reconstructions with greater spatial smoothness and dispersion. The reconstructions obtained from sLORETA and SKF exhibited a similar spatial distribution, reflecting their shared standardization framework. This standardization effectively mitigates the depth bias that exists in classical Kalman filters. dSPM reconstructions consistently encompassed the amygdala–hippocampal region but also extended toward more superficial areas such as the cingulate gyrus. This likely reflects limitations related to the noise-covariance–based standardization, which appears less effective than sLORETA-type resolution-matrix standardization in mitigating depth bias. In contrast, the UNG beamforming method provides more focal source reconstructions than other SPM methods. By estimating non-target activity from the signal covariance, this spatial filtering method produces more focused reconstructions.

The value of spatiotemporal tracking of epileptic source dynamics lies in its ability to identify the onset of epileptic abnormalities and seizures, thereby enabling precise delineation of the epileptogenic zone for potential therapeutic interventions. Among all conventional inverse approaches, SKF was the only method that consistently revealed the earliest temporal involvement of the left amygdala, compared with other regions of interest, during IES. SKF revealed an amygdala-leading activity of approximately 4 ms, detectable at both the onset and the peak of the IES time-series curve (Fig. 2(d)). This 4 ms delay closely matched the 6 ms latency measured at the onset of IES in sEEG. This spatiotemporal precision represents a substantial methodological improvement, allowing a more physiologically meaningful interpretation of epileptic dynamics. 

Overall, our findings support SKF as a powerful and reliable framework for mapping deep and dynamic epileptogenic generators in clinical contexts. By combining robustness, temporal resolution, and depth sensitivity, SKF holds promise for noninvasive presurgical evaluation and advancing the understanding of the complex dynamics of epileptic networks.
