Introduction

Resting-state alpha oscillations are widely used to study large-scale functional organization in the human brain (Hipp et al., 2012). EEG signals, however, reflect a combination of rhythmic oscillations and a broadband aperiodic 1/f component that varies across subjects, brain states, and recording locations (He, 2014; Donoghue et al., 2020). This aperiodic structure influences the shape of the power spectrum and can bias estimates of oscillatory activity if not explicitly modeled.

Estimates of EEG functional connectivity are sensitive to such spectral properties and to preprocessing choices more generally. Broadband signal components and shared spectral structure can introduce spurious correlations, affecting the magnitude and spatial pattern of inferred connectivity (Colclough et al., 2016; Palva et al., 2018). As a result, differences in how oscillatory and aperiodic activity are separated may substantially alter connectivity estimates, even when using simple correlation-based measures.

Recent methods provide  ways to separate oscillatory and aperiodic components of neural signals. Spectral parameterization approaches such as FOOOF model the power spectrum as an aperiodic background with superimposed oscillatory peaks (Donoghue et al., 2020), while time-domain models such as the Decomposed Oscillator Model (DecOsc) isolate individual oscillatory components directly from the signal. Despite increasing use of these methods, their effects on alpha-band EEG connectivity estimates have not been systematically compared.

Here, we examine how different approaches of separating the aperiodic component influence alpha-band functional connectivity in resting-state EEG. By comparing connectivity derived from raw signals, SpecParam-corrected data, and DecOsc-derived oscillatory time series, we assess how preprocessing choices shape correlation-based connectivity patterns.

Methods

EEG Data Acquisition

EEG Acquisition: Resting-state EEG was recorded from 60 healthy participants (mean age = 26 years) in a noise-isolated room at the Cognitive Brain Dynamics Laboratory, National Brain Research Centre. Participants completed a five-minute eyes-open recording while viewing a fixation cross on a blank screen.EEG was acquired using a 63-channel BrainVision actiCHamp system arranged according to the 10–20 layout, sampled at 1000 Hz, referenced at Cz, grounded at AFz, with electrode impedances maintained below 10 kΩ using SuperVisc gel to ensure stable and low-noise contact.

Data Preprocessing

Data Preprocessing: EEG preprocessing was performed in EEGLAB. Continuous data were downsampled to 100 Hz and band-pass filtered between 1–45 Hz. Bad channels were visually inspected and interpolated, and the data were re-referenced to the common average. The continuous recordings were then segmented into non-overlapping epochs, and independent component analysis (ICA) was applied to identify and remove ocular, muscular, and other physiological artifacts. The resulting cleaned epoched data were used for spectral parameterization and aperiodic-removal analyses.

Signal  Decomposition

The EEG signal at sensor was modeled as a superposition of oscillatory and aperiodic components.

                                     

Frequency domain Aperiodic removal (SpecParam)

Power spectral density was estimated for each sensor using Welch’s method. Spectra were parameterized using SpecParam, which models the signal as the sum of an aperiodic 1/f background and Gaussian-shaped oscillatory peaks. The fitted aperiodic component was removed, and oscillatory activity within the alpha band (8–12 Hz) was retained for subsequent connectivity estimation.

Time Domain Decomposition (DecOsc)

In parallel, aperiodic removal was performed in the time domain using the Decomposed Oscillator Model (DecOsc). This model represents the EEG signal as a superposition of latent oscillatory components and a broadband aperiodic background. The estimated aperiodic component was removed to obtain oscillatory-only time series, from which alpha-band activity was extracted.

Connectivity estimation 

Sensor-level functional connectivity between sensors i and j was quantified using Pearson correlation of alpha-band (8–12 Hz), 1/f–corrected EEG time series. Connectivity matrices were computed separately for each preprocessing pipeline to enable direct comparison of connectivity structure across methods

Linking Time and Frequency Domains

Time-domain and frequency-domain analyses were related through Parseval’s identity, which ensures that signal energy is conserved between the time and frequency representations. This provides a principled basis for comparing connectivity estimates derived from SpecParam- and DecOsc-processed signals.

Results

Spectral decomposition demonstrated that both SpecParam and DecOsc successfully separated oscillatory and aperiodic components of the EEG signal, yielding distinct representations of alpha-band activity (Fig. A–B). Frequency-domain parameterization identified Gaussian-shaped peaks superimposed on an aperiodic 1/f background, whereas time-domain decomposition isolated latent oscillatory components across a range of frequencies.

Group level Sensor-level alpha-band connectivity patterns differed systematically across preprocessing pipelines (Fig. C–D). Connectivity estimated from SpecParam-corrected signals exhibited widespread positive correlations across sensors, whereas DecOsc-based signals showed altered connectivity structure with reduced global coupling and increased spatial differentiation. Direct comparison of connectivity matrices indicated that modeling and removal of aperiodic activity substantially influenced the magnitude and spatial organization of alpha-band envelope correlations.

Conclusion

This study shows that estimates of alpha-band functional connectivity depend strongly on the approach used to model and remove aperiodic activity. By directly comparing frequency-domain spectral parameterization (SpecParam) and time-domain generative decomposition (DecOsc), we demonstrate that different models for separating oscillatory and aperiodic components lead to distinct connectivity representations. These results suggest that apparent connectivity patterns may partly reflect broadband signal structure rather than oscillatory interactions alone, highlighting the importance of explicitly accounting for aperiodic activity in resting-state EEG network studies.
