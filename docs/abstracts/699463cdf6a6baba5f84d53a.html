Introduction

Although brain aging is widely recognized as a complex, nonlinear, and highly individual process, most current brain age analyses still assume that deviations from expected norms are equally informative across the entire lifespan1,2,3. This overlooks the fact that the implications of premature aging effects can differ across the lifespan, potentially masking clinically relevant information. We hypothesize that interpreting brain age gaps in relation to each individual’s chronological age more accurately reflects meaningful norm deviations and clinical risk. To test this, we compare how well brain age-based risk markers detect neuropsychiatric diseases both with and without age-specific interpretation, demonstrating the added value of age-sensitive analysis for the detection of disease-relevant norm deviations.

Methods

Using a Monte Carlo resampling framework, we trained 100 brain age models on T1‑weighted structural MRI scans from cognitively unimpaired participants drawn from three large, publicly available cohorts, ADNI, OASIS‑3, and multiple OpenNeuro datasets (n=9,300 healthy individuals). In each of 100 iterations, we drew a random training set by stratified sampling across age, sex, and study. In each iteration, we applied the trained models to independent samples from AIBL, FTLDNI, and the MACS study (n=2,600), including individuals with Alzheimer’s disease (AD), mild cognitive impairment (MCI), frontotemporal dementia (FTD), and major depressive disorder (MDD). Structural MRI data were processed using the CAT12 toolbox, yielding unsmoothed gray‑matter tissue maps in standard MNI space. Brain age models were based on the Simple Fully Convolutional Network (SFCN) architecture, following hyperparameters of Peng et al.4 and the linear output layer proposed by Leonardsen et al.5 Models were trained using mean squared error loss and the Adam optimizer with a learning‑rate scheduler reducing the initial learning rate of 0.01 every 35 epochs over 300 training epochs. Data augmentation (random flips and small spatial shifts) was applied to enhance generalization. Chronological age and predicted age were then used to compute the brain age gap (BAG = predicted age − chronological age) for all individuals. We evaluated the clinical informativeness of BAG as a disease risk marker under three modeling strategies, all based on logistic regression with balanced accuracy as the primary performance metric. First, we train a global risk-marker model for each disease predicting diagnostic status (patient vs. control) from BAG alone. This model reflects the common practice of treating a given BAG as equally meaningful across the adult age range. Second, we extend the global risk-marker model to additionally include chronological age, and sex as predictors, testing whether the demographic information improves the risk estimation relative to BAG alone. Finally, to explicitly focus on age‑specific meaning of the BAG, we estimated disease risk separately within one-year-age bins. For each age group, we trained two logistic models: one using BAG alone and one using BAG plus sex. This age‑wise approach evaluates BAG relative to same-aged peers rather than globally across the entire sample.

Results

When used in isolation, the BAG provided only limited diagnostic information for all diseases except FTD. Balanced accuracy of the BAG-based risk marker was near‑chance performance (0.50-0.51) for AD, MCI and MDD and 0.67 for FTD (see Fig. 1). Augmenting BAG with age and sex as covariates consistently improved global risk‑marker performance. Balanced accuracy increased 5% for AD, 4% for MCI and MDD, and 3% for FTD, suggesting that part of the disease signal is only expressed relative to age and sex rather than in the raw BAG alone. Age‑wise interpretation of BAG further enhanced diagnostic performance beyond the global covariate-enriched models. When BAG was evaluated within 1-year-bin age groups, balanced accuracy rose to 0.60 for AD, 0.72 for FTD, 0.56 for MCI, and 0.61 for MDD. Expressed as absolute gains over the global BAG‑only marker, age‑wise BAG improved balanced accuracy by approximately 5% for FTD, 6% for MCI, 9% for AD and 10% for MDD. Incorporating sex within these age‑wise models yielded very similar or slightly higher performance (AD 0.60, FTD 0.73, MCI 0.57, MDD 0.62), indicating that the major improvement comes from acknowledging age‑specific meaning of BAG, with sex providing a smaller additional refinement.

Conclusion

Taken together, these findings support our hypothesis that brain age gaps are most informative when interpreted relative to an individual’s chronological age. Rather than treating a fixed deviation as uniformly abnormal across adulthood, modeling BAG relative to chronological age and demographic context substantially enhances disease discrimination, particularly for disorders with more subtle structural alterations. This highlights the importance of incorporating age‑sensitive analysis frameworks when developing and deploying brain age markers in neuropsychiatric research and, ultimately, for risk stratification in clinical settings.
