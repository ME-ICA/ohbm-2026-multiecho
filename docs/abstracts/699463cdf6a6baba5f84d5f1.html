Introduction

Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) is used to investigate changes in functional connectivity, defined as the temporal correlation of BOLD signal fluctuations across brain regions1. Rs-fMRI is commonly used to study these changes, but imaging protocols must balance animal welfare while still acquiring high-quality data. Many studies rely on anesthetized imaging to reduce motion and decrease stress; however, anesthesia can influence synchrony across brain regions and lead to muted or fragmented connectivity patterns. To improve translational validity, awake imaging in animal models could be preferable.

In addition to affecting functional connectivity, anesthetized protocols can also influence the subsequent testing of cognitive processes such as attention. Attention refers to the ability to sustain focus over time and plays a key role in learning, performance, and problem-solving2. It is often disrupted in neuropsychiatric disorders, including depression, anxiety, autism, and schizophrenia2. Since anesthesia can impact brain function, it is important to understand how awake vs. anesthetized imaging protocols affect subsequent measurements of cognitive processes such as attention.

To observe changes in attention in mice, we used the Continuous Performance Test (CPT), which measures attentional changes using metrics such as hit rate, false alarm rate, discrimination sensitivity (d′), and response bias (c)3. In this study, we examined CPT performance alongside resting-state fMRI to investigate how repeated imaging of animals in an awake vs. anesthetized protocol may influence attention and functional connectivity. Comparing performance across longitudinal timepoints taking sex into account allows us to relate connectivity patterns to behaviour and identify which imaging protocol best preserves functional connectivity and cognition.

Methods

Seventy-two C57BL/6J mice (12 males and 12 females per protocol group) were assigned to an awake imaging protocol, an anesthetized imaging protocol, or a CPT-only control. This longitudinal design included two imaging exposure timepoints (3-month and 12-month resting-state fMRI) and two behavioural timepoints (6-month and 12-month CPT performance). All procedures followed institutional animal care guidelines. 

Awake imaging protocol:
Mice in the awake group underwent stereotaxic headpost implantation followed by a one-week recovery period and an 8-day MRI acclimation protocol. Acclimation sessions progressively increased from 15 to 90 minutes, with scanner audio gradually introduced (50–120 dB). During scanning, mice were head-fixed, monitored through a infrared-light camera, on a Bruker 15.2T preclinical scanner with a Neo console running Paravision 360-3.5 (Bruker BioSpin Corp, Billerica, MA) for approximately 1.5 hours.

Anesthetized imaging protocol:
Mice in the anesthetized group were scanned at the same timepoints under isoflurane anesthesia (4% induction, 1.5–2% maintenance). Respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, and temperature (~37.5 °C) were continuously monitored. After imaging, mice recovered under a heat source and received 1-2 ml of warm saline subcutaneously.

fMRI acquisition:
Resting-state fMRI was acquired using a T2*-weighted EPI sequence (TR = 1.2 s, TE = 10 ms, flip angle = 50°). Images were collected at 0.1562 × 0.1562 × 0.5 mm3 resolution with 600 volumes. Both awake and anesthetized animals were scanned using identical acquisition parameters. Scans were approximately 1.5 hours, and 4 runs were acquired per subject, 12 minutes per run. 

CPT behavioural testing:
All mice completed the Continuous Performance Test (CPT) touchscreen task at 6 and 12 months to assess sustained attention changes. Mice were food-restricted to 85–90% of baseline weight and trained through all CPT stages. During testing, mice discriminated between target (S+) and non-target (S–) stimuli. CPT metrics included hit rate, false-alarm rate, discrimination sensitivity (d′), and response bias (c). Probe sessions were completed by repeating stage 4 of CPT training using shortened stimulus durations (4 s, 1 s, 0.5 s, 0.2 s), with re-baseline sessions between probes.

Preprocessing and analysis:
All fMRI data were preprocessed using the RABIES pipeline, including motion correction, skull stripping, spatial smoothing, regression of motion-related signals, temporal filtering, and automated quality control4. High-motion volumes were identified and removed prior to analysis.

Preprocessed datasets were submitted to group independent component analysis (ICA) using FSL MELODIC. Subject-specific spatial maps and time courses were obtained using dual regression. Voxel-wise between-group comparisons were performed on the dual-regression spatial maps using PALM (Permutation Analysis of Linear Models)5. Family-wise error (FWE) correction was applied to control for multiple comparisons.

CPT behavioural data were analyzed in GraphPad Prism. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Group differences were evaluated using two-way ANOVA with appropriate factors (e.g., Sex × Protocol or Timepoint × Protocol), and Greenhouse–Geisser corrections were applied when sphericity assumptions were violated. Post hoc comparisons were performed using Sidak tests where applicable. Behavioural metrics (hit rate, false alarms, d′, and c) were compared across protocol groups and timepoints to assess protocol-related effects on attentional performance.

Results

Rs-fMRI data showed clear differences between the awake and anesthetized protocols. Across both timepoints, awake mice showed more clustering in medial prefrontal, cingulate, hippocampal, and striatal regions, reflecting more preserved synchrony across those brain regions. In contrast, anesthetized mice showed weaker and more fragmented clusters, especially in hippocampal and striatal regions. These patterns are consistent with the prior networks described in the RABIES paper.

Sex differences were also evident. Females consistently showed larger and more preserved clusters than males, most notably in the cingulate cortex and hippocampal regions, regardless of imaging state. In anesthetized mice, fragmentation effects were more prominent in males, compared to females, especially in cingulate and hippocampal areas. 

Connectivity patterns differed across exposure timepoints in a protocol-dependent manner. In anesthetized males, 12-month maps showed more clustering in the cingulate and striatum compared to 3 months, whereas anesthetized females showed less clustering in attention-related regions across the same timepoints. Despite these differences, the overall DMN pattern were very similar across both sexes at both timepoints. 

CPT behavioural results showed protocol-dependent differences in sustained attention. At 6 months, a two-way ANOVA (Protocol × Stimulus Duration) showed a significant main effect of protocol on Hit Rate (p < 0.05). Anesthetized mice performed worse than both awake and CPT-only controls at high attentional demands (1 s, 0.5 s, 0.2 s; p < 0.05). False Alarm Rate was also higher in the anesthetized group relative to controls (p < 0.05), while awake-imaged mice performed similarly to controls. Females showed the same pattern, with anesthetized animals performing worse at 1 s and below (p < 0.05). Discrimination sensitivity (d′) decreased most strongly in the anesthetized group at short durations (p < 0.05).

At 12 months, protocol-related behavioural differences persisted. Anesthetized males showed a drop in discrimination sensitivity (d′) at 1 s, 0.5 s, and 0.2 s (p < 0.05), along with higher false alarm rates at the shortest durations. CPT-only controls maintained the strongest performance across all durations. The single awake-imaged male aligned more closely with controls, though full cohort completion is required for statistical analysis.

In females, 12-month probe performance showed very similar patterns between anesthetized and CPT-control groups across most stimulus durations. Discrimination sensitivity (d′) declined as stimulus duration shortened for both groups, and large overlaps were observed at 4 s, 2 s, 0.5 s, and 0.2 s. A small separation appeared at the 1-s duration, where anesthetized females showed slightly lower d′ than controls. Aside from this difference, overall performance across the protocol group and the controls, a complete cohort would be needed to run statistical analysis and to better correlate the differences across protocol groups and controls. 

Across imaging and behaviour, a similar pattern was observed. When clustering was lower in attention-related areas such as the medial prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, and hippocampus, CPT performance under high attentional load was also affected and was low, particularly in the anesthetized protocol. Awake imaging showed more clustering at younger ages, and this aligned with stronger CPT performance. Females showed more consistent clustering across these brain regions compared to males, and their behavioural performance was more consistent across protocols.

Conclusion

This study shows that the choice of awake vs. anesthetized imaging protocol influences both functional connectivity and sustained attention in mice. Awake imaging preserved synchrony across brain regions and this observation coincided with stronger CPT performance, especially at younger ages. The anesthetized protocol showed less clustering and lower performance under high attentional load, with these effects more noticeable at the 12-month timepoint.

Sex differences were also evident. Females showed more consistent synchrony across the medial prefrontal cortex, cingulate cortex, hippocampus, and striatum and maintained a consistent CPT performance across protocol groups compared to males. By contrast, males in the anesthetized protocol showed steeper declines in discrimination sensitivity as stimulus duration shortened.

Together, these findings underline that protocol group, sex, and age all contribute to differences in connectivity and behaviour in animal models. When combining rs-fMRI with attention tasks, selecting an imaging approach that minimally affects cognition is important, particularly in longitudinal studies.
