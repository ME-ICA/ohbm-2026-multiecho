Introduction

Sex bias is widely present in preclinical research, it is frequently ignored or poorly reported1, despite evidence of sexual dimorphism in gene expression in the brain2, differences in disease phenotypes and response to treatment3,4. In humans, multiple neurodevelopmental disorders are more prevalent in males than females, while depression, anxiety, and Alzheimer’s disease are more prevalent in females than in males5. Taking into account sex as a biological variable of interest and exploring its impact on the outcomes of experiments is thus highly justified, and has been recently more widely advocated for6. Recent work has shed a cross-species translational light on the congruence between volumetric sex differences across homologous brain regions between the mouse and the human, and this congruence correlated with cross-species gene expression homology7. However, direct comparison of whole mouse and human brains with their respective patterns of regional sex differences has yet to be undertaken.

Methods

We analysed the volumetric sex-differences between the mouse and the human from Guma et al., by bringing both brains into a latent space for direct, quantitative comparison. The latent space is built by training a deep learning algorithm on mouse gene expression data8 with the task of reconstructing a faithful representation of the input, while classifying latent vectors into brain regions9. After training, the model is used as a projection from gene space to latent space, where both mouse and human data are represented. By computing a latent similarity matrix, we establish a bridge between mouse brain space and human brain space, which can be used to translate data in both directions. We then used permutation-based statistical comparisons in volume space between translated data and real data. We also performed a latent statistical approach, whereby significant areas from both species presenting male bias on one hand, or female bias on another hand, are mapped onto the latent space. Using permutations, we assessed whether the distance between these significant areas in latent space is smaller than what would occur by chance, as a means to check whether sex biases correlate between species in a quantitative manner.

Results

The mouse and human sex-differences data were taken from the results of Guma et al., 2024. The human data were remapped to the AHBA atlas10 for compatibility with the cross-species latent projection analysis. In the human, after correcting for total brain volume, significant female-biased regions were found in the frontal and cingulate cortex, in the primary somatosensory and motor cortex, in auditory areas, in parahippocampal areas, and nucleus accumbens. Human male biased regions included visual areas, parieto-occipital areas, medial prefrontal areas, piriform cortex, insula, as well as the cerebellum, brainstem, hippocampus, amygdala, and thalamus-hypothalamus. In the mouse, the cerebellum was mostly female-biased, as was the caudoputamen, the insula, the auditory and somatomotor areas, the frontal and orbital areas, and the entorhinal cortex. Male-bias was found in the olfactory bulb, the hippocampus, brainstem, amygdala, thalamus and hypothalamus.

In both the latent space statistical analysis and the translational statistical analysis, we found that on average, sex-differences correlated significantly across species (p < 0.05). 

Conclusion

We used a novel cross-species common space embedding approach to further explore the statistical results of an existing sex differences study of regional brain volume variations. Rather than comparing the results between the species in qualitative manner, we performed a quantitative statistical analysis to formally establish the translatability of the results. Both the translational and the latent space approaches showed that the similarity across species between the regions that highlighted specific sex effects was higher than what would be expected by chance, evaluated through permutations.  Interestingly, some regions appear as having opposite sex-bias, such as the cerebellum. Such differences likely cause the effect size of the cross-species statistics to remain small yet significant, especially for male biased regions (correlation = 0.2 for male biased regions in the translational approach, 0.10 in the latent space approach). 

Taken together, these results provide validation for the model developed for cross-species embedding, beyond simple anatomical translations.
