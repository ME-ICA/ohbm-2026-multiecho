Introduction

Gliomas often lead to profound functional deficits, with cognitive impairment imposing a particularly heavy medical and socioeconomic burden.1 The human frontal lobe plays a central role in regulating higher-order cognitive functions, such as decision-making, attention, and executive control.2 However, it is still unclear how frontal gliomas cause cognitive impairment. In fact, cognitive impairment is a complex symptom that cannot be explained solely by focal brain damage.3 In the present study, we applied a diffusion MRI (dMRI) technique, a unique neuroimaging tool for mapping brain white matter (WM) structural connectivity in vivo, to investigate cognitive impairment in patients with frontal gliomas. Different network-based analytical tools, such as threshold-free network-based statistics (TFNBS),4 enable connectome-wide inference to identify groups of connections exhibiting significant alterations while controlling for family-wise error (FWE), thereby offering a robust method to map and quantify distributed network dysfunction. Using TFNBS, a previous study has shown that glioma-related alterations in WM structural connectivity can disrupt information transfer across distributed brain regions.5 However, it is still unclear how these specific connectivity alterations relate to deficits in distinct higher-order cognitive functions in glioma patients. 

The present study aims to address how glioma-related alterations in WM structural connectivity affect domain-specific cognitive deficits in patients with frontal gliomas. To this end, we employed a dMRI technique to map individual WM structural connectomes and applied TFNBS to detect the altered subnetworks in patients. We also analyzed the relationship between these connectivity changes and the performance on cognitive tasks. We hypothesized that glioma-induced disruptions in brain WM structural connectivity are associated with specific cognitive deficits.

Methods

Subjects

This study included 35 adult presurgical glioma patients (14 F/21 M, mean age = 43.82 ± 12.51 years, aged 23-70 years old). The gliomas were located predominantly in the frontal lobe, with the majority of lesion volume localized to either the left (nL = 21) or right (nR = 14) hemisphere. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center. All subjects provided written informed consent prior to the study.

Behavioral assessment

Cognitive performance for each patient was assessed across multiple domains, including sustained attention, working memory, inhibitory control, and cognitive flexibility. Patients completed a battery of standardized neuropsychological tasks, including the Psychomotor Vigilance Test (PVT), a 1-back task, a Go/No-Go task, and a Rule-Shift task.

MRI data acquisition

The preoperative MRI data were acquired on a 3.0 T GE Discovery MR750 scanner. High-resolution T1-weighted brain structural images were acquired using a T1-weighted 3D BRAVO sequence. The dMRI data were obtained using a spin-echo multi-shell diffusion-weighted EPI sequence in two phase-encoding directions (PA and AP). Additionally, high-resolution T2-weighted brain structural images were obtained with a T2-weighted 3D CUBE sequence. All the data were acquired in the same session for each subject.

Data preprocessing and streamline tractography

Fig. 1 shows the workflow for the data processing for each subject. First, tumor segmentation was performed manually in the native space using ITK-SNAP (v4.2.0) based on the T1-weighted and T2-weighted images. Subsequently, T1-weighted images underwent preprocessing for skull-stripping and bias field correction using ANTs (v2.6.3) and FreeSurfer (v7.4.1). These images were then used in MRtrix3 (v3.0.5) to generate five-tissue-type (5TT) images, where lesion masks were manually assigned to pathological tissue. In parallel, the preprocessing of dMRI data included the following steps: (1) denoising and Gibbs-ringing artifact removal using MRtrix3, (2) correction of eddy currents, EPI distortions, and motion using FSL (v6.0.7.13), and (3) N4 bias-field correction using ANTs. Next, a probabilistic tractography was performed with the 2nd-order integration over fiber orientation distributions (iFOD2) algorithm and additional usage of the anatomically constrained tractography (ACT) framework using the 5TT image. The whole-brain tractogram was filtered with spherical-deconvolution informed filtering of tractograms (SIFT).

Connectome construction

For each subject, we constructed the brain structural network based on the Automated Anatomical Labeling 3 (AAL3) atlas, which parcellated the whole brain into 170 cortical and subcortical regions. Each region was defined as a node, and the inter-nodal structural connectivity strength was defined as the edge. The edge is quantified by the number of streamlines connecting the two nodes. After whole brain tractogram generation and filtering, we constructed WM structural connectome networks by mapping the streamlines to the 170 regions defined by AAL3. To compare between-hemispheric differences in network parameters, we partitioned each matrix into two separate matrices, the ipsilesional and contralesional 80 × 80 matrices, excluding the midline region of the brain.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analysis of network edges was performed using MRtrix3, implementing a non-parametric permutation testing (5,000 permutations) with the TFNBS algorithm. Based on the edges identified by TFNBS, we extracted three connectivity measures per subject, contralesional connectivity strength, ipsilesional connectivity strength, and their interhemispheric difference in connectivity strength. We estimated Pearson's correlation between each of these three measures and the tumor volume or behavioral performance. The significance threshold was set at p < 0.05 after false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple comparisons.

Results

Fig. 2a shows a significant reduction in WM structural connectivity in the ipsilesional hemisphere relative to the contralesional hemisphere, with 164 edges showing decreased connectivity. Fig. 2b shows that only 3 edges exhibited increased connectivity in the ipsilesional hemisphere.

Fig. 2c shows the relationship between the WM structural connectivity and the tumor volume or behavioral performance for edges exhibiting significantly reduced ipsilesional connectivity. We found that each of the 92 edges showed a negative correlation with the tumor volume, consistent with mass effect and tissue infiltration of structural pathways. In contrast, the contralesional connectivity strength of 27 edges showed a positive correlation with the tumor volume, suggesting compensatory reorganization in the opposite hemisphere. At the behavioral level, the ipsilesional connectivity strength of 32 edges was positively associated with accuracy in the incongruent condition of the Rule-Shift task. This suggests that a subset of structurally preserved ipsilesional connections continues to support cognitive performance. Conclusion

We constructed brain white matter structural networks in patients with frontal lobe gliomas and assessed their clinical and cognitive performance. By analyzing the properties of brain white matter structural connectivity networks, we detected glioma-induced structural connectivity disruption in the patients. We found that gliomas located in the cognitively pivotal prefrontal cortex induce widespread reorganization of structural connectivity. This involves a large-scale decrease and focal aberrant increases in ipsilesional connectivity, as well as compensatory alterations in the contralateral hemisphere. 

The present study offers a network-level explanation for cognitive impairment in specific domains, particularly executive functions, in patients with frontal gliomas. This insight underscores the clinical value of brain white matter connectomics. Preoperative mapping of individual connectomes allows for the precise identification and preservation of connections distant from the gliomas, which are crucial for maintaining frontal lobe-associated cognitive functions. Our findings offer a novel perspective for neurosurgical planning aimed at cognitive preservation.
