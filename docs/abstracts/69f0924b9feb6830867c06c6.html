Introduction

Objective social participation refers to an individual physically taking part in social activities and is associated with better life quality1 and mental health outcomes. Despite this, the neurobiological underpinnings of social participation are poorly understood. Identifying brain phenotypes influencing social participation in healthy populations may help understand social trajectories in psychiatric disorders and inform wider social health interventions. 

Variability in brain structure and function has been previously associated with objective social phenotypes such as social activity participation, social engagement, and social network size. Various brain regions have been implicated in these findings including grey matter volume of the amygdala and anterior cingulate, fractional anisotropy in the cingulum and corpus callosum, and functional connectivity in the default and frontoparietal networks. However, findings from these investigations are extremely variable, with methodological inconsistencies, regionally limited hypothesis-driven approaches and small sample sizes contributing to bias in identifying key regions. 

Data-driven variable selection may overcome these limitations, facilitating unbiased replication and discovery of social brain regions based on patterns in the data, without hypothesis constraints.2 The present investigation uses a data-driven feature selection method to identify multi-modal brain imaging-derived phenotypes (IDPs) that best explain social participation in a large, older population. We additionally utilize an independent sub-sample to compare these to literature-identified IDPs in explaining social participation variation in the UK Biobank dataset. 

Methods

In 37,576 participants (mean age 65 ± 8, 53% female) with structural and functional magnetic resonance (MR) data, a social participation score (range 0-10) was derived by combining leisure activity participation [Data-Field 6160] and friend/family visits [Data Field 1031]. 

Pre-processed IDPs derived from a pipeline developed and run on behalf of the UK Biobank were used (September 2022, v1.9 data release). Structural brain IDPs consisted of measures of cortical thickness, area, and volume derived using the Destrieux a2009s parcellation, and subcortical volume and intensity were derived using the Freesurfer subcortical segmentation from T1-weighted MR images. Fractional anisotropy values were derived from diffusion-weighted MR images using both Tract-Based Spatial Statistics and probabilistic tractography. Finally, resting-state functional MR-derived IDPs consisted of Fisher-transformed partial correlation values between independent-component analysis-derived components provided by the UK Biobank. These are referred to throughout as within- and between-network functional connectivity edges. Within-network edges consisted of the partial correlation between components of the same network and between-network consisted of partial correlations between components of two different networks.

            In order to compare  data-driven and literature-driven methods in explaining social participation variance, two subsets of IDPs were derived. Firstly, a literature review was conducted to identify a subset of brain regions previously associated with social measures. A search strategy of multiple literature databases was employed, and studies were selected if they used an objective social phenotype and a general/non-psychiatric population. Any studies not utilizing a whole-brain approach or IDP measurements corresponding to UK Biobank IDPs were excluded. 

Secondly, Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) was used to select a subset of IDPs in 25% (n=9,394) of the participant sample. Prior to feature selection, IDPs were scaled and assessed for multicollinearity where any IDPs with an R2 value > 0.80 were averaged. RFE 3 is a supervised feature selection method for deriving an optimal subset of independent variables that best explain variance in an outcome of interest. This study used 10-fold cross-validated RFE (5 repeats) with a 100-fold boot-strapping loop to select a sub-set of IDPs that best explain variance in social participation. Any IDPs selected as predictors of SP across > 50% of the iterations were carried into the regression analysis.

Hierarchical regression in the remaining 75% (n=28,152) compared whether the data-selected or literature-identified IDP subsets explained more variance in social participation, covarying for age, sex, years of education, and head size. Individual p-values were corrected for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate with the q-value R package. 

Results

Literature associations identified 137 IDPs previously associated with objective social phenotypes. In parallel, RFE selected 198 IDPs, 42 of which (35.6%) overlapped with the literature-identified subset. The RFE-selected IDPs explained significantly more social participation variance (1.33%) than the literature (0.85%; F=3.36, p<0.001). Within this, resting-state fMRI-derived edges contributed the highest additional variance explained across imaging types (R2=0.85%). 

Several IDPs were significantly associated with social participation in the independent regression analysis after FDR-correction. Associated structural IDPs included the thickness of the mid-posterior cingulate (β = 0.05, q-value = 0.001) and superior insular sulcus (β = -0.045, q-value = 0.002). Significant functional IDPs additionally included functional connectivity between the pericentral with medial-frontoparietal and cerebellar networks, and between the medial- with the medial-occipital and lateral-frontoparietal networks (Figure 1). No IDPs derived from diffusion-weighted MRI were significantly associated after correction.

Conclusion

Adopting a data-driven multimodal imaging approach, we show that pericentral, medial fronto-parietal and limbic regions can explain small but significant social participation variations. Anatomically, these findings suggest that social participation in an older population may relate to functions such as goal-directed and self-referential processing as well as theory-of-mind. Leveraging a large sample size with an out-of-sample validation procedure and considering a variety of multimodal brain-imaging phenotypes, the present study overcomes many of the limitations present in existing literature linking the brain with social phenotypes.

We additionally demonstrate that data-driven feature selection outperforms a literature-driven approach, highlighting that methods like RFE combined with large datasets may be advantageous when investigating brain-behavior associations. Notably, regions such as the amygdala were more frequently reported in region-of-interest than whole-brain investigations, suggesting their relevance may be reliant on approach and amplified by hypothesis-driven methods. The small effects detected in this study highlight that brain IDPs may not be as strongly associated with social phenotypes as previously argued. This likely reflects a limit to the neural contribution, with additional factors such as environmental, psychosocial and cognitive influences potentially offering additional explanatory value. 

 Many of the neural regions implicated in our findings overlap with those consistently linked with social cognition, such as the medial fronto-parietal (or default mode) network.4 Social-cognitive abilities have previously been shown to mediate associations between the brain and social phenotypes.5 Therefore, future investigations leveraging longitudinal data and integrating cognition in a younger sample may provide deeper insights the neural basis of social behavior. Overall, these findings highlight the value of complimentary data-driven approaches used in parallel with previous knowledge to consolidate the neural bases of the social brain network.
