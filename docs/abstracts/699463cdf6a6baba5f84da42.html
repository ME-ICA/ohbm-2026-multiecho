Introduction

Functional MRI (fMRI) studies of brain activations elicited during working memory (WM) have primarily focused on WM load, defined as the number of information streams maintained simultaneously. However, cognitive control (CC) represents another critical dimension of WM that remains relatively understudied. Switching and updating constitute two core components of CC. Switching refers to the ability to flexibly shift between multiple information streams, whereas updating involves monitoring and revising information content as the stream evolves. Prior work from our lab indicates that these CC components are differentially modulated by factors such as predictability, age, and expertise, and that they rely on partially distinct neural substrates.1,2 However, existing studies have been limited to paradigms involving only two information streams. It therefore remains unclear whether CC-related neural responses scale with increasing WM load, and whether these effects are domain-general or domain-specific. The present investigation is guided by the hierarchical Theory of WM Adaptability, which provides a framework for examining how control demands interact with load across levels of task complexity.3

Methods

Twenty-four young adults (18–35 years; 14 male, 10 female; mean age = 24.7 years) participated in the study. All participants were right-handed, had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, and were MRI-safe. Each visit included informed consent and screening, cognitive testing, a mock MRI session, and MRI scanning. During scanning, participants performed an unpredictable n-match task with a hybrid block and event-related design, modified from prior work.1 Working memory (WM) load was manipulated by the number of possible stimulus locations (0-, 2-, and 3-match): one central location (0), left/right locations (2), or left/right/upper-center locations (3). Stimulus domain was manipulated using two stimulus types (digits vs birds), presented in separate blocks. Each run contained all load × domain conditions, yielding six blocks per condition. Participants completed three runs in counterbalanced order.

Structural MPRAGE and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) data were also acquired. Functional MRI data were preprocessed using fMRIPrep v20.2.1. Whole-brain effects were estimated using a three-level general linear model (GLM), from the individual to the group level (voxel-wise Z > 2.3; cluster-corrected p < .05). Mean percent signal change was extracted from load-sensitive (2 + 3 > 0) and domain-sensitive (Birds > Digits) contrasts for block-design analyses. Event-related analyses yielded switching- and updating-sensitive contrasts for the 2- and 3-match conditions. Behavioral performance and ROI-level BOLD responses were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models, with fixed effects of load, domain, switching, and updating, and random intercepts for subjects.

Results

Accuracy showed a significant non-linear effect of WM load. Relative to the 0-match condition, accuracy decreased significantly in the 2-match condition (β = –0.084, p < .001) and remained lower in the 3-match condition (β = –0.076, p = .002), with no further significant decrease from 2- to 3-match (p = .76). In contrast, reaction time (RT) exhibited a more linear increase with WM load, with significantly longer responses in both the 2-match (β = 159.33 ms, p < .001) and 3-match (β = 193.07 ms, p < .001) conditions relative to 0-match, as well as a significant increase from 2- to 3-match (p = .008). No significant main effect of stimulus domain was observed for either accuracy (p = .54) or RT (p = .081), and no WM load by stimulus domain interactions were found for either measure (all ps > .18), indicating that WM load effects on behavioral performance were consistent across stimulus domains.

Significant switching, updating, and switch + update costs were observed in the 2- and 3-match conditions. Switch trials were associated with significantly lower accuracy than non-switch trials (β = –0.107, p = .012), whereas updating was associated with higher accuracy (β = 0.095, p = .026). The magnitudes of switching and updating effects were comparable, and the combined switch + update condition exhibited an underadditive pattern, as indicated by the absence of a significant switching × updating interaction in either accuracy or RT (accuracy: p = .264; RT: p = .866).

In terms of RT, updating was associated with a significant response cost (β = 47.30 ms, p = .003), while switching showed a non-significant trend toward increased RT (β = 26.87 ms, p = .087). Importantly, stimulus domain selectively modulated switching, as reflected by a significant switching × stimulus domain interaction (β = 64.29 ms, p = .004), indicating greater switching costs for bird than digit stimuli. No stimulus-domain effects were observed for updating or for switch + update costs, and no three-way interaction reached significance (all ps > .14).

Whole-brain block-design analyses identified seven regions of interest (ROIs) sensitive to WM load, characterized by increased activation in frontoparietal, occipital, and thalamic regions for 2- and 3-match conditions relative to 0-match. Among these ROIs, the lateral occipital cortex exhibited a pattern paralleling behavioral accuracy, with increased activation from 0- to 2-match and no further increase from 2- to 3-match; this pattern was consistent across stimulus domains.

In contrast, domain effects emerged at higher WM loads in frontoparietal and subcortical regions. The left supramarginal gyrus showed greater activation for digits than birds at 2-match, whereas the posterior cingulate cortex (PCC), right middle frontal gyrus (MFG), and thalamus exhibited greater activation for birds than digits at 3-match. Within the PCC specifically, bird stimuli elicited a significant increase in activation from 2- to 3-match, while digit-related activation remained stable.

Additionally, the domain contrast (Birds > Digits) revealed greater engagement of bilateral temporal–occipital regions, including ventral visual cortex, even at 0-match, suggesting domain-specific differences in early visual processing.

Event-related whole-brain analyses are being conducted to identify regions sensitive to switching and updating, and to determine whether these effects are domain-general or domain-specific.

Conclusion

This study investigated how working memory (WM) load and stimulus domain jointly shape behavioral performance and neural responses within a framework that emphasizes cognitive control demands. Behaviorally, increasing WM load produced a non-linear reduction in accuracy alongside a more linear increase in reaction time, indicating that higher WM load primarily imposes processing costs rather than uniformly degrading accuracy. These load effects were comparable across stimulus domains, consistent with a domain-general account of WM performance and aligning with the hierarchical Theory of WM Adaptability.

At the neural level, increasing WM load recruited a network of frontoparietal control regions, occipital cortex, and subcortical structures, reflecting capacity-limited WM engagement. Critically, however, stimulus domain modulated neural responses under higher WM load despite comparable behavioral performance. At the highest WM load of 3-match, regions including the PCC, MFG/paracingulate cortex, and thalamus showed greater activation for birds than digits, with the PCC exhibiting a selective increase from 2- to 3-match for bird stimuli. Domain contrasts further revealed stronger engagement of bilateral temporal–occipital regions, including ventral visual cortex, for bird stimuli across load levels, suggesting sustained domain-specific visual processing demands.

Together, these findings indicate that WM is supported by a domain-general system that scales with WM load, whereas neural responses to increasing WM load vary by stimulus domain, particularly under higher control demands. This emphasizes the importance of examining cognitive control–related neural activity in addition to behavioral measures, and shows that stimulus domain can influence the neural responses of WM load even when behavioral performance appears domain-general.
