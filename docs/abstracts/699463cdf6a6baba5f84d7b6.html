Introduction

Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) is a neurodegenerative disease defined by cognitive decline which can interfere in daily living. At the molecular level, AD is characterized by the accumulation of amyloid-beta (Aβ) plaques and neurofibrillary tangles. The accumulation of Aβ can be detected in cerebrospinal fluid or with Positron Emission Tomography (PET), aiding diagnosis and the identification of preclinical AD. Other techniques, such as functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI), are being explored to better understand AD pathophysiology.1

Resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) measures spontaneous BOLD signal fluctuations to study measures as Functional Connectivity (FC) defined as temporal correlations between brain regions, and amplitude reflecting regional activity. The estimation of these measures typically relies on defining brain nodes (spatial maps) and computing edges via correlation. While anatomical or functional atlases are commonly used, data-driven maps from Independent Component Analysis (ICA) offer diverse advantages. ICA is a blind source separation method that decomposes multivariate signals into additive subcomponents. In fMRI, ICA is applied to identify spatially distinct brain networks characterized by temporally coherent BOLD signal fluctuations.2 Importantly, ICA allows the user to specify the number of components, enabling analyses at different levels of granularity.

Some studies have focused on gaining insight in the AD pathophysiology by studying FC differences between healthy patients and subjects with dementia focusing on more advanced AD stages, but little insight has been made in studying the FC in preclinical stages and how integrative methodological approaches can contribute to the findings. Here, we develop a method to identify and compare network maps across low and high ICA dimensionalities and systematically test their utility in detecting differences and correlations with clinical and demographic data in an early AD cohort.

Methods

Baseline data from the A4 and LEARN studies was used which comprises cognitively unimpaired individuals aged 65-85 years.3 All participants underwent Florbetapir PET imaging to assess cerebral Aβ deposition. Inclusion criteria were equal for both studies and based on cognitive assessments. The mean cortical standardized uptake value ratio (SUVr) was the main discriminative measure for A4 (≥ 1.15) and LEARN (< 1.15).

We used rs-fMRI, T1-weighted MRI and demographic data (age & gender), amyloid values in centiloid (CL) scale, and the Preclinical Alzheimer’s Cognitive Composite (PACC) score to measure cognition. Only subjects with all three images were included. Imaging data was transformed to BIDS4.

The rs-fMRI inclusion acquisition parameter criteria consisted of having a TR = 3s and number of volumes = 200. Images were preprocessed with an in-house developed pipeline including brain extraction, anatomical segmentation, slice timing correction, when possible, motion correction, spatial registration to the MNI152 space, temporal filtering by a highpass filter (0.01Hz), normalization of the time series to unit variance and nuisance regression to remove CSF and WM components. All steps were implemented using available functions in FSL, HD-BET and ANTS, and functions directly implemented in Python.

A Quality Control (QC) was implemented including an automatic step identifying gross segmentation errors, and a visual QC consisting of individualized reports. 3 reviewer(s) reviewed all subjects’ reports and accepted them using a Yes/Maybe/No pass button. Majority voting between the 3 decisions determined the final decision. 

rs-fMRI images were decomposed into statistically independent spatial patterns with ICA. Individual preprocessed rs-fMRI scans were concatenated into a single 4D file before decomposition. To manage computational complexity, random subsets of 150 subjects per cohort were selected (n = 300). The number of ICA components was manually specified. Z-score normalization was applied to the components obtained voxel-wise and component-wise.

An ICA of 30 components (ICA30) was conducted as a reference according to literature in the field and previous studies in the group5.  The resulting components were evaluated by 3 experts to determine whether they were Resting State Networks (RSNs) or not. We assigned functional labels to each RSN: Default Mode Network (DMN), Executive Networks (EN), Sensorimotor Network (SM), Salience network (SN), Subcortical Network (SC) and Visual Network (VN).

Another ICA of 150 components (ICA150) was conducted and matched to ICA30 by majority voting between Dice Similarity Coefficient (DICE), the Spatial Correlation Coefficient (SCC) and the Mutual Information (MI). If a tie happened, the highest score between DICE and SCC selected the final decision.

Dual regression was employed to obtain subject-specific timeseries for each network using the FSL tool dual regression (https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl). Functional Connectivity (FC) matrices were estimated using Pearson’s Correlation between components’ timeseries, reordered and renamed for both ICA30 and ICA150. Each component’s amplitude was calculated as its timeseries standard deviation.

We tested several hypotheses between functional features and clinic/demographic data: a) Gender differences; b) Age, CL and PACC correlation; c) PACC and CL correlation covariating by age; d) Age and PACC correlation covariating by CL; e) CL and gender interaction. T-tests and linear regression models were applied to compare groups and test correlations, respectively, all with False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction (p<0.05). 

To identify subgroups of subjects, the amplitude features of the ICA30 were introduced into a KMeans clustering algorithm, selecting the optimum number of clusters by the highest silhouette score. Afterwards, clinical and demographic variables were compared between groups.

Results

The database contained 1890 subjects (A4 and LEARN). The final sample (subjects meeting the imaging and acquisition parameter criteria) consisted of 1590 subjects: 1109 from A4 (663 female (59.8%), mean age 71.9 ± 4.7, mean CL 66.3 ± 33.2 and mean PACC -0.38 ± 2.6 ) and 481 from LEARN (298 female (61.9%), mean age 70.5 ± 4.4, mean CL 4.8  ± 12.41 and mean PACC 0.23 ± 2.4).

After the preprocessing and QC, 1018 were selected for further analysis: 698 from A4 and 320 from LEARN. We observed gender differences in the sample. Males from A4 were significantly older than females and showed higher CL values and lower PACC scores. In the LEARN sample, only PACC differed across genders males showing lower PACC scores.

22 components of the ICA30 were identified as RSNs: 5 DMN, 9 EN, 1 SC, 2 SM, 1 SN and 4 VIS. 101 components of the ICA150 were identified as RSNs: 13 DMN, 52 as EN, 9 as SC, 5 as SM, 4 as SN and 18 as VIS.

For each subject, two symmetric FC matrix were obtained with the full correlation measurements between the RSN timeseries, one for the ICA30 study and another one for the ICA150 study. Only the non-duplicated values (upper diagonal of each matrix) were used for further analyses. 22 amplitude features for the ICA30 study, and 101 amplitude features for the ICA150 study were obtained.

Regarding the FC, significant differences (p-value<0.05) between gender were found showing that there is a gender-specific network organization in both directions. These results were found in both, low (ICA30) and high (ICA150), dimensionalities.  

Significant correlation with age was also found in several edges showing positive and negative correlations. When adding CL or PACC as covariates, age effects persisted. These results were found in both, low and high, dimensionalities.

While no correlation with CL was found in low dimensionality, they were found in the high dimensionality suggesting a reorganization of the networks at a finer level. These correlations disappeared when controlling for age. Interestingly, we found interactions between CL and gender in ICA30, suggesting a gender-dependent effect of amyloid on FC. 

Correlation with PACC was found in low dimensionality, almost no results were found in the high dimensionality. The correlation with PACC disappeared in both dimensionalities when adding age as a covariate. Results were found in low dimensionality but not in high dimensionality when controlling by CL.

For amplitudes, we found differences for gender, and correlations with age, CL and PACC in both dimensionalities. In general, amplitudes were higher in female than in male participants. For age and CL, the correlations found were negative while for PACC were positive. When controlling for age, some negative correlations were still found for CL, no results were found for PACC, in neither dimensionality. Results in age persisted in both dimensionalities and no results for PACC were found when controlling for CL. Covariating by PACC, age results persisted with lower amplitudes for older subjects, no results were found in CL for low dimensionality, but results were found for high dimensionality showing lower amplitudes for higher CL values. Interactions between gender and CL were not found in neither dimensionality.

The silhouette score suggested to use 2 clusters to divide the data. Therefore, a KMeans with K=2 was fit into the data (all subjects’ amplitude features of ICA30) obtaining 2 groups: cluster 1 (349 subjects, 67.4% females and 69.2% subjects from A4) and cluster 2 (669 subjects, 60.45% females and 67.33% subjects were from A4). The groups did not differ significatively by age but differed by the CL (p-value = 0.017) and also by the PACC (p-value = 0.0297).

Conclusion

In this work we presented a systematic pipeline to perform preprocessing, quality control and ICA analysis in two dimensionalities in the A4/LEARN dataset. 

To perform multidimensional analysis, a novel specific methodology was implemented which allows matching ICs across dimensionalities in an objective, automatic and fast way. 

We systematically tested differences and correlations between FC features and demographic and clinical measures in the different dimensionalities. We found a major effect of age, both as FC increases and decreases, suggesting overall reorganization of the brain, which is independent of amyloid level (in early AD stages, with low CL). We found a little effect of amyloid in FC, that appeared at higher dimensionalities. However, effects were small compared to age. We also found important interactions with gender, that appeared already when studying the sample distribution and are persistent in FC features. More results were found with amplitudes, suggesting amplitudes are more sensitive to pathology-changes than correlations.

The cluster analysis suggests the existence of subgroups within the overall sample that may explain cognitive differences and may suggest different trajectories within a sample that is rather homogeneous. Longitudinal studies and relationship with Tau pathology may help to disentangle this.
