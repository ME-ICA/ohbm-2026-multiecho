Introduction

The GLP-1 receptor agonist semaglutide provides not only remarkable weight loss and metabolic benefits but also shows promise for a range of neurological conditions. Beneficial changes in behaviors related to craving and addiction, extending beyond food to other substances have been reported (1,2). Additionally, improved cognitive function and reduced symptoms of neurodegenerative disorders, such as Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease, have been observed (3). The central effects of semaglutide have been partly ascribed to GLP1 agonism potentially altering monoaminergic neurontransmission as a downstream effect. However, acute whole-brain effects of GLP1 agonists have up to date only been observed in a task-based setting. Functional ultrasound (fUS) is a powerful method enabling indirect whole-brain assessment of neuronal activity via neurovascular coupling in vivo (4). In rodents, fUS has similar technical specifications to functional MRI (fMRI) (here: 2.4s temporal resolution, 0.2x0.2x0.525mm3 spatial resolution).Here, we applied functional ultrasound (fUS) imaging to investigate dose-dependent effects of Semaglutide on brain-wide hemodynamics and connectivity. To better contextualize the effects seen and interpret the potential mechanisms driving the effects of Semaglutide centrally, we additionally performed a dose-response study with tetrabenazine, a vesicular monoamine transporter 2 (VMAT2) blocker, known to inhibit the packaging presynaptic release of monoamines, and compared it to the effects seen with semaglutide. To our knowledge, this is the first study performed in either animals or humans investigating the acute effects of a GLP1 agonist on baseline, and not task-modulated, hemodynamic-derived neuronal activity and functional connectivity

Methods

We performed three functional ultrasound studies in lean 8-week-old C57/Bl6J mice. The pilot study aimed to determine whether fUS could capture significant CBV and FC of semaglutide in the brain at a clinically relevant dose. Therefore, we divided animals into two groups: semaglutide group and a vehicle group (n=11 per group). Scan duration was 90 minutes and semaglutide/vehicle was given subcutaneously at a dose of 20 nmol/kg 15 minutes after the scan started. We then followed up with a dose-response study, including four cohorts with the animals receiving either vehicle or semaglutide at 5, 25 or 50nmol/kg i.v (n=11 per group). For the TBZ dose-response study, the animals received either vehicle or TBZ at 2.5, 5 or 10mg/kg s.c. (n=11 per group).  For both dose-response studies the compound was administered 30 minutes after scan start and the effects were observed over 60 minutes after injection, resulting in 90-minute scans. For all studies, anesthesia was maintained by a combination of dexmedetomidine (0.09 mg/kg bolus + 0.05 mg/kg/h infusion) and 0.5% isoflurane. The scans were conducted using the Iconeus One scanner (Iconeus, Paris) and the IcoPrime Multiarray 4D ultrasound probe.

For data preprocessing, we performed slice-timing correction, realignment, co-registration to the Allen Brain Atlas, and spatial smoothing (0.2x0.2x0.2mm3) using Statistical Parametric Mapping 12 (SPM12). Following preprocessing,  conducted regional cerebral blood volume (rCBV) analysis by averaging the data over the baseline block (10 minutes pre-injection) and 50-60 minutes post-injection. We divided the averaged images for all animals to generate rCBV maps for each animal, indicating changes in CBV relative to baseline. 

These maps were used to perform a General Linear Model analysis with SPM12, revealing significant alterations from baseline. Additionally, we plotted rCBV time curves to show the dynamics of the observed changes. For functional connectivity analysis, we calculated ROI-to-ROI functional connectivity for each animal using a sliding window approach (10-minute blocks, 1-minute sliding steps), with functional connectivity calculated using Pearson's r. Changes from baseline (10 minutes pre-injection) were assessed using ANCOVA, including baseline values of all groups as covariates of no interest, and results were displayed as Z-score matrices, indicating changes to vehicle and as dynamic functional connectivity for exemplary ROIs to depict the dynamics of the observed changes. The mentioned metrics were computed for each compound at every dose used.

Results

We generated voxel-wise maps indicating significant changes in CBV induced by Semaglutide or TBZ application compared to vehicle, after including baseline values as covariates of no interest. Both compounds showed prominent dose-dependent, partly overlapping, decreases in CBV (Figure 2A). For semaglutide, the strongest effects were seen in the septum and hypothalamus, both areas being adjacent to circumventricular organs and thereby directly accessible to the compound. Additionally, we saw effects in subcortical areas including the basal ganglia, thalamus, midbrain, as well as in the olfactory and frontal cortices. Tetrabenazine elicited more widespread effects, in addition to subcortical changes also comprising wider portions of the cortex including somatosensory, motor, auditory and visual cortices. Exemplary timecourses for semaglutide are provided for two regions: septum and hypothalamus (Figure 2B). Notably, the effects mirror the different routes of administration and corresponding PKs, with constant, steady decreases observed after s.c. application and more abrupt reductions followed by a plateau after i.v. injection. Similarly to the spatial extents seen for CBV effects, semaglutide exerted more precise alterations in FC compared to tetrabenazine (Figure 2C). For semaglutide, these were largely limited to a network comprising the prelimbic area, olfactory cortex, basal ganglia, thalamus and claustrum at the highest dose. For tetrabenazine, the decreases were qualitatively similar at the 5mg/kg dose with the strongest effects in the frontal cortex, VTA and basal ganglia, likely driven by dopaminergic depletion. However, at the highest dose, a widespread FC drop comprised numerous areas distributed across the entire brain. In Figure 2D the dynamic FC computed using a sliding-window approach before and after injection is shown for both compounds exemplarily for the prelimbic area, indicating the comparable dose-response effects of both drugs in this brain region.

In addition to the imaging data, we performed a behavioral battery on a different cohort mice indicating strong effects of both compounds on several metrics such as impulsitivity and locomotion (data not shown here).

Conclusion

Semaglutide elicited strong and reproducible reductions in CBV following both subcutaneous and intravenous applications, effects occurring mostly in subcortical areas including septum, hypothalamus, amygdala and striatum. Functional connectivity revealed dose-dependent reductions in complementary areas, especially prominent in a network encompassing the prelimbic cortex, orbitofrontal cortex, striatum, claustrum and olfactory cortex. Notably, the directionality of change in both metrics was the same for TBZ. However, especially at higher doses, TBZ elicited effects over more widespread areas of the brain. We speculate that the more specific changes observed with semaglutide arise from its potential interaction with fewer neurotransmitter systems, for instance mainly dopamine, compared to TBZ, since blocking the VMAT2 affects not only dopamine, but all catecholamines. The data demonstrates in vivo engagement of semaglutide beyond regions adjacent to circumventricular organs, paving the way towards understanding the central circuits behind its mechanism of action. Additional analysis will aim to disentangle the molecular substrate of the observed changes by applying advanced methods such as receptor-enriched analysis of functional connectivity (REACT) (5).
