Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder resulting in motor symptoms such as bradykinesia, rigidity, and tremor. Among its most disabling manifestations, freezing of gait (FoG) is characterized by brief episodes of gait arrest despite the intention to move, thus significantly increasing fall risk and compromising patients’ autonomy. Its pathophysiology remains unclear, involving disrupted cortical-subcortical communication and impaired motor control. The transient and context-dependent nature of FoG makes objective, multimodal assessments essential to improve its characterization and clinical management. This study aimed to characterise neural correlates of FoG by analysing cortical activation patterns through functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) and to develop a machine-learning framework for automatic detection of FoG using fNIRS, surface electromyography (sEMG), and inertial measurement unit (IMU) signals.

Methods

Eleven PD patients were enrolled, including four individuals reporting daily FoG episodes (PD-FoG) and seven without FoG experience (PD-noFoG). Multimodal recordings (fNIRS, sEMG, and IMU) were acquired during motor tasks designed to evaluate gait on straight paths and turns and to elicit FoG episodes. The protocol consisted of single-task (ST) and dual-task (DT) variations of walking and turning within a 50×50 cm box marked on the floor in both clockwise and counterclockwise directions. All signals underwent dedicated preprocessing pipelines to ensure data quality and synchronization across modalities. fNIRS signals were sampled at 5 Hz using the NIRSport2 system with 16 sources and 15 detectors positioned to cover prefrontal and sensorimotor cortical regions previously associated with FoG episodes.1 Raw optical intensity was converted to cortical haemodynamic activity maps using NIRSTORM through motion artifact removal, band-pass filtering (0.01-0.5 Hz), short-separation channel regression, and 3D source reconstruction based on photon propagation models. The cortical surface was parcellated using the Automated Anatomical Labeling atlas 3 (AAL3) atlas, and the mean fNIRS activity was extracted from each region. Event-related responses were estimated using a generalised linear model (GLM) by convolving design matrices with a canonical hemodynamic response function, whose output was used for single-subject and between-group statistical comparisons. IMU signals were acquired at 128 Hz using Opal sensors with 9 degrees of freedom positioned bilaterally on wrists and feet, as well as on the sacrum and sternum. Following noise filtering and standardization to a common reference frame, feet's angular velocity was used to detect step phases which were used in conjunction with lumbar angular velocity and orientation to identify gait events (turn and walk). sEMG signals were acquired at 1000 Hz using the FreeEMG system with bilateral electrodes on Tibialis Anterior, Gastrocnemius Medialis, Rectus Femoris, Biceps Femoris, and Gluteus Maximus. After normalization and band-pass filtering (20-450 Hz), muscle activation envelopes were extracted using a fourth-order low-pass Butterworth filter with 4 Hz as cutoff frequency. All three processed signals were segmented into 2-second epochs with 0.25-second overlap, and features from each modality were extracted. Each epoch was labelled into three categories: walk, turn, and FoG, with ground-truth labels for FoG established through independent video annotation by two trained evaluators, ensuring reliable event identification. Epochs underwent outlier detection and removal before being used to train and evaluate multiple classifier architectures for binary (FoG vs. no-FoG) classification. The classifiers tested included: support vector machine with minimum redundancy maximum relevance (mRMR) feature selection, random forest (RF) with feature importance derived from out-of-bag permutation error, k-nearest neighbours (kNN) with ReliefF feature ranking, and LASSO logistic regression with embedded L1 regularization for feature selection. Leave-one-subject-out cross-validation was employed to assess model generalization to unseen participants.

Results

Single-subject analysis revealed distinct activation patterns during FoG episodes, with increased activity in prefrontal, premotor, and supplementary motor regions. At the individual level, PD-FoG patients showed bilateral prefrontal and motor activation during FoG compared to walking and turning in ST conditions, while DT conditions were characterized by prominent frontal and SMA activation with relative deactivation in primary motor cortex. Group-level analysis confirmed these patterns across the PD-FoG cohort, revealing activations in SMA during ST FoG and increased frontal activation during DT FoG. Between-group comparisons showed significant differences: higher PD-noFoG activation in right temporal regions during ST walking, and higher PD-FoG activation in right inferior parietal areas during DT walking and in right frontal cortex and SMA during DT turning. Binary classification (FoG vs noFoG) demonstrated high performance across all tested classifiers, with balanced accuracy consistently exceeding 90% for every validation subject. RF and kNN achieved the highest mean performance (98% with 2.8% standard deviation across folds).

Conclusion

This study contributes to the characterization of neural correlates of FoG and demonstrates the feasibility of a multimodal machine-learning framework for automatic FoG detection in PD. fNIRS measurements revealed cortical activation patterns during FoG episodes that may indicate reduced movement automaticity or engagement of compensatory mechanisms. Multimodal classifiers achieved optimal performance with robust cross-subject generalization. Capturing multiple signatures of gait may enable exploration beyond clinical assessment and provide comprehensive characterization of freezing episodes, paving the way for personalized rehabilitation strategies and interventions to reduce FoG burden and improve patient autonomy.
