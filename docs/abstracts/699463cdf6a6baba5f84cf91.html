Introduction

The brain’s exceptionally high oxygen demand renders it particularly susceptible to hypoxia: Despite accounting for only 1-2% of total body weight, the brain receives around one-fifth of all cardiac output, and oxygen deprivation induces irreversible damage within seconds to minutes. In both hemispheres, three major blood vessels, the anterior (ACA), middle (MCA), and posterior cerebral artery (PCA), supply spatially discrete cortical territories. Cortical watersheds, i.e., boundaries between major vascular territories, are of special clinical interest as they are often affected by microemboli from degenerative vascular disease, or terminal-vessel hypoxia in states of globally decreased circulation1,2. At the same time, the increased presence of collateral vessels might have a protective effect and facilitate cerebrovascular reorganization. Whether the increased distance to major blood vessels can be outweighed by potential resilience advantages through high collaterality remains unknown. 

We hypothesize that cortical microstructure and functional organization are spatially related to vascular patterns, potentially as a result of evolutionary adaptation to both promote chemoarchitectural homeostasis and retention of function after a hypoxic injury. To date, there is no comprehensive, system-level vascular contextualization of large-scale brain organizational patterns. Here, we first create a continuous space of blood supply, leveraging distance to large vessel territories and their watersheds, and describe how cortical microarchitectural markers covary along this trajectory.

Methods

Leveraging a normative atlas of large vessel supply territories3, we determined the mean distance of every cortical vertex to each vessel territory. To this end, we utilized geodesic distance, i.e., distance along the pial surface, computed on a standard fslr32k surface. Each vertex was first assigned to its closest vessel, leading to a normative cerebrovascular territory map (Fig. 1A). Watershed territories were identified at 30% overlap, i.e., where the ratio of the two closest vessels was between 0.7-1.3, with robustness analyses considering overlaps of 10, 20, and 40%. We then calculated the ratio of distance to the closest vessels for each vertex in the watershed zone. In our primary approach, we selected an ACA-MCA-PCA trajectory, as the MCA supplies the largest cortical territory and thus has the longest border to other vessels’s supply territories. In robustness analyses, similar trajectories were constructed using ACA and PCA as midpoints. 

We then identified 50 spatial patterns available via neuromaps4 that reflect on cortical microarchitecture, function, and receptor expression through specialized magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or positron emission tomography (PET)5. These maps were converted to surface space, where applicable, and mapped onto the vascular continuum. To understand how these parameters vary along vascular trajectories, we then fit a nonlinear function without pre-specification of curvature parameters for each map. The resulting functions allowed us to describe how normative imaging patterns vary along the vascular trajectory. We then determined inflection points, i.e., zero crossings of the second derivative of each function, corresponding to the localizations of greatest change along the vascular continuum.

Results

The continuous vascular map followed a double sigmoid function (Fig. 1A). Major vessel territories were distributed evenly across the continuous space. Of note, the ACA-MCA watershed appeared broader than the MCA-PCA watershed in our primary approach, following our assumptions of topographical anatomy, as the MCA and ACA supply the largest and second-largest territories, respectively. For further analysis, microarchitectural maps were grouped into 12 large-scale systems: vasculature, intrinsic function, metabolism, structure, and receptor maps (acetylcholine, GABA, cannabinoid, dopamine, histamine, norepinephrine, serotonin, opioid). Fig. 2A shows the nonlinear fit of each map along the vascular continuum. Then, we ranked each map by the percentage of inflection points that localize within a watershed. Fig. 2B shows the total number of inflection points (grey bar) and the percentage within the ACA-MCA (purple) or MCA-PCA (blue) watershed. All maps had at least one inflection point outside of the watershed. For 16 (32%) of the 50 investigated maps, all inflection points were localized outside of watershed territories. The highest percentage of in-watershed inflections was 67% in arterial spin labeling MRI-driven cerebral blood flow (one of three inflections in ACA-MCA and MCA-PCA each), a 5-HT6-serotonin receptor map (two points in ACA-MCA), and a D2 dopamine receptor map (two points in MCA-PCA). Robustness analyses revealed consistency across overlap thresholds. There was a considerable influence of starting points on the breadth of the watershed, possibly because this caused values along the expansive MCA territory boundary to disseminate across the continuum.

Conclusion

Using a continuous approach to describe vascular supply along the cortical surface, we found that the majority of selected microarchitectural systems show inflection points outside of watershed areas, describing localizations of greatest change in microarchitectural markers in areas of single-vessel supply. This stability of microarchitecture across the watershed potentially reflects a preferential stability across brain areas that are easily affected in settings of globally decreased blood supply. Of note, vasculature mediates functional and tracer-based imaging signals, necessitating source-specific interpretation of our findings. For each system, the degree of change in inflection points has a different biological meaning and warrants further exploration.
