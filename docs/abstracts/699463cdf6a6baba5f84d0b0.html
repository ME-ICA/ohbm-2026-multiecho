Introduction

Reliable volumetry is fundamental for modeling early neurodevelopment, building normative growth charts, and detecting deviations, yet automated brain segmentation in young children remains challenging due to age-dependent tissue contrast, motion, and rapidly changing anatomy. As a result, pediatric studies often mix tools or switch pipelines across age ranges, which can introduce method-driven differences that may be mistaken for biological effects. More broadly, there is still no clear consensus on the optimal segmentation approach in early childhood, and substantial variability across methods remains a recognized challenge for MRI studies in the first years of life.1 Here, we evaluate how segmentation pipeline choice influences regional volume estimates in the Calgary Preschool longitudinal MRI dataset (ages 2-8 years). We compare three used (and differently parameterized) approaches—(i) SynthSeg2, an automatic segmentation tool (ii) MaCRUISE segmentations3 with manual corrections, and (iii) an age-adaptive pipeline4 that combines outputs from pediatric tools (e.g., iBEAT/Infant FreeSurfer/Standard FreeSurfer) to produce segmentations. Because the three pipelines yield different native parcellations (18, 71, 31 regions respectively), we mapped them to a common set of 11 bilateral (L+R) regions and one unilateral region to enable direct comparisons of volumetric agreement, spatial overlap, and age-related patterns across methods.

Methods

Dataset and quality control. We analyzed longitudinal T1-weighted MRI from the Calgary Preschool dataset (ages 2-8 years), acquired on a 3T GE MR750w scanner.5 We incorporated pipeline-specific segmentation QC to ensure comparability across methods. For MaCRUISE, we excluded any segmentation rated by 4 trained raters as “do not use” or “exclude.” For the age-adaptive pipeline, we excluded scans with mean surface/segmentation QC < 1.5 on a 0-2 scale. Finally, to enable paired, scan-wise comparisons, we retained only scans that were successfully processed by all three pipelines and contained valid labels for all harmonized regions. The final sample comprised 101 MR images from 47 participants (median 2 timepoints/subject; age range 32.7–82.8 months; sex: 49% female, 51% male).

Segmentation pipelines. We compared three segmentation strategies that produce different native label sets:

  1. SynthSeg: v2.0 whole-brain parcellation was run on native T1w NIfTI images with a fixed configuration (18 regions), using the default CPU workflow with resampling and per-scan QC; scans with QC <0.65 were excluded.
  2. MaCRUISE + manual corrections: MaCRUISE parcellations providing 71 regions, with manual corrections for a subset of scans; all segmentations failing QC were removed.
  3. Age-adaptive pipeline: an age-adaptive workflow producing 31 regions. For scans >50 months, unmodified FreeSurfer v7.3 recon was used. For scans in the 25–50 months range, FreeSurfer processing was complemented by parallel iBEAT runs, and outputs were hybridized by combining iBEAT-derived cortical (pial/white) boundaries with FreeSurfer subcortical parcellations to generate a FreeSurfer-style segmentation. Only scans with QC ≥ 1.5 were retained for this pipeline.

Label harmonization and spatial standardization. Because native parcellations differed in granularity (18, 31, and 71 regions), we mapped all outputs to a common set of regions present in all 101 scans: 11 bilateral structures with L+R volumes summed plus the right lateral ventricle (21 labels total). Regions included: Accumbens area, amygdala, hippocampus, pallidum, putamen, thalamus, ventral diencephalon (DC), 3rd and 4th ventricles, right lateral ventricle, cerebellum white matter, and cerebral white matter. All segmentations were resampled to 1 mm isotropic and analyzed in native subject space.

To facilitate qualitative comparison (Fig. 1), we additionally generated an “agreement map” for each region by intersecting the corresponding binary masks across pipelines and encoding voxel-wise overlap patterns. This produced seven mutually exclusive overlap classes (present in all three; in each of the three pairwise combinations; or unique to each pipeline), enabling rapid visualization of where segmentations converge or diverge anatomically.

Volume extraction and agreement metrics. Regional volumes (mm³) were computed as label voxel count × voxel volume. We report both raw volumes and normalized volumes (region/total brain volume (TBV)) to distinguish global scaling effects from region-specific biases.

For descriptive developmental trends, we summarized volume-by-age relationships using 3-month age bins. Within each bin, we computed the mean volume per method; shaded bands represent ±1 SD to reflect inter-scan/inter-subject variability. These plots are intended as empirical summaries rather than parametric growth-curve fits.

For quantitative agreement, we computed: (i) ICC(3,1) (two-way mixed effects, absolute agreement, single measurement) on regional volumes and proportions based on TBV, and (ii) the Jaccard index (MaCRUISE ⋂ Age-adaptive ⋂ SynthSeg/MaCRUISE ⋃ Age-adaptive ⋃ SynthSeg) on the region masks to assess spatial overlap. For both metrics, values near 1 indicate high agreement and values near 0 indicate minimal agreement.

Results

Across 101 scans, cross-pipeline volumetric reliability (ICC(3,1), absolute agreement) differed markedly by structure. Agreement was excellent for the lateral ventricles (ICC=0.92) and good for the hippocampus (ICC=0.84) and 4th ventricle (ICC=0.81), but only moderate for several subcortical structures (e.g., putamen/3rd ventricle/amygdala/accumbens/pallidum/ventral DC; ICC≈0.61-0.72) and lower for cerebral white matter (ICC=0.53) and thalamus (ICC=0.46).

After normalizing volumes by total brain volume (region/TBV), reliability decreased for most regions, indicating that a portion of the agreement in raw volumes reflects global scaling rather than consistent regional delineation. The right lateral ventricle remained highly reliable (ICC=0.90, CI 0.87-0.93), whereas agreement dropped to moderate for the 3rd and 4th ventricles (ICC=0.61 and 0.57) and for putamen and pallidum (ICC=0.52 and 0.50). Accumbens and thalamus showed low agreement (ICC=0.34 and 0.30), hippocampus was also low (ICC=0.24), and amygdala and ventral DC showed no reliable agreement (ICC=0.09 and 0.06; CIs crossing zero). White matter proportions exhibited no agreement (ICC≈0 or negative; CIs crossing zero), consistent with pipeline-specific definitions of white matter and tissue boundaries.

Spatial concordance was generally modest and strongly structure-dependent (mean Jaccard ≈0.15–0.52). Overlap was highest for thalamus (0.52) and cerebral white matter (0.49), and remained relatively high for putamen (0.44) and hippocampus (0.41). In contrast, overlap was lowest for the 3rd ventricle (0.15) and comparatively low for amygdala (0.27) and accumbens (0.25), consistent with reduced concordance for smaller or more ambiguous structures.

In the age-binned trajectories (Fig. 2), SynthSeg and MaCRUISE show broadly similar age-related shapes for most parenchymal regions, with the clearest increase observed for cerebral white matter and more gradual upward trends for several subcortical structures (e.g., putamen, hippocampus, thalamus, and ventral DC), albeit with noticeable method-dependent offsets in absolute volume. SynthSeg largely matches the age-adaptive pipeline’s age trends for most subcortical regions, consistent with its FreeSurfer-derived training. The biggest differences are in white matter (cerebral/cerebellar) and around the age threshold where the age-adaptive workflow switches processing. In contrast, ventricular measures (right lateral ventricle, 3rd and 4th ventricles) are more variable across bins and do not show a consistent monotonic age trend, with occasional spikes and wider uncertainty bands. Accumbens volumes appear comparatively stable across the sampled age range.

Each pipeline has distinct constraints that likely contribute to the observed method dependence. SynthSeg uses a fixed configuration with a comparatively coarse label set, which may prioritize robustness over anatomical detail and can be sensitive to preprocessing choices at tissue boundaries. MaCRUISE provides high label granularity and benefits from manual corrections, but this introduces rater- and protocol-dependence and may alter reproducibility; QC-based exclusions may also bias retained scans toward easier cases. The age-adaptive workflow is explicitly pediatric-oriented but is age-adaptive and hybridizes outputs (e.g., cortical boundaries from iBEAT with FreeSurfer subcortical labels), meaning processing is not uniform across age and regional definitions may differ from any single tool. This makes direct pipeline-to-pipeline comparisons less straightforward. Finally, harmonizing heterogeneous parcellations to 12 shared regions and resampling to 1 mm may disproportionately affect small structures, consistent with the lowest agreement observed for the 3rd ventricle and amygdala.

Conclusion

Despite harmonizing three segmentation strategies with markedly different native label granularities (18, 31, and 71 regions) to a common set of 11 bilateral regions plus one unilateral region, we found substantial pipeline-dependent variability in both regional volumes and voxel-wise delineations on the same subjects and scans. Agreement was strongly structure-specific: ventricular volumes were relatively robust, whereas several subcortical regions, and especially white matter fractions, showed limited reliability and modest spatial overlap across methods. The pronounced reduction in ICC after TBV normalization indicates that apparent agreement in raw volumes is partly driven by shared global scaling, while estimates of relative brain composition are more sensitive to segmentation choices. Together, these results underscore the importance of using a single, age-independent segmentation strategy for longitudinal pediatric studies: switching between age-specific pipelines can introduce discontinuities that may be misinterpreted as developmental change, particularly given the substantial between-pipeline variability observed here.

More generally, segmentation pipeline choice can materially affect regional measures and their inferred age-related patterns, with important implications for cross-study comparisons and pooled analyses. Future work will quantify within-subject longitudinal stability and trajectory consistency per pipeline, and extend beyond three-way comparisons to targeted pairwise analyses to localize which processing choices drive disagreement.
