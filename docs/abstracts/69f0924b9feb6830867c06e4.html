Introduction

Evidence increasingly suggests that socioeconomic context, and gender inequality in particular, may shape human brain structure. While sex-related differences in brain anatomy are often attributed to biological factors such as hormones or sex chromosomes, emerging work highlights the role of environmental and social determinants.

Recent studies have linked gender or economic inequality to structural brain differences in either young^1 or older cohorts^2, but none has examined how these associations evolve across the entire lifespan within a globally diverse sample. In fact, most neuroimaging datasets remain geographically restricted or span limited age ranges, making it unclear whether observed associations generalize across populations and throughout the lifespan. This limits our understanding of whether inequality-related brain differences reflect early developmental effects, later-life processes, or cumulative exposure across life.

Here, we address this gap by analysing, for the first time, data from the volBrain platform^3. This yielded a large, world-representative neuroimaging dataset spanning childhood to late adulthood across 55 countries and the five continents. Using volBrain, we investigate how country-level gender inequality relates to female–male differences in regional grey matter distribution beyond overall brain size, how these associations change with age, and to what extent they are driven by economic factors.

Methods

We analysed T1-weighted MRI data from 13,277 individuals (52 countries, five continents) processed using the vol2Brain pipeline of the volBrain platform. Cortical and subcortical grey matter volumes were extracted for 135 regions and scaled to unit sum per subject to focus on relative regional distributions independent of total brain size.

Sex differences were first assessed using out-of-sample machine-learning prediction. Within 10-year sliding age windows (17–80 years), we trained L2-regularised regression models to predict sex from relative grey matter volumes, using balanced bootstrapped samples (n=1,000 per window). Age, intracranial cavity volume (ICV), and image signal-to-noise ratio were regressed out within training data and applied to test sets to avoid leakage. Model interpretation used SHAP values to quantify regional contributions.

To assess links with structural inequality, we computed Euclidean distances between female–male pairs within each country and age window. These distances were regressed on country-level Gender Inequality Index (GII), controlling for age difference, ICV difference, and signal-to-noise difference. Statistical significance was assessed using cluster-based permutation testing across age windows.

To disentangle gender inequality from economic factors, we incorporated gross domestic product (GDP) and the Gini index into the models. Given strong collinearity between socioeconomic indicators, we designed a constrained random subsampling approach to minimise correlations between regressors, enabling interpretable estimates of their independent effects.

Results

Relative grey matter distributions predicted sex significantly above chance across the lifespan, even after rigorous correction for brain size and confounds. Prediction accuracy declined with age, and models trained at one age generalized poorly to distant age ranges, indicating that the anatomical patterns underlying female–male differences change across life. Subcortical regions contributed disproportionately to sex prediction compared to cortical regions.

As shown in Fig 2, female–male brain differences were significantly associated with gender inequality, and this association increased steadily with age. GII explained additional variance in brain differences beyond confounds from early adulthood onward, becoming consistently significant from approximately 28 years of age. The age-related increase in GII regression coefficients suggests a cumulative effect of gender inequality across the lifespan rather than an effect confined to development.

Including GDP as a covariate reduced but did not eliminate the association between GII and brain differences, indicating that general national wealth does not fully account for the effect. In contrast, including the Gini index largely suppressed the GII effect, while the Gini index itself showed strong associations across ages. These results indicate that the observed link between gender inequality and brain structure is largely driven by economic inequality components captured within gender inequality measures.

Conclusion

Using a uniquely large and globally diverse neuroimaging dataset, we provide evidence that female–male differences in regional grey matter distribution are associated with structural inequality beyond brain size and that this association strengthens across the lifespan. Our findings suggest that gender inequality may exert a cumulative influence on brain structure rather than acting only during early development.

Importantly, the dominance of economic inequality in explaining these effects highlights the role of material and structural conditions in shaping sex-related brain differences. While the use of country-level indicators limits causal inference at the individual level^4, the results underscore the importance of social context in understanding brain variation.

These findings challenge interpretations of sex differences in brain structure as solely biologically determined and emphasize the need for lifespan-sensitive and globally inclusive approaches in neuroimaging research. Addressing structural inequality may be critical not only for social justice but also for understanding population-level brain diversity and for building robust, generalizable models of brain–behavior relationships.
