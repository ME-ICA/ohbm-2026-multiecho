Introduction


Sleep-associated slow neural oscillations have been linked to hemodynamic changes and large, macroscopic cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) inflow waves in adults; a mechanism proposed to facilitate metabolic waste clearance

Slow neural oscillations during sleep have been proposed to play a key role in coordinating brain activity and supporting restorative processes. In adults, these oscillations can be assessed using BOLD fMRI, which has revealed that they are accompanied by coordinated hemodynamic changes and large-scale cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) inflow waves1,2. Such dynamics are thought to facilitate the clearance of metabolic waste from the brain.Understanding this relationship in the neonatal period is of particular interest as sleep is the predominant behavioural state during this important juncture in life, at a time when the brain is undergoing rapid structural and functional maturation3. However, as major maturational changes in cerebrovascular and glymphatic organization are also occuring in early infancy, it is thus unclear whether the same sleep-related homeostatic and waste clearance mechanisms are present4. If present, we hypothesized that slow CSF fluctuations in the fourth ventricle would be coupled with hemodynamic activity in neonates, reflecting the early emergence of coordinated brain–fluid homeostatic function.

Methods

Data:

We analysed resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) data from 66 neonates (median postmenstrual age at scan: 40.4; range: 32.6–44.1 weeks) acquired as part of the dHCP5. Whole brain BOLD fMRI data were collected following feeding and during natural sleep using a 32ch neonatal receive coil and imaging system on a 3T Philips Achieva system (Best, NL) using a multiband EPI sequence (TE = 38 ms; TR = 392 ms; multiband factor = 9; voxel size = 2.15 mm isotropic) over 15 minutes6. Datasets were included from the total cohort of 783 subjects if at least 600 consecutive TRs (~3 min 55 s) exhibited low head motion (framewise displacement < 0.3 mm), ensuring reliable estimation of CSF inflow dynamics.

Data preprocessing and ROI selection:

For analysis, continuous 600-TR (~3 min 55 s) motion-free epochs were extracted from each dataset. To characterise CSF dynamics, fMRI data were minimally pre-processed (including only rigid body motion correction by aligning each volume to a reference volume using MCFLIRT and band-pass temporal filtering (0.01–0.1Hz)). To quantify CSF inflow within the fourth ventricle1, a region of interest (ROI) was manually defined in native functional space by manually selecting a single voxel with the highest signal in the single-band reference image (Figure 1A). The timecourse was extracted from this mask for each subject.

In a similar way, grey matter signal analysis focused on the same 600-TR (~3 min 55 s) segments of BOLD fMRI data that were minimally affected by head motion, enabling assessment of neural and CSF temporal dynamics. BOLD fMRI data were pre-processed using the dHCP fMRI pre-processing pipeline6, which, in brief, included motion distortion correction, ICA-based physiological denoising, and band-pass temporal filtering (0.01–0.1Hz). Cortical grey-matter and whole-brain ROIs were defined using T2 tissue-type segmentations from the dHCP anatomical pipeline7, registered to native functional space (Figure 1B). The grey matter BOLD timecourse was extracted for each subject.

Data analysis:

To isolate how changes in BOLD signal may drive changes in CSF inflow, the negative temporal derivative of the cortical grey matter BOLD signal was computed, and negative values were set to zero. This procedure restricts analysis to putative inflow-related haemodynamic changes.

CSF and processed BOLD signals were z-scored prior to analysis. Normalized cross-correlation was computed between CSF and BOLD-derived signals over a ±10 s lag window. For each subject, the zero-lag correlation (r₀), the lag corresponding to the maximum absolute correlation (best lag), and the corresponding correlation coefficient (best r) were extracted. Positive lag values indicate CSF signal changes occurring after cortical BOLD signal changes.

Results

Across all subjects, low-frequency (<0.05 Hz) fluctuations were observed in both the fourth-ventricle CSF signal and cortical grey matter BOLD signal (Figure 2B). Power spectral analysis revealed dominant oscillatory components within the expected physiological frequency range (0.01–0.05 Hz), consistent with slow haemodynamic and CSF-related dynamics during sleep (Figure 2A).

Cross-correlation analysis revealed substantial inter-individual variability in the temporal relationship between CSF and brain tissue BOLD-derived signals. The majority of infants did not show a significant anticorrelation relationship between CSF and brain BOLD fluctuations, with only a small subset of 9 out of 66 neonates (~14%) demonstrating a pattern consistent with physiological coupling (characterized by a negative correlation between CSF and the derivative of cortical BOLD) occurring with a positive lag consistent with CSF changes following BOLD-derived inflow-related fluctuations. This temporal ordering is consistent with the haemodynamic–CSF coupling reported in adults during non-REM sleep1 (Figure 2B, shown for a representative subject), although the average lag time 3.86 was considerably longer than that described in adults. Furthermore, this relationship did not appear to be influenced by preterm status or age at scan. As shown in Figure 2C, cross-correlation values are roughly equally distributed above and below zero across all subjects, indicating no systematic trend with developmental age.

These findings suggest that while adult-like CSF–haemodynamic coupling can be detected in a subset of neonates, it is not a dominant or consistently expressed feature across the neonatal population.

Conclusion

Discussion

Using an analysis framework motivated by prior adult work1, we do not observe a temporally ordered relationship between BOLD-derived inflow and CSF signal changes. While the direction and timing of this relationship were qualitatively consistent in a small subset of subjects with haemodynamic–CSF coupling reported in adults during NREM sleep, this pattern was not robust at the group level, indicating substantial heterogeneity and suggesting that adult-like coupling is not yet consistently expressed at this developmental stage.

This suggests that there could be developmental variability in the strength or consistency of haemodynamic–CSF coupling. This heterogeneity may also reflect genuine inter-individual differences in neurovascular or cerebrovascular maturation or differences in arousal or sleep state during data acquisition.

Conclusion

Dynamic coupling between haemodynamics and CSF inflow does not yet appear to be consistently established during sleep in the neonatal period, with inter-individual variability likely reflecting ongoing neurovascular maturation and state-dependent influences early in life. Interpretation of these findings is further complicated by methodological challenges inherent to neonatal imaging, including small brain size, sensitivity to motion artefacts, limited spatial resolution at 3T, and the absence of concurrent sleep staging in the available dHCP data. Future studies combining fMRI with EEG-based sleep staging,  imaging at ultra-high field which will greatly enhance SNR and achievable spatial resolution, and improved motion-robust acquisition strategies may help clarify how vascular–CSF interactions emerge over development and how they relate to early-life brain homeostasis and later neurodevelopmental outcomes.
