Introduction

Cerebral palsy (CP), the most common childhood motor disability, is characterized by lifelong deficits that limit independence and participation. Hemiparetic cerebral palsy (HCP) is a subtype caused by a unilateral brain injury, and if it occurs prior to six months of age, abnormal corticospinal tract (CST) development often results in the non-lesioned hemisphere controlling both limbs due to an unpruned ipsilateral motor pathway. This contributes to mirror movements, which is unintentional movement in one extremity during voluntary, homologous movement in the other extremity. Despite the prevalence of mirror movements in early-onset HCP, it is unclear how preserved ipsilateral CST projections are organized and distributed in the cortex. And although CP is diagnosed based on motor behavior, individuals often have impairments in multiple systems including sensation, communication, and vision, so it is probable that the effects of altered CST development are widespread. We used an advanced multi-shell diffusion MRI (dMRI) protocol to map CST microstructure with our laboratory’s complexity measures to comprehensively characterize the motor pathway in early-onset HCP. This measure utilizes multiple b-value scans to fit the data to a model, mathematically comparable to kurtosis, that quantifies the non-Gaussian diffusion of the tissue.1,2 We will also lay the foundation for the presence of atypical CST tractography termination in the superior parietal gyrus (SPG) of the non-lesioned hemisphere. Disrupted development of the CST in this cortical area may have profound implications, as it is functionally an association area responsible for spatial awareness and visuomotor transformations, and does not typically house CST projections. Furthermore, we combined these dMRI data with neuro-navigated TMS, which enables precise stimulation targeting, to demonstrate the bilateral activation of distal upper extremity muscles when these atypical motor cortical areas are stimulated.

Methods

Participants underwent structural and diffusion-weighted scans on a 3T Siemens Prisma scanner (Siemens Medical Systems Inc, USA) with a 64-channel head coil. Structural T1-weighted scans were acquired using an MP-RAGE sequence with the following parameters: TR=2.3s, TE=2.94ms, TI=900ms, FOV 256x256mm2 with a voxel resolution of 1mm3. Diffusion-weighted images were collected from all participants with a spin-echo EPI sequence: TR=5s, TE=85ms, matrix size=150x150, FOV=225x 225mm, slice thickness = 1.5mm, interslice gap = 0.1 mm, number of slices = 90. Voxels had 1.5mm isotropic resolution to prevent biasing the orientation of the major axis of the white matter. The sequence consisted of 3 b-value shells of diffusion weighting at 1000, 2000, and 3000 s/mm2 in 60 different directions, as well as a total of 8 scans without diffusion weighting (b=0 s/mm2).

T1-weighted scans were used to identify anatomical regions of interest (ROIs) using standardized atlases. dMRI data were processed consistent with our laboratory’s established protocols1-3 using the FSL software library. Complexity (C) maps were created using previously published procedures and custom MATLAB codes. Diffusion tensor parameters were calculated from the preprocessed diffusion data to generate FA, MD, AD, and RD maps. For tractography of the CST, the anatomically segmented image was used to mask the seed in the cerebral peduncle and a waypoint in the posterior limb of the internal capsule. This ensured identified tracts followed established anatomy of the CST while allowing for potential atypical tractography termination to cortical regions caused by brain injuries sustained in the early-onset HCP population. An exclusion mask was created at the midline to exclude commissural fibers. Probabilistic tractography was performed and a threshold was applied, and diffusivity metrics were recorded for the tractography-derived CST in each hemisphere. Tractography termination was calculated by finding the number of voxels that overlap between the ROIs and the tractography-derived CST with respect to its entire volume of overlap with cortical gray matter, normalized by intracranial volume. Tractography termination acts as a measure for structural connectivity of the tractography-derived CST and anatomical ROIs. Coordinates corresponding to a region of clustered voxels where the CST and SPG overlap were recorded for TMS application.

Prior to TMS testing, the T1-weighted scan was registered to the participant’s head in Brainsight (Rogue Research, Montreal, Canada) for neuro-navigation. Surface electrodes were applied to the first dorsal interosseus (FDI) muscle on both hands. All TMS measures were recorded at rest to avoid the confounds of co-activation of muscles prior to stimulation and participant fatigue. TMS was delivered to the participant through a figure-of-eight coil connected to a Magstim 2002 stimulator (Magstim, Wales) while they were seated in a comfortable, upright position. The tractography-informed coordinates were loaded in to the neuro-navigation system and 10 paired-pulse stimulations were applied at that cortical location using previously defined parameters that our lab has demonstrated can optimally facilitate both contralateral and ipsilateral motor evoked potentials (MEPs).4 The number of trials where bilateral MEPs occurred out of ten total trials (i.e., MEP count) was recorded.

Imaging metrics (FA, MD, AD, RD, and C in the CST, tractography termination) followed a non-normal distribution, therefore nonparametric tests were used for statistical analysis. Group comparisons were performed using Mann-Whitney U tests to evaluate differences in outcome measures between participants with HCP and controls. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Northwestern University.

Results

Our preliminary results include 15 participants with early-onset HCP (mean age: 14.7y ± 6.2y, 3M) and 15 age-matched controls (mean age: 14.3y ± 5.9y, 7M) with typical development (TD) and no neurological impairment who completed the MRI protocol. All early-onset HCP participants sustained a brain lesion prior to 6 months of age in either the pre-or perinatal period of development. All were scored as Gross Motor Function Classification System (GMFCS) Level I, meaning they are able to walk without limitations, and as Manual Ability Classification System (MACS) Level I or II, meaning they are able to handle objects with little or no difficulty. Participants completed the Test of Arm Selective Control (TASC), a clinical assessment measuring selective voluntary motor control of the upper extremity, scoring between 15 and 31 (mean score: 23 ± 5.8) out of a total score of 32, with lower scores indicating higher impairment.

In the lesioned hemisphere CST, participants with HCP had significantly higher MD values (p=0.049), higher AD values (p<0.001), and lower C values (p=0.042) compared to the non-dominant hemisphere CST in controls. Tractography termination was calculated for the superior frontal gyrus, precentral gyrus, postcentral gyrus, and superior parietal gyrus. When compared to the non-dominant hemisphere in participants with TD, participants with HCP had significantly less tractography termination in the precentral gyrus (p<0.001) and more in the superior frontal gyrus (p=0.038).

In the non-lesioned hemisphere CST of participants with HCP, there were no significant differences in diffusivity metrics compared to the dominant hemisphere CST in controls. Figure 1, top panel shows CST tractography and ROI results for (A) the non-lesioned hemisphere of a participant with HCP and (B) the dominant hemisphere of a participant with TD. When compared to the non-dominant hemisphere in participants with TD, participants with HCP had significantly more tractography termination in the superior parietal gyrus (p=0.018) (Figure 1, top panel, C).

One participant with early-onset HCP (16y, M, TASC score: 25/32) and one control (27y, F) completed the TMS protocol. Figure 1, bottom panel, A shows the stimulation locations, indicated by the light blue arrows, in the SPG derived from the dMRI data and tractography of the CST. Figure 1, bottom panel also shows the average MEPs in the (B) ipsilateral, paretic hand (i.e., Paired-pulse iMEP) and (C) contralateral, non-paretic hand (i.e., Paired-pulse cMEP) for these stimulation trials in the non-lesioned hemisphere SPG. The vertical black bars indicate the timing of the first and second pulses of the paired-pulse paradigm. For the participant with HCP, all 10 trials of stimulation of the tractography-informed coordinates elicited bilateral MEPs in the FDI muscles. A latency of 23ms was found for both the ipsilateral and contralateral MEPs (Fig. 1, bottom panel, B and C). In the control participant, no MEPs were elicited in either the ipsilateral or contralateral FDI muscles for any trials (Fig. 1, bottom panel, B and C).

Conclusion

This preliminary analysis investigated differences in diffusivity metrics of the CST in both the affected and “unaffected” hemispheres of participants with early-onset HCP. We also aimed to show the feasibility of using probabilistic tractography to identify potential motor pathway projections in atypical cortical areas and demonstrate their function using TMS.

The group comparisons in this cohort showed that participants with HCP have higher MD and AD and lower C in the lesioned hemisphere CST. The microstructural properties of the lesioned hemisphere CST may reflect hypomyelination and an absence of a complex glial cellular environment,5 a result of early unilateral brain injury and contributions to impairment in this population. Additionally, participants with HCP demonstrated less tractography termination to the precentral gyrus, which is expected with a white matter injury that primarily affects strength and coordination.

Even though the injury model of early-onset HCP would suggest an altered diffusion profile due to the presence of maintained ipsilateral projections, there were no apparent microstructural differences in the non-lesioned hemisphere CST between participants with HCP and controls. However, there were differences in tractography termination, specifically in the SPG (Figure 1, top panel). This may reveal that the maintained ipsilateral pathway results in a more diffuse, macrostructural spatial organization of the CST that causes altered connectivity of descending motor projections. To further clarify whether these atypical pathways contribute directly to motor activity, TMS was performed with neuro-navigation to ensure precise targeting of the cortical area where these projections are located.

When the tractography-informed coordinates were stimulated in the participant with HCP, consistent bilateral MEPs (Fig. 1, bottom panel) at the FDI muscles were elicited. All bilateral MEPs demonstrated similarly short latencies, heavily implying they are due to activation of monosynaptic CST projections. This establishes the presence of functional CST projections from an atypical cortical region in an individual with early-onset HCP, potentially indicating a diffuse organization of the CST resulting from an early unilateral brain injury. The presence of functional CST projections from this region in the individual with HCP is further supported by the lack of activation in either hand of the control participant when the same region was stimulated (Fig. 1, bottom panel), which suggests that the MEPs elicited from the SPG in the participant with HCP was not simply a result of current spread to the motor cortex.

Overall, this preliminary work lays the foundation for altered spatial organization of the CST in the non-lesioned hemisphere of individuals with early-onset HCP. We also demonstrated the ability to elicit bilateral MEPs through stimulation of a cortical region where CST termination was identified with dMRI, indicating the presence of both typical contralateral motor pathway projections and preserved ipsilateral projections to the hands. Previous studies have demonstrated mirror movements in more proximal musculature as well, with behavioral EMG measurements of bilateral activation occurring at the wrist and elbow. Given our preliminary results, it will be beneficial to investigate the anisotropy and complexity of other motor and non-motor regions in a larger cohort to further explore and confirm these structural, functional, and behavioral relationships. In a larger cohort, bilateral MEP activation from both typical and atypical motor areas can be compared to clinical and quantitative measures of mirroring severity. This integrated, multimodal approach will not only identify novel cortical targets for individualized neuromodulation (e.g., high-definition transcranial direct current stimulation, HD-tDCS) but also provide new outcome metrics for evaluating therapeutic interventions.
