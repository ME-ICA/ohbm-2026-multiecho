Introduction

High-definition transcranial alternating current stimulation (HD-tACS) is a frequency-specific, non-invasive brain modulation technique that can entrain endogenous neural oscillations and modulate resting-state functional connectivity (rsFC) between brain regions. Previous studies have shown that tACS applied to the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) can alleviate clinical symptoms in various psychiatric disorders and modulate cognitive functions such as memory and attention. Additional evidence indicates that such stimulation can significantly alter rsFC patterns in key brain networks, including the frontoparietal network1. However, most prior research has focused on immediate effects following a single stimulation session, leaving the cumulative influence and temporal dynamics of repeated stimulation on functional connectivity underexplored.

To address this gap, the present study employed HD-tACS to precisely target the left DLPFC (F3 site) with 6 Hz stimulation. Using a longitudinal design, high-field 5T functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) data were collected at three distinct time points to assess dynamic changes in rsFC: 30 minutes after the first stimulation, 24 hours after five and ten consecutive daily sessions. This design allowed us to systematically investigate how repeated HD-tACS dynamically modulates whole-brain resting-state functional networks seeded from the F3 region. The specific aims were to determine whether repeated 6 Hz HD-tACS induces temporally distinct alterations in rsFC across short- and long-term follow-up periods.

Methods

Study Design and workflow

Participants completed 13 laboratory visits. The first visit (T0) served as the baseline assessment. After a one-week interval, participants received the first tACS session, followed by the first post-stimulation assessment (T1). They then underwent nine consecutive daily tACS sessions. The second (T5) and third (T10) post-stimulation assessments were conducted on the day following the 5th and 10th tACS sessions, respectively. Each assessment included the Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD) and Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ) scales, as well as a resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) scan (Fig 1).

Participants

The final analysis included 75 participants: 38 in the active stimulation group (18 male, 20 female) and 37 in the sham stimulation group (16 male, 21 female). There were no significant differences between groups in age, anxiety, or depression scores, confirming successful randomization.

HD-tACS

Active stimulation involved 6 Hz, 2 mA biphasic sinusoidal current for 20 minutes, with 30-second linear ramps at onset and offset. The sham condition involved the current ramping up and down within the first 60 seconds, after which no current was delivered. Electrodes were positioned according to the international 10-20 system:

•    Left hemisphere: Center electrode at F3, return electrodes at F5, F1, FC3, AF3.

•    Electrodes were Ag/AgCl, and scalp impedance was maintained below 5 kΩ.

Resting-State Functional Connectivity Analysis

A spherical region of interest (ROI) centered on the left DLPFC (MNI coordinates: −38, 44, 27) with a 15 mm radius was used for rsFC analysis. The mean time series of all voxels within this ROI was extracted and correlated with the time series of every voxel in the whole brain using Pearson’s correlation. The resulting correlation coefficients were Fisher z-transformed.

Statistical Analysis

A multi-level statistical modeling approach was used:

1.    Full factorial model: Examined the Group (Active/Sham) × Session (T0/T1/T5/T10) interaction.

2.    Stage-specific interaction contrasts: Compared Group differences between T0 vs. T1, T0 vs. T5, T0 vs. T10, T5 vs. T1, and T10 vs. T5.

3.    Within-group pairwise comparisons: Conducted separately for the Active and Sham groups at all time points.

Statistical inference was performed with a voxel-level threshold of p < 0.001 and a cluster-level threshold of p < 0.05, corrected for multiple comparisons using both FDR and FWE methods. For clusters with significant effects, mean rsFC values were extracted and analyzed using ANOVA in R with the BruceR package.

Results

We identified significant enhancement in functional connectivity between F3 and the right anterior cingulate cortex (F3–rACC) (MNI = (2,32,26),  cluster size = 225, t = 4.52,p < 0.001) and between F3 and the right middle frontal gyrus (F3–rMFG) (MNI = (18,68,6) , cluster size = 197, t = 4.85, p < 0.001) under the overall trend contrast, which reflects an increase in the active group and a decrease in the sham group (Fig. 2).

For these significant clusters, mean functional connectivity values were extracted and subjected to mixed-design ANOVA with Group (Active vs. Sham) and Session (T0, T1, T5, T10) as factors. Significant Group × Time interactions were found for both F3–rACC (F(2.88, 210.44) = 7.716, p < 0.001) and F3–rMFG (F(2.91, 212.66) = 8.730, p < 0.001).

For F3–rACC (Fig. 2a), the active group showed marginally higher connectivity than the sham group at T0 (t(73) = 1.994, p = 0.050), but this pattern reversed at T10, where the active group exhibited significantly lower connectivity than the sham group (t(73) = –3.909, p = 0.003). Moreover, within the sham group, connectivity at T10 was significantly lower than at T0 (t(73) = –4.743, p < 0.001) and T1 (t(73) = –4.037, p < 0.001).

For F3–rMFG (Fig. 2b), the active group again showed higher connectivity than the sham group at T0 (t(73) = 2.127, p = 0.037), with a reversal at T10 (active < sham; t(73) = –2.325, p = 0.023). Within the sham group, T10 connectivity was significantly lower than at T0 (t(73) = –3.522, p = 0.004), T1 (t(73) = –3.572, p = 0.004), and T5 (t(73) = –2.844, p = 0.035). Crucially, within the active group, connectivity at T10 was significantly higher than at T0 (t(73) = 3.015, p = 0.021), indicating that repeated tACS significantly enhanced this functional pathway.

Conclusion

The core finding of this study is that the active and sham stimulation groups exhibited significant and temporally dissociated patterns of change in the F3–rACC and F3–rMFG pathways. although the active stimulation group showed lower baseline connectivity at T0, a critical divergence emerged by the end of the intervention (T10): the sham group displayed a significant decline in connectivity strength across both pathways, whereas the active group showed an upward trend, with the F3–rMFG connection demonstrating a significant enhancement at T10 compared to baseline.

These two pathways serve distinct functions within the cognitive control network: the F3–rACC pathway integrates top-down executive control with attentional and monitoring signals, acting as a key hub for behavioral adaptation and adjustment; the F3–rMFG pathway supports the coordination and transmission of information within the prefrontal cortex, underpinning the efficiency of the executive control network. The present findings indicate that tAC can selectively enhance both the attentional/conflict-monitoring and internal integration functions of the cognitive control network—thereby stabilizing the behavioral monitoring circuitry while improving executive control efficiency.

These results align with several prior studies2–5. For example, Bouchard et al. (2023) reported widespread reductions in resting-state functional connectivity involving the DLPFC and regions such as the superior parietal lobule, posterior cingulate cortex, dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, and primary sensory cortex during sham transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) interventions. Such declines may stem from time-related effects in repeated measurements or natural fluctuations in brain state. Across multiple fMRI scans, the brain is not in a completely static state; cognitive control networks may exhibit weakened connectivity due to fatigue or habituation, with frontal networks being particularly susceptible to fatigue-related desynchronization.

It should be noted, however, that at the pre-intervention stage (T0), the active stimulation group already showed significantly lower baseline levels in both connectivity pairs compared to the sham group, despite the two groups being balanced in terms of age, years of education, sex, and anxiety and depression scores. Although such baseline differences may arise from random variation, they could nevertheless influence the stability and interpretation of the results. Future research should further validate the robustness of this effect in larger samples and more systematically control for or analyze the impact of baseline differences on long-term intervention outcomes.
