Introduction

Functional connectivity maps in neuroimaging are highly sensitive to methodological choices, such as brain parcellation. Limited temporal data worsens this scenario. This limitation, often due to scan time or artifacts, cannot be remedied by simply collecting more samples, leaving many experimental datasets statistically unreliable. We address this using the Laplacian Renormalization Group (LRG), a principled coarse-graining method. This approach reduces structural network dimensionality by grouping nodes via their intrinsic diffusion properties. This creates statistically-reliable coarse-grained networks (T > N'), enabling Random Matrix Theory (RMT) to precisely filter noise and recover robust functional networks. We demonstrate our method by comparing three FC pipelines. We show that common methods, such as correlations of raw signals, are dominated by non-stationary noise that creates an artificial similarity across subjects. In contrast, our LRG-RMT procedure filters this noise to reveal a rich, multimodal distribution of inter-subject variability. This hidden population structure, which points to distinct functional 'biotypes,' is completely obscured by standard methods. Overall, our work provides a robust strategy for extracting reliable networks from temporally limited data. This principled approach is essential for moving beyond noise-driven artifacts to uncover true patterns of human brain function and variability.

Methods

1. Empirical Datasets. Two primary human connectome datasets were used in this study. Dataset 1 (for Kuramoto Model): The structural network used for the Kuramoto simulations was the well-established human connectome detailed in [1], comprising N=998 nodes and mean degree = 30. Dataset 2 (for LRG-RMT Analysis): The main analysis of inter-subject variability used a high-resolution multimodal dataset from [2]. This dataset includes structural connectivity (SC) from diffusion MRI and functional time series from resting-state fMRI for P=136 healthy subjects. The SC was parcellated at the highest available resolution (N=2165 nodes) and the fMRI time series had a length of T=652 time points. 

2. Real-space Laplacian Renormalization Group. The LRG coarse-graining procedure is based on network diffusion, governed by the graph Laplacian L=D-A,  where A is the structural adjacency matrix and D is the diagonal degree matrix. The solution to the diffusion equation ṡ(τ) = −L̂s(τ) defines the network propagator K(τ) = exp(-τ L). From this, we define the Laplacian density matrix [3,4] : ρ(τ) = exp(-τ L) / Tr[exp(-τ L)] which provides a normalized measure of diffusion-based connectivity. Using this density matrix, we define the Laplacian distance between nodes: 𝒟ᵢⱼ(τ) = (1 − δᵢⱼ) / ρᵢⱼ(τ)  which is inversely proportional to the diffusion strength ρᵢⱼ(τ). For a small, fixed diffusion time τ (which preserves fine structural detail), we constructed a hierarchical dendrogram from these distances using  average linkage. Coarse-graining was achieved by cutting this dendrogram at levels that produced Kadanoff supernode networks of size N' (e.g., N' = 600, 300, 60). 3. Spectral Filtering via Random Matrix Theory (RMT). We used the Marchenko-Pastur (MP) distribution to filter noise from the derivative-based correlation matrices. The MP law describes the eigenvalue density ρ(λ)  for a random matrix C constructed from N random time series of length T: ρ(λ) = (T/N) · √[(λ₊ − λ)(λ − λ₋)] / (2πλ) where λ± = (1 ± √(N/T))² define the theoretical bounds of the noise-dominated eigenvalues. Our filtering procedure consists of: (1) Computing the eigenvalue decomposition of an empirical correlation matrix Cderiv. (2) Identifying the noise bound λ₊ based on the matrix's dimensions (N', T). (3) Constructing the "noise" correlation matrix Cnoise by summing over all eigenvector |vk> whose eigenvalues  λk fall within the MP bound (λk < λ₊ ). (4) Constructing the final, filtered RMT matrix CRMT by substracting this noise component from the original matrix: CRMT= Cderiv-Cnoise. This process selectively removes the variance associated with noise, leaving only the components that represent true, non-random signal [5].

Results

De-noising Functional Brain Networks

Estimating functional connectivity requires filtering correlation matrices to distinguish genuine interactions from noise. We analyze N neural units over T time points, computing Pearson correlations. Traditional approaches rely on arbitrary thresholds or bootstrapping. We use spectral filtering via Random Matrix Theory (RMT), leveraging the Marchenko-Pastur distribution to establish eigenvalue bounds for noise. This identifies statistically significant correlations without arbitrary cutoffs. We validated this on synthetic benchmarks testing spurious correlations from non-stationary signals and separation of emergent correlations from noise.

Non-correlated Gaussian Activity

We tested N independent random walks representing non-interacting units. Eigenvalue distributions of original signals x(t) show power-law tails (P(λ) ∼ λ⁻³/²), producing spurious correlations from cumulative fluctuations—a common error in brain studies. Analyzing temporal derivatives ẋ(t) recovers the Marchenko-Pastur law, eliminating non-stationarity artifacts and enabling proper noise detection. This derivative-based method underpins real neural recording analysis.

Emergence of Non-Trivial Correlations in Critical Network Dynamics

After removing spurious correlations, we tested if genuine structure is preserved using the Kuramoto model on the human connectome. At zero coupling (K=0), derivative analysis identifies pure noise matching Marchenko-Pastur bounds. At higher coupling, synchronization emerges, and eigenvalues deviate from noise bounds. Reconstructing matrices from signal components recovers modular structure, confirming that our pipeline discards noise while preserving functional architecture.

Finite time-series in real recordings

Neuroimaging faces temporal limits (T  N) producing noise-dominated correlation matrices. RMT filtering requires T > N for reliable signal-noise separation, needing dimensionality reduction while preserving network structure.

Coarse-graining limited temporal data

The Laplacian Renormalization Group (LRG) coarse-grains structural networks via diffusion properties. Computing K(τ) = e⁻ᵗᴸ for small τ preserves fine structural details, allowing hierarchical grouping into N′ supernodes. Applied to empirical data (N=2165, T=652), we generated reliable networks with N′ = 600, 300, 60. Eigenvalue distributions deviate from Marchenko-Pastur bounds, confirming non-random structure. As N′ decreases, noise bounds narrow, showing substantial noise reduction. Coarse-grained matrices preserve multiscale organization at all resolutions, enabling robust RMT application (see Figure 1).

Calculation of population-level functional-connectivity matrices

We compared three FC pipelines on LRG-coarsened networks (N′ ∼300): raw BOLD, derivatives, and RMT-filtered derivatives. Inter-subject cophenetic distances differ markedly: raw BOLD shows narrow unimodal distributions, derivatives increase mean distance with weak bimodality, and LRG-RMT filtering yields broad, multimodal distributions, revealing distinct population subgroups. Group-averaged matrices show raw data has spuriously high correlations, which decrease with derivatives and RMT filtering, exposing cleaner modular architecture. Our LRG-RMT framework reveals true inter-subject variability, providing robust methodology for temporally limited neuroimaging data (see Figure 2).

Conclusion

Our work presents a statistically rigorous pipeline for estimating functional correlations from neuroimaging data with limited temporal length. Temporal derivation removes non-stationary trends, the Laplacian Renormalization Group (LRG) reduces the structural network to satisfy the requirement T > N', and RMT then isolates the non-random eigenmodes of the resulting correlation matrices.

Beyond the insights gained from theoretical models, it is essential to demonstrate how our pipeline enhances the analysis of empirical fMRI data. The method used to compute functional connectivity is not a minor technical choice: different strategies can emphasize noise, mask genuine differences, or reveal stable features of brain organization. Figure 2 illustrates this point by comparing three processing schemes applied to the same LRG-reduced structural partition. Using temporal derivatives and then applying RMT filtering progressively attenuates spuriously high correlations, highlighting more subtle inter-subject differences. These steps leave the large-scale modular scaffold set by anatomy essentially unchanged, but they increase the reliability with which individual variations can be detected. Thus, selecting an appropriate FC pipeline is critical for drawing statistically robust conclusions about structure–function relationships in the human brain.

The LRG is a data-driven method that iteratively groups structurally-connected brain regions into larger 'supernodes.' Unlike traditional atlases that often create partitions of uniform size, the LRG produces heterogeneous 'supernodes' that reflect the brain's complex structural organization. By optimizing the graph Laplacian, this coarse-graining method is specifically designed to preserve the most important diffusion dynamics and functional pathways of the original, high-resolution network.
