Introduction

Speech perception involves complex hierarchical processing of multiple sensory modalities1, especially in noisy environments. Speech segmentation & analysis of phonemes, syllables & visual cues like lip movements significantly influence auditory perception.

 The "McGurk effect" is a classic example, illustrating how incongruent audio-visual (AV) stimuli create illusory cross-modal speech sounds. Key brain regions for multisensory speech perception include the frontal, parietal, and superior temporal sulcus (STS), primary & secondary auditory cortices, occipital & prefrontal cortex2.  

Neural integration of AV streams relies on temporal mechanisms like neural tracking of rhythms in multisensory signals. Individual alpha frequency (IAF, 8-12 Hz) is a prominent brain rhythm linked to the temporal window of multisensory integration. Findings suggest, IAF correlates with temporal illusions, making it plausible that the speed of IAF modulates illusion perception3. A recent study found that lower alpha and beta band oscillations over parieto-occipital sensors predicted illusion perception4. IAF has been linked to cognitive performance in working memory, AV integration & visual acuity.  Speed of alpha band oscillations is also reported to predict temporal resolution of visual perception3. Hence, we hypothesise, alpha oscillation speed could modulate illusion perception. 

Furthermore, new methods of neuromodulation might provide stronger corroborative evidence of causal relationships between brain regions and sensory processing and integration2. Increasing evidence demonstrates the possibility of modulating perceptual & cognitive processes with tACS3. However, the relationship between IAF & modification of subjective illusory perception & its variability remains unclear. We tried to understand this by applying transcranial electric stimulations (tACS) at IAF & IAF±2 Hz (offset) over the occipital region during an ongoing McGurk task & studying the changes in illusory perception.

Methods

We collected EEG resting state and multisensory illusory speech perception task data, along with tACS over the occipital cortex. Data from 27 participants (10 females) were collected and analysed, with a mean age of 24.96 ± 2.75 years. Written informed consent was collected under the experimental protocol, and ethical clearance was taken from Institutional Human Ethics Committee (IHEC) of the Indian Institute of Technology, Jodhpur.

The experiment spanned two sessions (average interval: 3.3±2.5 days). On Day 1, resting-state EEG (5min) was recorded to estimate individual alpha frequency (IAF; range: 8.15–12.15Hz, mean: 9.9Hz). Participants then completed a multisensory speech perception task, reporting their subjective experiences of audio-visual (AV) stimuli using a McGurk paradigm5. On Day 2, participants repeated the task during tACS at O1/O2 (1,500μA) delivered at their IAF or IAF±2Hz (Fig. 1B). Four AV stimuli were used: three congruent syllables (/pa/, /ta/, /ka/) and one McGurk (auditory /pa/, visual /ka/) (Fig. 1A) stimulus eliciting the /ta/ illusion.

Day 1 experiment was carried out for five blocks, each block with 120 trials. Day 2 experiment (Fig. 1C) was carried out for three blocks at IAF, and one block for IAF-2 and IAF+2, respectively. Each block had the four stimulus types (30 trials): Both congruent and incongruent videos were displayed with zero AV lags. The subjects were instructed to report what they heard while watching the articulator, using a set of four keys. The four choices were /pa/, /ta/, /ka/, and “anything else”. 

The stimuli were presented via Presentation software and displayed through an LED monitor. Articulatory sounds were delivered through headphones.
Behavioural data were analysed in terms of the percentage response corresponding to each stimulus trial. Wilcoxon signed rank test was performed to compare pair of responses. For analysing the interaction effect of tACS on responses, Friedman Test was performed. Overall a decrease in illusory perception was recorded for responses during tACS condition as compared to control (no stimulation) condition. The EEG data used for IAF calculation was downsampled at 250 Hz, filtered with a high-pass frequency cut-off at 0.5-1 Hz and a low-pass frequency cut-off at 40-50 Hz. After common average rereferencing, ICA was performed to remove eye movements and muscle artifacts. The IAF was then calculated from this cleaned data using p-welch method.

Results

Hit rates were measured by calculating the average percentage response of each response category across all participants. In the no-stimulation condition, hit rates for congruent syllables /ta/ and /ka/ were 94.72% and 97.08%, respectively, while /pa/ was 85.2%. Under tACS (IAF and offsets), hit rates were 96.74% (/ta/), 97.84% (/ka/), and 88.52% (/pa/), averaged across participants. Wilcoxon signed rank test was performed for pairwise comparisons of hit rates of different responses. Hit rates for /pa/ were significantly lower than both /ta/ and /ka/ (p < 0.001) in both conditions, with a non-significant difference between /ta/ and /ka/(p > 0.05) (Fig. 2A). This difference in hit rates for the no stimulation condition aligns with previous literature5. Additionally, the difference between illusory /ta/ and non-illusory /pa/ during McGurk trials was significant without tACS (p < 0.001) and tACS at IAF-2 (p < 0.01) but not under tACS at IAF or IAF+2 Hz (Fig. 2B). The median of the percentage response of McGurk /ta/ i.e., illusory percept, without tACS was 80.0%, with tACS at IAF was reported to be 70.0%,  at IAF+2 at 73.33 and IAF-2 at 76.67 indicating a shift in the illusory perception among the participants, from more illusory perception in the case of no stimulation to lower illusion perceived upon application of neurostimulation, lowest being at IAF(Fig. 2C). Furthermore, Friedman’s test was performed on the percentage response of both congruent and incongruent stimuli with stimulation as independent variable. We observed an overall decrease in the illusory response (χ2 = 3.836, p=0.279, df=3), however, post-hoc tests revealed no significant difference (for adjusted p-values). There was no significant difference in the percentage response of congruent stimuli across the stimulation conditions except for /pa/, which showed a significant increase in hit rates for tACS at IAF (p < 0.05) as compared to without stimulation. No such significant difference was observed for tACS at offsets.

We wanted to see if there exists any correlation between subject specific IAF and their respective illusory response. For the control we saw no such relation, but for all 3 tACS condition, a positive correlation was found, indicating subjects with higher IAF tend to perceive more illusory response, with IAF-2 condition showing the most positive correlation between IAF and percentage illusory response. Spearman’s correlation was calculated for the above analysis with initial p values < 0.05 but after FDR correction p_fdr = 0.0756 (Fig. 2d).

Conclusion

This study suggests that occipital alpha oscillations modulate audiovisual speech perception, particularly in resolving cross-modal illusions, such as the McGurk effect. Participants showed higher accuracy for congruent syllables (/ta/, /ka/) while /pa/ elicited more ambiguous responses due to less distinctive features.

The primary finding of this study is the reduction of the McGurk effect under tACS at the individual’s alpha frequency, indicating that alpha-band modulation can causally affect multisensory integration. The effect appeared to be driven by stimulating the occipital cortex at IAF and offsets, suggesting disruption of temporal alignment, which is needed for effective audiovisual integration.  Furthermore, in the absence of stimulation (and at IAF-2 Hz), participants exhibited a robust McGurk effect, significantly favouring the illusory /ta/ percept over the veridical auditory /pa/. However, this dominance of the illusion was disrupted when tACS was applied at IAF and IAF+2 Hz. Specifically, the significant difference between illusory and non-illusory responses observed in the baseline condition (without stimulation) disappeared under stimulation at IAF.

Another critical insight from our analysis (Fig. 2d) is the "unmasking" effect of tACS on the relationship between IAF and illusion perception. In the absence of stimulation, the correlation between a participant's IAF and their susceptibility to the McGurk effect was weak or noisy. However, applying tACS appeared to stabilize neural activity, revealing a latent correlation: under stimulation, participants with faster intrinsic alpha tended to perceive the illusion less, while those with slower alpha perceived it more. This suggests that non-invasive brain stimulation can reduce neural "noise," allowing the intrinsic structural/functional relationships between oscillation speed and perception to manifest more clearly.

The use of individualized stimulation and balanced conditions strengthens these findings. Overall, this study demonstrates that non-invasive modulation of occipital alpha oscillations via tACS alters subjective audiovisual speech perception. We show that stimulating the occipital cortex at the Individual Alpha Frequency (IAF) reduces the dominance of the McGurk illusion. Furthermore, our results indicate that tACS can unmask latent relationships between intrinsic neural rhythms and behavioural phenotypes. These findings move beyond correlational observations, providing causal evidence that the speed of alpha oscillations plays a governing role in how the human brain segments and integrates multisensory speech information. As a next step, we plan to develop a computational framework to model how tACS dynamics interact with intrinsic oscillators to modulate multisensory perception as well as investigate the underlying neural mechanisms of the obtained behavioural results, using the EEG dataset.
