Introduction

Foundation models have rapidly transformed the landscape of AI by enabling general-purpose, pretrained tools that rapidly adapt to new tasks. In several fields, foundation models (such as the well-known GPT model) have transformed our ability to handle novel tasks. Yet, neuroscience remains largely untouched by this new paradigm, in part due to data scarcity, high dimensionality, and variability in acquisition protocols of neuroimaging data.1,2 In particular, structural brain MRI has largely been left behind by this paradigm. Here, we introduce NeuroFM, a foundation model trained entirely on synthetic (AI-generated) structural MRI data, designed to overcome these limitations and support a wide range of neuroimaging applications. The model learns compact, anatomically meaningful latent representations by jointly predicting multiple morphometric targets and requires only minimal preprocessing (Fig. 1a). Leveraging numerous small test datasets, we demonstrate that NeuroFM enables efficient inference across five neuroscience domains: clinical, cognitive, developmental, socio-behavioural, and quality metrics (Fig. 1b). With linear regression models trained on extracted features, we adapt the model to tasks such as Alzheimer’s disease classification, ADHD subtype differentiation, population-level brain age modelling, and subject-level prediction of future dementia risk. The model also supports MRI quality control estimation and produces anatomically valid attribution maps. NeuroFM offers a foundation for scalable, individualized modelling in neuroimaging that paves the way toward precision neuroscience (Fig. 1c).

Methods

We used 17 publicly available datasets: UK Biobank (n=44,790), ADNI (n=2,011), NIFD (n=259), IXI (n=569), OASIS3 (n=2,633), LDM100k (n=100,000), ADHD-200 (n=880), ABIDE (n=904), 1000-FCP (n=7,496), AOMIC (n=1,911), BRATS 2023 (n=1,244), EDSD (n=290), HCP (n=890), IADNI (n=98), IBSR (n=18), MALC2012 (n=34), and MindBoggle101 (n=92), for a total of 182,280 unique MRI volumes. Only LDM100k was used for supervised pretraining. All other datasets were used for evaluation and task adaptation. All volumes were minimally preprocessed: resampling with FreeSurfer to 1 mm isotropic in a 256³ grid, followed by skull stripping with SynthStrip.3 No intersubject template registration was applied.

NeuroFM is a 3D CNN with parameter-efficient bottleneck blocks. The base model outputs four targets: predicted brain age, sex, intracranial volume and ventricular volume. These targets guide the model to understand structural morphology and relevant age-related changes.4 Pretraining used all 100k synthetic samples with strong data augmentation, an exponentially decaying learning rate and early stopping. Logistic/linear regression models were trained on these features for each external task without updating the foundation model. 5-fold cross-validation and permutation testing (500 iterations) were used to validate all results.

In the clinical domain, we applied NeuroFM to predict fronto-temporal dementia presence in the NIFD dataset, Alzheimer’s disease presence in the ADNI dataset, and glioma tumour size prediction in the BRATS 2023 dataset. All of these tasks were performed by training a logistic regression model on NeuroFM’s extracted features for all training volumes. Relevance maps/highlights were created by finetuning NeuroFM on the target task (e.g. AD presence) and then applying layerwise relevance propagation (LRP). LRP was performed on NIFD, ADNI, and BRATS 2023.

For the cognitive domain, we looked at the ability of NeuroFM to predict two common cognitive metrics: MMSE (mini-mental state exam), and CDR (clinical dementia rating) on the NIFD and ADNI dataset. We performed two types of analysis: correlation with the brain age gap (BAG), and direct prediction of impairment using logistic regression on NeuroFM’s extracted features.

We used the UK Biobank dataset to analyse brain age relationships in the socio-behavioural domain, looking at a number of pathological and lifestyle factors that are purportedly associated with worse brain health. To do this, we used NeuroFM to predict the brain age for each volume in the UK Biobank. BAG associations were performed using ANOVA with FDR correction.

Using linear regression on NeuroFM’s features, we trained it to predict different quality measures from MRIQC,5 such as SNR (signal-to-noise ratio), and FWHM (smoothness of voxels). Training was performed on a broad set of datasets to increase training artifact diversity: IXI, OASIS3, 1000-FCP, AOMIC, EDSD, HCP, IADNI, IBSR, MALC2012, and MindBoggle101. A logistic regression was also trained with NeuroFM features to predict the presence of augmented motion and ghosting artifacts in the ABIDE dataset. A model was also trained using MRIQC’s raw predicted features for prediction performance comparison.

On ADNI, we computed NeuroFM derived estimates of BAG, intracranial volume and ventricular volume for healthy T1w scans. Sex-specific normative models were built using mixed effects polynomial fits over ages 50 to 95. These curves generally aligned with prior population level work4 and matched FreeSurfer-based true values. Using the normative z scores, we fit Cox proportional hazards models for three longitudinal transitions: healthy to MCI, healthy to AD and MCI to AD. Covariates included the three normative scores, age and sex. Individual five year risks were computed from subject specific survival functions and updated at each follow-up visit.

Results

Across neuroscience domains, NeuroFM has demonstrated broad transferability from synthetic pretraining to real neuroimaging applications. In clinical classification, features from the encoder consistently outperformed comparison architectures. In NIFD, dementia vs healthy classification reached AUC=0.83 (Fig. 2a), and FTD subtype prediction reached 0.76–0.83. Relevance analyses indicated stable highlighting of prefrontal and temporal regions. In ADNI, AD vs healthy yielded AUC=0.77 (Fig. 2b), and AD vs MCI (mild cognitive impairment) vs healthy produced one–vs–rest AUCs of 0.51–0.73, all above chance. For BraTS-2023, tumour size classification achieved AUC = 0.72, and relevance maps identified lesion locations without tumour-specific pretraining (Fig. 2c). Model-derived features also captured cognitive status. BAG increased significantly with CDR and MMSE impairment in both NIFD and ADNI, with larger shifts in FTD cases (all pairwise p < 0.0001 after FDR correction) (Fig. 2d). Linear models trained on NeuroFM’s embeddings predicted impaired cognition with AUCs of 0.69–0.78 in NIFD and 0.65–0.75 in ADNI, consistently above baselines. Effects were weakest near clinical cutoffs, which matched the modest BAG separation in those ranges.

For the socio-behavioural BAG analyses on the UK Biobank, higher BAG was found to be significantly correlated with Type 2 diabetes, stroke, Parkinson’s disease, bipolar disorder, traumatic brain injury, regular alcohol use, high blood pressure, and obesity, with effects surviving multiple comparison correction (all p < 0.001) (Fig. 2e). Cognitive performance and higher educational attainment were associated with lower BAG. Sex interaction models showed stronger BAG effects (in years) in females for depression history (β ≈ -0.35) and APOE ε4 carrier status (β ≈ -0.25), and in males for obesity (β ≈ 0.45).

For neurodevelopmental disorders, structural separation was limited but reproducible. In ADHD200, ADHD classification reached AUC=0.59 and subtype AUCs up to 0.56. In ABIDE, autism classification reached AUC=0.57. t-SNE visualizations showed no diagnostic clustering, indicating that the signal comes from weak distributed variations rather than gross morphometry. Only the largest encoder variant performed reliably, which aligns with small effect sizes reported in the literature.

For image quality assessment, linear regressors trained on the encoder embeddings predicted MRIQC metrics with high fidelity, with R² of 0.89 for CNR, 0.91 for SNR, 0.71 for FWHM and 0.54 for white matter volume (for an example, see Fig. 2f). Predicted metric distributions resembled MRIQC outputs. For artifact detection on the ABIDE dataset, the NeuroFM method outperformed MRIQC on ghosting detection (AUC=0.70 vs AUC=0.61). Overall, this approach offered near MRIQC-level performance at substantially lower computational cost and without the pipeline failures that plague MRIQC.

Finally, normative modelling in ADNI showed that NeuroFM derived trajectories can support individual level risk estimation. Brain age, ICV and ventricular predictions were integrated into Cox hazards models that identified elevated dementia risk up to five years before diagnosis. NeuroFM’s brain age predictions also distinguished progressing from non progressing MCI (Fig. 2g) and produced subject level risk profiles consistent with clinical outcomes. Hazard ratios indicated consistent effects of accelerated brain ageing, ventricular enlargement and reduced intracranial volume on progression risk. Overall, our combined NeuroFM-derived risk model demonstrated reliable predictive ability of dementia risk over 5 years prior to diagnosis, even in individual subjects (Fig. 2h). This result serves as a critical test-bed for future precision medicine applications of this technology.

Conclusion

NeuroFM demonstrates that a structural MRI foundation model trained entirely on synthetic data can transfer reliably to real clinical and population datasets. The model produces features that generalize across scanners, cohorts and tasks, and the downstream models built on these features outperform conventional approaches while remaining lightweight and simple to adapt. Critically, NeuroFM can produce meaningful morphometric estimates (brain age, ICV, ventricle volume) with no modification and minimal preprocessing, while also being adaptable for advanced use-cases. Overall, we find that the model’s internal features capture clinically meaningful variation in ageing, neurodegeneration, cognition, image quality and subtle neurodevelopmental effects, and they remain robust even after correcting for acquisition related confounds. Our normative modelling work in ADNI indicates that NeuroFM’s outputs can be formed into patient-level risk estimates that perform with stability over time. Taken together, our results suggest that our methods can move structural MRI closer to rapid, individualized assessment. The remaining limitations concern modest effect sizes for some phenotypes and the need for broader validation, but our framework provides practical foundation for precision neuroimaging.
