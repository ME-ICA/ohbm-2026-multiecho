Introduction

Interactive accounts of social cognition propose that inferring others’ intentions arises from the interaction between two distinct networks: the mirror neuron system (henceforth MNS), supporting covert simulation and action decoding from observed movements, on one hand, and the mentalizing network, supporting higher-order abstract reasoning required for mental state attribution, and also referred to as theory of mind, on the other [1]. However, whether these two networks share overlapping nodes remains a matter of debate [2].

Methods

We examined the causal contribution of the MNS and mentalizing networks using direct electrical stimulation (henceforth DES) in six patients undergoing awake craniotomy for tumor resection (four with right-hemisphere tumors and two with left-hemisphere tumors). All patients (M = 46.57 years; SD = 11.21) were recruited from the Cruces University Hospital in Bilbao, and screened for cognitive status using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) (M = 29.2; SD = 1.07). During surgery, DES was applied to cortical and subcortical sites while patients performed an action comprehension task, targeting the MNS [3], as well as a for intraoperative use adapted version of the Reading the Mind in the Eyes task [4], targeting mentalizing processes. Task-related behavioral responses were used to identify functionally critical regions to be preserved during resection, thereby establishing functional boundaries from a clinical standpoint.

Results

At the cortical level, overlapping DES-positive stimulation sites across tasks were observed in the right inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), indicating a shared or integrative node between both, the mirror neuron and the mentalizing system. At the subcortical level, positive stimulation sites across both tasks involved the right superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF).

Conclusion

While mirror and mentalizing networks are largely dissociable, the right IFG and the right SLF emerge as critical cortical and subcortical convergence sites, respectively. Beyond theoretical implications, these results directly inform functional mapping strategies aimed at preserving social cognitive abilities during awake brain surgery.
