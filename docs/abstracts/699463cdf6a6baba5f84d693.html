Introduction

Mood disturbances can be associated with reproductive events across the female reproductive lifespan. Examples include Premenstrual Syndrome (PMS), linked to the menstrual cycle, and Postpartum Depression (PPD), occurring during the postpartum period. It is well established that such mood disturbances are not caused by abnormal hormonal levels per se, but rather by an increased vulnerability to fluctuations in ovarian hormones across the menstrual cycle, or during pregnancy, and postpartum.1 Why some individuals are more sensitive to these fluctuations remains unknown, and it is not yet clear whether reproductive mood disorders share underlying mechanisms or involve distinct pathways. There are hypotheses suggesting that individuals sensitive to these hormonal changes may have a dysregulation in neurobiological pathways involved in hormone-related mood and stress regulation, supporting the idea of a shared vulnerability.2 This is further supported by recent evidence showing that a reported history of premenstrual symptoms is associated with an increased risk of developing PPD, with greater severity of premenstrual symptoms associated with greater PPD symptom severity.3 However, to date, no study has delineated the neural underpinnings of hormonal sensitivity of PPD moderated by a history of premenstrual symptoms, and it remains unclear whether increased risk across reproductive events reflects shared neural vulnerability or distinct neurobiological pathways. Therefore, the present study examined whether early postpartum brain structural correlates differ as a function of PPD status, a reported history of PMS, and their interaction, in order to test whether PMS-related vulnerability to PPD is reflected in shared or distinct neuroanatomical patterns.

Methods

The study included data of 143 women postpartum who participated in the RiPoD (Risk of Postpartum Depression) study,4 conducted at the University hospital in Aachen. In the RiPoD study, participants were followed from the first week of postpartum (T0) until twelve weeks postpartum (T4). At T0, an MRI assessment was conducted, acquiring structural (T1-weighted) and diffusion tension imaging (DTI) data, as well as a clinical-anamnestic interview based on the DSM-5 criteria. Every third week between T0 and T4, the participants were monitored using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS), and at T4 they went through a final clinical interview to assess a diagnosis of PPD according to the DSM-5 criteria. At T4, participants also completed a retrospective self-report regarding premenstrual symptoms prior to pregnancy, using the 36-item Premenstrual Tension Syndrome Scale (PTSS). Participants were categorized into two groups based on the PTSS score, those with a history of low (PTSS ≤ 10) versus high (PTSS ≥ 11) premenstrual symptoms, classifying the high symptom group as having a history of PMS prior to pregnancy. Participants were further divided into subgroups based on both PMS status and PPD status, resulting in four subgroups used in the analyses. Structural MRI data were preprocessed using SPM12 and then analyzed conducting both a whole-brain and ROI-based approaches. Flexible factorial designs were used in the Voxel Based Morphometry (VBM) analysis, to assess group differences in grey matter volume (GMV) and white matter volume (WMV) on a whole-brain level, and in the Surface Based Morphometry (SBM) analysis, to assess group differences in cortical thickness on a whole-brain level. Additionally, a ROI-based analysis was conducted for ROIs previously associated with PMS and/or PPD in the literature, for each of the brain measures, using two-way ANOVAs with brain measures  as outcome. A prediction model using multiple logistic regression was also implemented to predict PPD status based on brain measures showing significant group differences and PMS status. DTI data are being further preprocessed using FSL and will then be analyzed using tract-based spatial statistics (TBSS) and probabilistic tractography.

Results

No significant GMV alterations were found neither on a whole-brain or ROI-based level. One significant cluster was found for the volume of white matter in the whole-brain level analyses, near the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), when testing the interaction between groups. The participants without a history of PMS had significantly smaller volume in the first week postpartum if they later developed PPD, compared to those who did not develop PPD. However, the participants with a history of PMS showed no significant differences in WMV the first week postpartum, regardless of if they later developed PPD or not. On the ROI-based level, a significant interaction between PMS and PPD status was found in the WMV of the right uncinate, where the WMV was significantly larger the first week postpartum for those without a history of PMS if they later developed PPD, while for participants with a history of PMS there was no significant difference the first week postpartum, regardless of if they later developed PPD or not. Additionally, the volume of the right uncinate in the first week postpartum could, together with PMS status, predict PPD status in later postpartum. These results suggest WMV features of women with PPD, moderated by a history of premenstrual symptoms. Furthermore, a main effect of PPD, independent of PMS status, was found in WMV on the ROI-based level,  within the left and right superior longitudinal fasciculus, where a significantly larger WMV was found in the first week postpartum for those who later developed PPD. Lastly, no significant alterations were found in cortical thickness on a whole-brain level or when testing the interactions between PMS and PPD on a ROI-based level, but a main effect of a history of PMS, independent of PPD status, was found in the cortical folding of the right insula, where those with a history of PMS had significantly less cortical folding in this region, compared to those without a history of PMS.

Conclusion

These results provide evidence that WMV differences near the OFC and within the right uncinate are associated with postpartum depression, in ways that depend on women’s history of premenstrual symptoms. However, distinct structure alterations for the main effect of both PPD and PMS were also discovered, indicating partly independent neuroanatomical patterns rather than consistent brain features across the two conditions. More research with larger sample sizes and groups preferably diagnosed with Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder (PMDD), the more severe form of PMS, is needed to draw further and more definite conclusions about shared and unique brain features across reproductive mood disorders.
