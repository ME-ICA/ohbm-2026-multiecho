Introduction

Human vestibular processing engages a distributed network that has been mapped using fMRI in combination with artificial vestibular stimulation, such as caloric or galvanic vestibular stimulation. This network involves multisensory regions, including the posterior insula, parietal operculum, temporo-parietal junction, cingulate and frontal areas1. However, isolating the neural circuits specifically underlying vestibular processing of self-orientation within the gravitational field, upward and downward self-motion, cannot be achieved in MRI scanners. Yet, causal mapping of these circuits can be achieved using invasive electrical brain stimulation (EBS) and stereoelectroencephalography (SEEG) for functional connectivity analysis in epileptic patients.

Methods

We retrospectively analyzed the effects of EBS in 354 patients with drug-resistant epilepsy (176 females; mean age ± SD: 25.5 ± 14 years) who underwent SEEG at the Epileptology Department of La Timone Hospital, France, between August 2000 and December 2021. We analyzed the effects of 11,004 high-frequency (50 Hz) and 8,704 low-frequency (1 Hz) EBS, that elicited 3,015 clinical responses. We specifically examined the occurrence and characteristics of gravity illusion (upward or downward translation) and identified the electrode contacts responsible for producing these perceptions.

For functional connectivity analysis, we extracted an 8-s pre-stimulation window and a 4-s post-stimulation window. Functional connectivity between distant SEEG contacts were quantified using the non-linear correlation coefficient h²2 computed from broadband signals (1–45 Hz). For each stimulation, we computed h²pre-EBS and h²post-EBS connectivity matrices, whose difference captured stimulation-induced changes in connectivity.

We assessed resting-state connectivity of each electrode contact whose stimulation evoked vertical self-motion perception using a normative functional connectome derived from 1,000 healthy adults. For each stimulation site, we generated whole-brain seed-based functional connectivity maps, showing positive and negative correlations3. A sensitivity analysis identified voxels consistently connected to translation-evoking sites across patients (threshold: t ≥ 7). A specificity analysis then compared these maps with connectivity profiles from 452 EBS that did not evoke vertical self-motion illusion, thereby isolating networks uniquely associated with these illusions.

Results

11 vertical self-motion illusion from 9 patients were reported. Illusions were evoked by EBS in the parahippocampal cortex (n=3), anterior hippocampus (n=2), anterior insula (n=2), inferior frontal gyrus pars opercularis (n=2), supplementary motor area (n=1), and middle cingulate cortex (n=1) (Figure 1).

Electrophysiology-based functional connectivity revealed significant decrease of the connectivity within temporal networks, particularly significant of the anterior hippocampus (p=0.04), anterior middle temporal gyrus (p=0.04), and temporal pole (p=0.03). At the network level, we observed reduced connectivity between temporal and frontal lobes (p=0.01), between mesial frontal and parietal lobes (p=0.04), between mesial parietal and mesial temporal lobes (p=0.01), and within temporal areas (p < 0.001).

Sensitivity analysis of resting state functional connectivity showed that 88% of vertical motion-evoking stimulation sites were functionally connected to the middle cingulate cortex, insula, and superior frontal gyrus, and anticorrelated with the inferior parietal lobule and cerebellar Crus I/II. Specificity analysis identified the middle cingulate cortex as the only region whose connectivity profile uniquely characterized vertical self-motion illusion. Taken together, the middle cingulate cortex emerged as the most sensitive and specific hub associated with illusory self-motion along the gravitational axis. 

Conclusion

Our study provides causal evidence that self-motion perception along the gravitational axis engages a distributed network comprising the anterior hippocampus, insula, frontal regions, and middle cingulate cortex. Converging results from electrophysiology-based functional connectivity and connectome analyses consistently identified these regions as key components of the network. Notably, the middle cingulate cortex emerged as a central hub in both connectome-based sensitivity and specificity analyses, supporting its key role in otolithic information processing, and multisensory self-motion perception4. In parallel, the involvement of the anterior hippocampus revealed by electrophysiological connectivity analyses is consistent with its established role in vestibular processing and spatial navigation. Its disruption during EBS, along with reduced connectivity with other brain regions, suggests that the anterior hippocampus contributes to the experience of vertical motion by altering egocentric spatial representations.
