Introduction

Source-based morphometry (SBM) effectively estimates independent gray matter patterns1; however, traditional analyses of subject-specific loading parameters often overlook how these networks co-vary in a coordinated manner. To capture these networks’ structural interactions, we introduce a co-modulation framework that extends SBM by quantifying the degree to which structural components vary together across subjects. We applied this framework to schizophrenia, a disorder characterized by widespread abnormal brain integration2, to investigate system-level pathology that component-specific analyses may miss. Using structural MRI (sMRI) data from patients and healthy controls, we demonstrate widespread reductions in structural co-modulation in the schizophrenia group, with the most prominent disruptions observed within and between visual, default-mode, and cognitive control networks. 

Methods

The co-modulation framework extends traditional SBM by quantifying interactions between structural components, providing a network-like representation of coordinates structural cortical organization. We performed a spatially constrained independent component analysis (sc-ICA) using the 53-component Neuromark_fMRI_1.0 template3, yielding a 53-dimensional loading vector (si) for each subject. To capture large-scale structural coupling, we computed a subject-specific co-modulation matrix (Mi) via the outer product Mi = sisiT. This formulation characterized how spatially distinct gray matter networks are jointly expressed. Group differences were assessed using element-wise two-sample t-tests, with significance corrected for multiple comparisons using FDR (𝑝 < 0.05). Figure 1 shows the workflow of this study. 

We applied this approach to the fBIRN dataset, analyzing T1-weighted structural scans acquired on 3T Siemens and GE scanners. The sample consisted of 210 participants with schizophrenia (SZ; mean age 39.4 ± 11.7; 161 males) and 195 healthy controls (HC; mean age 37.7 ± 11.2; 139 males).

Results

Group analyses revealed a global reduction in structural co-modulation in SZ compared to HC (Figure 2a and Figure 2c). T-tests corrected for multiple comparisons (FDR 𝑝 < 0.05) confirmed these widespread deficits, with the strongest reductions (HC > SZ) observed within and between the visual, cognitive control, and default-mode networks (Figure 2b). Connectogram visualizations (𝑝 < 0.01) further illustrated this extensive disruption of structural integration, particularly highlighting diminished coupling between cognitive control and visual domains in patients (Figure 2d). 

Conclusion

This study introduces co-modulation to quantify inter-network structural dependencies, revealing that schizophrenia is characterized by a profound reorganization of structural architecture rather than simple localized deficits. We observed a widespread reduction in structural integration in patients, particularly the dorsal visual stream of its normal coupling with frontal executive regions, providing a structural basis for known attentional deficits2. However, this pattern was not a simple, uniform loss. The hippocampus showed an increased coupling with primary sensory cortices, suggesting a breakdown in the brain's ability to sort relevant from irrelevant information. The anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) exhibited over-coupling with the cerebellum, which could explain why patients get stuck on certain thoughts or experience the disorganized thinking often seen in schizophrenia2, since the ACC acts as the brain's error-detection system, and the cerebellum helps coordinate and fine-tune complex thoughts 2,4,5. In sum, this study introduces structural co-modulation as a novel and valuable method for quantifying the organization of brain structure. We demonstrated its utility in schizophrenia, revealing a global reduction in the coordination between structural networks, particularly among visual, default-mode, and cognitive control systems. As next steps, we will examine how these structural patterns relate to cognitive performance and clinical symptoms.
