Introduction

Psychosis comprises a spectrum of severe mental disorders marked by hallucinations, delusions and disorganized cognition and behavior1. Its biologically heterogeneity has hindered biomarker biomarkers discovery and precision treatment2. Sex differences further contribute to this complexity, with inconsistent findings across prevalence, cognition and brain function ranging from female advantages to male advantages or no difference at all3. Resting- state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rsfMRI) studies have linked sex-specific functional network connectivity (FNC) alterations between central executive, salience, and default mode networks to schizophrenia psychosis in both males and females, sex-differentiating differences as symptom severity in males but not females4,5, yet sex has not been directly accounted in subtyping frameworks. These inconsistencies suggest the presence of sex-varying neurobiological heterogeneity that remains unexplored. Here, we hypothesize that sex-related neurobiological heterogeneity in psychosis manifests as sex-dominant neurosubtypes, characterized with male or female overrepresentation3⁄4but not exclusively confined to them. Using rsfMRI, we aimed to identify the presence of sex-dominant psychosis imaging neurosubtypes (PINs) with distinct functional connectivity patterns and differential associations with cognitive performance.

Methods

We analyzed rsfMRI data from the Bipolar-Schizophrenia Network on Intermediate Phenotypes (B-SNIP 1&2), including 1127 participants with psychosis (571 females, 556 males) and 626 healthy controls. Participants with psychosis met the criteria for Bipolar I Disorder (BP), Schizophrenia (SZ), or Schizoaffective Disorder (SAD). Cognitive performance was administered through Brief Assessment of Cognition (BACS) composite score. Leveraging the standardized Neuromark 2.2 template, which incorporates 105 multiscale intrinsic connectivity networks (ICNs) derived from a large population, through spatially constrained Independent Component

Analysis framework, we extracted subject-specific msICNs and 105×105 multiscale FNC (msFNC) connectivity matrix (Figure 1.A).

Data were split into discovery (~80% of the sample) and a replication sets (~20% of the sample). After age correction, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was applied to discovery set msFNC from participants with psychosis, retaining components with 99% of the variance. Two sample t-tests were then used to identify components exhibiting significant sex effects (Figure 1.B). Projections onto these sex-specific components were clustered separately within females and males, yielding two optimal clusters for each sex group. All psychosis participants, including those in replication set were subsequently assigned to one of the four clusters based on minimum- distance proximity, enabling sex-informed clustering while allowing individuals of either sex to map to the most neurobiologically similar subtype. To ensure robustness and reproducibility, this procedure was repeated using an ensemble framework with 100 bootstrap models, and final sex- dominant PIN labels were defined by the most frequent assigned cluster across ensembles.

To evaluate validity of sex-dominant PINs, we performed non-parametric permutation tests. Characterization efforts also examined whole brain-wide msFNC group differences of sex- dominant PINs relative to controls, and PIN-specific distinct dysconnectivity was isolated. Sparse modeling (LASSO regression) evaluated associations between PIN-specific dysconnectivity patterns and cognitive performance, with replication testing in the hold-out sample.

Results

Four representative sex-dominant PINs were identified: female PIN-1 (fPIN-1), female PIN-2 (fPIN-2), male PIN-1 (mPIN-1), and male PIN-2 (mPIN-2). Three PINs showed significant overrepresentation of one sex in the replication set (fPIN-1: p < 0.05, fPIN-2: p < 0.05, mPIN-2: p < 0.05), while mPIN-1 showed a trend toward sex dominance that did not reach the significance threshold (p = 0.14) (Figure 1.C). Additionally, PIN membership were independent of diagnoses (BP, SZ and SAD; Replication set ꭓ2 = 7.9, p = 0.24) (Figure 1.D and E).

All sex-dominant PINs exhibited robust replicable msFNC dysconnectivity relative to controls. Overall, fPIN-1, fPIN-2, mPIN-1, and mPIN-2 exhibited 460, 511, 748, and 272 replicated dysconnected features, respectively, out of 5460 tested connections. Isolating PIN- specific distinct dysconnectivity yielded 225, 341, 488, and 147 respectively (Figure 2.A). Notably, fPIN-1 was characterized by cerebellar-sensorimotor hyperconnectivity, whereas fPIN-2 showed hypoconnectivity between subcortical and triple network domains. mPIN-1 exhibited replicable sensorimotor-subcortical hyperconnectivity alongside cerebellar-higher cognition and subcortical hypoconnectivity, whereas mPIN-2 showed sensorimotor-higher cognition hypoconnectivity.

Sparse modeling demonstrated that PIN-specific dysconnectivity patterns reliably predicted cognitive performance across all neurosubtypes. For fPIN-1, 29 distinct dysconnectivity

features predicted BACS composite score, yielding a significant correlation between observed and predicted scores that replicated in an independent hold-out set (ρ = 0.14, p = 3.17 × 10−2). Similar, robust and replicable cognitive predictions were observed for fPIN-2 (62 features; ρ = 0.23, p = 4.58 × 10−4), mPIN-1 (60 features; ρ = 0.16, p-value = 1.68 × 10-2) and mPIN-2 (36 features; ρ = 0.18, p-value = 6.8 × 10-3) (Figure 2.B).

Conclusion

These findings establish sex as a relevant biological variable in psychosis subtyping and highlight the complexity of its neurobiological heterogeneity. Although neurosubtypes showed distinct dysconnectivity patterns, sparse modeling reveal that these PIN-specific patterns of functional dysconnectivity differentially predicted cognitive performance. This indicates that cognitive deficit can arise from divergent neurobiological mechanisms. By establishing neurosubtype links between brain networks, cognitive performance, our study underscores the utility of multiscale approaches for developing sex-stratified biomarkers and advancing personalized treatment interventions in psychosis.
