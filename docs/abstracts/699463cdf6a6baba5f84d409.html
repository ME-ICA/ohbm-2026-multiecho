Introduction

Began in 1958, the Baltimore Longitudinal Study of Aging (BLSA) is the United States’ longest ongoing prospective study of human aging. Its neuroimaging program, initiated in 1994, offers a uniquely rich resource for multimodal investigation of brain changes over time due to its extensive characterization of participants over several decades, making it a valuable resource for longitudinal modeling, data harmonization, and machine learning. However, legacy data is difficult to incorporate in newer pipelines. Here we introduce a Brain Imaging Data Structure (BIDS)-compliant1 BLSA dataset with raw and processed 3 T structural, diffusion, and functional magnetic resonance imaging data, managed by DataLad2 for reproducibility and version control. This cloud-based repository is available to researchers upon approval of a formal research proposal and data use agreement. 

Methods

MPRAGE, FLAIR, dual echo T2w/PDw, diffusion-weighted MRI, and resting-state fMRI scans collected between 2014 through 2025 on a 3 T Philips Achieva, Ingenia Omega, or MR 7700 scanner. Scanner sequences were ported across sequential scanners and tested for harmonization. MRI data were migrated from a legacy directory and naming structure to the BIDS format, providing standardized filenames with participant and visit level identification and consistent metadata in JSON sidecars. To enhance ease of access, each participant’s raw and derived data were nested as sub-datasets, allowing lightweight, modular cloning with version control at each level. Structural derivatives include MUSE-generated regional and tissue segmentations3 and voxel-based tissue density maps (RAVENS), while diffusion derivatives via PreQual4 include diffusion tensor maps, QA reports, and spatially normalized outputs. MRIQC5 outputs for all available modalities are provided as well. Figure 1 illustrates the types of images and processing derivatives available in the BLSA neuroimaging dataset.

Data were pushed to AWS S3-based object storage, configured via Git-Annex special remotes for distributed hosting. This allows users to retrieve data while preserving file level versioning, and consistent provenance trails across raw and derivative datasets. All derivatives adhere to BIDS Derivatives conventions with explicit links back to originating raw data.

A separate centralized demographic and phenotypic data warehouse enables multimodal correlations with cognitive, vascular, blood biomarker, genotyping, and clinical variables.

Results

As of November 2025, MRIs for 865 individuals (M=382) (2,235 visits from October 2014 through October 2025) have been incorporated into the DataLad-managed BIDS framework, with work ongoing to incorporate over 1,240 scans acquired prior to October 2014.

The resulting resource provides a multi-scanner, longitudinal MRI dataset with subject-level DataLad sub-datasets, harmonized derivatives, and AWS-backed distribution. MRIQC metrics provide scanner and epoch level quality variation typical of long-term studies. MUSE segmentation generates consistent neuroanatomical labels across sessions, while PreQual markedly improves diffusion data quality and yields robust diffusion tensors suitable for voxel-wise and tract-based analyses. Figure 2 shows corpus callosum regions modeled by age within subject, with population level trends, for example. DataLad provenance ensures traceability from raw acquisitions through all derivative workflows, supporting harmonization studies, reproducible ML pipelines, and longitudinal analyses.

Conclusion

This BIDS-organized, DataLad-managed, AWS-hosted multimodal imaging resource provides a technically rigorous platform for studying normative and pathological brain aging. The combination of longitudinal depth, multi-scanner harmonization, standardized QC, and reproducible DataLad infrastructure makes BLSA a valuable dataset for computational neuroimaging methods, harmonization research, and machine learning model development. Imaging data and integrated phenotypes are available to qualified investigators through a proposal and data use agreement via https://blsa.nih.gov/.
