Introduction

Brain Foundation Models (BFMs) have demonstrated strong generalization across cognitive and clinical tasks by leveraging large-scale unlabeled neuroimaging data and self-supervised learning1. However, their interpretability remains limited, and the learned representations are not explicitly grounded in established neuroscience knowledge. We hypothesized that incorporating knowledge priors derived from large-scale neuroimaging literature can improve both model performance and interpretability. To test this hypothesis, we integrated meta-analytic brain activation knowledge decoded from a large-scale literature database into a state-of-the-art brain fMRI foundation model. We expected that across multiple phenotype prediction tasks, the proposed approach would yield performance gains. Moreover, the injected knowledge priors would guide the BFM toward task-relevant brain systems, resulting in more biologically meaningful and neuroscience-grounded representations.

Methods

We built upon Brain-JEPA¹, a state-of-the-art fMRI BFM that equipped with: (i) Sine–Cosine encoding to capture the temporal dynamics of fMRI timeseries, and (ii) Brain Gradient Positioning to encode the functional topology among brain regions. To inject explicit neuroscience knowledge, we leveraged NeuroSynth², a large-scale coordinate-based meta-analysis database linking brain regions to diverse neuroscientific concepts. Here, we constructed a new NeuroSynth-based Positional Encoding (NPE) that embedded these brain- behavior associations into the latent fMRI embedding space of Brain-JEPA.

As illustrated in Fig. 1, we pretrained two model variants, vanilla Brain-JEPA and Brain-JEPA w/ NPE. For pretraining dataset, we used 80% of resting-state fMRI from 40,162 UK Biobank (UKB)3 subjects, with 20% remaining as held-out set. Both models were fine-tuned and evaluated on the external Human Connectome Project (HCP)-Aging dataset4, consisting of 656 cognitively healthy elderly participants. We evaluated the performance of both models on three phenotype prediction tasks: Flanker (executive control), ER40ANG (anger emotion recognition), and Neuroticism (personality trait).

All fMRI data was parcellated into 450 ROIs using Schaefer-400 for cortical regions and Tian-Scale III for subcortical regions. Cortical ROIs are grouped into seven functional networks: the control network (CN), the default mode network (DMN), the dorsal attention network (DAN), the limbic network (LN), the salience ventral attention network (SAN), the somatomotor network (SMN), and the visual network (VN). Subcortical regions are categorized into seven anatomical regions: hippocampus, amygdala, thalamus, caudate, putamen, globus pallidus, nucleus accumbens. For all phenotype tasks, the HCP-Aging dataset was randomly split into train/val/test (6:2:2) across all subjects, and results were averaged over five random data splits. To assess model interpretability, we extracted the self-attention weights from fine-tuned models and aggregated them within 14 predefined cortical-subcortical systems. We then compared the resulting attention distributions to examine whether NPE preferentially emphasizes phenotype-relevant neural circuits. 

Results

As shown in Fig. 2A, Brain-JEPA w/ NPE showed consistently lower mean squared error and higher Pearson correlation across all three phenotype tasks compared to the vanilla model, although the differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.05).

Interpretability analysis revealed that incorporating the knowledge priors alters the attention distribution of the BFM in meaningful ways. Comparing the vanilla Brain-JEPA with Brain-JEPA w/ NPE, we observed shifts in attention toward phenotype-relevant neural systems. For the Flanker task (Fig. 2B row 1), Brain-JEPA w/ NPE revealed higher attention to CN, DAN, and SAN (networks supporting inhibitory control and attention) and lower attention to lower-level sensorimotor networks (VN and SMN). For anger emotion recognition task (Fig. 2B row 2), attention increases in amygdala and thalamus, together with larger contribution of CN and LN, reflecting the involvement of subcortical pathways and cognitive-emotional integration in cortical region. For Neuroticism task (Fig. 2B row 3), we found higher contribution in DMN, LN, CN, amygdala, and hippocampus, accompanied by reduced SAN and SMN attention, reflecting emotion generation, regulation, and self-referential processing.

Together, these results demonstrate that integrating broad neuroscientific priors not only improved prediction accuracy but also encouraged more meaningful and neurobiologically aligned attention pattern.

Conclusion

This pilot study shows that incorporating meta-analytic neuroscience knowledge into a Brain Foundation Model can improve predictive performance and neuroscientific alignment. The proposed NeuroSynth-based positional encoding (NPE) guided the model toward task-relevant brain systems, yielding more meaningful and interpretable representations. These findings highlight the potential of NeuroSynth-based meta-analytic decoding as a simple yet effective plug-in module for enriching functional semantics in fMRI representations. In future work, we will assess the generalizability of NPE across different BFM architectures and extend the interpretability analysis to finer-grained functional organization. Overall, this pilot study provides initial evidence for neuroscience-grounded BFMs and motivates further investigation toward more reliable, knowledge-driven, and neuroscientifically interpretable BFMs.
