Introduction

Sex differences have been well documented at the genomic and neurobiological levels in psychosis. Nevertheless, investigations are limited regarding sex differences in imaging genomic associations (i.e., interaction) from a fusion perspective. It is crucial to characterize sex-specific genomic factors of brain abnormalities for a complete picture of biological mechanisms in psychosis. In this work, we integrate tissue-specific expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) with single nucleotide polymorphism (SNPs) to derive genetic-regulated expression, a more powerful and informative latent variable, to be linked to functional network connectivity for identification of more reliable and interpretable genetic-neurobiological pathways in psychosis.

Methods

We used the single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) and resting-state fMRI (rsfMRI) data of the aggregated COBRE and FBIRN cohort, including 367 individuals with both data modalities available (21% female, aged 18-65, 160 patients with schizophrenia (SZ) and 207 controls (HC)). The SNP data went through the standard quality control and genotype imputation following the ENIGMA protocol. Genetic-regulated expression was then computed using the MetaXcan toolbox [1], which essentially estimated the gene expression based on genotypes leveraging the eQTL regulatory effects. Specifically, we estimated tissue-specific expression profiles for 52,522 gene markers across 13 brain tissues (Fig. 1a), with additional 6,842 whole-blood gene markers included for contrast. Independent component analysis (ICA) [2] was then applied to the expression data to extract 35 components. Each component represented a gene interaction network with the component’s associated loadings reflecting how this gene network was loaded onto each subject.

The rsfMRI data were processed using the fully automated NeuroMark pipeline [3]. The Neuromark_fMRI_1.0 network template served as a reference in a spatial-guided independent component analysis [4] to derive 53 intrinsic connectivity networks (ICNs), based on which functional network connectivity (FNC) was estimated [5]. Again we conducted ICA to extract 25 FNC components, each capturing a brain network of synergized activity.

We first identified the expression components showing significant sex-diagnosis interaction effects. In parallel, we identified FNC components showing significant diagnosis effects. We then examined whether these sex-specific expression components showed differential associations with SZ-related FNC components across sex groups. All the regression models were corrected for age, site effects, SNP population stratification, and rsfMRI mean framewise displacement. For sex-specific expression components, their top important gene markers (|z-score| > 3) were compared with the input for tissue enrichment by Fisher’s exact test.

Results

Expression Component 3 and 14 showed female-specific diagnosis effects (Fig. 1b and 1c, p = 0.02 and p = 0.05, respectively). Expression Component 3 also showed a significant female-specific association with FNC Component 1 (Fig. 1d, r = -0.24, p = 0.03), which highlighted visual-auditory and visual-sensorimotor connectivity (Fig. 1e). Expression Component 14 showed a female-specific association with FNC component 3 (r = -0.25, p = 0.03) highlighting visual-cerebellum connectivity (Fig. 1g). Both FNC Component 1 and 3 showed significant diagnosis effects but insignificant to marginal sex differences (Fig. 1f and 1i). Additionally, the top important tissue-specific gene markers of expression component 3 were significantly enriched in amygdala, cerebellum, and substantia nigra (p = 0.01, 3.60E-05, and 2.21E-03, respectively). For expression component 14, tissue enrichment of gene markers was observed for amygdala, anterior cingulate cortex, and cerebellum (p = 4.52E-03, 4.64E-03, and 1.60E-03, respectively). In contrast, no enrichment was noted for whole blood (p > 0.99). These observations underscore the sex and tissue/region specificity of imaging genomic associations in SZ.

Conclusion

Functional dysconnecitivty in visual, sensory, and cerebellum networks have been well documented for SZ. Our findings provide preliminary evidence that the underlying genetic drivers of brain disruptions in psychosis might be different across sex groups, emphasizing the necessity of sex-stratified analyses for precise mechanistic characterization. In addition, while the associated gene markers await further interpretation, the enrichment tests indicate these gene markers being are specific to brain tissues rather than detectable in whole blood, supporting using genetic-regulated expression as an informative and effective genetic variable for application in imaging genomic fusion.
