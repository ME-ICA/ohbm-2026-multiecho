Introduction

Climate change threatens global health not only through warming but increasingly through erratic temperature variability (TV). While heatwaves are well-studied, unstable temperature patterns—characterized by rapid, non-linear fluctuations—may inflict a greater cumulative burden on physiology. The brain, central to thermoregulation and stress, is particularly vulnerable. Although epidemiological studies link climate volatility to depression, the biological mechanisms converting external stressors into internal neural alterations remain obscure.

Two critical gaps persist. First, conventional metrics fail to capture rapid "polarity reversals"—sudden shifts between temperature extremes that exceed homeostatic limits. We address this by introducing "TV-flip," a novel index quantifying these specific non-linear shocks. Second, the "black box" linking climate exposure to behavior lacks mechanistic resolution, as few studies integrate multi-omics layers.

To bridge these gaps, we analyzed the large-scale CHIMGEN and CLHLS cohorts, combining high-resolution meteorology with genome-wide DNA methylation and multimodal neuroimaging (sMRI, fMRI, ASL, DTI). Analytically, we employed Generalized Additive Models (GAMs) to characterize non-linear dose-response relationships between TV/TV-flip and depression. Subsequently, using Partial Least Squares Path Modeling (PLS-PM), we aimed to: (1) validate the impact of temperature instability; (2) identify methylation clusters as molecular sensors; and (3) delineate "Environment-Epigenome-Brain" cascades. These findings provide the first mechanistic evidence of how climate volatility biologically imprints onto the human brain.

Methods

Study Population and Design
 We utilized data from the China Imaging Genetics (CHIMGEN) project, a large-scale cohort recruiting 7,306 healthy Han Chinese participants (aged 18–30) across 21 cities. Participants provided written informed consent, and the study was approved by institutional review boards. This analysis included participants with complete quality-controlled data and retrospective longitudinal geolocation history from birth to recruitment.

Environmental Exposure and "TV-flip" Construction
High-resolution meteorological data were extracted via Google Earth Engine. We constructed a novel index, "TV-flip," to quantify rapid, non-linear temperature fluctuations exceeding homeostatic thresholds. A sliding window approach established a dynamic baseline for each participant’s temperature time series. High and low temperature events were defined as deviations >1 SD from the local mean. A "flip" was defined as a rapid polarity switch between consecutive extremes. ^1To quantify cumulative burden, we extracted the frequency of flips and the intensity of temperature change during transitions. These parameters were normalized, and the final TV-flip index was calculated as the Euclidean distance in feature space, effectively distinguishing intense thermal instability from stable environments.

Multimodal Neuroimaging and Methylation Profiling
Multimodal neuroimaging was acquired on 3.0T scanners using a unified protocol: (1) sMRI: Cortical thickness, area, and volume were calculated to characterize morphology. (2) rs-fMRI: ALFF and ReHo were computed for regional activity and synchronization. (3) DTI: White matter integrity was evaluated via Fractional Anisotropy and Mean Diffusivity. (4) ASL: Cerebral blood flow was quantified for perfusion. All data underwent rigorous preprocessing including motion correction and normalization. Additionally, a subset of participants underwent genome-wide DNA methylation sequencing. After quality control, reads were aligned to the reference genome to identify CpG sites. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II).

Statistical Analysis
Non-linear Associations (GAMs & DLNM): We employed Generalized Additive Models (GAMs) with thin plate regression splines to investigate non-linear associations between TV/TV-flip and outcomes, adjusting for confounders like age and sex. ^2First derivatives of smooth functions identified ranges of significant effect. To delineate temporal dependency, Distributed Lag Non-linear Models (DLNM) were used to model exposure history from birth, identifying developmental "sensitive windows" of elevated susceptibility.

Integrated Path Modeling (PLS-PM): To dissect causal architecture, we implemented a two-stage framework. First, Multi-Response Partial Least Squares Regression (PLS-R) extracted biologically relevant Latent Variables (LVs) from high-dimensional omics and imaging data, maximizing covariance with the exposure-outcome axis. Second, these LVs were entered into a Partial Least Squares Path Modeling (PLS-PM) framework to test competing structural hypotheses (e.g., Environment → Epigenetics → Brain → Behavior) versus alternative pathways. Path significance was assessed via bootstrap resampling.^3

Results

TV and TV-flip Characteristics and Population Distribution. We first characterized the temperature variability (TV) and the novel "TV-flip" metric across the study locations. TV-flip, designed to capture rapid polarity reversals, showed distinct distribution patterns compared to traditional TV. While TV reflects general variability, TV-flip specifically highlights non-linear fluctuations. Demographic analysis of the CHIMGEN and CLHLS cohorts confirmed that participants were widely distributed across diverse climatic zones, providing sufficient variation for exposure assessment. Basic demographic factors (age, sex) were balanced across exposure quartiles, minimizing potential confounding.

Non-linear Associations of TV/TV-flip with Depression. Utilizing Generalized Additive Models (GAMs), we examined the dose-response relationships. Both TV and TV-flip exhibited significant non-linear associations with depression scores (P<0.05P<0.05). Notably, TV-flip demonstrated a stronger explanatory power for depressive symptoms than conventional TV metrics, particularly at the extremes of the distribution. The GAM plots revealed a complex pattern where the risk of depression increased sharply after surpassing specific thresholds of temperature instability. This suggests that the rapidity and unpredictability of temperature changes (captured by TV-flip) may be more detrimental than variability alone.

Identification of DNA Methylation Signatures. To explore molecular mechanisms, we conducted an Epigenome-Wide Association Study (EWAS). We identified specific DNA methylation sites (CpGs) that were significantly associated with both TV and TV-flip exposure. These methylation alterations were not randomly distributed but clustered in genomic regions relevant to stress response. Importantly, the methylation signals associated with TV-flip showed partial overlap but also distinct specificities compared to those associated with general TV, suggesting that rapid temperature flips might trigger unique epigenetic adaptations.

Neuroimaging Alterations Linked to Climate Stress. Multimodal neuroimaging analysis revealed significant brain structural and functional changes correlated with temperature instability. High exposure to TV and TV-flip was associated with alterations in gray matter volume and white matter integrity. Specifically, regions involved in emotion regulation showed significant morphological differences in individuals exposed to high TV-flip. Furthermore, functional metrics (such as ALFF) indicated disrupted local brain activity. These neuroimaging findings provide physical evidence of the brain's vulnerability to erratic climate patterns.

The Mediating Role of Epigenetics and Brain Structure. Finally, Partial Least Squares Path Modeling (PLS-PM) integrated the multi-omics data to test causal pathways. The model confirmed a significant cascade: Temperature Instability (TV/TV-flip) →→ DNA Methylation →→ Brain Structure/Function →→ Depression. The PLS-PM results showed that TV-flip exerted a significant total effect on depression, largely mediated through epigenetic modifications and subsequent neuroanatomical changes. This "Environment-Epigenome-Brain" path demonstrated good model fit, statistically validating that molecular and neural adaptations are key intermediates linking climate volatility to mental health outcomes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides the first comprehensive multi-omics evidence delineating the biological pathways linking climate volatility—specifically rapid, non-linear temperature fluctuations—to depressive symptoms. By introducing the novel "TV-flip" index, we demonstrated that the rapidity and unpredictability of thermal polarity reversals exert a cumulative physiological burden often missed by conventional meteorological metrics. Our analysis revealed robust non-linear dose-response relationships, establishing that the risk of depression escalates sharply once temperature instability exceeds specific homeostatic thresholds.

Crucially, by integrating high-resolution neuroimaging with genome-wide DNA methylation data, we successfully bridged the "black box" between external meteorological exposure and internal psychiatric outcomes. The validated Partial Least Squares Path Modeling (PLS-PM) framework confirms a sequential causal cascade: external thermal stress triggers specific adaptive epigenetic modifications, which subsequently drive structural and functional remodeling of neural circuits involved in emotion regulation, ultimately precipitating behavioral phenotypes. This "Environment-Epigenome-Brain" axis suggests that molecular adaptations to climate stress act as early sensors that precede macroscopic neural deficits.

These findings have profound implications for both environmental neuroscience and public health policy. Theoretically, they challenge the exclusive focus on global mean temperature rise, urging a paradigm shift toward monitoring climate volatility and non-linear shocks. Practically, the TV-flip index offers a sensitive tool for identifying vulnerable populations and "sensitive windows" during development. As climate change intensifies, our study suggests that mitigating the mental health crisis requires not only emission reduction but also targeted neuroprotective strategies and precision interventions informed by the biological mechanisms of climate stress.
