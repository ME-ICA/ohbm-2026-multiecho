Introduction

Large-scale brain network models link anatomical connectivity to observed neural dynamics. Models such as the Kuramoto oscillator, have shown that structural connectivity (SC), conduction delays, and noise can reproduce characteristic features of resting-state activity^1. A key input data of these models is the intrinsic frequency parameter assigned to each brain region. Many whole-brain modelling studies assign a homogeneous intrinsic frequency around ~40 Hz to all regions^1. However, experimental evidence shows that several subcortical nuclei exhibit intrinsically slow oscillations in the 2-4 Hz range, driven by specific ionic mechanisms and local circuitry^2. Besides, recent studies reveal a cortical-subcortical hierarchy of intrinsic neural timescales^3. Together, these findings motivate incorporating fast intrinsic frequencies for cortical regions and slow ones for subcortical structures in whole-brain models, although the impact of such anatomically informed heterogeneity on reproducing empirical magnetoencephalography (MEG) dynamics remains largely unexplored. 

In this work, we directly test whether incorporating biologically informed intrinsic frequencies improves the capacity of a large-scale Kuramoto model to reproduce resting-state MEG data. We compare three configurations: (i) a physiologically motivated model with slow (~3 Hz) subcortical and fast (~40 Hz) cortical intrinsic frequencies; (ii) a traditional homogeneous model with 40 Hz assigned to all regions; and (iii) slow frequencies (~3 Hz) assigned to a subset of cortical regions and fast (~40 Hz) frequency for the rest. Using both power spectral divergence and functional connectivity metrics, we show that only the physiologically motivated model simultaneously improves spectral and (FC) fits across realistic coupling ranges. These results suggest that subcortico-cortical frequency differentiation is not merely a modelling detail but a necessary constraint for reproducing large-scale neural dynamics.

Methods

We used preprocessed structural and diffusion MRI (dMRI) datasets from 186 healthy subjects (96 females, aged 22–35) from the 1200 Subjects Release (S1200) of the Human Connectome Project (HCP). The brain parcellation that gives form to the network is the AAL90. The atlas, available in MNI volumetric space, was projected on the subject's fsaverage 32K cortical surface, calculated with Freesurfer and provided by the HCP database, also in MNI space. 

We used preprocessed rMEG data from 86 healthy subjects (40 female) from the HCP database.  Signal envelopes were released, on a surface of around 4k vertexes, for 6 frequency bands (delta 1-4 Hz, theta 4-8 Hz, alpha 8-15 Hz, beta low 15-26 Hz, beta high 26-35 Hz and gamma low 35-50 Hz). The subject-averaged FC matrices for each band were computed using Pearson's correlation between pairs of region envelopes. The Power spectral density (PSD) of each real signal at the sensor space was calculated using the Welch's method with a Hamming window of 4 seconds and 50% overlap, to get a resolution of 0.25 Hz. The final empirical PSD is averaged across sensors and subjects of the subject's PSD, discarding the aperiodic component and normalized by its total power between 1 and 50 Hz.

The AAL90 mask was projected to the T1-image in MNI space using interpolation with the nearest-neighbor method of Nilearn (Python). Probabilistic tractography data were calculated from the dMRI data using MRtrix3. Volume-weighted SC and delay matrices were calculated with MRtrix3 software and averaged across 100 subjects. For each configuration proposed, the time delayed version of the Kuramoto model^5 was used to simulate brain oscillations at each region. The model's global parameters, coupling strength (K) and mean delay (MD) were varied in a range from 0 to 1000, and from 0 to 50 ms, respectively. We simulated 80 seconds for each pair of K and MD.

As with the empirical data, we computed the simulated functional connectivity (FC) matrices for each frequency band in source space. Both the real MEG and simulated FC profiles are composed of the values on the upper triangular part of the FC matrices collected over the six frequency bands. The real and simulated FC profiles were compared using Pearson's correlation to obtain a FC fitting for each configuration.

We projected the simulated oscillations from cortical sources to sensor space using the spherical model of three nested concentric shells available in the MNE software along with sensor location and orientation provided by the database. Subsequently, the PSD of simulated data was calculated on the sensor's space using the same methodology that we used for the empirical data's PSD. The Jensen Shannon Distance (JSD) between real and simulated PSD was calculated to generate the PSD fitting as 1-JSD for each configuration.

For each configuration, we used a threshold equal to the mean plus twice the standard deviation of all the PSD fitting values across the grid to create a parameter mask of higher PSD fitting. Within the range of global model parameters yielding the best power spectral density (PSD) fit, we evaluated a cost function that jointly combines PSD and functional connectivity (FC) fitting metrics to identify the best-performing configuration, simultaneously optimizing both spectral and FC fits across the three proposed configurations.

Results

For the three configurations, we compared the PSD derived from empirical and simulated data in sensor space using the JSD. The thresholds used to construct the parameter mask differed across configurations, as the PSD fitting values varied between them. The specific thresholds were 0.49, 0.45, and 0.46 for configurations (i), (ii), and (iii), respectively. The size of the subsets of global parameters with highest PSD fitting was similar for the three configurations, concentrated in a nearly central area of the grid. PSD fitting values within the parameter mask of each configuration range from 0.49 to 0.70 for (i), 0.45 to 0.62 for (ii), and 0.46 to 0.68 for (iii), indicating that configuration (i) achieved the highest overall PSD fitting.

The subset of K and MD parameters yielding the highest FC fitting exhibit different shapes across configurations; however, they all display a dispersed pattern, spreading over a large region of the grid. In general, in the three configurations, simulations with K > 700 and MD < 8 ms produced low FC fitting. Within the parameter masks for each configuration, FC fitting values vary in the range of 0.26-0.51 for (i), 0.22-0.41 for (ii) and 0.20-0.40 for (iii), consistently revealing the highest FC fitting for configuration (i).

For configuration (i) the best performance point coincides with the absolute best performance of the model; it was accomplished at K=500, MD=20 ms, FC fitting 0.51 and PSD fitting 0.70. For (ii) the best performance was achieved at K=450, MD=35 ms, FC fitting 0.34, and PSD fitting 0.62. In the case of (iii) the best performance was obtained at K=450, MD=32 ms, FC fitting 0.35, and PSD fitting 0.62.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates that incorporating slower intrinsic frequencies in subcortical structures markedly improves the ability of a large-scale Kuramoto model to reproduce empirical MEG dynamics. This finding indicates that realistic resting-state activity cannot be captured solely through structural connectivity, conduction delays, and noise; instead, biologically grounded regional frequency differentiation is a necessary modelling constraint. In particular, we show that assigning ~3 Hz intrinsic frequencies to subcortical nodes and ~40 Hz to cortical nodes yields a parameter regime in which both spectral features and FC are simultaneously matched, an outcome that homogeneous 40 Hz models fail to achieve. This suggests that subcortical-cortical frequency heterogeneity is not a minor modification but an essential feature of whole-brain dynamics. These results resonate strongly with decades of neurophysiological work demonstrating that slow oscillatory modes originating in nuclei such as the thalamus, thalamic reticular nucleus, and inferior olive act as pacemakers that regulate cortical excitability, gating, and large-scale coordination. Intrinsic membrane properties in these regions naturally generate delta-range (2–4 Hz) rhythms through T-type Ca²⁺ channels, Ih currents, and recurrent inhibitory loops. Our modelling results reinforce this view by showing that omitting these slower subcortical dynamics leads to whole-brain simulations that are unable to recover empirical MEG signatures, despite using the correct connectome and realistic coupling ranges. Studies show that subcortical and medial temporal regions operate with longer intrinsic timescales than neocortical areas, implying slower effective dynamics and greater temporal integration. By embedding these slower frequencies at the level of regional intrinsic parameters, our model reproduces the empirical observation that fast cortical rhythms coexist with, and are partially organised by, slower subcortical signals. In this respect, the model highlights a mechanistic pathway through which the brain’s hierarchical organisation of timescales emerges from its structural and biophysical constraints.  Importantly, these findings challenge the dominant assumption that the connectome alone determines dynamical organisation. Frequency priors, often treated as arbitrary or uniform for computational convenience, turn out to be as consequential as coupling strength or network topology. Homogeneous models implicitly assume that all brain regions share a common dynamical regime; our results show that this assumption reduces the model’s ability to generate realistic MEG. By contrast, introducing even a simple cortical–subcortical frequency distinction increases model fidelity, suggesting that intrinsic frequencies play a central role in shaping whole-brain dynamics. A model that explicitly represents subcortical slow pacemakers therefore provides a principled framework for investigating stroke, Parkinson’s disease, epilepsy, or disorders of consciousness. More broadly, generative models that incorporate biologically grounded frequency hierarchies may offer improved predictions for neuromodulation strategies, including how stimulation at specific nodes or frequencies propagates through large-scale networks.

In summary, our findings show that realistic intrinsic frequencies are not optional modelling choices but necessary biophysical constraints. Embedding slower subcortical dynamics alongside faster cortical rhythms yields a more coherent match to empirical MEG, revealing a mechanistic role for subcortical pacemakers in shaping large-scale cortical activity. This advances current whole-brain modelling by integrating structural connectivity with physiological timescale hierarchies, providing a more complete and biologically plausible account of resting-state brain dynamics
