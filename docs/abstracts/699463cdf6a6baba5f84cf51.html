Introduction

The automatic detection of changes in a sensory stream elicits a well-known potential in electroencephalography (EEG), called the mismatch negativity (MMN). Classical theories of MMN propose that repeated stimuli form a short-lived echoic memory against which incoming inputs are compared. A deviant (oddball) stimulus elicits a mismatch response because it violates the memory trace established by the repeated standards. Recently, MMN has been argued to originate from a predictive process generated by the interaction between top-down predictions and bottom-up sensory inputs. A match between sensory input and predictions results in a suppression, while a mismatch between the two induces an increased prediction error signal. 

Irrespective of the framework, it is still a matter of debate how and where these memory traces/predictions are represented in the brain. While the processes how expectations form and update are commonly studied by delivering external stimuli that meet or violate regularities (prediction error), purely endogenous memory/prediction related brain activity 2 is rarely isolated. Anticipatory waveforms, such as the stimulus-preceding negativity (SPN), have previously been shown to be modulated by stimulus predictability 3, suggesting that they may offer an important window into the processes of formation and maintenance of memory traces/predictions.

We tested whether the anticipatory brain activity (both at the sensor and source level) would reflect the build-up of memory traces/predictions during a somatosensory roving paradigm, which allowed us to probe neuronal responses upon subsequent repetitions of sensory stimuli and upon violations of statistical regularities.

Methods

33 healthy participants underwent a somatosensory roving paradigm, where trains of electrical pulses of two different supra-threshold intensities alternated. Each stimulation at any trial (n) had a probability of 20% to be of the other intensity. Each of 12 experimental blocks, containing 200 electrical pulses (2400 stimuli in total), differed for the inter-stimulus interval (ISI) that changed across blocks between 1 and 2 seconds. This jitter was introduced to average out the potentials evoked by the stimulations occurring during the preceding trial 3. No active task was required from participants. 

Data were collected using a 64-channel active electrode EEG system with electrodes placed according to the extended 10–20 system. First, bad channels were interpolated, the data were then referenced against the average reference, down-sampled to 512 Hz, high-pass filtered (0.01 Hz) and corrected for eye movements. Epochs were defined as periods spanning from -1.5 to 0.5 s around stimulus administration.Each trial was visually inspected to remove bad data segments (on average: 211 ± 132 trials). The epoched dataset was then low pass filtered (45 Hz) and EEG data was baseline-corrected on the -100 to -5 ms interval for the post-stimulus, and the -1100 to -900 ms interval for the pre-stimulus epoch.  

For sensor-space analyses on the post-stimulus activity, the first pulse of each new stimulus train served as the deviant, while the preceding stimulus was defined as the standard. We further categorised trials based on the number of stimuli within each train: 2, 3, 4-5, 6-8, 9+. To test for differences across conditions and their parametric relationship across train lengths, we performed a mass-univariate multiple regression analysis, having one dummy regressor for each condition of interest, as well as for each subject. Mismatch responses (MMR), i.e., differences between standard and deviant conditions, were tested by contrasting standard and deviant conditions parameter estimates. The parametric relationship to train lengths was tested by specifying a linear parametric contrast vector with opposite trends for standards and deviants. 

In the pre-stimulus window, to first give an initial description of the waveform preceding each stimulus administration, we performed a one-sample t-test containing all trials across participants. Then, to test how the anticipatory activity could reflect the build-up of expectations across the experiment, we divided the trials into the following train length categories: 1, 2, 3, 4-5, 6-8, 9+ and tested the parametric trend across conditions. 

For source-space analyses, a structural MRI was acquired for each participant prior the EEG-session and used to calibrate a neuronavigation system through which we collected MRI-registered scalp electrode positions. Source reconstruction was conducted using the multiple sparse priors algorithm, which estimates sparse cortical activity from sensor-level data. The results are presented as weighted percentages of activation within the regions defined by the Destrieux 2009 atlas. In the post-stimulus epoch, we source-estimated the cortical generators of the P50 and N140 by inverting all trials in the 30-60 ms and the 100-150 ms time-windows. Surface sparse activation maps for each participant were then taken into a one-sample t-test at the group level to confirm that the cortical generators of prototypical SEP matched the findings in the literature. We then grouped and inverted trials according to the conditions as specified in the sensor-level time-domain analysis and tested for differences between standard and deviant source estimates in the N140 window.

In the pre-stimulus epoch, a window ranging from -700 to 0 ms was selected for the source inversion over all trials and surface maps were subjected to a one-sample t-test at the group level. This analysis allowed us to further describe the pre-stimulus brain activity, and, particularly, its cortical generators. 

Results

To verify whether our paradigm elicited the formation of predictions across the experiment, we tested the difference between the electrophysiological correlates of the perception of standard and deviant trials. The earliest significant difference was observed in contralateral electrodes, starting at 109 ms and ending at 172 ms post-stimulus (peak at 139 ms, centroid: T8, pFWE < 0.001, T = 5.30). This activity corresponds to the typical somatosensory MMN 4. Later in time, our analyses revealed a second cluster showing a difference in activity between standard and deviant stimuli, in central electrodes, between 248 ms and 418 ms (peak at 359 ms, centroid: C2, pFWE < 0.001, T = 5.80), which we identified as the P300 MMR. In both clusters, the activity evoked by deviants was larger in amplitude compared to standards, which reflect the typical findings of MMR 4. We then explored the parametric trend across standard and deviant trials. The analysis revealed both N140 and P300 MMR to be parametrically modulated by train length. Specifically, the N140 revealed to be more positive for increasing standard train lengths and more negative for increasing deviant train lengths (small volume correction within significant MMR clusters, peak at 152 ms, centroid: FT8, pFWE = 0.029, T = 3.37). The P300 MMR was found to be negatively modulated by increasing train lengths for standard trials and it increased its amplitude over repeating deviant train lengths (peak at 365 ms, centroid = C2, pFWE < 0.001, T = 5.35). 

The distributed source reconstruction for the P50 identified a large contralateral network which extended mainly over contralateral somatosensory regions (post-central gyrus [SI]: 12.42%, middle-frontal gyrus: 12.37%, post-central sulcus [SI]: 11.58%, pre-central gyrus: 7.35%, central sulcus [SI]: 7.32%). The cortical generators of the N140 followed a similar pattern but were mainly localised in more ventral regions of contralateral primary sensory cortices and secondary somatosensory cortices (post-central gyrus [SI]: 13.00%, post-central sulcus [SI]: 11.08%, pre-central gyrus: 10.18%, sub-central gyrus and sulcus [SII]: 6.87%, central sulcus [SI]: 6.33%). We also tested the difference between standard and deviant trials in the N140 window. Results revealed that the second somatosensory cortex contributes differently to the generation of the MMR, with stronger activations for deviant trials (sub-central gyrus and sulcus [SII]: 70.76%). The exploration of the parametric trends across standard and deviant trials revealed no significant clusters. 

To provide a first description of the pre-stimulus waveform, we conducted a one-sample t-test across participants. Results showed a slow-building negative waveform starting at 773 ms pre-stimulus that reached peak negativity before stimulus onset in central electrodes (peak at -47 ms, centroid: C2, pFWE < 0.001, T = 7.47).  We then further explored the parametric relationship across train lengths in the window of significant pre-stimulus activity. Our analyses revealed a positive parametric trend in central electrodes between -164 ms and -109 ms (peak at -145 ms, centroid: CP2, pFWE = 0.044, T = 4.40). Specifically, shorter train lengths elicited a greater negativity, while the anticipatory electrophysiological activity in longer sequences was characterised by a smaller potential. 

At the source space, the cortical generators of the SPN were localised mainly in frontal and pre-frontal regions with an additional involvement of primary somatosensory regions (inferior frontal sulcus [IFG]: 12.52%, inferior frontal gyrus opercularis [IFG]: 10.97%, middle frontal gyrus: 9.8%, post-central sulcus [SI]: 9.09%, transverse frontopolar gyri and sulci [frontal pole]: 9.07%). 

Conclusion

Our results revealed that two components in the post-stimulus window showed a significant MMR: the N140 and P300. Clusters at the same time-points and sensors also showed opposite linear dependencies with train lengths for standards and deviants. This is coherent with recent views of MMN, where repetition of subsequent identical stimuli minimizes prediction error, resulting in a reduced ERP. The administration of unexpected stimulus changes, on the other hand, induces an error of prediction which results in an increase of brain activity 1. Source-space analyses further substantiate the functional significance of those components, suggesting a top-down gradient in somatosensory processing which begins in primary somatosensory cortices and follows in secondary cortices, which is in line with previous studies 4. In the post-stimulus window, therefore, our results might reflect a prediction error minimization process that possibly involves the interaction between hierarchically lower and higher cortical levels 1. 

Anticipatory brain activity, such as the SPN, has been shown to be modulated by stimulus uncertainty in minimal task designs and to correlate with the amplitude of the post-stimulus activity 3. These anticipatory signals are thus theorised to reflect processes involved in sensory readiness, although it remains unclear whether they represent processes more involved in prospective predictions or a deposited memory trace. 

Our results revealed that across the experiment a slow-building negative potential (SPN) reflected the anticipation of the incoming somatosensory stimulus. The amplitude of the pre-stimulus waveform also scaled with the number of stimuli within a train, suggesting a role of the SPN in preparing for upcoming stimulations and possibly in maintaining information derived from earlier repetitions. Specifically, our results showed that a greater number of stimuli within a train resulted in a reduced amplitude in the pre-stimulus window, which may indicate that the anticipation for upcoming stimuli diminished for stronger model priors built over the course of numerous repetitions. 

At the source level, the involvement of both primary sensory regions and frontal regions such as the IFG as the cortical generators of this anticipatory brain activity supports the hypothesis of an interaction between higher and lower cortical levels in the generation of predictions 5. 

It seems therefore that the statistical dependencies learned throughout the task not only shape MMRs but also prediction signals in the pre-stimulus window (SPN). This anticipatory activity may offer a new view of the memory trace/predictions, and they represent a crucial missing link in the context of contemporary accounts of the MMN.
