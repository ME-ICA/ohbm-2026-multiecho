Introduction

The human brain exhibits a functional architecture characterized by inter- and intra-hemispheric lateralization, which underpins distinct cognitive processes. Previous studies have identified that the left hemisphere preferentially engages in intrahemispheric functional segregation, while the right hemisphere shows stronger interhemispheric integration.1 Although this bivariate model has linked lateralization to cognitive performance, earlier methodologies failed to consistently correlate functional connectivity (FC) in key regions, such as Broca's area, with verbal abilities. Also, the computation of functional laterality metrics is heavily influenced by methodological choices, including how correlation coefficient signs are handled and how statistical thresholding is applied. This study evaluates multiple laterality formulations to clarify how these analytic choices influence functional asymmetry patterns and brain-behavior relationships. 

Methods

Resting-state fMRI data (8 min 10 s; TR=3.5 s; 140 volumes) and Wechsler Abbreviated Scale of Intelligence (WASI) scores were analyzed in 44 right-handed males (21.2±5.1 years). Imaging data were preprocessed using AFNI and FreeSurfer, including physiological noise correction, despiking, and ANATICOR regression.2,3 Then, each data was mapped to standard cortical surfaces and smoothed using an 8 mm full-width-at-half-maximum (FWHM) kernel. 

For each homotopic seed pair, mean within-hemispheric and across-hemispheric correlations were computed and combined to derive segregation and integration metrics following previously proposed framework.1 Four sign-processing models consisting full FC, positive-only FC (PFC), absolute FC (AFC) and negative-only FC (NFC) were compared.

To examine how statistical thresholding influences laterality estimates, correlation maps were thresholded at multiple significance levels of p<0.1, p<0.01, p<0.001 and p<0.0001, and the number of surviving connections was used to compute laterality metrics. Group-level laterality maps were then generated using paired t tests, with statistical significance assessed using false discovery rate correction. Mean laterality values from significant regions were then correlated with WASI subscores using partial regression analysis.

Results

Across full FC, PFC, and AFC, group-level laterality maps showed leftward segregation in language- and social-related regions and rightward integration in visuospatial and attention related regions. Thresholding progressively altered cluster extent, while NFC showed marked loss of integration clusters at stringent thresholds. (Figure 1A)

Figure 1B summarizes regional level associations between laterality value and cognitive performance across models and thresholds. The most selective brain-behavior associations were observed for PFC with thresholding at p<0.1. Under this condition, segregation magnitude correlated with vocabulary performance in left lateral temporal, inferior frontal, and superior temporal regions. (Figure 1C) Integration magnitude was associated with visuospatial performance in right ventral temporal and superior parietal regions. (Figure 1D) Full FC showed significant associations in selected ROIs but with reduced consistency across cognitive domains. AFC did not show reliable segregation-language associations, and NFC did not yield robust behavioral correlations. (Figure 1B) Distribution-level analysis indicated substantial data loss under conservative thresholding, particularly for weaker and negative correlations.

Conclusion

In this study, we evaluated how sign handling and statistical thresholding influence functional laterality measures and their associations with cognition. Building on the segregation and integration framework of functional lateralization, we demonstrate that laterality metrics are highly sensitive to analytic choices applied during connectivity processing.1 In particular, different treatments of correlation signs and statistical thresholding substantially alter which lateralized patterns emerge and whether meaningful brain-behavior associations can be detected.2,4 

By comparing full FC, PFC, AFC and NFC models, this study shows that a distribution-aware approach that preserves positive correlations under a lenient threshold yields laterality measures that more reliably capture domain-specific associations between language-related segregation and visuospatial integration. These findings refine existing functional laterality metrics and emphasize the importance of explicitly considering correlation distributions and thresholding strategies when interpreting resting-state fMRI-based asymmetry measures.1,4
