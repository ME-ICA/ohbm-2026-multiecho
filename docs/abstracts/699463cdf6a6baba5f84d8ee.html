Introduction

In 2012 Nedergaard et al. redefined our understanding of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) dynamics. Using CSF tracers and intravital microscopy, they revealed that CSF enters the brain parenchyma along periarterial pathways (Virchow-Robin spaces), where it exchanges solutes with interstitial fluid (ISF), facilitating clearance of metabolic waste toward perivenous pathways. Given functional similarities to peripheral lymphatics they termed this the “glymphatic system”^1. Subsequent rodent studies have suggested that during sleep or anaesthesia when this sytem is most active, CSF-ISF exchange is facilitated by a ~60% expansion of the interstitial space (from 13-15% to 22-24% of total brain volume)^2.

It is currently unknown whether a similar expansion of the interstitial space occurs in humans. Sodium-23 MRI (²³Na MRI) provides an ideal means for probing water-containing tissue compartments in vivo as it measures total sodium concentration (TSC) and maintenance of the ~12-fold sodium gradient across the intra/ extra-cellular space (intracellular [Na⁺]≈12mM; extracellular [Na⁺]≈144mM) is fundamental to life. Indeed ~20% of all energy intake is consumed by the sodium-potassium pumps (Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase) dedicated to maintaining this sodium gradient. In the human brain, the intracellular: extracellular aqueous compartment ratio is ~8:2. TSC reflects the weighted sum of these compartments ^3. Any expansion of the extracellular space during sleep should yield a measurable increase in TSC in the human brain. Thus, if an expansion of the extracellular-space analogous to that observed in rodents occurs during human sleep, ²³Na MRI will provide a theoretically robust and sensitive means of capturing this. Here, we used 7T ²³Na MRI to scan healthy participants during both wake and natural sleep, aiming to quantify ISF volume dynamics and provide new insight into sleep-dependent glymphatic function and CSF-ISF exchange in the human brain.

Methods

Study design: 24 participants (aged 23-33 years, 14 male) with regular sleep cycles and sleep efficiency >80% on the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) were recruited after providing written informed consent. All underwent two MR sessions: once during sleep and once during wakefulness (Fig. 1). Participants arrived at the laboratory the night before the scan and were scanned the following morning after a full night of sleep (Wake-Scan) or sleep deprivation (Sleep-Scan). Participants wore a GeneActiwatch, a MUSE S headband and an Apple Watch S8 the night before each scan to assess total sleep time, sleep efficiency and sleep stages before the wake-scan, and to monitor compliance with sleep deprivation before the sleep-scan. Sleepiness levels immediately prior to each scan were assessed using the Karolinska Sleepiness Scale (KSS). Because simultaneous EEG recording is not possible at 7T, we used a multimodal approach to determine sleep and wakefulness during scanning. This included real-time button press feedback, breathing patterns, heart rate and post-scan subjective report (ratings of sleepiness, duration and depth of sleep).

MRI acquisition: Sodium imaging was acquired on a Siemens 7T MAGNETOM scanner using a 30-channel ²³Na coil with a 3D density-adapted radial sequence (TR/TE = 180/0.35ms, flip angle 90°, voxel size ≈3mm, 3333 projections)^4. After coil exchange, T1-weighted structural images were obtained using a 32-channel head coil and an MP2RAGE sequence (TR/TE = 4.3/1.83ms, 1-mm isotropic resolution) for anatomical reference^5.

MRI data processing: MP2RAGE images were segmented using FreeSurfer to generate binary masks of the gray matter (GM) and white matter (WM) for subsequent analysis. In addition, Vitreous humor regions were segmented using a seeded 3D active contour (Chan–Vese) approach. After visual inspection, these masks were used to extract corresponding signal metrics, then registered and applied to sodium images to extract tissue-specific signals. ²³Na MRI data reconstruction by NUFFT and analytical density compensation with SENSE channel combination. B1 compensation was performed using a double-angle B1 map, and all outputs were saved as NIfTI volumes for subsequent single/group-level analysis.

For each participant and session, sodium images were rigidly registered to the corresponding MP2RAGE using antsRegistration. Sodium signal calibration used voxel values from the vitreous humor as a reference for true sodium concentration (134mM). This reference enabled whole-brain voxel-wise linear normalisation to obtain quantitatively comparable TSC maps across participants and conditions. GM and WM masks transformed into sodium space were applied to extract tissue-specific TSC during wake and sleep.

For group analysis, structural images were skull-stripped (HD-BET) and nonlinearly registered to MNI152 using ANTs (antsRegistrationSyN). Sodium images were first affine-aligned to MP2RAGE using subject-specific sodium→T1 transforms and subsequently normalised to MNI152 by applying T1→MNI fields with antsApplyTransforms (2mm grid). Normalised sodium images were smoothed (Gaussian, 6mm FWHM) to generate individual sleep–wake difference maps. A study-specific brain mask was obtained from the intersection of all subject-level masks. Group-level voxel-wise inference was performed with FSL randomise (5000 permutations, TFCE) using a paired general linear model (GLM) contrasting sleep versus wake while controlling for inter-individual baseline differences.

Results

Activity watch and headband data confirmed that all participants adhered to a complete night of sleep deprivation to increase sleep pressure prior to the Sleep-Scan. The night prior to the Wake-Scan, participants slept for mean 7.5 (± 0.98) hours, with an average sleep onset time of 22 (± 12.7) minutes, and all exhibited good sleep structure. KSS scores of >=7 (scale 0-9 from very alert to very sleepy) pre Sleep-Scan and <=5 pre Wake-Scan in all participant confirmed manipulation of sleep pressure. During the Wake-Scan, all participants continuously pressed the button confirming wakefulness throughout. Onset of the sodium Sleep-Scan was based on real-time monitoring of button-press feedback. Consequently, no button-press responses were observed in any participant during the sodium Sleep-Scan acquisition confirming sleep throughout. 

As anticipated, reconstructed sodium data showed higher TSC in the ventricles, consistent with previous studies (Fig. 2). After calibration, ROI-based quantitative TSC within GM (38-51mM) and WM (27-37mM) were found to be very consistent across participants, with absolute values in alignment with values reported in the literature. Across 24 healthy participants, total brain sodium concentration during natural sleep did not significantly differ from the awake state in GM (mean difference=−0.06±0.61 s.e.m.; paired t-test: t(23)=−0.11, p=0.91; Cohen’s d=−0.02) or WM (paired T-test: WM p=0.99)). In GM, the 95% confidence interval ranged from −1.28 to +1.15, indicating that any true sleep-related increase in TSC, if present, must be smaller than approximately +1.15mM.

To address this in more detail, we next investigated voxel-wise effects using permutation-based paired comparison between sleep and wake sodium MRI. Again, this revealed no significant voxel-wise effects after TFCE correction (p<0.05, family-wise error corrected). Compared with wakefulness, sleep was not associated with a statistically significant increase in sodium signal either at the whole brain or  regional level indicating no altered extracellular-space expansion associated with sleep. No significant sex differences were observed in TSC.

Under a two-compartment model assuming fixed intra- and extracellular sodium concentrations (12mM and 144mM, respectively), a 60% increase in extracellular volume fraction would be associated with a large i.e ~40% increase in the total sodium signal. Given the observed wakefulness mean, this corresponds to an expected increase of ~15mM. Importantly, this predicted effect lies well outside the upper bound of the observed 95% confidence interval (+1.15mM). To quantify the sensitivity of the paired design, we also estimated the minimum resolvable effect (MRE) for the wake–sleep contrast. Using the observed within-subject standard deviation of the paired differences (sd=2.87mM, n=24), a two-sided paired α=0.05 has 80% power to detect a mean difference of approximately 1.7mM (Cohen’s 𝑑𝑧≈0.6). Relative to the mean wakefulness signal (42.6mM), this corresponds to a ~4% change. Therefore, effects smaller than ~4% may remain below the reliable detection threshold of the present sample, whereas effects of moderate magnitude should be detectable.

Conclusion

In contrast to data reported in rodents of a ~60% increase in extracellular volume fraction during sleep/ anaesthesia, we observed no statistically significant increase in grey (or white) matter sodium concentration in the human brain during sleep, either at the whole brain or regional level. Indeed, given our observed 95% confidence interval of (+1.15mM) we estimate that the minimum resolvable effect (MRE) is approximately 4% suggesting that if an expansion of the extracellular space does occur in humans during sleep it is likely to be substantially smaller than that reported in rodents. This work makes a number of assumptions. Firstly that intra- and extracellular sodium concentrations remain constant during sleep. Were this assumption violated, and the 60% expansion of the extracellular fluid compartment not accompanied by any increase in net sodium this would be associated with a drop in extracellular [Na⁺] to ≈95mM during sleep, which would be incompatible with life. The second assumption is that the expansion of the extracellular fluid compartment begins shortly after sleep onset, this assumption needs to be further investigated in future work.
