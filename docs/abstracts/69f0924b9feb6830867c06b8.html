Introduction

Hemispheric specialization is a core organizing principle of the human brain, yet how multiple lateralized functions interact within individuals remains poorly understood. The hemispheric complementarity framework proposes that cognition benefits when different functions preferentially segregate across hemispheres, whereas co-localization within the same hemisphere may induce functional crowding. For example, language functions are typically left-lateralized, while attention is typically right-lateralized; however, substantial interindividual variability exists, including individuals with atypical right-hemisphere language dominance. When both functions lateralize to the same hemisphere and rely on partially overlapping anatomical and functional substrates, individuals are said to express a crowded phenotype. Such hemispheric crowding is hypothesized to reflect an alternative mode of hemispheric organization with potential functional and biological consequences. Using the Human Connectome Project (HCP) cohort, we systematically mapped both language and attention lateralization to characterize population-level patterns of hemispheric complementarity. Here, attention refers to sustained, goal-directed control under cognitive demand, rather than attentional orienting. In doing so, we uncovered the presence of a robust, non-random crowded phenotype arising from specific combinations of language and attention lateralization, accompanied by distinct microstructural, connectivity, and genetic signatures that underpin functional asymmetries in the human brain.

Methods

We examined how variation in language and attention lateralization shapes large-scale hemispheric organization by identifying complementarity and crowded phenotypes and characterizing their microstructural, connectivity, and genetic correlates. Participants from HCP S12001 with complete language (Story vs. Math), goal-directed attention (2-back vs. 0-back contrast), resting-state, and diffusion data were included (n=981). Attention phenotypes were derived using Labache and colleagues’ method2 applied to attention networks3 (Fig.1a), with task and resting-state markers projected onto a 400-region homotopic atlas4 and clustered into three groups based on their patterns of functional organization across hemispheres (typical, bilateral, and atypical; Fig.1b). Attention lateralization was quantified using the 2-back vs. 0-back contrast, indexing sustained, top-down attentional control under increasing cognitive demand rather than attentional orienting, with analyses restricted to dorsal attention network regions.

These attention phenotypes were then crossed with established language lateralization phenotypes5, defined from task-based language asymmetry measures as typical or mild typical (both left-dominant) or atypical (right-dominant), to define hemispheric complementarity categories. This procedure yielded three categories (Fig.1c): split, where each hemisphere is dominant for one function; mixed, where individuals show no dominant hemisphere for attention but either left- or right-dominant language; and crowded, where the same hemisphere is dominant for both functions.

Anatomical correlates of hemispheric crowding were assessed via vertex-wise GLMs on cortical myelin and ANOVAs on white matter fibre density across the connectome. Cognitive implications were tested across ten HCP domains using ANCOVAs controlling for demographic and neuroanatomical factors. Finally, we estimated the heritability of the crowded phenotype using twin modelling.

Results

Clustering revealed three attention phenotypes (Fig.1) reflecting hemispheric organization of sustained, goal-directed attentional control under cognitive demand: typical (right-dominant), atypical (left-dominant), and bilateral. Bilateral individuals showed significantly stronger intrinsic connectivity within attention networks and greater interhemispheric coupling than lateralized groups (Fig.2a-c, all p<10-4). Males were less likely to exhibit a bilateral phenotype (p<10-3), relative to female participants, with no effects of handedness or other demographics (all p>0.22).

Crossing attention and language phenotypes showed (Fig.1c) a robust non-random association (p<10-3): mild typical language aligned with typical attention, strong typical language with bilateral attention, and atypical language with bilateral attention. These combinations yielded split, mixed, and crowded hemispheric classes.

Crowding showed no significant cognitive disadvantages (all p>0.17). Anatomically, mixed individuals displayed greater cortical myelination in the planum polare (Fig.2d, p<10-4), whereas crowded individuals showed increased white matter fibre density (Fig.2f), especially in tracts associated with the dorsal attention and default networks (all p<10-2). The crowded phenotype was heritable (h2=23%), exceeding the estimate for attention (h2=17%) but slightly below language (h2=26%).

Conclusion

While a significant relationship exists between the hemispheric dominance of language and attention associated brain systems, this relationship is more complex than predicted by simple models of hemispheric complementarity. Here, we establish the presence of distinct complementarity and crowding phenotypes with clear microstructural, connectivity, and genetic signatures. Of note, in the present data, we did not observe a measurable impact of crowding on cognitive performance. This integrated multimodal framework advances our understanding of how multiple functional asymmetries co-organize to support hemispheric specialization.
