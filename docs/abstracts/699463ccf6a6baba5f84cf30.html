Introduction

Translational neuroscience seeks to identify the most effective means for spatially mapping preclinical findings (in animal models) to the human brain. Useful cross-species mappings enable the locations of brain-related effects found in animal models to be predicted in humans, even when direct one-to-one anatomical comparisons are not possible1. In particular, mice have become a critical model in neuroimaging research as they possess rapid life cycles, straightforward husbandry, are genetically modifiable, and we can perform invasive experiments that aren’t possible in humans, allowing us to gain a causal understanding of brain function1. However, differences in brain anatomy have prevented mouse and human researchers from directly comparing the locations of their findings. Although there exist methods that compare mouse and human neuroanatomy within latent spaces1, an explicit method that assigns voxels of the mouse brain with a human regional identity from another (i.e. a cross-species brain atlas) has yet to be produced. Such a cross-species brain atlas, consisting of a common set of regions and their boundaries defined on anatomical images of both species, would enable direct comparison of findings across species. Building on previously established methods for identifying homologous brain regions through gene expression similarity1, utilizing supervised machine learning techniques for discriminating region identity based on high-dimensional gene expression data2, and leveraging existing mouse and human spatial gene expression data from the Allen Institute3,4, we produced a human-defined hierarchical atlas parcellating the entire mouse brain. The classifier, trained on human transcriptomic patterns to learn region-defining features from an existing human brain atlas were applied voxelwise to mouse gene expression data, resulting in the assignment of a human-equivalent region label to every point within a reference mouse brain.  

Methods

In this study, we aimed to train classifiers that predict human region identity from gene expression and apply these to gene expression data available for almost all voxels across the mouse brain at a 200 um resolution. To achieve this, we used the Allen Mouse Brain Atlas (AMBA) and Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA). The AMBA provides comprehensive genome-wide gene expression profiles obtained from in situ hybridization (ISH), resulting in a standardized 3D coordinate framework with high-quality expression data for ~4000 genes3. Similarly, the AHBA uses microarray methods to profile gene expression from brain structures that are organized within a hierarchical tree of regions4.

Data processing: Human tissue sample (N = 3,702) expression data were acquired from the AHBA, with region labels assigned by an expert neuroanatomist. Microarray data underwent probe filtering, log-normalization, and donor-wise z-scoring to reduce inter-donor variance. Mouse ISH expression volumes were reconstructed from coronal sections into the Allen Mouse Common Coordinate Framework (CCFv3). Voxels with incomplete or low-quality signals were excluded. Gene expression data from the AHBA (3,702 postmortem tissue samples with expression profiles) and AMBA (3D expression dataset for ~4000 genes) were filtered to 3,197 homologous genes via the NCBI HomoloGene database. For each gene, a gene-wise histogram-matching procedure was done to convert each mouse gene’s voxel-wise expression to fit the corresponding homologous human gene expression distribution. Histogram matching was performed on all 3,197 genes separately and combined to create a voxel-by-gene matrix that formed the input into machine learning classifiers. Missing values in the matched data were assigned a low sentinel value (–50) to distinguish them from true low-expression values.

Classification: The hierarchical classification followed the Allen Human Brain Atlas ontology (7 levels, 269 leaf regions). For each human brain region (organized as a tree from coarse to fine), a binary random forest classifier (500 trees) implemented in R 4.1.3 using the randomForest package predicted whether tissue samples belonged to that region based on gene expression (Figure 1). Tissue samples were restricted to those drawn from the parent brain structure from the tree, with predictions occurring iteratively down the hierarchy. 

Performance metrics were obtained from a test set drawn from the class-balanced data for that brain region. An 80/20 stratified train-test split on the training (human expression) data was used to reduce computational cost and obtain a reliable classifier performance estimate. To prevent overfitting, genes were ranked by importance from the parent-structure classifier, and the feature set (i.e. number of genes) was restricted to match the number of tissue samples used in training, retaining the most important genes. Specifically, permutation-based per-gene importance scores were obtained from parent classifiers. Only genes that contributed meaningfully to prediction at higher levels were passed down the hierarchy, and the number of predictors was controlled relative to the number of training samples, reducing the risk of overfitting in deeper levels.

Region prediction: The trained classifiers were then applied to brain-wide mouse gene expression data. Region masks were created using model predictions at multiple levels of the AHBA hierarchy, resulting in a sequence of human-equivalent region labels being assigned to each voxel. The associated voxel-wise confidence values were the predicted probability of a voxel belonging to the positive class in each binary classifier. Region masks and corresponding confidence values were saved as MINC files, providing a 3D representation of human-equivalent brain regions mapped onto the mouse brain (Figure 1). Combined, this forms a comprehensive atlas defining human-equivalent region labels directly on the mouse brain at varying levels of granularity.

Results

We confirmed that our classifiers learned human brain region labels from gene expression profiles, based on accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and positive and negative predictive values obtained through training on human expression data. The resulting mouse brain atlas is organized hierarchically: coarse levels (3-5) represent broad parent structures (e.g. telencephalon vs. non-telencephalon), while deeper levels (6-9) progressively refine to more granular, finer regions at the leaf nodes of the tree (e.g. left head of the caudate nucleus). Levels 1 and 2, which correspond to brain-wide classifications (e.g. brain vs non-brain) and distinctions between gray and white matter, were not used in this study. By starting at level 3, which contains the telencephalon, diencephalon, mesencephalon, metencephalon, myelencephalon, and telencephalic white matter, we were able to take a more focused approach by honing in on regions that were distinct enough to provide meaningful information on how gene expression profiles differ.

When applying these classifiers to the mouse expression data, the classifier generated detailed, voxelwise maps assigning human-equivalent labels across the entire mouse brain (Figure 2). At a global level, the atlas revealed systematic differences in how well human regions translated onto the mouse brain. Broader telencephalic divisions were mapped with high confidence, with particularly accurate predictions in the cerebral cortex (Level 4), striatum (Level 6), and putamen (Level 7), as well as in thalamic territories (Level 4). Myelencephalic regions also showed good cross-species correspondence, with region and confidence maps indicating stable, well-localized predictions. For regions with clear homologs across species (e.g. the striatum), predicted segmentations closely matched conventional mouse brain atlas labels3, indicating that the human-trained hierarchy generalizes to rodent tissue. 

In contrast, translation was less reliable in metencephalic territories, especially in deeper subdivisions of the cerebellum such as the vermis and cerebellar hemispheres (including anterior and posterior lobes), where confidence maps were more spatially diffuse. Mesencephalic regions showed a similar pattern of reduced accuracy, suggesting that midbrain and cerebellar subdivisions are less amenable to straightforward one-to-one mapping based solely on homologous gene expression. 

Across all major brain divisions, broadly expressed synaptic and excitability genes (e.g., NRGN, CAMK2A, GRIA2, GABA) contributed to classification at coarse levels, whereas each level 3 division showed distinct transcriptional signatures (e.g., cortical/striatal markers such as FEZF2, SATB2, PPP1R1B and DRD1 in the telencephalon; SIM1/OXT in the diencephalon; OTX2/TAC1 in the midbrain; and PCP2/RELN/CALB1 in cerebellar–hindbrain regions). At finer levels of the hierarchy, these broad markers were progressively replaced by more specialized, circuit-specific genes, such as PPP1R1B, DRD1, and GPR88 in the striatum, that remain informative even when differentiating closely related nuclei (e.g., caudate vs putamen or head-of-caudate vs not). At level 3 (coarsest division), voxelwise ambiguity was quantified by identifying voxels where the top two labels each exceeded 0.3 confidence and differed by ≤0.1; only 29% of voxels met this criterion (13,488/45,851), with ambiguity largely confined to boundaries between neighboring divisions (e.g., telencephalon vs diencephalon or metencephalon), rather than between distant territories.

The region masks and confidence maps together provide a cross-species, gene-expression-derived parcellation of the mouse brain that can be directly interpreted in terms of human anatomical labels (Figure 2).

Conclusion

We developed a novel cross-species brain atlas that utilized homologous gene expression profiles to define human brain regions directly onto mouse neuroanatomy explicitly. By leveraging spatially resolved transcriptomic data from the AHBA and AMBA, we trained hierarchical random forest classifiers to learn human region identities from gene expression and then applied these models voxelwise to mouse expression data to obtain both discrete labels and continuous prediction-confidence maps across the whole brain. 

Gene-level analyses highlighted how biologically meaningful features drove classification. At coarse levels, broadly expressed synaptic and excitability genes supported distinctions between major forebrain, midbrain, and hindbrain divisions, whereas deeper levels increasingly emphasized region-specific marker genes (e.g. PPP1R1B/DARPP-32, DRD1, and GPR88 for the caudate nucleus/striatum). This progression from broad forebrain signatures to fine-grained striatal and caudate marker mirrors known anatomical hierarchies and supports the interpretability of learned feature sets. 

Our ambiguity analysis further clarified where uncertainty exists within our model. At the coarsest level, a subset of voxels with similar confidence suggested competing labels, and these ambiguities predominantly occurred at boundaries between neighboring divisions (e.g. telencephalon vs diencephalon, telencephalon vs metencephalon), rather than between unrelated territories. Therefore, this suggests that classifier uncertainty is reflective of transition zones at anatomical borders, rather than instability or failure of the model as a whole. 

When applied to mouse data, the human-trained classifiers produced brain-wide voxel-wise maps of human-equivalent labels defined within the Allen Human Brain Atlasthereby allowing for the direct comparison of anatomical and functional data across species. These advances come with important limitations as the model is constrained to a subset of homologous genes shared across species, relies on bulk-tissue AHBA samples, and assumes that adult human expression patterns adequately capture cross-species correspondences. In addition, both datasets are predominantly male (all mouse data are male and 5 of 6 human donors are male), limiting inferences about sex-dependent differences. 

Despite these limitations, this work provides a concrete resource for the translational neuroscience community, as we have produced a tool that directly anchors mouse experimental findings into a human anatomical framework. This hierarchical, gene-expression-derived atlas has the potential to improve cross-species comparison for experimental findings and guide interpretations of preclinical models in human terms.
