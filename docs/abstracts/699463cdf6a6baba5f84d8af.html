Introduction

Neuroimaging datasets (e.g., magnetic resonance imaging, MRI; widefield optical imaging, WOI; and diffuse optical tomography, DOT) often present with variability in the field-of-view (FOV) across subjects. In MRI, FOV may be insufficient to cover the entire brain in all subjects. Additionally, voxels may be removed for quality control due to head motion or field inhomogeneities. With WOI in mice, surgical exposure varies, and pixels may be removed due to poor quality signal.1 Similarly, with DOT, pad positioning will vary, and optodes may be removed for quality control.2 In group-level analysis, voxels with incomplete data are commonly excluded (complete case analysis), reducing FOV. Alternatively, such voxels can be included while allowing degrees of freedom to vary across the FOV (available case analysis), which may reduce power and risk bias.

Multiple imputation (MI)3 is a rigorous statistical method for missing data that involves two steps. (1) Multiple pseudo-complete datasets are created with an imputation model; within each the downstream analysis is performed independently. (2) Results from all imputations are pooled to yield a final result. Despite its desirable properties, the use of MI in neuroimaging is underexplored. One complicating factor is that imputation may not preserve spatial autocorrelation (SA) in the imputed images. Proper accounting for spatial autocorrelation and the spatial extent of neuroimaging features is essential to proper inference (e.g., statistical parametric mapping). Traditional imputation models do not utilize spatial information.

Our long-term goal is to develop an MI pipeline suitable for high-dimensionality neuroimaging data. As a first, we characterized existing imputation models in their ability to reproduce the original covariance structure. We found that most imputation models led to biases that affected downstream analyses. Naïve adaptation of existing MI pipelines to neuroimaging may lead to statistical errors.

Methods

We created synthetic neuroimaging data for N=100 subjects as 2D Gaussian random fields (GRFs) of size 10-by-10 pixels with a 4-by-4 pixel Gaussian smoothing kernel. We included randomly generated values for covariates sex, age, and motion. To evaluate Type-I and Type-II error two datasets were generated. First, both groups had mean 0 and variance 1 for the GRFs, and we evaluated null hypothesis (H0) that there was no difference by sex. Type-II error was assessed by introducing a group difference in mean of 1 in a region defined by a vertical stripe. Structured missingness was added as a horizontal stripe to a specified proportion of randomly selected images.

We implemented MI in R4 with Multiple Imputation with Chained Equations (MICE)5. MICE was performed with 10 imputations using each of the following univariate imputation methods: predictive mean matching (PMM, MICE’s default), ridge regression, random forest, classification and regression trees, and LASSO linear regression. Predictors for the regression were age, sex, motion, and the values of all other pixels. We also compared MICE to mean imputation, in which the average value across subjects of the same sex was used to fill in missing pixels. 

To measure the extent to which pseudo-complete data recovered the ground truth SA, we calculated the local Moran's I statistic and Kiebel's roughness estimator at each pixel. These values were averaged across the 10 imputations of MICE and compared against the ground truth image (using root-mean-squared error, RMSE). Type-I and Type-II error were calculated by computing the two-sample, pixel-wise t-statistic and then using threshold-free cluster enhancement (TFCE). Family-wise error rate (FWER) correction was performed with permutation inference (Fig. 1).

Results

Imputation schemes varied wildly in their ability to reproduce the expected SA patterns. Qualitatively, MICE with LASSO linear regression produced subject-level images that best resembled the original complete data (Fig. 2A). Mean imputation resulted in data falsely close to zero, as expected. MICE with the default model of PMM led to substantial spatial noise. Local Moran’s I and Kiebel’s roughness measures provided further quantitative evidence that demonstrated superior ability of MICE with the LASSO model to reconstruct SA despite missingness (Fig. 2B-C). Mean imputation resulted in regions with spuriously high smoothness (high Moran’s I, low Kiebel’s roughness) in the middle of the imputed region as well as sharp transitions at the edges of the imputed regions. PMM resulted in false low spatial autocorrelation and high roughness. MICE with LASSO demonstrated the lowest (best) RMSE relative to the ground truth. Other imputation methods are not shown due to space constraints, with results intermediate between PMM and LASSO.

We further compared the performance of different approaches through permutation-based hypothesis testing via TFCE. Here, we highlight results from Type-II error simulation. With pixel-wise hypotheses, we expected the test to reject H0 within the vertical stripe with simulated group difference. Regions outside the stripe should fail to reject H0 about 95% of the time.  Mean imputation inflated t-statistics in the area with missingness (due to reduced variance), which led to spuriously enhanced TFCE scores (Fig. 2D-E). The true effect was obscured by MICE with default PMM given its noisy imputation (high Type-II error). MICE with LASSO preserved t-statistics and TFCE scores to the best extent.

After obtaining null distribution from maximum TFCE scores in data with shuffled group labels, we computed FWER-corrected p-values for each pixel. Out of all 1000 simulations, the proportion of times H0 was rejected was calculated for every pixel (Figure 2F). With complete data, we were able to identify the signals about 64.6% of the time. MICE with LASSO achieved comparable performance (62.6%), whereas PMM did not present sufficient power. Mean imputation showed a higher rejection rate (72.7%) in the area with true signal; however, it falsely rejected the null regions with missingness about 29.0% of the time.

Conclusion

In this study, we systematically evaluated imputation strategies for neuroimaging data with variable fields-of-view, assessing their ability to preserve spatial autocorrelation and support accurate group-level inference. Across qualitative and quantitative assessments, MICE with a LASSO linear regression model most faithfully reconstructed the covariance structure of the original data. It produced subject-level images that resembled the complete data, yielded the most accurate SA metrics (local Moran’s I, Kiebel’s roughness), and achieved the lowest RMSE. This approach further preserved TFCE scores for downstream hypothesis testing, achieving power comparable to analyses based on complete data and controlling Type-I error rate. In contrast, mean imputation severely shrank signal variance, inflated TFCE scores, and led to false positives in null regions. The default PMM model in MICE introduced spatial noise that degraded reconstruction quality and reduced power.

Robust handling of missingness in neuroimaging data is essential for ensuring accuracy of results and advancing biomarker development. Our findings demonstrate that the choice of imputation model crucially affects both the fidelity of reconstructed data and the validity of statistical inference. MICE with LASSO provides a promising foundation for a spatially informed multiple imputation pipeline for neuroimaging data, which will enhance reliability and reproducibility of neuroimaging studies.
