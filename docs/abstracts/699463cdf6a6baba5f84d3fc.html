Introduction

Accurate identification of epileptogenic lesions is critical for successful surgical outcomes in medication-resistant epilepsy. While structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is standard, many lesions remain subtle or invisible^1. We previously demonstrated that Inter-subject correlation (ISC)^2 during naturalistic movie viewing reveals group-level abnormalities in blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) synchronization among patients with epilepsy. However, clinical utility requires patient-specific localization. Region of Interest (ROI) analyses based on parcellations are attractive because they reduce data complexity and increase SNR.  However, previous attempts using atlas-based parcellations have proven unsatisfactory due to patient heterogeneity; lesions often do not adhere to standard anatomical or functional boundaries, leading to signal abnormalities getting diluted within larger parcels. Consequently, a fixed atlas may miss a lesion simply due to geometric misalignment. To mitigate this, we propose a multi-scale "searchlight" approach. By analyzing synchronization abnormalities at the vertex level across multiple scales, we aim to detect variable lesion sizes and shapes without the topological constraints of predefined atlases. 

Methods

Participants and data acquisition: We analyzed data from 18 focal epilepsy patients and 17 healthy controls. Participants underwent structural and functional MRI scans using a 3 Tesla SIEMENS Prisma with a 32-channel head coil. T1-weighted MPR sequences had a slice thickness of 0.8 mm and 192 slices. fMRI acquisition used multi-band, accelerated EPI, acquisition (TR=1250 ms, slice thickness=2.5 mm, and 60 slices). 384 volumes were acquired during an 8-minute film clip ('Bang! You're Dead', 1961). Preprocessing was performed using fMRIprep (v. 20.2.6) and FreeSurfer (v. 7.2), followed by confound removal, and projecting data to the fsLR 32k surface. For the searchlight analysis, data were smoothed using surface-based Gaussian filters. For the comparative atlas-based analysis, functional data were parcellated by averaging signals within ROIs defined by the Desikan (2006), Glasser (2016), Schaefer (2018), and Yan (2023) atlases. 

Significance masking (subject-wise bootstrapping): To define the cortical regions suitable for abnormality detection, we first identified units (vertices/ROIs) exhibiting significant synchronization in the healthy brain. We employed non-parametric subject-wise bootstrapping (SWB)^3 with 10,000 iterations to generate a group-level null distribution of the median ISC. Units showing statistically significant ISC after false discovery rate (FDR) correction (q<0.001)^4 were retained for the abnormality analysis, ensuring we only tested for lesions in cortex that reliably tracks the naturalistic stimulus. 

Multi-scale searchlight: To address the unknown geometry of epileptogenic tissue, we employed a multi-scale approach. The surface-based BOLD signal was spatially smoothed using gaussian kernels of varying full width at half maximum (FWHM) sizes (e.g., 0 mm, 5 mm, 7 mm, 10 mm, 12 mm, 15 mm, and 20 mm) to capture abnormalities at different spatial frequencies. 

Abnormality detection: For each unit (in at each smoothing scale or atlas), we calculated the ISC of the patient against each control (patient-to-control) and compared this distribution to the synchronization of controls amongst themselves (within-control). The abnormality at each unit was quantified using the area under the curve (AUC) of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC), measuring the separation between the patient-to-control and within-control correlation distributions. 

Statistical inference: To determine patient-specific significance, we established a normative threshold by treating each control subject as a "pseudo-patient" in a leave-one-out manner. We calculated the AUC maps for each control against the remaining group. A threshold was defined at the 95th percentile of this normative AUC distribution. Patient units exceeding this threshold at any smoothing scale were flagged as abnormal, and results were combined across scales to form a composite map of putative epileptogenic tissue. 

Results

Validation of normative model: The movie stimulus elicited robust, widespread synchronization across the control group (N=17). Median ISC maps revealed high synchronization in auditory, visual, and parietal cortices, establishing a reliable normative baseline. SWB confirmed that the cortex available for abnormality testing covered ~24, 51, 58, 64, 66, 69, and 72% of the total cortical surface at scales 0, 5, 7, 10, 12, 15, 20 mm respectively, validating the quality of the normative model (see Figure 2A). 

Patient-specific abnormality detection and clinical concordance: Using the 95th percentile threshold derived from the control leave-one-out analysis, we identified statistically significant clusters of asynchronous activity in all 18 patients. Preliminary validation against clinical ground truth in patients with diagnosed seizure onset zones using stereotactic electroencephalography (SEEG) demonstrated promising sensitivity. For example, in a patient with focal MRI-negative epilepsy (Figure 2B), the multi-scale searchlight successfully localized abnormalities in the left insula and temporal cortex, concordant with the seizure onset zone defined by SEEG and positron emission tomography (PET), despite the absence of structural findings for mesial temporal sclerosis (MTS). In this and other SEEG-implanted patients, the searchlight highlighted additional abnormal regions beyond the primary diagnosis; however, interpreting their specificity is limited by the lack of detailed SEEG electrode coverage information at this stage. These “putative abnormal zones” were detected at the individual level, completely avoiding the use of group-averaged patient data. This approach is essential for precision medicine, as it preserves the unique details of each patient's brain that group averages often obscure. The AUC metric successfully highlighted vertices where a patient's synchronization significantly deviated from the healthy normative distribution. 

Scale effects and lesion geometry: Varying the kernel size (i.e., from 5 mm to 20mm) revealed distinct abnormality profiles: smaller kernels isolated focal, high-intensity outliers likely representing discrete lesions, while larger kernels captured diffuse networks of desynchronization (see Figure 2B). Combining these scales allowed for the detection of irregular geometries that did not conform to standard spherical or parcel-based shapes. These findings demonstrate that fixed smoothing is insufficient for capturing the diverse geometry of heterogeneous pathologies. Consequently, the critical next step involves validating these functional abnormality maps against more clinical ground truth, specifically by quantifying their spatial concordance with the epileptogenic zones defined by invasive SEEG implantations and confirmed through post-surgical outcomes. 

Comparison with atlas-based approach: Crucially, the vertex-wise searchlight outperformed atlas-based methods. When compared to standard parcellations (e.g., Desikan, Glasser, Schaefer, and Yan), the searchlight method recovered distinct regions of abnormality (e.g., in the left insula of the example patient) that were otherwise obscured by averaging signals within parcels. Figure 2C illustrates atlas boundaries overlaid on searchlight-detected abnormalities, revealing regions that are either sub-parcel in size or cross borders without adhering to standard boundaries. These geometrically irregular regions were undetectable using the atlas-based approach (Figure 2D). 

Conclusion

This study presents a proof-of-concept, data-driven framework for detecting epileptogenic tissue using naturalistic fMRI. By abandoning fixed parcellations in favor of a multi-scale, vertex-wise searchlight, we overcome the geometric mismatch between pathological lesions and healthy anatomical landmarks or functional parcellations. The use of subject-wise bootstrapping ensures analysis is restricted to functionally valid cortex, while normative AUC thresholding provides a statistically rigorous method for individual-level anomaly detection. Preliminary results highlight the method’s capacity to detect MRI-negative lesions (e.g., insular/temporal sources) that are confirmed by invasive monitoring. However, we note that specificity must be interpreted with caution. First, invasive electrodes are placed relatively sparsely and may leave some true epileptogenic zones unverified. Second, because our method maps functional disruption, detected regions outside the seizure onset zone may represent downstream network effects driven by aberrant connectivity. While invasive intercranial EEG remains the state-of-the-art, this approach offers a rapid, non-invasive spatial prior to guide electrode implantation. Future work will focus on expanding the normative control database, extending the analysis to hippocampal surface maps, and rigorously validating the spatial concordance between these functional abnormalities and clinical ground truth derived from SEEG and post-surgical outcomes. Additionally, integrating structural and functional connectivity data will help differentiate between primary sources and propagation targets, further refining localization precision.
