Introduction

Altered functional connectivity among cortical, subcortical, and cerebellar regions is a well-established feature of psychosis. The triple network model, comprised of the salience (SN), central executive (CEN), and default mode networks (DMN), has been particularly informative in this context, as discordant interactions among these networks are linked to poor clinical outcomes. Familial genetic liability is thought to influence large-scale network organization; however, distinctions between network features associated with genetic risk versus those associated with illness expression remain unclear. Furthermore, the cerebellar contributions within this multi-network dysfunction are not well characterized. This study examined effective connectivity (EC) patterns, defined as directed statistical dependencies among regional blood-oxygen level dependent (BOLD) time series, across healthy controls (HC), individuals with schizophrenia (SZ), or bipolar disorder with psychosis (BPp), and their first-degree biological relatives (SZrel; BPprel). We hypothesized that healthy controls would exhibit coordinated cortico-cerebellar EC patterns that are different in both patients and relatives, and that patients would show EC patterns that differ from those of their unaffected relatives, possibly reflecting connectivity features associated with illness expression rather than genetic risk alone.

Methods

Resting-state fMRI data were obtained from the Psychosis Human Connectome Project (P-HCP), including data from HC (n=42), SZ (n=68), BPp (n=32), SZrel (n=43) and BPprel (n=23). 488 whole-brain volumes were collected using a 3T Siemens Prisma scanner.1 fMRI data were preprocessed using ENIGMA HALFpipe.2 BOLD time series were extracted for parcellations defined by the Schaeffer 17-network cortical atlas, Freesurfer subcortical atlas, and Buckner-17 network cerebellar atlas. Cortical regions of interest were selected from the triple network, including anterior insula (aINS), posterior cingulate gyrus (Cing), medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), dorsolateral PFC (dlPFC), and ventrolateral PFC (vlPFC). Subcortical regions included bilateral putamen and caudate. Three cerebellar regions, defined by their coactivation with triple-network cortices3, were also included. EC was estimated using group iterative multiple model estimation (GIMME).4 In this framework, paths refer to model-estimated directed connections between pairs of regions. GIMME estimates contemporaneous effects, reflecting directed associations across regions within the same time point, as well as lagged effects, representing directed associations between pairs of regions across consecutive time points. Each subgroup was modeled independently.

Results

HCs demonstrated both contemporaneous and lagged paths from the cerebellar representation of the CEN (C-CEN) to the right vlPFC, which was absent from SZ, BPp, and relative groups. Additional contemporaneous and lagged estimates from the left dlPFC to left vlPFC, as well as from the cerebellar DMN region (C-DMN) to right mPFC, were present in HCs and absent in both relative groups. Notably, these latter connections were identified in both SZ and BPp groups. Several paths were unique to BPprel, that included striatal-cortical, cortico-cerebellar, and cortico-cortical interactions. Contemporaneous paths from C-SN to the putamen and insula were present in all groups except SZ. A lagged connection from the left aINS to C-SN appeared in BPprel and both patient groups. Overall, the results revealed distinct EC architectures across healthy controls, patient groups, and unaffected relatives.

Conclusion

These findings demonstrate distinct patterns of cortico-cerebellar and triple-network effective connectivity across psychosis phenotypes and familial genetic risk. Coordinated cerebellar-prefrontal EC observed in HCs was absent in both patients and relatives, suggesting that disruption of these pathways may be associated with vulnerability to psychotic psychopathology. In contrast, several connections shared by patient groups were absent in relatives, indicating that certain EC features may be more closely associated with clinical manifestation than familial genetic risk alone. The unique connectivity architecture in BPprel raises the possibility that psychotic bipolar-specific genetic risk may involve distinct large-scale network configurations, particularly within striatal and salience-network circuits. These results highlight the cerebellum as a potential contributor to large-scale network differences across the psychosis spectrum and suggest that cortico-cerebellar EC signatures may help distinguish genetic risk from clinical manifestations of psychosis.
