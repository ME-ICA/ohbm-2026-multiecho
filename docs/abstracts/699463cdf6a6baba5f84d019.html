Introduction

A large body of evidence shows that disease progression in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is accompanied by alterations in functional network organization. Several studies have examined microstates in resting-state (RS-)EEG data, which reflect large-scale network dynamics, to explore their potential in demonstrating network configurations in AD patients.1 However, microstates are computed from amplitude values and omit phase-based connectivity, which may be critical given the decline in alpha amplitude with disease progression in AD. EEG phase-based connectivity states are computed in a manner similar to fMRI dynamic connectivity states, using a sliding window approach along the timescale.2 While fMRI studies have revealed altered connectivity states in AD patients,3 comparable EEG studies are lacking.

In this study, we analyzed microstates and alpha-band connectivity states from RS-EEG in AD patients and age-matched healthy controls (HC). We evaluated task-related changes before and after a memory task conducted with concurrent rTMS or sham stimulation. Firstly, we aimed to compare our EEG connectivity states with fMRI connectivity states reported in the literature. Secondly, we aimed to compare the ability of microstates and connectivity states to detect disease- and task-related alterations in network dynamics. 

Methods

Fifteen subjects (aged 53 to 84 years) for the AD group and fifteen healthy, age-matched controls were recruited through the University Hospital of Cologne. All participants took part in an experimental paradigm consisting of two conditions (STIM and SHAM), each with three sessions (RS-EEG1, memory task, RS-EEG2) (see Figure 1A).

RS-EEG was recorded for five minutes using 19 active Ag/AgCl electrodes placed according to the 10-20 system. The memory task tested the subjects’ visuospatial memory using twenty images and a 4x5 grid. 

In the STIM condition, subjects received 25 minutes of high-frequency (14 Hz), low-intensity (<10 mT) rTMS, which was applied using two coils over the parieto-occipital brain areas (P3, P4, Pz, O1, O2), while performing the memory task. In the SHAM condition, subjects received 25 minutes of sham stimulation during the memory task. 

After preprocessing the EEG data, microstates were computed from amplitude values (Figure 1B). Global microstate maps were computed from all epochs across all subjects. We chose the number of global microstates according to the elbow method applied to the global explained variance (GEV) curve, which resulted in four maps and a GEV of 78.92%. Each timepoint was then backfitted to the four global microstate maps. The mean duration and coverage of each microstate were determined from the microstate segmentations. 

For connectivity states (see Figure 1B), we estimated functional connectivity in the alpha frequency range using the corrected imaginary part of the phase-locking value (ciPLV) in sliding windows with a 300 ms time interval.2 This resulted in dynamic graphs from which we computed global connectivity states. After backfitting, we determined the mean duration and coverage of each connectivity state.

Statistical analyses were conducted using linear mixed-effects models.

Results

Figure 2 A-B illustrates the global microstates and connectivity states computed over all subjects. Overall, the duration of microstate C was shorter in the AD group than in the HC group (Figure 2 C). For connectivity states, the coverage of connectivity states A and B, which represent higher and focal connectivity, was lower in the AD group than in the HC group (Figure 2 D). However, coverage of connectivity state D, representing medium and diffuse connectivity, was higher in the AD group. 

Task-related differences in microstates were only observed in the HC group. Microstate B duration and coverage were higher in the HC group after the memory task, regardless of the presence of rTMS. 

Conversely, all task-related differences in connectivity states were identified in the AD group only. While the coverage of connectivity states A and B was lower in the AD than in the HC group in the baseline session (RS1), it increased after the memory task without rTMS to the point where it was no longer different to that in the HC group. When rTMS was added to the memory task, this alignment effect remained unchanged for connectivity state A, indicating that rTMS had no impact. Coverage of connectivity state D was higher in the AD group than in the HC group during the baseline session, but aligned to the level of the HC group after the memory task without rTMS. With rTMS, however, this alignment effect was diminished. 

There was a robust correlation between memory task performance scores and connectivity states A, B, and D in RS1. Higher scores correlated with longer duration and greater coverage of connectivity states A and B and lower scores correlated with shorter duration and lower coverage of connectivity state D in RS1. However, there were no correlations between performance scores and microstate variables.

Conclusion

Our observations were consistent with fMRI studies indicating a shift towards lower, more diffuse connectivity in patients with AD.3 Furthermore, the connectivity states revealed task-related differences in the AD group, with values shifting towards those observed in the HC group after the memory task. In contrast, the HC group did not show any task-related alterations. This finding supports prior evidence that smaller task-related alterations in functional connectivity correspond to better cognitive performance.4 One study also described an association between higher levels of tau in regions of the episodic memory network and increased task-related alterations in functional connectivity,4 suggesting a direct link between AD pathology and decreased memory network stability. These results are consistent with the “neural efficiency hypothesis”,5 which states that more efficient brain network optimization during tasks is linked to better performance. 

In conclusion, phase-based connectivity states captured disease-related differences between AD patients and healthy controls. They were also more sensitive than microstates to task-related network changes in AD patients. The results demonstrate the complementary nature of amplitude- and phase-based measures: While microstates are driven by local neural synchronization, connectivity states can reveal interactions between distributed neural populations. Therefore, EEG connectivity states serve as a powerful and cost-efficient tool for evaluating network alterations and cognitive functions in AD, demonstrating their potential as a sensitive biomarker for disease progression.
