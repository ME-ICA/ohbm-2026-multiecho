Introduction

Visual emotional perception relies primarily on facial expressions and bodily postures, which are integrated as a function of their emotional congruency (when both signals convey or not the same emotion). Studies showed that facial and bodily expressions interact bidirectionally, but bodily cues have a stronger influence on facial emotion recognition than vice versa. Studies also suggest that face-body integration operates automatically, independently of cognitive load1,2, speed of processing3, and level of awareness. However, these studies present limitations: (1) heterogeneous paradigms and stimuli across studies limit comparability and hamper cumulative knowledge; (2) the temporal dynamics of underlying neurocognitive processes are not well characterized; (3) low-quality stimuli coming from non-standardized datasets reduce the generalizability of the findings. 

 The aim of this work was to overcome these limits while examining across two studies, the automaticity of face–body integration at both behavioral and neural (event-related potentials, ERPs) levels. The first study manipulated cognitive load to assess whether the integration of congruent and incongruent face-body expressions persists when cognitive resources are constrained. The second study manipulated processing speed to evaluate its impact on the strength and timing of this integration. For both studies, we formulated three hypotheses: (H1) congruent expressions would yield higher accuracy and faster responses than incongruent ones. At the cerebral level, congruence status should be detectable at early neural stages (100ms post-stimulus); (H2) attentional focus would modulate integration, with bodily expressions exerting a stronger influence on facial expression recognition than the inverse. Such modulation would emerge in later ERPs (300ms); (H3) neither cognitive load (Study 1) nor processing speed (Study 2) would influence face–body integration, indicating an automatic processing.

Methods

Participants. We recruited 58 healthy, right-handed participants aged 18–35 years, 28 in Study 1 (22 females; Mage = 27 +/- 5 years), 30 in Study 2 (17 females; Mage = 24 +/- 3 years). 

 

Stimuli. Stimuli were selected from a specifically designed and validated stimulus set for the study of the processing facial and bodily expressions in congruent and incongruent conditions4. For both studies, 120 images were selected: 60 congruent (20 per emotion: happiness, anger, sadness) and 60 incongruent (10 per combination of incongruent facial and bodily emotions). 

Task/Procedure. In both studies, participants performed an emotion recognition task either on face or body. Throughout the task, EEG recordings (68 electrodes) captured the temporal dynamics of neural processing. In Study 1, we manipulated cognitive load. Participants completed a secondary memory task in parallel of the emotion recognition task. At the beginning of each trial block, they memorized a sequence of seven digits presented for 3000ms. This sequence was easy to remember (ex: 2222222) or not (ex: 5763419). Then, participants viewed the emotional face–body stimuli for 1500ms and indicated the emotion expressed by either the face or the body, depending on the instructed attentional focus. At the end of the block, participants reproduced the previously memorized digit sequence (figure 1). 

In Study 2, we manipulated the speed of responses. Participants viewed the face–body composites for 200ms and then identified, either under fast (response in less than 1000ms) or slow instructions, the emotion expressed by the face or the body. 

Measures/Analyses. The manipulated variables were expressed emotion (happiness, anger, sadness), face–body congruence (congruent or not), and attentional focus (face or body). In addition, Study 1 included a manipulation of cognitive load (high or low), while Study 2 manipulated processing speed (fast or slow).  

Behavioral data (Hu scores, which are unbiased scores of emotional recognition accuracy, and reaction times) were analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVAs and Bayesian repeated-measures ANOVAs. Only trials when series of digits were correctly remembered were analyzed (78%). Bayesian ANOVAs were used to quantify evidence for or against the influence of congruence, attentional focus, cognitive load (Study 1), and processing speed (Study 2). 

EEG data were preprocessed following validated guidelines, including re-referencing (average), filtering (0.1-30 Hz), ICA for ocular correction, epoching (−200 to 700 ms), and two rounds of artifact rejection targeting general noise and eye movements. On average, 96.5% of data were retained after artifact rejection. After preprocessing, we performed an analysis using Factorial Mass Univariate Toolbox (FMUT). Mass Univariate approaches such as FMUT enable data-driven identification of temporally precise effects without relying on predefined time windows. Bayesian repeated-measures ANOVAs were subsequently conducted on spatial and temporal windows identified by FMUT.

Results

H1: across both studies, congruent face–body expressions reliably improved emotion recognition accuracy compared to incongruent expressions (ps < 0.001), confirming integration between facial and bodily cues. At the brain level, FMUT analyses in Study 1 did not reveal significant ERP amplitude differences between congruent and incongruent stimuli at any processing stage (ps > .05). In Study 2, the same analyses showed that congruent stimuli elicited larger early (100–300ms, parietal electrodes) and late (>300ms, centro-parietal electrodes) amplitudes than incongruent stimuli (ps < .05). 

H2: In both studies, behavioral data further revealed a bidirectional yet asymmetrical influence, with bodily expressions exerting a stronger impact on facial expression recognition than the reverse (ps < 0.007). At the brain level, we found in Study 1 that focusing on facial expressions produced higher amplitudes of early (100ms, fronto-central and parieto-occipital sites) and middle-latency (250ms,

fronto-central and parieto-occipital sites) components than focusing on bodily expressions. This pattern was reversed in late components (after 300ms post stimulus, occipital sites) with higher amplitudes when attention was direction to bodies (ps < 0.05).

In Study 2, directing attention to facial expressions consistently elicited higher amplitudes for early (150ms, frontal, central, and parietal sites) and late components (after 300 ms, frontal and occipital sites) compared to directing attention to bodily expressions (ps < .001). 

H3: Importantly, neither cognitive load (Study 1) nor processing speed constraints (Study 2) modulated these behavioral and cerebral effects (ps > .05),. Finally, in both studies, across behavioral and electrophysiological measures, Bayesian analyses provided strong evidence for the absence of an effect of cognitive load or processing speed on face–body integration (BFinclusion > 10).

Conclusion

These two studies are the first to investigate in a systematic and standardized way the automatic integration of facial and bodily cues, while examining the bidirectionality of these effects. They provide not only findings consistent with previous research but also new insights into the underlying mechanisms of this effect. Moreover, these are the first studies to use high-quality visual stimuli that are closer to real-life emotional perception, allowing for better generalization of the results to everyday emotion perception. By manipulating cognitive load and processing speed, two key criteria of automaticity, this work provides a comprehensive evaluation of the automatic nature of face–body integration. At the behavioral level, both studies converge with prior literature by showing faster and more accurate recognition for congruent expressions and demonstrating asymmetrical integration, with bodily cues exerting dominant influence. This has been interpreted as evidence that facial and bodily cues are integrated into a holistic processing of emotions, with each type of cue contributing differently to this process. More precisely, facial expressions are prioritized over bodily cues and may require greater processing resources, potentially due to their higher ambiguity.

Critically, the EEG findings provide new insights into the temporal dynamics of this process. While Study 2 shows that rapid processing preserves both early and late neural activity, supporting the notion of an automatic integration pathway that remains functional under time pressure, Study 1 nuances this effect by demonstrating that reducing available cognitive resources dampens neural markers of integration, even though behavioral performance remains stable5. The modulation of cognitive resources in the integration of facial and bodily cues should be further investigated in future research to more precisely characterize how cognitive load influences the automaticity of this integration.

Our findings contribute to bring novel evidence to the field by clarifying how cognitive resources shape the temporal unfolding of face–body integration and by refining theoretical models of multisensory emotional perception. They underscore the importance of considering both behavioral and neural levels of analysis when assessing automaticity and advance our understanding of how emotional signals are integrated in more ecological conditions of perception than with only emotional facial expressions.
