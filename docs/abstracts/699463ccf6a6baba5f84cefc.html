Introduction

Gambling Disorder (GD) is recognized as an addictive disorder characterized by impaired executive control and maladaptive reward processing. While the DSM-5 criteria provide a clinical standard for diagnosis, the number of criteria met often fails to capture the neurobiological heterogeneity of the disorder, limiting its utility in predicting disease severity or treatment outcome1. The Cortico-Striatal-Thalamic (CST) circuitry is a prime candidate for investigating these deficits, as it orchestrates habit formation, reward evaluation, and impulse control2. Specifically, the "imbalance hypothesis" suggests a transition from goal-directed behavior (prefrontal control) to habitual, automatic behavior (striatal/motor drive) in addiction3,4. However, how specific subdivisions of the striatum and thalamus functionally decouple from cortical control networks in GD remains underexplored5. Therefore, we used functional MRI (fMRI) to map intrinsic functional connectivity alterations within the CST circuitry in patients with GD, testing the hypothesis that GD is characterized by a "hyper-habit/hypo-control" connectivity profile.

Methods

We recruited 14 patients with GD (Age 36.6±7.7; 79% Male; C-GSAS 17.3±7.1) and 14 age-sex matched healthy controls (HC). Diagnosis was confirmed using DSM-5 criteria. Imaging was performed on a 3.0T GE Discovery 750 scanner. We conducted a seed-based functional connectivity (FC) analysis using bilateral thalamus and striatal subregions as seeds. Regions of Interest (ROIs) included the thalamus and six striatal subregions defined by standard parcellation: inferior/superior ventral striatum (VSi, VSs), dorsal caudate (DC), dorsal caudal putamen (DCP), and dorsal/ventral rostral putamen (DRP, VRP). Preprocessing followed standard pipelines including motion correction and bandpass filtering including slice timing correction, normalization to MNI space, spatial smoothing, nuisance regression, and 0.01–0.1 Hz band-pass filtering. Second-level group comparisons were performed using independent samples t-tests. To ensure statistical rigor, we applied a voxel-wise threshold of p < 0.001 combined with a cluster-extent threshold (KE) to identify contiguous clusters in seed-based maps (cluster-level inference). Results were thresholded at p < 0.05, FWE-corrected (cluster-level), to identify robust connectivity differences.

Results

Hyperconnectivity in GD was observed between left DCP and right pallidum/putamen (MNI: 18, –2, 0; T = 5.12; KE = 592), between right DCP and right lingual gyrus (16, –56, –12; T = 3.76; KE = 202), and from right VRP to a left putamen/insula cluster (–24, 12, 8; T = 4.89; KE = 302). Furthermore, ventral striatum showed increased cross-hemispheric coupling with the putamen in GD (left VSi → right putamen: 22, –6, 12; T = 6.04; KE = 174; and right VSi → left putamen: –26, –12, 8; T = 4.27; KE=178). In contrast, GD exhibited significant hypoconnectivity in circuits linking right DCP to the right superior frontal gyrus (extending into the paracingulate gyrus; 22, 20, 42; T = 5.54; KE = 253), left VRP to the left occipital pole (–22, –90, –20; T = 4.55; KE = 176), and left DC to the left occipital pole (–2, –100, 2; T = 5.94; KE = 189). The left thalamus seed also showed reduced connectivity with a cluster in the right middle temporal gyrus (temporo-occipital region; 56, -46, 2; T = 6.2; KE = 157) in GD relative to HC.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that GD is characterized by an imbalance in network connectivity, with abnormally high coupling in certain subcortical circuits alongside weakened connections to cortical regions. Notably, GD patients showed heightened striatal connectivity with other basal ganglia and insular regions (e.g., left DCP–right pallidum hyperconnectivity), but reduced functional connectivity between striatal/thalamic seeds and higher-order cortical areas (e.g., right DCP–right superior frontal gyrus hypoconnectivity). This pattern of hyperconnectivity within reward/salience pathways and hypoconnectivity with frontal and visual association cortices suggests a dysregulated integration of reward, cognitive control, and visual processing networks in GD. These circuit-level alterations provide insight into the neural mechanisms of GD and may inform targeted neurobiological interventions.
