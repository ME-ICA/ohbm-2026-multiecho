Introduction

Survivors of childhood acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) have benefited from advances in chemotherapy, leading to high survival rates. However, many survivors experience persistent neurocognitive difficulties that can emerge years after treatment. These difficulties may affect higher-order cognitive abilities and negatively impact academic performance, daily functioning, and quality of life^1. Despite their relevance, the neural mechanisms underlying these long-term outcomes remain only partially understood.

Neuroimaging studies in survivors of childhood ALL indicate that chemotherapy-related neurotoxicity is associated with lasting alterations in brain organization, particularly involving white matter structure and brain networks. Structural and diffusion MRI studies have reported persistent abnormalities in white matter integrity, and functional MRI studies have described altered activation and connectivity during cognitive tasks^1. These findings suggest that treatment exposure during critical developmental periods may disrupt typical brain maturation. Importantly, variability in treatment intensity, including age at diagnosis, cumulative drug exposure, and biomarkers of treatment toxicity, may differentially influence brain connectivity and long-term cognitive outcomes^2.

In this work, we investigate how treatment intensity relates to brain connectivity alterations in long-term survivors of childhood ALL. Structural and functional connectomes are analyzed separately, together with clinical treatment variables and cognitive assessments. By identifying connections that differ between cognitively impaired and non-impaired participants and modeling their association with treatment variables, we aim to identify connections most strongly linked to treatment intensity and cognitive impairment, and to test whether treatment intensity predicts connectivity changes.

Methods

This study included 142 long-term survivors of pediatric acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) ^1, aged between 8 and 21 years, all treated under a standardized chemotherapy-only protocol designed for low and standard/high-risk disease. Treatment exposure was quantified using multiple clinical variables reflecting both cumulative drug burden and treatment timing. These variables included age at diagnosis (AgeatDX), intravenous methotrexate exposure quantified as area under the concentration–time curve (MTX_AUC), total number of triple intrathecal injections of methotrexate, hydrocortisone, and cytarabine (TOTAL_IT), dexamethasone exposure (DEX_DOSE), and plasma homocysteine area under the curve (HycAUC), included as a biomarker of treatment-related toxicity.

Neurocognitive outcomes were assessed approximately ten years after diagnosis using standardized tests covering four cognitive domains: Cognitive Flexibility, Working Memory, Cognitive Fluency, and Visuomotor Fluency. For each domain, outcomes were binarized as impaired or non-impaired according to established normative criteria. A global cognitive impairment index was defined by the presence of impairment in at least two of the four domains, allowing participants to be classified into cognitively impaired and non-impaired groups for subsequent analyses.

Neuroimaging data were acquired using magnetic resonance imaging protocols and included diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) (b = 1000; 3×1.8×1.8mm; acquisition time, 1.5 min each, with four acquisitions and 12 gradient directions) and task-based functional MRI (fMRI) (TR = 2.06s; TE = 30 ms; FOV = 192mm; matrix = 64x64; slice thickness = 5mm). Structural connectivity was derived from DTI data using deterministic tractography, and whole-brain structural connectivity matrices were generated using MRtrix3 software. Functional MRI data were preprocessed using the fMRIPrep pipeline, including standard steps for motion correction and spatial normalization. Functional connectivity matrices were computed using Pearson correlation between regional time series. In both modalities, brain regions were defined using the Desikan–Killiany atlas, which provided a consistent cortical parcellation scheme and node definition across analyses.

To investigate the relationship between treatment-related variables and brain connectivity, Partial Least Squares Regression (PLSR) was applied. PLSR^3 is a multivariate modeling approach well-suited for relating clinical predictors to high-dimensional connectome outcomes, as it can model multiple correlated response variables and is robust to collinearity among predictors. Given the dimensionality of the connectivity matrices, an initial feature selection step was performed to reduce noise and improve interpretability. Specifically, connections showing significant differences between cognitively impaired and non-impaired participants were identified using independent-samples t-tests. This procedure was conducted separately for structural and functional connectomes.

The resulting subsets of connections with significant group-level differences were used as response variables in the PLSR models, while treatment-related clinical variables served as predictors. Model performance was evaluated using prediction error metrics, enabling the identification of connections whose strength could be reliably predicted from treatment intensity measures.

Results

Group-level comparisons between cognitively impaired and non-impaired participants revealed a subset of connections with significant differences in both functional and structural connectivity. These connections were subsequently used as response variables in the Partial Least Squares Regression (PLSR) models to evaluate the extent to which treatment-related variables could predict brain connectivity patterns associated with cognitive impairment.

For functional connectivity, a total of 198 connections exhibited significant differences between groups and were included in the predictive analysis. Using these connections, the PLSR model achieved a global root mean squared error (RMSE) of 0.1211 across the test set. This result indicates that treatment-related variables were able to explain a substantial proportion of variability in functional connectivity strength for the selected features. Among the most accurately predicted functional connections were the bilateral precuneus connection and the connection between the right fusiform and right lingual regions (Fig. 2). These regions are known to play a central role in higher-order cognitive processing, including visuospatial integration and memory-related functions.

For structural connectivity, an initial set of 49 connections showed significant group-level differences between cognitively impaired and non-impaired participants. Before modeling, structural connectivity matrices were further filtered to retain only connections present in at least 75% of participants and exhibiting sufficient variance, in order to ensure robustness and reduce the influence of sparsely represented pathways. Structural connectivity values were standardized using z-score normalization before model fitting. Based on this subset of connections, the PLSR model achieved a global RMSE of 1.0503.

The structural connections with the lowest prediction error included pathways linking the superior parietal cortex with the rostral anterior cingulate cortex, as well as connections between the inferior temporal cortex and the rostral anterior cingulate. These regions have been previously implicated in cognitive functions and integrative processes ^4, suggesting that treatment-related effects may involve structural networks relevant for higher-order cognition.

Across both modalities, predictive performance improved when analyses were restricted to connections showing significant group differences, highlighting the relevance of these connections as potential markers of treatment-related brain alterations. Together, these results demonstrate that treatment intensity variables are meaningfully associated with specific patterns of functional and structural brain connectivity linked to cognitive impairment in long-term survivors of childhood ALL.

Conclusion

In this study, we examined the relationship between treatment intensity and brain connectivity alterations in long-term survivors of childhood acute lymphoblastic leukemia. By combining connectome-based analyses with multivariate modeling, we demonstrated that treatment-related clinical variables are associated with specific patterns of functional and structural brain connectivity linked to cognitive impairment.

Both functional and structural connectivity models achieved prediction errors below 10% of the corresponding feature value range, indicating stable predictive performance. Importantly, restricting the analysis to connections showing significant group-level differences between cognitively impaired and non-impaired participants improved model performance, highlighting the relevance of these connections as markers of treatment-related brain alterations. Among the most accurately predicted functional connections was the bilateral precuneus, along with other regions previously implicated in cognitive functions^5.

These findings suggest that treatment intensity leaves measurable and persistent effects on brain network organization, affecting connections that support higher-order cognitive functions. By modeling connectivity changes as a function of treatment exposure, this approach provides a framework for identifying neural circuits that may be particularly vulnerable to chemotherapy-related neurotoxicity.

Overall, this work supports the potential of connectome-based modeling to anticipate connectivity disruptions based on treatment-related variables, offering insights into mechanisms underlying long-term cognitive impairment in childhood leukemia survivors.
