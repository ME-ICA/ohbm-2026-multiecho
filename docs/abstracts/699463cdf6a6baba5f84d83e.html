Introduction

Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and magnetoencephalography (MEG) offer complementary advantages. MEG captures neuronal activity with millisecond temporal resolution but has lower spatial specificity and limited sensitivity to subcortical sources, while source reconstruction remains ill posed. In contrast, BOLD-fMRI provides higher spatial resolution and maps subcortical activity indirectly via haemodynamic responses, typically sampled every two to three seconds for whole-brain coverage. Integrating these modalities offers the potential to combine their respective strengths, but fundamental differences in signal properties present substantial challenges [1,2].

Neurovascular coupling studies have demonstrated close relationships between BOLD signals and neuro-electric activity, with particularly strong associations observed for high-frequency oscillations above 30 Hz [3]. However, these relationships are nonlinear and highly time-varying [4], complicating modelling of cross-modal temporal dependencies. While multimodal fusion research has largely focused on spatial alignment and decoding, comparatively little work has addressed temporal upsampling.

Building on prior work demonstrating spatial quality transfer between modalities [5], this study approaches MEG–fMRI temporal upsampling as a predictive dependency problem, leveraging high-temporal-resolution MEG signals to infer and reconstruct the time-varying relationship to slower BOLD responses, with the goal of data-driven temporal upsampling beyond the native fMRI sampling rate.

Methods

Sixteen volunteers underwent MEG and fMRI while viewing a 20-minute film sequence with diverse stimuli. BOLD responses were acquired with a 3T General Electric Signa 3T HDxt Scanner. Functional images were acquired using echo-planar imaging with a TR=2s, TE=30ms, flip angle of 76◦, and with SR=3mm³. Data were motion-corrected, high pass temporal filtered at 100Hz was used to remove low frequency drift and later registered to common space at 2mm³ and 6mm³ for comparative analyses. High-resolution (1mm³) anatomical images were collected via a fast-spoiled gradient echo sequence, and later used to aid projection to MNI space.

MEG was recorded with a CTF Omega 275-channel MEG system, bandpass filtered into delta (1–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–13 Hz), beta (13–30 Hz), and low gamma (40–60 Hz). Data at each frequency band were source localized using LCMV beamformer Synthetic-aperture magnetometry onto an MNI152 space at a resolution of 6 mm³. Using the Hilbert Transform, time-series were converted to an amplitude envelope which was de-spiked and high-pass filtered at >0.01Hz, removing drift, and convolved 
 with a canonical HRF to better match the BOLD profile.

Group-level Tensorial Independent Component Analysis (TICA) yielded 125 MEG (25 per extracted frequency band) and 30 fMRI components (ICs). For each fMRI IC, we trained a model to predict the fMRI time course at TR=2s from aligned MEG time courses sampled at 0.5s, giving us 4 MEG measurements for each fMRI measurement.

To emulate an upsampling scenario while retaining access to ground truth, we downsampled the fMRI time series form 2s to 4s by holding out odd-indexed timepoints as the test set, trained on even timepoints, and then upsampled back to the original temporal resolution using the models. Relevant model parameters were then tuned using inner split within even indices, with half serving as training, and half as validation subsets. After tuning, optimised parameters were refit on all even indices and evaluated on held-out odd indices.

For linear models, we preserved 1:1 mappings, with 1 timepoint in each of the 125 MEG timeseries predicting each fMRI timepoint. In all other models, sliding temporal windows were extracted from the MEG timecourses and aligned to individual fMRI samples. For each fMRI time point, we extracted a MEG window of 3 fMRI intervals on either side of the aligned index, with each interval containing four MEG samples. Thus, a window of w=3 results in (3+1+3) x 4 = 28 MEG time points per input sequence, paired with the corresponding fMRI value (See Figure 1b). In non-sequential models, these windows were flattened, resulting in a feature vector of a size of (125 x 28 = 3500 samples). In LSTM and Transformer models, we used the windows as inputs directly (See Figure 1c). Importantly, these windows overlap across neighbouring fMRI indices, allowing representations of local temporal structure for accurate interpolation. This approach enabled sequence-aware models to exploit short-range temporal dependencies without assuming global stationarity, which is beneficial for modelling complex inter-modal relationships.  

Benchmarked models included sequence-aware models such as transformer architecture and Long Short-Term Memory model (LSTM), and a range of models such as multilayer perceptron (MLP), XGBoost, and linear regression along with two regularised linear models (Lasso & Ridge), allowing for systematic comparison. Parameters were tuned using model-specific grids: Transformer (hidden size 64–256, heads 2–8, 2–3 layers), LSTM (hidden size 64–128, 1–2 layers, 0–0.2 dropout, optional bidirectionality),  MLP (128–256 units, 0–0.2 dropout), and XGBoost (300–800 trees, depth 3–6, learning rate 0.03–0.05) via inner validation (Figure 1c). As a baseline, we used linear interpolation anchored on even fMRI indices. Performance was computed on the odd indices, and quantified using the coefficient of determination (R²) and mean square error (MSE).

Results

For fMRI Component 1 (r = 0.72 with early visual areas, Figure 1a), the Transformer achieved the best upsampling performance (Train: R²=.932, MSE=.006; Test: R²=.932, MSE=.068), followed by LSTM (Train: R²=.998, MSE=.002; Test: R²=.925, MSE=.075), MLP (Train: Test: R²=.989, MSE=.011; Test: R²=.891, MSE=.110), XGBoost (Train: R²=.999, MSE=.001; Test: R²=.524, MSE=.481), Ridge (Train: R²=.735, MSE=0.253; Test: R²=-.59, MSE=1.600), Lasso (Train: R²=.735, MSE=.253; Test: R²=.109, MSE=.900) and OLS Linear model (Train: R²=.784, MSE=.207; Test: R²=-3.68, MSE=4.728). 

Performance was stable across validation splits, suggesting that improvements were not driven by overfitting to specific temporal segments. The even-anchored linear interpolation baseline provided a competitive reference on test subset, with R²=.92, and MSE=.080, but, despite this, both LSTM, and Transformer outperformed it when predicting ground-truth fMRI points from the same subset using only sliding windows from MEG as input, showing that the learned mappings captured cross-modal temporal relationships better. In XGBoost, we saw a degree of overtraining on train subset with not enough evidence of generalisation on test set. Linear models, with the exception of Lasso did not perform well on test, with Lasso showing signs of plateauing in terms of predictive performance, with similar results between training and testing subsets, but not nearly as powerful as sequential models. This tells us that in linear models, certain degree of feature selection could help with their training, but even then they do not possess nearly as much potential as models specialised to work with sequential data.

Overall, sequence-aware models consistently outperformed static baselines, indicating that explicitly modelling temporal structure provides a substantial advantage over treating windowed MEG inputs as unordered features. Conclusion

The strong training and upsampling performance of the Transformer, LSTM, and MLP models demonstrates that MEG-derived features contain sufficient information to reconstruct fMRI temporal dynamics at higher sampling rates when evaluated in-sample. Notably, even with reduced training data, all three models consistently outperformed linear interpolation when predicting held-out fMRI timepoints drawn from the same temporal sequence. This indicates that the learned mappings reflect meaningful dependencies between modalities rather than simple temporal smoothing, and that MEG signals provide predictive information beyond what can be recovered from BOLD timecourses alone.

Performance gains were most pronounced for models that explicitly preserved temporal structure, with sequence-aware architectures achieving the highest predictive accuracy. In contrast, models that treated windowed MEG inputs as unordered features showed more limited performances, showing that temporal ordering within MEG windows plays a critical role in capturing cross-modal dependencies. These findings support the use of temporal modelling strategies when attempting to infer detailed BOLD dynamics from electrophysiological signals.

Importantly, the observed improvements demonstrate that MEG temporal dynamics can be mapped onto slower BOLD fluctuations in a reproducible manner, offering strong potential for data-driven temporal upsampling. These capabilities could be integrated with previous work on spatial MEG upsampling and quality transfer [5] to produce fused multimodal representations that combine MEG’s temporal resolution with fMRI’s spatial specificity, enabling richer characterisation of brain dynamics than either modality alone.

It should be emphasised, however, that despite strong predictive performance, the primary contribution of the proposed framework lies in characterising predictive relationships between modalities rather than inferring direct neurophysiological mechanisms. Several limitations should therefore be noted. The analysis was conducted on data acquired during a shared naturalistic stimulus, and model performance may vary across experimental paradigms or resting-state conditions. Furthermore, the evaluation was performed at the group level and does not currently capture inter-subject variability, which may influence both predictive accuracy and learned temporal dependencies.

Future work should focus on improving generalisability to unseen data, which will likely require substantially larger and more diverse datasets. The framework could also be extended to subject-specific modelling, cross-task generalisation, modality-to-modality data synthesis, and the incorporation of spatial information and physiological priors to further constrain temporal inference and offer more biologically plausible insights into the relationships between the two signals. In addition, integration and further development of explainability methods will be essential for translating predictive performance into interpretable insights regarding cross-modal temporal relationships.

Overall, these results demonstrate that cross-modal temporal upsampling using MEG and fMRI can outperform interpolation-based baselines and reveal rich, mappable correspondences between electrophysiological and haemodynamic signals. While challenges related to generalisation and interpretation remain, the findings provide strong evidence that data-driven temporal fusion can effectively leverage the complementary strengths of MEG and fMRI, offering a promising framework for high-resolution modelling of brain dynamics.
