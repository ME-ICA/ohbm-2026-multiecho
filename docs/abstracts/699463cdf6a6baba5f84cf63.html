Introduction

Structure-function coupling (SFC) quantifies the extent to which structural connectivity (SC) supports functional connectivity (FC) across brain regions. SFC is thought to follow a unimodal-to-transmodal gradient, with stronger alignment between SC and FC in primary unimodal cortices such as early visual areas, and weaker alignment in higher-order association cortices.1 Bipolar disorder (BD) is a neuropsychiatric condition characterized by recurrent episodes of depression and mania or hypomania and is associated with widespread structural and functional dysconnectivity and abnormal network organization.2 Mapping integrative properties of brain connectivity in BD, including SFC, may therefore help clarify mechanisms of BD neuropathology. A prior independent component analysis study found higher SFC in the middle frontal gyrus and lower SFC in the supramarginal gyrus in BD,3 yet the distributed nature of the independent components restricts their regional specificity. 

In addition, previous research showed that both BD and SFC are heritable.2,4 However, the exact genetic architecture of SFC remains largely unexplored. Characterizing genetic influences on SFC may clarify the biological factors that shape the relationship between SC and FC. In regions where BD is associated with altered SFC, these findings may also provide insight into the genetic overlap between BD and neural disorganization. 

Here, we first estimated and characterized regional SFC across the brain. We then tested for SFC differences between individuals with BD and controls. Finally, for regions showing significant SFC alterations in BD, we conducted genome-wide association studies (GWAS) to investigate the genetic underpinnings of SFC.

Methods

We analyzed UK Biobank data from individuals with BD (n=163) and age and sex-matched controls (n=326). For each participant, regional SFC was computed using diffusion-weighted MRI-derived structural connectomes (SIFT2-weighted) and fMRI regional time series, both parcellated with the 200 / 500-region Schaefer 7-network cortical atlas combined with the 16 / 54-region Melbourne subcortical atlas, respectively. Functional connectivity matrices were generated twice per parcellation using Pearson and partial correlations to capture distinct functional dependencies.

To quantify SFC for each individual and each region, we applied multilinear regression. Predictors were derived from SC edges from each region i to all other regions j ≠ i (weighted communicability, weighted mean first passage time, and weighted shortest path length), along with Euclidean distance. The predicted variable was the FC of region i to all other regions j ≠ i. The adjusted R² between the observed and predicted functional edges of each region was taken as its SFC measure.1

To identify SFC differences in BD, we performed a hypothesis-free exploration of all regional SFC values using general linear models with a gamma distribution and log link, adjusted for age, sex, mean resting-state fMRI and dMRI head motion, total intracranial volume, and imaging site.

We then calculated SFC and conducted GWASs in an independent UK Biobank sample (n=38,190) for each region showing significant group differences. Quality control was performed using PLINK2, and GWAS was conducted using REGENIE5 while covarying for sex, age, age², imaging site, mean resting-state fMRI and dMRI head motion, total intracranial volume, and the first 20 genetic principal components. Functional annotation, gene mapping, and pathway and tissue enrichment analyses were performed using FUMA.

Results

The BD and control groups were matched for age (62±7 years) and sex (53% female). With Pearson FC, mean SFC across all participants was highest in the occipital pole (R²=0.29) and cuneus (R²=0.28) and lowest in the insula and hippocampus (both R²=0.03). With partial FC, SFC was highest in lateral occipital regions (R²=0.07) and lowest in the globus pallidus and nucleus accumbens (R²=−0.005). 

With Pearson FC, at the 216-region resolution, BD showed higher SFC in the left temporal pole (β=0.267, pFDR=0.018) and right superior frontal gyrus (β=0.211, pFDR=0.026). With partial FC, at 216-region resolution, BD showed lower SFC in the left precentral gyrus (β=−0.227, pFDR=0.029) and left anterior supramarginal gyrus (β=−0.206, pFDR=0.029). At the 554-region resolution, BD showed lower SFC in the right frontal pole (β=−0.275, pFDR=0.002) and right postcentral gyrus (β=−0.274, pFDR=0.007). 

GWASs were conducted in an independent cohort (mean age 55±8 years, 53% female) for the five regions showing consistent SFC difference directionality across parcellations: left temporal pole, right superior frontal gyrus, left precentral gyrus, right frontal pole, and left supramarginal gyrus. Significant loci (p<5×10⁻⁸) were identified for three regions: five loci for the left precentral gyrus, one for the left temporal pole, and one for the right frontal pole. 

FUMA mapped these loci to 25 genes for the left precentral gyrus, 12 for the left temporal pole, and 10 for the right frontal pole, highlighting INPP5A and PAX8, KAT6B and DUPD1, and PLCE1 and NOC3L genes, with functional annotations implicating immune and developmental pathways and overlap with prior neuroimaging and cardiometabolic traits.

Conclusion

The regional spread of SFC strength across the brain was aligned with previous evidence and displayed a unimodal-to-transmodal gradient.1 SFC alterations in BD were observed across regions, with stronger structure-function alignment in limbic and attentional regions and weaker alignment in sensorimotor, executive, attention, and salience regions. This pattern may be consistent with increased anatomical constraints on emotion-related processing alongside reduced network flexibility supporting attentional control, cognitive integration, and psychomotor function, processes that are commonly disrupted in BD. 

Genes linked to SFC are implicated in cellular signaling and regulatory mechanisms that may shape anatomical constraints on neural communication. Notably, key genes such as INPP5A and PLCE1, involved in intracellular signaling, NOC3L, related to DNA replication and cell proliferation, and KAT6B, which regulates chromatin remodeling and neurodevelopment, point to genetic influences on neurodevelopmental and transcriptional processes. These findings suggest that genetic variation affecting neuronal signaling, synaptic plasticity, and brain maturation contributes to individual differences in SFC, consistent with broad effects on brain organization.
