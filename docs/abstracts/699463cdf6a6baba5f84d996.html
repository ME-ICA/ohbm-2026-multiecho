Introduction

Alterations in subthalamic nucleus (STN) local field potentials (LFP) activity is a hallmark of Parkinson’s disease (PD) and a target of adaptive deep brain stimulation (DBS). This pathological neural activity has traditionally been characterized by exaggerated synchrony in the low-beta band (13–20 Hz), with dopamine medication-induced reductions in this band moderately linked to motor improvement (1). However, growing evidence indicates that PD-related electrophysiological abnormalities extend beyond low beta (2), involving theta, alpha, high-beta, and gamma activity associated with symptom fluctuations, medication effects, and dyskinesias(3). 

At the same time, neural oscillations are increasingly recognized as non-stationary, occurring as transient bursts rather than sustained rhythms. In PD, burst duration, amplitude, and rate are modulated by medication and DBS and relate to motor impairment (4). Yet most burst analyses rely on summary statistics that assume a characteristic scale, potentially obscuring richer structure in the underlying dynamics. Recent work suggests instead that neural activity may be organized in a scale-free manner, consistent with the criticality hypothesis, which posits that healthy brain networks operate near a critical point between order and disorder (5) 

Motivated by this framework, we examine whether power-law-derived metrics of burst dynamics (burst duration, magnitude, and inter-burst interval) capture clinically relevant variation in STN activity beyond conventional power-based measures. We analyse LFPs across five frequency bands (theta, alpha, low beta, high beta, and gamma) and assess their ability to predict medication state and symptom change in the most affected side (MAS) and least affected side (LAS) independently. This study provides a first systematic exploration of scale-free burst dynamics in PD, establishing a foundation for validation in an independent cohort

Methods

Participants and procedure 
Thirty-two individuals with PD who had previously undergone subthalamic nucleus DBS participated. During a laboratory session, DBS was switched off. Two 8-minute resting-state recordings were acquired per participant: one ON and one OFF levodopa medication, with order randomized. UPDRS-I and II were assessed once, and UPDRS-III was assessed in both medication states. 

Signal processing and feature extraction 
LFP recordings were minimally preprocessed to remove powerline noise. Aperiodic (1/f) and oscillatory components were modelled using the fitting oscillations & one-over-f (FOOOF) algorithm, yielding offset and slope parameters. Static spectral features were calculated as spectral mass in theta (5–8 Hz), alpha (9–12 Hz), low beta (13–20 Hz), high beta (21–30 Hz), and gamma (31–80 Hz) bands. 

Dynamic features were computed from oscillatory bursts in each band. Bursts were defined as envelope excursions above the 75th percentile of the ON-medication envelope, applied consistently across conditions. Burst duration, inter-burst interval, and burst magnitude were extracted. Distributions of each metric were fitted with power-law models to extract the scaling exponent and lower bound (xmin).   

Feature selection for medication status 
Elastic net logistic regression was used to identify features most relevant for classifying medication status (ON vs. OFF). For burst metrics, separate elastic net models were run for each frequency band, while spectral and aperiodic features were analyzed together in a single model. Nested cross-validation (CV) with leave-one-subject-out CV (LOSO-CV) outer loop and 5-fold CV inner loop for hyperparameter tuning was used to evaluate the model’s generalisation. Features were considered relevant if selected in ≥50% of folds with standardised coefficients exceeding ±0.1. All features deemed relevant in this first round were then combined into a second elastic net model to identify a final set of features predictive of medication state. Models were controlled for levodopa equivalent dose (LED) to account for individual differences in dopaminergic state. 

Feature selection for symptom changes 
To assess clinical relevance, we computed medication-induced changes in LFP features as (OFF–ON)/OFF and corresponding changes in UPDRS-III subscales (axial, bradykinesia, tremor, rigidity) as OFF–ON. Elastic net multiple regression was used to evaluate whether feature changes predicted symptom changes. Burst metrics were analyzed by frequency band, while all spectral and aperiodic features were analyzed together.  All features were analyzed separately for the MAS and LAS. Features identified as relevant in this first round were then included in a second, combined elastic net model. Featureimportance thresholds were the same as for medication-state analyses (≥50% of folds, coefficient > ±0.1), and models were again controlled for LED.  

Results

Features predictive of medication status 

Elastic net feature selection identified a distributed set of aperiodic, spectral, and burst-derived features associated with medication status, with both shared and side-specific patterns. In the final model (round 2), medication status for both the most affected side (MAS) and least affected side (LAS) was consistently associated with the fractal slope, alongside burst metrics spanning theta, alpha, beta, and gamma bands. 

For the MAS, selected features were dominated by burst dynamics, particularly burst duration (BD), inter-burst interval (IBD), and burst magnitude (BM) scaling parameters and lower bounds across theta, low beta, high beta, and gamma bands. For the LAS, a similar multiband profile emerged, with overlapping involvement of theta, alpha, low beta, high beta, and gamma burst metrics, though the specific parameters differed. Overall, medication status was best characterized by a combination of scale-free burst properties across multiple frequency bands rather than by spectral power alone.

Features predictive of  symptom change 

Elastic net models revealed symptom-specific and side-dependent patterns linking changes in LFP features to changes in UPDRS-III subscales. 

Bradykinesia 
Bradykinesia improvement was primarily associated with burst dynamics. On the MAS, predictive features were confined to the theta band, including BD slope, BD xmin, and IBD xmin. On the LAS, predictors extended across theta, alpha, and gamma bands, with contributions from multiple burst metrics. All features identified in the first selection round remained relevant in the combined model, confirming their robustness. 

Axial symptoms 
For axial symptoms, feature selection differed markedly by side. On the MAS, predictors included a mix of theta burst metrics and high-beta spectral mass and burst parameters. On the LAS, predictive features were exclusively gamma-band burst metrics. In the final model, only LAS features remained relevant, indicating a dominant contribution of gamma-band dynamics to axial symptom change. 

Tremor 
Tremor-related changes were associated with both spectral and burst features. On the MAS, predictors spanned alpha and high-beta spectral mass alongside multiple gamma-band burst metrics. On the LAS, tremor prediction relied on a smaller feature set, primarily involving alpha and low-beta spectral mass and gamma burst magnitude. Most features remained relevant in the final combined model. 

Rigidity 
Rigidity showed the broadest feature involvement. On the MAS, only high-beta burst metrics were predictive. In contrast, LAS rigidity was associated with a wide range of features, including fractal offset, spectral mass across multiple bands, and theta and gamma burst dynamics. In the final model, nearly all LAS features remained predictive, highlighting strong multiband involvement. 

Conclusion

This study examined whether power-law–derived metrics of STN burst dynamics predict medication status and levodopa-induced symptom change in PD. We provide novel evidence that scale-free properties of neural activity capture clinically relevant information beyond conventional spectral measures, offering new insight into how dopaminergic medication modulates pathological neural dynamics.

Using a two-stage elastic net approach, medication status was best explained by a distributed set of features spanning multiple frequency bands, including aperiodic slope and burst metrics in theta, alpha, beta, and gamma ranges. This supports evidence that levodopa effects extend beyond the canonical beta band. However, when all features were entered into a combined model, some lost importance, indicating shared variance across bands. Thus, optimal feature selection depends on the research question: multiband features best capture medication effects, whereas symptom-related changes show greater frequency specificity.

In contrast, symptom improvements were primarily associated with theta- and gamma-band power-law metrics. Bradykinesia related most strongly to theta and gamma burst dynamics; axial symptoms to gamma and high-beta features; rigidity to theta and gamma metrics with additional contributions from low-beta spectral mass; and tremor to gamma and high-beta power-law metrics alongside low-beta spectral mass. Notably, low-beta burst power-law metrics were less influential than expected. When low beta contributed, it did so mainly via spectral mass rather than burst-specific scaling, challenging the prevailing emphasis on low-beta bursting and suggesting that spectral mass may reflect multiple overlapping mechanisms, whereas power-law metrics in theta, gamma, and high beta provide more specific markers of symptom change.

Levodopa-induced changes in power-law metrics were bidirectional, with both heavier and lighter tails and variable shifts in xmin. Under criticality frameworks, heavier tails and lower xmin are often interpreted as movement toward healthier, near-critical dynamics. The observed bidirectionality suggests a more complex picture, potentially reflecting normalization of both sub- and hypercritical states or adverse medication effects such as dyskinesias. For example, gamma burst duration showed lighter tails with medication and was associated with tremor improvement, suggesting that gamma activity may reflect a hypercritical state in PD that is normalized by levodopa.

Analyses of more affected (MAS) and less affected sides (LAS) revealed notable hemispheric differences. While some effects converged, many predictors of medication status and symptom change were side-specific, possibly reflecting differential disease stages. Methodologically, although low-beta spectral mass was consistent across hemispheres, other metrics were not, indicating that treating hemispheres as independent observations may introduce noise for certain features.

Limitations include the absence of a control group, reliance on UPDRS scores, and the need for independent out-of-sample validation.

In summary, this study provides the first systematic evidence that scale-free burst metrics predict both medication status and symptom improvement in PD. Medication effects span frequency bands, whereas symptom improvements are primarily linked to theta and gamma dynamics, supporting a role for modulated neural criticality in PD and informing future biomarker and DBS optimization efforts.
