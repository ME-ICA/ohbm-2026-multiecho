Introduction

Spinal cord fMRI commonly relies on generalized linear models in which task regressors are convolved with a canonical hemodynamic response function (HRF) originally developed for the brain. This practice persists despite well-established physiological and vascular differences between the brain and spinal cord, as well as the limited empirical characterization of spinal cord hemodynamics. Early work by Giulietti et al. (2008) used an impulse-response approach during a motor task to demonstrate spinal HRF dynamics that diverged from canonical brain models, while Cohen-Adad et al. (2010) showed that spinal BOLD responses to controlled hypercapnia differed from cerebral responses in timing and amplitude. Together, these findings suggest that spinal HRF properties may vary across neural and vascular challenges, yet such differences are rarely incorporated into standard analysis pipelines, increasing the risk of false negative activation estimates.

To address this gap, we analyzed high-quality spinal cord fMRI data acquired during hand-grasp motor execution and hypercapnic breath-hold challenges, each paired with precise physiological recordings (force traces and end-tidal CO2, respectively). The hand-grasp dataset consists of a cross-sectional group analysis of approximately 30 participants, providing robust estimates of the group-average spinal BOLD response to a motor task. In contrast, the breath-hold dataset comprises a dense-sampling, precision-mapping experiment in two individuals, enabling detailed assessment of within-subject response dynamics and subject-specific differences. Using these complementary designs, we constructed data-driven block-averaged spinal BOLD responses and directly compared them with raw task regressors and their canonical HRF-convolved counterparts. This framework provides an empirical evaluation of canonical HRF assumptions in the spinal cord and informs the development of HRF models better tailored to spinal neurovascular dynamics.

Methods

Participants. Healthy adults participated in two task paradigms: hand-grasp (HG) and breath-hold (BH). Spinal cord MRI data were collected on a 3T Siemens Prisma using a 64-channel head/neck coil and a SatPadTM collar to improve B0 homogeneity. High-resolution sagittal T2-weighted images covered the brainstem–upper thoracic spine. Functional scans used ZOOMit EPI (TR = 2s), with axial slices positioned perpendicular to the cord (approx. C4–C7).

Hand Grasp Task. Before scanning, 30 participants performed maximal voluntary contraction (MVC) using a Jamar dynamometer; target force levels corresponding to 25% MVC were displayed during fMRI using bespoke MATLAB software for visual feedback. Data from four participants were excluded due to image artifacts, incidental findings, or force-recording failures. MR-compatible hand-grip devices equipped with single-degree-of-freedom load cells continuously recorded force. Each run lasted 10 min and included 24 pseudorandomized left/right 15s grasps separated by variable rest durations. Force, respiratory belt, pulse (on dorsalis pedis artery), and expired CO2 were recorded synchronously [3].

Breath Hold Task. The hypercapnic BH paradigm consisted of seven cycles of paced breathing (24s), end-expiration BH (18s), brief exhalation (2s), and recovery (6s), embedded in a 205-volume (~6.8 min) run. Two highly sampled participants (BH-01, BH-03) each completed 18 runs across three scan sessions, providing sufficient data for single-subject characterization. Physiological signals, including CO2, respiration belt, and pulse, were acquired continuously throughout each run [4].

Preprocessing. DICOMs were converted using dcm2niix_afni. Spinal cord fMRI underwent 2D slice-wise motion correction using the Neptune Toolbox, which applies Gaussian-weighted registration to a temporal median reference via AFNI tools. A Spinal Cord Toolbox (SCT) auto-generated spinal cord mask was used to constrain motion estimation. Anatomical T2-weighted images were segmented using sct_deepseg_sc and registered to the PAM50 template, yielding subject-specific warps for normalization [5]. The first and last five fMRI volumes were removed. Respiratory and cardiac signals were pre-processed and slice-wise RETROICOR regressors (eight respiratory and eight cardiac) were generated. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) regressors were derived from high-variance voxels within a CSF mask created by subtracting the spinal cord mask from surrounding tissue. Slice-wise in-plane motion parameters were extracted during Neptune correction. Nuisance regressors were regressed using AFNI’s 3dTproject, which also demeaned the data. The denoised fMRI time series were then warped into PAM50 space and cropped to spinal segments common across all task scans.

Block Averaging. Task-locked block averages were computed using AFNI’s 3dDeconvolve, restricting analyses to ventral gray matter in PAM50 space using a probabilistic gray matter mask thresholded at 50%. Task traces (force %MVC; end-tidal CO2 mmHg) were segmented into task-aligned epochs and averaged in raw and canonical HRF-convolved forms, then amplitude-scaled to the ventral gray matter (GM) BOLD range (Fig. 1). The denoised BOLD time series were entered into the same model to estimate voxel-wise block-averaged responses using triangular basis functions (TENT; 24s for HG, 50s for BH). Framewise displacement (FD) was processed in parallel, and standard errors for BOLD and FD block averages were estimated across runs or subjects using restricted maximum likelihood (REML).

Results

Across both motor and hypercapnic paradigms, ventral gray matter (GM) BOLD responses exhibited a clear task-locked structure. For the hand-grasp task, block-averaged ipsilateral ventral GM responses showed a robust increase during the contraction period for both left and right hands, with peak amplitudes occurring approximately 3–5 TRs (6-10s) after grasp onset. Both the onset timing and response width deviated from canonical predictions, with earlier rises and broader plateaus. Task-evoked activation was strongest within the ipsilateral ventral hemicord, consistent with lateralized motor drive during unilateral grasping. A smaller but reliable contralateral response was also observed, suggesting bilateral engagement of spinal motor circuitry during unilateral grasping and warranting further investigation.

Group-level FD profiles revealed reduced motion during active grasp execution, followed by small but systematic increases near task offset, likely reflecting muscle relaxation after contraction. Block-averaged CO2 traces showed minimal amplitude variation and no consistent task-locked modulation. CO2 fluctuations did not align with grasp onset, offset, or the timing of the ventral GM BOLD response and were comparable across left and right conditions. Consistent with this observation, Pearson correlation coefficients between FD/CO2 and ipsilateral BOLD responses were modest (FD: Left grip r = 0.069, Right grip r = 0.399; CO2: Left grip r = −0.539, Right grip r = −0.286). Together, these results indicate that CO2-related vascular effects are unlikely to account for the group-level task-evoked BOLD responses observed during hand grasp (Fig. 2). Furthermore, the negative correlation between CO2 and the BOLD response suggests that this confound may reduce our sensitivity to activation during hand grasping, rather than artificially enhance our estimation of neural activity.

In the breath-hold task, canonical HRF convolution again introduces an inherent temporal shift, and the HRF-convolved CO2 regressor peaked about 3 TRs (6s) later than the raw PETCO2 signal. In contrast, the block-averaged ventral GM response more closely followed the unfiltered PETCO2 time course. However, the absolute temporal offset between our task model and the BOLD data is expected to vary due to measurement delays and individualized vascular transit times, as well as due to potential local differences in hemodynamics. Interestingly, the unique dense-sampling dataset allows us to identify differences in the BOLD fMRI response shapes in the two individuals, distinct from their common PETCO2 measurement delays: Fig 2 shows that BH-01 has a wider and more prolonged response, and BH-03 has a faster return to baseline following the same breath-hold task, despite highly similar models derived from PETCO2 recordings. Respiratory belt traces exhibited clear task-locked structure across both BH participants, closely reflecting the imposed breathing paradigm. During the breath-hold period, belt signals remained relatively stable, followed by a sharp deflection at exhalation and the onset of recovery. During paced breathing, belt traces showed large-amplitude, periodic oscillations consistent with the paced respiratory cycles. These oscillations temporally coincided with increased framewise displacement and larger variability in ventral GM BOLD responses, indicating that respiratory mechanics introduce consistent, task-correlated physiological and motion-related fluctuations during the paced breathing phase (Fig. 2).

Conclusion

Across both neural and vascular paradigms, convolving task regressors with the canonical HRF systematically introduced a temporal lag relative to the measured spinal BOLD response. During the hand-grasp task, the raw force trace peaked within one TR of cue onset, indicating good task compliance and minimal behavioral delay. The spinal BOLD response exhibited a slightly delayed but rapid rise and sustained plateau, followed by a slower post-task decay consistent with hemodynamic washout. In contrast, the HRF-convolved force regressor showed a more pronounced delay and accelerated decay, misaligning with both the timing and temporal profile of the observed BOLD response.

For the breath-hold task, additional physiological delay arises because CO2 is sampled at the nasal cavity and only later influences the spinal vasculature. As a result, PETCO2-based analyses require temporal shifting of the regressor to account for subject-specific propagation delays. The canonical HRF convolution further smooths the PETCO2 signal, producing a broader response with a less well-defined peak that lags behind both the raw PETCO2 trace and the measured spinal BOLD response. Importantly, the dense-sampling breath-hold data enable within-subject characterization of response timing and shape, revealing individual differences in onset latency, peak timing, and response width that are likely driven by subject-specific vascular transit dynamics and would be obscured in group-averaged analyses.

Large standard error envelopes in the block-averaged responses highlight persistent challenges in spinal cord fMRI, particularly in the presence of structured motion-related fluctuations in the signal. Breath-holding induces heterogeneous CO2 dynamics and variable respiratory effort, contributing to the higher variance observed in BH-derived responses.

Despite these challenges, the strong correspondence among physiological recordings, motion estimates, and ventral gray matter BOLD signals demonstrates that block-design spinal cord fMRI can capture meaningful neural and vascular responses when supported by rigorous preprocessing and nuisance regression. At the same time, reliance on canonical, brain-derived HRFs risks systematic model misspecification in spinal cord fMRI, potentially biasing parameter estimates and obscuring physiologically meaningful inter-subject and inter-segment variability. Together, these findings motivate the adoption of subject-specific or data-driven HRF approaches, particularly for vascular challenges such as breath holding, and the continued development of spinal cord–specific modeling frameworks that explicitly account for distinct neurovascular and physiological dynamics.
