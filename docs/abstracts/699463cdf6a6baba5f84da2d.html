Introduction

Normative models have been used to estimate age- and sex-dependent normative ranges of brain-imaging phenotypes, to measure and detect individuals' brain atypicality, and to connect it to possible clinical characteristics1. The approach of detecting individual brain atypicalities had an advantage over standard case-control studies because it does not assume that atypicalities are shared across individuals with the same diagnosis. In principle, every person with the specific diagnosis can have unique and previously unseen brain atypicality, which can still be detected as a deviation from normative ranges of the brain imaging phenotype, if those are well estimated.

However, many variables derived from brain images are not measured directly but are only estimated, i.e., measured with error, including, among others, task-based brain activity maps, resting state network maps, and functional connectivity measures. Applying standard normative models to such data will not uncover the true distribution of these phenotypes in the population, but the distribution that is a mixture of the true distribution of phenotypes and the subject-specific measurement errors, leading to systematically biased estimates of the normative models and subsequent subject-specific brain atypicality scores.

Recently, we introduced a method for normative modeling that estimates the conditional distribution of the directly unobserved phenotype from noisy measurements by accounting for measurement error using a mixed-effect heteroskedastic meta-regression model. While this method accounts for measurement error in the model estimation, it does not account for it when estimating subjects' abnormality. In this work, we address this limitation by proposing novel measures of abnormality that account for the measurement error and comparing them with traditionally used measures.

Methods

The model used is a mixed-effect location-scale meta-regression model2, with subject measurements modeled as a distribution of random effects with known subject-level variance and unknown between-subject variance.

The between-subject variance can be modeled as a function of other variables, thus allowing for heteroskedastic normative distribution estimation. This is important because it is not guaranteed that the variance of imaging phenotypes does not change with age or other variables of interest. For the kth subject: y_k = θ + β_1x_1k + .. .+ β_nx_nk + ɡ_k+ ζ_k Where θ is an intercept, β_1, …, β_n are

coefcients of independent variables x_1, … x_n (e.g., age and sex). ζ_k are the between-subjects random effects assumed to be normally distributed with N(0, v), and ɡ_k is a normally distributed residual error term. The normative distribution variance equals the v estimate from the random effects model. Centiles are then calculated from the corresponding normal distribution.

The z-score can be calculated as

z = (y - y-hat) / σ

Where σ represents the estimated variance of the variable of interest in the reference sample, this can be extended to account for the uncertainty of the estimated parameters of the normative model1.

z = (y - y-hat) / sqrt(σ_a^2 + σ_b^2)

Where σ_a is the estimated variance of the reference sample, and σ_b is the variance of the estimated parameter of the normative model.

These z-scores can also be used with the proposed method. However, they do not account for the uncertainty in the estimated network coefficients (i.e., the measurement error), which can bias the estimated subject’s atypicality. This measurement error can make a person’s values appear atypical due to random chance, even though the subject’s actual uncertainty-unaffected parameter may be within the typical range for the population and vice versa. We might extend the z-score formulations to also include the measurement error (e.g. the standard error of the beta estimate of the voxel activity) by including an additional term:

z = (y - y-hat) / sqrt(σ_a^2 + σ_b^2 + σ_c^2)

Experiment 1, model fitting bias: We have randomly simulated data for 500 subjects with a uniformly distributed age variable between 20 and 80. The measurement-error-free outcome variable was simulated as 100 - age0.1 - (age - 50)^20.005 + e where e was normally distributed with mean 0 and variance 1. Then, we simulated measurement error by adding a random noise with mean 0 and variance 1.25 to the measurement-error-free outcome. We compared our proposed model with an ordinary least-squares model that did not account for the measurement error.

Experiment 2: Detection of atypical activation maps. The proposed and standard abnormality measures have been validated by their ability to detect atypical brain-activation maps. We already used the preprocessed AOMIC3 dataset using brain activation maps for the n-back task. 500 subjects had been randomly selected as a normative sample. We developed normative models of task-activation in the n-back 1 task. We fitted a normative model to every voxel with age and sex as predictors. The remaining subjects were used as the test set. In the test set, we calculated abnormalities for each voxel across two n-back contrast types: n-back 1 and n-back 3. The n-back one contrast was the same as that used when fitting normative models; therefore, the test-set n-back one maps should not, on average, be atypical with respect to the estimated models. However, n-back three should be atypical. Our goal is to calculate the atypicality measure that best discriminates between n-back 1 and 3. If a method can discriminate between these 2 contrasts, it will also be able to discriminate between other groups of parametric maps, for example, between healthy and unhealthy subjects. The whole map abnormality was calculated as the number of absolute z-scores exceeding 3. Statistical significance was assessed by a paired permutation test.

Results

The proposed method estimated the variance of the normative distribution without bias, whereas the standard method fitted noise, leading to an overestimated variance. 

Discrimination between typical and atypical activation maps was AUC=0.72 for the standard z-score, and AUC=0.81 (p<0.001 for the difference) for the z-score accounting for the measurement error in variables 

Conclusion

In this study, we presented a multi-level normative model that estimates the normative distribution of unobserved phenotypes from noisy measurements, thereby improving the accuracy of normative models and their ability to detect abnormal brain maps. We showed in simulations that the proposed approach, unlike standard normative modeling methods, can estimate the distributional parameters of variables without bias, including situations where measurement errors are subject-specific, correlated with other variables, or depend on other variables (heteroskedastic model). In real data, we show that our proposed method detects atypical brain activity maps with greater accuracy than standard normative models.

The multi-level normative models can also be seen as an application and extension of multi-level models, which are already widely used in neuroimaging to analyze functional data, accounting for different measurement errors in estimated effects across subjects and scanning sessions. The main difference is in the application. Neuro multi-level models are usually used to improve estimates of group-average brain activity or between-group contrasts, and to generalize results to the population. In normative modeling, the goal is to obtain valid estimates of normative distribution conditioning on other variables.

The model assumes that the conditional distribution of the unmeasured variables is Gaussian, whether homoskedastic or heteroskedastic. While normative models that relax this assumption and allow for the flexible modeling of non-Gaussian distributions exist4,5, extending these models to situations where outcome variables are measured with error remains unclear. The measurement error is also assumed to be Gaussian and to be known or estimated from the data, so that it cannot be used to model sparse functional connectivity matrices. Another issue is the possibility of the convergence of the variance estimate to 0 or near 0, rendering the estimated normative model useless. This happens if the within-subject variance is relatively large compared to the between-subject variance. The solution is to have higher-quality data (less within-subject variance) and more subjects. Another solution might be to use Bayesian models with priors on the variance estimates, or to pool estimates across voxels.

The proposed z-score improved the discrimination power of the standard z-score by downweighting uncertain data points, which are more likely to be outliers due to measurement noise. The higher noise level makes the measurement appear more normal. However, this is not the ideal solution, since it doesn't discriminate between data points that are deemed to be within the norm with small measurement error (i.e., we are highly certain they are indeed within the norm) and those within the norm with high measurement error. Another room for improvement is how individual voxel abnormalities are combined into a per-map abnormality. Counting the number of voxels above threshold is common but very rudimentary, and it does not propagate the uncertainty intervals from individual voxels to the whole brain atypicality score. 

In conclusion, we have presented a multi-level normative model, which can incorporate measurement error into model estimation and brain anomaly detection. Our findings demonstrate the necessity of accounting for measurement error in normative modeling. Otherwise, models will be systematically biased, regardless of sample size, less sensitive to detecting atypical subjects, and more likely to yield false positives or negatives due to measurement error. These methods will allow us to develop more reliable models of task activity and resting state networks. Although normative models for these modalities exist, they do not account for the measurement error, making them vulnerable to these limitations. Future normative models should incorporate measurement error during the model estimation phase and when scoring the atypicality of new subjects.
