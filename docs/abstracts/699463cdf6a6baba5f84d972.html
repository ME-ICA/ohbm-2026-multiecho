Introduction

Intimate partner violence against women (IPVAW) is one of the most severe social and public health concerns worldwide. About 30% of women around the globe suffer from IPVAW at least once in their lifetime, and 26% specifically experience physical or sexual violence by their male partner or ex-partner1. Survivors of IPVAW are at increased risk of serious and widespread sequelae, including mental health problems, with post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) being one of the most common psychological disorders in this population 2. 

Meta-analytic resting-state functional connectivity studies point to large-scale network alterations associated with PTSD symptoms3, particularly within the triple network (TPN), which comprises the default mode network (DMN), the central executive network (CEN), and the salience network (SN)4. Nonetheless, existing literature has focused on military personnel or survivors of natural disasters, and much less is known about women survivors of IPVAW. Although recent work has begun to characterize the intrinsic neural architecture in IPVAW survivors, no study to date has examined resting-state effective connectivity (EC) within the TPN. As a result, key aspects of network dynamics in IPVAW-related PTSD —i.e., the directed influence that one neural region exerts on another and whether that influence is excitatory or inhibitory—remain largely unknown3. The present study aims to examine for the first time EC within the TPN in a sample of women who have suffered IPVAW and to assess whether EC patterns are modulated by PTSD symptoms. Given limited prior work, we adopt an exploratory approach and hypothesize that (1)  IPVAW survivors will show different EC patterns within the TPN compared to non-victims, and (2) that, within the IPVAW group, specific connections will be associated with PTSD symptoms.

Methods

Sixty-four women aged 18–63 years participated in the study. Half were survivors of IPVAW (WS-IPVAW; n = 32; mean age = 42.6 years, SD = 11.2), and the remaining 32 had not experienced IPVAW (Non-victims; mean age = 42.5 years, SD = 14.0). In the first session, participants completed a clinical interview and questionnaires assessing sociodemographic and mental health information, including ACE to assess Adverse Childhood Experiences, and the PCL-5 to measure PTSD symptomatology which encompasses four dimensions: PCL-5B for intrusion symptoms; PCL-5C for avoidance symptoms; PCL-5D for negative alterations in cognitions and mood, and PCL-5E for alterations in arousal and reactivity. In a second session, all participants underwent MRI scanning on a 3T Siemens scanner, consisting of a high-resolution T1-weighted structural scan and a 6-minute resting-state functional MRI echo-planar imaging sequence.

Preprocessing was performed using the CONN functional connectivity toolbox. Theoretical regions of interest (ROIs) for the TPN were selected based on a meta-analysis of resting-state connectivity in PTSD populations4. The selected regions for the DMN were the medial orbitofrontal cortex (medOFC), posterior cingulate cortex (PCC), precuneus (Prec), and bilateral angular gyri (rAG and lAG). For the SN, the ROIs included the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), and the bilateral anterior insulae (rAI and lAI). Finally, for the CEN, the nodes comprised the bilateral middle frontal gyri (rMFG and lMFG), and the bilateral inferior parietal lobes (rIPL and lIPL). Peaks for each ROI were identified using a spatial independent component analysis (ICA) conducted in the same sample and spherical ROIs with a radius of 6 mm were created in MNI space using the MarsBaR toolbox. Spectral Dynamic Causal Modelling5 was used to estimate the EC within the TPN. For each participant, a fully connected 12-node model was specified and model estimation was carried out using Bayesian Model Inversion using variational Laplace on cross-spectral densities.

Second-level analyses were conducted within the Parametric Empirical Bayes (PEB) framework. Five separate models were estimated: A first model was designed to examine differences in EC between WS-IPVAW and Non-victim participants, and four additional models were fitted exclusively within the WS-IPVAW group to examine the modulation of EC by PTSD symptoms (i.e., one model for each PTSD subscale). All models included education level, age, and ACE scores as control variables. Bayesian Model Reduction (BMR) was then applied to restrict the parameters and connectivity strengths to find the best model to explain the data. Only parameters with posterior probability ≥0.99 were considered. Finally, to test if specific EC could predict PTSD symptoms a leave one out cross validation (LOOCV) was performed and accuracy was assessed using a threshold of ≥ 95%. 

Results

What are the differences between the WS-IPVAW and non-victim group in EC?

Of the 41 connections that showed strong evidence for group differences, WS-IPVAW showed greater EC in 27 and reduced EC in 14 compared to Non-victims, with a predominance of inhibitory (26/41) over excitatory (15/41) connections (Figure 1). More concretely, WS-IPVAW group revealed increased within-network connectivity in the DMN, alongside reduced overall connectivity in the CEN, compared to non-victims. At the between-network level, connections from SN to CEN, DMN to CEN, and SN to DMN were notably stronger in the WS-IPVAW group. The PCC (DMN) and lMFG (CEN) emerged as the regions with the highest number of inputs and outputs, each contributing 12 connections.

How do PTSD symptoms modulate EC within the WS-IPVAW group?

Among the four PTSD dimensions, the avoidance symptoms (PCL-5C) exerted the strongest modulation effect on the TPN connectivity, followed by intrusion symptoms (PCL-5B) and to a lesser extent by reactivity and arousal symptoms (PCL-5D) and by negative alterations in cognition and mood (PCL-5E) (Figure 2). While different EC patterns can be observed for each subscale, four connections overlapped across the PTSD dimensions: from rAI (SN) to both the lAG (DMN) and the lMFG (CEN), and lIPL (CEN) to rIPL (CEN), as well as rMFG (CEN) to precuneus (DMN). Of these, three were systematically negatively associated with all subscales, while the connectivity rMFG (CEN) to Prec (DMN) was found positively associated with all subscales, except for the avoidance symptoms.

Which PTSD symptoms can be predicted by resting-state EC within the WS-IPVAW group?

LOOCV analysis showed that 6 connections showed effect sizes large enough to predict intrusion symptoms (PCL-5B). These were mostly positively associated and inhibitory, with within-DMN connections (lAG to PCC), within-CEN connections (lMFG to lIPL, lIPL to rIPL), and between-network connections from the SN to CEN (rAI to lMFG), and from DMN to CEN (PCC → lIPL).

Regarding avoidance symptoms (PCL-5C), the analysis showed that 17 connections revealed effect sizes strong enough to predict this subscale. These connections were evenly distributed between negative (9/17) and positive (8/17) associations with the subscale, and most were excitatory (10/17). The lIPL (CEN) stood out as the node with the highest number of outputs, projecting to DMN regions (PCC and rAG) and to one CEN region (rIPL). Additionally, the lMFG was connected with the bilateral insula, receiving input from the rAI and sending output to the lAI.

Concerning alterations in arousal and reactivity (PCL-5E), the LOOCV analysis identified 11 connections that predict these symptoms. Most of these connections were positively associated with the subscale and were predominantly inhibitory (7/11). The lMFG (CEN) emerged as a central hub, receiving input from the SN (ACC and rAI) and projecting back to the SN (bilateral AI). Notable connections were also observed from the SN to the DMN (i.e., rAI to PCC and ACC to medOFC) and between the DMN and CEN (PCC to lIPL and rIPL to precuneus).

Finally, three connections showed effect sizes large enough to predict scores on the negative alterations in cognition and mood (PCL-5D) subscale. Two of these were negatively associated with the subscale and inhibitory, sourcing from CEN to DMN (lMFG to Prec) and from SN targeting CEN (rAI to lMFG), while one connectivity was positively associated with the subscale and was excitatory, sourcing from SN to CEN (ACC to lMFG).

From all connections, the rAI (SN) to lMFG (CEN) connectivity was the only one that showed effect sizes large enough to predict all 4 subscales of the PCL-5.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that women who have survived IPVAW exhibit a distinct pattern of EC within the TPN. Survivors showed increased EC within the DMN and reduced EC within the CEN compared to non-victims, suggesting a disrupted balance between networks underlying internally oriented processing and executive control functions. Regarding PTSD symptoms, avoidance exerted the strongest influence on TPN connectivity, while the remaining PTSD dimensions showed more modest, but still observable effects. These findings suggest that avoidance symptoms capture a particularly pervasive form of intrinsic neural connectivity pattern within and between the DMN, SN and CEN, and may play a central role in the psychopathology and functional impairment associated with IPVAW-related PTSD. These results emphasize the need for symptom-specific approaches in future research in order to improve clinical interventions.
