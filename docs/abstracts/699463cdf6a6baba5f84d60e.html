Introduction

Working memory is a core component of executive function and undergoes prolonged development from childhood through adolescence.1 Early work emphasized age-related increases in activation within frontoparietal regions, whereas more recent evidence suggests that functional maturation is better characterized by network reorganization and increasing neural efficiency. Task-evoked brain activity during development has been shown to relate more strongly to individual differences in cognitive performance than to chronological age alone.2 However, it remains unclear how age and working-memory performance jointly shape developmental trajectories of network activation, and whether brain–behavior coupling itself changes across adolescence.

Methods

Participants were drawn from the Executive Function and Neurodevelopment in Youth (EFNY) cohort, a developmental neuroimaging database established by our group. The sample included 294 individuals aged 6 - 23 years who completed a digit N-back fMRI task. Working-memory load was manipulated using 0-back and 2-back conditions, and task-evoked activation was defined using the 2-back > 0-back contrast.

Behavioral performance was indexed by accuracy, reaction time, false alarm rate, and d’. Age-related trajectories of behavioral measures were characterized using generalized additive models (GAMs) with smooth functions of age, allowing for nonlinear developmental effects. Sex was included as a covariate.

For fMRI analyses, individual contrast maps were projected onto the Yeo-17 functional network atlas to derive mean task-evoked activation within each network. For each network, we fit a set of competing GAMs to test whether activation was best explained by age alone, working-memory performance (d’) alone, their additive effects, or a nonlinear age × performance interaction using tensor-product smooths. All models controlled for sex and head motion, and statistical significance was assessed using false discovery rate correction across networks.

Results

Behavioral performance exhibited pronounced nonlinear developmental trajectories. Under high working-memory load (2-back), accuracy and d’ increased steeply from childhood into adolescence, accompanied by reductions in reaction time and false alarm rate. In contrast, performance under the low-load condition (0-back) reached an earlier plateau, indicating load-dependent developmental improvement (Figure. 1A). At the group level, increased working-memory load elicited robust engagement of frontoparietal control and dorsal attention networks, alongside suppression of default mode networks, confirming recruitment of canonical executive systems during the N-back task (Figure. 1B).

Network-level modeling revealed dissociable contributions of age and working-memory performance to task-evoked activation. Age-only models identified significant age-related modulation in control and attention networks; however, when performance was included, age effects were selectively attenuated in some systems, particularly within default mode networks, suggesting shared variance between age and behavioral performance. Models including working-memory performance demonstrated that behavioral ability independently modulated activation in limbic and default mode subsystems after accounting for age, indicating performance-related network differences beyond chronological maturation. 

Critically, nonlinear age × performance interaction models revealed significant effects in dorsal attention, control, limbic, and default mode networks, indicating that the relationship between task-evoked network activation and working-memory performance itself changes across development (Figure. 1C). Higher performance in younger participants was associated with atypical engagement of non-executive networks, whereas in older adolescents and young adults, superior performance was characterized by stronger disengagement of default mode networks, consistent with increasing neural efficiency and functional specialization.

Conclusion

These findings suggest that working-memory development during adolescence is characterized by performance-dependent reorganization of large-scale brain networks, rather than simple age-related increases in task activation. By integrating network-level fMRI with nonlinear modeling, this work highlight dynamic changes in brain–behavior coupling as a key feature of cognitive maturation.
