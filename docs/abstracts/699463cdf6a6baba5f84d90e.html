Introduction

Schizophrenia-spectrum disorders (SSD) and bipolar disorder (BD) show substantial overlap across multiple clinical domains, including psychotic, cognitive, negative, and mood symptoms, that cut across traditional disease categories. This convergence, alongside marked heterogeneity within each diagnosis, challenges the long-standing Kraepelinian dichotomy1 that posits distinct clinical trajectories for SSD and BD. Contemporary evidence instead suggests a continuum of psychotic expression wherein shared and disorder-specific pathophysiological mechanisms unfold along overlapping dimensions.

SSD and BD are also characterized by brain-wide functional connectivity alterations that align with multiple, often overlapping symptom dimensions. Large-scale brain networks can be further described along principal axes of cortical organization using functional gradients, which reduce high-dimensional connectivity profiles to continuous axes capturing dominant modes of cortical variation across the cortex. Yet, how these gradient axes are reshaped across the psychosis spectrum, and the extent to which such reconfigurations track symptom dimensions and familial risk, remains insufficiently understood. Integrating two independent fMRI datasets, this investigation addresses these gaps by delineating macroscale cortical gradient reorganizations in SSD and BD and mapping them onto transdiagnostic clinical dimensions.

Methods

Resting-state fMRI data were obtained from two separate ongoing studies. Study I, included preprocessed and parcellated data derived from the publicly available UCLA Consortium for Neuropsychiatric Phenomics (UCLA-CNP) dataset (SSD = 37, BD = 40, HC = 110)1 and described in a prior publication2. Study II utilized the Bipolar–Schizophrenia Network on Intermediate Phenotypes (B-SNIP) dataset (SSD = 256, BD = 68, HC = 60)3 and was processed with Micapipe and parcellated using the Glasser atlas. Analyses in UCLA-CNP targeted categorical distinctions along the bipolar–schizophrenia continuum, whereas B-SNIP enabled transdiagnostic examinations and familial-risk extensions.

For each dataset, parcellated resting-state signals were used to compute functional connectivity matrices. Interregional similarity was quantified using cosine distance, producing whole-brain affinity matrices from which gradients were derived through diffusion map embedding4. This nonlinear technique embeds connectivity profiles into a low-dimensional manifold that preserves their intrinsic geometry, revealing the principal axes of cortical organization. The first three gradients, collectively explaining the majority of variance, were retained, aligned with healthy control templates via Procrustes rotation, and statistically compared across groups, controlling for demographic covariates. Group comparisons in UCLA-CNP were performed on mean gradient values averaged within the seven canonical Yeo networks. In B-SNIP, participant-level gradients were additionally related to symptom dimensions based on a five-factor Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) model encompassing positive, negative, cognitive disorganization, depression/anxiety, and hostility domains. 

Results

Across both cohorts, the first three gradients (G1–G3) recapitulated canonical cortical axes, with some cohort-specific variations (Figure 1A). In UCLA-CNP, they accounted for 49.9% of the total variance (19.4%, 18.3%, and 12.2% for G1–G3), while in B-SNIP, 50.1% (19.3%, 16.4%, and 14.3%, respectively).
In UCLA-CNP, the first two gradients together captured the principal axis of cortical organization, though their order was reversed relative to canonical results previously reported5. G1 and G2 contributed comparably to the explained variance, suggesting that this axis inversion likely reflects marginal differences in network weighting rather than a reorganization of cortical topology. No significant group differences emerged along G1. G2, spanning sensory to associative areas, revealed opposing shifts across its poles: higher values in the frontoparietal control network ( t = 3.1, pFDR < 0.05) and lower values in somatomotor regions (t = -2.5, pFDR < 0.05) among patients (Figure 1B). This bidirectional pattern indicates a compression of hierarchical differentiation, suggesting weaker segregation between sensory and higher-order networks. Moreover, Gradient 3 showed higher somatomotor values in patients (t = 4,1, pFDR < 0.05; Figure 1B), indicating stronger integration of task-positive signals within typically task-negative associative domains. Together, these alterations denote a selective flattening of cortical hierarchies, consistent with models positing disrupted bottom-up information flow as a core mechanism in psychosis.

Analyses in B-SNIP recapitulated these hierarchical axes and extended them to transdiagnostic dimensions (Figure 1C). Gradient 2 values averaged within the frontoparietal network correlated positively with depression/anxiety severity (ρ = 0.11, pFDR < 0.05; Figure 2B), implying that functional shifts toward anterior, segregated profiles accompany heightened affective dysregulation. Conversely, posterior displacement of default mode regions along Gradients 2 and 3 correlated negatively with cognitive disorganization (Gradient 2: ρ = −0.11, pFDR < 0.05; Gradient 3: ρ = −0.18, pFDR < 0.05; Figure 1D), indicating that DMN regression toward sensory-like profiles may undermine higher-order integrative processes. Notably, stronger Gradient 3 integration of the visual network was associated with positive symptom expression (ρ = 0.11, pFDR < 0.05, Figure 1D), suggesting an aberrant recruitment of task-positive sensory systems into task-negative associative circuits underlying perceptual distortions. No significant associations were observed along G1.

Conclusion

Together, these results suggested a shared axis of cortical hierarchical disruption across the psychosis spectrum, characterized by sensory-associative compression and aberrant somatomotor integration, with network-specific signatures aligning to symptom dimensions.

Schizophrenia spectrum disorders and bipolar disorder thus occupy proximal but nonidentical positions along a continuum of hierarchical reorganization, where common disruptions in information integration manifest as both shared and divergent clinical expressions. This framework reconciles categorical and dimensional perspectives by showing that psychosis reflects graded perturbations of the brain’s functional architecture rather than discrete network failures. Ongoing extensions incorporating first-degree relatives and large-scale ENIGMA harmonization5 aim to trace these gradient perturbations across genetic and clinical continua. By embedding symptom variation and heritable risk within the topology of functional hierarchies, this work advances a unifying model of transdiagnostic brain organization, elucidating how misalignment of cortical gradients may underpin the emergent complexity of psychotic disorders.
