Introduction

Introduction: Accurate electric‐field modelling for transcranial direct‐current stimulation (tDCS) relies on tissue segmentation and is especially sensitive to how cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) is labelled. Pipeline comparisons show that, even with the same subject and montage, predicted fields can differ depending on upstream modelling choices, with segmentation being a major contributor to within-subject variability.1 Prior work indicates that segmentation accuracy can measurably affect computed electric fields.2 In this study, we assessed how two CSF segmentation approaches influence simulated electric field strength and focality.

Methods

Methods: T1‐weighted MRIs from 47 healthy adults (age 26–69 years) were used. Head models were created with SimNIBS 4.1.0 using CHARM, a contrast-adaptive multi-tissue segmentation approach for individualized head modelling.3,4 As a second approach, T1-weighted images were also processed with a commercial volumetry pipeline to obtain GM/WM/CSF tissue maps.5 To generate a head model with an alternative CSF definition while keeping the rest of the workflow consistent, we created a modified label image in which the CHARM CSF mask was replaced by the commercial CSF mask, and the CHARM-based head-model construction was re-run (Fig 1). Electric fields were simulated with a 1 mA frontal montage (based on the international EEG 10–20 system: F3 anode, F4 cathode; 5×5 cm rectangular pads; 4 mm thickness) using the quasi-static FEM solver in SimNIBS.4 From cortical gray matter, we extracted the 99th percentile of |E| (E99) and the volume with |E| ≥ 75% of E99 (focality volume). CSF volume was computed from the CSF masks. Paired comparisons used Wilcoxon signed-rank tests; p-values and effect sizes (r) are reported.

Results

Results: The commercial segmentation produced larger CSF volume (Δ= 188.8 mL, p < 0.001). E99 changed slightly, from 0.236 ± 0.058 V/m (for CHARM) to 0.224 ± 0.041 V/m (for commercial), with a median paired difference Δ= -0.004 V/m; no significant change was observed (p=0.071, r=0.29). In contrast, focality volume was strongly affected: the volume of cortex experiencing ≥ 75 % of the peak decreased from 10.9 ± 2.3 cm³ to 6.43 ± 2.34 cm³ (median Δ= -4.41 cm³, p = 2.91×10⁻⁹) with a large effect size (r = 0.87). Results are shown in fig 2.

Conclusion

Conclusion: The segmentation with larger CSF volume produced a broader current spread and substantially lower focality volume, while peak field strength changed little. This suggests CSF labelling mainly affects how focused the stimulation appears and highlights the need to report and control segmentation choices when comparing tDCS simulations across pipelines.
