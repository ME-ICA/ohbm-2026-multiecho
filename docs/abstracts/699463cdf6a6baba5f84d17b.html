Introduction

Recent animal studies highlight how particulate matter pollution (PM2.5) affects the central nervous system. Due to their small size, they can enter via the olfactory bulb or enter the bloodstream through the alveoli, and have been found in human blood, CSF, and the fetal side of the placenta. Particulate matter pollution has been identified as a potential contributor to interindividual differences in cognitive and neural developmental trajectories. Two major limitations hamper our understanding 1) the need for large multitimepoint longitudinal neuroimaging data, and 2) an over-reliance on neighborhood PM2.5. These pollution measures are highly problematic, as they are heavily confounded with a myriad of other socioeconomic and individual-level characteristics. Here we break this link by focusing on wildfire-specific particulate matter pollution. This is only possible by merging two rich open-access resources with overlapping geographic and temporal characteristics: 1) daily 10km grid estimates of wildfire-specific PM2.5 pollution in the continental United States, with 2) a large longitudinal (4 waves over 22 sites) neuroimaging cohort (ABCD v6). Allowing us to develop perinatal and childhood-specific wildfire exposure measures to determine how prior exposure impacts cognitive and neural developmental trajectories over adolescence. 

Methods

To determine the impact of prior wildfire PM2.5 pollution on cognitive and neural levels (e.g., intercept, at 10 years of age) and the change (e.g., slope) over six years of development, we combined two open-access data sets; 1) high-resolution daily local-level PM2.5 wildfire smoke estimates (v 1.0; https://dataverse.harvard.edu/dataset.xhtml?persistentId=doi:10.7910/DVN/DJVMTV) 10 and 2) behavioral and neural data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD v 6.0; https://docs.abcdstudy.org/) consortium 11,12,16.

The ABCD consortium is an ongoing multi-center study that recruited 9 – 11-year-old children (N = 11,868) and is longitudinally following them in two-year increments for ten years. Our analysis focuses on the first six years with four equally 2-year spaced waves (ABCD 6.0). As the ABCD consortium consists of twins and siblings, we sampled the individual with the most follow-up attendance (if equal random sampling was used) this resulted in a sample of 9807 children.

IVs) Childhood Smoke Exposure (CSE) was defined as cumulative PM2.5 wildfire exposure from 3 months of age up until the first behavioral timepoint of ABCD (Figure 1a). This resulted in a CSE measure for all eligible children, bar one, whose date of birth fell outside of the range of smoke exposure measures. This limit is due to the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration Hazard Mapping System smoke plume data starting in 2006 10. We also constructed a perinatal smoke exposure (PSE) measure by summing cumulative wildfire exposure in the first 3 months of life and 9 months prior.

DVs) We examined the effect of smoke pollution on 6 cognitive and neural outcomes: cognitive performance, surface area, cortical thickness, subcortical volume, fractional anisotropy, and mean diffusivity. We take a global and regional approach, where we first test the effect of our two smoke exposure measures on whole brain measures before testing regionally (and FDR correcting). Design and neuroimaging procedures have been extensively outlined by the consortium (Garavan et al.; https://docs.abcdstudy.org/).

To study the effect of these two prior smoke exposure measures on adolescent trajectories, we use longitudinal mixed effects modeling with a random slope for our measure of time (i.e., age) and random intercept per subject nested within the ABCD site. We draw our conclusions using longitudinal mixed effects modeling, yet employ a strategy of robustness testing different modeling strategies to see if our results survive.

Results

We fit a series of mixed effects models using four time-point developmental data to test whether prior smoke exposure affects initial levels at 10 years of age (intercept) and the rate (i.e., slope) of development over adolescence (i.e., 10-16 years). We focus on how wildfire pollution impacts the rate of intraindividual change over adolescence (‘trajectories’; Figure 1d), as this is less likely to suffer from unobserved residual confounding. We find negative effects of childhood smoke exposure across all of our outcomes (except cortical thickness), and no effects from perinatal smoke exposure. 

Cognitive Performance: Using linear mixed effects modeling (Equation 1), we find a significant negative effect of PSE on cognitive performance at 10 years of age (B = -.0023, p = .014, β = -.02), yet no effect on change over adolescence (B = -0.0001, p = .088, β = -.00; Sup. Table *). In contrast, CSE has a significant effect on initial levels at 10 years of age (B = -0.00120, p = .023, β = -.05) and on the rate of change over adolescence (B = -0.0002, p < .001, β = -.01; Sup. Table *). Robustness testing, in the form of minor model reparameterizations (see Methods: Robustness Testing), confirmed the presence of an effect (all p’s < .05 & same sign direction) of CSE on cognitive trajectories over adolescence.

Cortical Gray Matter: Over this period of early adolescence (10–16 years of age), we find that each additional year results in -.01 mm (β = -.35) of cortical thinning, with a rather static trajectory for cortical area (B = 304 mm², β = -.07; Sup. Figure *). Examining how smoke pollution impacts these adolescent trajectories, we find PSE to have no effect (p > .05) on total surface area or mean cortical thickness (Sup. Table *). In contrast, we find a significant effect of CSE on total surface area development (B = -6.22 mm2, p < .001, β = -.04), yet no effect on the development of mean cortical thickness over adolescence (Sup. Table *). CSE’s significant negative effect on adolescent surface area was confirmed with robustness testing (all p’s < .05). Regional results broadly mirror our global findings, with no significant effects (pFDR > .05) across the board for PSE on trajectories of cortical thickness or surface area (Sup Table * & *). In contrast, CSE demonstrates significant surface area trajectory effects across 33 regions (pFDR < .05), predominantly in higher association areas. 

Subcortical Regions: During this period of early adolescence (10–16 years of age), we observe that each additional year results in an increase of 574.3 mm³ (β = .14) in total subcortical volume (Sup Figure *d). Additional perinatal smoke exposure (PSE) does not affect this increase over adolescence (p > .05), yet additional childhood smoke exposure (CSE) results in damping this increase over adolescence (B = -2.15 mm3, p < .001, β = -.04). Robustness testing confirmed the presence of a negative effect (all p’s < .05) for CSE on total subcortical volume trajectories. Testing each subcortical region separately mirrored the global pattern, with no subcortical areas surviving multiple comparison correction for PSE, yet consistent negative effects of CSE on the adolescent trajectories of 16 subcortical regions.

White Matter Diffusion: We examined perinatal and childhood smoke exposure on two white matter diffusion measures: average fractional anisotropy (FA) and average mean diffusivity (MD). On a whole-brain level, it was not possible to fit models including random slopes for age, despite high (~.8) correlations between the four timepoints, indicating that these measures do not show intra-individual change in our sample. When we removed individual random slopes, the models converged, and we found a similar pattern to most of our other neuroimaging measures: no effects from PSE, yet significant negative effects from CSE on average trajectories of both white matter measures. 

Conclusion

We find consistent negative effects from childhood smoke exposure (CSE) on cognitive and neural trajectories over adolescence, yet no effects from perinatal smoke exposure (PSE). Neither of these measures is correlated with an individual's socioeconomic status, helping us disentangle pollution from a myriad of other individual-level factors. Moreover, our focus on within-person change (i.e., trajectories) adds strength to the causal nature of our CSE findings, yet it also limits our conclusions on PSE. While we find no lasting effect of PSE on adolescent trajectories, this early-life smoke exposure has most likely already had an effect (captured by the intercept in our models). CSE's effects on adolescent trajectories are quite broad, affecting many neural modalities and regions, consistent with its pathway into the brain. Particulate matter pollution (both wildfire and residential) is entirely within our control - almost 95% of the world lives in areas over WHO guidelines - successful governmental policy can lessen the consequences of PM2.5 on the developmental trajectories of children.
