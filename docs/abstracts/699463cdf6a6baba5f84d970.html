Introduction

Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) maps brain activity noninvasively using blood-oxygenation-level–dependent (BOLD) signal. Resting state fMRI (rs-fMRI) reveals intrinsic functional connectivity from spontaneous low-frequency fluctuations, and most dynamic functional network connectivity (dFNC) work focused on gray matter (GM), where BOLD contrast is strong. White matter (WM), despite supporting long-range communication, has been treated as functionally silent because of  weaker signal and physiological basis is less established [1]. Accumulating evidence challenges this view. WM BOLD fluctuations are measurable, reproducible, and relate to activity in connected GM systems, typically with smaller amplitudes and longer latencies. Similar oxygen extraction and the metabolic demands of axonal support and myelin maintenance further motivate WM-specific connectivity analyses that complement GM-centric models of brain organization [2]. A practical barrier has been extracting WM networks with consistent inter-subject correspondence. Large-scale intrinsic connectivity network (ICN) templates are now available using template-guided spatially constrained ICA (scICA), producing individualized network maps and time courses while preserving network identity across participants. The NeuroMark framework supports reproducible estimation of WM ICNs and their interactions [3,4]. Most WM connectivity studies remain static, averaging interactions across the scan. In GM, connectivity reconfigures into recurring states, and metrics such as fractional occupancy and dwell time capture meaningful individual and disease variation. Whether WM exhibits similarly structured dynamics, and whether WM state expression differs between groups, remains unclear. Here, we apply NeuroMark tempate based scICA and sliding-window to compute recurring WM dFNC states and quantify fractional occupancy and dwell time to test for group differences in WM dynamical organization.

Methods

Resting-state fMRI data were obtained from the BSNIP2 cohort, comprising 590 participants (338 healthy controls and 252 patients with schizophrenia) recruited across six U.S. sites. Functional scans were acquired with gradient-echo EPI (TR = 1.5 s, TE = 28 ms; 30 slices; voxel size 3.4 × 3.4 × 5 mm; slice thickness 5 mm), and participants were instructed to fixate on a cross with eyes open and remain still during scanning. 

Preprocessing followed the NeuroMark/standard rs-fMRI workflow, using available preprocessed data when possible and performing additional steps as needed in FSL/SPM12. Data were realigned (INRIalign), slice-timing corrected, distortion corrected, spatially smoothed (6-mm FWHM), normalized to MNI space, and resampled to 3 × 3 × 3 mm isotropic voxels; the first 10 volumes were discarded. Quality control required at least 120 time points, mean framewise displacement < 0.25 mm, translation < 3 mm, and high-quality registration to an EPI template.

Subject-specific intrinsic connectivity networks (ICNs) were estimated using spatially constrained ICA (scICA) within the NeuroMark 2.0 framework, with spatial priors given by established GM ICN templates and 97 WM ICN templates.  For each subject, scICA produced WM ICN spatial maps and corresponding network time courses with inter-subject correspondence.  WM ICN time courses were post-processed prior to connectivity estimation using mean removal, linear detrending, despiking/outlier attenuation, and variance normalization; motion regressors were regressed out and temporal filtering was applied as appropriate.

dFNC was estimated using a tapered sliding-window approach, where windowed segments were extracted using a rectangular window of length 20 convolved with a Gaussian kernel (3 TRs) and shifted by 2 TRs to yield 169 windows per subject. 

Within each window, WM dFNC was computed using Pearson correlation, producing one WM connectivity matrix per window; matrices were vectorized (upper triangle) for clustering. Recurring WM dFNC states were identified by concatenating all window-wise feature vectors across subjects and applying k-means clustering with correlation distance (100 random initializations; best solution by minimum within-cluster distance). 

The number of states was set to K = 4 using the elbow criterion, and each subject’s windows were assigned to the nearest centroid to obtain an individual state sequence over time. From each subject’s state sequence, we computed fractional occupancy, mean dwell time, number of transitions, and transition probabilities. Group comparisons for dFNC temporal metrics   identify significant dynamic states at q < 0.05.

Clustering of windowed WM dFNC matrices identified four recurring connectivity states (K = 4). The four state centroids in Figure 1 exhibited distinct WM coupling configurations, indicating that WM network interactions during rest are not stationary and can be summarized by a small set of reproducible connectivity patterns. Visual inspection of the centroid matrices suggests that the states differ in overall coupling strength and in how connectivity is distributed across network blocks, consistent with temporally varying regimes of more integrated versus more segregated WM coordination.

We next computed state centroids separately within each group and examined their differences. Group-specific centroid maps Figure 1 preserved the same four-state structure, suggesting that both groups express a shared repertoire of WM dFNC configurations. However, the magnitude and spatial distribution of connectivity varied by group in a state-dependent manner.

Results

The group difference maps, computed as healthy controls (HC) minus  schizophrenia (SZ) for each state Figure 1 highlighted connectivity alterations that were not uniform across states. Differences were most prominent in States 1, 4, where SZ group showed stronger patterns. These results indicate that SZ group effects are expressed most strongly within specific dynamic configurations rather than as a single global shift.

 To quantify temporal expression of the states, we computed fractional occupancy (FO) and mean dwell time (MDT) for each subject and compared groups using two-sample t-tests (ttest2). FO differed significantly between groups in State(s) 1 and 4, with Group SZ showing higher FO relative to the comparison group. MDT also showed significant group differences in State(s) 1, 3,4, with Group SZ exhibiting longer MDT. Together, these findings indicate that group differences manifest not only in state-specific connectivity patterns but also in how frequently and how persistently individuals occupy specific WM connectivity configurations.

Conclusion

This work examined WM dynamic functional network connectivity using template-guided ICA-derived WM networks and clustering of windowed connectivity patterns. Three central findings emerged. First, WM connectivity could be summarized by four recurring dFNC states, supporting the idea that WM coupling during rest is temporally structured rather than adequately described by a single static connectivity matrix. Second, both groups expressed a common set of states, but connectivity strength and patterning differed between groups in a state-dependent manner, as shown by the group-specific centroids and centroid difference maps. Third, group differences were observed in temporal state metrics, with significant effects in FO and MDT for specific states, indicating altered dynamical organization of WM connectivity rather than a uniform shift across time.

The four-state solution suggests that WM interactions fluctuate between qualitatively distinct connectivity regimes. Although interpretation should remain cautious given the weaker WM BOLD signal and the evolving understanding of its physiological basis, the presence of stable and recurring state patterns is consistent with accumulating evidence that WM BOLD fluctuations can reflect coordinated activity linked to distributed neural processing. Importantly, the observation that group differences concentrate in particular states provides a natural explanation for why static WM connectivity effects can appear modest: averaging connectivity across the entire scan can obscure alterations that occur primarily during specific dynamic configurations.

The centroid difference maps further suggest that group effects depend on the brain’s momentary connectivity regime. If the most prominent group differences occur in a state characterized by relatively weaker or more segregated coupling, then increased occupancy or prolonged dwell time in that state may reflect reduced dynamic flexibility or reduced capacity to engage more integrated WM configurations. Conversely, if group differences are strongest in a state characterized by stronger or more globally integrated coupling, then increased occupancy or prolonged dwell time in that state may reflect hyper-coordination or compensatory engagement of WM pathways. In either case, the FO and MDT results are compelling because they translate complex time-varying connectivity into interpretable subject-level measures that may be useful as biomarkers and that directly complement the connectivity patterns captured by state centroids.

Several methodological considerations should temper conclusions and guide future work. Windowed dFNC results depend on the choice of window length, step size, and connectivity estimator, and these choices can influence both the number and the appearance of states. WM analyses may also be more sensitive to residual motion and physiological noise; therefore, demonstrating that group effects are not driven by motion differences, and ideally confirming findings with covariate-adjusted models (e.g., including age, sex, mean FD, and site), strengthens interpretability. Finally, when testing FO and MDT across multiple states, controlling the family-wise error rate or false discovery rate is important for robust inference; if correction was not applied, the current findings should be framed as prioritized targets for confirmatory analysis.

Overall, the results support a dynamic view of WM functional connectivity in which a small number of recurring WM connectivity states capture meaningful temporal structure, and group differences appear both in state-specific connectivity configurations and in state temporal expression. These findings motivate future studies that relate WM state metrics to clinical and cognitive variables, evaluate robustness across modeling choices, and integrate WM dFNC with gray-matter dynamics and structural connectivity to better understand the mechanistic basis of WM functional fluctuations.
