Introduction

When people are upset or stressed, they rarely regulate their emotions in isolation; instead, they turn to others for reassurance, advice, distraction, or simply a listening ear. This reliance on others to shape emotional experience is captured by the construct of interpersonal emotion regulation (IER), and intrinsic IER refers to cases where people engage with others primarily to regulate their own emotions. Although intrinsic IER is central to everyday life and mental health, most emotion regulation research has focused on intrapersonal strategies (e.g., reappraisal, distraction, suppression) without considering the social context in which regulation unfolds. It remains largely unknown how affective properties of a situation, such as emotional intensity, guide strategy selection when social support is available as a regulatory resource. 

To investigate the affective determinants of IER, we conducted a functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) experiment (n=39, mean age = 25.58 years, 26f) examining the impact of stimulus intensity (high/medium/low) on the selection and implementation of reappraisal, both with and without social support (intrapersonal/interpersonal). We hypothesized that intrapersonal reappraisal would be favored for low-intensity stimuli and social support would be preferred for high-intensity stimuli and that this will lead to differential activation within emotion-related networks, especially the dorsolateral and ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (PFC), ventromedial PFC, and temporo-parietal junction.

Methods

Three task conditions are implemented in the experiment. (1) In the Intrapersonal ER condition, participants are asked to reduce the intensity of their negative emotional experience using reappraisal: i.e., by distancing themselves from the image by becoming a detached observer, taking the perspective of a professional observer, or thinking that the depicted situation is not real. (2) In the Interpersonal ER condition, participants are asked to reduce the intensity of their negative emotional experience using the reappraisal statement provided by the experimenter. (3) In the Control condition, participants are instructed to look at the picture, respond naturally to the presented image and allow themselves to experience any emotional responses without trying to modify them.

We implemented the paradigm as an event-related design. Each trial starts with the intensity cue (Cue high, Cue medium, Cue low) (2s), indicating the intensity of the subsequent stimulus (cue phase). Based on the intensity, the participant can choose to reappraise alone (Intrapersonal ER) or with the experimenter's support (Interpersonal ER) in the regulation conditions (3s) (selection phase). In both conditions, a photo of the participant or the experimenter is presented to illustrate the interpersonal and intrapersonal conditions. A counter is displayed during the selection phase, showing participants how many times they can choose each condition within each run. The feedback counter is implemented to ensure that, across the experiment, each strategy condition is selected a comparable number of times, which is essential for stable estimation of condition-specific regressors in the multivariate fMRI analyses. Crucially, the counter does not constrain choices within each intensity level: participants remain free, for example, to choose intrapersonal regulation more often at low intensity and interpersonal regulation at high intensity. Thus, the counter safeguards the fMRI design against extreme overall imbalances, while preserving the intensity-dependent variation in choice that constitutes our primary behavioural outcome. After the selection phase, the instruction on how to regulate is presented (3s) (instruction phase). Subsequently, participants are asked to implement the selected strategy while an aversive image is presented (4s) (implementation phase). A rating of the current emotional state (4s) (continuous scale from “very negative” to “very positive”; -200 to +200) follows. At the end of the run, participants are asked to rate the overall efficacy of the social support provided by the experimenter (3s) (continuous scale from “not at all” to “very supportive”; -200 to +200). All events are separated by a jittered fixation cross (2-6 s).

The task consisted of 126 trials in total. Trials were randomized within each run. The experimental session consisted of six runs (60 minutes). Stimuli consist of aversive images from the Nencki Affective Picture System (NAPS) and the EmoPics. 

fMRI data were acquired using a 3.0 T Siemens Magnetom MRI scanner (Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a 64-channel head coil. High-resolution anatomical images were obtained using a 3D T1-weighted sequence (1 slab of 208 slices, voxel size = 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.8 mm³; matrix = 256 × 256). Functional images were collected using a T2*-weighted gradient-echo echo planar imaging (EPI) sequence, oriented parallel to the anterior–posterior commissure, with the following parameters: voxel size = 2.5 × 2.5 × 2.5 mm³, slice thickness = 2.5 mm, TE = 33 ms, TR = 1500 ms, flip angle = 70°, FoV = 200 × 200 mm², matrix = 64 × 64.

Results

Behaviorally, there were robust main effects of regulation condition (F(2) = 26.78, p < .001) and intensity (F(2) = 185.71, p < .001), as well as a significant regulation × intensity interaction (F(4) = 2.69, p = .03). Participants showed better downregulation for low- than for high-intensity images, and they chose interpersonal reappraisal more often for high- than for low-intensity stimuli (t = 2.16, p = .18), consistent with a tendency to seek social support under higher emotional load. 

At the neural level, high- versus low-intensity cues elicited greater activation in left ventrolateral prefrontal and right temporal cortex, suggesting enhanced anticipatory appraisal and social–affective processing. During the selection phase, both reappraisal conditions engaged a fronto-parietal and striatal network including bilateral ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (PFC), right dorsolateral and dorsomedial PFC, and supramarginal gyrus. Critically, contrasts between interpersonal and intrapersonal reappraisal revealed phase-specific differences (see Figure): during the instruction phase, interpersonal (vs. intrapersonal) reappraisal elicited stronger activity in lateral PFC, temporal, and ventromedial PFC regions, and during the implementation phase, interpersonal ER was associated with increased activation in lateral prefrontal, temporal, and parietal regions as well as the striatum.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine strategy choice in an interpersonal setting across all major phases of emotion regulation. The findings highlight how emotional intensity and social context jointly shape regulatory decisions and neural control systems, providing a mechanistic basis for leveraging interpersonal emotion regulation in prevention and intervention.
