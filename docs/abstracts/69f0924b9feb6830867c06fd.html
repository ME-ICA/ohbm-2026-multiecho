Introduction

Many neurological disorders are associated with characteristic patterns of structural brain change on MRI, affecting both regional volumes and cortical surface morphology.1 These patterns could provide significant diagnostic and prognostic information. However, most studies examine a limited subset of voxel- or surface-based features, typically within a single disorder, using modest sample sizes and heterogeneous analytic pipelines.2 As a result, the field still lacks a systematic, cross-disorder mapping of structural alterations into robust “morphological fingerprints” that generalise and reliably distinguish disorders from one another and from healthy variation.

Methodological constraints have contributed to this gap. Common tools, such as FreeSurfer, are slow, sensitive to pathology, require complex preprocessing and perform inconsistently on clinical scans.3,4 Multisite and multiscanner effects further inflate measurement noise in machine learning models, obscuring disease-related effects. 4 Establishing such fingerprints would provide a basis for cross-disorder mapping and support the development of precision neurology.

Realising the potential of morphological fingerprinting requires a unified fast, accurate, and scalable framework that can estimate and compare fingerprints across neurological and psychiatric disorders. Here, we introduce a deep-learning framework that derives a 13-feature fingerprint from a single native-space T1-weighted MRI in ~12 seconds. Applied to 116,087 scans spanning 18 disorders , it characterises disease-specific fingerprints across diagnostic families, quantifies the predictive value of individual and combined features for distinguishing diagnoses, and maps lifespan trajectories of morphology in health and disease. Together, the study addresses key methodological limitations, provides a large-scale assessment of structural fingerprints in brain disease and demonstrates their potential to transform research and clinical practice.

Methods

The framework for morphological fingerprint extraction is composed by two deep learning methods (Fig 1a). Volumetric features were obtained using LODBrain, which generates an 8-label segmentation mask (including an additional CSF label) from each T1-weighted scan. This 3D convolutional neural network operates at multiple resolutions, first learning a coarse, site-independent anatomical prior and then refining tissue boundaries at full resolution. It was trained on ~27,000 T1-weighted scans from ~160 acquisition sites, enabling robust inference directly on minimally pre-processed out-of-scanner images (Svanera et al., 2024). For each label, volumetric features were computed as voxel counts normalised by total intracranial volume.

Surface-based features were obtained using DeepThickness, a 3D U-Net trained on 1,049 T1-weighted volumes with 131 validation cases from mixed clinical and research cohorts and multiple scanners. Inputs were a 256³ T1-weighted image together with grey/white matter and segmentation probability maps generated by LODBrain. For each surface type (white matter and pial), the model outputs a level set and distance field. Cortical surfaces are reconstructed using marching cubes (pial level = 1.0, white-matter level = 0.25) followed by minimal mesh post-processing. Cortical thickness values are obtained by interpolating the distance field at each mesh vertex. End-to-end generation of a complete morphological fingerprint, including inference and surface post-processing, requires ~12 s per scan.

Utilising both models in a single framework facilitates the generation of eight volumetric and five surface-derived features per scan. Quantitative and qualitative benchmarking demonstrated improved cortical segmentation and surface reconstruction accuracy, robustness, and inference speed relative to FreeSurfer.

We applied this framework to 116,087 T1-weighted scans spanning 18 neurological and psychiatric diagnoses, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD), mild cognitive impairment (MCI), frontotemporal dementia (FTD), primary progressive aphasia (PPA), Parkinson’s disease (PD), progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP), corticobasal syndrome (CBS), motor neuron disease (MND), multiple sclerosis (MS), meningitis, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, major depressive disorder (MDD), autism spectrum disorder (ASD), traumatic brain injury (TBI), epilepsy, ADHD, stroke, and tumour. For multiclass and one-vs-rest classification, we primarily used ridge classifiers. We evaluated models trained on individual components of the fingerprint, on all volumetric features, on all surface features, and on the full combined fingerprint. All features were residualised for age, and sex was included as a covariate. For combined feature sets, we additionally evaluated random forest classifiers and report performance gains, where present.

For longitudinal analyses, we applied principal component analysis (PCA) to the entire fingerprint and modelled subject-specific trajectories over time. To differentiate stable from progressive MCI, longitudinal changes in PCA components were compared between groups and age/sex-matched healthy controls, aligned to years before conversion from MCI to AD (or matched follow-up in stable MCI). Normative age-dependent models (70–90 years) were estimated for healthy control, MCI, and AD groups, and PCA scores from example held-out subjects were compared to these distributions to identify model-based group transitions.

Finally, Cox proportional hazards models were fitted in the ADNI cohort (excluding 12 held-out subjects) comprising stable healthy controls, converters from healthy to MCI, stable MCI, and converters from healthy to MCI to AD. We modelled time to AD as a function of PCA-derived fingerprint scores and predicted 8-year AD risk trajectories for held-out subjects, which were compared with clinically determined conversion times.

Results

We applied the described framework (Fig 1a) to generate morphological fingerprints across 18 neurological and psychiatric diagnoses. For each diagnosis, we constructed a radar-profile fingerprint capturing deviation from age- and sex-matched healthy controls (HC), providing, to our knowledge, the most comprehensive set of disease-specific morphological signatures to date (see Fig 2a for eight examples). Each diagnosis exhibited a distinct morphological fingerprint, separable from healthy controls and from other diagnoses, even when clinical presentations overlapped.

Although many individual features carried a unique diagnostic signal, the composite fingerprint achieved the highest balanced accuracy (BA), supporting multi-class and one-vs-rest classification across all 18 diagnoses (Fig 2b,c). Morphological fingerprints reliably delineated disease families (Fig 2d) and differentiated healthy from non-healthy individuals (Fig 2e). In healthy ageing, many measures followed inverted-U curves peaking in early adulthood, whereas disease trajectories often remained close to healthy over substantial age ranges, indicating that robust separation requires integration across the full fingerprint.

To demonstrate clinical utility, we focused on Alzheimer’s disease (AD) in the ADNI cohort. Normative models of the morphological fingerprint features for HC, MCI and AD showed that individual scans could enter the AD range several years before diagnostic reclassification, indicating that deviations at the feature level can act as early warning signals of progression (see Fig 2f for whole fingerprint analysis). Fingerprint deviations from healthy norms differentiated progressive from stable MCI up to approximately 4.5 years before clinically confirmed conversion (see Fig 2g for whole fingerprint analysis). Proportional hazards models using fingerprints alone estimated 8-year risk of developing AD, with low risk in stable HC, modest non-progressive risk in stable MCI, transient increases in non-converting HC to MCI cases (see Fig 2h for whole fingerprint analysis). Notably, risk steadily increased in individuals who progressed through HC, MCI and AD, diverging from all other groups particularly around MCI diagnosis, despite the models having no access to diagnostic labels, indicating that MRI-derived morphology alone encodes information about future clinical course.

Beyond diagnostic categorisation, morphological fingerprints also captured variance in cognition by directly predicting MMSE scores (R²=0.33) and classifying cognitive impairment (BA=0.72) in ADNI. Functional status showed similar associations when predicting FAQ scores (R²=0.28) and functional impairment (BA=0.70). In the OASIS cohort, returning the top five disease probabilities per subject revealed comorbid patterns suggesting utility for detecting co-occurring disease burden and probing shared aetiology. In ADNI we also compared morphological fingerprints with other modalities. As expected, CDR and MMSE provided the strongest single-modality signal, but combined fingerprints outperformed blood biomarkers and APOE status and achieved diagnostic precision comparable to amyloid and tau PET.

Conclusion

Using a fast and accurate deep-learning framework, we derived morphological fingerprints spanning 18 neurological and psychiatric disorders from more than 100k brain MRI volumes. These fingerprints yield distinct disease-specific profiles, support accurate diagnosis, prognosis and comorbidity detection, and capture variance in cognition and function. The results address key methodological limitations of conventional methods and provide a practical route to integrating structural fingerprints into neuroscience research and precision medicine.
