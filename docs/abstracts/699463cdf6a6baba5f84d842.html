Introduction

Schizophrenia spectrum disorders (SSD) are chronic, debilitating conditions characterized by hallucinations, delusions, affective flattening, and prominent cognitive impairment. Cognitive dysfunction, which includes deficits in working memory, processing speed, reasoning, and attention, is a core feature of SSD that strongly predicts functional outcomes. However, antipsychotics offer only limited efficacy in treating cognitive deficits in SSD1. Therefore, understanding the neural substrates of these deficits may provide novel treatment targets.  While cortical abnormalities such as cortical thinning are well-established in SSD and correlate with poorer cognition, the underlying changes to the cortical microstructure remain poorly understood. 

Quantitative MR (qMR) techniques offer biologically interpretable metrics that can probe tissue microstructure, yet cortical relaxometry studies in SSD are limited. The goal of this study was to evaluate cortical microstructure in early SSD using quantitative transverse relaxation time (T2), a metric sensitive to the tissue microenvironment, including water, macromolecular, and iron content. We further tested whether changes in T2 were associated with cognitive function, and we additionally evaluated peripheral blood markers to contextualize our findings. We hypothesized that cortical T2 would be altered in SSD relative to healthy controls (HC) and would relate to cognitive functioning.

Methods

Fifty-five participants (29 SSD and 26 HC) aged 18-35 years old were included in this interim analysis. All subjects underwent a diagnostic interview, neurocognitive battery, and fasted bloodwork. Neurocognition was assessed using the MATRICS Consensus Cognitive Battery (MCCB). For brevity, analyses focus on the Mazes subtest, a measure of executive functioning, which showed the most robust group differences and associations with cortical T2. Bloodwork included a complete blood count (CBC) and C-reactive protein (CRP). The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) was calculated as an additional inflammatory marker. 

Imaging was performed on a 3T Siemens Prisma whole-body scanner using a 32-channel head coil. Multi-echo turbo spin echo (mTSE) images were acquired with TR = 11000 ms and TE = 25, 66, and 108 ms, and 0.8 mm isotropic T1-weighted (T1w) images were acquired for anatomical reference. mTSE images were skull stripped and Gibbs-ringing corrected using FSL. T2 maps were generated by voxel-wise mono-exponential fitting across TEs and thresholded at 300ms to avoid CSF contamination (Figure 1). Mean cortical thickness (CT) and T2 values of the frontal and temporal regions were extracted based on the Desikan-Killiany atlas produced by FreeSurfer recon-all on the T1w images. 

Between-group differences in mean T2 and CT (44 frontotemporal regions), standardized MCCB scores (z-scores), and peripheral markers were tested using independent t-tests. Pearson’s correlations were used to assess the relationship between T2 values and cognitive scores, and between T2 values and peripheral markers. For group differences in cognitive scores and blood markers, we used a significance threshold of p<0.05. For ROI-wise comparisons, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery rate (FDR) procedure, with q<0.05 considered significant.

Results

Imaging biomarkers. Mean T2 was significantly longer in SSD relative to controls in the left temporal pole, right entorhinal, and right fusiform (FDR-corrected; Cohen’s d = 0.86-0.90) (Figure 2A). The left parahippocampal and entorhinal cortices also showed nominally prolonged T2 (uncorrected p<0.01). No group differences in cortical thickness survived FDR correction. Only the right temporal pole was nominally thicker in controls (uncorrected p<0.01).

Cognitive testing. SSD patients scored worse on the MCCB mazes test than controls (p<0.001). Across all participants, longer T2 in 22 of the 44 frontotemporal regions was associated with worse performance on the Mazes test (FDR-corrected; Pearson’s r = -0.32 to -0.53). To determine whether this association was driven by group differences, we modeled Mazes z-scores using group and T2 as predictors. Group remained a significant predictor in all 44 regions (FDR-corrected), and T2 independently predicted poorer performance in 9 regions (FDR-corrected, model R2 = 0.44-0.50). Predicted versus observed Mazes scores from a linear regression model including T2 in the right caudal anterior cingulate and group as predictors are shown in Figure 2B. These regions included the bilateral caudal anterior cingulate and superior frontal gyri; the left temporal pole, caudal middle frontal gyrus, precentral gyrus, and banks of the superior temporal sulcus; and the right fusiform gyrus. The group by T2 interaction term was not significant in any region after FDR correction.

Peripheral markers. There were no group differences in peripheral markers, including NLR and CRP. Greater NLR was significantly associated with prolonged T2 in the bilateral frontal poles (FDR-corrected, Pearson’s r = 0.43-0.45).

Conclusion

Individuals with early SSD exhibited region-specific prolongation of cortical transverse relaxation with a predominance in temporal regions. While prolonged T2 has been reported in both whole grey and white matter in schizophrenia, region-specific cortical measurements have not been well characterized, particularly in early disease course. Iron deposition is unlikely to account for the observed effect sizes, as cortical iron concentrations are normally low, and iron accumulation would be expected to shorten T2. Demyelination could prolong T2, but myelin density in the cortex is modest, and the group differences effect sizes we observed (d ~ 0.9) exceed what would be expected from subtle myelin differences alone. Instead, prolonged T2 likely reflect increased extracellular water, a feature often linked to neuroinflammatory and related glia-mediated processes. Cortical T2 prolongation has similarly been described in disorders with established inflammation, such as multiple sclerosis and mild traumatic brain injury.2,3 Other mechanisms may also contribute to increased cortical water: reduced neurite or synaptic density, astrocytic swelling, oligodendrocytes abnormalities affecting axonal packing, and increased blood-brain barrier permeability. Notably, we did not observe cortical thinning, suggesting that microstructural changesmay precede or occur independently of gross morphometric alterations.

Inflammation has long been implicated in the pathophysiology of SSD, with prior free-water diffusion studies suggesting that neuroinflammatory changes may occur early in the disease course and contribute to cognitive deficits.4,5 We found that longer T2 was associated with poorer Mazes performance across groups, even after controlling for diagnostic status, indicating that T2 may provide information beyond case-control differences alone. T2 independently predicted MCCB Mazes performance in prefrontal cortex (PFC) regions, including the caudal anterior cingulate, superior frontal gyrus, and caudal middle frontal gyri, which are key areas supporting planning, goal-directed behavior, working memory, error detection, and impulse control. Several additional regions also showed independent associations with Mazes scores; these findings may reflect broader network-level microstructural alterations that co-vary with executive performance, although the most anatomically interpretable effects were in the PFC. Although we did not observe group differences in peripheral inflammatory markers, higher NLR correlated with prolonged T2 in frontal pole regions, suggesting that peripheral immune tone may relate to cortical microstructure even in the absence of group-level serum differences. However, larger samples are needed to clarify this relationship. 

Overall, our findings support qMR relaxometry as a sensitive, noninvasive tool for probing cortical microstructural alterations in SSD. Region-specific T2 prolongation in early disease course highlights cortical inflammation, or related processes that increase extracellular free water, as a candidate mechanism underlying both structural abnormalities and cognitive changes. More broadly, this work demonstrates the utility of T2 relaxometry for detecting subtle cortical alterations in neuropsychiatric populations and provides a framework for integrating imaging, cognitive, and peripheral biomarkers to better characterize early SSD pathophysiology.
