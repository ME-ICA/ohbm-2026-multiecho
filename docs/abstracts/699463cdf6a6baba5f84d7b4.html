Introduction

Cortical similarity networks have become a widely used framework for characterizing cortical organization. These networks are constructed by extracting anatomical features from structural MRI and modeling inter-regional similarity using diverse mathematical metrics, yielding a comprehensive profile of an individual’s brain network. The underlying basis is that cortical regions with similar microstructural properties or axonal connections are reflected in similar MRI features. Morphometric similarity networks (MSNs), in particular, have demonstrated robust biological relevance, capturing biologically meaningful patterns including cytoarchitecture and axonal connectivity1

However, existing approaches typically summarize cortical properties at the regional level using metrics averaged across the full cortical thickness, implicitly assuming intracortical homogeneity. Such an approach overlooks the intrinsically laminar and depth-dependent heterogeneity of the human cortex. Evidence from histology, transcriptomics, and quantitative MRI has shown that microstructural features, such as myelination, vary systematically across cortical depths and follow distinct developmental trajectories2.

We therefore hypothesized that modeling cortical similarity using multivariate, depth-resolved microstructural information would more effectively capture inter-regional relationships. To this end, we propose the Laminar Myelin Similarity Network (Laminar MySN), a novel framework that constructs cortical similarity based on multi-layer myelin joint distributions. Experiments on HCP-YA and HCP-D datasets show that Laminar MySN better captures macroscale cortical organization, improves individualized prediction, and is more sensitive to developmental changes. Overall, this framework provides a biologically grounded, layer-integrative approach for more comprehensive and effective analysis of cortical similarity and its alterations in brain development.

Methods

We used T1w and T2w structural 3T MRI data from the Human Connectome Project Young Adult (HCP-YA; 0.7 mm isotropic) and Human Connectome Project Development (HCP-D; 0.8 mm isotropic) cohorts. Only data that successfully passed all subsequent computational steps, including cortical layerification and surface registration, were retained, resulting in 1108 HCP-YA and 611 HCP-D participants for analysis

Cortical layerification. Cortical myelin was estimated using the ratio of T1-weighted to T2-weighted images (T1w/T2w), which reflects myelination1. Pial and white matter surfaces were reconstructed using FreeSurfer, and intermediate surfaces were generated at equidistant cortical depths of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 (depth 0–1 corresponding to pial-white matter surface), dividing the cortex into three laminar compartments, which define the superficial (depth 0.1–0.3), middle (depth 0.4–0.6), and deep (depth 0.7–0.9) layers. To avoid contamination from non-cortical voxels, the outermost pial and innermost white surfaces were not included. Considering the limitations of image resolution, no finer laminar subdivisions were attempted. (Figure 1)

Myelin Sampling and Network Construction. Vertex-wise myelin values were sampled within three layers, smoothed within each layer (FWHM = 2 mm), and registered to fsaverage space. For each cortical region (DK-308 parcellation), laminar myelin distribution profiles were measured. With reference to the MIND network3, inter-regional similarity was quantified using the multivariate symmetrized Kullback–Leibler divergence (i.e., Jeffrey’s divergence) between multi-layer joint myelin distributions, yielding individual Laminar Myelin Similarity Networks (Laminar MySN). Although previous studies constructing morphometric similarity networks incorporated features such as cortical thickness, surface area, or curvature, these properties are highly correlated across corresponding vertices in different layers. Therefore, we focused solely on myelin for laminar network construction. In addition, to assess the contribution of the added laminar information, we also constructed a conventional Non-laminar MySN by computing similarity between regions using myelin values aggregated across the entire cortical thickness. (Figure 1)

Experimental Details. We first compared myelination levels and distribution patterns across three layers using HCP data. Then for group level, Laminar and Non-laminar MySN were averaged across individuals to obtain group-level similarity matrices. Principal gradients were computed from these matrices to characterize large-scale cortical organizational patterns, and the primary gradient was compared with the canonical sensorimotor–association (S–A) axis pattern4 using Spearman rank correlation. The S–A axis reflects macroscale cortical organization, capturing developmental, microstructural, and transcriptomic patterns. This analysis was designed to test whether Laminar MySN can effectively capture the cortical organization pattern and provide more reliable biological interpretability

At the individual level, two analyses were conducted to evaluate individualized prediction and developmental sensitivity. Predictive performance was assessed in the HCP-YA cohort by predicting total cognitive composite scores (CogTotalComp) using ridge regression based on the upper triangular elements of individual similarity matrices. Age and gender were regressed out as covariates, and 10-fold cross-validation was employed to evaluate model performance. Given the close relationship between myelination and cortical development, we evaluated developmental sensitivity in the HCP-D cohort by computing the correspondence between non-adult similarity networks and an adult average reference network, and relating this measure to age after adjusting for gender, in order to assess whether the proposed method can reliably capture age-related changes.

Results

    Laminar myelin exhibited clear depth-dependent differences across the cortex, with deeper compartments showing higher T1w/T2w values and each of the three layers exhibiting distinct distributional profiles. These differences varied systematically across cortical regions, reflecting the laminar heterogeneity (Figure 2a). Gradient analysis demonstrated that the primary gradient of the Laminar MySN closely aligned with the S–A axis (Figure 2c, d), exhibiting a stronger Spearman rank correlation than the gradient derived from Non-laminar MySN (r = 0.55 vs 0.35). These findings indicate that incorporating laminar information enhances the capacity of similarity networks to capture canonical macroscale cortical organization

       In predicting individual cognitive scores in the HCP cohort, Laminar MySN, which incorporates multi-layer distribution information, outperformed Non-laminar MySN (r = 0.32, Figure 2e,f), highlighting the advantage of inter-layer interactions in capturing subtle individual behavioral differences beyond region-averaged measures. Developmental sensitivity was assessed using the HCP-D dataset. To avoid potential confounding effects arising from differences between datasets, we did not construct the adult reference network using HCP-YA data; instead, the reference average network was derived from 144 adult participants (age≥18 years) within the HCP-D cohort. The Spearman correlation between each minor participant’s (n = 467) similarity network and the adult reference network was computed as a measure of cortical myelination maturation. This metric showed a significant positive association with age (r = 0.344), indicating that Laminar MySN effectively captures age-related maturation trajectories (Figure 2g, h). Permutation testing confirmed that Laminar MySN exhibited significantly stronger developmental sensitivity than Non-laminar MySNs (pperm < 0.01), suggesting that laminar-resolved networks more sensitively reflect age-related differences through inter-layer interactions.

Conclusion

We proposed Laminar MySN, a novel cortical similarity network that extends existing region-level approaches by incorporating joint, depth-resolved information. This framework explicitly accounts for laminar heterogeneity and inter-layer relationships, providing a more detailed and biologically grounded representation of cortical similarity.

Our findings demonstrate that laminar-informed similarity networks more effectively capture macroscale cortical organization, enhance prediction of individual cognitive differences, and show greater sensitivity to developmental processes compared with conventional non-laminar methods. These results indicate that laminar microstructural organization provides unique, layer-specific information that cannot be captured by layer-averaged measures alone.

Looking ahead, this framework presents novel avenues for more precise laminar analyses, particularly when combined with higher-resolution imaging such as 7T MRI5, which could support finer cortical subdivision. Moreover, integrating laminar MySN with additional modalities—such as functional MRI or transcriptomic data—has the potential to further advance our understanding of layer-specific structure–function coupling and its role in brain development and neuropsychiatric disorders.
