Introduction

Within the predictive coding framework, pre-stimulus baseline activity can be interpreted as reflecting predictions generated by heuristic models, whereas post-stimulus activity corresponds to prediction errors arising from the comparison between predictions and actual sensory input.1 Accordingly, baseline brain activity provides valuable insights into predictive mechanisms underlying perception. Corroborating this idea, pre-stimulus baseline brain activity has been suggested to play an essential role in the anticipation and processing of upcoming stimuli.1 Previous studies have also linked pre-stimulus baseline activity to post-stimulus neural responses and to variability in behavioral reactions to identical stimuli.2 Additionally, predictive coding is proposed to be hierarchically organized, with predictions generated in higher-level brain areas being more abstract compared to lower-level, sensory-bound predictions.3 In multisensory regions, predictions should then be amodal, and as they propagate to lower areas, they will become modally specific, especially in sensory cortices.4 Hence, investigating modality transitions is particularly informative, as anticipating different sensory modalities should elicit distinguishable pre- and post-stimulus brain activity, allowing for causal inferences about modality-specific predictions and prediction errors. Here, we used a within-subject design comprising cue-target pairs, in which cues predicted the modality of target stimuli. The associations between cue-target pairs were systematically manipulated so that cues not only predict the modality but also the categories to which targets belong. We hypothesized that modality-specific induced anticipatory predictions result in distinct patterns of pre- and post-stimulus neural activity for target stimuli. 

Methods

Seventy-two healthy adult participants (aged 18 years or older, with no history of neurological disorders) took part in an EEG experiment. Participants were presented with cues that consisted of both auditory and visual information. The auditory part of the cues comprised sine-wave tones, while the visual part was a solid-color screen, with each color consistently paired with a specific sine-wave frequency. The associations between tones and colors were counterbalanced across participants. Target stimuli included either ecological images, drawn from standardized stimulus banks, or ecological sounds, which were created to ensure consistency across conditions. The experiment consisted of seven conditions: six experimental and one baseline. In the six experimental conditions, to avoid a deterministic relationship between cues and targets, cues predicted the target's modality with 90% accuracy. Furthermore, in the baseline condition, cues did not predict the target's modality (i.e., 50% visual, 50% auditory). The six experimental conditions (i.e., three predominantly visual and three predominantly auditory) varied in the predictability of the target stimuli's category (i.e., multiple categories, a single category, or a single token). Each condition consisted of 60 trials, resulting in a total of 420 trials. Figure 1 presents a schematic of the experimental design.

Each trial began with a fixation cross (500 ms), followed by a bimodal cue (500 ms), a second fixation period (1000 ms), and then the target stimulus (1500 ms). In a subset of trials, a question was presented following the stimulus to maintain participants’ attention. 

EEG data were recorded throughout the task, and pre-stimulus baseline activity as well as post-stimulus activity were analyzed using EEGNet, a convolutional neural network (CNN) architecture designed for EEG classification.5 The model was trained to determine whether neural activity preceding or following stimulus onset could distinguish among the different experimental conditions.

Results

The EEGNet model trained on the pre-stimulus brain activity achieved classification accuracy approximately twice that of chance (I.e., 14%), with over 30% accuracy in distinguishing between conditions, with per-condition accuracies ranging between 14% for the baseline (50% visual - 50% auditory) condition and 35% for the condition with the token visual item. On the other hand the model trained on the post-stimulus data achieved around 40% accuracy. Similarly the per-condition accuracies ranged between 14% for the baseline condition and 47% for the token visual condition. These results open the possibility of testing the post-stimulus trained machine learning model on pre-stimulus brain activity to examine whether pre-stimulus brain activity can be directly related to the signal of the following stimulus.

Conclusion

The predictive coding framework postulates that pre-stimulus baseline brain activity can be interpreted as a prediction signal. The current study provides initial evidence that anticipatory neural activity contains sufficient information to distinguish between different expected sensory modalities and stimulus structures. The above-chance classification of pre-stimulus EEG signals suggests that modality-specific predictions can be captured prior to the stimulus presentation at the level of scalp-recorded neural activity. Further tests to ensure that the pre-stimulus brain activity is related only to the condition rather than the cue will be carried out.
