Introduction

Building on the legacy of the Human Connectome Project (HCP), which established standards for high-quality neuroimaging acquisition and processing, the Connectomes Related to Human Disease (CRHD) initiative was launched in 2015 to extend the advancements to 14 specific brain disorders1. Among these, four CRHD studies focused on emotion-related disorders2, including Boston Adolescent Neuroimaging of Depression and Anxiety (HCP-BANDA), Mapping Connectomes for Disordered Emotional States (HCP-DES), Dimensional Connectomics of Anxious Misery (HCP-DAM), and Perturbation of the Treatment of Resistant Depression Connectome by Fast-Acting Therapies (HCP-MDD). The reliability of detecting robust univariate brain–behavior associations in small samples has been challenging3, and even multivariate approaches remain constrained by small effect sizes of brain–behavior associations. More severe cases of psychopathology exhibit higher reliability  of mental assessment, which is expected to enhance brain–behavior associations. However, in practice, there is a trade-off between sample size and symptom severity, making it challenging for any single study to achieve both a large sample size and enough patients at each level of severity.

To address these challenges, the HARMONY project has created  a harmonized large-scale mental health neuroimaging dataset by integrating the four emotional-disorder CRHD studies. The harmonization framework encompassed inclusive data representation, standardized neuroimaging pipelines with rigorous quality control, harmonization of behavioral measures, and open-science oriented data sharing4. In this work, we  describe and implement  this  harmonization framework and  demonstrate key neural correlates with psychopathology (depression and anhedonia) in the HARMONY dataset.

Methods

Data Representation

The HARMONY dataset (770 cases and 228 controls, total N=998 subjects, age range: 17-76 years) covers a broad spectrum of emotional disorders, from mild anxiety and depression to severe and treatment-resistant depression (Figure 1A).

Standardized HCP Pipeline

Building on the HCP preprocessing framework, we applied the latest HCP pipelines (Figure 1B) for structural (sMRI), resting-state functional (rfMRI), and diffusion MRI (dMRI). Neuroimaging quality control (QC) included signal-based metrics (T1w Contrast-to-Noise Ratio [CNR]; rfMRI temporal Signal-to-Noise Ratio [tSNR]; dMRI Signal-to-Noise Ratio [SNR] (b=0) and CNR (b=1500)) and motion-based metrics (rfMRI DVARS; dMRI percentage of outliers). Imaging data were summarized as imaging-derived phenotypes (IDP) across multiple spatial resolutions using well-established brain atlases as listed in Figure 1B.

Site/Batch Effect Correction

Although site adjustment is recommended5, prior work using the CovBat technique (which attempts to preserve variance related to psychopathology and demographics by including them as covariates) may be suboptimal in the presence of systematic site differences in severity, raising concerns that true site-specific disease effects may be eliminated. We therefore compared the latest ComBat-GAM model because it can be trained on controls only (and subsequently applied to patients) to be truly ‘blind’ to systematic site differences in severity (mean and variance adjustment).  ComBat-GAM was compared against CovBat-GAM by testing associations between harmonized IDPs and age, anhedonia, and depression severity using principal component regression (k=25). Model performance was quantified using adjusted R², and the overall p-value for each model was obtained from the F-statistic.

Behavioral Harmonization

Retrospective harmonization of behavioral measures is challenging due to differences in scale coding, inverse coding, differential summary score calculation and potential mid-study transitions. These issues were systematically addressed, and data availability across studies was summarized (Figure 1C). For Lifespan HARMONY (adolescents to adults, including all 4 CRHD), the primary psychopathology measure was the Snaith–Hamilton Pleasure Scale (SHAPS), whereas for Adult HARMONY (excluding HCP-BANDA), the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD) was used. These two scales  were examined using univariate FDR-corrected Pearson correlations with selected multimodal harmonized IDPs from sMRI, rfMRI, and dMRI to illustrate brain–behavior associations in our dataset.

Data Sharing

The HARMONY dataset will be publicly released via the NIMH Data Archive (NDA), including QC metrics, preprocessed imaging, pre- and post-harmonized IDPs, and post-harmonized behavioral data.

Results

QC profiles indicated overall good neuroimaging quality, with relatively small variation in sMRI and fMRI metrics across sites (Figure 2A). Specifically, HCP-DES showed lower signal-based metrics in dMRI, yet higher in sMRI and fMRI, which could be explained by  its different scanner vendor (GE compared to Siemens). Harmonization with CovBat-GAM and ComBat-GAM successfully reduced site-specific trends in the IDPs (Figure 2B).

For  age-associations, harmonized models improved statistical power compared with site-specific models, particularly for studies with restricted age ranges like HCP-DES and HCP-BANDA (Figure 2C). sMRI IDPs provided the best performance, with adjusted R² values of approximately 0.6–0.7 (p<0.001). For anhedonia, ComBat-GAM yielded the strongest and most consistent effects, whereas CovBat-GAM and site-specific models showed no significant findings. The strongest correlates of anhedonia were rfMRI functional connectivity and cortical thickness, with adjusted R² values around 0.4 (p<0.001).

Depression severity (HAMD) associations were weaker, consistent with prior results. Harmonized models based on functional IDPs achieved modest but statistically significant performance (adjusted R²≈0.1, p<0.001), while site-specific models showed inflated effects (Figure 2C). In site-specific results, only the HCP-MDD cohort reached statistical significance, consistent with its more severely affected sample.

Figure 2D illustrates key neural biomarkers of anhedonia and depression in the HARMONY dataset based on ComBat-GAM–harmonized IDPs, including cortical volume (HCP2016), partial functional connectivity (ICA25), and fractional anisotropy (JHU-81). Overall, depression severity showed robust associations with frontal and parietal cortical volumes as well as with both default-mode and subcortical connectivity. In contrast, diffusion metrics were not reliably associated with depression severity, whereas they showed robust associations with anhedonia.

Conclusion

HARMONY is  a large-scale mental health neuroimaging dataset with unique characteristics that facilitate data harmonization across sites  including inclusive data representation across emotional-disorder neuroimaging cohorts, the use of standardized state-of-the-art HCP pipelines with rigorous quality control, and the use  of ComBat-GAM for correcting site and scanner effects. The goal of HARMONY is to enable neuroscientists to investigate neural correlates of mental health in the large-scale cohort. HARMONY is also aimed for public release to promote open science. By integrating multiple CRHD studies, HARMONY overcomes the key limitation of restricted sample size while preserving clinically meaningful variation. The resulting dataset substantially increases statistical power for brain–behavior mapping and supports more reliable, generalizable inferences across levels of symptom severity.

The next phase of HARMONY will prioritize clinical data imputation using validated external populations and robust modeling approaches, enabling more complete and informative use of the existing datasets. In parallel, we will expand the spectrum of imaging-derived phenotypes to incorporate additional feature types across sMRI, rfMRI, and dMRI. Together, these efforts will enhance the data richness and utilization of the HARMONY dataset. Therefore, we particularly encourage future investigations to leverage this resource for neurobiologically informed mood-related research questions.
