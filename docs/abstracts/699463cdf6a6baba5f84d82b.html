Introduction

Ocular artifacts are a major source of structured noise in EEG recordings. The blink high-amplitude, low-frequency content with broad spectral leakage disrupt the estimation of covariance and cospectra matrices and alter the construction of spatial filters. Such perturbations compromise the identifiability of neural sources, making robust artifact correction essential for reliable EEG analysis. Despite substantial methodological progress, achieving automatic, stable removal of ocular artifacts while preserving neurophysiological activity of interest remains a central challenge in EEG signal processing, particularly in online settings such as in Brain-Computer Interfaces (BCIs) and neurofeedback.

Here, we evaluate a blind source separation framework based on Approximate Joint Diagonalization of Fourier Cospectra (AJDC) ^1. By jointly diagonalizing multi-frequency cospectra matrices, AJDC exploits second-order statistical structure to estimate demixing matrices theoretically more robust than variance-based approaches such as Independent Component Analysis (ICA). AJDC has the advantage of requiring only few seconds of calibration and remaining compatible with real time use. Our previous work showed that AJDC effectively attenuates blink-related components while preserving relevant spectral features, achieving performance comparable to ICA, a reference method ^2. Here, we wanted to assess further if AJDC can effectively suppress ocular artifacts (blinks) while preserving the temporal, spectral, and spatial structure of EEG signals, introducing minimal distortion and remaining compatible with real-time constraints. Thus, we propose a systematic validation of AJDC using an artificial contamination protocol in which controlled ocular artifacts are injected into clean EEG data. This enables direct comparison between reference and corrected signals, allowing quantitative assessment of temporal reconstruction accuracy, spectral fidelity, and spatial pattern stability.

Methods

We performed an offline analysis using three open-access EEG datasets derived from the unified eye-blink paradigm described in ^3, comprising systematically-blink-artifacted trials and blink-free, clean trials. The released data consisted of fully preprocessed and segmented 8-s epochs following the standardized pipeline of the original publication. From the 69 available participants, we retained 29 subjects based exclusively on two criteria: (i) visually confirmed clean EEG trials and (ii) a sufficient number (≥9) of usable epochs after exclusion of trials contaminated by noise, electrode instability, or residual artifacts. Electrode montages followed a standard 10–10 configuration, with the number of available EEG channels ranging from 58 to 64 across subjects. 

To obtain ground-truth signals with controlled artifact levels, we generated artificial ocular contamination by transferring blink activity from a donor participant to a distinct recipient. For each donor, a blind source separation decomposition was performed on blink-related epochs, and the component associated with ocular activity was identified visually. Its spatial weight vector was extracted and normalised to unit maximal amplitude, yielding a stable and physiologically interpretable blink topography. The donor’s vertical EOG (VEOG) time course served as the temporal reference, and the artifact pattern was reconstructed as the outer product of the ICA spatial weights and VEOG signal, producing a trial-wise channel-by-time representation of blink activity. Donor and recipient trials were matched in trial number across experimental blocks to ensure balanced contamination conditions.

Clean fixation epochs from the recipient were contaminated by adding this reconstructed artifact pattern after scaling it to match a series of nominal signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) levels (Figure 1) ^4. Scaling coefficients were computed analytically from the global power ratio between the recipient’s clean EEG (signal) and the donor’s blink EEG epochs (noise) and combined with predefined SNR targets ranging from −15 dB (strong contamination) to 0 dB (minimal contamination), in 1-dB increments. This procedure yielded paired clean and contaminated datasets with fully controlled temporal, spectral, and spatial artifact characteristics.

We evaluated two artifact-correction methods compatible with real-time implementation: AJDC and Artifact Subspace Reconstruction (ASR)^5. AJDC was calibrated on an initial 8-s blink-contaminated epoch to manually identify and remove the ocular component. ASR was calibrated on an initial 8-s clean epoch to define automatically the covariance of artifact-free EEG.

Correction quality was quantified by systematically comparing each corrected epoch to its clean counterpart, channel by channel and epoch by epoch, using only the 0.5–7.5 s segment of each trial, to avoid boundary effects induced by the correction methods, particularly ASR. Temporal fidelity was assessed using Pearson correlation, temporal root mean-square error (RMSE), and percentage change in variance. Spectral accuracy was evaluated using magnitude squared coherence, phase angle deviation, and spectral RMSE. Given that ocular artifacts predominantly affect low frequency activity, spectral analyses were conducted separately within EEG frequency bands, namely delta from 1 to 4 Hz, theta from 4 to 8 Hz, alpha from 8 to 13 Hz, beta from 13 to 30 Hz, and low gamma from 30 to 49 Hz. Spatial integrity was assessed using Representational Similarity Analysis (RSA) applied to scalp topographies, quantifying the preservation of multichannel spatial representations after correction. All metrics were averaged at the subject level after channel-wise and epoch-wise computation, and group-level results were obtained by averaging across subjects.

Results

Visual inspection of the contaminated epochs confirmed that the artificial blink injection behaved as intended. Across all subjects, artifact amplitude increased systematically as the imposed SNR decreased, yielding a clear and monotonic degradation of signal quality. The temporal waveform, spectral footprint and spatial distribution of the injected blinks were all visibly consistent with the expected properties of ocular activity, confirming that the contamination procedure produced controlled and realistic artefict levels.

All metrics were summarized as mean ± standard deviation across SNR levels. We first assessed AJDC-corrected signals relative to their clean reference. Across subjects, SNRs and scalp regions, AJDC showed consistently high reconstruction fidelity. Variance-change metrics, whose ideal value is 0, remained very small (−10.5 ± 2.5 % in frontal region; −0.1 ± 0.3 % in other regions), indicating effective blink removal with negligible distortion in regions outside the primarily affected frontal region. Temporal correlations approached the theoretical optimum of 1 (0.89 ± 0.02 in frontal region ; 0.98 ± 0.002 in other regions ), while temporal RMSE values remained near the ideal value of 0 (≈4×10⁻⁶ µV in frontal region; 2×10⁻⁶ µV in other regions ). Spectrally, AJDC preserved magnitude and phase structure, maintaining coherence values close to 1 (0.91–0.94), minimal phase-angle deviations near 0°, and low spectral RMSE (0.50–0.58 dB) (Figure 2). RSA similarity scores, which ideally approach 1, were high for AJDC in every frequency band (≈0.82–0.87), reflecting preserved spatial organization. AJDC also showed minimal variability across SNRs, and non-frontal regions reached near-ceiling reconstruction, indicating highly localized and minimally invasive correction even at extreme SNRs.

We then compared AJDC with ASR under identical conditions. ASR produced substantial and unstable variance inflation (+26.9 ± 39.0 % in frontal region; +23.6 ± 31.5 % in other regions), deviating markedly from the ideal value of 0. Temporal correlations were consistently lower than those obtained with AJDC (0.81 ± 0.05 in frontal region; 0.90 ± 0.01 in other regions), and temporal RMSE values were larger and more SNR-dependent. Spectral metrics revealed the strongest divergence in the delta band: coherence fell far from the ideal of 1 (0.64 ± 0.05), spectral RMSE increased sharply (1.86 ± 0.12 dB), and phase-angle deviations were markedly larger (−1.31° ± 0.28°). RSA similarity scores dropped dramatically in delta band (≈0.38), reflecting poor recovery of spatial patterns in blink-dominated frequencies. Outside delta band, ASR performance improved but remained less stable and consistently inferior to AJDC.

Conclusion

The artificial contamination procedure provided a controlled framework for evaluating artifact-correction performance against ground truth. Visual inspection confirmed that blink amplitude scaled monotonically with SNR, and the injected patterns reproduced the expected temporal and spatial characteristics of ocular artifacts. This controlled environment enabled quantitative assessment of signal reconstruction fidelity. Some limitations remain in that artificially injected blinks may not capture the full variability of naturally occurring ocular activity, including muscle co-activation or interactions with ongoing neural dynamics. 

When evaluated relative to its clean reference, AJDC demonstrated high reconstruction fidelity across all metrics, with values consistently close to their theoretical optima and stable across SNR levels. These results indicate that AJDC can effectively isolate blink-related activity while minimally altering non-frontal neural signals, supporting the robustness of this method based on second-order, frequency-resolved joint diagonalization for artifact suppression, which has the crucial to require only minimal calibration time and is therefore adapted to online use.

In contrast, ASR showed lower accuracy and higher variability across metrics, particularly in frontal and low-frequency components. The substantial variance inflation and degraded delta-band metrics suggest that ASR was less effective at removing blink artifacts under the present calibration constraints. Since ASR typically benefits from longer calibration periods, the short-segment calibration used here for methodological comparability may have contributed to its reduced stability. Thus, the comparison should be interpreted with caution, as it reflects both algorithmic differences and imposed calibration limitations.

Beyond blinks, the datasets used in this study also include horizontal and vertical saccade conditions. Applying the same contamination–correction–evaluation framework to these oculomotor events would allow assessment of whether AJDC generalizes to artifacts with distinct temporal profiles and spatial distributions. Such an extension would further clarify the range of ocular phenomena that AJDC can effectively suppress.

The present findings highlight the suitability of AJDC for real-time EEG cleaning. Its rapid calibration, stability across SNR levels, and minimal distortion of physiologically relevant information make it a strong candidate for integration into closed-loop systems such as neurofeedback (NF) and brain–computer interfaces (BCIs). Evaluating AJDC within operational BCI/NF pipelines and assessing its impact on learning and behavioral outcomes represent important directions for future work.
