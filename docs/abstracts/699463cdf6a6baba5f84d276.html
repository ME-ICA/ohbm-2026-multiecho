Introduction

Learning in uncertain environments requires determining whether deviations from expectation reflect noise or meaningful change. Bayesian accounts propose that surprise and confidence regulate belief updates, and both behavioral and neural data show signatures of these computations. Influential theories suggest that neuromodulatory systems such as dopamine, acetylcholine, norepinephrine, or serotonin implement these regulatory roles 1,2. We propose that these neuromodulators may also shape the cortical organization of learning signals: because their impact depends notably on receptors and transporters, their spatial topography may constrain where learning-related fMRI effects arise. We therefore tested whether cortical topographies of surprise and confidence are invariant across tasks, whether this invariance can be explained by PET-derived receptor and transporter density maps, and which specific receptors are most important to the relationship between receptor/transporter density and learning-related fMRI effects.

Methods

We analyzed fMRI data from four sequential learning tasks that varied in sensory modality (visual vs. auditory), generative structure (Bernoulli, Gaussian, or transition probabilities), and the quantity to be learned (probability vs. reward magnitude). For each observation, we derived core Bayesian quantities from an ideal-observer model: confidence as the log precision of the posterior distribution and surprise as the log improbability of the observation. These variables entered a general linear model (GLM) applied uniformly across studies, and the resulting coefficients in each voxel of the brain constitute the subject-level effect maps used in all subsequent analyses. As a prerequisite, we first assessed whether learning-related fMRI topographies were consistent across tasks by examining spatial overlap of significant clusters and correlations between effect maps across studies for confidence and surprise. We then tested whether neuromodulatory systems contribute to this invariance by predicting the effect-map topographies from 20 receptors3,4 and transporter density maps measured with PET, using leave-one-subject-out cross-validation. Model performance was compared against a null model generated via spatial “spin” permutation5 that preserves data distribution and spatial autocorrelation. Finally, we identified specific receptors and transporters associated with surprise and confidence using a dominance analysis at the subject level. This analysis partitions the model fit among the predictors, while accounting for dependence or collinearity between them and reflects the relative contribution of predictors in an interpretable way. 

Results

Confidence-related effects were highly consistent across studies, showing extensive overlap (clusters thresholded at p < 0.001 and corrected for multiple comparisons across voxels at the cluster level with pFWE < 0.05: Figure 1A) and strong correlations between effect maps (all p < 0.001). Surprise effects also exhibited robust cross-study overlap (Figure 1A) and significant correlations among the probability-learning tasks (studies 1–3: all p < 0.001). Receptor and transporter density distributions explained a significant proportion of variance in both confidence and surprise effect maps. For every study and variable combination, cross-validated models outperformed the null model (all p < 0.05; Figure 1B). To assess specificity, we applied the same approach to a publicly available fMRI language-network dataset, whose organization is thought to reflect factors other than neuromodulation. Here, receptor and transporter densities did not explain significantly more variance than the null model (Figure 1B), and the ratio of explained variance was significantly lower compared to most learning datasets (studies 1-3, all p < 0.05), suggesting that the observed relationship is specific to cognitive domains with strong neuromodulatory involvement. Across receptors, the 𝜇-opioid receptor (MOR) consistently contributed most strongly to the described relationship between receptor/transporter densities and cortical confidence effects (Figure 1C). For surprise-related effects in probability-learning tasks, the norepinephrine transporter (NET) made the largest contribution (Figure 1C).

Conclusion

We showed that distinct receptor and transporter density patterns underlie the topography of learning-related activity in the brain. Our results further demonstrate that linking fMRI effect maps to receptor/transporter topographies makes it possible to identify neuromodulatory pathways associated with specific computational variables, such as confidence and surprise. These findings provide a framework for probing the neuromodulatory basis of cognition and for developing receptor/transporter-level theories of cognitive function.
