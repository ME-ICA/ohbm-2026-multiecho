Introduction

Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) has been widely used to investigate brain functional connectivity at rest, providing key insights into the brain's intrinsic spatiotemporal organization. Among the various analytical approaches, Independent Component Analysis (ICA) has proven particularly effective in identifying large-scale functional brain networks, commonly referred to as resting-state networks (RSNs), based on shared spatial and temporal characteristics. These RSNs are biologically meaningful, reproducible across subjects, and closely associated with distinct cognitive and sensory functions [1].

Many studies have focused on metrics derived from the temporal dynamics of RSNs, such as network amplitude or functional connectivity between pairs of networks. In contrast, the spatial properties of large-scale network maps derived from ICA, which contain distributed regions that are functionally connected, have been less consistently explored. A more detailed characterization of RSN spatial organization may provide complementary and potentially crucial information for advancing our understanding of functional brain architecture. 

In this work we focus on the spatial characteristics of functional networks obtained from ICA, with a particular emphasis on network morphology and spatial segregation to investigate connectivity within RSN subnetworks. We use metrics as the shape of networks and their subparts. We applied this framework to a cohort of individuals in the preclinical stage of Alzheimer's disease, using data from the A4 and LEARN clinical trial [2].

Methods

The data used for this work was the rs-fMRI acquisitions from the A4 and LEARN studies cohorts of preclinical Alzheimer’s disease [2]. Both cohorts comprise cognitively unimpaired individuals, with A4 individuals having lightly elevated amyloid as determined from the Florbetapir PET images. After checking for uniformity in the fMRI data (TR=3 s, 200 volumes) and quality control, 1018 were kept. Of those, 698 were from the A4 cohort (444 female, 71.4 ± 4.4 years, centiloid 66 ± 33) and 320 from the LEARN cohort (218 female, 70.1 ± 4 years, centiloid 5 ± 12). 

All images were preprocessed with an in-house developed pipeline which includes brain extraction, time slicing correction, motion correction, normalization to a MNI152 space, temporal filtering with a highpass filter at 0.01 Hz, nuisance regression, normalization of the time series to unit variance. The pipeline was implemented in python using tools from AFNI, HD-BET, FSL, ANTS and nilearn.

The processed rs-fMRI images were decomposed into statistical independent spatial patterns using MELODIC-ICA in FSL. To manage computational load, ICA was carried out using 300 subjects, combining 150 from each cohort randomly selected. The number of components was set to 30 to obtain large-scale networks. From these components 22 were identified as functional RSNs by visual inspection and automatic matching with own templates. The obtained components were normalized voxel-wise and component-wise using z-score.

To further study the spatial distribution of RSN, we aimed to identify subnetworks as separated regions belonging to the same RSN. For that, we applied a threshold of z=2.3 and a binarization obtaining one mask per network followed by a labelling strategy (from scikit-image). We kept, as subnetworks, regions with volumes corresponding to at least the 3% of the whole RSN and excluding the rest of spurious regions or noise. 

We then performed dual dual regression to derive subject-specific temporal and spatial RSN contributions. The group-level spatial ICA maps were regressed into each subject data for the whole cohort. Using the masks for each network or subnetwork we computed the shape, as the average Z-value in the area of the mask [3], for the individual spatial maps from the stage 2 of the dual regression for each network and for each subnetwork.

To evaluate the subject specific temporal contribution for the segregated subnetworks we carried out a second dual regression considering the mask of each subnetwork separately. With this individual temporal series, we computed the functional connectivity matrix which is a representation of intra-network and inter-network connectivity. 

We used amplitude, shape and correlation matrices to perform group-statistics. We primarily tested differences between the two study groups as well as gender differences using independent t-student tests. Correction for multiple comparisons was carried out with false discovery rate (FDR) and significance threshold was set at corrected p-value of 0.05.

Results

We first performed an accurate descriptive analysis of the RSNs and how they segregate into spatially distributed subnetworks. From the ICA 22 components were identified as RSNs, corresponding to 5 major networks or systems: the Default Mode Network (DMN, 5 components), the Executive Network (EN, 9 components), the Visual networks (VIS, 4 components), the Sensorimotor network (SM, 2 components), the Salience network (SN, 1 component) and the Subcortical network (SC, 1 component). When studying subnetworks as segregated regions within a component, we observed that while some RSNs were highly distributed across brain areas, others were localized into a single region. We identified the following subnetworks for each RSN: DMN1: 6, DMN2: 5, DMN3: 2, DMN4: 6, DMN5: 5, EN1: 5, EN2: 5, EN3: 7, EN4: 6, EN5: 5, EN6: 5, EN7: 1, EN8: 3, EN9: 5, SC1: 3, SM1: 5, SM2: 5, SN1: 3, VIS1: 2, VIS2: 1, VIS3: 1 and VIS4: 1. In total 87 subnetworks identified from 22 networks.

When studying network shape, both for the whole RSNs and for the subnetworks, we observe that there are systematic differences between them. With one of the subnetworks being always of larger magnitude than the overall RSN or the other subnetworks. As the shape is a measure of network integrity, this result might indicate that some RSN parts are more consistently integrated across subjects, while others may carry inter-subject variability, resulting in lower group average shape. 

To further investigate this inter-subject variability, we analyzed differences in the shape of the RSNs and their networks across clinical groups and genders. We found differences in 19 out 22 RSNs that were also observed when looking at the subnetworks, with females showing systematically higher shape than males (corrected p<0.05). In the 3 RSNs (DMN5, EN6 and SM1) with no gender differences, these appeared at some of the subnetworks. When studying clinical groups, we found differences between in 20 RSNs, with subjects showing elevated amyloid (the A4 group) showing smaller shape values. Again, in the networks with no significance at the large-scale level (DMN5 and SC1), there were differences in some subnetworks (Figure 2A).

We then analyzed the connectivity matrix obtained from the subnetworks’ timeseries, as a joint measure of intra-network and inter-network connectivity. As observed from the average FC matrix, RSNs are organized as squared blocks in the diagonal, while the off-diagonal elements capture inter-network connectivity (Figure 2B). We found widespread differences between male and female, distributed within and between networks, with large effects involving the connectivity between DMN and EN. When considering the differences between A4 and LEARN groups there were only significant differences in 5 subnetworks. 

Conclusion

In this work we presented a methodology to analyze the segregation of the functional networks obtained from ICA on resting-state fMRI data. We focused on the evaluation of spatial characteristic of the RSNs and their subnetworks, evaluating their shape, as a metric of signal integrity within the network, and their independent contribution to the functional connectivity matrix. 

We have applied this methodology to evaluate a cohort of preclinical AD subjects from the A4 and LEARN study, and we have observed how the subnetworks shapes were different between males and females and between A4 and LEARN groups, which differ mainly in amyloid level. Our findings suggest that the the spatial integrity of the RSNs and their subnetworks carries important information to model inter-subject variability and is related to clinical and biological characteristics. 

We also obtained the connectivity matrices considering the segregated subnetworks. These matrices may serve as representations to study the overall hierarchical organization of functional brain architecture, as seen when looking at their morphometric distribution. Importantly, we found large significant differences in the connectivity between males and females. However, sparse differences emerged in the comparison between A4 and LEARN groups, suggesting that the effects of gender in whole-brain connectivity are much stronger than the ones caused by early amyloid decomposition. Notably, both groups are equivalent in terms of cognitive status, supporting a direct link between FC and cognition.

It is noteworthy that our study tests differences across genders and clinical groups, as a proof-of-concept of our implemented methodology. Further analysis should characterize the sample, considering other variables such as age and cognitive status, to provide a full picture of changes in brain functional organization in early AD. 

Overall, we conclude that the study of spatial information from ICA provides information of the spatial segregation of the functional networks, improving the full understanding of functional connectivity patterns in rs-fMRI. The shape results encourage the implementation of more spatial metrics in the analysis of functional connectivity.
