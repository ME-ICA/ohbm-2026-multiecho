Introduction

The hand is the primary interface between the body and the environment. Through its movements, humans can reach, grasp, lift, and manipulate objects by continuously integrating visual, somatosensory and motor information. According to recent neuroscientific models, the biomechanics of habitual actions shape highly probable state distributions that become internalized and stably represented within the brain’s spontaneous activity. In this view, intrinsic neural activity maintains internal models of common movements that constrain and facilitate the processing of incoming sensorimotor signals. Consistent with this framework, intrinsic brain activity preferentially codes ecological manual behaviors that are frequently performed in daily life1,2.

How the brain adapts these internal models provides critical insight into how content-specific representations emerge and are updated. A recent fMRI study has shown that the observation of uncommon hand movements elicits stronger activation in the precentral gyrus and superior parietal lobule compared to common actions, suggesting increased recruitment of sensorimotor and attentional resources when internal models are challenged2. Similarly, electrophysiological studies have shown that action execution and observation are accompanied by event-related desynchronization (ERD) of sensorimotor and parietal rhythms.

Within this framework, the adaptation of internal motor models must remain stable enough to preserve behaviorally relevant action priors, yet sufficiently flexible to accommodate novel kinematic patterns. Despite the appeal of this hypothesis, direct evidence on the spectro-temporal dynamics supporting such adaptive processes is still lacking. The present study aims to characterize frequency-specific cortical oscillatory dynamics during the observation of reach-to-grasp actions, contrasting common everyday movements with uncommon actions characterized by perturbed hand kinematics.

Methods

Experimental procedure 

The study was conducted on twenty right-handed healthy participants (10 females; mean age ± standard deviation = 27.1 ± 3.5 years). In two separate recording sessions, each participant underwent high-density electroencephalography (Hd-EEG) and fMRI recordings, in counterbalanced order. The experimental protocol was approved by the local ethics committee. 

Following a previous study conducted on an independent sample2, we defined two observational conditions: ecological (Common - C) and unusual (Uncommon - U) reach-to-grasp movements. In the ecological condition, participants observed movements with a regular kinematic pattern of the upper limb; in the unusual condition, movements featured perturbed kinematics (Fig. 1A). Each video began with a static prone hand position. The hand then grasped an object (a cup), turned back, and placed the cup. The videos were presented in 24 mini-blocks - 12 C and 12 U - interspersed with a 1500 ± 250 ms fixation. Series of five consecutive C or U movements were counterbalanced across participants. The task session was divided into three blocks (Fig. 2B). All videos were presented from a first-person perspective and lasted 3 s. To minimize habituation effects, the videos included variations as described in2. 

Hd-EEG acquisition and pre-processing

Hd-EEG was recorded using 256 recording channels sampled at 2048 Hz with 0.1–40 Hz bandpass filtering and subsequently downsampled to 256 Hz. Electrode positions were digitized for each subject using a 3D scanner. Structural T1-weighted MRI scans were acquired for source-level analysis. Preprocessing was performed using a customized adaptation of the megconnectome pipeline v3.0. 

Time-frequency analysis

Source reconstruction was performed as in3. Source-projected independent components were linearly combined, weighted by vertex-wise power estimates, to reconstruct cortical time series for each parcel defined in the Schaefer atlas n = 100 (Fig. 2C). Time–frequency representations (TFRs) were estimated for each subject, trial, and parcel using a Short-Term Fast Fourier Transform with a sliding Hanning window (500 ms). Analyses were performed over a temporal window spanning from −0.5 to 3 s relative to stimulus onset (T=0), corresponding to the onset of the video clip (Fig. 2D). Event-related desynchronization/synchronization (ERD/ERS) was quantified as the percentage change in spectral power relative to the pre-stimulus baseline [−0.5, 0] s:

ERD/ERS j (ν, t) = 100 · ( p j (ν, t) − base j (ν) ) / base j (ν)

where ν denotes frequency, p j (ν, t) the spectral power at vertex j, and base j the mean pre-stimulus power. TFRs were computed per trial and averaged within condition for each block. 

Statistical Analysis

Statistical comparisons were conducted during the 3-s video observation period, relative to stimulus onset and restricted to: mu rhythm (μ, 8–12 Hz), sensorimotor rhythm (SMR, 12–14 Hz), beta-low (β-low, 15–20 Hz), and beta-high (β-high, 20–26 Hz). Statistical analyses were performed at the parcel level, separately for each frequency band, using a within-subjects design based on dependent-samples t-statistics. Non-parametric cluster-based permutation tests were employed to control for multiple comparisons across time and frequency dimensions4. For each parcel, statistical significance was evaluated using Monte Carlo permutation testing (5,000 randomizations), with cluster-based correction applied across the time–frequency plane (cluster-forming threshold p < 0.05). A two-tailed test was used, and statistical significance was assessed at a cluster-level alpha of 0.05. Significant effects were visualized as time–frequency statistical maps (t-values). Finally, within the Common condition, grasp-related effects (Whole hand vs Pinch), were assessed through data averaged across blocks.

Results

Data were analyzed separately for each experimental block to assess potential adaptation effects related to repeated stimulus exposure. In the first block, cluster-level statistics revealed frequency- and network-specific effects lateralized to the right hemisphere. In the mu band, a significant cluster (Common ≠ Uncommon) was observed in one parcel of the somatomotor network (SMN) between 1.5 and 2.0 s after stimulus onset (p=0.04). In the SMR, significant clusters emerged in three parcels within the Dorsal Attention Network (DAN) and four SMN parcels (Fig.2A), between 1.6 and 2.5 s post-stimulus. Notably, these effects spatially overlapped with those identified by the fMRI GLM contrast, on the same subjects (shown in cyan). Among them, the SMN parcel showed the strongest statistical effect (p=0.004) and was selected for representative time–frequency visualization (Fig.2B,C). Remarkably, this SMN parcel is the one selectively associated with hand representation. No significant condition effect was found in the β-low band. In contrast, in the β-high band a significant cluster was identified in a DAN parcel located in the superior frontal gyrus, overlapping with one of the SMR-sensitive regions, between 1.4 and 1.9 s (p=0.01). 

In the second block, a significant condition effect was observed in the mu rhythm, with a cluster detected in a single SMN parcel between 0.5 and 1.5 s after stimulus onset. In addition, DAN involvement emerged at later latencies, with a significant cluster located in the superior frontal gyrus between 1.5 and 2.5 s (p=0.04). No significant clusters were observed in the other frequency bands. 

In the third block, no significant condition-related differences were detected in any parcel or frequency band. 

Qualitative inspection of time-frequency representations indicated that activity in mu and β-high bands exhibited more negative ERD values (i.e., reduced spectral power) during the observation of Uncommon compared to Common movements; conversely, the SMR showed relatively stronger ERS values (i.e., increased spectral power) during the observation of Common action. 

Overall, these results indicate that the observation of uncommon hand movements elicits stronger engagement of sensorimotor and attentional networks, reflected by enhanced cortical desynchronization. These condition-specific effects progressively attenuated across repeated stimulus presentations and were accompanied by a parallel increment of power synchronization at higher frequencies. 

Then, we tested whether oscillatory dynamics within the Common condition were modulated by grasp precision. Trials were subdivided according to grasp type (Whole hand vs Pinch) and averaged across experimental blocks. Qualitative inspection of ERD/ERS maps computed at the vertex-wise level for the four Common grasping conditions (right/left effector × whole-hand/pinch grasp) revealed a consistent and orderly modulation of oscillatory activity during the return phase in the right hemisphere (Fig. 2D). In this task phase, when we extracted the EEG power time courses from the representative SMN parcel by averaging time–frequency results across trials and experimental blocks, we observed an increase in ERS magnitude with the increasing grasping difficulty. Overall, these effects suggest a gradual modulation of SMR as the grasp precision demands increase (Fig. 2 C,D,E). 

Subsequently, based on the results of the primary analysis, statistical testing was restricted to parcels within the DAN and the SMN. Cluster-level statistics revealed significant clusters (Whole hand ≠ Pinch) in the SMR within five DAN parcels (Fig. 2F), two of which were bilateral, between 1.2 and 2.2 s. Significant clusters reflected more negative ERD values in the SMR during the observation of Whole-hand grasps compared with Pinch actions.

Conclusion

Our findings provide spectro-temporal evidence that the observation of uncommon hand movements induces a stronger and more selective engagement of cortical oscillatory dynamics compared to common actions. In particular, uncommon movements elicit enhanced desynchronization in the mu rhythm and in the β-high band within the SMN and DAN, while common movements were accompanied by SMR synchronization. This frequency-specific pattern highlights a functional dissociation between oscillatory rhythms supporting active model updating and those reflecting well-established sensorimotor representations. Interestingly, in the first block, the effects were temporally aligned to hand-object contact and, in the second block, preceded this movement phase. 

Crucially, these effects were most prominent during the first block and progressively attenuated across repeated stimulus exposures, suggesting a gradual adaptation process. This temporal evolution supports the idea that internal motor models, initially mismatched by uncommon kinematics, are progressively updated through repeated observation, leading to a reduction in cortical demand. 

At the network level, the involvement of parcels within the SMN and the DAN suggests a joint contribution of sensorimotor and attentional systems to the updating of internal action models. Remarkably, the spatial distribution of significant effects partially overlapped with regions identified using fMRI in the same participants, including the precentral gyrus and superior parietal lobule2. This cross-modal convergence reinforces the functional relevance of these regions in processing action (un)familiarity and supports the use of functional parcellation schemes to integrate electrophysiological findings. Notably, our effects are unlikely to reflect the detection of biomechanically impossible or erroneous actions. Unlike previous studies on biologically implausible movements5, the observed actions here were biomechanically possible and goal-complete. The absence of effects in regions and frequencies associated with error detection or attentional reorienting further supports the interpretation that uncommon movements primarily engage adaptive updating of internal motor models.

Finally, within the Common condition, oscillatory dynamics showed a spatially and temporally specific modulation related to grasp type. SMR activity within the hand-related sensorimotor region exhibited a graded modulation during the return phase, with differences between whole-hand and precision grips. Specifically, SMR synchronization increased progressively with grasp difficulty, from dominant-hand whole-hand grasps to non-dominant-hand precision grips. This effect was further supported by cluster-level statistics revealing significant SMR differences between grasp types within parietal regions of the DAN. Together, these findings indicate that even highly familiar actions engage precision-dependent oscillatory mechanisms, selectively expressed in both their spectro-temporal dynamics and spatial localization. 

Overall, our results suggest that cortical oscillations provide a flexible neural substrate supporting stable action priors while remaining capable of adapting internal motor models as experience progressively challenges them during action observation.
