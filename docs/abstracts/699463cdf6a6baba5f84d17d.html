Introduction

Social cognition (SoC) is a set of cognitive abilities that enable us to interact with others, share emotions and information, and predict other’s behavior. The distinct processes necessary to perform successful social interaction range from basic emotion recognition to higher-order processes including empathy and mentalizing, although the field still lacks a comprehensive consensus-based nomenclature for SoC processes.1 The relevance of social interaction for human functioning and well-being is highlighted by the detrimental effects of social isolation as well as the strong transdiagnostic impact of SoC deficits on patients’ quality of life.2

The complexity of social interaction is reflected in a high number of different brain areas associated with SoC, subsumed into functionally specific networks and clusters.3 For neural interaction across the brain to be successful, the relevant regions need intact structural connections. As reviewed by Wang et al. (2018) the relevance of integrity in white matter (WM) pathways is evidenced by marked SoC deficits resulting from clinical as well as virtual (e.g. via tDCS) WM lesions. Moreover, SoC deficits in clinical samples (e.g. autism) correlate with deviant structural measures. This two-way association between SoC deficits and structural connectivity has led to increased scientific interest in the role of WM pathways for successful SoC.

Since to date, no meta-analysis has assessed the effect sizes of the proposed correlation, this systematic review and meta-analysis (MA), aims to: examine the overall relationship between continuous measures of SoC and structural connectivity, identify specific WM structures and locations associated with SoC processing, and investigate important moderators including SoC constructs, DTI metrics, and population/diagnosis-specific effects.

Methods

Literature screening

PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science were searched for literature containing at least one keyword for SoC 1 and one for structural connectivity. The screening was performed by two independent raters, according to PRISMA guidelines 7 and conflicting assessments were solved in discussion with the other co-authors. Studies were excluded if: measures were not directly correlated, SoC measures were purely perception-based, WM measures were not diffusion-based, target variables were manipulated, or crucial information was missing. For- and backward search was conducted for the seed review by Wang et (2018). Finally, authors of included studies were inquired about missing information and for potential additional statistical maps.

The relevant study details were extracted by the first author, including information on the investigated sample (n, age, diagnosis), the applied measure of SoC, diffusion imaging analysis and measures for WM integrity and details about statistical procedures.

The relevant moderator variables were grouped by the research team composed of neuroscience and clinical psychology experts on SoC. Psychological measures derived from different assessment tools were grouped by the SoC constructs they aimed to assess 1, diagnoses were grouped in broader disorder types and tracts of interest were grouped into larger WM bundles.

Meta-analysis

Due to the anticipated methodological heterogeneity of preceding literature, two different types of meta-analyses were conducted.

For studies performing whole brain voxel-wise correlation between measures of fractional anisotropy (FA) and SoC measures, a neuroimaging meta-analysis was conducted, to identify brain locations associated with SoC across studies. The Seed-based d mapping (SDM) software was used. Subgroup analyses were performed for different socio-cognitive constructs (emotion recognition, empathy, mentalizing) using linear contrasts.

For studies performing correlations with tract-wise mean DTI values, effect size meta-analysis were performed. Subgroup analysis was applied to evaluate tract-specific results and assess population-, socio-cognitive-construct, and age-specific associations. The R-package metafor was used for the analysis.

Results

Literature screening

Figure 2 visualizes the screening workflow according to the PRISMA guidelines. A final set of n=63 records and n=809 effect sizes were included in the review and meta-analysis. The main reason for exclusion was the use of non-correlative statistical methods, followed by the lack of an adequate an behavioral SoC measure, or a measure for WM that was not diffusion tensor based. N=13 records had to be excluded because voxel-wise correlation was only performed in regions with significant group differences but not the whole brain and n=10 studies investigated lesion patients which are also not comparable to non-lesioned brains.

The most investigated group was healthy samples (n=62), followed by autism spectrum disorder (n=16), schizophrenia (n=11), genetic disorders (e.g. 22q11.2 Microdeletion Syndrome, Wilson’s disease), aphasia and neurodegenerative diseases (e.g. dementia, multiple sclerosis). N=17 studies investigated minors, n=44 adults and n=12 older adults (>55 years).

The applied SoC measures were grouped into emotion recognition (eg. Ekman facial emotion recognition task, reading the mind in the eyes task), empathy (empathy quotient, emotion attribution task, Interpersonal Reactivity Index, Trait Emotional Intelligence Questionnaire), mentalizing (false belief task, The Awareness of Social Inference Test, social sensitivity (RSMS)), social communication, and broader measures of social functioning (peer problems (SDQ), subtasks of ADOS and SRS).

Whole brain analyses

A total of n=15 studies could be included in the whole-brain voxel-wise analysis. The mean analysis across all studies yielded no significant converging results. In the linear model differentiating between SoC measures only yielded significant coordinates for empathy located within the corpus callosum, the left striatum and the left superior longitudinal fasciculus.

ROI effect size analysis

For all tracts, most of the up to 96 calculations per study yielded non-significant effect-sizes. The most commonly investigated tracts are the uncinate fasciculus (UF), the cingulate (CING), inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (IFOF), the inferior longitudinal fasciculus (ILF) and the superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF) as well as the corpus callosum (CC) and tracts connecting to the amygdala and thalamus. The highest number and proportion of significant effect sizes was found for the UF (37; 31,5%) CC (n=10; 16%), ATR (6; 20,69%), CING (10; 9,57%); CR (9; 35%); IFOF (14; 11,4%); ILF (12; 17,46%) and SLF (11; 8,86%), all associated with multiple social constructs in different populations.

The meta-analysis supports a significant overall association between SoC and tract-wise diffusion measures (r=0.0541; se=0.0128; p<.0001) while also evidencing significant heterogeneity across studies (Q(df=595)=3466.0777, p< .0001). Adding the moderators tract, SoC construct, age-group and diagnosis improved the model from H²=84.84% to H²=80.52% of unexplained heterogeneity and showed evidence for significant moderation (R²= 20.09%; QM(df=54)=153.4874, p<.0001.  Calculating one model per moderator, the highest SoC model (QM(df = 5) = 30.0236, p<.0001) coefficients in SoC were found for empathy (beta=0.1195; p=0.0037), followed by the reference category emotion recognition (beta=0.0811; p<.0001) and mentalizing (beta=0.0565; p=0.0389) while the broader constructs of social communication and social functioning had no significant associations. Regarding age groups (QM(df=2)=7.5102; p=.0234), older adults showed highest associations (beta=0.1178; p=.0129) and regarding diagnoses (QM(df=7)=15.7511; p=.0275), only the reference category autism showed significant moderation (beta=0.0653; p=.0268).

Conclusion

Taken together, the growing literature on associations between SoC and WM integrity is very heterogenous, investigating diverse social constructs and populations. Despite a high number of non-significant findings, the overall effect size meta-analysis showed significant correlation effects. Importantly, diagnoses, age, and investigated construct significantly moderate the association, although the considerable heterogeneity across studies was not fully explained by these moderators.

Among the most frequently associated tracts, the uncinate fasciculus showed the highest proportion of significant effects, followed by ATR and ILF. These tracts were all significantly associated with different SoC constructs. In contrast, the voxel-based meta-analysis showed no significant overall convergence but yielded significant associations between empathy scores and FA in the corpus callosum. This tract has received less scientific attention in the field but might play a crucial role for SoC due to the lateralization of many relevant functions and brain areas. The limited findings in the voxel-based analysis might be explained by the fact that the precise location of WM disruption is less relevant than the overall tract integrity, which is better investigated by tract-wise analyses, ideally performed using the more individualized tractography approaches as compared to the use of atlas-based templates.

While the meta-analyses of tract-wise results highlighted the relevance of certain WM tracts, the associations did not seem to be bound to one sub-type of SoC, but the bundles appear to be relevant across SoC constructs. Since all different SoC tasks are in themselves complex and require similar underlying cognition, this overlap is plausible. Network-wise analysis quantifying the overall structural connectivity in task specific networks might yield better insights into construct-specific associations. Moreover, further regression and subgroup analysis are necessary to tease apart tract-specific effects which should also aim to better integrate tractography results with atlas based tract definition.
