Introduction

Fairness, as a cornerstone of morality, facilitates equitable resource distribution and respectful treatment of individuals.1 Previous research has established that fairness emerges in early childhood and is linked to the maturation of prefrontal regions.2 For instance, while 6- to 7-year-olds often reject disadvantageous inequality out of egocentrism,3 children aged 7-8 begin to adhere to social norms by also rejecting advantageous inequality.4 However, the neural substrates of fairness continue to develop throughout adolescence--a period marked by growing autonomy and self-interest,5 yet remains to be understood in terms of its interplay with fairness evaluation.

This study aimed to investigate the developmental trajectory and neural mechanisms of fairness perception from middle childhood to adolescence, by utilizing EEG data from the Ultimatum Game paradigm. We proposed a two-stage model. In the first stage (during childhood), individuals progressively formed and then internalize fairness norms. Behaviorally, acceptance rates for equal offers increase. Neurally, the medial frontal negativity (MFN) in response to unfair allocations is expected to emerge and stabilize, reflecting the formation of fairness expectations. In the second stage (adolescence), individuals maintain a concern for fairness while placing greater emphasis on self-interest. Behaviorally, acceptance rates for advantageous inequal offers increase. Neurally, heightened N1 responses to inequality indicate increased vigilance against threats to self-interest.

Methods

We analyzed EEG data from 2,100 participants (ages 6-19) from the Chinese Child Brain Development (CCBD) project. Participants completed an Ultimatum Game, deciding whether to accept or reject offers from peers, including equal (EQ, 5:5), disadvantageous inequality (DI, 1:9 or 2:8), and advantageous inequality (AI, 9:1 or 8:2) allocations, with 60 trials per condition. To identify participants with distinct fairness vs. self-interest preferences, K-means clustering was applied to acceptance rates to identify behavioral profiles, with developmental differences assessed using permutation tests.

Event-related potentials were analyzed at standard electrode sites: the N1 (103-143 ms) at Fz for early attention, the MFN (peak within 200-240 ms) at Fz for expectation violation, and the late positive potential (LPP, 400-600 ms) at Pz for emotional motivation. Mixed ANOVAs were conducted with school stage (early primary grades, late primary grades, junior high school, senior high school) as between-subject factor and allocation type (EQ, DI, AI) as within-subject factor. Simple effects analyses were further conducted separately for each school stage to compare pairwise differences among the allocation types. Pairwise comparisons within stages were FDR-corrected.

Results

Behavioral Results

Analysis of acceptance rates revealed significant main effects of school stage (F(3, 1969) = 6.65, p < 0.001) and allocation type (F(1.75, 3452.65) = 3163.53, p < 0.001) , as well as a significant interaction between school stage and allocation type (F(5.26, 3452.65) = 10.90, p < 0.001; Fig. 1A). Simple effects analyses indicated that acceptance rates for equal offers increased from early to late primary grades. For advantageous inequality, acceptance rates decreased from early to late primary grades and then rose from junior to senior high school, while for disadvantageous inequality, acceptance rates declined from early to late primary grades (ps < 0.05).

Cluster analysis of acceptance rates identified four distinct profiles: “fairness matters” (high EQ acceptance), “avoiding disadvantage matters” (high EQ and AI acceptance), “accept all”, and “indifference” (Fig. 1B). Developmental shifts were observed: the “fairness matters” profile was less prevalent in early primary and senior high school compared to late primary and junior high school (ps < 0.05). “indifference” and “accept all” profile declines from early primary grades to later stages (ps < 0.05).

ERP Results

The MFN emerged gradually with age (Fig. 1C). No stable MFN was observed at ages 6-7. By age 8, an MFN was present only in response to equal offers, while a stable MFN for inequal allocations emerged around age 12. In junior and senior high school, significant main effects were found for both school stage (F(1, 936) = 24.56, p < 0.001) and allocation type (F(1.99, 1865) = 17.95, p < 0.001), with advantageous inequality eliciting the most negative amplitudes, followed by disadvantageous inequality and equal offers (Fig. 1D). The gradually emerging MFN pattern indicates that the expectation of equal offers becomes stable around age 8, and the expectation of inequality becomes stable around age 12; meanwhile, stable, low expectations of disadvantageous inequality and even lower expectations of advantageous inequality develop during junior and senior high school.

For the N1 component, significant main effects were observed for school stage (F(3, 2096) = 135.46, p < 0.001), with amplitudes becoming smaller across age. A main effect of allocation type was found (F(2, 4185) = 13.58, p < 0.001), as the DI allocation elicited the strongest amplitudes, followed by EQ and then AI, indicating heightened attention to disadvantage inequality and, to a lesser extent, increased attention to equality. Allocation type effects were significant differently in late primary grades (DI, EQ > AI) and senior high school (DI > AI, EQ; Fig. 1E). These findings suggest a consistent attentional bias toward disadvantage, possibly due to increased alertness to threats against self-interest. Additionally, they indicate that fairness automatically captures attention in primary grades, but this effect diminishes in later stages.

LPP analysis revealed main effects of school stage (F(3, 2096) = 12.81, p < 0.001) and allocation type (F(2, 4145) = 228.80, p < 0.001). The EQ allocation elicited more positive amplitudes than AI and DI. As LPP represents positive motivation, the results indicate a sustained positive motivation towards fairness across all stages.

Conclusion

This study delineates a two-stage developmental trajectory of fairness perception from childhood to adolescence. In the primary school stage, children progressively form and internalize fairness norms, as evidenced by increasing acceptance of equal offers and decreasing acceptance of unequal offers. This behavioral emergence is paralleled by the neural stabilization of the MFN, reflecting the consolidation of fairness expectations. Meanwhile, the fairness-related N1 responses were heightened in the late primary grades but gradually diminished thereafter, suggesting a decrease in attention to fair allocations over time. This reduction in bottom-up processing could potentially reflect the internalization of fairness.

During adolescence, a nuanced shift toward balancing fairness with self-interest occurs. While a sustained positive motivation toward fairness persists (reflected in the LPP), increased acceptance of advantageous inequality and heightened neural vigilance (N1) toward disadvantageous inequality indicate a growing emphasis on self-interest. This period is characterized by the integration of pre-established fairness principles with an escalating drive for autonomy.

In summary, fairness development evolves from the initial learning and external reinforcement of norms in childhood to a more complex, internalized regulatory system in adolescence that negotiates between upholding fairness and advancing personal interests.
