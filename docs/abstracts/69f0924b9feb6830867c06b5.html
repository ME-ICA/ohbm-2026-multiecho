Introduction

Aesthetic experience is a fundamental aspect of visual perception, shaping responses to artworks, natural scenes, social media content and everyday objects around us. Traditional models of neuroaesthetics suggest that the sensory system merely serves as the preliminary gateway for perceptual input, with aesthetic evaluation emerging from downstream cognitive systems such as those implicated in subjective valuation. Recent empirical evidence, however, suggests that aesthetic appeal is partly grounded in both low-level and high-level visual features of artworks1, and may be explicitly encoded within the visual cortex itself2, which requires further investigation.

A major challenge in isolating aesthetic representations lies in disentangling visual properties and semantic content of artworks from subjective preference, which are often highly correlated. As a result, the cortical topography of distinct aesthetic feature spaces remains poorly characterised. In this study, we address this gap by using ultra-high field 7T fMRI while participants aesthetically evaluated a deeply characterised set of traditional watercolour paintings. Using vertex-wise encoding models3,4, our primary objective was to dissociate the independent contributions of visual, semantic and preference features to neural responses in the human visual cortex during aesthetic evaluation of artworks. 

Methods

A total of 34 participants (mean age = 23.21 years old, SD = 2.41, F/M ratio = 22/12) underwent an HCP-compliant 7T fMRI scanning protocol (TR = 1.5 s, TE = 25 ms, 1.5 mm iso voxels) while performing four runs of an Art Recognition Task. Using event-related design (4 s ON, 1 s OFF), participants were presented with a set of 96 images of traditional Chinese watercolour paintings and were instructed to attend to how aesthetically moving they found each artwork. Following preprocessing via HCP pipelines, including ICA+FIX denoising and MSMAll areal-feature-based cortical surface registration, trial-wise neural responses were estimated using GLMsingle. This procedure yielded image-specific activation patterns across approximately 59,000 cortical vertices (Fig. 1a).

In total, we extracted three feature spaces for each stimulus to model their neural responses: (i) visual features (43 quantitative descriptors of colour, composition, symmetry and complexity based on the Aesthetics Toolbox5), (ii) semantic features derived from a behavioural multi-arrangement task measuring rater estimated distances of semantic similarity (10 principal components), and (iii) preference features derived from inter-subject similarity of aesthetic ratings (10 principal components). Preference features were included to account for subjective aesthetic valuation and to dissociate feature-based encoding in visual cortex from individual differences in liking, rather than to maximize predictive variance. Feature spaces were entered into vertex-wise encoding models employing banded ridge regression, providing a separate regularization parameter for each feature space4. Model performance and feature contributions were evaluated via leave-one-run-out cross-validation and split-R2 analyses.

Results

Encoding models incorporating all three feature spaces reliably predicted neural responses across the visual cortex (Fig. 1b). Importantly, variance decomposition revealed distinct cortical topographies for different aesthetic features. Visual features were primarily encoded in early visual regions (V1, V2, and V3), whereas semantic features were strongly represented along the ventral visual pathway, including fusiform and higher-order occipitotemporal regions (Fig. 2a). Preference features illustrated minimal contribution to model performance, which indicates that subjective liking was not explicitly encoded in visual cortex, while allowing us to estimate visual and semantic feature representations independently of valuation-related variance.

When projecting the frequency of positive contributions (thresholded at > 2/3 of the group) onto a composite map, results revealed that regions within the bilateral V2 and left fusiform gyrus jointly encoded visual and semantic feature spaces, with minimal contributions from preference features. Importantly, analyses of neural responses to artworks with low versus high ratings for aesthetic appeal revealed opposing effects across feature-selective regions of the brain. High-rated images elicited stronger responses in visual-feature regions (t[14] = 4.36, p < 0.001), whereas semantic-feature regions showed greater activation for lower-rated images (t[14] = -13.52, p < 0.001; Fig. 2b). This dissociation suggests that aesthetic appeal modulates neural responses differentially within feature-selective visual and semantic representations without constituting a standalone representational dimension.

Conclusion

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that aesthetic features of artworks are explicitly encoded within the human visual cortex through distinct but overlapping representations of visual and semantic properties. Rather than being a sole gateway to downstream evaluative systems, aesthetic information is distributed across multiple stages of visual processing, with differential sensitivity to aesthetic appeal. Importantly, aesthetic appeal appears to act as a modulatory signal on visual and semantic feature representations rather than being explicitly represented within visual cortex itself, consistent with accounts in which subjective preference emerges from interactions between perceptual representations and downstream evaluative systems. By disentangling visual, semantic and preference feature spaces using encoding models, this work provides a refined account of how the visual cortex contributes to aesthetic experience, highlighting its active role in constructing aesthetic representations beyond basic sensory inputs.
