Introduction

Decision-making does not occur in isolation: our current choices are shaped by what we chose before. In perceptual decision-making, this choice history bias manifests as a shift in how we accumulate evidence, potentially signaling that new stimuli appear more similar to previously chosen options [2]. However, the mechanisms underlying choice history bias in decisions under risk remain unclear.

Understanding these biases has real-world implications. Whether we are choosing insurance coverage, evaluating job offers, or making financial investments, a tendency to repeat or switch from previous risky decisions could significantly impact outcomes. Recent work suggests that arousal systems may play a role in counteracting choice history biases, as increased pupil dilation (a marker of arousal) is associated with reduced choice history effects [3]. This raises the question: what neural systems support switching away from previous choices?

Here, we re-analyzed data from the NARPS mixed gambles task [1] to address three questions: (1) Does choice history bias occur in decisions under risk? (2) What cognitive mechanisms drive this bias? (3) Which brain networks activate when people switch from their previous choices?

We hypothesized that choice history would bias both decisions and response times, and that switching would engage cognitive control networks associated with overcoming behavioral inertia. Furthermore, we explored the cognitive mechanisms of choice history bias via drift diffusion modeling analysis. 

Methods

We analyzed data from the NARPS study [1], which includes 54 subjects in each of two conditions (equalIndifference and equalRange). Participants made accept/reject decisions on mixed gambles with varying gains and losses. We estimated subjective values for each gamble by fitting a generalized linear mixed-effects model (GLME) with gain and loss as fixed effects and random slopes for gain, loss, and intercept per subject. Individual subjective values were computed as: SV = β_intercept + β_gain × gain + β_loss × loss.

Behavioral Analysis: Testing for Choice History Effects To test whether choice history influences decisions under risk, we fit GLMEs predicting current choice (accept/reject) with gain and loss as baseline predictors (Model 1). We then performed model comparison against models that included:

  • M2: Previous choice (accept/reject)

  • M3: Previous subjective value

  • M4: Change in subjective value (current - previous)

  • M5: Previous gain and loss

  • M6: M3 + previous choice

  • M7: M4 + previous choice

  • M8: M5 + previous choice

All models were compared to M2 (Choice history model) using Bayes factors 

We also tested whether choice history affects response times. Separately for trials following accept vs. reject decisions, we fit linear models predicting log(RT) from the absolute subjective value, then we compare these to models including previous choice as an additional predictor.

Drift Diffusion Modeling: Mechanisms of Choice History To identify the cognitive mechanisms underlying choice history bias, we fit hierarchical drift diffusion models (hDDM) with 7 parameters: drift rate components for gain and loss, drift intercept, starting point, boundary separation, boundary collapse rate, and non-decision time. We compared four models per condition:

  • Model 1: Baseline (no history effects)

  • Model 2: Starting point bias (starting point ~ 1 + previous choice)

  • Model 3: Drift rate bias (drift ~ 1 + previous choice + gain + loss)

  • Model 4: Full model (both biases)

All models were compared to the Baseline model via approximate Bayes factor analysis.

fMRI Analysis: Neural Correlates of Switching To identify brain regions supporting switches away from previous choices, we modeled the fMRI data of the two conditions with regressors for subjective value, entropy [4], individual button presses (strongly/weakly accept/reject), repeat choices, and switch choices. We performed whole-brain cluster analysis on the Switch > Repeat contrast using FDR correction (p < 0.05, cluster size > 10 voxels).

Results

Choice History Strongly Influences Decisions Under Risk Model comparison revealed overwhelming evidence for choice history effects. The choice history model (M2) was strongly preferred over the baseline (M1) in both conditions (log10(BF) = 49.82). M2 was also preferred over alternative models (M3-M5, M8), with the exception of models that included both choice history and previous subjective value information (M6 in equalIndifference: log10(BF) = -1.87; M7 in both conditions: log10(BF) < -15.36).

Critically, the fixed effect of the previous choice regressor remained significant across all models (equalIndifference: β > 0.49, p < 1.77e-23 | equalRange: β > 0.302, p < 4.51e-08, Bonferroni corrected), indicating a robust tendency to repeat previous choices. This effect was positive in direction, meaning participants were biased toward making the same choice (accept or reject) as on the previous trial.

Choice History Affects Response Times Response time analysis further supported the influence of choice history. Models including previous choice were strongly preferred over baseline models (log10(BF) > 15.83) for both conditions and both previous choice types, with one exception: equalRange trials following reject decisions (log10(BF) = -0.82). Nonetheless, the previous choice remained a significant predictor in the full model (equalIndifference previous accept: β = -0.036, p < 2.2e-25 | equalIndifference previous reject: β = 0.025, p < 2.46e-10  | equalRange previous accept: β = -0.027, p < 1.16e-13 | equalRange previous reject: β = 0.009, p < 0.008).

Starting Point Bias Is the Primary Mechanism Drift diffusion model comparison identified the starting point as the primary locus of choice history bias. The starting point model (Model 2) showed the highest evidence compared to both the drift bias model (equalIndifference: log10(BF) = 57.85) | equalRange: log10(BF) = 27.27) and the full model (equalIndifference: log10(BF) = 2.83) | equalRange: log10(BF) = 0.59). 

This indicates that previous choices shift the initial bias toward one response option, rather than altering how evidence is accumulated. This starting point was biased toward repeating the previous choice (equalIndifference: shift mean = 0.196, std = 0.0432 | equalRange shift mean = 0.096, std = 0.008), suggesting that residual information from past decisions is encoded in stimulus-independent systems, potentially motor or preparatory networks that maintain a trace of the previous response.

Switching Engages Widespread Cognitive Control Networks Cluster analysis of the Switch>Repeat contrast revealed extensive activation in regions associated with cognitive control and attentional reorientation. In the equalIndifference condition, significant clusters (p < 0.05 FDR corrected) were found in: lateral occipital cortex (superior division), posterior cingulate gyrus, precentral gyrus, frontal pole, superior and middle frontal gyri, paracingulate gyrus, and posterior middle and inferior temporal gyri. Peak coordinates: X = -61, Y = 29, Z = 54, z =6.15.

The equalRange condition showed similar but more focused activation in: postcentral gyrus, lateral occipital cortex (superior division), precentral gyrus, superior frontal gyrus, and superior parietal lobule. Peak coordinates: X = -27, Y = 47, Z = 42, z = 5.88.

These regions overlap substantially with the cognitive control network, suggesting that switching from previous choices requires active engagement of executive systems to override the default tendency to repeat [5].

Conclusion

We demonstrate that choice history robustly influences decisions under risk, manifesting in both choice behavior and response times. Across two conditions, participants showed a strong bias to repeat their previous accept/reject decisions, and this bias was not simply explained by correlations in the subjective values of consecutive gambles. Instead, choice history had an independent effect that persisted even when controlling for value-based information.

Mechanistically, we identified the starting point of evidence accumulation as the primary locus of this bias. Rather than changing how evidence is integrated over time (drift rate), previous choices shift the initial predisposition toward one response. This suggests that residual information from prior decisions is maintained in stimulus-independent systems that create a default toward repeating the same action.

Importantly, participants can overcome this bias, but doing so requires effort. Our neuroimaging results show that switching from previous choices activates extensive cognitive control networks [5], including dorsolateral and medial prefrontal cortex, parietal cortex, and cingulate regions. This pattern is consistent with the need to actively counteract an automatic tendency to repeat, engaging top-down control systems to reorient attention and suppress the pre-potent response.

These findings have implications for understanding real-world decision-making. The tendency to repeat risky (or safe) choices may lead to suboptimal patterns in financial decisions, health behaviors, or policy choices. Understanding that this bias operates at the level of response preparation, rather than value computation, suggests potential interventions: increasing awareness of the bias, introducing explicit choice reflection, or manipulating arousal states may help people make more independent, context-appropriate decisions.

Our results also raise intriguing questions about the relationship between arousal and choice history. Prior work has shown that pupil dilation correlates with reduced choice history bias [3]. Our finding that switching engages widespread cortical networks suggests that arousal systems may facilitate switching by broadly activating cognitive control resources. Future work combining pupillometry, fMRI, and computational modeling could directly test whether arousal modulates the starting point bias or the ability to engage control networks to override it.

In summary, choice history is a fundamental component of decisions under risk, operating through shifts in response preparation rather than value computation. Switching from previous choices is not automatic but requires active engagement of cognitive control systems. Recognizing and understanding these biases is a crucial step toward improving decision-making in consequential real-world contexts.
