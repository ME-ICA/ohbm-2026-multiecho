Introduction

Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) is a noninvasive method that operates by delivering rapidly changing magnetic fields that induce electric fields (E-fields) in the brain. Due to safely generating suprathreshold E-fields without causing discomfort, TMS is widely used in both research and clinical setting for pre-surgical localization of language or motor areas, as well as for treatments of neuropsychiatric conditions, such as for depression and OCD1.

Brute-force motor mapping typically uses grid searches and center-of-gravity estimates based on motor-evoked potentials (MEPs), often requiring 150–250 stimulations depending on grid size and desired resolution2. These grid methods largely infer the hotspot from scalp coil position, rather than modeling how the induced cortical E-field generates the MEP. To address this, Weise et al. related local induced E-field strength to MEP amplitude and localized the hotspot within ~3 mm using <150 stimulations2. More recently, 3D-Boost leveraged multi-channel TMS with a Bayesian optimization strategy to identify the hotspot location and orientation within ~3 mm, utilizing only ~47 stimuli on average3.

Despite progress, current approaches give little data-driven guidance on where to stimulate next. As a result, they often require many stimulations and rarely quantify spatial uncertainty, a limitation that is most evident in sulcal regions, where nearby sites can evoke similar responses and blur localization. Our method exploits computationally estimated E-fields to steer the mapping trajectory and to deliver principled hotspot estimates whose uncertainty shrinks as additional positions are sampled.

Methods

TMS and EMG setup: Stimulation was delivered with a figure-8 Cool-B35 coil (MagVenture, Farum, Denmark; outer diameter 2×46 mm) driven by a MagPro X100 stimulator and neuronavigated with a Localite system (Localite, Bonn, Germany). Surface EMG was recorded from the dominant-hand first dorsal interosseous (FDI) using TMS-compatible Ag/AgCl C-shaped electrodes (belly–tendon montage) with a ground placed over the ulnar styloid. Skin was prepped to keep impedance <10 kΩ. Signals were amplified (×1000), band-pass filtered (20–1000 Hz), and digitized at 20 kHz (Bittium NeurOne, Oulu, Finland). 

Hotspot identification: TMS stimulation was performed on a right-handed healthy participant after obtaining informed consent. The participant was informed to relax the hand muscles and continuous EMG monitoring verified minimal muscle pre-activation. Stimulation began at low intensity near the precentral hand area (precentral gyrus / presumed motor sulcus). Single pulses were applied at randomly selected nearby sites; if no response occurred, intensity was increased in small steps until an MEP >50 µV was observed. We then sampled surrounding sites at a suprathreshold level to locate a tentative hotspot, defined as the location yielding the most consistent, robust MEPs at that fixed suprathreshold intensity.

Grid mapping: A 3×5 grid centered on the tentative hotspot was defined in Localite, with 7 mm spacing in both the anterior–posterior and medial–lateral directions. The 7 mm distance set the spatial resolution of sampling while yielding a sufficiently large search area for structured exploration around the candidate site. The coil was held tangential to the scalp, with the induced E-field oriented approximately perpendicular to the central sulcus to favor corticospinal activation. The resting motor threshold (rMT) was defined as the minimum stimulator output that elicited reliable MEPs (typically ≥50 µV) in 50% of trials1. RMT at each grid site was estimated using a Bayesian MT estimation algorithm4. Head model surfaces were derived from the subject’s T1/T2 -weighted MRI data. E-fields were computed using a Boundary Element Method accelerated by the Fast Multipole Method (BEM-FMM)5 with the E-field profiles shown in Figure 1b and Figure 2c.

Sequential probabilistic guidance for TMS targeting and spatial uncertainty reduction: Each run began with two coil positions: the grid center plus one site sampled at random. For every measured site, we estimated rMT and computed the induced E-field. To suppress far-field contributions unrelated to motor function, values below 30% of the site’s maximum E-field were set to zero. Hotspot localization was framed as E-field consistency across sites. At each cortical vertex, we computed a pairwise E-field agreement using the pairwise squared-difference sum across the measured positions with smaller differences indicating greater agreement. Agreement was accepted within a fixed noise tolerance (σ_rel = 4% of amplitude). We then converted dispersion to a per-vertex likelihood via an exponential mapping, large where the measured E-field profiles collectively match within tolerance, near zero elsewhere, and normalized it to obtain a probability map over the cortical surface.

The next stimulation site was selected directly from the probability map. We identified the high-probability region and computed its boundary as vertices whose neighbors exhibit a marked probability drop. Among unmeasured grid sites, we chose the site whose center lay closest to this boundary, enforcing a minimum separation of 7 mm from previously tested sites to avoid clustering. This measure-update-select loop was repeated for a total of ~5 targets/iterations (center + random + 3 guided). This procedure is a probabilistic estimator that fuses subject-specific E-field profiles to guide stimulation and reduce spatial uncertainty over iterations.

Results

Hotspot localization via mid-row E-field comparison: We began with a mid-row (medial–lateral) sweep of the grid without predictive stimulation, providing an initial baseline for the pairwise analysis and a reference for subsequent comparisons with the probabilistic guidance method. The 3×5 rMT map (Figure 1a) shows that the central site yielded the lowest threshold, consistent with a motor hotspot near the hand knob. Sample E-field profile of position 5 is shown in Figure 1b and exhibits a broad cortical footprint. Using the measured positions, the probabilistic map concentrated into a hotspot region that spanned the motor cortex and adjacent areas (Figure 1c). 

Across targets 2–5, the highest-probability region progressively contracted toward areas with similar E-field values (Figure 1d). Colored overlays depict successive regions (with red indicating the most recent and most probable), and the black dot marks the grid-center location. With each iteration, the probability distribution map narrowed and converged on the region whose E-field intensities were most consistent with the observed thresholds. Notably, because only the mid row was sampled in this initial analysis, the predicted hotspot band tends to align perpendicular to the tested row.

Hotspot localization via guided TMS and E-field comparison: Starting from the center and one random site, the guidance policy selected subsequent optimized grid locations that lay closest to the probability map boundary. The rMT map and chosen sites (Figure 2a) drove the probability to concentrate over the anatomically correct precentral hand area. The E-field profile for the guided site (position 5) is shown in Figure 2c. Combining all measured positions, the probabilistic map collapsed to a compact hotspot region on the motor strip (Figure 2d). Across targets 2–5, the highest-probability region contracted monotonically and stabilized along the precentral gyrus (Figure 2e), indicating convergence to a consistent motor hotspot with few guided stimulations. 

For comparison, we implemented the method of Weise et al.2, which relates local E-field strength to MEP amplitude using ~150 randomly sampled stimulation sites (Figure 2b). Our guided approach localized the hotspot to the same cortical region with greater spatial specificity and far fewer pulses. Relative to our baseline mid-row sweep without guidance, which showed lower specificity and often highlighted postcentral (somatosensory) cortex because multiple sites produced similar E-field values, the guided method was more selective and robust. Operationally, after identifying a tentative hotspot, Bayesian rMT estimation converged in ~10 pulses per site and localization used 5 sites, so the hotspot was identified in ~50 pulses. Conventional grid-based or center-of-gravity protocols typically require 150–250 stimulations, while some algorithmic approaches achieve ~50 on average3. Unlike the algorithmic approaches, our probabilistic E-field guidance iteratively shrinks spatial uncertainty and provides real-time feedback (Figure 2e).

Conclusion

We present a sequential, probabilistic E-field guidance framework for TMS hotspot localization. Measured E-fields from a small set of grid locations are converted to a normalized probability map on the cortical surface. We extract the highest-probability region and choose the next site on its boundary to maximally reduce spatial uncertainty. In practice, each additional site sharpens the probability peak over the precentral hand area and contracts the candidate hotspot, yielding an interpretable recommendation of where to stimulate next and how confident we are. Using human data with rMT-derived E-fields, the method localized the hotspot with few guided targets and showed consistent uncertainty reduction across iterations. Qualitative comparison to an established E-field–to-MEP mapping approach2 reached a comparable hotspot location while requiring far fewer pulses.

Beyond motor mapping, the same sequential guidance can direct language or network-level mapping, recommending the next stimulation site in real time while continuously updating the highest-probability region as new evidence arrives. The framework is readily compatible with robotic TMS positioning (e.g., Axilum Robotics TMS-Cobot). The system operates as follows: the robot iteratively moves the coil to the predicted cortical grid site, the Bayesian algorithm estimates the rMT online from EMG, the E-field profile is computed in real time, the probability map is updated, the next location for stimulation is relayed to the robot, and the shrinking hotspot predicted region is rendered on the navigator display. Such a closed-loop, uncertainty-aware workflow would reduce operator dependence, increase throughput, and standardize acquisition across operators and sites.

This level of automation creates a path to reproducible neurophysiological biomarkers for clinical trials, rehabilitation monitoring, and longitudinal assessments of cortical excitability in neurological and psychiatric disorders. By reducing manual workload, removing observer bias, and quantifying confidence at each step, the proposed approach advances precision neuromodulation and supports individualized dosing. Future work will expand validation across subjects and tasks, integrate anatomical/functional priors to mitigate sulcal ambiguity, and formalize information-theoretic site selection, moving from heuristic boundary sampling to principled, real-time optimal guidance.
