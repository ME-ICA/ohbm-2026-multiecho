Introduction

Huntington’s disease (HD) is a genetic disorder characterized by progressive neurodegeneration affecting both the cerebral cortex and the striatum. Importantly, previous studies have reported language and cognitive difficulties in individuals with HD 1,2 making this condition a strong model for investigating structure–function relationships involving frontal and fronto-striatal white matter pathways, such as the inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (IFOF), the frontal aslant tract (FAT), and fronto-striatal tracts (FST).

In addition to its distinctive anatomical profile, HD can be genetically identified before symptom onset during the premanifest stage. This provides a unique opportunity to detect the point at which white matter degeneration begins before clinical manifestation, as well as to determine when structural brain damage becomes sufficient to produce cognitive or language impairments. Although the prevalence of HD is similar in men and women, it remains unclear whether their cognitive control loss, as well as neurodegeneration of the frontal and fronto-striatal pathways affect both sexes to the same extent.

The primary objective of this study was to examine macrostructural and microstructural changes in key frontal and fronto-striatal white matter tracts (IFOF, FAT, and FST) across disease stages, with particular emphasis on the premanifest phase and potential sex differences. A secondary objective was to investigate how degeneration of these tracts relates to cognitive performance as HD progresses.

Methods

Thirty-two controls and 39 HD participants (21 premanifest, 18 manifest) underwent structural MRI and comprehensive neuropsychological assessment. White matter microstructural integrity indices (fractional anisotropy- FA and mean diffusivity -MD) and volume were extracted for the IFOF, FAT (inferior and middle frontal projections separately) and FST pathways, bilaterally. Language assessment was performed using Peabody Picture Vocabulary Test, phonemic verbal fluency, as well as, in patients group, the Boston Naming test. Cognitive measures included Trail Making Test (A and B), Digit Span (forward and backward), and, for patients, the Stroop test. Linear mixed-effects models were used to test effects of group, hemisphere, tract and their interaction on FA, MD and volume values within each tract, with subject included as a random intercept. Fixed-effect significance was assessed using Type III F-tests with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom (lmerTest, lme4 package, R). When appropriate, post-hoc comparisons were carried out using estimated marginal means (emmeans), with FDR correction for multiple contrasts. To evaluate which tract most clearly distinguishes between our patients’ groups (premanifest and manifest), we initially split our sample according to a 70:30 ratio. We then trained a random forest with 500 trees, using 2 randomly selected features at each split.

To assess the relationship between micro and macrostructure of the tracts and cognition, linear models were applied for each tract and test, with FDR correction for multiple comparisons. For HD participants, disease burden was quantified using the CAG–Age Product (CAP) score, which indexes cumulative genetic exposure to mutant huntingtin, and was included as a covariate in anatomo-functional analyses to account for individual differences in disease progression.

Results

At the microstructural level, mean diffusivity (MD) differed significantly across groups (F(2) = 7.75, p = .0009), with controls showing lower MD than manifest  (B = -0.00003, p = .001) and premanifest HD patients (B = 0.00004, p = .001) bilaterally. Fractional anisotropy (FA) showed no group or hemispheric effects. Tract volume also differed by group (F(2) = 22.38, p < .00001), with manifest HD patients exhibiting smaller volumes than controls (B = 1222, p = .002) and premanifest HD patients (B = -676, p = .001), and controls larger than premanifest HD patients (B = 546, p < .0001). No significant interaction with laterality was observed (F(2) = 0.27, p = .76), but a significant group-by-tract interaction (F(2) = 14.30, p < .00001) indicated that volumetric differences were tract-specific, with premanifest HD and controls differing only in the inferior frontal projections of FAT in both hemispheres (left: B = -1365.79, p = .0005; right: B = -908.78, p = .01).

Classification analyses revealed that microstructural MD changes distinguished premanifest from manifest HD patients with 67% accuracy, driven by the right and left inferior frontal projections of FAT and left FST. Similarly, distinguishing premanifest HD from controls (67% accuracy) was primarily informed by the right FST, left IFOF, and right middle frontal projections of FAT. Volume-based classification achieved 64% accuracy, with the left inferior and middle frontal projections of FAT, as well as the right FST as the strongest predictors.

No sex-related differences were observed in neurodegeneration patterns across tracts or hemispheres. However, at the behavioral level, female patients showed significantly better performance on the TMT B–A compared to male patients (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, W = 193, p = .021), with lower TMT B–A scores indicating greater executive efficiency.

Anatomo-functional analyses revealed significant associations between MD and cognitive performance across frontal and fronto-temporal pathways. MD in the left FAT (inferior projections) was associated with TMT-A (t = 2.74, p = .024), Digit Span forward and backward (t = −3.31 to −4.08, p ≤ .01), and Stroop color naming (t = −2.48, p = .019). MD in the left IFOF was related to executive measures, including TMT-A, TMT-B, and TMT B–A (t = 2.76–3.14, p ≤ .03), as well as Digit Span and Stroop performance (t ≥ |3.0|, p ≤ .02). In addition, MD in the left middle frontal FAT and left fronto-striatal tract was selectively associated with working memory and processing speed, showing significant relationships with Digit Span forward and backward and TMT-A performance (t ≥ |2.74|, p ≤ .03). Comparable patterns were observed in the right hemisphere, with MD in the right FAT (both projections), FST, and IFOF showing robust associations with TMT, Digit Span, and Stroop measures (t up to 4.53, p ≤ .02). Language performance was primarily associated with MD in the right IFOF, including Boston Naming and Peabody Picture Vocabulary scores. Importantly, after controlling for disease burden (CAP), the association between right IFOF MD and Peabody scores remained significant (B = −206,451.32, SE = 65,777.85, p = .02).

Conclusion

At the structural level, volumetric differences in the inferior frontal projections of the frontal aslant tract (FAT) were observed bilaterally between premanifest Huntington’s disease (HD) patients and healthy controls. In addition to the left inferior FAT projections, left middle frontal FAT projections and the right fronto-striatal tract (FST) emerged as the strongest predictors distinguishing premanifest HD patients from controls.

At the microstructural level, mean diffusivity (MD) alterations confirmed the involvement of bilateral inferior FAT projections and further indicated that left FST MD contributed to differentiating premanifest from manifest HD patients. When classifying premanifest HD patients versus controls, the most informative MD-based predictors were the right FST, the left inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (IFOF), and the right middle frontal FAT projections. Together, these findings suggest that white matter alterations in frontal pathways may serve as early biomarkers of disease progression, even before symptom onset. However, future studies are needed to clarify why volumetric and microstructural alterations show limited overlap.

Regarding sex effects, no significant differences were observed in structural measures across tracts. However, female patients showed a significant advantage in set shifting, as compared to men.

At the functional level, after controlling for disease burden (CAP score), MD in the right IFOF remained significantly associated with performance on the Peabody Picture Vocabulary Test. In contrast, when CAP was not included as a covariate, multiple tracts showed associations with executive control measures, suggesting that cumulative disease burden substantially contributes to the relationship between tract integrity and cognitive performance. In this sense, and overall, the IFOF exhibited the strongest and most widespread relationships with executive sequencing and set-shifting performance (TMT B–A). Working memory, assessed via Digit Span, was the cognitive domain most broadly associated with tract integrity, showing robust relationships across fronto-striatal and frontal aslant pathways. Inhibitory control, measured by the Stroop task, was preferentially associated with FAT and FST integrity, with additional contributions from the IFOF. Language and semantic performance, assessed using the Boston Naming and Peabody tests, was selectively associated with right-hemisphere IFOF MD. 

In summary, frontal white matter pathways show early and progressive alterations in HD, closely linked to deficits in cognitive control. These findings underscore the critical role of frontal connectivity in the neurocognitive profile of HD and highlight white matter metrics as sensitive markers of disease progression, including during the premanifest stage.
