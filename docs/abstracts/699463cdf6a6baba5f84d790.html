Introduction

Renpenning syndrome (RS) is a rare X-linked neurodevelopmental disorder caused by mutations in the PQBP1 gene and characterized by intellectual disability, microcephaly, and a recognizable dysmorphic and somatic phenotype.1 Although microcephaly is a defining feature of RS, clinical MRI reports have repeatedly suggested additional regional brain abnormalities, including cerebellar and brainstem hypoplasia, corpus callosum peculiarities, and white matter signal changes.2 However, these observations have remained largely qualitative, and no comprehensive radiological series combined with quantitative morphometric analyses has yet been reported.

A detailed volumetric characterization may be particularly informative in the context of microcephaly, since reduction in brain size may not affect all regions equally. In the general population, evidence suggests non-linear scaling between total brain volume and the one of its substructures, such as the cerebellum: smaller brains are not simply reduced versions of larger ones.3 Allometric relationships between total brain volume and regional structures such as the cerebellum or subcortical nuclei are well documented in the general population. In this context, allometric and normative modeling frameworks provide a valuable way to assess whether regional brain volumes deviate from expected values given overall brain size. Such approaches are particularly relevant for rare disorders with small sample sizes, where classical group comparisons are underpowered.4

Here, we present the first systematic MRI characterization of RS, combining detailed qualitative neuroradiological assessment with quantitative volumetric analysis using allometric normative modeling. We hypothesized that RS would be associated with disproportionate undersizing of specific brain regions, particularly the cerebellum and subcortical structures, beyond the effect of global microcephaly.

Methods

Nine genetically confirmed RS patients (mean age 25.1 years) and 39 typically developing controls were scanned on a 1.5 T device with a 3D MPRAGE T1-weighted, 1 × 1 × 1 mm resolution sequence, and T2-FLAIR complementary sequences. All scans were systematically reviewed by a senior neuroradiologist to assess cortical architecture, commissural structures, white matter signal, posterior fossa anatomy, and ventricular configuration.

Automated volumetric segmentation was performed using the VolBrain pipeline to extract total brain volume and regional volumes of the cerebrum, cerebellum, brainstem, basal ganglia, thalami, hippocampi, and lateral ventricles. The fourth ventricle was manually delineated, and a “filled cerebellum” metric was computed by summing cerebellar and fourth ventricle volumes.

To account for non-linear scaling between total brain volume and regional structures, power-law allometric models Y=bXa were fitted in the control group. Individual deviations in RS patients were quantified using a permutation-based shuffle-and-split procedure, providing normative z-score-like estimates corrected for multiple comparisons using a Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction. Linear proportional models were used as comparators to assess the added sensitivity of the allometric approach.

Results

Radiological visual analysis revealed no major malformations of cortical organization or commissural structures. Cortical gyrification appeared preserved, and the corpus callosum was consistently present. In contrast, infratentorial abnormalities were prominent, with visually apparent cerebellar undersizing and enlargement of the fourth ventricle across patients. Age-related white matter FLAIR hyperintensities were observed in older individuals, suggestive of premature microstructural aging.

Quantitative analyses confirmed global brain volume reduction consistent with microcephaly. Beyond this global effect, normative allometric modeling revealed marked and disproportionate volume reductions in the cerebellum (mean deviation = -1.85 SD), basal ganglia (-2.76 SD), and hippocampi (-2.00 SD), as well as a milder but significant reduction in brainstem volume (-1.02 SD). In contrast, cerebral hemispheric volume was relatively preserved relative to total brain size (+1.74 SD). Ventricular volumes did not show consistent allometric scaling.

Importantly, linear proportional models failed to detect cerebellar undersizing, underscoring the necessity of non-linear scaling approaches. 

Conclusion

Taken together, these findings show that Renpenning syndrome is characterized by a selective and regionally patterned neuroanatomical profile rather than a uniform reduction in brain size. Although global microcephaly was a consistent feature, the combined qualitative and quantitative analyses demonstrated that several brain structures were disproportionately reduced relative to total brain volume. The radiological review complements these quantitative findings by highlighting a reproducible infratentorial phenotype, including visually apparent cerebellar undersizing and fourth ventricle enlargement, in the absence of major cortical or commissural malformations.

Most notably, normative allometric modeling reveals a consistent pattern of disproportionate undersizing affecting multiple brain regions, including the cerebellum, basal ganglia, and hippocampus, all of which show marked negative deviations relative to total brain volume. By contrast, thalamic or cerebral hemispheric volume is relatively preserved, indicating that microcephaly in RS is not associated with homogeneous scaling across brain regions but rather with a selective redistribution of brain tissue.

From a methodological perspective, this work underscores the necessity of accounting for non-linear allometric relationships when analyzing brain volumes, especially in microcephalic conditions. Linear or proportional normalization approaches failed to detect key regional deviations, whereas allometric normative modeling revealed clear and interpretable differences at both the group and individual levels. These results demonstrate that appropriate scaling models are essential for valid interpretation of neuroimaging data when overall brain size differs substantially from the reference population.

Finally, the observed pattern of selective regional undersizing suggests that RS involves differential effects on distinct neuroanatomical systems rather than generalized brain hypoplasia, with potential functional consequences for cognitive and motor domains. While these outcomes remained beyond the scope of the present work, our findings provide a robust neuroimaging framework for future studies aimed at refining phenotype characterization and improving structure-function correlations in Renpenning syndrome and related microcephalic disorders.
