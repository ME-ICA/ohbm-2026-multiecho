Introduction

Value-based decision making is a dynamic process involving multiple, interacting neural computations that unfold over time. Although previous neuroimaging studies have identified brain regions involved in valuation and choice, the rapid whole-brain dynamics underlying these processes remain poorly understood due to the limited temporal resolution of fMRI. In this study, we used magnetoencephalography (MEG) combined with hidden Markov model (HMM) analysis to investigate subsecond whole-brain dynamics associated with value-based decision making (Quinn et al., 2018).

Methods

Participants (N = 35) performed a binary choice task (Levy and Glimcher, 2011) involving risky and sure options during MEG recording. After preprocessing the MEG data, source-reconstructed MEG data were parcellated into 39 brain regions using a weighted parcellation. Parcel time series were extracted as the first principal component across voxels and transformed into 2–10 Hz amplitude envelopes via the Hilbert transform. The HMM model estimated state time courses, parcel-wise mean activity, and covariance (functional connectivity) for each state. Results are reported for four states (K = 4), with robustness confirmed across different numbers of states. To relate HMM state dynamics to decision variables, epoched state time series were regressed onto trial-wise measures using GLMs. Analyses were conducted time-locked to option onset or to participants’ responses.

Results

Using the HMM analysis, we identified that an HMM state (“State 1”) characterized by higher mean amplitude envelopes in anterior brain regions, exhibited task-evoked occupancy changes related to decision variables. In the option-locked analysis, the State 1 occupancy increased gradually after option presentation as a function of value sum, reaching a plateau around 700 ms. A similar but clearer ramping pattern was observed in the response-locked analysis, leading up to the participant’s response and outcome feedback. Comparable effects were also found for reward magnitude, indicating that these dynamics largely reflected objective trial attributes. In contrast, value difference showed a clear dissociation between analyses. The option-locked analysis revealed no robust modulation of HMM state occupancy by value difference. However, in the response-locked analysis, value difference was associated with transient but pronounced changes approximately 600–200 ms before the response, characterized by a decrease in State 1 and an increase in State 3. Additional GLM analyses demonstrated that similar patterns emerged when modeling absolute value difference or reaction time, consistent with the strong correlation between value difference and RT and participants’ tendency to choose the higher-value option.

Conclusion

We demonstrated that subsecond whole-brain dynamics in the 2–10 Hz band encode the subjective value of choice options, independently of perceptual and motor-related neural activity. These findings highlight the central role of distributed 2–10 Hz neural coordination in value-based decision-making.
