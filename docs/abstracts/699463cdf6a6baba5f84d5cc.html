Introduction

Recovery following ischemic stroke is supported by widespread reorganization of functional brain networks extending beyond the lesion site. Resting-state fMRI studies show that functional connectivity (FC) changes dynamically across recovery, involving shifts between ipsilesional and contralesional hemispheres and evolving inter- and intra-network coupling. However, the principles constraining where and how these changes emerge over time remain poorly understood.^4

Recent work in network neuroscience demonstrates that intrinsic gradients of cortical organization capture continuous transitions from unimodal sensory–motor regions to transmodal association cortex, reflecting hierarchical and cytoarchitectonic properties of the human cortex.^1 These gradients provide a compact framework for interpreting large-scale FC organization and are selectively disrupted following ischemic stroke.^2,3 While cross-sectional studies suggest that stroke-related FC alterations are distributed non-uniformly along these gradients, their longitudinal evolution from subacute to late-chronic recovery remains unclear.

In parallel, the spatial extent of FC reorganization—whether changes are focal or broadly distributed—has emerged as a key determinant of recovery. Diffuse reorganization may reflect early compensatory or maladaptive processes, whereas spatially focused changes are thought to support efficient network reintegration.^4,5 Both gradient position and spatial dispersion may further depend on stroke laterality, given hemispheric asymmetries in functional specialization.

Here, we investigate longitudinal FC reorganization after ischemic stroke using a lesion-matched, gradient-informed framework. By integrating cortical gradients with spatial dispersion metrics across recovery stages, we aim to clarify how hierarchical organization, anatomical laterality, and recovery phase jointly shape post-stroke network dynamics and cognitive outcome.

Methods

Participants and Study Design

We analyzed longitudinal resting-state fMRI data from 129 individuals with ischemic stroke and age-matched healthy controls. Stroke participants were assessed at 3 months (subacute), 12 months (early chronic), and 36 months (late chronic) post-stroke. Only participants with unilateral ischemic stroke and no history of neurodegenerative or major psychiatric illness were included. Stroke severity, lesion volume, and demographic variables were comparable between left- and right-hemisphere stroke groups.

MRI Acquisition and Preprocessing

MRI data were acquired on 3T scanners using standardized protocols. Functional images were preprocessed using established pipelines, including motion correction, slice-timing correction, normalization to MNI space, spatial smoothing (7 mm FWHM), nuisance regression, and band-pass filtering (0.008–0.09 Hz). Volumes with excessive motion were identified and excluded using standard artifact detection procedures.

Functional Connectivity Analysis

Seed-based functional connectivity (FC) maps were computed using bilateral precentral gyrus seeds. FC strength was estimated as Fisher-z transformed Pearson correlations between seed time series and all cortical voxels. Longitudinal FC change (ΔFC) was calculated between adjacent recovery phases (subacute–early chronic; early–late chronic) separately for ipsilesional and contralesional hemispheres, consistent with prior approaches to characterizing FC reorganization after stroke.^4

Lesion Matching and Disconnectome Validation

To minimize anatomical heterogeneity, participants with left- and right-hemisphere stroke were bilaterally lesion-matched using mirror-symmetric lesion mapping. This approach ensured comparable lesion topography and reduced confounding effects of structural disconnection on functional reorganization.^5

Gradient-Based Analysis

Voxelwise ΔFC maps were aligned to three orthogonal cortical gradients of macroscale organization: Gradient 1 (unimodal–transmodal), Gradient 2 (visual–sensorimotor), and Gradient 3 (sensorimotor–default mode). Gradient values were sampled at each voxel within significant ΔFC clusters to assess the distribution of FC changes along intrinsic cortical hierarchies.^1,2

Spatial Dispersion Metrics

The spatial extent of FC reorganization was quantified by computing Euclidean (volumetric) and geodesic (surface-based) distances of ΔFC voxels from each subject’s lesion centroid. Dispersion was defined as the standard deviation of these distances, calculated separately for positive and negative ΔFC, capturing the degree to which reorganization was spatially focal or distributed.^4,5

Behavioral Analysis

Executive function was assessed using the Trail Making Test Part B. Linear mixed-effects models examined associations between ΔFC, gradient position, their interaction, and behavioral performance, controlling for age and subject-level random effects.

Results

Lateralized and Longitudinal Functional Connectivity Changes
Longitudinal analyses revealed marked hemispheric asymmetries in functional connectivity (FC) reorganization following ischemic stroke. During subacute to early-chronic recovery, right-hemisphere stroke was associated with widespread increases in contralesional FC, predominantly within unimodal cortical regions (U = 2.13×10⁷, p < 0.001). In contrast, left-hemisphere stroke showed comparatively modest contralesional increases alongside pronounced reductions in ipsilesional FC within transmodal association cortex. Although overall FC differences between stroke groups diminished during early- to late-chronic recovery, distinct hemispheric patterns of reorganization persisted.

Gradient-Constrained Reorganization
FC changes were non-uniformly distributed along intrinsic cortical gradients. The principal unimodal-to-transmodal gradient (Gradient 1) revealed persistent lateralized differences, with right-hemisphere stroke showing contralesional upregulation in unimodal regions and left-hemisphere stroke exhibiting sustained ipsilesional reductions in transmodal cortex (H = 123.8, p < 0.001). Similar effects were observed along the secondary sensorimotor–default mode gradient (Gradient 3; H = 309.4, p < 0.001), indicating that post-stroke reorganization is constrained by cortical hierarchy and remains hemisphere-dependent across recovery phases.

Spatial Dispersion of Functional Connectivity Changes
The spatial extent of FC reorganization evolved over time. Dispersion of positive FC changes increased significantly from subacute to early-chronic recovery (β = +3.45 mm, p < 0.001), indicating broader network engagement. However, during late-chronic recovery, positive FC changes became more anatomically focused (β = −7.34 mm, p < 0.001). Right-hemisphere strokes exhibited greater spatial dispersion than left-hemisphere strokes across all stages (β = +4.32 mm, p < 0.001), consistent with hemisphere-specific compensatory mechanisms. Negative FC changes showed a distinct pattern, remaining more diffusely distributed across recovery.

Relationship to Cognitive Performance
In the early-chronic to late-chronic phase, the behavioral relevance of FC reorganization increased. Linear mixed-effects models demonstrated that the alignment of FC changes (ΔFC) with cortical gradients was significantly associated with Trail Making Test Part B performance. Specifically, a significant ΔFC × Gradient 1 interaction was observed (β = 26.3, p= 1.2×10⁻⁴), indicating that greater FC change within higher-gradient (more transmodal) regions was associated with poorer executive performance. These effects were not observed during the subacute phase, suggesting that the spatial organization of reorganization becomes behaviorally meaningful only in later recovery.

Conclusion

Conclusion
This study demonstrates that functional connectivity reorganization following ischemic stroke is not random or purely local, but unfolds along intrinsic cortical hierarchies and is strongly shaped by anatomical laterality and recovery stage. Using a lesion-matched, gradient-informed framework, we show that early recovery is characterized by lateralized patterns of contralesional upregulation and ipsilesional suppression, while chronic phases exhibit more structured and gradient-constrained reorganization, consistent with prior gradient-based models of cortical organization.^1,2

Importantly, the spatial distribution of connectivity changes evolves over time. Early post-stroke increases in functional connectivity are relatively diffuse, whereas later recovery is marked by more anatomically focused reorganization, particularly for positive connectivity changes. Right-hemisphere strokes consistently show broader spatial dispersion than left-hemisphere strokes, extending previous work linking spatially constrained reorganization to functional recovery.^4,5

Crucially, the behavioral relevance of network reorganization emerges in the chronic phase. The interaction between functional connectivity change and position along the principal unimodal–transmodal gradient predicts executive function performance, indicating that not only the magnitude but also the hierarchical location of reorganization is critical for cognitive recovery. Connectivity changes occurring in higher-gradient, transmodal regions were associated with poorer executive outcomes, consistent with inefficient or maladaptive compensatory processes.^4

Together, these findings establish cortical gradients as a unifying framework for understanding the direction, spatial extent, and functional relevance of post-stroke network plasticity. Integrating gradient-based and spatial metrics may improve mechanistic models of recovery and support the development of individualized prognostic markers and targeted rehabilitation strategies.
