Introduction

Vascular networks in the brain provide the energetic supply supporting neuronal activity. Evidence suggests that vasculature across multi-scales bear important relationship to functional organization within the cerebral cortex. In particular, diving pial arterioles and venules, as the primary conduits for blood entering/exiting the cortex1, are key to understanding the resiliencies and vulnerabilities of blood delivery to neurons2. The quantification of intracortical volumetric blood flow is thus essential for understanding the role of vascular function in health and disease. 

As a step towards assessing in vivo vascular physiology in humans, our recent work developed 7T MRI methods for noninvasively mapping intracortical vasculature in nonhuman primates, demonstrated the ability to measure vascular anatomy at scales finer than 100 microns using a whole-body clinical 7T scanner suitable for humans (Fig. 1A)3. Intracortical arterioles and venules were imaged using multi-echo time-of-flight MRA, and were distinguished by different concentrations of deoxyhemoglobin in arterial and venous blood. The vascular distributions mirrored the spatial patterns seen in human histology (Fig. 1B)4. 

A comprehensive understanding of vascular physiology requires further evaluation of parameters such as blood flow velocity and vessel diameter. Here, we extended our previous work by advancing an alternative method, phase‑contrast MRA, which achieved an in‑plane resolution of 64 μm2 for quantitatively measuring blood velocity. These small voxel sizes are still, however, at the edge of resolving in such small vessels; low SNR of these extremely high resolutions necessitates careful optimization of blood-tissue contrast and accounting for velocity estimation biases caused by partial‑volume effects. Additional challenges remain in quantitatively assessing intracortical vessels that are smaller than the smallest achievable voxel size, making partial-volume effects an important consideration.

Methods

All animal procedures adhered to the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Zhejiang University. Two macaques were scanned under anesthesia (1–1.5% isoflurane); EtCO2, SPO2 and heart rate were continuously monitored.

Data were acquired using a 7T whole-body human MRI scanner (MAGNETOM, Siemens Healthineers) and a local 16-channel RF receive coil array and a single-channel transmit coil (Figure 1B). Intracortical vessels were imaged using single-slice 2D PC-MRA oriented parallel to and within the cortex, targeting pre-motor (F2) areas (see Fig. 1C): 0.064×0.064×1 mm3, VENC=3 cm/s. Several excitation flip-angles (FAs: 15°, 30°, 45°, 60°, 75° and 90°) were used to examine effects of flow-related enhancement (FRE) on velocity estimation sensitivity (Fig. 1D).

To correct for partial-volume effects and estimate blood velocity (v) and vessel radius (r) of intracortical arterioles and venules, whose sizes were similar to the imaging voxel, the Model-based Analysis of Complex-Difference images (MACD) method5 was applied, assuming an FA-independent slice profile as used in the previous study5. Contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) was defined as the difference of vessel signal (either phase- or magnitude-valued) and the surrounding corresponding tissue signal, divided by the standard deviation of the background in the magnitude-valued image. To quantify the precision of our velocity estimates, corresponding vessels (n = 53) were manually identified in data acquired across two experimental sessions and the Pearson's correlation coefficient of the velocities measured within each individual vessel was calculated.

Results

Fig. 1D demonstrates detection of intracortical vessels. Velocities traveling into (bright dots) or out of (dark dots) the slice were identified, enabling differentiation of arterioles from venules. Images acquired using various FAs show that increasing contrast between putative intracortical arterioles or venules and tissue is observed with a concurrent reduction in tissue SNR. Fig 1E shows CNR of the phase-contrast data increasing with FA. Fig. 1F compares complex-difference and magnitude image reconstructions (with velocity-encoding off) across FAs. CNR of the complex-difference images—presumed to be less affected by partial-volume effects—peaks at FAs of 45–60° then declines for arterioles. While venules show higher values at FAs of 15-60°, likely due to higher noise level in higher FA images. The magnitude images show decreasing intensity for both arterioles and venules with increasing FA, with higher CNR in arterioles than venules.

Data acquired either from two sessions or from two runs (Fig. 2A & B and C & D, respectively) show consistent arrangement of intracortical vessels and values of blood velocity, with spatial patterns of arterial-venous units similar to those seen in human cortex revealed by histology (cf. Fig. 1A). Magnitude (Fig. 2E&G) and complex-difference (Fig. 2F&H) images reveal clear spatial patterns of vessels. The velocities measured within individual vessels are positively correlated between two sessions (arteriole: r = 0.78, p = 3 × 10⁻7; venule: r = 0.62, p = 2 × 10⁻3), indicating good reliability of our method and suggesting that intracortical velocity itself is physiologically stable over time (Fig. 2I&J). MACD enabled estimation of blood velocity and vessel diameters corrected for partial-volume effects. After correction, median velocity of intracortical arterioles was 0.90 cm/s at FA=45°, 0.99 cm/s at FA=60° (Fig. 2K). Median diameter was 0.040 mm and 0.034 mm, respectively (Fig. 2L).

Conclusion

Using a standard human 7T MRI scanner, we non-invasively measured the velocity of intracortical vessels in nonhuman primate cerebral cortex with high-inplane-resolution (64 μm²) PC-MRA. By varying FAs, we identified optimal acquisition parameters. Consistent spatial patterns of intracortical vessels and velocity estimates across sessions demonstrated high precision of our anatomical and physiological imaging. The velocity and diameter of intracortical vessels in macaque cortical area F2 were reported, establishing baseline physiological parameters in the primate brain. This study contributes to an important step towards understanding intracortical vessel function and further develops a non-human primate model for clinical translation.
