Introduction

Photobiomodulation (PBM) uses near-infrared light to stimulate mitochondrial activity and vasodilation, thereby influencing neural activity1. Transcranial (tPBM) and intranasal PBM (iPBM) are two modes of light delivery. tPBM most often delivers light through the frontal scalp and skull, while iPBM utilizes the highly vascularized nasal cavity. It has been suggested that tPBM and iPBM may target different brain regions, but both have been shown to influence cerebral blood flow and the blood-oxygenation level-dependent (BOLD) fMRI signal, but the underlying neurovascular mechanisms have not been quantified. In this work, we use fMRI to quantify not only the spatial location of the tPBM and iPBM responses, but also the potential neurovascular coupling underlying each.

Methods

45 healthy young adults (age 20-32, 23 M/22 F), underwent four simultaneous tPBM-fMRI sessions and four iPBM-fMRI sessions, totalling n=180 datasets for each of tPBM and iPBM. The mean stimulus-locked BOLD and CBF data were extracted from regions of interest (ROIs) derived from an independent component analysis (ICA) and subsequent dual regression, separately for tPBM and iPBM datasets. Percent BOLD and CBF changes relative to baseline were calculated for each ROI, and statistically significant responses during stimulation or pre- and post-stimulation were identified using t-tests. 

To characterize neurovascular coupling, the BOLD and CBF response time courses were fit to a BOLD biophysical model based on the deoxyhemoglobin dilution framework 2 3. The M-value was estimated from the initial time window and used to calculate the neurovascular coupling ratio (n), in subsequent time windows for each ROI. Moreover, the mean BOLD and CBF responses across all subjects were stratified into bins of 6 points when deriving the slope between BOLD and CBF responses. This was done for different time windows in the during- and post-stimulation periods. 

Results

To quantify neurovascular coupling during PBM, we modeled the BOLD-CBF relationship during PBM across the subgenual ROI plus the site of stimulation. The cortical site of stimulation was determined via Monte Carlo simulations4. Coupling dynamics were examined in four temporal windows across stimulus and post-stimulus periods, spanning 2 minutes each. 

One cannot determine from the BOLD signal alone whether changes arise from increased CBF or altered metabolic demand (CMRO2). Thus, applying the biophysical BOLD model allowed us to separate flow-driven responses from those linked to CMRO2. As shown in Fig. 1, the modeling approach captures how coupling evolved across the stimulation period. Data is shown for two of the primary ROIs: the subgenual cortex and the cortical site of stimulation. 

For tPBM, both the n-value and the BOLD-CBF slopes were highest during the early stimulation window (minutes 4-5) and steadily declined thereafter (Fig. 1 and 2). This pattern was observed in both the site of stimulation and the subgenual region. The early rise in n suggests a flow-dominant mechanism, consistent with strong initial increases in CBF driving the BOLD response, followed by progressively reduced metabolic influence relative to CBF as stimulation progressed. In contrast, iPBM produced a higher and more sustained n-value in the subgenual ROI, compared to tPBM. Whereas the stimulation site showed a gradual decrease in both n-value and BOLD-CBF slope over time, the subgenual region demonstrated a sustained elevation in n-value, reaching peak n-value and BOLD-CBF slope values at minutes 8-9. 

Conclusion

The apparent change in n-value  (a declining n-value during stimulation followed by a rise in n-value post-stimulation) suggests that CBF and CMRO2 responses may not be fully coupled. Regionally, these dynamics likely reflect each ROI’s baseline metabolic and electrophysiological profile The regional hemodynamic response to PBM can depend on regional baseline ATP, oxygen and glucose metabolism state as well as oscillatory profile. Regions that consume more oxygen at baseline, or exhibit more high-frequency neural activity (such as gamma rhythm), may show stronger or more sustained hemodynamic effects to PBM. As PBM boosts mitochondrial activity and CBF, if a region is already metabolically active, the hemodynamic effect is amplified. 

Both tPBM and iPBM show a declining n-value and slope during the stimulation periods (Minutes 4-5 and 6-7), however, post stimulation, the metrics differ by PBM application and by ROI. Across techniques, these trends indicate stimulation-specific neurovascular mechanisms: tPBM predominantly evokes an early flow-driven BOLD response, whereas iPBM engages a stronger, sustained metabolic component, particularly in the subgenual. It has been suggested that iPBM can target deep and midline brain structures via the nasal cavity5, where thin bones and rich vascular networks may facilitate greater transmission of photons. Together these temporal responses suggest that tPBM and iPBM modulate BOLD-CBF coupling through distinct neurovascular pathways, with implications for targeted therapeutic applications. 

This study is the first to model the dynamic relationship between CBF and CMRO2 during real time PBM, demonstrating that tPBM and iPBM elicit distinct temporal profiles of neurovascular coupling. These results highlight that PBM mechanisms depend not only on the site of stimulation, but also on the balance between blood flow and metabolism. By modeling the quantitative relationship between CBF and CMRO2, during real-time PBM, this work moves beyond traditional BOLD-only interpretation and provides direct insight into the physiological mechanisms underlying PBM. By clarifying whether PBM affects blood flow, metabolism or both, this approach improves interpretation of fMRI results and can help to distinguish the effects of tPBM and iPBM separately.
