Introduction

Epilepsy is a prevalent neurological disorder, affecting approximately 1% of the global population, with one-third developing drug-resistant epilepsy. For these patients, the primary treatment option is resective surgery targeting the epileptogenic zone (EZ). However, the success rate for epilepsy surgery has stagnated at around 60% for decades. Improving outcomes hinges on better pre-surgical identification of the EZ.

Intracerebral Stereo-EEG (SEEG) offers high spatial resolution but is invasive, limited by implantation sampling bias, and carries risks inherent to a surgical procedure. Conversely, non-invasive scalp EEG is highly attractive because it covers the entire brain, is easy to set up, widely available and used, reproducible, and affordable. However, EEG suffers from limited spatial resolution and struggles to detect deep brain activity. In this work, we aimed to determine whether the spatial resolution of EEG is sufficient for accurate identification of the epileptogenic network. In addition, we sought to measure the limits of scalp EEG in identifying the epileptogenic network depending on its depth from the cortical surface.

Virtual brain twins (VBT) can reproduce relevant biomarkers of brain diseases, such as Parkinson disease (PD), epilepsy, schizophrenia and important society health concerns as aging and have been used for diagnosis and treatment purposes.1 The Virtual Epileptic Patient (VEP) enables to capture important patient-specific features and shows beneficial preliminary results in the clinical trial EPINOV in diagnosing the epileptogenic network responsible of the seizure onsets with SEEG.2 

This research aims at identifying the epileptogenic zones responsible for seizure generation using personalized whole-brain models combined with scalp EEG for drug-resistant epileptic patients.

Methods

The pipeline integrates personalized whole-brain modeling to introduce biophysical information to the analysis of scalp EEG, ultimately improving its ability to localize the epileptogenic zone in refractory epilepsy. A personalized whole-brain model, built from patient T1-MRI and dw-MRI and discretized by the VEP atlas, integrates individual anatomy and the structural connectome. Each brain area is modeled using a Neural Mass Model (NMM) formalism, representing average regional activity, with connections defined by the patient's connectome.

To simulate realistic scalp EEG, a forward model based on the three-layer Boundary Element Method (BEM) is employed, accounting for volume conduction of the brain, the skull and the scalp compartments. Source space is defined using a high-resolution representation incorporating both cortical and subcortical regions. The surface-based cortical sources are modeled at an Icosahedron 5 (ico5) resolution. Subcortical sources are modeled with regional complexity to ensure biophysical fidelity: the hippocampus is represented by a surface-based source model; the amygdala, thalamus, caudate, putamen, and nucleus accumbens sources are modeled as inner volumetric grids with a random orientation; while the pallidum is modeled as an inner volumetric grid whose dipoles are specifically oriented along the principal axis of its surface envelope.3 The sensor space is created accordingly to sensor locations defined by standard electrode configurations of 19-electrodes and 64-electrodes EEG caps. Synthetic EEG signals are generated using patient-specific source time series based on a clinical hypothesis of epileptogenicity. These time series incorporate healthy baseline activity and pathological epileptiform activity, generated using the Epileptor model, within the predetermined epileptogenic zone regions.2

These simulated EEG traces are subsequently processed to extract diagnostic parameters. First, an envelope of the traces is computed, corresponding to the product of sliding log power and sliding standard deviation, simplifying the signals while retaining dynamic information. These smoothed envelope traces are then fitted to a low dimensional Epileptor model by inferring key regional brain parameters using a robust Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) algorithm.4 The inferred parameters reflect critical brain properties such as tissue excitability associated with seizure onset. Finally, Epileptogenic Values (EVs) are derived from these parameters. Brain areas are designated as the potential epileptogenic zone if two criteria are met: (1) their EVs surpass a predefined threshold, and (2) the posterior distribution of the EV is narrow.

Results

The workflow was performed on a cohort of 15 patients from La Timone Hospital in Marseille. All patients experienced spontaneous seizures, which were all recorded by SEEG. The recorded ictal activity was first inferred to produce source-level time series within the virtual brain framework.5 These source time series are then mapped to the EEG-level using the forward solution and the BEM model formalism detailed in the Methods section. The virtual brain twins were constructed for these patients, and their epileptogenic values were inferred. 

A preliminary analysis focused on a subgroup of seven patients with 10 spontaneous seizures exhibiting a cortex-only epileptogenic network. For this specific analysis, the subcortical modeling component was intentionally excluded. The model inversion demonstrated high performance within this subgroup, achieving an average balanced accuracy of 90-95%. Additionally, a sensitivity analysis was performed on one subject by artificially setting the source-level activity to epileptiform in a specific cortical region and healthy elsewhere. The outcome revealed a regional bias in the model's ability to localize the epileptogenic zone, where certain cortical areas were more accurately identified than others, i.e. lower parts and medial wall.

The complete pipeline, comprising both cortical and subcortical brain regions, was finally applied to the full cohort. The performance of the model inversion was compared based on the depth of the ground truth EZ. Results indicate that performance is moderately dependent on whether the EZ is cortical or subcortical, with a high detection of superficial layers. Furthermore, the analysis highlighted a differential detectability, showing that the identification rate varies across different deep brain regions.

Conclusion

Preliminary results from a patient cohort reveal good performance in model inference, with variations based on epileptogenic zone locations and depth of seizure foci. The study underscores the potential of scalp EEG combined with virtual brain twins as a diagnostic tool for drug-resistant epilepsy, offering insights for effective non-invasive diagnostic options. 

Future work will aim to further refine the methodology by exploring more complex forward models, such as using a more detailed BEM model or transitioning to the Finite Element Method (FEM) to incorporate anisotropy for more precise EEG time series generation. Additionally, we plan to apply the inversion pipeline using a high-resolution virtual brain twin to fully leverage the patient-specific anatomical details.
