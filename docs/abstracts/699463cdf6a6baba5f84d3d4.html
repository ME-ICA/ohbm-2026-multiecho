Introduction

Visuospatial neglect is a prevalent and debilitating cognitive deficit following stroke, where patients fail to attend to the side of space (egocentric) or to the side of individual objects (allocentric) opposite to their brain lesion. While egocentric neglect is well documented, object-based deficits remain markedly understudied and clinically underestimated, despite their critical impact on daily life. Here we investigated the behavioral and neural bases of object-based deficits in subacute to chronic stroke patients. Our aims are twofold: (1) To improve the quantification of allocentric impairments by increasing sensitivity and ecological validity, and (2) to characterize the neural correlates of allocentric neglect and more generally of object-based deficits. 

Methods

To achieve these goals, we designed an integrated, multi-technology cognitive battery to probe distinct behavioral components of post-stroke deficits. The battery combines traditional neuropsychological tests, computerized tasks, immersive virtual reality paradigms, and realistic touch based dynamic tasks (Fig. 1a, b). Across modalities, it yields objective quantitative measures of behavior, including task performance, eye- and body-tracking. These behavioral data are integrated with multimodal MRI to examine lesions distribution and associated patterns of structural and functional disconnections. 

Results

Data from 22 stroke patients revealed that 23% of patients show signs of allocentric deficits which were partly undetected with a traditional battery. Principal component analysis across traditional neuropsychological and virtual reality-based behavioral measures (Fig. 1c) identified three dissociable components: (1) constructional/object-based inattention, (2)  exploratory/visuomotor behavior, and (3) perceptive/visuospatial processing. Notably, the third component loaded exclusively on allocentric deficits measured in naturalistic tasks, indicating that our battery captures aspects of visuospatial processing that are overlooked in conventional assessments. Finally, a group-based comparison of lesion distributions showed that patients with allocentric deficits tended to have more focal lesions in right temporo-parietal and subcortical frontal regions, whereas patients without allocentric impairments more often exhibited extensive bilateral subcortical–frontal lesions (Fig. 1d).

Conclusion

These findings indicate that object-based deficits can persist into the subacute and chronic phases post-stroke. By leveraging quantitative, fine-grained behavioral metrics, our results highlight the added value of experimental, ecologically grounded tools for identifying nuanced deficits. This approach holds promises for improving patient stratification, informing functional recovery trajectories and guiding personalized intervention strategies. Ongoing work will expand the sample size and further characterize behavioral profiles and the neural mechanisms underlying these complex disorders, with the long-term goal of optimizing rehabilitation strategies.
