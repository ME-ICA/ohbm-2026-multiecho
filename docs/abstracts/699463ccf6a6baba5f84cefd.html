Introduction

Traditional neuroimaging modalities, including MR relaxometry, diffusion imaging, and functional MRI, provide rich information on the brain’s macroscopic architecture and network dynamics, yet it remains largely insensitive to the underlying molecular and biochemical processes that sustain those networks. In contrast, systems biology and multi-omics approaches, such as transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, reveal microscopic biochemical mechanisms, but are typically restricted to ex vivo samples, lacking spatial temporal continuity at the whole brain scale. Recent ultrafast Magnetic Resonance Spectroscopic Imaging (MRSI), enabled by SPICE technology captures whole-brain multi-metabolite mapping at 3 millimeter resolution within clinically feasible timeframe1,2; Thus, MRSI acquired maps nicely interfaces microscopic neurometabolites and the macroscopic whole brain networks. By treating each voxel as a molecular network observation, MRSI offers the potential to extend the omics paradigm into the living human brain—linking molecular composition with macroscopic organization.

In this study, we leveraged whole brain MRSI to obtain high-resolution maps of key neurometabolites such as N-acetyl aspartate (NAA), Choline (Cho), myo-inositol (mI), and creatine (Cr), and constructed metabolic networks using couplings between each pairs. We further treated MRSI data as a high-dimensional molecular feature space and applied purely unsupervised learning to extract metabolic gradients across the brain and to parcellate the brain into metabolically defined territories. External structural, functional, and molecular brain maps were leveraged to further elucidate the underpinnings of neurometabolic network gradients. 

Methods

Data acquisition: 

In this IRB approved study, 42 healthy volunteers (M/F 23/19, age 21–43) underwent MRSI on a 3.0 T MR system (Biograph mMR; Siemens Healthineers, Forchheim, Germany). All subjects were required to maintain awake with their eyes closed throughout the scan. The MR experimental protocols included high-resolution MRSI scans using SPICE (nominal resolution 2.0 x 3.1 x 3.8 mm3, FOV = 240 x 240 x 160 mm3, TR/TE = 160/1.6 ms, 12 min 35 sec) and T1-weighted anatomical images using MPRAGE (1.0 x 1.0 x 1.0 mm3, TR/TE = 1900/2.44 ms, matrix size = 256 x 256, 192 slices). 
Data processing: 

Reconstruction of the MRSI spatiospectral functions was performed using a union-of-subspaces model, incorporating pre-learned spectral basis functions as described in previous publications.1 Then, spectral quantification was done using an improved LCModel-based algorithm that incorporated both spatial and spectral priors,3 which generated metabolite maps of NAA, Cho, mIn, and Cr. The estimated metabolite concentrations were normalized using water reference to compensate for the B1 inhomogeneity of the head coil used in the experiment. Maps of NAA, Cho, mIn, and Cr were spatially smoothed using a Gaussian kernel with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 4 mm.
Construction of molecular networks at voxel level: 

For each voxel, we took the concentration of each metabolite as a node, and took ratios to compute the edges between each pair. Pairwise ratios among the four metabolites were computed, resulting in twelve ratio maps, which constitute the fundamental elements of the spatial metabolomic profile. Individual metabolic maps were spatially normalized to the MNI space via two-step registrations using first-echo water signal (which is in MRSI native space) and T1-weighted MPRAGE as intermediate registration references. These registrations were employed with affine transformation through the ANTs toolkit (http://stnava.github.io/ANTs/).  Group-averaged maps were then computed for the healthy control cohort.
Macroscale gradients extraction of the whole brain: 

We put molecular network representation in one dimension, and all voxels as another dimension. Principal component analysis (PCA) then extracted dominant components, which together explained more than 90 percent of the variance. The first three components were taken as major gradients for subsequent analysis. 

Fuzzy C-means clustering: 

We employed a voxel-wise fuzzy C-means to classify metabolic profiles across the brain.  To jointly account for metabolic similarity and spatial contiguity, the gradient vectors were concatenated with the normalized MNI-space coordinates (scaled to [0, 1]) before clustering. A weighting parameter of 𝜆=0.02 was used to control the contribution of spatial distance. Candidate cluster numbers (𝑘=3–20) were evaluated; the optimal 𝑘 maximized the mean silhouette indices. For each 𝑘, k-means with squared Euclidean distance was run with 100 random starts, and the minimum-WCSS solution was retained. For visualization, each cluster probability map was further smoothed using a 3D Gaussian kernel (3 mm, 𝜎=1).
Statistical tests: 

Each dominant gradient was projected onto the cortical surface and compared with degree centrality maps from functional and structural connectivity based on publicly available Human Connectome Project (HCP) data.4 We also leveraged data from MitoBrainMap5 to investigate whether mitochondrial density and proteins of the respiratory chain is associated with metabolic gradients. Briefly, MitoBrainMap quantified mitochondrial phenotypes across hundreds of 3×3×3 mm tissue voxels—OXPHOS complex activities (CI/CII/CIV), citrate synthase (CS), and mtDNA—and produced whole-brain estimates in MNI space via MRI-guided regression. We used spin permutation tests to assess the robustness of correlation significance.

Results

Three dominant whole-brain metabolic gradients are shown in Figure 2A in triplanar views. With unsupervised clustering, five optimal clusters were identified by maximizing mean silhouette index. Gray matter (clusters 1,2,4), white matter (cluster 3), and cerebellum (cluster 5) were classified into different clusters; further, the superficial cortex was separated from deep cortical layers (Figure 2B). 

On the macroscopic level, gradient 1 was found to have strong association with functional connectivity (r= 0.77, Pspin < 0.001), but only moderately with structural connectivity (r = 0.22, Pspin = 0.08); gradient 2 showed weak negative associations with both functional connectivity (r= -0.32, Pspin = 0.079) and structural connectivity (r= -0.22, Pspin = 0.071)  (Figure 2C). On molecular level, we found that the gradient 1 is strongly associated with mitochondrial density (r = 0.57, Pspin < 0.001) , complex I (r = 0.32, Pspin < 0.01), and complex II (r = 0.62, Pspin < 0.001) of the respiratory chain (Figure 2D). 

Conclusion

In this study, we have demonstrated in vivo unsupervised brain metabolomic imaging framework using ultrafast whole-brain MRSI. The results revealed distinct molecular profiles among anatomical regions and uncover coherent metabolic gradients aligned with brain network organization and mitochondrial architecture. By parcellating the brain into naturally emerging metabolic territories using unsupervised learning, we demonstrated that metabolic organization showed coherent, biologically meaningful structure beyond conventional anatomical or functional boundaries. Looking forward, higher spectral resolution with sequence advances or higher field with expanded metabolite coverage may provide finer parcellations and improved sensitivity to subtle neurochemical variations in the brain. Whole-brain MRSI may serve as a scalable and integrative modality for linking molecular biology to systems-level brain organization in vivo.
