Introduction

     Subjective pain ratings depend on both an individual’s interpretation of rating scales and the degree of engagement during reporting, introducing variability unrelated to underlying neural processes. Complementary methods that do not rely exclusively on self-report may therefore improve pain quantification. Descending pain-modulation networks centred on the periaqueductal gray (PAG), rostral ventromedial medulla (RVM), locus coeruleus (LC), parabrachial nucleus (PBN), and other brainstem nuclei dynamically regulates spinal nociceptive processing and pain sensitivity in humans 1. These interconnected regions integrate sensory, autonomic, and cognitive-affective information to shape pain experiences.

     Functional MRI (fMRI) work from Stroman and colleagues has demonstrated robust BOLD responses to noxious heat within brainstem and spinal cord networks2, and shown that connectivity within these systems varies with individual pain sensitivity, chronic pain status, and expectation-based modulation of pain 3,4,5. This highlights the importance of autonomic and modulatory structures such as the hypothalamus, PAG, nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS), PBN, and thalamus in moment-to-moment pain processing 2,3,4.

     Eye-tracking provides a continuous, objective measure of overt visual attention and task engagement, yet how gaze behaviour during pain reporting relates to BOLD activity in central pain-modulation circuits remains unexplored. Building on prior work linking continuous pain ratings to brainstem and subcortical regions involved in sensory activity, the present study investigates whether engagement with the pain scale – operationalized through fine-grained gaze position – predicts BOLD activity in regions supporting sensory integration, autonomic regulation, and descending control 2,3,4. We hypothesized that engagement-related gaze patterns would correlate with BOLD activity in these regions, providing a novel neurobehavioral marker that complements subjective pain ratings.

Methods

     Participants completed fMRI scanning while receiving three calibrated thermal stimuli (38°C, 46°C, 51°C) and providing continuous pain ratings. Multiple stimulation blocks were administered, and eye-tracking data were collected simultaneously using an EyeLink 1000 Plus system. Gaze position was converted into a continuous engagement metric reflecting moment-to-moment interaction with the rating scale. All behavioural signals were time-aligned with the fMRI time series at the TR level.

     Analyses for the present abstract focused on the highest-intensity stimulus (51°C), which provided the clearest engagement-related fluctuations. Time-aligned gaze and BOLD signals were evaluated using correlation analyses to identify regions whose activity covaried with visual engagement, with pain ratings incorporated as a covariate where appropriate.

     Region-wise BOLD signals were extracted from 50 brainstem and subcortical structures implicated in sensory integration and descending pain modulation, spanning the C6RD, dorsal reticular nucleus (DRt), hypothalamus, locus coeruleus (LC), nucleus gigantocellularis (NGc), nucleus raphe magnus (NRM), nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS), periaqueductal gray (PAG), parabrachial nucleus (PBN), and thalamus. Statistical significance was assessed using region-wise p-values derived from the correlation analyses.

Results

Engagement-related gaze patterns showed significant associations with BOLD activity across a subset of pain-relevant brainstem and subcortical regions (Figure 1). Region-wise statistical testing during the 51°C stimulus condition revealed the strongest effects in the hypothalamus (region 14; p = 0.0006), NGc (regions 22-23; p = 0.0055, 0.0086), NTS (region 35; p = 0.0324), PAG (region 37; p = 0.0475), and thalamus (regions 45-47; p = 0.0006, 0.0425, and 0.0000). Additional trending effects appeared in regions 10, 24, 35, 37, 46, and 49.

     Across regions, gaze-BOLD relationships included both positive and negative correlations, indicating heterogeneous engagement-related modulation across descending and integrative pain circuits. These results demonstrate that visual engagement with the pain scale is systemically associated with neural activity in specific pain-relevant structures, capturing variance in BOLD responses that is not fully explained by pain ratings alone.

Conclusion

     This study provides initial evidence that engagement with the pain scale predicts BOLD activity in multiple brainstem and subcortical pain-relevant regions. Significant associations in the hypothalamus, NGc, NTS, PAG, and thalamus suggest that attentional state may influence modulatory and integrative components of the pain experience during fMRI. These preliminary findings highlight gaze behavior as a promising, objective complement to subjective pain ratings and support the broader goal of developing more precise neurobehavioral markers of pain. Ongoing work is refining these analyses and exploring individual differences, but the present results establish a robust foundation for understanding engagement-related neural dynamics during pain reporting.
