Introduction

Understanding the functional organization of the brain is central to neuroscience, with descriptors ranging from parcellations and gradients to connectivity modeling. While such methods can be applied to either task-based or resting-state fMRI, resting-state has become the dominant approach due to its ease of acquisition and analysis. However, the brain is fundamentally an organ of behavior, and characterizing its organization during active cognitive states, like task performance, may offer more direct insights. While task-based approaches are well-suited for this, they impose a risk of biasing the fMRI responses to particular task sets. Multi-task fMRI paradigms offer a richer sampling of cognitive states and may therefore present a solution. Yet, multi-task and resting-state approaches have not been systematically compared in their ability to capture functional brain organization. This study presents a first systematic comparison of matched-duration resting-state and multi-task fMRI data across several preprocessing pipelines and validation metrics.

Methods

Methods

We used 20 minutes of resting-state and task-based data each, acquired from the same seventeen subjects in the Multi-Domain Task Battery dataset (MDTB, ). The multi-task data comprised of 17 tasks tapping into cognitive, motor, perceptual, and social functions. During the resting-state session, subjects fixated on a fixation cross while letting their mind wander.

Data were preprocessed using SPM and Freesurfer with a minimal preprocessing pipeline including motion correction, registration to structural data, and cortical surface mapping. We then applied filtering steps commonly used in the field to compare their effects on the data (Figure 1). These were independent component analysis (ICA) based cleaning and connectivity fingerprinting. For connectivity fingerprinting, we used previously estimated connectivity weight maps , consisting of 32 neocortical networks. We regressed the 32 group network spatial maps into each subject’s data, to obtain subject-specific network time courses and calculated connectivity as the Pearson’s correlations of each voxel time series with each cortical network time course. For task data, we additionally calculated connectivity fingerprints on the residual time series after regressing out task-related fluctuations. Finally, we estimated beta maps using a general linear model (GLM) implemented in SPM12. Neocortical data was averaged within 1442 icosahedrons.

As sufficient statistics from which parcellation methods, gradient methods and connectivity models derive, we calculated voxel-by-voxel covariance matrices on the data. For this, we mean-centered the data for each voxel run-wise before calculating a covariance matrix for each subject and each data type. We calculated a neocortical, a cerebellar and a cortico-cerebellar covariance matrix, but focus our reporting here on the neocortical data for brevity. Cerebellar and cortico-cerebellar data showed a similar pattern of results and will be presented in full at OHBM.

We first examined split-half reliability of the covariance matrices, by calculating Pearson correlation between the vectorized covariance matrices estimated from two halves of the data (10 minutes scan time each).

While reliable data is necessary for capturing functional brain organization, it is not sufficient. In fact, high reliability can be driven by shared noise between adjacent structures, for example superior cerebellar voxels and the directly adjacent inferior occipital lobe, which are physically close but not functionally coupled . We used this characteristic of cerebellar data to examine the extent to which different data is influenced by these spurious correlations. We calculated a measure of internal validity as the normalized ratio between superior cerebellar and neocortical adjacent voxel correlations and superior cerebellar voxels and the rest of the neocortex as , with positive values indicating higher internal validity.

A common concern with using a task-based approach is that the resulting functional activity will be biased towards the task set. We hypothesized that any bias towards a particular task state should reduce with inclusion of more tasks into the battery. We randomly sampled task batteries of 2, 3, 6, 10 and 13 tasks from 17 possible tasks, with 20 minutes scan time each. We then correlated the resulting covariance matrices with all other covariance matrices of the same task battery size.

The ultimate goal of describing functional organization is to generalize to a wide variety of states. We therefore evaluated the ability of the covariance matrices derived from different data types in predicting functional organization in a separate task session of 9 new tasks with 144 minutes scan time. We calculated similarity as the Pearson’s correlation of the vectorized covariance matrices.

Results

ResultsTask betas have low split-half reliability

Although all data types showed some reliability (all , one-sample t-tests all , all Bonferroni-adjusted ), we observed significant differences between the data types.

Connectivity fingerprints calculated on task and rest data showed highest reliability followed by the cleaned task and rest time series, and then by the raw time series and task betas. Task betas showed lower split-half reliability than all other data types, apart from the raw task and rest time series.

Task betas have highest internal validity

As a sanity check, we first calculated internal validity for crossed task betas, where the neocortical and cerebellar beta maps were acquired in different runs, as these pose the most stringent test of internal validity. As expected, we found positive internal validity for the crossed betas (mean: 0.60 ± 0.46, one-sample t-test against 0: , ). Next, we calculated internal validity for data from the same runs. We found that task betas showed the highest internal validity, exceeding the internal validity of all other data types (mean 0.147 ± 0.142, all , all Bonferroni-adjusted ). This confirmed that despite its relatively low split-half reliability, task betas were least influenced by proximal noise correlations.

Covariance patterns stabilize with more tasks

A multi-dimensional scaling (MDS) plot shows the first two dimensions of the similarity space of the covariance matrices (Figure 2a). As hypothesized, we found that the similarity of task batteries increased with task battery size (Figure 2b)., with similarity plateauing at 6 tasks sampled (significant difference of 6 tasks to 3 and 2 tasks: all , , but no significant difference to 10, 13 or 17 tasks: all , ). The influence of the specific task set therefore reduced with the number of tasks.

Task predicts unseen brain states the best

A multi-dimensional scaling (MDS) plot shows the first two dimensions of the similarity space of the covariance matrices (Figure 2c). We observed that cleaning the data reduced the distance to the new tasks: cleaned time series data showed higher similarity with the evaluation task set than raw time series data numerically (Figure 2d). Connectivity fingerprinted data, however, showed significantly lower similarity than cleaned time series data (rest: , ; task: , ). Task time series data consistently captured functional organization better than rest data (rest vs. task data types: all , all Bonferroni-adjusted ). Finally, task betas captured functional organization in the evaluation task set best, showing highest numerical similarity to the new tasks. This difference was significant for all data types apart from the cleaned task time series (all , all Bonferroni-adjusted ; cleaned task time series: , Bonferroni-adjusted ).

Conclusion

We here show that rest or task-based data modality and filtering choices strongly influence the ability of fMRI data to capture functional organization. While time series data shows higher split-half reliability than task betas, especially when subjected to cleaning, task betas are much more robust to shared noise correlations. Further, task batteries of few tasks bias the resulting activity strongly towards the task set, but including more tasks mitigates this influence. Finally, we find that task shows a substantial advantage in predicting functional organization in a separate deep phenotyping session of unobserved task states.
