Introduction

One of the primary motives of communication is to reduce uncertainty. Uncertainty Reduction Theory (URT), by Charles R. Berger and Richard J. Calabrese, states that people seek info in initial interactions to predict behaviors, ease anxiety, avoid errors, and build trust [1]. However, modern information can increase uncertainty. Critics like Kellermann and Reynolds challenge URT's idea that high uncertainty drives information-seeking, as new data may add complexities [3]. This is key in computer-mediated communication (CMC), with social media causing overload, contradictions, and manipulation [4; 5].

Our study examines manipulative content's effect on uncertainty reduction, focusing on Need for Cognition (NFC) differences. Per Cacioppo and Petty's Elaboration Likelihood Model (ELM), high-NFC prefer central route (logical analysis), low-NFC use peripheral cues like emotions [6; 7]. Paradoxically, high-NFC individuals may face increased uncertainty when exposed to content that exploits their analytical tendencies, such as complex but misleading arguments or data manipulation. Similarly, UT reflects an individual’s ability to cope with ambiguous or unpredictable information; those with low UT may experience greater discomfort and engage in more effortful processing when faced with uncertainty [11].

Thus, to examine how manipulative content interacts with NFC during uncertainty reduction, we designed an experiment with three types of stimuli: cognitive manipulations (exploiting logic), affective manipulations (exploiting emotions), and neutral control texts.Uncertainty reduction measured via reading cessation and eye-tracking: fixations count/duration, saccades count/velocity, blinks. Hypotheses: 1) Manipulative slows reduction vs. neutral. 2) Cognitive faster for high-NFC than affective. 3) Affective faster for low-NFC than cognitive. 4) Neutral faster for high-NFC than low-NFC. 5) Higher UT will show less oculomotor arousal and faster reduction in uncertain contexts

Methods

METHODOLOGY

  1. Participants

For the study, 42 right-handed participants were recruited via online advertisement. The final sample included 42 individuals (36 females, 6 males; aged 18-40, M=22.51, SD=3.09). All had normal or corrected vision and hearing, were university-educated, and reported no neurological impairments.

  1. Stimuli

To ensure ecological validity while maintaining experimental control, AI-generated stimuli consisted of three blocks per condition, each with 50 messages themed around a specific natural disaster (e.g., forest fire, landslide, extreme heat). Total: 650 messages—200 for cognitive (4 blocks of 50), 200 for affective (4 blocks of 50), and 250 for neutral (5 blocks of 50). A separate neutral block (50 messages) was used for training purposes, allowing participants to familiarize themselves with the procedure without exposure to manipulative content, thereby reducing potential learning effects during experimental trials. Blocks combined theme and manipulation type (e.g., 50 flood texts with cognitive manipulations) and were presented in randomized order.

Texts were equalized for readability using Flesch-Kincaid to control lexical influences. Topic sequences (scale, geography, cause, duration, etc.) were identical across conditions. For ecological validity, texts mimicked Telegram channel screenshots. Affective manipulations included emotional expressions, value appeals, sensationalism, and fear inducement. Cognitive manipulations featured false causality, slippery slope, overgeneralization, false analogy, and epistemic cues.

  1. Procedure

Participants sat in a lab equipped for eye-tracking. Stimuli appeared on a 24-inch ASUS VG248QE monitor (1920x1080 resolution, 1ms response, 144Hz refresh, 22pt Courier New font). PsychoPy handled presentation. Eye movements were recorded via EyeLink 1000+ (SR Research, 2024) at 1000Hz with chin support. Only dominant eye tracked at 1000Hz. Saccades and fixations detected using SR Research's algorithm in Data Viewer.

Participants were told the study examined reactions to natural disasters. They read texts carefully and pressed spacebar when ready to answer questions, ending the block. Post-block, screen questions were answered orally (not analyzed/recorded; used for engagement). After 13 blocks, participants completed the Need for Cognition (NFC) questionnaire to measure analytical thinking tendency. However, 4 individuals did not complete the questionnaires, resulting in a sample of 38 respondents for correlation analyses.

  1. Behavioral Data Processing

Texts read per block were averaged by participant and condition. One-way repeated-measures ANOVA tested manipulation type (cognitive, affective, neutral as control) as independent variable, followed by pairwise t-tests.

Average reading time per condition was compared via one-way repeated-measures ANOVA and pairwise t-tests. This was preliminary; future analysis will use mixed-effects linear regression [10] to assess reading time dynamics across blocks.

  1. Eye-Tracking Data Processing

Preliminary analysis used metrics for uncertainty: fixations (count, duration per trial), saccades (count, length, duration, average velocity per trial), and blink count. Metrics computed per trial, averaged per block. Future: evaluate parameter slopes from block start to end.

One-way repeated-measures ANOVA compared averaged parameters between conditions. Pearson correlations examined relations with NFC and UT scores, separately for manipulative (cognitive/affective) and non-manipulative trials.

Results

RESULTS

A one-way repeated-measures ANOVA showed no significant effect of text type (affective manipulative, cognitive manipulative, or neutral) on number of texts read , F(2, 82) = 0.828, p = 0.4404, η_p² = 0.020. Pearson correlations indicated no significant links between Need for Cognition (NFC) and number of texts read overall (r = -0.132, p = 0.161) or by condition (all |r| < 0.155, p > 0.352). Uncertainty tolerance (UT) also showed no associations (overall r = 0.000, p = 1.000; by condition |r| < 0.058, p > 0.731). No significant reading time effect in analysis. Despite the absence of significant differences in reading time and the number of texts read across conditions, eye-tracking metrics revealed distinct patterns of visual processing. This dissociation suggests that while overt reading behavior remained similar, covert cognitive processing differed significantly between manipulative and neutral texts.

One-way repeated-measures ANOVAs showed significant text type effects on oculomotor metrics, indicating different processing for manipulative vs. neutral texts, not due to linguistic differences (see below). A significant main effect was found for saccade count, F(2, 82) = 5.26, p = 0.0071, η_p² = 0.114. Post-hoc tests revealed more saccades in affective manipulative trials than neutral, t(41) = 2.74, p_corr = 0.0268, d_z = +0.42, with a trend for more in cognitive manipulative trials versus neutral, t(41) = 2.47, p_corr = 0.0521, d_z = +0.38; no difference between affective and cognitive. Average saccade duration showed a significant effect, F(2, 82) = 6.43, p = 0.0025, η_p² = 0.13, with shorter durations in affective versus neutral, t(41) = -3.61, p_corr = 0.0024, d_z = -0.55, and a trend for shorter in cognitive versus neutral, t(41) = -2.12, p_corr = 0.1203, d_z = -0.32; no difference between affective and cognitive. Saccade distance exhibited a strong main effect, F(2, 82) = 12.08, p < 0.0001, η_p² = 0.22, with shorter distances in affective, t(41) = -4.57, p_corr = 0.0001, d_z = -0.70, and cognitive manipulative trials versus neutral, t(41) = -3.62, p_corr = 0.0024, d_z = -0.56; no difference between affective and cognitive. Fixation count showed a significant effect, F(2, 82) = 5.86, p = 0.0042, η_p² = 0.12, with more fixations in affective versus neutral, t(41) = 2.83, p_corr = 0.0210, d_z = +0.43, and cognitive versus neutral, t(41) = 2.62, p_corr = 0.0364, d_z = +0.40; no difference between affective and cognitive. Average fixation duration had a significant overall effect, F(2, 82) = 3.32, p = 0.0408, η_p² = 0.07, but no pairwise comparisons survived correction, suggesting a weak trend toward shorter fixations in manipulative conditions. No significant effects emerged for blink count, F(2, 82) = 0.42, p = 0.6534, η_p² = 0.01, or average saccade velocity, F(2, 82) = 0.50, p = 0.6036, η_p² = 0.01. Pearson correlations revealed significant negative associations between NFC and average summed saccade duration (overall r = -0.316, p = 0.001; particularly in neutral, r = -0.374, p = 0.021*), and average fixation duration (overall r = -0.274, p = 0.003; in neutral, r = -0.328, p = 0.044). A weak negative correlation was observed between UT and average saccade velocity overall (r = -0.185, p = 0.048). No other significant correlations emerged.

Stimuli analyzed via Textometr API for confounders. Mann-Whitney U tests showed no differences in word count (p > 0.066), character count (p > 0.753), lexical density (p > 0.099), complexity (p > 0.363). Other differences not aligning with eye-tracking (affective = cognitive ≠ neutral): sentence count affective > neutral > cognitive (p < 0.004); mean word length cognitive < affective/neutral (p < 0.022); mean sentence length cognitive > neutral > affective (p < 0.015); Flesch-Kincaid cognitive > affective, neutral > affective (p < 0.002); structural complexity neutral > cognitive > affective (p < 0.039); narrativity/descriptiveness cognitive/affective > neutral (p < 0.048).

Conclusion

CONCLUSION 

The analysis found no significant differences in the number of texts read and reading time across experimental conditions. This indicates that manipulative content does not influence the overall volume of information consumed, which does not support our behavioral hypothesis regarding increased engagement.

No significant effect was observed for reading time in the updated analysis, contrasting with preliminary trends. However, eye-tracking metrics revealed differential processing. Significant effects emerged for several oculomotor measures, suggesting greater visual attention to manipulative texts compared to neutral ones. Specifically, there were more saccades in affective manipulative trials versus neutral, t(41) = 2.745, p_corr = 0.0268*, d_z = +0.424, with a trend for cognitive manipulative trials versus neutral, t(41) = 2.479, p_corr = 0.0521, d_z = +0.383. Shorter average saccade durations were found in affective versus neutral, t(41) = -3.616, p_corr = 0.0024**, d_z = -0.558, with a trend for cognitive versus neutral, t(41) = -2.120, p_corr = 0.1203, d_z = -0.327. Saccade distances were shorter in both affective, t(41) = -4.575, p_corr = 0.0001***, d_z = -0.706, and cognitive manipulative trials versus neutral, t(41) = -3.626, p_corr = 0.0024**, d_z = -0.560. More fixations occurred in affective versus neutral, t(41) = 2.839, p_corr = 0.0210*, d_z = +0.438, and cognitive versus neutral, t(41) = 2.625, p_corr = 0.0364*, d_z = +0.405. Average fixation duration showed a significant overall effect, F(2, 82) = 3.327, p = 0.0408, η_p² = 0.075, but no pairwise differences survived correction, indicating a weak trend toward shorter fixations in manipulative conditions. No effects were found for blink count or saccade velocity. These findings suggest that both types of manipulative content demand more visual and cognitive attention than neutral texts, potentially through different mechanisms, and the effects were not attributable to linguistic differences in the stimuli.

The hypothesis concerning the relationship between Need for Cognition (NFC) and behavioral measures of uncertainty reduction was not supported, as no significant correlations were found with reading timet. However, NFC showed significant negative associations with average summed saccade duration (overall r = -0.316, p = 0.001***; particularly in neutral, r = -0.374, p = 0.021*) and average fixation duration (overall r = -0.274, p = 0.003**; in neutral, r = -0.328, p = 0.044*), suggesting that higher NFC may lead to more efficient processing in certain metrics. Uncertainty tolerance (UT) had a weak negative correlation with average saccade velocity overall (r = -0.185, p = 0.048*). These individual differences warrant further exploration.

This study demonstrates that manipulative content - both cognitive and affective - disrupts normal information processing by increasing oculomotor engagement, even when overt reading behavior remains unchanged.
Key findings partially support our hypotheses:

  1. Hypothesis 1 (Manipulative content slows uncertainty reduction) is supported by eye-tracking data but not by behavioral measures.

  2. Hypotheses 2–5 regarding NFC a differences were not clearly supported, though NFC correlated with certain efficiency metrics (e.g., shorter fixations)

In summary, the results indicate that manipulative content, both affective and cognitive, elicits greater oculomotor engagement compared to neutral texts, likely reflecting increased cognitive processing. However, behavioral measures like text volume and reading time showed no differences. The role of individual differences, such as NFC, appears limited but present in specific eye-tracking metrics. Future studies with advanced modeling may further elucidate dynamic processes in uncertainty reduction and the influence of manipulative content.
