Introduction

Explicit emotion regulation through cognitive reappraisal involves intentionally reinterpreting negative stimuli to lessen their emotional impact and   reduces limbic responses via top-down input from frontal control regions 1,2. Because this strategy relies on language, the language in which it is carried out may affect its effectiveness in regulating negative emotions. Prior neuroimaging work suggests that implicit (unintentional) emotion regulation is disrupted in the non-native (L2) context3. In contrast, behavioral and psychophysiological findings suggest that using a foreign language may facilitate explicit reappraisal by increasing psychological distance from aversive stimuli4. However, the neural correlates of performing cognitive reappraisal in a non-native language remain unknown. Here, we investigate how neural responses within regions implicated in emotion regulation differ when reappraisal is implemented in L1 versus L2.

Methods

Sixty unbalanced Spanish-English bilinguals were assigned to complete the task in either L1 Spanish (Native group; n = 30) or L2 English (Foreign group; n = 30), the groups were matched on age and sex. Participants performed a block-design cognitive reappraisal task adapted from Phan et al.5 using highly arousing, negative IAPS images (e.g., accidents, violence, war) presented via MRI-compatible goggles (VisuaStim) with E-Prime 3.0. During MAINTAIN blocks, participants simply viewed the images and allowed negative affect to arise naturally. In SUPPRESS (cognitive reappraisal) blocks, participants were instructed to down-regulate negative affect by reminding themselves, in the task language, that the scenes were not real and were part of a movie, and thus attempt to distance themselves from the unpleasant images. Each block began with 5 grey fixation screens (4 s each; 20 s total), followed by a condition cue (“MAINTAIN” or “SUPPRESS”; 4 s), then 5 negative images (4 s each; 20 s total). After each block, participants provided a spoken rating of negative affect intensity on a 1–5 scale (4 s), and responses were recorded with an MRI-adapted microphone. The task lasted 8 min and included 5 MAINTAIN blocks and 5 SUPPRESS blocks, presented in pseudo-randomized order. 

fMRI data were acquired on a 3T GE Signa Architect scanner. T1-weighted structural images were collected using a BRAVO sequence (TR/TE = 7.4/2.9 ms; voxel size = 0.5×1×1 mm). Functional images were acquired with multiband gradient-echo EPI (multiband factor = 2; TR/TE = 2000/23.5 ms; voxel size = 3.75×3.75×3.0 mm). Data were preprocessed in SPM12 (MATLAB), including motion correction, distortion correction (HySCO), coregistration of the T1 to the mean EPI, segmentation, normalization to MNI space (3-mm isotropic), and spatial smoothing (6-mm FWHM). First-level GLMs included MAINTAIN and SUPPRESS regressors convolved with the canonical HRF, six motion parameters as nuisance regressors, and a high-pass filter (263 s). Subject-level contrasts were computed for MAINTAIN > SUPPRESS comparison and used for ROI analyses. 

Guided by prior meta-analytic evidence showing that cognitive reappraisal is associated with decreased activity in bilateral amygdala and insula and increased activity in the right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, left posterior parietal cortex, posterior cingulate cortex, right posterior parietal cortex, and left posterior dorsomedial prefrontal cortex2, we defined these regions as a priori anatomical ROIs and examined between-group differences in the MAINTAIN > SUPPRESS contrast. 

Results

ROI analysis showed significantly stronger down-regulation of neural activity in the Native (L1) vs. the Foreign (L2) group for left insula (t(58) = 2.84, p =  .006, d = 0.73, mean difference = 0.1107, 95% CI [0.0326, 0.1889]), left amygdala (t(58) = 2.60, p =  .012, d = 0.67, mean difference = 0.1111, 95% CI [0.0255, 0.1967]), and right amygdala (t(58) = 2.69, p =  .009, d = 0.70, mean difference = 0.1156, 95% CI [0.0297, 0.2016]). The effect for the right insula was marginally significant (t(58) = 1.87, p =  .067, d = 0.48, 95% CI [-0.0048, 0.1389]). 

Due to technical issues, negative affect ratings were available only for 18 participants in the L1 group and 19 participants in the L2 group. Within this subsample, behavioral ratings mirrored the neural pattern: affective ratings did not differ during MAINTAIN condition (p = .896), but the L2 group reported higher negative affect during SUPPRESS condition (L1: M = 2.55, SD = 0.79; L2: M = 3.13, SD = 0.64; t(35) = -2.47, p = .019, mean difference = -0.586, 95% CI [-1.068, -0.105], d = 0.81), consistent with less effective regulation in L2.

Conclusion

These findings provide the first neuroimaging evidence that explicit emotion regulation through cognitive reappraisal is less effective when implemented in a non-native (L2) context, as evidenced by higher responses in amygdala and insula despite instruction to reappraise. These results are consistent with prior reports indicating that non-native language context disrupts emotion regulation3. More broadly, the results suggest that language context is a critical dimension of emotion regulation in bilinguals and should be taken into account when designing experimental paradigms and, potentially, therapeutic interventions that rely on verbal reappraisal.
