Introduction

Motor anticipation relies on predictive coding mechanisms that integrate evolving kinematic cues with internal motor representations, enabling the brain to infer forthcoming actions within tens of milliseconds. Temporal occlusion paradigms provide a controlled framework to probe how predictive computations unfold as visual information becomes increasingly limited. Although behavioral studies consistently point to the crucial contribution of late kinematic cues, the oscillatory mechanisms that transform dynamic visual evidence into anticipatory motor plans remain poorly characterized. Here, we used high-density Electroencephalography (EEG) to examine how theta, alpha, and beta oscillations encode early evaluative processing, sensorimotor resonance, and decision-related integration during action observation. By systematically manipulating the timing at which movement information about an unfolding stroke became unavailable, we sought to map the temporal architecture through which large-scale cortical networks support predictive action monitoring.

Methods

High-density EEG (128 channels, 250 Hz) was recorded from 25 expert tennis players while they observed temporally occluded videos of forehand and backhand groundstrokes. Each video was cut at one of six time points relative to ball–racket contact (−320, −240, −160, −80, 0, +80 ms). After occlusion, participants indicated the predicted landing location of the ball. All EEG recordings were preprocessed with 0.5–40 Hz filtering, artifact correction via ICA, and segmentation from −2000 ms before video onset through the motor response. Time–frequency decomposition (2–40 Hz, Morlet wavelets) quantified event-related changes in theta (3–5 Hz), alpha (8–12 Hz), and beta (13–25 Hz) power, expressed as ERD/ERS relative to a pre-stimulus baseline. Six regions of interest were defined (frontal, left/right sensorimotor, centro-parietal, left/right posterior). Anticipatory dynamics were analyzed using four observation windows (T1: 0–300 ms, T2: 300–600 ms, T3: 600–900 ms, T4: 900–1200 ms) and two post-video windows (T5: 0–300 ms after offset; T6: last 300 ms before response). Repeated-measures ANOVAs assessed the effects of Time, Region, and Occlusion. To evaluate the behavioral relevance of oscillatory activity, correlations were computed between ERD/ERS values and the inverse efficiency score (IES), combining accuracy and response time. Only correct trials were included to ensure that EEG activity reflected valid anticipatory computations.

Results

Behaviorally, prediction accuracy increased systematically as occlusion approached or exceeded contact, and response times decreased accordingly, demonstrating the critical value of late kinematic cues. 

ANOVA designs on ERD/ERS values consistently showed significant main effects of Time and Occlusion on theta, alpha, and beta activity, as well as significant Time × Region interactions (all p < .05).  Early in the observation window (T1–T2), theta power showed significant task-related increases in centro-parietal regions compared with the control condition, indicating early engagement of predictive evaluation mechanisms. Middle periods (T3–T4) elicited robust alpha and beta  (desynchronization across sensorimotor and posterior regions, consistent with the recruitment of action-observation and sensorimotor resonance systems. Immediately before video offset, the +80 ms condition produced the strongest theta synchronization across multiple areas (the only occlusion preserving complete contact information), highlighting theta’s role in integrating high-informational kinematic evidence.

During the post-video period, frontal and sensorimotor theta increased further, marking the transition from perceptual inference to motor preparation. Theta power during T5 was negatively correlated with IES across frontal, sensorimotor, centro-parietal, and posterior regions, indicating that stronger theta engagement was associated with more efficient anticipation. Beta desynchronization intensified during T6, indicating the release of motor inhibition before response execution.

Conclusion

The results outline a hierarchical temporal architecture for motor anticipation. Early theta synchronization supports predictive evaluation and top-down allocation of attentional resources as soon as movement cues appear. Mid-stage alpha and beta desynchronization reflect progressive sensorimotor coupling to the observed action, indexing the internal simulation of unfolding kinematics. Late theta activity, strongest when full contact information is available, provides an electrophysiological signature of evidence integration immediately before commitment to a predictive response. Finally, the increase in theta and the decrease in beta during the post-video period demonstrate the convergence of perceptual inference and motor preparation, linking cortical dynamics directly to behavioral efficiency. These findings provide mechanistic evidence that predictive coding principles operate at fine temporal scales during action observation, and that theta oscillations form a key neural substrate for transforming kinematic evidence into anticipatory motor plans.
