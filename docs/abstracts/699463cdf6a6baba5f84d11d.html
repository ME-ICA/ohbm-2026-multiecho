Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) involves progressive accumulation of the tau protein, accompanied by reductions in functional network integrity and cortical thickness, yet it remains unclear how these processes are related. Prior evidence suggests that tau spreads along axonal connections and preferentially affects association networks, but few studies quantify these relationships at a network-resolved level, particularly across the diagnostic spectrum of Cognitively Normal (CN), Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI), and AD groups1.

Functional Connectivity and Network Segregation are sensitive indicators of not only aging, but also AD-related network decline2. Additionally, cortical grey matter thickness provides a complementary structural marker that is well established to decline globally due to normal aging, but may be accelerated by biological changes that occur in AD3. The degree to which regional tau burden disrupts segregation and thickness within specific cortical systems, such as the Association System (Cingulo-Opercular (CGO), Dorsal Attention (DAN), Default Mode (DMN), Fronto-Parietal (FPN), Retrosplenial Temporal (RST) and Ventral Attention (VAN) Networks) or Sensory System (Auditory (AUD), Visual (VIS), and Somatomotor (SMM, SMH) Networks), remains poorly characterized.

In this project, we utilize a multimodal imaging framework (Tau PET, resting-state fMRI, and structural MRI) to model network-specific changes due to tau, segregation, within-network connectivity, and cortical thickness using cortical networks as defined based on the Gordon atlas4,5. This method of analysis enables identification of network-vulnerability to different factors that may be responsible for declines and provides the foundation for future studies to determine how functional and biological markers may mediate or moderate global or network vulnerability before clinical impairment.

Methods

Our data were collected from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) database. To capture the most accurate sample, we constrained our search to only include participants whose full set of imaging data was collected between two timepoints in which the diagnosis was unchanged (i.e. if Subject X was diagnostically evaluated to be MCI on 01/01/2022 but their status on 01/01/2023 had progressed to AD, imaging data collected between those two dates was not eligible for analysis). This allowed us to assume that participants were not undergoing significant disease related changes during this timeframe. This resulted in 327 participants (168 Female, 159 Male, Mean Age = 73.38 ± 7.56 years), including 206 Cognitively Normal, 94 MCI, and 27 AD individuals.

For each participant, grey matter cortical thickness was measured using FreeSurfer version 8.1.0 by measuring the orthogonal distance from pia mater to cortical surface. Average cortical thickness per network was evaluated by summing the orthogonal distances and dividing by the total number of vertices located within a network. 

Tau deposition was quantified using Flortaucipir (AV-1451) PET, with SUVR inferior cerebellum normalization1,5. Resting-state Blood Oxygen Level Dependent (BOLD) fMRI data were preprocessed using fMRIprep and movement-regressed using standard Nilearn and CIFTIfy pipelines. Because our data was collected from multiple different sites via ADNI, resting state fMRIs differed slightly, with some scans having a TR = 3.0s (N=214) and other scans having TR = 0.607 seconds (N=113). The motion correction movement threshold (framewise displacement) was made slightly less strict for the fast TR group, increasing from 0.5mm to 0.75mm of motion, due to the higher sensitivity to movement in the shorter TR data.

Network-based connectivity measures were derived using the 13-Network Gordon 2016 parcellation4 using  Connectome Workbench v1.4.2. Connections between parcels part of the same pre-defined networks are considered ‘within-network’ connections, while connections between those parcels and parcels who are members of different networks are considered ‘between-network’ connections. Network Segregation is defined by the equation [Within Connectivity - Between Connectivity) / Within Connectivity]. Cingulo-Parietal and Salience networks were excluded from further analyses due to the limited number of parcels for comparison, making measures of segregation unstable. The “None” network, which represents the boundary areas between networks, was also excluded. This left us with a total of 10 canonical networks for our analysis comparisons. [Figure 1].

A full Spearman’s Rho (ρ) correlation matrix was generated using the raw data across 40 variables, 4 levels (Tau, Segregation, Within-Network Connectivity, Thickness) x 10 networks. Spearman was selected as opposed to Pearson to capture more representative biological processes that follow monotonic, not linear, patterns and potential biologic coupling. Significance (* = p < 0.05, multiple comparison corrected using FDR) is denoted for each cell, and data is blocked into 10 rows by 10 columns for easier visualization.

Results

Figure 2 displays the Spearman’s Rho correlation matrix across Tau SUVR, functional network segregation, within-network connectivity, and cortical thickness for each of ten networks. Color grading represents positive correlations and negative correlations. Cells with p < 0.05 are marked with an asterisk. Group level analysis was evaluated using Fisher-Stabilized mean ρ and P-values, thus indicating the strength of monotonic associations between variables.

Across all 10 networks, Tau SUVR in a given network was strongly correlated with Tau in other networks (Fisher’s ρ = 0.8374, P < 1x10-100), indicating that if one network has high tau, it is likely that any other network has high tau. Tau SUVR showed a negative mean correlation with cortical thickness (Fisher’s ρ = -0.1081, P = 1.011x10-47). Tau demonstrated a weaker, but consistently negative, relationship with segregation (Fisher’s ρ = -0.1205, P = 5.851x10-56), but no correlation when comparing tau and within-network connectivity at the group level (Fisher’s ρ = -0.02253, P = 0.2811).

A network-level analysis (Figure 2) reveals more detailed patterns in the data. Tau deposition on the network-level in RST and VIS networks showed the strongest negative correlation with thickness across all other networks. Further, segregation within the Cingulo-opercular network was very strongly associated with tau in every network, a suggestive pattern, shared (though less strongly) with Dorsal Attention network and SM Hand network.

Functional network segregation showed strong positive mean correlations with segregation across all networks (Fisher’s ρ = 0.4213, P < 1x10-100), indicating that if one network is well-segregated, it’s likely that any other network is also well-segregated. However, individual network pairs varied: for example, Visual network segregation showed negligible correlations with segregation of the DMN (ρ = -0.027, P = 0.724) and FPN (ρ = -0.0178, P = 0.811). Segregation showed strong positive associations with within-network connectivity (Fisher’s ρ = 0.03820, P = 8.052x10-29, consistent with the fact that the two measures are mathematically related. Segregation and thickness were only weakly related (Fisher’s ρ = 0.0269, P = 0.4971), suggesting that these two measures of network health may not share etiology.

Within-network connectivity also showed strong within modality correlations across all networks (Fisher’s ρ = 0.3937, P < 1x10-100). Within-network connectivity correlated modestly with tau and segregation as described above, and with cortical thickness (Fisher’s ρ = 0.1018, P = 3.126x10-33). Within Network Connectivity produced the strongest cross-modal effects in the VAN, where decreases in within-VAN connectivity correlate to increases in Tau in all other networks and correlates to decreases in cortical thickness in all other networks. This result warrants further investigation about how connectivity of the association system correlates to tau accumulation across the brain. Within-network connectivity also shows strong positive relationships on the diagonal in the within connectivity vs segregation correlations, as anticipated from their mathematical relationship. However, puzzlingly, within-network connectivity in the auditory network does not predict that network’s segregation (Fisher’s ρ = -0.00649, P = 0.937), which suggests there are other factors determining segregation of this network.

Cortical thickness measures exhibited strong positive correlations with each other across networks (Fisher’s ρ = 0.7875, P < 1x10-100), reflecting a highly coherent pattern of global structural variation which follows the accepted hypothesis of generalized cortical thinning throughout aging. When analyzing each row of correlations, thickness changes in the Default Mode, Retrosplenial Temporal, and Ventral Attention networks have strong negative correlations to tau deposition in most other networks. 

Conclusion

On a gross level, our results are consistent with previous work in this domain. Strong correlations in the on-diagonal ‘boxes’ show that if individuals have tau in one network, they likely have it in other networks, too, and that atrophy in one network predicts atrophy in other networks. Similarly, overall atrophy is correlated to presence of tau, and stronger segregation is correlated to thicker cortex and less tau, consistent with previous work. Interestingly, segregation demonstrated moderate but highly consistent negative associations with tau PET, supporting the hypothesis that reductions in functional network differentiation may represent an early systems-level vulnerability that permits or accelerates tau spread. 

Beyond these gross effects comparing modalities, examining these patterns on a network level allows identification of network-specific impact on various measures of network health on each other. These finer-scale patterns suggest that the relationship between, for example, atrophy and tau depends on the network. Further, segregation of the cingulo-opercular network is strongly related to tau deposition in all networks, suggesting a potential functional role in the disease process.

Such findings beg the question of whether, potentially, interventions on a network basis (such as biofeedback or stimulation) might differentially impact the progression of the disease. Future work will examine these networks in more detail with an eye to understanding how these various measures of network health interact.
