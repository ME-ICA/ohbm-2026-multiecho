Introduction

Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI) is a critical health concern that has far-reaching implications for both individuals and public health. Approximately 70-90% of TBIs are categorised as mild (mTBI), with incident rates likely exceeding 600 per 100,000 persons worldwide 1. Despite being classified as ‘mild’, mTBI can result in persistent and enduring symptoms across multiple domains, including somatic, cognitive, and affective functioning 2. A key clinical challenge of mTBI is the frequent absence of abnormalities on conventional neuroimaging techniques, despite a patients’ clinical presentation. Whilst techniques such as computed tomography and T2-weighted fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) MRI sequences can detect significant structural changes like skull fractures, haemorrhages and contusions, they remain limited in detecting the subtle neuropathological changes that occur after “mild” injury 3. The inability to detect these subtle injuries has consequently led to under diagnosis and incomplete understanding of reported symptoms. Accordingly, there is a clear need for tools that can reliably detect these subtle brain changes and explain symptom presentation and progression.

A promising solution is the ultra-high contrast (UHC)-MRI method reported to increase the image contrast between small changes in T1 by a factor of ten or more compared to conventional MRI techniques 4. Previous UHC-MRI research demonstrated clear qualitative differences in white matter tissue between nominal controls and symptomatic mTBI patients 4,5. However, visual assessment is inherently subjective to the rater, potentially limiting reproducibility, and may be less sensitive at detecting subtle tissue alterations. Therefore, the aim of this research is to develop a quantitative pipeline to analyse UHC-derived signal intensity. We present preliminary analysis on quantitative white matter signal versus qualitative UHC ratings in participants with mTBI.

Methods

38 sets of imaging data were acquired from 21 unique participants with mTBI under approval from the New Zealand Health and Disability Ethics Committee (#2024EXP17862). MRI data was acquired on a 3T MRI scanner (GE Signa Premier; General Electric, MI, USA; 48-channel head coil), consisting of 3D Axial T1-BRAVO (TR = 7.3ms, TE = 3ms, acquisition resolution = 0.9 x 0.9 x 1.2 mm), 3D Sagittal T2-FLAIR (TR = 6,300ms, TE = 103ms, TI = 1853ms, acquisition resolution = 1 x 1 x 1.6 mm), and two 2D Axial fast spin echo inversion recovery (IR) sequences timed at 350ms and 500ms (TR = 6000-9000ms, TE = 7ms, acquisition resolution = 0.9 x 1 x 5 mm, slice spacing 0.5 mm).

The UHC-MRI image was then created using an algebraic manipulation of the two T1-weighted fast spin echo IR sequences. The resultant image is the pixel-by-pixel (after co-registration) subtraction of these images, divided by the sum of the two sequences (i.e. TI short – TI long / TI short + TI long), where the signal is minimum at the short inversion time, and maximum at the long inversion time (and greatly nulled outside the range). Further descriptions on this sequence can be found in work published by Newburn et al 4.

To segment the UHC-MRI image, the T1-weighted images were first segmented using the CAT12 toolbox (SPM12) to generate a white matter probability map. Because UHC images exhibit extremely high signal on the boundary line between grey and white matter, the probability map was thresholded at 0.8, and three-dimensionally eroded by 2 and 5 voxels to minimise inclusion of boundary voxels in the T1 space. All resultant masks (no erosion, 2-voxel, and 5-voxel erosion) and the original T1 image, were rigidly co-registered and resliced (nearest neighbour interpolation) to the UHC image (see Figure 1). White matter signal intensity was extracted from the UHC-MRI using the three masks.

To assess qualitative differences, a board-certified radiologist rated each UHC image on a scale of 1 (‘normal’ appearing white matter, dark signal) to 5 (severe abnormality, bright signal). Example images for each rating are shown in Figure 2. One-way ANOVAs were conducted to investigate group differences in mean signal intensity extracted from each of the three masks across the radiologists rating groups (see Figure 2). Tukey (HSD) post-hoc tests were used to identify specific differences between groups means.

Results

Upon review, no T2-FLAIR images presented clinically significant findings. Concerning the UHC-MRI however, of the 38 scans, 8 were rated Grade 1, 18 were rated Grade 2, and Grades 3 and 4 each consisted of 6 scans, respectively. No scan in the current dataset was rated a Grade 5.

For the un-eroded mask (F(3, 34) = 17.1, p <.001, η² = 0.601), the 2-voxel mask (F(3, 34) = 22.1, p <.001, η² = 0.661), and 5-voxel eroded mask (F(3, 34) = 23.6, p <.001, η² = 0.675), mean signal intensity differed significantly across groups rated by the radiologist.

Post-hoc comparisons showed that across all three masks, Grade 1 differed significantly from Grade 3 (un-eroded: p <.001, 2-voxel: p = .002, 5-voxel: p = .009) and Grade 4 (all p <.001). Grade 2 significantly differed from Grade 3 for the un-eroded (p = .013) and 2-voxel erosion masks (p = .031), but not for the 5-voxel erosion mask. Across all masks, Grade 2 and Grade 4 differed significantly (p <.001). Significant differences between Grade 3 and 4 were also found for the 2-voxel (p = 0.016) and 5-voxel (p = 0.001) erosion mask, but not the un-eroded mask.

Conclusion

UHC-MRI revealed changes to white matter tissue that appears normal in conventional gold-standard imaging (e.g. T2-FLAIR). These visually observed changes are supported by quantitative differences in signal intensity, reflecting true variation in T1-weighted tissue properties. Across the three investigated mask types, the 2-voxel erosion provided results most consistent with radiological ratings of differences. The 5-voxel erosion may be too conservative and omit areas of meaningful difference between images, whereas no erosion may not be  sensitive to these differences due to inclusion of areas of high signal in boundary voxels skewing the mean signal. Inclusion of more balanced sample sizes across groups is required to provide stronger support for these conclusions.

Further work is needed to refine this pipeline. Integrating hyperintensity maps from T2-FLAIR images will help ensure these are excluded, and investigating more targeted white matter regions of interest may enhance the sensitivity and specificity of this approach.
