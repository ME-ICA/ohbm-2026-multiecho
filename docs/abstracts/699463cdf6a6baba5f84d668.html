Introduction

Programming is a complex skill involving many cognitive modules. Programmers performing coding tasks engage language processing, attention, and working memory areas. Many have shown activation in left-lateralized brain regions, high-order motor areas, and language areas1, and a deactivation in the default mode network2 while coding. For new programmers, coding is a working memory task, and they rely more on visuo-spatial processes3. 

These differences are also related to the level of expertise, which was correlated with language, motor, and visuo-spatial brain activity. These patterns predicted success in later learning. It was also suggested that the more expertise the programmer has, the brain activation during programming tasks becomes closer to reading3. Beginner programmers showed activity in a large variety of language and visual areas but only activity in the right Broca’s area changed after the learning phase.

Despite this significant body of work, many studies examining the neuroplasticity of programming rely heavily on measures of gray matter volume or task-dependent neural activity. Functional results are often highly dependent on the specific task presented during scanning, leading to varied findings. This study focuses on the diffusion-based characteristics of the programmer’s brain. We examined the variation in gray matter mean diffusivity (MD), which reflects microstructural characteristics such as cell density, diffusion-based connectivity, and axon diameter estimation4 in a large cohort of experts, novice, and naïve programmers. Our results show the difference between the memory- and attention-based skill of a novice programmer and the intuitive, language-based skill of experts, providing insight into the structural reorganization underlying the shift in cognitive strategy. Furthermore, by examining network connectivity, we sought to determine how the global and local network organization is related to programming expertise.

Methods

362 participants (mean age 31, (8.15), 104 female) were scanned as part of the TAU Brain Bank project. Participants were assigned into expert, novice, and naïve groups based on self-reported programming experience. 

MRI data were acquired using a 3T Magnetom Siemens Prisma scanner with a 64-channel RF coil, at the Tel-Aviv University Strauss Center for Computational Neuroimaging. Each MRI session included anatomical and diffusion scans. T1-weighted images were acquired with a 3D magnetization-prepared rapid acquisition gradient echo (MPRAGE) sequence, with TR/TE = 2400/2.78 ms and a resolution of 0.9 mm isotropic. Diffusion-weighted images were scanned using a multi-shell acquisition scheme. Diffusion weightings of b=1000, 2000 and 4000 s/mm2  were applied in 180 different directions each, along with 6 B0 images. In addition, one B0 image was acquired with reversed phase encoding, for the purpose of EPI distortion correction. The scans were acquired using a spin-echo EPI protocol with TR/TE = 3500/94 ms. and  a resolution of 1.6 mm3 isotropic. 

Diffusion Images were corrected for movement and eddy-current distortions using FSL topup and eddy. For ROI-based analysis, a scan-specific atlas was created using FSL flirt and fnirt algorithms to register the Brainnetome atlas to the diffusion space. A 5 tissue probability segmentation was created using mrTrix 5ttgen and a gray-matter mask was extracted by adding the cortical and sub-cortical maps. This mask was thresholded at 0.2, and the MD data was masked to create a gray-matter MD map.

Multi-shell multi-tissue CSD-based whole brain tractography was computed for all diffusion scans using MRtrix. Two connectivity matrices were extracted for each scan: normalized streamline-based using the dipy connectivity_matrix function and axon diameter based using AxSi algorithm4. 

MD maps were extracted using Dipy’s dti-fit. Mean MD per area was computed, as well as whole-brain density, efficiency and clustering coefficient. The same measures were computed for the attention, frontoparietal and default node networks, as those were assumed to be most involved in programming. A linear regression model was used to examine the differences between the groups for all non-learners. Either mean MD per area or the network measures were used as the dependent factor, and group assignment was the independent factor along with age and gender as covariates. 

Connectivity conservation^5 was also examined across groups. Hemispheric efficiency was computed as the mean efficiency across both hemispheres’ subnetwork, and commissural connections were computed as the number of tracts assigned to the corpus callosum using DiPy reco-bundles. The correlation between these two measures was computed for each of the groups. And the difference between correlations was measured using Fisher’s Z transformation.

Results

MD group comparison revealed differences that varied between expert/naïve and novice/naïve comparisons. Naïve participants exhibited higher MD than experts in most (8 of 12) significant areas. Differences (p < 0.05) were found in areas mostly associated with the attention network. Notably (p < 0.01), higher MD was measured in the naïve group in regions associated with attention, executive control and visuospatial processing. Lower MD values were measured in the naïve participants in language and salience networks. Novices showed an overall higher MD than naïve participants in the majority of significant areas (9 of 12). This was especially pronounced in the cerebellum. Other areas with high MD for novice programmers include reward-system areas and right-hemisphere language areas. Lower MD was measured in the motor areas. While no p-values remained significant after multiple comparison adjustments, these results align with previous functional studies.

When comparing connectivity measures, both the attention network and the DMN showed significant differences between the novice and naïve groups. For the DMN, density was higher in expert programmers over naïves (p = 0.03, Cohen's d = -0.27). For the ADD-weighted networks, a significant effect was found for both the AN and DMN mean clustering coefficient (p=0.1, Cohen’s d = -0.4; p=0.02, Cohen’s d = -0.36). For the novice-naïve comparison, both the streamline-based clustering in the DMN as well as the entire right hemisphere network was significantly higher for the non-programmers (DMN: p-corrected=0.025, cohen’s d=0.44; right hemisphere: p-corrected=0.015, Cohen's d=0.34), and whole brain mean node degree was significantly higher for novice programmers (p-corrected=0.03, Cohen's d=-0.34). FPN mean node degree both for the streamline-weighted and the ADD weighted networks also had a moderate effect size for difference between groups, but was not significant after multiple comparisons correction (streamline weighted: uncorrected p=0.047, cohen’s d=-0.28; ADD weighted: uncorrected p = 0.047, cohen’s d=0.37). 

Consistently with past comparisons in other datasets, hemispheric efficiency and commissural connectivity were negatively correlated across all scans (r = -29, p<<0.0001). The correlation appeared to be stronger for naïve (r=-0.35 p<<0.001) than for the experts (r=-22, p=0.03). Because of the large imbalance in gender across the groups, the same analysis was repeated with male participants only (80 naïve, 84 experts), and the resulting effect was similar (r=-0.18, p=0.09 for experts; r=-0.3, p < 0.01 for naïve). Fisher’s transformation test showed a low but insignificant p-value for the difference between the groups’ r values (p=0.12). No significant difference was found between groups in mean or variance of both hemispheric efficiency and for CC tract number. The same analysis was repeated with 162 of the participants for whom ADD weighted connectivity matrices were computed (85 expert, 77 naïve). The overall connectivity conservation effect in this case was stronger (r=-0.35, p<<0.0001), and while the correlation for expert programmers remained similar (r=-0.22, p=0.04), the correlation for naïve participants appeared even stronger and more significant (r=-0.43, p<<0.001), despite the lower number of participants.

Conclusion

This study utilized a comprehensive, diffusion-based approach to characterize the neurobiological signatures of programming expertise, moving beyond conventional gray matter volume and task-dependent functional measures. By examining mean diffusivity (MD) and diffusion-based connectivity across expert, novice, and naïve programmers, we were able to pinpoint differences reflecting the shift from memory- and attention-based novice skills to intuitive, language-based expert abilities.

Our MD analysis revealed distinct patterns across the groups. naïve participants generally exhibited higher MD than experts in areas predominantly associated with the attention network (AN), including bilateral premotor areas, the left frontal eye field (FEF), and precuneus. Conversely, experts showed higher MD in areas homologous to language regions (right temporal gyri and inferior frontal gyrus), potentially reflecting a reorganization or increased complexity of local microstructure in these language-related areas with expertise.

The comparison between novice and naïve individuals indicated a higher overall MD for novices, notably in the cerebellum and reward-system areas like the putamen and orbital gyrus. This pattern, particularly the involvement of the putamen, suggests that the initial stages of programming acquisition may be accompanied by microstructural changes in areas linked to motor learning and habit formation, consistent with the view of novice coding as an effortful, skill-acquisition task.

Regarding connectivity, differences were observed between novices and naïve participants in both the Attention Network (AN) and the Default Mode Network (DMN). Specifically, novice programmers demonstrated a significantly higher whole-brain mean node degree, suggesting a more integrated and widely connected network structure compared to naïve individuals. Conversely, the naïve group exhibited significantly higher streamline-based clustering in the DMN and the entire right hemisphere, which may reflect a less efficient or specialized network organization prior to skill acquisition. For expert programmers, only the DMN showed a higher density over naïve participants.

A key finding relates to the conservation of connectivity between hemispheres. We observed a negative correlation between hemispheric efficiency and commissural connectivity across all participants. Crucially, this negative correlation was significantly stronger for naïve participants compared to experts, even when controlling for gender imbalances and using axon diameter weighted (ADD) connectivity measures. This suggests that as programming expertise develops, the strong inter-hemispheric constraint on network organization-where low intra-hemispheric efficiency is balanced by high inter-hemispheric connection strength-may weaken, potentially indicating a move towards a more specialized or localized processing efficiency in expert brains.

In conclusion, the variations in MD and connectivity profiles across expertise levels provide compelling evidence of neuroplasticity driven by programming learning. MD differences support the microstructural remodeling in areas related to attention, reward, and language. Connectivity analyses, particularly the findings related to whole-brain node degree and the correlation between hemispheric and commissural connections, suggest that programming expertise is associated with a more integrated and less constrained network organization. Future work should focus on longitudinal designs to directly track these diffusion-based changes and their correlation with performance gains, thereby solidifying the structural basis of programming skill acquisition.
