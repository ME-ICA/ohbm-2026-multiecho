Introduction

Sensorimotor rhythms (13-17Hz) over the motor cortex have been linked to motor processing and memory-guided action anticipation. More specifically, decreases in sensorimotor rhythms have been associated with the selection and prospective planning of an upcoming action. Traditionally, findings of memory-guided action planning in healthy adults are solely based on correlational evidence from EEG or MEG data1,2. Therefore, it remains unclear whether the desynchronized sensorimotor rhythms detected in EEG are causally related to behaviour in memory-guided action planning. Recent data by Gresch et al. show how internal motor representations are reinstated after task-relevant visuomotor distraction 2. These results of sensorimotor rhythms desynchronisation indicate that working memory representations of planned actions are rapidly reselected, highlighting the dynamic and proactive nature of action preparation. Building on this, I will present our project aiming to causally link sensorimotor rhythms activity in the primary motor cortex during memory-guided action planning with behavioural performance.


Methods

Using an online repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) protocol at 13Hz we tried to transiently disrupt the pattern of contralateral suppression in sensory-motor rhythms during initial action selection in a visual-motor working memory task. The participants (n=43) either received rTMS at 80% intensity of the resting motor threshold or a placebo rTMS without stimulating the participant, where the stimulation coil was tilted 90°. We hypothesized that (i) during trials with rTMS delivered to the primary motor cortex, accuracy and reaction times would decrease compared to trials with placebo rTMS. (ii) This difference between rTMS and placebo rTMS was expected to be more pronounced in trials when the memory delay included a task relevant visuomotor distractor, compared to trials without distraction.

Results

The analysis of errors and reaction times revealed that the transient rTMS lesion in the primary motor cortex did not significantly affect behavioural outcomes as hypothesized. This null result suggests either that 13-Hz sensorimotor rhythms activity in primary motor cortex is not essential for the selection of actions maintained in working memory, or that the applied stimulation parameters were insufficient to modulate the relevant neural processes.

Conclusion

Overall, our findings do not provide evidence for a causal link between sensorimotor rhythms activity and action planning. This challenges theories on sensorimotor rhythms desynchronization in prospective action planning and underscores the need to identify precise cortical generators and mechanisms underlying sensorimotor rhythms-related signals.
