Introduction

Brain age quantifies how the brain deviates from normative aging trajectories. Traditionally derived from T1-weighted MRI, the brain age gap (BAG) condenses complex neuroanatomical patterns into a single individualized index reflecting neurodegenerative change. Numerous studies have linked T1-based BAG with cognitive performance and Alzheimer’s disease (AD)–related biomarkers,1,2 suggesting its value as a marker of neurodegeneration. However, dementia is a multifactorial syndrome, and cerebrovascular disorders (CeVD) contribute substantially to its onset and progression. Conventional T1-based brain age models capture neuronal loss and cortical atrophy but overlook vascular pathology3—one of the most prevalent and modifiable drivers of cognitive impairment in aging populations. Existing studies on vascular aging mainly rely on systemic risk factors (e.g., hypertension, cholesterol, diet) and neuroimaging indices like white matter hyperintensity volume, which fail to capture individualized cerebrovascular aging patterns. Moreover, most existing models are trained on Western datasets, raising concerns about generalizability to Asian cohorts where CeVD is more prevalent. To bridge these gaps, we propose a deep-learning (DL) based multidimensional framework to derive neurodegenerative (T1-based) and cerebrovascular (T2-FLAIR-based) brain age. We hypothesized that the neurodegenerative and cerebrovascular BAGs would exhibit differential associations with blood biomarkers and domain-specific cognitive trajectories. Importantly, we posited that integrating neurodegenerative and cerebrovascular brain age measures would enhance sensitivity and specificity for detecting early disease progression and predicting future dementia conversion, particularly in populations with mixed neurodegenerative and vascular pathology.

Methods

The study aimed to develop and validate multidimensional brain age models using neurodegenerative (T1-weighted) and cerebrovascular (T2-FLAIR) MRI to capture distinct pathways of brain aging and dementia. The overall workflow is shown in Figure 1.

Datasets. Model training utilized an aggregated multi-cohort dataset combining both public datasets (UK Biobank, DLBS and QTAB) from Caucasian populations and Singaporean cohorts, including 31,947 paired T1-weighted and T2-FLAIR scans from cognitively healthy participants aged 8-99 years, providing comprehensive lifespan coverage and improving cross-site and cross-ethnic generalizability.

Independent validation was conducted on the MACC cohort,4 a clinical cohort of approximately 700 older adults aged 50-95 years spanning normal cognition (NCI), mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and dementia due to Alzheimer’s disease (AD) or vascular dementia (VaD). Each participant underwent baseline multimodal MRI and plasma biomarker assessment, including pTau217 (AD pathology), neurofilament light chain (NfL, neurodegeneration), as well as hsTNT and GDF15 (cardiovascular risk). Longitudinal cognitive scores up to 5 years across seven domains, attention, executive function, language, visual and verbal memory, visuoconstruction, and visuomotor function, as well as clinical scores such as Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) were available. A subset of 195 non-demented participants (NCI + MCI) at baseline had up to 10 years of diagnostic follow-up, of whom 85 converted to dementia (68 AD, 17 VaD), enabling predictive analysis of future dementia conversion risk.

Preprocessing. All T1-weighted and T2-FLAIR images underwent minimal preprocessing—bias-field correction, skull stripping, linear registration to MNI152 space and intensity normalization. Non-linear registration or segmentation-based processing were avoided to maintain modality consistency and minimize preprocessing bias.

Model Training. Brain age models were implemented using a simple fully convolutional network (SFCN).5 Separate SFCN models were trained from scratch for each modality to derive neurodegenerative- and cerebrovascular-specific brain age estimates. Both models were trained and validated with 5-fold cross validation. The Adam optimizer was applied with initial learning rate as a hyperparameter, training for 50 epochs per fold. The model output represents a continuous estimate of brain age in years.

Model Evaluation and Analysis. The ensemble of trained models was applied to the independent dataset MACC to predict individual brain ages. Predicted brain ages were bias corrected4 and then compared with chronological age to derive (i) T1-based neurodegenerative BAG and (ii) T2-FLAIR-based cerebrovascular BAG.

Associations between BAGs and blood, clinical variables and domain-specific cognition were assessed using Pearson and partial correlations adjusted for age, sex, education, race, and diagnostic group, with Holm–Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Longitudinal cognitive trajectories were modelled using linear mixed-effect models to estimate annual rate of change for each domain. 

Finally, to evaluate prognostic utility, logistic regression analyses were performed to predict 10-year dementia conversion in the subset of 195 non-demented participants using 5-fold cross validation. Two hierarchical base models were constructed: Model A, including demographic and clinical covariates (chronological age, sex, education, and MMSE score), and Model B, further incorporating APOE ε4 status and baseline cognitive status. Predictors were sequentially added, first pTau217, followed by T1-weighted BAG and T2-FLAIR BAG, to quantify incremental predictive value. Comparative model performance was quantified using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), providing an integrative test of whether multidimensional brain-age metrics add predictive value beyond standard risk factors.

Results

Brain Age Prediction Accuracy. Both brain age models demonstrated strong performance in estimating chronological age across the training and external validation datasets. Using five-fold cross validation on the aggregated multi-cohort training dataset, the T1-weighted and T2-FLAIR models achieved high prediction with mean absolute errors (MAE) below three years and a correlation coefficient of 0.95 (Figure 2A-B).

External validation on the independent memory-clinic cohort MACC, comprising participants spanning normal cognition to dementia, further demonstrated strong generalizability. The T1-weighted and T2-FLAIR models achieved MAEs of approximately 4.5 years and 4.4 years, and correlation coefficients of 0.78 and 0.74 respectively (Figure 2C-D). In the cohort, predicted brain ages were systematically higher than chronological ages among dementia patients, consistent with accelerated aging in clinical populations. Notably, individuals aged 50-60 years exhibited a larger discrepancy between T1-weighted and T2-FLAIR brain ages, corresponding to a higher burden of cerebrovascular lesions such as white matter hyperintensities (WMH) prevalent in the clinical cohort. These findings confirm that both brain age models reliably capture age-related variance across diverse neurodegenerative and cerebrovascular profiles.

Brain age gaps from the T1-weighted and T2-FLAIR models differentially relate to cognition scores and blood biomarkers. Associations between modality-specific BAGs and cognitive and biological markers were first examined independently. Both T1-weighted and T2-FLAIR BAGs demonstrated significant correlations (p < 0.05, Holm-Bonferroni corrected) with baseline global cognition, seven cognitive domains, and plasma biomarkers including pTau217, NfL, hsTNT, and GDF15. Both BAGs also correlated with longitudinal rates of cognitive decline over a five-years follow-up period, reinforcing their biological relevance as aging biomarkers.

Importantly, when analysed jointly within multivariate models, distinct modality-specific patterns emerged. The T1-weighted BAG correlated more strongly with verbal and visual memory performance, longitudinal rate of decline in visual memory (Figure 2E), and AD-related pTau217 levels, reflecting its sensitivity to neurodegenerative processes related to AD pathology. In contrast, the T2-FLAIR BAG was preferentially associated with visuomotor performance at baseline, longitudinal rate of decline in executive and language functions (Figure 2F), and the vascular biomarker GDF15, indicating its specificity for cerebrovascular contributions to the disease pathophysiology. These modality-differentiated findings support the hypothesis that neurodegenerative and vascular brain age indices reflect distinct yet complementary biological pathways of aging and disease progression.

Neurodegenerative and cerebrovascular brain age gaps enhance prediction of dementia progression. Logistic regression models using 5-fold cross validations were constructed for 5-year and 10-year dementia conversion prediction. Across both basic Model A (Figure 2G) and clinical Model B (Figure 2H), sequential addition of T1-weighed and T2-FLAIR BAGs to both models significantly improved classification accuracy of 10-year dementia risk, as reflected by increased area under the ROC curve (AUC). Similar results were observed for 5-year dementia risk prediction.

Conclusion

Cognitive decline arises from interacting neurodegenerative and cerebrovascular mechanisms, with cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) playing a critical yet often underrecognized role. This study presents a novel multi-modality brain-age framework that jointly models T1-weighted and T2-FLAIR MRI to capture complementary aspects of brain aging—neurodegenerative and cerebrovascular pathways, respectively. Trained on a multi-cohort training dataset spanning the lifespan and validated in an independent Singaporean memory-clinic cohort, the two modality-specific models achieved high predictive accuracy. The T1-weighted BAG captured predominantly neurodegenerative patterns, showing stronger associations with AD-related biomarkers (pTau217) and memory decline, whereas the T2-FLAIR BAG reflects vascular burden more associated with vascular pathology (GDF15) and executive and language function decline. Importantly, the combination of both neurodegenerative and cerebrovascular BAGs significantly improved the prediction of 10-year dementia conversion, surpassing models based solely on demographic and clinical variables, underscoring the value of multidimensional brain-age modelling in disentangling overlapping mechanisms of cognitive impairment and enhancing early dementia prediction.

Together, these findings establish a clinically translatable, multidimensional brain age framework for early detection, risk stratification, and longitudinal monitoring of dementia. Future work will extend this paradigm toward lifespan brain-pace modelling, integrating additional MRI modalities such as susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI) and diffusion to characterize individual trajectories of brain health. Ultimately, this multidimensional approach could enable personalized prevention and treatment strategies targeting distinct biological pathways of cognitive decline and dementia.
