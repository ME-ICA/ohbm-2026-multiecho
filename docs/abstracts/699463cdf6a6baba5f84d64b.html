Introduction

As one of the few non-invasive neuroimaging techniques, functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) indirectly reflects neural activity by measuring blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) signals, and is widely used to characterize the intrinsic dynamics of brain functional networks during rest. The frequency and energy profiles of these networks reflect the temporal organization of neural oscillations, supporting functional segregation and integration which are closely linked to cognitive processing and brain states. 

However, most traditional analyses depended on the implicit assumptions of stationarity and linearity of the fMRI signal. In recent years, growing evidence has highlighted the inherent non-stationarity and nonlinearity of BOLD signals and also general human neural processes, challenging the validity of traditional methods[1]. 

The Hilbert–Huang Transform (HHT) offers an adaptive, data-driven framework for time-frequency analysis[2]. HHT can provide derived metrics such as Hilbert weighted frequency (HWF) and Hilbert marginal spectrum (HMS) to characterize the dynamic frequency and energy distributions of brain networks. Despite the promise of HHT, a systematic evaluation of the whole-brain features of HHT and their reproducibility remains lacking. 

In this current study, we utilized two independent public rs-fMRI datasets to evaluate the cross-dataset consistency of these metrics within the typical brain functional networks, and to investigate the reliability of HHT-based rs-fMRI network analysis. 

Methods

We analyzed rs-fMRI data from two independent datasets: BNU1 (50 participants, each with 2 sessions) and BNU3 (48 participants, each with 1 session) from the Consortium for Reliability and Reproducibility (https://fcon_1000.projects.nitrc.org/indi/CoRR/html/index.html). Preprocessing was carried out by DPABI (https://rfmri.org/DPABI), which included slice-timing correction, realignment, spatial normalization to MNI space, and noise regression using the component-based noise correction (CompCor) method. This step regressed out the first five principal components derived from white matter and cerebrospinal fluid masks, together with headmotion parameters and linear trends. Following preprocessing, the whole-brain signal was parcellated into 246 regions of interest (ROIs) according to the Brainnetome Atlas (https://atlas.brainnetome.org/). [3]For each participant and session, the mean BOLD time series within each ROI was extracted and individually decomposed via empirical mode decomposition (EMD) —the standard first step of the HHT—into several intrinsic mode functions (IMFs). The first three IMFs (IMF1–3), with dominant frequencies within the canonical resting-state range (0.01–0.1 Hz), were retained for subsequent analysis. For each retained IMF, we then applied the Hilbert transform to obtain the analytic signal, from which instantaneous amplitude, phase, and frequency were derived. To enable network-level characterization, we mapped the Yeo 7-network parcellation[4] onto the 246 Brainnetome ROIs. ROIs not assigned to any of the seven cortical networks were visually inspected and found to correspond predominantly to subcortical nuclei. These ROIs were consequently grouped into an eighth “subcortical” network, resulting in a total of 8 functional networks for analysis. 

Then Hilbert weighted frequency (HWF) and Hilbert marginal spectrum (HMS) were calculated for each region. HWF is defined as the energy-weighted mean of instantaneous frequency, and HMS computed as the time integral of squared instantaneous amplitude. To visualize more clearly at network-level, the metrics were averaged across all ROIs within each of the 8 brain networks, yielding one scalar value per network per IMF. For each dataset and each IMF, one-way repeated-measures ANOVAs were performed to test for differences in HWF and HMS across the eight networks. All ANOVA results were corrected for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR) with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. The cross-dataset consistency of the resulting network-level patterns was visualized using radar plots (for HWF) and bar plots (for HMS).  

Results

Through EMD, the signal from each ROI was decomposed into 4–7 IMFs of approximately narrowband frequencies. Among these, three IMFs (2nd, 3rd and 4th highest frequency), hereafter referred to as IMF1, IMF2, and IMF3, were selected as their dominant frequencies are 0.05Hz, 0.02Hz, and 0.01Hz, fell within the typical resting-state range considered to reflect meaningful neural information, namely, 0.01–0.1 Hz[5]. For each of the three IMFs, we computed the HWF within 8 brain networks, and assessed the reproducibility of these network-specific central frequencies across two independent datasets (BNU1 and BNU3). In IMF1, both datasets exhibit nearly identical spatial patterns. In IMF2, overall alignment is preserved, with divergence in dorsal attention networks. In IMF3, the profile remains robustly correlated, though inter-network contrasts are slightly attenuated. One-way ANOVAs revealed significant differences in HWF among networks in all IMFs (all p < 0.001), with the Limbic network consistently showing the highest HWF, suggesting preferential involvement in high-frequency dynamics. Critically, the network-level HWF profiles were highly consistent between BNU1 and BNU3 across all IMFs (Pearson r = 0.82–0.997, all p < 0.05; Figure 1), demonstrating robust cross-cohort reproducibility of frequency-specificity in rs-fMRI. 

Across three IMFs, the HMS exhibited a highly structured spatial distribution across the 8 brain networks. The consistency observed between BNU1 and BNU3, especially in IMF1 and IMF2, underscores the reliability of HMS as a measure for frequency-specific energy profiling. One-way ANOVAs confirmed significant differences in HMS energy among networks for all IMFs (all p < 0.001), with the default mode network consistently showing the highest energy and the limbic network the lowest. Critically, this network-specific energy profile was remarkably reproducible between BNU1 and BNU3 across all IMFs (Pearson r = 0.963–0.987, all p < 0.001; Figure 1). These results indicate that the energy structure of brain networks is robustly preserved across independent datasets.  

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that HHT derived metrics like HWF and HMS yield highly reproducible, network-specific profiles across two independent resting-state fMRI datasets (BNU1 and BNU3). For all three IMFs within the 0.01–0.1 Hz range, both HWF and HMS exhibited significant and consistent spatial organization across eight functional brain networks. The Limbic network consistently displayed the highest central frequency, implicating its role in high frequency neural dynamics, while the Default Mode Network showed the greatest energy concentration, aligning with its known metabolic and functional prominence during rest. Critically, cross-dataset correlations for network-level HWF (r = 0.82–0.997) and HMS (r = 0.963–0.987) were robust and statistically significant, confirming the reliability of HHT-based characterizations beyond single-dataset idiosyncrasies. These results validate HHT as a powerful and reproducible framework for capturing frequency-resolved functional architecture in rs-fMRI, offering a data-driven alternative to stationary spectral methods. Future work may leverage this approach to probe frequency-specific alterations in neuropsychiatric disorders or developmental trajectories.
