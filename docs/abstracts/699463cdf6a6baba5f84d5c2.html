Introduction

Cranioplasty is a surgical procedure that reconstructs skull defects from various causes, directly impacting brain protection and patient social reintegration. At the University Hospital of Western Paraná (Hospital Universitário do Oeste do Paraná - HUOP), cranioplasty with polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) is routine, but intraoperative manual molding often yields unsatisfactory aesthetic results.1 Financial limitations within Brazil's Unified Health System restrict access to sophisticated techniques, as industrial molds and ceramic or titanium prostheses cost R$100,000-R$200,000.2 This study specifically aims to demonstrate that by leveraging pre-existing cranial computed tomography(CT) imaging data and open-source software platforms, it is possible to develop an accessible methodology for fabricating personalized cranial prostheses that ensure effective brain protection. We validate this approach using non-industrial 3D-printed molds produced from routinely acquired hospital imaging, establishing a replicable workflow that transforms readily available neuroimaging resources into functional brain-protective devices at minimal cost.

Methods

We report our experience with five patients who underwent cranioplasty using a novel low-cost 3D-printed mold technique. All patients had persistent cranial vault defects that required reconstruction after clinical stabilization and resolution of complications. For each case, computed tomography (CT) images were processed using free software (3D Slicer and Blender) to create customized virtual molds of the missing bone segment for the patient. The physical molds were 3D-printed using polylactic acid (PLA) on a Creality K1 MAX printer, then sterilized using low-temperature hydrogen peroxide gas plasma.3 During surgeries performed in 2025, each sterile mold was wrapped in surgical latex film to prevent PMMA adhesion and maintain sterility.4 The PMMA prostheses were molded intraoperatively and fixed to the skull using titanium miniplates and screws. The study protocol was approved by the HUOP Research Ethics Committee with informed consent obtained from all patients or their legal guardians.

Results

The prosthesis adapted precisely to the bone margins without requiring intraoperative adjustments. Postoperative CT scans demonstrated correction of previous bulging, with restoration of cranial contour and appropriate symmetry relative to the contralateral side. The CT cuts showed the prosthesis faithfully respected the patient's cranial curvature, integrating adequately with the remaining bone edges without discontinuities or irregular protrusions.5 No complications were directly attributed to the PLA molding technique or PMMA prosthesis. The total cost for the most expensive case was R$746.92 (approximately US$137,94), which represents the highest cost among all five prostheses fabricated using our methodology. This approach represents a dramatic cost reduction of 99.3-99.6% compared to conventional industrial prostheses (R$100,000-200,000), making personalized cranial reconstruction financially viable within resource-limited public healthcare systems. The aesthetic outcome was satisfactory in all cases, significantly improving patients' self-esteem and social reintegration while substantially reducing waiting times for reconstructive surgery within the public health system.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that low-cost neuroimaging-based methodologies for brain protection are achievable using widely available computed tomography. By leveraging existing hospital CT infrastructure combined with free software and affordable 3D printing technology, we achieved significant cost reduction while maintaining anatomical precision for cranial prostheses. The technique preserves brain protection through accurate reconstruction of cranial contour and symmetry, with outcomes comparable to expensive conventional methods at less than 1% of the cost. This approach addresses critical healthcare disparities in public systems by democratizing access to quality cranial reconstruction without requiring specialized equipment. The methodology represents a practical integration of existing neuroimaging resources with innovative fabrication techniques, offering a replicable model for resource-limited settings worldwide. Future validation with larger cohorts will strengthen evidence for widespread implementation of this cost-effective approach to brain protection and cranial reconstruction within constrained healthcare budgets.
