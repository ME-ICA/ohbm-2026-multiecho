Introduction

In 1877 the Austrian anatomist R. Heschl1 published a brief note relating a description of a “bridged” central sulcus (CS) seen at the brain surface2,3  to a common deep winding within the CS at the same location by applying a novel scientific approach. In this short communication, Heschl criticized the practice of reporting unusual anatomical findings without specifying the underlying sample size as well as the missing of an overall general description of the brain surface windings, against which these atypical findings could be evaluated. To address these shortcomings, he states that a general “scheme of the windings of the cerebrum has to be the result of a statistical work“, and as the prosector of the Vienna General Hospital, was able to implement it by examining 1087 brains within a single year. 

This large sample enabled Heschl to quantify the rarity of the “bridged” CS, identifying six brains with a unilateral “bridged” CS. He also accomplished his second goal, the description of the general anatomical scheme of the CS at the location of the “bridged” CS, by measuring the height of the common deep winding connecting the pre- and postcentral gyrus at the fundus of the CS. The resulting height distribution revealed a distinct skew with deep winding nearly approaching the brain surface. These observations supported Heschl’s hypothesis that the “bridged” CS represents an extreme variant of the common deep winding within the CS.  

The present study4 replicated and extended Heschl’s findings using 1112 T1-weighted MRIs of the Human Connectome Project. We determined the contemporary prevalence of the “bridged” CS through visual inspection of brain surface reconstructions and derived the height distribution of the CS deep winding - in the HCP-YA data set called “pli de passage fronto-pariétal moyen” (PPfpm) - from 1983 MRI-based hemispheric CS depth profiles from the same data set. 

Methods

1112 unprocessed whole brain structural MRI datasets (3T, T1-weighted MPRAGE, spatial resolution: 0.7 x 0.7 x 0.7 mm3) were obtained from the Human Connectome Project S1200 young adult release (van Essen et al. 2012). Subjects (506 males, 606 females) had a mean age of 28.8 ±3.7 years. 

Image preprocessing, surfaces reconstruction and extractions of ‘Cortical Folds Graphs’ (CFG) representing the attributed relational graph structures of cortical sulci were performed using Brain VISA 4.5.0 and the Morphologist 2015 pipeline yielding 2224 hemispheric reconstructions. 

“Bridged” CS: CFGs of the CS were identified and visually searched by at least two out of three raters who worked independently on the dataset. Criteria for the detection of a “bridged” CS were: (1) accurate identification of the CS, (2) correct localization of the “bridge” along the CS, and (3) completeness of the “bridged” CS in reaching the brain surface.

PPfpm height distribution: CFGs were obtained from 2165 left and right hemispheres with a continuous CS. Using Brain VISA ‘Sulcus Parametrization’, CFGs were parameterized to obtain a depth profile, representing the distance between the brain surface boundary and the grey matter surface at the fundus of the CS at 101 equidistant positions along the CS mesh. Absolut PPfpm height was calculated as the difference between the maximal depth value at positions >45 and the next adjacent local maximum at position ≤45. Relative height was computed as the ratio of absolute PPfpm height to the respective maximum depth at the position >45 of the individual CS5. 

Comparison of Heschl’s and HCP-YA height distributions:  Heschl reported his data in 4 bins: 0 – 1/6 | 1/6 – 2/6 | 2/6 – 5/6 | > 5/6 = “bridged” CS; HCP-YA data are reported in 100 bins. For comparison with Heschl’s data the cases of the HCP-YA distribution were summed within the four Heschl’s bins. A second comparison of the two distribution is based on the standard deviations, which are estimated, based on the normal distribution, for the Heschl dataset and obtained through descriptive statistics for the HCP-YA distribution. 

Results

Prevalence of “bridged” central sulcus in HCP-YA dataset and in its subset of twins

The visual search of 1112 T1-weighted whole brain surface reconstruction found 9 brains with a unilateral “bridged” CS (3 LeftHemiphere/6 RightHemisphere, 3 males/6 females) resulting in a prevalence of 0.8%. Heschl reported 6 brains (2 LH / 4 RH, 5 males/1 female) resulting in a comparable prevalence of 0.6%.  

As 40% of the participants in the HCP-YA dataset are mono- and dizygotic twins, we could also obtain the prevalence of the “bridged” CS in these groups. Six of the nine “bridged” CS are present in twin individuals: three in a dizygotic twin (prevalence: 1.8%) and three in monozygotic twin individuals (prevalence: 1.1%). As a consequence, the prevalence of the “bridged” CS in non-twin individuals decreases to 0.5%, becoming more similar to the 6% prevalence in the Heschl dataset. None of the three “bridged” CS occurred within a dizygotic twin pair. Of the three “bridged” CS in monozygotic twins, two occur within a twin pair, but in an incongruent way, i.e. in different hemispheres. 

PPfpm height distribution in HCP-YA dataset and comparison to Heschl’s dataset

The heights of the PPfpms in the HCP-YA dataset were estimated based on the MRI-based CS depth profile and by applying an automized identification of two landmarks related to the PPfpm. The resulting distribution of 1983 PPfpm of left and right hemispheres (Fig.: pink columns) exhibits a generally wider distribution and an overall shift toward generally larger heights. This can be seen when the HCP-YA PPfpm height distribution is summed up within Heschl’s binning (Fig.: red columns within blue lines indicating Heschl’s binning) as the HCP-YA data are distributed across the first (50%) and the second (39%) bin and exhibit a tail of 11% of cases with larger heights, up to almost reaching the brain surface. This is in contrast to the Heschl data set (Fig.:, blue columns within the blue lines) where 93% of cases are within the first bin, 4% in the second bin, being twofold higher as the common deep winding  and 3% in the third and largest bin with cases reaching three- to fivefold elevations, nearly approaching the brain surface.   

A more direct comparison of the shapes of the two distributions within Heschl ‘s binning was accomplished by a translation of the boundaries of Heschl’s binning from specific heights (0 – 1/6 fraction of maximal CS depth) into standard deviations (µ-2s - µ+2 s). Boundary values were estimated for Heschl’s distribution and obtained for the HCP-YA distribution. The frequencies within the standard deviation binning exhibit a high consistency between the two distributions (1. bin= Heschl: 93.2%, HCP-YA: 95.7% | 2. bin = Heschl: 3.5%, HCP-YA: 4.3% | 3. bin = Heschl: 3.1%, HCP-YA: none). 

Conclusion

The replication of Heschl’s historical study, using the equally large HCP-YA MRI dataset, aimed at examining the linkage between the “deep winding” and the “bridged” CS. This goal seemed both reasonable and promising, given that the statistical principles advocated and implemented by Heschl, closely align with current methodological standards. 

The visual search for identifying the ”bridged” CS in the HCP-YA data set, plausibly also Heschl’s original approach, resulted in a comparable prevalence of the ”bridged” CS. An additional analysis of the subset of 40% twin individuals in the HCP-YA data set revealed a slightly higher prevalence in both mono- and dizygotic twin individuals, but no concordant “bridged” CS in any twin pair, suggesting that the “bridged” CS may be less strongly influenced by genetic factors and more shaped by environmental or developmental variability. The prevalence in HCP-YA non-twin individuals closely matches Heschl’s original prevalence, demonstrating the robustness of this rare anatomical variation across difference in samples and time.  

The height distribution of the PPfpm in the HCP-YA data set was extracted using an automated PPfpm landmark detection procedure on MRI-based CS depth profiles, a methodology fundamentally different from Heschl’s unspecified measurement techniques. We attribute the general shift of the HCP-YA height distribution toward slightly higher heights and the increased spread to the improved precision of the modern analysis methods. Never the less, Heschl’s key finding, a markedly skewed distribution with a long tail of cases exhibiting substantially elevated deep windings, ranging from twice as high to almost approaching the brain surface, was clearly replicated in the HCP-YA data set. When both data sets were transformed into the Heschl binning expressed in standard deviations, the similarity in skewness became evident, indicating that this anatomical feature displays remarkable stability across samples, historical periods and methodological frameworks. 

This successful replication not only corroborates Heschl’s hypothesis that the “deep winding” and the “bridged” CS represent different manifestations of the same anatomical structure but also highlights Heschl’s remarkable anatomical expertise and innovative application of statistical approaches to investigate both, the rare and the common. By combining historical insights with contemporary methodology, this study refines the anatomical understanding of the CS and facilitates more precise and comprehensive anatomical descriptions in future research.
