Introduction

The human capacity to engage in complex cognition and experience emotions relies heavily on the hippocampus and amygdala, structures adjacent to each other in the medial temporal lobe.1 Both are connected to each other and also to regions in the isocortex including prefrontal cortex, entorhinal cortex, parietal cortex and various subcortical areas for the hippocampus2; prefrontal cortex, temporal cortex and various subcortical areas for the amygdala.3 Yet it remains unclear how the connectivity profiles of the hippocampus and amygdala to the cortex are unique and where they overlap and how these structures are jointly embedded within large-scale cortical organization. Because both structures comprise multiple subfields/nuclei, a subregion-resolved mapping is needed to characterize divergence versus convergence in their connectivity profiles. 

Here, we mapped how the amygdala and hippocampus subregions show functional connectivity to the isocortex. We examined their relative positions in large-scale brain networks by analyzing their functional connectivity to the cortex at the subfield/subnucleus level, contrasting global co-fluctuation patterns (Pearson correlation) with more direct interactions captured by sparse partial correlation (graphical lasso; GLASSO).

Methods

We analyzed 1 hour of resting-state fMRI per subject from HCP-Young Adult (N = 722, Mean age = 28.48 +- 3.75, 380 females). Hippocampus was segmented using HippUnfold4 ( 5 subfields x 3 anterior-posterior bins x 2 hemispheres; 30 parcels) and amygdala using the Saygin et al segmentation5 (9 nuclei x 2 hemispheres; 18 parcels) subregions, yielding 48 seeds in total, all segmented in a subject-specific manner in native space. Targets were 358 Glasser parcels (hippocampal cortex removed). Functional connectivity (FC) was estimated using Pearson correlation (dense) and GLASSO (sparse), Fisher-z transformed and averaged across subjects. From the 48 x cortex block we constructed a seed-seed affinity matrix and applied diffusion map embedding to estimate joint hippocampus-amygdala gradients. Gradients were projected back to the cortex by correlating each component with seed-to-cortex profiles (restricted to top union 10% strongest connections of hippocampus/amygdala subregions for Pearson and the union of top 10% most prevalent connections per subregion from the sparse connectivity matrices for GLASSO, these masks were used for the follow-up analyses.). We derived (1) dominance metrics (hippocampus vs amygdala; anterior vs posterior hippocampus) and (2) a sharedness index quantifying how often and how evenly both hippocampus and amygdala strongly target the same cortical parcels.

Results

Top-10% seed-cortex connectivity showed consistent hippocampus versus amygdala biased territories, but also identifiable ‘shared hubs’ where both structures strongly project, indicating a graded dissociation. Across both Pearson and GLASSO, hippocampal subregions showed stronger coupling to posterior medial temporal areas, anterior cingulate, sensory and visual areas, whereas amygdala subnuclei preferentially targeted anterior insula, orbito-medial frontal, superior temporal gyrus, midcingulate, lateral occipito-temporal and motor cortices. GLASSO sharedness peaked in anterior temporal cortex (with additional medial prefrontal/paralimbic involvement), whereas Pearson sharedness shifted toward somatomotor and broader cortex, consistent with denser co-fluctuation structure. For the anterior to posterior hippocampus dominance, there was an orbitofrontal to cingulate axis present for GLASSO while a more general anterior posterior axis was present for Pearson. 

GLASSO gradients revealed: (1) G1 separating amygdala from hippocampus, with anterior hippocampus closer to amygdala than posterior hippocampus. Cortical projections of G1 mirror the hippocampus-amygdala dominance map; (2) G2 as a pronounced left-right lateralization axis; and notably (3) a consistent within-BLA axis across GLASSO gradients that is ordered paralaminar, lateral, basal, accessory basal, with paralaminar being most similar to hippocampus across gradients.

Conclusion

Hippocampus and amygdala subregions show both divergence and convergence in cortex, with convergence forming method-dependent “shared hubs.” Pearson captures broader co-fluctuation, while GLASSO isolates a sparser, more stable backbone that yields clearer lateralization and interpretable joint gradients. This work provides a large-sample, subregion-resolved framework jointly linking hippocampus and amygdala to cortical functional organization and comparing their direct and indirect connectivity patterns.
