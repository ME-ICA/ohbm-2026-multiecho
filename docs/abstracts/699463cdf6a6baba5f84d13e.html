Introduction

White matter hyperintensity (WMH), defined as increased brightness on MRI in T2-weighted fluid-attenuated images, has been linked to increased cognitive decline and dementia risk, and it typically indicates structural changes and potential small vessel damage in the brain.1 There’s also a verified relationship between WMH and the onset of Alzheimer’s Disease (AD).1,2 However, the underlying link between WMH and AD progression is still debated. Investigating this relationship is particularly helpful for understanding the development of AD pathology and the potential treatment plan.

Meanwhile, previous literature reported a systematic failure of neurotransmitter networks in AD patients, including the cholinergic, serotonergic and dopaminergic system,2 and multiple neurotransmitter-based drugs have proved to be successful in AD symptoms relief. However, the relationship between WMH and neurotransmitter network damage, and the neurochemical mechanism of WMH formation in AD remain unclear. Therefore, this study aims to unveil the relationship between neurotransmitter network damages and the small-vascular white matter changes indicated by WMH on MRI across the AD clinical spectrum.

Methods

1024 subjects from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) database were selected, which were categorized into six groups, including healthy control (CN, n = 98), Subjective Memory Complaints (SMC, n = 45), Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI, n = 508), Early MCI (EMCI, n = 138), Late MCI (LMCI, n = 61), and AD (n = 174). Inclusion criteria includes having at least one pair of FLAIR and T1-weighted images acquired at the same timepoint, the availability of basic demographic data and at least one cognitive assessment score available.

WMH segmentation was performed on all subjects with Lesion Growth Algorithm (LGA) from the LST toolbox (version 3.0.0) on SPM, running on MATLAB R2023a. All segmentations were manually inspected to ensure the quality. Abnormal results caused by an algorithm failure (defined as >10% of the total brain volume was classified as WMH, and the segmentation did not correspond with hyperintensity visible on the FLAIR images) were removed from further study. Then, the Total Brain Volume (TBV) of each subject was calculated by the FSL toolbox (version 6.0.7), running on Python 3.13.4. Finally, the percentage WMH volume was calculated by dividing the total WMH volume by TBV. Additionally, lobular WMH volume was calculated by applying the inversewarp of the registration on the MNI structural atlas from FSL library and overlapping with the WMH mask.

After the WMH segmentation mask was registered into MNI152 space, neurotransmitter profiles for 13 systems were calculated with the NeuroT-Map toolbox using the registered WMH lesion mask,3 including white-matter tract disconnection, receptor and transporter lesion, and pre to postsynaptic ratio (which is defined as the quotient of presynaptic damage proportion and postsynaptic damage proportion). These measurements were used to assess the neurochemical fingerprints of WMH in AD.

Amyloid-Beta (Aβ) and tau pathology data for PET, demographics data, and cognitive scale data were acquired from the ADNI database. Aβ and Tau data were classified into different regions according to Braak Staging, and the weighted average of each region was taken as the input to statistical analysis. Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) and Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) were selected as cognitive decline evaluation scales.

For statistical analysis, the linear mixed effect (LME) model was used to compare differences of WMH and neurotransmitter profiles between different groups (CN, SMC, EMCI, MCI, LMCI, and AD), controlling the site of MRI acquisition as a random factor, and age, gender, education, and total brain volume as the covariates. Benjamin-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was carried out after getting p-values. Multiple linear regression model was carried out to assess relationships between individual cognitive scores and imaging measurements. Lastly, mediation analysis was used to evaluate whether the effect of Aβ and Tau pathology on cognitive decline was mediated through mediators, such as WMH and neurotransmitter damage. The Aβ and Tau pathology was first evaluated across the entire brain volume, and then according to each Braak stage.

Results

Compared to CN group, there’s a significant increase in the WMH percentage volume of each group except for MCI, with the difference between CN and AD being the most significant (p < 0.001). At the lobular level, the frontal lobe WMH volume significantly increased in the AD group than MCI and SMC groups, and in SMC, EMCI, and LMCI groups compared to CN. Also, MCI patients had a significant increase compared to SMC patients. Furthermore, AD patients’ WMH volume showed significant differences in the temporal and occipital lobes compared to CN. However, after FDR correction, no lobular differences remain significant.

Several neurotransmitters markers showed significant differences across clinical groups after FDR. For receptor and transporter lesion, M1, VAChT, D1, D2, 5HT4, 5HT6, and 5HTT showed significant group-level differences in more than one comparison, and D2, 5HT4, and 5HTT shows the most significant heterogeneity, with three or more groups having significant differences. For the white matter tract disconnection comparison, the most robust finding was observed in the comparison between CN and AD, with significant differences found across all neurotransmitter systems (p < 0.01 for all). Additionally, seven neurotransmitter tracts show significant differences between the MCI and AD comparisons. No significant difference is found in other tracts. Next, several significant differences were found among clinical groups in the pre and postsynaptic ratio. Compared to SMC group, EMCI and LMCI both have significantly higher pre-synaptic damage ratio in D1, D2, and DAT. LMCI also specifically showed this pattern for 5HT-4 damage (p<0.01). AD has significant higher ratio of M1 and lower ratio of 5HT-2a compared to CN. Lastly, MCI has higher pre-synaptic ratio in M1, 5HT-1a, and 5HT-2a than CN. 

Additionally, multiple linear regression analysis revealed a significant correlation between the total WMH and MoCA score (p=0.0002, β=-0.444). When adding neurotransmitter markers into the linear regression variables, the best-performing model includes the receptors and transporters lesion of 5HT-4 and D1. Notably, the parameter estimate for D1 term is statistically significant (p=0.0097, β=-0.753).

Mediation analysis revealed WMH volume as a significant partial mediator between Aβ plague and cognitive impairment. In addition, all 13 neurotransmitter tract markers showed significant partial mediating effects. Among receptor and transporter lesion markers, M1 and 5HT-6 act as significant partial mediators. After classifying the Aβ and Tau pathology into Braak sub-regions, no significant mediators were found. However, the 5HT-4 presynaptic ratio exhibited a near significant mediating effects between Braak I/II and III/IV pathology and cognitive scale (p=0.0548 and 0.0656, respectively). Furthermore, receptor and transporter lesion markers of A4B2 and norepinephrine act as significant partial mediator between WMH volume and MoCA. 

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that WMH is a significant predictor of cognitive decline, and its contribution starts very early in the disease progression, as the between-group comparison demonstrated significantly different WMH volume between healthy control and almost all clinical groups. Besides, this study found frontal lobe volume difference across multiple clinical stages, signifying its vulnerability in AD progression. 

This study also confirmed a progressive and stage-dependent disruption in the neurotransmitter system. Unlike WMH, the neurotransmitter’s profile difference is distributed more evenly across multiple stages. Differences in receptor and transporter lesion across stages, such as D2, 5HT-4, and 5HT-T, showed heterogeneity and demonstrated the susceptibility of those systems in AD progression. In addition, significant differences were found in pre and postsynaptic ratio, mostly in the early stages, suggest the damage site will add an extra layer of intricacy to the impact of neurotransmitter disruption and help determine effects on patients’ treatment.  

The regression results and mediation analysis further corroborate the clinical relevance of the neurochemical fingerprint. WMH and multiple tract integrity and lesion markers act as significant partial mediators and predictors over cognitive decline. This is consistent with the earlier research works that reported cholinergic, dopamine, and serotonergic neurotransmitter connectivity changes in AD patients4,5. The further analysis shows receptor and transporter lesions are significant mediators between WMH and the cognitive decline, demonstrating their interconnectedness. The analysis above shows that while the complete cause of AD remains complex, the damage driven by Aβ and Tau pathology on cognitive decline operates at least in part due to damage to white matter tracts and neurotransmitter pathways.

To sum up, our study places Aβ and Tau pathological changes, WMH, neurotransmitter fingerprint, and cognition decline inside a single framework. It suggests Aβ and Tau pathology drives cognitive decline through vascular damages and neurotransmitter system disruption. The future research could target specific neurotransmitter pathways to understand the rationale behind the AD progression. Additionally, it also indicates that protecting white matter tract integrity and neurotransmitter pathways may have therapeutic value during the AD treatment.
