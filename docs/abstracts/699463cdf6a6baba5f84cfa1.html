Introduction

Head motion remains a major challenge in resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI), systematically biasing functional connectivity (FC) estimates. These biases typically inflate correlations among nearby regions and reduce correlations between distant ones (Power et al., 2012; Satterthwaite et al., 2012). To mitigate such effects, many studies use frame censoring, and the exclusion of high-motion runs. 

Frame censoring and run-level motion quality control are among the most widely used and effective preprocessing steps for reducing motion artifacts (Power et al., 2014). However, emerging work shows that stringent censoring reduces temporal degrees of freedom, worsening the reliability of FC estimates (Phạm et al., 2023), individual-specific parcellations (Laumann et al., 2015; Mejia et al., 2015), and personalized stimulation targets (Cash et al., 2021; Kong et al., 2025).

We examined how frame censoring and high-motion run exclusion influence two individualized applications: functional brain parcellations and rs-fMRI–guided transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) targeting as both depend on reliable FC estimates.

Using precision fMRI datasets with hours of data per participant, we defined “ground-truth” individualized references using aggressively censored, long-duration datasets (≥1 hour post-censoring). We then simulated runs with varying motion levels and applied different censoring and run-exclusion strategies to assess how closely each approach reproduced the ground-truth parcellations and TMS targets.

Lenient or no censoring consistently produced individualized parcellations and TMS targets that more closely matched the ground truth than those derived from strict censoring and run exclusion. These within-participant results complement prior across-participant evidence supporting strict motion exclusion (Power et al., 2014; Gratton et al., 2020), however suggest that less stringent censoring may better preserve individualized parcellations and TMS targeting fidelity.

Methods

Overview

For each densely sampled participant, we split rs-fMRI into (1) high-quality data used to estimate “ground-truth’’ individualized parcellations and TMS targets, and (2) remaining data used to generate simulated runs with varying motion levels. Simulated 10-min runs were created by recombining 1-min windows of non–ground-truth data, categorized into motion bins based on the percentage of frames that would be censored at strict thresholds (FD > 0.08 mm; DVARS > 50). Two simulated runs formed a session. Each simulated session was processed under different censoring strategies (strict censoring with run exclusion vs. lenient or no censoring). Resulting parcellations and TMS targets were compared with ground-truth using Dice overlap and Euclidean distance, respectively.

Datasets

We analyzed 51 densely sampled adults from nine publicly available MRI datasets (HBNSSI, IPCAS6, Kirby, MSC, MyConnectome, NSD, Newbold, Utah2, Yale Test–Retest). All datasets included structural MRI and repeated rs-fMRI sessions (6–30 min per run; 1.5T–7T scanners). After ground-truth criteria were applied, 23 participants contributed sufficient clean data to be selected for analyses.

Ground-truth definition

Ground-truth parcellations and targets were estimated from ≥1 hour of post-censored rs-fMRI – after applying strict censoring of FD ≤ 0.08 mm and DVARS ≤ 50 – and spanning ≥3 sessions to account for inter-session variability, reflecting stable individual network architecture. All runs underwent initial preprocessing (slice-timing correction, motion correction, respiratory pseudomotion filtering). Frames with FD > 0.08 mm or DVARS > 50, as well as adjacent frames, were censored; segments <5 frames were discarded. Runs were ranked by motion (mean z-scored FD and DVARS), and the cleanest runs were accumulated until criteria were met.

Preprocessing of ground-truth data

Ground-truth runs were processed using a standardized pipeline: motion correction, pseudomotion filtering, frame censoring, nuisance regression (global/WM/ventricular signals; motion parameters), Lomb–Scargle interpolation of censored frames, bandpass filtering (0.009–0.08 Hz), surface projection to fsaverage6, and smoothing (6 mm). Structural–functional alignment used BBR, and ANTs was used for MNI152 registration.

Ground-truth parcellations and targets

Individual-specific cortical parcellations were estimated using the MS-HBM (Kong et al., 2019). Personalized TMS depression targets were derived from these parcellations using the tree-based algorithm (Kong et al., 2025).

Simulated runs

Non–ground-truth fMRI was preprocessed identically (without censoring), z-scored, windowed into 1-min segments, and recombined into 10-min simulated runs drawn from different motion bins – no repeated windows within a simulated run were allowed. Two 10-min runs formed a simulated session – no common windows across paired simulated runs were allowed. 50 sessions were generated per participant per motion bin; 20 participants contributed sufficient data.

Censoring strategies

Each simulated session was reprocessed under multiple FD and DVARS thresholds (FD: none, 0.50, 0.20, 0.15, 0.10, 0.08 mm; DVARS: none, 100, 65, 60, 55, 50). Runs with <5 min of usable data were labeled “high-motion.” Analyses compared including vs. excluding such runs. Parcellation–ground-truth similarity was quantified using Dice coefficients; TMS target accuracy was measured via Euclidean distance.

Statistical analyses

Comparisons within motion bins used paired t-tests applied to participant-level averages across the 50 simulated sessions per condition per motion bin. To assess overall effects across all motion bins, linear mixed-effects models were fit with participant identity as a random intercept. We further included motion bin identity as a nested random intercept within participant. Models tested effects of censoring strategy or motion level while controlling for percent censored frames and number of surviving runs.

Results

1. Parcellation Analyses

We evaluated how different motion-censoring and high-motion run exclusion strategies influence the recovery of individual-specific ground-truth functional network parcellations. Parcellation quality was defined as the Dice coefficient between the estimated and ground-truth parcellations.

No censoring outperformed strict censoring with high-motion run exclusion.

Comparing no censoring to strict censoring (FD = 0.08 mm, DVARS = 50) with high-motion runs discarded revealed that no censoring produced parcellations with consistently higher or comparable Dice overlap across nearly all motion levels. Even in high-motion bins, parcellations generated without censoring retained substantial correspondence with the ground truth, indicating that high-motion runs still contain useful signal. In contrast, strict censoring discarded so much data—especially at higher motion levels—that parcellation quality degraded substantially. These findings demonstrate that aggressive data removal may be more harmful than retaining even noisy data.

Retaining high-motion runs improves ground-truth parcellation recovery.

To directly test whether high-motion runs should be retained, we compared parcellations derived from two high-motion runs without censoring to those from one lower-motion and one high-motion run under strict censoring (where the high-motion run is discarded). Contrary to the common assumption that including only “cleaner” data yields better estimates, parcellations from two high-motion runs without censoring more closely matched the ground truth. This indicates that preserving high-motion runs may retain more relevant signal than excluding motion-contaminated runs.

Lenient censoring outperformed strict censoring (results attached as image, on the left).

We next evaluated a wide range of FD and DVARS thresholds while retaining all runs. Across motion bins, stricter thresholds consistently reduced Dice overlap, whereas lenient thresholds (e.g., FD = 0.5 mm, DVARS = 100) achieved parcellations closer to the ground truth and were statistically indistinguishable from the no-censoring condition. These results suggest that excessive censoring removes meaningful signal and that lenient censoring is preferable when some form of censoring is required. This result is attached as an image, on the left.

Overall, across all analyses, parcellation quality decreased as censoring became stricter, demonstrating that preserving data—even when motion is high—yields more accurate individual-level parcellations.

2. TMS Target Simulations

We evaluated whether the same censoring principles that affected parcellation quality also influenced individualized TMS targets generated using the Kong2025 TMS depression target. Target accuracy was quantified as Euclidean distance to the ground-truth target.

Across analyses, results mirrored the parcellation findings:

(1) No censoring outperformed strict censoring with high-motion run removal.

Targets estimated without censoring were consistently closer to the ground truth than those obtained after strict censoring, particularly because strict censoring frequently eliminated signal relevant to targeting.

(2) Retaining high-motion runs improved target accuracy.

Targets derived from two high-motion runs (no censoring) outperformed targets computed from a lower-motion + high-motion run pair under strict censoring, again emphasizing the importance of retaining runs — even when they are high-motion.

(3) Lenient censoring outperformed strict censoring (results attached as image, on the right).

Lenient thresholds yielded appreciably smaller distances from the ground-truth target than strict thresholds and were comparable to the no-censoring condition. This result is attached as an image, on the right.

Overall, the optimal strategy for TMS targeting paralleled the optimal strategy for parcellations: retain all runs when possible, and if censoring is used, apply lenient rather than strict thresholds.

Conclusion

Leveraging precision fMRI datasets to construct individualized ground-truths, our simulations showed that lenient censoring and retaining high-motion runs produced individual-specific cortical parcellations and brain stimulation targets that were more consistent with the ground-truth than those derived from aggressive censoring and run-exclusion strategies. Overall, our results suggest that less stringent censoring and retaining high-motion runs can better preserve the fidelity of individualized FC-derived outcomes.
