Introduction

Rigorous assessment of data quality is a fundamental step for reliable functional MRI (fMRI) analyses, as fMRI data are highly vulnerable to multiple sources of noise, including head motion, geometric distortions, and signal dropouts. These artifacts can lead to spatial mislocalization and spurious connectivity patterns, which ultimately bias downstream results. Visual inspection has long been the standard approach for fMRI quality control (QC), relying on rater judgment to identify artifacts. While recent preprocessing pipelines have facilitated assessment, visual QC remains inherently subjective, labor-intensive, poorly reproducible, and increasingly unrealistic as massive datasets become available 

Here, we introduce a set of four straightforward metrics designed to strengthen fMRI QC in the era of large-scale neuroimaging, while enabling explicit modeling of data quality effects in statistical analyses. Specifically, we leveraged established alignment-based measures to objectively assess registration quality, and we have proposed a new, easily implementable and interpretable metric for quantifying signal dropout.

Methods

We analyzed 2,204 resting-state fMRI scans from the Anti-Amyloid Treatment in Asymptomatic Alzheimer’s (A4) and Longitudinal Evaluation of Amyloid Risk and Neurodegeneration (LEARN) study. The sample encompassed 437 cognitively unimpaired older adults at baseline (mean age = 71.9 ± 4.8 years, 58.4% women), followed for an average of 2.6 ± 2.1 years, with 5.0 ± 1.9 exams per participant.

Preprocessing was performed using fMRIprep, adapted with the Omni pipeline to correct for distortions (Fig. 1). Denoising was conducted using XCP-D, regressing the standard 36-parameters, and time series were extracted from individual-tailored Schaefer-400 parcellations. Connectomes were computed using pairwise Pearson correlations. Functional brain organization was characterized using global connectivity, intra-network connectivity and regional connectivity strength.

Visual QC was performed on a subset of 478 scans by ten raters with varying expertise. Raters assessed EPI–T1w coregistration accuracy and the presence of distortions and/or signal loss (0=Definite fail; 1=Some issues; 2=Minor/no issues). Uncertain ratings were resolved through consensus.

Quality metrics were derived for all scans (Fig. 1). Mean framewise displacement (FD) was computed to reflect motion issues. Normalized mutual information (NMI) was calculated with ANTs (Mattes) to quantify the cost of spatial alignment between i) EPI and T1w images in native space and ii) normalized T1w images and the MNI template. A dropout mask was defined as gray matter voxels outside the EPI-based functional brain mask. The dropout ratio was computed as the volume of this mask divided by the total gray matter volume.

EPI-T1w alignment and dropout metrics were evaluated against visual QC ratings of coregistration accuracy and distortion/dropout, respectively, using logistic regressions and Spearman correlations. Distribution-based thresholds (median + 3x median absolute deviation) of these metrics were used to automatically identify poor-quality scans. Dropout ratio threshold was further validated through a separate visual QC (n=30 scans stratified by dropout ratio) performed by a single experienced rater, specifically focused on signal dropout. Effects of data quality on functional connectivity measures were examined using bootstrapped linear mixed-effects models, comparing effect sizes before and after removing scans automatically identified as QC fails.

Results

Using a threshold-based classification, 21.2% of scans were identified as poor quality, primarily driven by aberrant values in mean FD and dropout ratio (Fig. 2A-B). NMI between EPI and T1w images correlated with visual coregistration ratings (ρ = -0.13, p < .001), and predicted visually identified coregistration failures (χ2 = 79.92, β = 54.01, z = 8.61, p < .001).

Dropout ratio was significantly correlated with visual ratings (ρ = -0.34, p < .001), and higher values strongly increased the probability of visual exclusion (χ2 = 489.07, β = 27.78, z = 18.76, p < .001; Fig. 2C). However, as a caveat,  a significant dropout × rater interaction (χ2 = 182.69, p < .001) revealed inter-rater variability, highlighting that visual QC is not a fully reliable ground truth.

The dropout ratio threshold demonstrated good concordance with an independent dropout-focused visual assessment (balanced accuracy=80%; κ = 0.60). As expected, disagreement was most frequent for scans with dropout ratios near the exclusion threshold (Fig. 2C).

Mean FD and dropout ratio were both associated with global functional connectivity, and explained variance decreased for both after filtering automated QC fails (R2[full] = 0.14, R2[filtered] = 0.11;  t = 35.48, p < .001). However, absolute effect estimates were greater in the filtered subset (FD: mean bootstrapped β=0.041 [0.035, 0.047]; dropout ratio: β=-0.086 [-0.01, -0.068]) compared to the full dataset (FD: β=0.030, 95%CI = [0.023, 0.037]; dropout ratio: β=-0.036 [-0.046, -0.027]). Metrics showed region-specific effects on parcelwise connectivity, especially intranetwork connectivity, and these effects persisted even after filtering. (Fig. 2D). 

Conclusion

Together, these findings suggest that a substantial proportion of resting-state fMRI data is affected by quality issues beyond motion that can be objectively captured using quantitative metrics that are easily collected during image processing. Alignment-based measures such as NMI effectively predicted visual coregistration failures despite their low prevalence. Meanwhile, the dropout ratio robustly tracked visual dropout judgments, highlighting the relevance of this novel and straightforward metric. Critically, identical levels of signal loss led to divergent decisions across raters, emphasizing the inherent subjectivity and limited reproducibility of visual inspection, and positioning quantitative metrics as a more robust and consistent alternative. Perhaps most importantly, subthreshold variation in these data quality metrics had a pervasive influence on connectivity patterns, even after aberrant values were removed. Accounting for this variability is critical to prevent downstream analyses from being confounded by artifacts. We are currently validating these findings in two additional large-scale aging cohorts.
