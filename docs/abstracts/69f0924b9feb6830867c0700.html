Introduction

Once viewed primarily as a memory structure, the hippocampus is increasingly recognized as a key node in predictive processing networks1. Intracranial recordings and fMRI studies have demonstrated that hippocampal activity tracks both prediction errors during learning and predictions during stable associations (see e.g.2). Evidence also suggests that connectivity between the hippocampus and visual cortex increases when an association has been learned2, with recent evidence suggesting that information flows from hippocampus to the neocortex via deep layers of the entorhinal cortex3.  

Also in the auditory domain, the influence of predictive processing has been amply documented by behavioral, neuroimaging and computational research (see e.g.4 for a review). However, to date, direct evidence of the role of hippocampal cortical connectivity in the processing of (auditory) expectation is scarce. Moreover, predictive processing in humans likely involves large-scale interactions of the hippocampus with structures beyond primary sensory areas such as the frontal cortex. 

Here we take advantage of the large coverage afforded by ultra-high-field (UHF) fMRI (at 7 Tesla) to investigate hippocampal-cortical interaction with layer specificity and covering a large portion of the neocortex. Participants learned that visual cues predicted specific auditory syllables5. Importantly, we also included unpredictable trials to isolate context-dependent effects. Using single trial correlations, and their modulation with context, we investigated the connectivity within a network of areas involved in the prediction of acoustic content. 

Methods

Participants

Sixteen participants (10 females) took part in this study (no history of neurological or hearing disorders and had normal or corrected to normal vision). Data were collected at the Scannexus facility (Maastricht University, The Netherlands). The study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee for Psychology and Neuroscience (ERCPN) at Maastricht University. Five participants were excluded from the analysis due to distortions, inability to complete the task and head coil size restrictions, leaving a total of eleven participants (8 females).

Experimental design

All participants completed one behavioral training session and two fMRI sessions (84 minutes each). Experimental conditions and procedures were adapted from5, focusing on temporally regular stimuli and including rare omission trials apart from invalid trials (fig.1A). Trials were presented in two contexts: predictable and unpredictable. Predictable runs consisted of 24 trials per run (eight runs in total), in which visual cues reliably predicted the upcoming auditory syllable (75% valid, 12.5% invalid, 12.5% omissions). Unpredictable runs (16 trials per run, six runs in total) preserved the same sequence structure but lacked predictive contingencies (12.5\% omissions). Across the two sessions, each participant completed 288 trials in total: 192 predictable and 96 unpredictable.

Acquisition

We collected data using a 7T System (Siemens Medical Systems, Erlangen) with a 32-channel receive NOVA head coil (Nova Medical Inc., USA). Data collection included T1 weighted images (MP2RAGE - 0.7 mm isotropic, 240 slices, TR = 5000 ms, TE = 2.39 ms, TI1/TI2 = 800/2750 ms, FA1/FA2 = 4°/5°, GRAPPA Factor 3) and functional (T2* weighted) time series (multi-band GE-EPI; 0.8 mm isotropic, 66 slices, TR = 2500 ms, TE = 26 ms, MB Factor 2, GRAPPA Factor = 3). For every subject we collected 5 reversed phase encoded volumes for distortion correction. 

Data Analysis

BrainVoyager (v22.2, Brain Innovation, Maastricht, The Netherlands) was used in all anatomical and functional pre-processing steps. Anatomical data were skull stripped, corrected for residual inhomogeneities, up-sampled (0.4 mm isotropic) and segmented to delineate the cortical gray mater ribbon. Within the cortical gray matter, we defined, using an equi-volume approach, deep, middle and superficial cortical layers.

Neocortical regions of interest were limited to a sub-network of areas that in an independent analysis showed univariate effects for validity and context (pSTG, pMTG, TPOj, pars Orbitalis and pars Triangularis). Apart from cortical regions, we segmented the hippocampus bilaterally for each individual participant and defined five sub-regions: the subiculum (SUB), Cornu Ammonis (CA1, CA2-3), and the dendate gyrus (DG) (https://hsf.readthedocs.io/en/latest/).

Functional data were slice-scan-time corrected, motion corrected, distortion corrected and co-registered with the anatomical data using boundary base registration. We estimated the response (in percent signal change) to single trials using GLMSingle (https://github.com/cvnlab/GLMsingle).

Functional connectivity was estimated using (single trial) beta series correlations (Pearson) independently for predictable and unpredictable trials (fig.1B). All analyses were limited to valid trials in the predictable condition and trials in which a sound was presented in the unpredictable condition. In all regions of interest, prior to beta series correlations, voxels were selected on an omnibus test (vs. silence) at an uncorrected threshold of <0.05. Bootstrapping (n=1000) was used to equalize the number of trials across predictable and unpredictable contexts. The resulting correlation matrices were Fisher-z transformed and subtracted (predictable - unpredictable) to obtain contextual effects. Statistical significance was computed at the group level using permutations and corrected for multiple comparisons using False Discovery Rate (FDR). 

Results

In both predictable and unpredictable contexts, functional connectivity highlighted strong correlations between hippocampal subfields and neocortical regions. Predictability significantly modulated these correlations in a layer and regions specific manner (Figure 2). We identified significant stronger correlation during predictable trials between DG and deep layers of pSTG and of the CA3 with middle and superficial layers of the pSTG. Frontal cortical areas on the other hand showed stronger connectivity with CA1 during predictable trials, with this effect being localized to deep and  middle layers of pars Orbitalis and superficial layers of pars Triangularis.

Conclusion

The hippocampus has been identified of fundamental relevance to predictive processes1. Previous evidence from visual experiments indicates its role in representing predictable information and its communication to para-hippocampal and primary sensory areas. We here extend these results to the auditory domain and highlight a distinct laminar interaction between temporal and frontal areas.  The response of temporal cortices during predictable trials correlated with DG and CA3 (more than in an unpredictable context), indicating that temporal cortical areas may be affected by pattern completion hippocampal processes. This connectivity exhibited an interesting laminar pattern with deep layers of pSTG correlating with DG and middle and superficial layers with CA3, possibly indicating a role of the hippocampus in both establishing cortical predictive models. The responses in frontal areas, on the other hand, correlated with CA1 during predictable trials more than unpredictable ones, indicating a possible influence of pattern completion mechanisms within this circuitry.
