Introduction

Healthy aging is characterized by a simultaneous decline in cognitive control and an enrichment of semantic knowledge, which is thought to reflect a change in in neurocognitive mode, and notably language production as this function involves controlled lexico-semantic access and retrieval (i.e., semantic control).^1

Resting-state fMRI and DWI analysis have provided substantial microstructural and functional evidence for the change in midlife, with implications for processes engaging a brain-wide interaction between perceptual-motor, control, and semantic systems that support lexical production. ^2,3

  • Up until midlife, correct semantic control performance correlates with flexible DMN–FPN modulation, along with high microstructural integrity of subcortico-frontal and dorsal association pathways. 
  • Beyond midlife, to face the cognitive decline, older adults may rely on a semantic strategy for maintaining lexical production performance. This strategy involves increased connectivity within lower-level attentional (CON) and sensorimotor (SMN) networks, as well as enhanced integrity within a left-dominant subcortico-sensory white matter network. 

Nonetheless, how structure-function (SF) dynamics jointly drive age-related cognition remains unclear. A thorough model requires both imaging modalities, explicitly modeling the constraints that structure imposes on functional activity. 

Our main hypothesis is that midlife represents a critical period at the junction of two SF architectures :

  • the first, dominant in younger adulthood, characterized by the decoupling of DMN-FPN activity from the underlying structure, offering flexible attentional processes for controlled access to the semantic store. 

the second, dominant in older adulthood, characterized by enhanced SF coupling of sensorimotor cortices, providing the basis for comparing sensory information to long-term semantic memory traces.

Methods

To propose a comprehensive multimodal model of healthy aging, we examined the reorganization of structure-function-cognition relationships at rest in a population-based sample of 597 individuals aged 18-88 yo from CamCAN (Approval by the Cambridgeshire 2 Research Ethics Committee; ref: 10/H0308/50).^4

1.1. MRI Preprocessing

Structural connectome: DWI data. MRtrix3 (v3.0.4) pipeline including denoising, Gibbs artifact removal, eddy current, motion, bias correction, 1mm3 upsampling. FODs computed using the MSMT constrained spherical deconvolution (CSD) algorithm with group-averaged response functions. Whole-brain probabilistic tractography: iFOD2 (5M streamlines, SIFT2). 

Functional connectome: Resting-state fMRI data for 8 min 40 s (261 volumes, TR = 1.97 s). Standard preprocessing pipeline (fMRIPrep). Confound removal strategy that preserves signal continuity (high-pass, full 24 motion parameters, 2 WM/CSD, 1 GSR, 6mm FWHM kernel).

Atlas: HCP-MMP 1.0 (360 nodes).

1.2. Structure-Function analysis

We used a graph signal processing (GSP) framework (NiGSP toolbox, v0.19.0). The core idea is to apply signal-processing operations to graph-structured data, assuming that the fMRI signal resides on the graph vertices defined by the structural connectome. ^5

Our procedure involves 4 key steps:

  1. Extract structural connectome harmonics. The Laplacian of a graph is defined as L = D - A, where D is the degree matrix and A is the adjacency matrix. Harmonics are obtained through eigendecomposition of the normalized Laplacian and carry a notion of spatial frequency: low-eigenvalue harmonics vary smoothly along the graph, whereas high-eigenvalue ones capture more localized variations (Figure 1A).
  2. Align subject-level harmonics. To enable comparison between subjects, we aligned eigenspaces to a reference space. This reference is built from a group-representative connectome using distance-dependent consensus-based thresholding. The alignment procedure exploits the Hungarian algorithm to find the optimal permutation of harmonics that maximizes the cosine similarity with the reference. This procedure resulted in a significantly improved harmonic similarity between subjects (Figure 1B).
  3. Project the fMRI signal onto harmonics. Using the Graph Fourier Transform (GFT), we projected each timepoint of the fMRI signal onto each structural harmonic, thus expressing the signal in the graph spectral domain.
  4. Structure-Decoupling Index (SDI). Our main metric is the SDI which measures the ratio between the decoupled and coupled component of the signal. A high SDI indicates high SF decoupling and conversely, a low SDI indicates high SF coupling. For each subject, structurally-informed null models were generated to assess significance using a spectral randomization procedure. 

1.3. Statistical analysis

All models included sex, handedness, total intracranial volume, and mean framewise displacement as covariates.

  • The effect of age on SDI was examined with generalized additive modeling in R (v4.3.2), with age as a smooth predictor (k = 3, REML), adjusted for significance at the FDR.
  • The relationship between SDI values (Y) and lexical production (X) across the lifespan was examined using Partial Least Squares (PLS) correlation analysis in MATLAB R2020b. 

We included 8 cognitive scores derived from neuropsychological tasks (picture naming, verbal fluency, tip-of-the-tongue, proverb comprehension, sentence comprehension, story recall, Cattell task, Hotel task), that assess control and semantic processing. 

We also included two age variables derived from piecewise polynomials to infer potential nonlinear age trajectories. This approach has been validated in previous teamwork. Statistical significance and robustness were assessed via 10,000 permutations and 1,000 bootstrap resamples, respectively. Features with a bootstrap ratio (BSR ± 3) – the salience weight divided by its bootstrapped standard deviation – were considered robust at the 99% confidence level.

Results

1.1. Age effect on the structure-function decoupling index (SDI)

At the whole-brain level, healthy aging was associated with enhanced decoupling (F = 16.19, p < .001, edf = 1, partial R² = .03), suggesting reduced support from large-scale structural axes (i.e., low-frequency harmonics).

At the region level, we found nonlinear alterations with an inflection point at age 54. Further analysis showed increased coupling with age in transmodal cortices such as the DMN and attentional networks such as the DAN and FPN. In comparison, increased decoupling with age was observed in the SMN, Visual, and Auditory networks (Figure 2A).

Moreover, key regions exhibited a U-shape trajectory with age (i.e., greater coupling until midlife followed by greater decoupling), potentially acting as key structure-function interfaces during healthy aging. Further analysis based on TractSeg’s white matter bundle segmentations revealed that these regions straddle associative (e.g., 91% of fibers of left SLF II transit via these regions), callosal (CC4: 79% and CC7: 84%), and projection fibers (left optic radiations: 85%, left thalamo and striato-thalamo occipital pathways: 79% & 76%).

1.2. Structure-Function-Cognition analysis

We further sought to relate SDI changes to lexical production performance using the Partial Least Squares (PLS) correlation analysis (Figure 2B).

In line with our hypothesis, we identified one major cognitive control component (LC1) which explained 72.6% (pFDR = .001) of the total shared variance and one semantic component (LC2) which explained 9.4% more variance (pFDR = .002). As shown in Figure 4, key lexical production tasks such as picture naming (BSR = 21.55/11.05) and verbal fluency tasks (BSR = 9.87/16.45) were positively correlated to both components, thereby confirming the cognitive control and semantic aspects of age-related lexical production when fusing imaging modalities.

At a cognitive level, LC1 was mainly associated with better picture naming performances (21.55), enhanced fluid abilities Cattell task (20.27), long-term memory (13.36) and fewer tip-of-the-tongue occurrences (11.41). These tasks performances significantly decreased beyond midlife. This pattern of cognitive decline was mostly associated with reduced decoupling in the inferior parietal, posterior and mid cingulate cortices, and dlPFC, combined with reduced coupling in the SMN and visual cortices.

At a cognitive level, LC2 was mainly driven by semantic abstraction as measured by the proverb task (18.07), verbal fluency (16.45), sentence comprehension (12.49) and picture naming (7.1), after accounting for the decrease in fluid abilities (-4.19). These tasks performances peaked in midlife and remained relatively stable until very old age. This pattern of cognitive enhancement was mostly associated with increased coupling in the SMN, combined with increased decoupling in key regions including the left medial prefrontal (area L-10r), left middle insular (area L-MI), left lateral temporal (area L-TE1), and left posterior visual (area L-V3CD) areas, which appear to outline the left inferior frontal occipital fasciculus (IFOF). Similarly, increased decoupling in the right anterior cingulate (area R-a24pr) and right precuneus at the junction with the occipital lobe appears to outline terminations of a superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF) branch.

Conclusion

Consistent with our overarching hypothesis, the results reveal that control and semantic systems undergo distinct age-related patterns of structure–function reorganization, with midlife marking a pivotal turning point. 

  • Up until midlife, cognition is supported by a combination of structurally coupled sensorimotor integration and structurally decoupled transmodal activity, facilitating cognitive control. 
  • Beyond midlife, cognitive processes become increasingly semanticized and embodied: stronger structure–function coupling within sensorimotor regions coexists with greater decoupling along long-range semantic pathways. This architecture likely supports the preservation of semantic control in later adulthood. 

Collectively, these findings suggest that sensorimotor dynamics provide a core scaffold for lexical production across the lifespan, extending current neurocognitive aging models such as DECHA, LARA, and SENECA. ^1,2,3

Structure-function basis of cognitive control decline

While healthy aging globally altered the relationship between large-scale structural axes and functional activity, this effect was not uniform across functional networks. 

Higher-level networks, such as the DMN, DAN, and FPN, showed the most reduction in decoupling with age, as shown in Figure 2A. This pattern further correlated with the onset of control deficits related to lexical production in midlife, as shown in Figure 2B, reaffirming word finding as a multimodal phenomenon. In comparison, lower-level networks, such as the SMN, Visual, and Auditory networks, showed reduced coupling associated cognitive control deficits.

Collectively, these results align with our previous teamwork in showing that structure-function dynamics reconfigures along the unimodal-to-transmodal gradient, further establishing this gradient as a foundational organizational principle of neurocognition across imaging modalities and of spatiotemporal functional growth across the human lifespan. ^2,3 

Structure-function basis of the semantic strategy

After accounting for cognitive control decline, we identified the expected semantic trajectory which grew from younger adulthood to midlife and remained stable with age. As noted in the Introduction, previous research had already identified a left subcortico-sensory white matter network supporting this strategy in middle-aged adults. ^3

Here, our study confirms that pathways converging on the SMN play a central role in this semantic strategy. Indeed, Age-related lexico-semantic reorganization involves an interaction between the structurally-coupled sensorimotor system and structurally-decoupled regions outlining the IFOF, a pathway crucial to long-term semantic representation.

Embodied semantics offers a useful framework for interpreting these findings. In face of cognitive control decline, enhanced sensorimotor structure-function coupling could provide faster, “phase-locked” response to the environment that enhances the fidelity of “embodied internal models” in older adulthood. Such an embodied semantic strategy could provide the basis for predictive or exploitative cognition where sensory information is compared against a lifetime of semantic memory representations to help with lexical access and retrieval.
