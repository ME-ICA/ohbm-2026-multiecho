Introduction

Reaching to spatial targets can rely on body-centered (egocentric; EGO) or landmark-centered (allocentric; ALLO) reference frames. Target memory for EGO reaching is associated with activity in superior occipital cortex whereas ALLO target memory was associated with inferior occipital / temporal activation, leading to parietofrontal activation during the action (Chen et al. 2014). More recently, a functional network analysis showed that EGO reach is associated with four network modules: a superior occipital-parietal / somatomotor module, an inferior parietal / lateral frontal module, a superior temporal / inferior frontal module, and an inferior occipital-temporal / prefrontal module, whereas ALLO reaching showed only three modules, in part because inferior occipital nodes were incorporated into the superior occipital-parietal / somatomotor module (Musa et al. 2025). These modules included two prominent occipital hubs: a superior occipital hub (ExstrSup-2) shared by both EGO and ALLO reaching, and an inferior occipital hub (ExstrInf-2) associated more specifically with the ALLO task. These findings are in general agreement with neuropsychological findings from damage to the dorsal and ventral visual streams (Schenk et al., 2005) but causal data linking the network hubs directly to behavior in healthy individuals is lacking. The purpose of the current investigation was twofold: 1) to establish this causal link between our superior and inferior occipital network hubs to EGO and ALLO reach behaviors, respectively, and 2) to explain any such TMS effects in terms of established functional network properties. Based on our previous results (Musa et al. 2025) we hypothesized that TMS over the inferior temporal site would impair ALLO reaches, whereas TMS over the superior occipital site would influence both EGO and ALLO behaviors, and engage broader networks. 

Methods

Sixteen participants performed a delayed reach-to-touch task in the presence of a visual landmark, which they were either instructed to ignore (EGO task) or use (ALLO task) for target coding (Musa et al., 2024). Online triple-pulse TMS (1 Hz, 110% of motor threshold) was delivered during the memory delay phase (Figure 1) to either our superior occipital hub, or the inferior occipital hub (ExstrInf-2) identified in our previous study (Musa et al. 2025). Brainsight (Rogue Research) was used for online neuro-navigation. To account for individual variability, TMS coordinates were defined using Group Prior Individual Parcellation (GPIP) based on previously collected resting state MRI data. Behavioral performance during TMS and no-TMS controls was assessed through accuracy, quantified by the absolute error in reach endpoint, movement precision, quantified by reach endpoint ellipse area at a specific target location and reaction time, the time from the go signal to movement onset. Behavioral data (collapsed across participants) were predicted using functional connectivity strengths derived from the 7- and 17-network Schaefer atlas parcellations (Schaefer et al., 2018). Connectivity measures were computed from each participant’s resting-state connectivity matrix as Fisher r-to-z–transformed averages for regions of interest, calculated separately for bilateral networks and for each hemisphere.

Results

Consistent with our hypothesis, TMS to the superior occipital hub significantly impaired both accuracy and precision (Figure 2) across both the EGO and ALLO tasks, whereas stimulation of the inferior occipital hub selectively disrupted accuracy and reduced reaction time in the ALLO task. Control no-TMS behavior (reach accuracy and precision in both tasks) was best predicted by inter-subject variations in the Visual (Vis), Somatomotor (SM), Dorsal Attention, and Control (Con) networks. Default Mode (Def) network solely predicted accuracy and precision of control ALLO reaches. The influence of TMS (absolute error relative to controls), was best explained using a 17 network and lateralized to the left. Across participants, behavioural changes due to the superior TMS site stimulation was predicted by the Ventral Peripheral (VP), Visual Central (VC), SM and Con subdivisions (both tasks), whereas the inferior TMS site was predicted by more specific left, VP, VC, and DefA networks. In the latter, higher connectivity was linked to greater EGO errors but reduced ALLO errors.

Conclusion

These findings provide causal evidence that dorsal stream regions contribute to both egocentric and allocentric reaching in healthy individuals, whereas ventral stream regions are specialized for allocentric, landmark-based actions. Further, the behavioral perturbations were predicted by inter-participant network variations, with broader network effects associated with the superior occipital stimulation site (compared to the inferior occipital site), likely due to its integrative role for both EGO and ALLO information into the motor system (Chen et al. 2018; Musa et al., 2025). Overall, the findings confirm the previous dorsal-ventral distinction for EGO / ALLO visual coding, prove a causal role of specific network hubs, and support specific functional network properties for the elaboration of these behaviors in the brain.
