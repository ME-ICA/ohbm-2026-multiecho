Introduction

Human social interaction is a powerful engine of emotional alignment1,2, yet the conversational ingredients that ignite group-level convergence—and the degree to which topic valence steers the process—remain elusive. The present study tested two hypotheses: (1) emotional convergence is modulated by topic valence, and (2) both visible cues (facial expressions, language) and invisible signals (physiological and neural synchrony) drive the process. To answer these questions, we orchestrated controlled, face-to-face discussions among strangers while tracking multimodal affective signals in real time.

Methods

Two hundred and forty adults were recruited via campus advertisements and got compensated. Same-gender participants were randomly assigned to 30 positive-topic groups or 30 neutral-topic groups. Each group first read a short priming text to establish common ground, then engaged in a 10-minute leaderless discussion seated in a circular arrangement that ensured mutual eye contact. Every 120 seconds, participants privately rated momentary positive affect and negative affect on 10-point Likert scale. Concurrently, 46-channel functional near-infrared spectroscopy monitored oxy-haemoglobin dynamics in the prefrontal cortex, frontal lobe, temporal lobe, and temporoparietal junction; a BIOPAC system recorded electrocardiogram and electrodermal activity; and four cameras in front of each participant captured their facial expression.

Time-resolved synchrony was quantified at multiple levels. Pairwise electrodermal activity correlations were computed within each 2-minute epoch; wavelet transform coherence indexed inter-brain coupling in pre-processed functional near-infrared spectroscopy data; Edinburgh Distance summarized divergence in self-report positive affect, negative affect, and heart-rate time series; and dynamic time warping aligned facial-action trajectories to yield a continuous mimicry score. Linear mixed-effects models included fixed effects of Time (5 levels), Condition (positive vs. neutral), and Emotion Type (positive affect vs. negative affect), with random intercepts for groups.

Results

Self-reported affect revealed a significant main effect of Time (β = -0.39, SE = 0.01, p < 0.001) and Emotion Type (β = -0.28, SE = 0.07, p < 0.001), but no Condition effect or interactions. Physiological channels mirrored this pattern: heart-rate synchrony increased over time (β = -0.03, SE = 0.01, p < 0.001) and electrodermal activity synchrony rose steadily (β = -0.01, SE = 0.003, p = 0.002), both independent of topic valence. Facial mimicry remained stable during conversation, indicating obligatory, topic-invariant mirroring. Preliminary functional near-infrared spectroscopy analyses (60 groups processed) show inter-brain coherence across multiple channels and frequencies; permutation-based cluster correction for multiple comparisons is currently running.

Conclusion

These results establish that unstructured conversation among strangers is sufficient to elevate positive mood and sculpt physiological rhythms into collective resonance—effects that emerge independently of conversational content. The immutable facial mimicry we observed points to an evolutionary “social glue” that operates continuously rather than contingently. By demonstrating topic-invariant contagion, our findings illuminate a core mechanism of group psychotherapy: therapeutic gains may partly reflect spontaneous emotional synchronization rather than deliberate thematic processing.

Future work should manipulate turn-taking dynamics and acquaintance level to pinpoint the minimal social ingredients for contagion.
