Introduction

Cancer-related cognitive impairment (CRCI) is reported in up to 50-75% of survivors at some point in treatment, with executive functioning and processing speed often affected.1 Structural neuroimaging studies have begun to elucidate the neural substrates likely underlying CRCI, and have predominantly shown regional reductions in gray matter volume, with the most consistent findings being frontal volume reductions and associated cognitive deficits (i.e., executive functioning) in survivors relative to controls. Although there is structural MRI literature demonstrating gray matter volume reduction, particularly in the frontal cortex,2 notable gaps remain in our understanding of cancer-related brain changes, especially in older adults facing compounding aging-related cognitive decline and comorbid chronic conditions. This issue is particularly relevant within the framework of the “multiple hit” model, which posits that when the brain is subjected to many stressors, there is a compounding effect that accelerates biological and brain aging in addition to individual differences. The cause(s) of CRCI and accelerated biological aging in cancer survivors are unknown but are thought to include biological damage due to cancer and/or its treatments (DNA damage, epigenetic alterations, altered cellular signaling, telomere shortening, mitochondrial dysfunction), increased psychosocial stress, and changes in health behaviors.3 Furthermore, the combined effect of cancer and comorbidities may accelerate biological aging,3 resulting in neural structural and functional decline beyond that expected for chronological age through mechanisms including oxidative stress, compromised DNA repair, dysregulated calcium signaling, and aberrant network activity. Elucidating how comorbidities interact with cancer- and treatment-related brain changes could provide critical insight into individual risk and inform personalized medicine approaches.

Methods

The Thinking and Living with Cancer (TLC) study is a multicenter, prospective cohort study examining the impact of cancer and cancer treatment on cognition in older breast cancer survivors. Neuroimaging was conducted at Indiana University, one of 6 participating TLC sites, following their Alzheimer’s Disease Research Center (ADRC) protocol. Survivors aged 60 years or older with non-metastatic breast cancer (n=64) and frequency-matched non-cancer controls (n=62) underwent structural MRI and neuropsychological testing, and self-reported cognition and health information at baseline (pre-systemic therapy/enrollment) and 12- and 24-month follow-up. Exclusion criteria included neurological or major psychiatric disorders, sensory impairment, or MRI incompatibility. Scans were performed using the same Siemens Prisma 3T scanner and 64-channel head and neck coil. A T1-weighted 3D magnetization-prepared rapid gradient-echo (MPRAGE) sequence was used for ROI-based FreeSurfer analysis and brain age estimation using brainageR.4 We selected regions known to be related to the cognitive domains commonly affected in cancer-related cognitive impairment and aging, and selected regional values were summed bilaterally to create frontal, temporal, parietal, basal ganglia, thalamic, limbic, and insular volumes (as in our prior work).5 Objective cognitive assessment included z-scores for two domains: attention, processing speed, and executive function (APE), and learning and memory (LM). The Wide Range Achievement Test-4 (WRAT-4) Word Reading subtest was used to estimate baseline cognitive reserve. Comorbidities were assessed in all participants as a count of self-reported comorbidities. Longitudinal linear mixed models tested the effects of survivor/control group, time, and group-by-time interactions on regional volumes, controlling for age and comorbidities. Exploratory secondary analyses examined relationships between group, comorbidities, age, gray matter volume, and cognition.

Results

Survivors exhibited significantly greater gray matter decline relative to controls over 24 months in frontal (p=0.025), thalamic (p=0.008), and limbic (p=0.015) regions, with similar but non-significant patterns in parietal (p=0.055) and temporal (p=0.091) regions. There was a trend-level group-by-time interaction for brain age (p=0.093), indicating survivors tended to have an acceleration of brain aging compared to controls. When examining volume reduction over time as a function of age across groups, survivors lost volume in frontal and thalamic regions at nearly twice the average annual rate of controls. In secondary analyses, survivors with high comorbidity burden (≥3 comorbidities) exhibited lower frontal volume than controls; this difference became more significant over time: baseline (p=0.053), 12 months (p=0.064), 24 months (p=0.019). Survivors and controls with ≤2 comorbidities did not significantly differ at any time point (all p-values>0.72). No trends toward such an interaction were apparent for thalamic or limbic volume. Cognitively, survivors failed to show practice effects in the attention, processing speed, and executive functioning neuropsychological domain, whereas controls improved significantly over time (group-by-time interaction: p=0.030).

Conclusion

This study indicates that older breast cancer survivors experience gradual reductions in brain volume that persist beyond the acute treatment period. The most clinically actionable finding from this study is the comorbidity-by-group trend related to frontal volume, whereby high comorbidity burden (≥3 comorbidities) tended to amplify frontal vulnerability to volumetric decline in survivors but not controls. If confirmed, this finding could support the idea of a “multiple hit” model where comorbidities and cancer converge to affect additive pathological pathways. Furthermore, this could provide an explanation for why only a subgroup of survivors exhibits CRCI. The regional pattern of volumetric declines in this sample highlights systems-level vulnerability likely affecting connected neural networks, which is consistent with prior literature, where frontal changes have been most observed and are sometimes accompanied by parietal and/or temporal declines. Accelerated brain age may be used in conjunction with frontal gray matter loss to help identify individuals at greatest risk for cognitive deficits. Importantly, this study highlighted that thalamic and limbic regions also show greater decline in survivors after the acute treatment phase. Thalamo-cortical circuits are critical for executive control and are altered in various neurological and psychiatric disorders, and limbic regions are sensitive to stress, inflammation, and hormonal levels, all of which can be altered in the experience of breast cancer and its treatment. As the number of older cancer survivors continues to rise, integrating standardized cognitive and comorbidity care into survivorship care is clinically important and an opportunity to improve long-term outcomes.
