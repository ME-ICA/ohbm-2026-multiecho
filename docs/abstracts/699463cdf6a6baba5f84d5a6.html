Introduction

Understanding how the brain encodes and organizes visual information remains a central challenge in cognitive neuroscience. It requires analytical approaches that capture distributed, multivoxel patterns of neural activity. Representational Similarity Analysis (RSA) provides such a framework by quantifying the similarity structure of neural activation patterns across stimuli, brain regions, individuals, or computational models.^1

RSA has become a powerful framework for studying how perceptual information is encoded across the cortex and how stable these representations are across individuals. However, the factors that shape representational similarity remain unclear. Testing the stability of these represetnations under different condtions, and in particular, across different participants, allows us to map what features these multivoxel representations  are sensitive to. Changes in representation which are consistent across participants open a window to understanding the information carried by these similarity structures, and perhaps uncover universal principles of how the brain encodes information. 

Using a publicly available, large-scale 7T fMRI data of natural image viewing (the Natural Scenes Dataset), we test how repeated exposure to the same stimulus affects representational similarity, and whether any such change will be systematic across participants. We uncover two phenomena, which both point to shared information coding principles across participants:  (1) The Novelty Effect, in which we find that initial stimulus presentations evoke more consistent neural representations across individuals than later repetitions, and (2) The Repetition Identity Effect, namely that representations are more similar across participants for the same repetition number. Together, these findings indicate a universal structured consistency in how repeated stimuli are processed.

Methods

We analyzed publicly available fMRI data from the Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD), which comprises 7T fMRI recordings from eight participants viewing up to 10,000 naturalistic images each, with 1,000 images shared across all participants.^2 Images were presented within a continuous recognition memory task, in which participants judged each image as “new” or “old.” Each image was shown three times in a pseudo-randomized order, allowing us to probe repetition-related effects. We focused on the four participants (subj01, subj02, subj05, subj07) who completed the full set of 40 sessions to maximize cross-participant comparability. 

We used preprocessed single-trial beta estimates provided by the NSD authors, along with parcellation metadata. Each voxel was assigned to one of 180 cortical regions from the HCP-MMP1 atlas.^3 For each participant, repetition (1–3), and region, we constructed an activity matrix (images x voxels) containing the beta responses to the 1,000 shared images. Activity matrices were z-scored across images to normalize voxel-wise signal before similarity analyses.

For each participant, repetition, and region, we computed a first-order Representational Similarity Matrix (RSM) by correlating multivoxel response patterns between all pairs of images (Pearson correlation, yielding a 1000x1000 symmetric matrix). To assess representational consistency across participants, repetitions, and regions, we then derived second-order RSMs by correlating the upper triangular portions of first-order RSMs, producing 12x12 matrices (4 participants x 3 repetitions) for each region pair (see Figure 1).

From these second-order RSMs, we defined two key metrics. The Novelty Effect quantified whether first presentations (repetition 1) showed higher inter-participant representational similarity than later presentations (repetitions 2–3). The Repetition Identity Effect tested whether similarity was higher when comparing the same repetition across participants (e.g., repetition 2 vs 2) than different repetitions (2 vs 3), focusing on later repetitions to avoid confounds from novelty. All within-participant entries were excluded to isolate inter-participant effects.

Statistical significance of novelty and repetition identity metrics was assessed with permutation tests. For the Novelty Effect, repetition labels were jointly shuffled across participants; for the Repetition Identity Effect, only repetitions 2 and 3 were shuffled within participants, ignoring repetition 1. For each null model, we recomputed second-order RSMs and derived null distributions of effect sizes (100–110 permutations), from which p-values were estimated. Effects were considered significant at p < 0.01.

Results

We first assessed stability of representation across individuals. Second-order Representational Similarity Matrices (soRSMs) revealed robust cross-participant consistency in multivoxel representations. Averaging within-region soRSMs across all cortical parcels yielded a 12x12 matrix (4 participants x 3 repetitions) in which off-diagonal blocks (cross-participant comparisons) showed reliable positive correlations, indicating shared representational geometries across individuals (Figure 2.A). Representational stability was highest within early and mid-level visual regions, dorsal attention network (DAN), and posterior multimodal regions, and lowest in higher-order multimodal networks, consistent with a posterior-to-anterior gradient of decreasing stability.

Two systematic exposure-related effects emerged. First, a Novelty Effect: across many region pairs, inter-participant similarity for first presentations (repetition 1) was higher than for later presentations (repetitions 2–3; Figure 2.B). Although modest in magnitude, this effect was spatially widespread and strongest in visual cortex and DAN. Permutation tests confirmed that novelty scores exceeded null distributions in multiple regions (p < 0.01), whereas several higher-order networks, especially frontoparietal, showed near-zero or negative novelty values, indicating increased similarity with later repetitions rather than decline.

Second, a Repetition Identity Effect: correlations between the same repetition across participants (e.g., repetition 2 vs 2) were consistently higher than correlations between different repetitions (2 versus 3). This effect was significant in all examined regions (p < 0.01) and particularly pronounced in visual areas (e.g., V2–V6), DAN, and selected higher-order regions such as parahippocampal cortex and inferior parietal cortex, indicating that repetition-specific representational patterns are preserved across individuals.

A control “Time Effect,” defined as the difference in similarity between second and third repetitions, was generally small and near zero across most regions, with only a subset of visual regions showing modest changes. This argues against a simple monotonic decay of similarity over time and supports the specificity of novelty and repetition-identity effects to first exposure and repetition structure.

Across regions, Novelty and Repetition Identity were strongly correlated (R² = 0.69, p < 0.001), though Novelty showed stronger correlations to overall stability (R² = 0.42, p < 0.001 for novelty, R² = 0.42, p < 0.001 for repetition identity). Time-related differences showed only weak relationships with these metrics and did not survive multiple-comparisons correction in relation to repetition identity. Partial correlation analysis, controlling for stability, indicated that the association between Novelty and Repetition Identity remained significant (r = 0.29, p < 0.001), suggesting that they reflect related but distinct dimensions of representational organization.

Conclusion

Using RSA on large-scale 7T fMRI data from the Natural Scenes Dataset, we show that neural representations of complex natural images exhibit robust cross-participant stability, especially in visual and dorsal attention networks, and follow a posterior-to-anterior gradient of decreasing consistency. Beyond this global stability, we identify two systematic and statistically robust organizing principles: a Novelty Effect, whereby first exposures induce more similar representations across individuals than later repetitions, and a Repetition Identity Effect, whereby the same repetition number is encoded more consistently across participants than different repetitions.

These effects are spatially heterogeneous, strongest in sensory and attention networks, and not explained by generic time-dependent changes. These effects capture structured, experience-dependent aspects of representational geometry that go beyond static cross-subject similarity. Together, these findings demonstrate that cross-participant representational similarity is systematically shaped by exposure history, providing insight into the information carried by these representations.
