Introduction

Converging neuroimaging evidence suggests that suicidal ideation (SI) is not driven by a single brain region, but rather by abnormalities within a distributed “suicide network” involving fronto-limbic circuits, the default mode network (DMN), and cognitive control systems.1 The subgenual anterior cingulate cortex (sgACC) plays a central integrative role in this network, receiving limbic and self-referential signals and regulating affect and sustained mood states.2,3 The sgACC interacts with lateral prefrontal regions that support cognitive control, as well as with the DMN that mediates self-referential processing. Disruption of these network organizations is thought to exacerbate SI by coupling negative affect and self-referential processing with insufficient cognitive control. Stanford Neuromodulation Therapy (SNT), an accelerated high-dose repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation protocol targeting the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) based on its functional anticorrelation with sgACC, has demonstrated rapid anti-suicidal effects in treatment-resistant depression (TRD).4 However, the causal connectivity changes underlying these therapeutic effects remain poorly understood. This study aimed to investigate effective connectivity (EC) changes in the sgACC-centered networks associated with improvements in SI following SNT. We hypothesized that SNT would improve SI by normalizing aberrant directional connectivity among the sgACC-prefrontal control regions, and DMN by restoring top-down cognitive control of the DLPFC.

Methods

The 8-min resting-state functional MRI was obtained from 25 participants with TRD who received active SNT (12 from an open-label study and 13 from a randomized controlled trial). SI was evaluated using the item 10 of the Montgomery-Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS10). Data were collected at baseline, immediately post-treatment (IP), and 1 month after SNT (1M) for all participants except one who did not complete the 1-month follow-up. Changes in SI were calculated as the difference (Δ) in MADRS10 between IP and BL and between 1M and BL. Regions of interest (ROIs) were selected based on significant functional connectivity with the sgACC (medial prefrontal cortex [mPFC]; posterior cingulate cortex [PCC]; bilateral DLPFC). Spectral dynamic causal modelling (spDCM) implemented in Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM; version 25) was used to estimate EC among ROIs.5 Group-level statistical inferences were made using the parametric empirical Bayes (PEB) framework. Bayesian Model Reduction and Bayesian Model Averaging were applied to optimize model evidence and parameter estimation within PEB models. The estimated mean of EC parameters (Ep) was considered significant when their posterior probability (Pp) was greater than 95%. The total MADRS scores, excluding the MADRS10, and their changes were included as covariates to control for the effects of other depressive symptoms.

Results

At baseline, participants exhibited mild-to-moderate SI (mean ± SD = 2.2 ± 1.2). Following SNT, SI resolved in most individuals (immediate post-treatment [IP]: mean ± SD = 0.2 ± 0.4), and remission was largely sustained at the 1-month follow-up (1M: mean ± SD = 0.3 ± 0.6). Of the 20 participants who achieved SI remission at IP, 16 remained in remission at 1M. Baseline EC among the sgACC, mPFC, PCC, and bilateral DLPFC showed increased intrinsic excitability in the sgACC, mPFC, and PCC, accompanied by inhibitory influences from these regions on the DLPFC, particularly from the sgACC (Figure 1A and 1B). Improvements in SI immediately after SNT were associated with decreased excitatory EC from the mPFC to L DLPFC (Ep = 0.23; Figure 1C). At the 1-month follow-up, sustained SI improvement was associated with decreased inhibitory EC from the sgACC to both DLPFC regions (left: Ep = -0.30; right: Ep = -0.28), increased intrinsic excitation of the L DLPFC (Ep = -0.28), and decreased intrinsic excitation of the PCC (Ep = 0.36; Figure 1D).

Conclusion

Baseline EC patterns in participants with TRD were characterized by increased intrinsic excitability of the sgACC and DMN regions, consistent with previous evidence of hyperactivity in these circuits in depression. The sgACC was found to have strong inhibitory influences on both DLPFC, suggesting that a well-established anticorrelation between sgACC and DLPFC may be driven by the sgACC-directed suppression of prefrontal control regions. Following SNT, SI improved rapidly, and this improvement was sustained over one month. Immediate improvement of SI was associated with a shift toward more negative EC from mPFC to L DLPFC. Given that this connection was inhibitory at baseline (Ep = -0.42), whereas the L DLPFC-to-mPFC connection was excitatory (Ep = 0.22), this finding may indicate an acute effect of the stimulation of the L DLPFC enhances these reciprocal L DLPFC-mPFC EC pathways. Longer-term improvement in SI after SNT was associated with decreased inhibitory influence from the sgACC to bilateral DLPFC, accompanied by increased intrinsic excitability of the L DLPFC and reduced intrinsic excitation of the PCC. These findings suggest the sustained effects on remitting SI may arise from the reorganization of sgACC-centered regulatory circuits, enabling restoration of the prefrontal cognitive control mechanism.  Taken together, SNT appears to produce both rapid and sustained effects on remitting suicidal ideation by rebalancing of excitatory-inhibitory dynamics within the sgACC-centered network. Understanding these neural dynamics provides insight into the mechanism of neuromodulation and may help refine neuromodulation targets and guide the more effective, mechanism-based interventions for SI in TRD.
