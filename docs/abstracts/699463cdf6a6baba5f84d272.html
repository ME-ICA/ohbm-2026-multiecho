Introduction

Bipolar disorder (BD) is a severe psychiatric illness characterised by recurrent episodes of mania and depression that lead to profound impairments in cognition, behaviour, and daily functioning. Growing evidence implicates inflammatory-immune dysregulation and alterations in brain structure and function as core of its pathophysiology. 1
The choroid plexus (ChP) is a neuroimmune interface responsible for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) production and regulation of the blood–CSF barrier has emerged as a potential biomarker of neuroinflammatory processes in psychiatric disorders. This study aims to examine the association between ChP morphology and immune-inflammatory markers in BD using structural MRI and peripheral blood analyses.

Methods

MRI data were collected from 125 healthy control (HC) (77 females, 48 males; 19–64 years) and 119 individuals with BD (69 females, 49 males; 18–69 years), during mania (n = 43), depression (n = 45), and euthymia (n = 43).  Blood samples were collected  from a subset of subjects, including 20 HC subjects and 60 BD patients (mania, n=20; depression, n=20; euthymia, n=20).
First, structural T1-weighted MRI scans were processed to quantify choroid plexus (ChP) volume using a deep-learning segmentation model.²  The model automatically generated bilateral ChP masks. FreeSurfer was used to estimate intracranial volume (ICV), lateral ventricular volumes, and other structural metrics, as potential confounding factors. ChP volumes were then compared between HC and BD patients (with age, sex, ICV, and lateral ventricle volume as covariates).

Second, circulating levels of T-cells were then compared between HC and BD patients, with age and sex as covariates. 

Third, correlation analyses between ChP volumes and T-cell subpopulations were performed in HC and BD patients, controlling for age and sex.

Results

First, individuals with BD exhibited significantly larger total ChP volume relative to HC (Tukey post-hoc p = 0.004), even when using covariates age, sex, and intracranial volume as covariates (Tukey post-hoc p = 0.0038).

Second, compared to HC, BD patients showed significantly elevate levels of circulating  CD4+ T cells, (Tukey post-hoc p = 0.001), and their subpopulations  CD4+CD28+, and CD4+CD28+CD45- (Tukey post-hoc p<0.001), T cells, while lower levels of CD8+ T cells (adjusted p= 0.008) and their subpopulations CD8+CD28- (Turkey post-hoc p= 0.015), CD8+CD28-CD45- (adjusted p<0.001), and CD8+CD28-CD45+ (Turkey post-hoc p= 0.023) T cells, the p-value remained unchanged after adjustment for age and sex. 

Third, in HC, ChP volume positively correlated with levels of for CD4⁺CD28⁻ (r = 0.648, p = 0.002) and CD4⁺CD28⁻CD45⁻ cells (r = 0.680, p < .001) T cells, whereas negatively correlated with levels of CD4⁺CD28⁺ (r = −0.516, p = 0.020) and CD4⁺CD28⁺CD45⁺ cells (r = −0.670, p = 0.001) T cells. In addition, ChP volume positively correlated with levels of CD8⁺CD28⁻ (r = 0.464, p = 0.039), CD8⁺CD28⁺ (r = 0.464, p = 0.039), and CD8⁺CD28⁺CD45⁺ cells (r = 0.597, p = 0.005) T cells. In contrast, in BD, ChP volume showed no significant correlations with T cells (all p>0.05).

Conclusion

These findings highlight an altered interplay between choroid plexus and T cells in BD. The combination of an enlarged choroid plexus and altered T cell profile, along with their altered relationship, suggests immune dysregulation and inflammation in BD. In this contest, the choroid plexus might thus represent a key neuroimmune interface in the potential immune-mediated damage of the brain. Future research is needed to determine the specific relationships between the choroid plexus, immune signatures, and brain alterations, along with their potential use as integrated biomarkers for diagnosis, prognosis, or treatment monitoring in BD.
