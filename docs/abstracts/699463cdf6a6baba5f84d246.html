Introduction

The diagnosis of Alzheimer’s disease, mild cognitive impairment, and dementia remains a major challenge, as these neurodegenerative conditions often involve subtle brain changes that conventional MRI analysis fails to detect1. Traditional performance metrics such as loss and entropy quantify error and uncertainty but do not reveal deeper structural patterns within MRI data or model behavior. These limitations restrict early detection and accurate classification, highlighting the need for more informative computational approaches. Statistical complexity2, which integrates entropy and disequilibrium, offers a promising way to capture hidden organizational features that traditional metrics overlook. In this study, we introduce a statistical complexity–based framework to characterize subtle brain patterns and model dynamics in Alzheimer’s disease MRI classification. By applying this metric to deep learning models across varying dataset sizes, we demonstrate its ability to reveal nuanced learning behavior and improve interpretability. This approach provides a novel diagnostic signal that may enhance early detection and model reliability in clinical settings.

Methods

This study evaluates the learning dynamics of two state-of-the-art convolutional neural networks—DenseNet-121 and EfficientNet-B1—for automated classification of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) from axial T2* MRI scans. A total of 1,654 MRI slices from ADNI (965 AD, 689 MCI) were curated, preprocessed, and partitioned into stratified subsets ranging from 10% to 100% (increments of 10%) to evaluate model behavior under varying data availability.

Both architectures were trained end-to-end using standardized preprocessing pipelines. For DenseNet-1213, images were downsampled to 64×64; EfficientNet-B1 received 240×240 inputs to satisfy its compound-scaled design. All images were converted to single-channel tensors with an appended channel dimension to ensure compatibility with each model. Training incorporated Kaiming initialization and gradient accumulation to enhance stability and reduce variance when operating on small subsets.

The experimental framework introduced a computational metric called statistical complexity2,4, which is derived from synaptic weight distributions. For each model, synaptic weights were extracted layer-wise at every epoch, normalized into probability distributions, and decomposed into entropy (randomness), disequilibrium (deviation from uniformity), and statistical complexity, defined as the product of entropy and disequilibrium. This metric quantifies how models encode structural information during learning, providing insight beyond traditional metrics such as loss or accuracy.

Model training was conducted over 100 epochs for each dataset size. Learning dynamics were compared using four metrics: training loss, entropy, disequilibrium, and statistical complexity. Additionally, layer-wise complexity profiles were computed for both models at 100% data to inspect internal representational evolution. Comparative analyses were performed to reveal architectural differences in learning efficiency, robustness to limited data, and capacity to encode AD-specific structural patterns.

Results


 

Across all experimental conditions, DenseNet-121 and EfficientNet-B1 demonstrated distinct and consistently divergent learning behaviors. Statistical complexity increased monotonically with dataset size for both architectures; however, DenseNet-121 exhibited smooth, incremental trajectories, whereas EfficientNet-B1 showed instability and collapse under limited data.

At 10% dataset size, DenseNet-121 maintained moderate entropy reductions and rising disequilibrium, yielding a positive complexity trend, reflective of stable representational organization even under extreme data scarcity. In contrast, EfficientNet-B1 demonstrated a near-total entropy collapse (≈–99.99%), sharp complexity decline, and volatile convergence—evidence of aggressive overfitting and sensitivity to low-data regimes.

As dataset size increased from 30–70%, DenseNet-121 continued to exhibit smooth declines in entropy, increasing disequilibrium, and steadily rising complexity—indicating effective feature integration and robust generalization. EfficientNet-B1’s complexity remained inconsistent: despite stable entropy behavior at larger data volumes, its complexity often plateaued near zero or declined, suggesting limited capacity to exploit richer datasets for structural learning.

At 100% data, the layer-wise analysis revealed distinct architectural signatures. DenseNet-121 displayed rising complexity in deeper layers—particularly in denseblock3 and near the classifier—reflecting progressive refinement of high-level neuroanatomical features. EfficientNet-B1 exhibited fluctuating early-layer complexity and consistent mid-layer declines, stabilizing only at the final layer. This pattern indicates reduced representational depth and weaker hierarchical feature organization compared to DenseNet-121.

Three-dimensional complexity surfaces further highlighted the contrast: DenseNet-121 produced smooth, continuous complexity expansion across epochs and dataset sizes, whereas EfficientNet-B1 generated irregular, plateau-dominated surfaces with limited growth5. Validation accuracy at 100% data reinforced these trends, with DenseNet-121 showing superior stability and generalization.


Conclusion

This work demonstrates that statistical complexity is a robust, interpretable, and highly discriminative metric for characterizing the learning behavior of deep neural networks in Alzheimer’s disease MRI classification. Unlike conventional accuracy-focused assessments, statistical complexity exposes deeper organizational patterns within the model’s internal representations, offering a more sensitive view of how networks integrate and encode neurodegenerative signatures. DenseNet-121 consistently exhibited superior representational structure, smoother convergence dynamics, and stronger alignment with clinically relevant MRI features across all data regimes. Its progressive increase in complexity reflects stable and coherent feature formation, even under constrained training conditions.

Conversely, EfficientNet-B1—despite its efficiency and strong performance in natural-image tasks—displayed marked instability in the medical imaging domain. The pronounced entropy collapse at low data volumes, together with minimal gains in complexity at full dataset capacity, underscores its limited adaptability to the subtle anatomical patterns characteristic of Alzheimer’s pathology. These observations highlight that architectural compactness does not inherently confer representational depth or robustness in high-granularity clinical settings.

Two important conclusions emerge. First, model architecture plays a decisive role in shaping the hierarchical structure of learned features, extending far beyond its influence on accuracy alone. DenseNet-121’s resilience and expressiveness demonstrate its suitability for neurodegenerative disease modeling. Second, statistical complexity serves as a sensitive diagnostic lens, capable of revealing overfitting, representational collapse, and architectural limitations that remain hidden when using standard metrics.

By integrating complexity-based analysis into deep learning evaluation, this work advances the development of more reliable, interpretable, and clinically aligned AI systems for neuroimaging. Such complexity-aware frameworks hold substantial promise for enhancing early-stage Alzheimer’s detection, guiding model selection, and informing future architectures that more faithfully capture disease-related structural patterns.
