Introduction

Enlarged perivascular spaces (EPVS) are a common finding in multiple sclerosis (MS) and are more frequent than in healthy controls. Several studies have linked EPVS to clinical disability1. Perivascular spaces (PVS) surround vessels and contain cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). Their size scales with the diameter of the underlying vessel, forming a hierarchical network. Structural MRI visualizes only the largest Virchow–Robin spaces, typically on the order of millimeters, representing a small fraction of the total perivascular network.

Diffusion MRI (dMRI), in contrast, probes water motion at micrometer scales, providing sensitivity to tissue microstructure far below voxel resolution. PVS larger than ~25 µm have unrestricted diffusion and are therefore captured as a free-water (FW) compartment. dMRI-derived FW samples a substantial portion of the perivascular network that is invisible to conventional structural imaging.

In dMRI, FW represents the signal component remaining after modeling and removing intra- and extra-axonal tissue contributions. Most diffusion models estimate FW using several b-values at a fixed echo time, relying on strong priors that can reduce the specificity and robustness of FW separation. The Standard Model (SM) of diffusion in white matter (WM) provides an umbrella framework that unifies more than a decade of biophysical modeling efforts by lifting constraints on diffusivities and fiber orientation2. By incorporating multiple echo times, SM enables direct sensitivity to the long-T2 signature of FW, improving its isolation from surrounding tissue.

In this study, we investigate whether FW derived from Standard Model Imaging (SMI)3 is associated with clinical disability in MS. We compare it with FW derived from commonly used Neurite Orientation Dispersion and Density Imaging (NODDI) and 2 shells Diffusion Tensor Imaging (DTI) as well as DTI along the perivascular space (DTI-ALPS) index, and assess their relationships with disability measures.

Methods

This IRB-approved prospective study included 92 patients with MS, of whom 68 were female. The median age was 48 years (range 24-73), with a median Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) score of 3 (range 0-8.5). Clinical disability was assessed using the Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT) for cognitive function, the 25-Foot Walk Test (25FWT) for motor performance, and the Multiple Sclerosis Functional Composite (MSFC) as a composite disability measure.

All participants underwent brain MRI on a 3T scanner (Siemens Prisma, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) using a 20-channel phased-array RF coil for signal reception. The MRI protocol included both structural and diffusion-weighted sequences.

Anatomical MRI was performed using a 3D magnetization-prepared rapid gradient-echo (MPRAGE) to visualize EPVS (repetition time (TR) = 2300 ms, inversion time (TI) = 900 ms, echo time (TE) = 2.98 ms, flip angle (FA) = 9°, matrix = 256 × 256, voxel size = 1 mm isotropic, total acquisition time = 5 min 17 s). In addition, a 3D T2-weighted fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequence was acquired to assess WM lesions (TR = 5000 ms, TI = 1800 ms, TE = 393 ms, FA = 120°, matrix = 256 × 256, voxel size = 1 mm isotropic, total acquisition time = 5 min 23 s). Lesion segmentation was performed using the free tool Samseg, followed by manual correction.

dMRI was acquired using a spin-echo echo-planar imaging (SE EPI) sequence with the following parameters: TR = 4300 ms, TE = 92 ms, FA = 90°, 68 slices, matrix size = 110 × 110, voxel size = 2 mm isotropic.  Diffusion encoding was performed along directions uniformly distributed on a sphere at b = 1000 s/mm² (25 directions) and b = 2000 s/mm² (60 directions), with b = 0 images acquired at multiple TE (TE = 60, 78, 92, and 130 ms) (Figure 1). Additional acquisition parameters included a multiband factor of 2, parallel imaging acceleration (GRAPPA) of 2, and partial Fourier acquisition of 6/8, resulting in a total diffusion acquisition time of approximately 7 minutes. 

dMRI data were preprocessed using the DESIGNER v2 pipeline (Python implementation), including denoising, Gibbs artifact correction, EPI-induced distortion correction, motion and eddy-current correction, and Rician noise floor correction. SMI FW was estimated using the Tissue Microstructure Imaging (TMI) toolbox.

For comparison, FW maps were also obtained using NODDI and two-shell DTI estimators and the subset of data with a fixed TE (Figure 1). In addition, the DTI-ALPS index was computed using in-house code for region-of-interest (ROI) placement based on color-coded fractional anisotropy (FA) maps, followed by values extraction from the diffusion tensor components and the index calculation.

ROI analyses were performed using a global WM derived by combining regions from the Johns Hopkins University WM atlas, with ROIs registered to individual subject space using Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs). Normal-appearing white matter (NAWM) masks were subsequently generated by excluding lesions.

Associations between dMRI measures and clinical disability scores (SDMT, 25FWT, and MSFC) were assessed using Spearman rank correlation analyses. Sex and age at disease onset were included as covariates. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses. All analyses were performed using MATLAB v9.13.0 (R2022b) (The MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA).

Results

Macroscopic EPVS identified on T1 MPRAGE spatially aligned with regions of increased FW. Despite this qualitative agreement across methods, the magnitude and spatial extent of EPVS on FW maps differed substantially between models. In particular, approaches relying on two diffusion shells at a fixed TE showed larger zones of increased FW extending into surrounding WM compared with SMI (Figure 2A).

These differences are consistent with known limitations of FW estimation based solely on diffusion weighting. FW cannot be robustly isolated from tissue compartments when using only two b-values at a fixed TE. Previous studies have demonstrated that such models require strong priors, which can compromise the specificity of FW separation4. In contrast, SMI leverages additional information from b = 0 images acquired at multiple TE, enabling direct sensitivity to the long-T2 signature of FW and improving compartment separation4. As a result, despite overall spatial agreement, SMI-derived FW showed the most specific correspondence with macroscopic EPVS on structural MRI, without apparent overestimation in adjacent tissue. Consistent with this improved specificity, SMI-derived FW also demonstrated stronger associations with clinical disability (Figure 2B-E).

Within NAWM, higher FW values were associated with worse clinical disability. SMI FW showed significant correlations with all disability measures, including lower SDMT scores (ρ = −0.47, p < 0.05), MSFC scores (ρ = −0.42, p < 0.05), and longer 25FWT times (ρ = 0.42, p < 0.05), after adjustment for sex and age at disease onset. FW estimates derived from DTI using two b-values also showed significant associations with disability, though with lower effect sizes (SDMT: ρ = −0.43; MSFC: ρ = −0.41; 25FWT: ρ = 0.34; all p < 0.05). NODDI-derived FW demonstrated weaker but still significant correlations (SDMT: ρ = −0.33; MSFC: ρ = 0.24; 25FWT: ρ = 0.31; all p < 0.05). In contrast, the DTI-ALPS index did not show significant associations with cognitive, motor, or composite disability measures.

Overall, among the evaluated dMRI approaches, SMI FW demonstrated the strongest and most consistent associations with clinical disability across cognitive, motor, and composite tests.

Conclusion

FW derived from dMRI is a sensitive marker of perivascular involvement and clinical disability in MS1. Macroscopic EPVS visible on structural MRI spatially correspond to areas of elevated FW, indicating that FW reflects microstructural fluid compartments associated with perivascular water (Figure 2A). It captures PVS larger than approximately 25 µm, providing sensitivity to a substantial portion of the perivascular network that is not accessible with conventional structural imaging. 

Several dMRI approaches enable estimation of FW. However, most of those methods rely on data acquired with two b-values and a fixed echo time, and therefore require strong priors to separate FW from tissue compartments. Previous works have shown that these constraints limit the robustness and specificity of FW estimation4. More robust separation of FW requires explicit biophysical modeling of tissue compartments, followed by isolation of the FW component using additional signal dimensions, including variation in TE. 

SMI provides a unified biophysical framework that incorporates these principles, accounting for assumptions introduced in earlier models while leveraging varying echo times to achieve direct sensitivity to the long-T2, high-diffusivity (~3 µm²/ms) signature of FW2,5. This enables more specific separation of FW from tissue compartments. Importantly, this increased specificity is achieved with minimal additional scan time, supporting the feasibility of incorporating multi-TE dMRI into clinical protocols.

Among the evaluated approaches, SMI FW showed the strongest and most consistent associations with cognitive, motor, and composite disability measures in MS. The DTI-ALPS index, which is not a measure of global FW but has been proposed as a marker sensitive to diffusion abnormalities along perivascular trajectories, did not show significant associations with disability in this cohort (Figure 2B-E).

Together, these findings suggest that dMRI-derived FW estimated using multi-TE SMI is a promising imaging biomarker of perivascular alterations and clinical disability in MS. Disruption of perivascular fluid dynamics, potentially related to glymphatic system dysfunction, that may help explain the observed associations between EPVS enlargement, increased FW, and should be explored in future work.
