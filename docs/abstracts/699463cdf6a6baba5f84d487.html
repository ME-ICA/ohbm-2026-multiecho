Introduction

The human claustrum is a small bilateral structure located between the putamen and insula. It is one of the most interconnected brain areas with connections to most cortical and subcortical structures. The claustrum's interconnectivity has led to numerous hypotheses about its function including sleep, pain, attention, multisensory integration and consciousness. However, much of the empirical work on the claustrum has been carried out in non-primate species, making the translation of claustrum function to humans uncertain 1. The human claustrum's thin shape and location within the brain make it a difficult region to image using MRI.

With the developments in MR imaging methods and improved spatial resolution, the investigation of the human claustrum has become possible using 7 Tesla fMRI. In our previous 7 Tesla fMRI study, we showed that claustrum activity could be measured in healthy participants and found evidence for a visual sensory zone 2. However, 7 Tesla systems are scarce, and therefore the possibility of using more conventional MRI needs to be explored for claustrum research to be more widely accessible.

In the current study, we investigated if it is possible to measure the visual claustrum responses using 3 Tesla fMRI and how many fMRI sessions are needed to achieve reliable visual claustrum activity across participants. 

Methods

Four participants (mean age = 30.25 years, SD = 3.77 years; 2 females) completed five experimental sessions each. Each session included 6 runs involving a center fixation task. Stimuli consisted of 12 naturalistic, 15-second, visual-only videos presented in a pseudorandomized order, interleaved with 15-second baselines. Stimuli were selected from a set of 48 videos used in our previous 7 T fMRI study by choosing those that evoked the strongest visual responses. Acquisition was performed using GE-EPI at 3T (39 sagittal slices at 1.5mm × 1.5mm resolution; slice thickness = 1mm; TR = 3510ms; TE = 33ms; FA = 90°, GRAPPA = 2). For subject 3 and the first 4 sessions of subject 4 the parameters deviated slightly from the above (TR = 2901, slice thickness = 1.02). Preprocessing was performed using fMRIprep. We computed first-level GLMs for every session, modeling Movie and Baseline conditions convolved with a canonical HRF, alongside nuisance regressors for head motion and drift. Data were smoothed with a 1.5mm FWHM kernel. Fixed-effects analyses were performed cumulatively across sessions (e.g., Session 1; Sessions 1-2; etc.).

The visual claustrum ROI was defined using the HCP 7T retinotopy dataset 3 and a "left vs. right" visual field contrast 4. A whole claustrum mask 5 was non-linearly registered to the HCP dataset, with the final ROI consisting of voxels within the mask active at z>2.5 in the contrast map. To confirm spatial specificity of the observed responses, we generated control ROIs by shifting the claustrum mask along the x, y and z axes so that there was no overlap with the original ROI. Additionally, a dilated mask was created by dilating the original ROI by 2 voxels and subtracting the original mask. Finally, we examined responses of the lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) for comparison (Fig. 1). Contrast estimates were extracted and averaged across each ROI and a one-sample t-test (FDR-corrected) against 0 was conducted for each region for each set of sessions. 

Results

To determine the amount of data required to detect robust visual claustrum activity, we assessed the cumulative number of sessions needed for contrast estimates to significantly exceed baseline (one-sample t-test vs. 0). The claustrum showed significant activation (pFDR < 0.001) after three sessions for three participants, and after four sessions for the remaining participant (Fig. 2A). As a positive control, the LGN yielded significant activity (pFDR < 0.001; Fig. 2B) after one session for three of the participants and after two sessions for one participant. 

Crucially, spatially shifted claustrum masks yielded no significant activation after four sessions (all pFDR > 0.05), confirming spatial specificity. While the dilated surrounding mask showed a significant response after four sessions (Fig. 2C), the proportion of significant voxels (p < 0.05 uncorrected) within this mask was substantially lower (6–9%) compared to the core claustrum ROI (8–15%). This suggests that the signal observed in the dilated mask is likely attributable to partial volume effects or smoothing-related signal spillover from the claustrum, rather than intrinsic activity in the surrounding structures.

Conclusion

These results demonstrate that reliable, spatially specific BOLD responses can be detected in the human visual claustrum at 3 Tesla given sufficient signal averaging (approximately 3-4 sessions with our acquisition parameters). By validating the signal against shifted and dilated control masks, we confirm that these responses are not driven by signal leakage from another area located close to the claustrum. This finding demonstrates the feasibility of claustrum research at 3 T, enabling a broader inquiry into the functional role of this nucleus.
