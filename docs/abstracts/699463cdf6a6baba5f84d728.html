Introduction

Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and traumatic brain injury (TBI) are highly prevalent and frequently co-occurring conditions in military populations. Both disorders have been independently linked to alterations in limbic structures implicated in emotional regulation, threat detection, and stress responsiveness, particularly the amygdala and hippocampus 1. Prior studies of PTSD report heterogeneous findings, ranging from smaller amygdala volumes 1 to inconsistent or null effects 2. More detailed subnuclei analyses have revealed reductions in lateral and centromedial regions 3 and associations between smaller basolateral and central nuclei and greater symptom severity 4. In contrast, individuals with comorbid TBI and PTSD have shown larger amygdala volumes relative to those with TBI alone 5. Collectively, these findings highlight substantial heterogeneity driven by sample characteristics, imaging methodologies, and analytic focus, including whether the amygdala is evaluated globally or at the subnuclear level. Moreover, the neural consequences of comorbid PTSD and TBI remain poorly understood, and little is known about whether their combined effects on amygdala structure are additive, interactive, or reflect distinct neurobiological processes. 

This study aimed to characterize group-level differences in the volumes of bilateral amygdala subnuclei across four groups—normal controls, PTSD-only, TBI-only, and comorbid TBI+PTSD—in Vietnam War Veterans several decades after the traumatic event. By examining distributions of subnuclear volumes rather than relying solely on whole-amygdala measures, the study seeks to determine whether TBI and/or PTSD exhibit distinct or overlapping structural patterns within specific amygdala subregions.

Methods

Structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data were obtained from 256 male Vietnam War veterans enrolled in the Department of Defense–Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (DOD-ADNI). Data usage approval was obtained from the ADNI team, and the study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee at United Arab Emirates University. Participants ranged in age from 60 to 85 years and were classified into four groups: normal controls, PTSD, TBI, and comorbid TBI+PTSD. TBI history was determined based on documentation of moderate-to-severe, non-penetrating traumatic brain injury sustained during military service. PTSD diagnoses were established using the Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale (CAPS) for DSM-5, with scores of 40 or higher indicating clinically significant PTSD.

All participants underwent structural MRI using high-resolution T1-weighted scans acquired on 3T scanners across multiple sites. Imaging data were processed using FreeSurfer (version 7.4.1), and amygdala subnuclei volumes were derived using the HippocampalSubfieldsAndNucleiOfAmygdala segmentation pipeline (https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/fswiki/HippocampalSubfieldsAndNucleiOfAmygdala) to ensure standardized, anatomically precise delineation of subregions. The results of the segmentation were visually inspected across all participants to verify the accuracy and overall quality of the amygdala subnuclei delineations. Cases showing boundary irregularities, mislabeling, or artifacts were reviewed and reprocessed as needed to ensure reliable volumetric estimates. Each subnuclei volume was converted to a percentage in relation to the TotalBrainSeg volume, to account for individual brain size. 

Data analyses were conducted in R (version 4.5.2). Group differences in amygdala subnuclei volumes were assessed using nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis tests, followed by Bonferroni-corrected pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for post hoc comparisons. For each region showing a significant group effect, violin plots were generated to depict distributional differences, with individual data points overlaid to illustrate inter-subject variability and summary markers indicating group-level central tendencies. Left and right hemisphere subnuclei were analyzed and plotted separately to evaluate potential lateralization effects. All volumetric measures were statistically adjusted for age and education, and all results were corrected for multiple comparisons using Bonferroni correction (p < 0.05).

Results

A total of 71 normal controls, 38 TBI, 79 PTSD, and 68 TBI+PTSD were included in the analysis. The ethnic composition of the sample was predominantly Caucasian/White (76.7%), followed by African American/Black (11.3%), Hispanic/Latino/Mexican-American (3.9%), Biracial/Multiracial (4.3%), Asian/Asian American/Pacific Islander (2.3%), and Other (1.6%).

Group comparisons revealed selective differences in amygdala subnuclei volume fractions across the four groups (Normal Control, PTSD, TBI, and TBI+PTSD), and distributional patterns are shown in Figure 1. In the right amygdala, significant group effects emerged for the Accessory-Basal nucleus, the Cortical nucleus, and the Corticoamygdaloid-transition region  (p <0.01). Individuals with PTSD demonstrated slightly higher volume fractions in the right Accessory-Basal nucleus (0.024 ± 0.003) compared with Normal Controls (0.023 ± 0.003; p = 0.0054). The right Cortical nucleus volume also differed between groups, with a significant contrast between PTSD and Normal Controls (p = 0.0066), although absolute differences were small given the low overall magnitude of this subregion. Additionally, the PTSD group showed a higher volume fraction in the right Corticoamygdaloid-transition region (0.017 ± 0.002) relative to normal controls (0.015 ± 0.002; p = 0.00033).

In the left amygdala, significant pairwise differences were detected in the Cortical, Accessory-Basal, and Medial nuclei (p < 0.01), with all significant effects occurring exclusively between the PTSD and TBI+PTSD groups. Relative to individuals with PTSD, the TBI+PTSD group exhibited reduced volume fractions in the left Cortical nucleus (p = 0.006), left Accessory-Basal nucleus (p = 0.005), and left Medial nucleus (p = 0.006). No significant differences in any of the left amygdala subnuclei were observed among the TBI, PTSD, and TBI+PTSD groups when compared with Normal Controls.

Conclusion

This study investigated volumetric differences in discrete amygdala subnuclei among individuals with PTSD, TBI, comorbid TBI+PTSD, and healthy controls. Distinct patterns of group differences emerged within both right and left amygdala subregions, suggesting that subtle structural alterations may characterize stress- and injury-related neuropathology. The most consistent effects appeared in the PTSD group, in contrast to the normal control group, which exhibited elevated volume fractions in several right amygdala nuclei relative to healthy controls. These findings align with prior evidence of heightened amygdala alterations and potential stress-related plasticity in PTSD, which may correspond to structural differences in nuclei implicated in threat processing and associative learning.

In the left amygdala, significant reductions were observed exclusively in the TBI+PTSD group compared with the PTSD group. This pattern may indicate that traumatic brain injury modifies or attenuates amygdala alterations typically observed in PTSD alone, potentially reflecting injury-related neuronal loss, disrupted connectivity, or differential neurobiological pathways underlying comorbidity. Importantly, no left- or right-hemisphere subnuclei differences were detected between clinical groups and healthy controls beyond those observed in PTSD, suggesting that amygdala alterations in TBI are subtle and may emerge more prominently only when combined with PTSD.

The hemispheric asymmetry observed—greater right-hemisphere elevations in PTSD and left-hemisphere reductions in TBI+PTSD—may reflect divergent functional specializations, with the right amygdala more closely associated with rapid threat appraisal and the left with contextual processing. Although speculative, such patterns warrant further investigation using multimodal imaging approaches to determine whether nuclei-specific structural differences correspond to functional dysregulation across diagnostic groups.

Several limitations should be noted. The cross-sectional design limits causal interpretations, and future longitudinal studies are needed to clarify temporal trajectories of amygdala changes following psychological trauma and brain injury. Additionally, the comorbidity group may exhibit substantial heterogeneity in clinical presentation, complicating interpretation.
