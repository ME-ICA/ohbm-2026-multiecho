Introduction

BOLD fMRI has been widely employed in neuroimaging and is assumed to reflect neural activity via neurovascular coupling. However, conflicting fMRI reports are not rare but are difficult to validate without simultaneous neuronal imaging1. In this report, we conducted simultaneous fMRI and wide-field optical imaging (WOI) in mice and compared resting-state functional connectivity (FC) from BOLD and vascular volume signals (total hemoglobin absorption, HbT) to FC from concurrent neuronal membrane voltage signals or intracellular calcium signals in varied oxygen breathing conditions to address the question. 

Methods

Simultaneous fMRI/WOI2 was conducted in 10 mice, genetically encoded voltage indicator (GEVI, N=3) and genetically encoded calcium indicator (GECI, N=7), using single-shot gradient echo EPI: TR=1000ms/TE=12ms for 16 continuous axial slices of whole-brain coverage. The 20 min resting-state scans were conducted under 1% isoflurane delivered through a nose cone in either 100% O2 or mixed 20% O2 and 80% air. SpO2 was monitored.   WOI was obtained as 50Hz neuronal fluorescence signals (green emission excited by blue light illumination) and 50Hz green light illuminated reflection (HbT) acquired alternatively from a wide field of cortical areas. Both types of imaging data were preprocessed using standard methods and FC analysis of various networks was examined using seed-based correlation.

Results

We observed a reproducible negative BOLD correlation between bilateral areas of the barrel field network during pure oxygen breathing (hyperoxia). Despite the negative BOLD correlation, positive neuronal correlation (GEVI) and a positive vascular correlation (HbT) were observed in WOI. Typical positive BOLD correlation was exhibited when breathing in mixed 20% O2 and 80% air, Figure 1. In this case, the BOLD correlation matched the GEVI and HbT correlation, unlike the hyper oxygenated condition. The findings of confused BOLD FC across hemispheres involved in multiple networks in the oxygen breathing conditions (not shown).

Conclusion

The report demonstrated the left and right hemispheres may respond differently to blood oxygen challenges therefore complicating BOLD FC interpretation. Our findings agree with the classic view of neurovascular coupling on vascular volume changes but suggest that the neurovascular coupling may not always induce positive BOLD signals in resting state, demonstrated in oxygen breathing. The inverse BOLD signals might be a result of delayed de-oxygen cleanup locally in the case of hyperoxia. The observed negative BOLD correlation across hemispheres in networks might be a result of out of pace on de-oxygen cleanup between hemispheres, involving less or more networks in variations of oxygen breathing. Why the similar vascular distribution within a network across hemispheres could have a different de-oxygen cleanup response to neurovascular coupling is still in unknown mechanism3. How the BOLD signals in different brain regions change following varied breathing conditions would be an open question but highly relevant to accurately interpretating BOLD signals and fMRI networks. Cautions should be on BOLD-fMRI studies with oxygen changes globally as well as locally in physiological or pathological conditions.
