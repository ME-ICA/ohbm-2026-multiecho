Introduction

Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) diagnoses have drastically increased since 2019. In fact, the World Health Organization (WHO) predicted that MDD would be the leading cause of disability by the year 2030 because of its dramatic increase in prevalence, healthcare costs, and unemployment and lost work costs1. While the amount of research into the pathogenesis of MDD has also risen, the heterogeneity of the disorder has made it difficult to delineate its correlates, highlighting the need for the identification of biomarkers. Foundational research on the neural correlates of MDD have focused on the limbic system, and the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) has been identified as somewhat of a hub for the generation of depressive symptoms2. Evidence of structural abnormalities and connectivity deficits of the ACC in people with MDD has been established, but more recent work has suggested imbalances in neurometabolic concentrations3. Proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) has been used to examine in vivo neurometabolic concentrations, though there has been debate over which neurometabolites specifically are involved in MDD. Further, neuroimaging studies are often discredited as being underpowered to detect meaningful differences in psychiatric patient populations because of their small sample sizes, emphasizing the need for alternative statistical approaches to assessing these topics4. The purpose of this study is to assess the concentrations of key neurometabolites in the anterior cingulate cortex in people with MDD compared to healthy controls by conducting a systematic review and meta-analysis.

Methods

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across four electronic databases (APA PsycINFO, PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science) using the search terms, (“magnetic resonance spectroscopy” OR “spectroscopy” OR “MR spectroscopy”) AND (“anterior cingulate cortex” OR “ACC” OR “anterior cingulate”) AND (“major depressive disorder” OR “MDD” OR “depression” OR “depressive symptoms”). The screening process proceeded in two stages: first based on title and abstract, and then by full text with eligibility dependent on a priori-defined inclusion criteria. Full-length and short articles were included, along with cross-sectional studies, randomized control studies, longitudinal studies, and pilot studies, while conference abstracts, case studies, and review papers were excluded. General inclusion criteria included the reporting of average concentrations of neurometabolites of interest (N-acetylaspartate, GABA, glutamate, glutamine, Glx, myo-inositol, creatine, and choline) in the ACC for both MDD and healthy control samples, and additional inclusion criteria included: 1) only adult human subjects, 2) MDD diagnosis based on either DSM or ICD criteria, 3) use of 1H-MRS, 4) MRS voxel in any ACC subregion, 5) resting-state or baseline data reported, and 6) at least one metabolite of interest reported. Along with metabolic data and general article information, data collected for systematic review included percentage of male and female subjects, average and standard deviations for age, diagnostic technique and measures used, MRI scanner information and MRS sequencing information, and so on. To calculate effect sizes, differences in average metabolic concentrations were quantified using standardized mean differences (SMD; Hedges’ g), and effect sizes were pooled for each metabolite using random effects models with the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) technique. Additionally, each study underwent quality assessment (Newcastle-Ottawa Scale), and each group of studies per metabolite underwent heterogeneity tests, outlier detection, sensitivity analysis, and publication bias assessment. Moderators considered included the strength of the MR magnet, medication status, ACC subregion, whether or not the data was creatine scaled, percentage of female subjects in the sample, and average age of the sample.

Results

The initial database search yielded 1,666 studies, and after removal of duplicates, 844 studies were screened by title and abstract. A total of 749 studies were removed based on exclusion criteria, leaving 95 studies to undergo full-text assessment. After full-text assessment, 43 studies were ultimately eligible for meta-analysis. The total number of subjects included in analysis for the eight metabolites ranged from n = 296 to n = 992 with an average of n = 496 for the MDD samples and from n = 260 to n = 727 with an average of n = 404 for the healthy control samples. Metabolic concentrations that were significantly different in MDD subjects compared to healthy controls included N-acetylaspartate (NAA), gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), and glutamine (Gln). NAA was significantly decreased in the ACC in subjects with MDD, with a small negative effect size (g = -0.16, p = 0.017). There was significant moderate heterogeneity between studies (I2 = 42.44%, p < 0.001), which was significantly moderated by magnetic field strength (p = 0.018). Specifically, subgroup analyses suggested that studies conducted using MR magnets with field strengths greater than 1.5T showed significantly decreased NAA, but this effect was not seen in studies that only used 1.5T scanners. GABA was significantly decreased in subjects with MDD, with a small negative effect size (g = -0.26, p = 0.004). There was no evidence of heterogeneity between studies (I2 = 0.00%). Finally, Gln was significantly increased in the ACC in people with MDD, with a small positive effect size (g = 0.21, p < 0.001). There was no evidence of heterogeneity between studies (I2 = 0.00%). No other metabolic differences reached significance, though there were more interesting findings regarding heterogeneity. As mentioned, NAA was significantly moderated by magnet strength. Glutamate (Glu) had significant moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 39.70%, p = 0.017) that was significantly moderated by ACC subregion (p = 0.023), such that Glu was significantly decreased in MDD subjects in the dorsal ACC, but not the pregenual ACC. Finally, myo-inositol (mI) and choline (Cho) had significant moderate and substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 43.41%, p = 0.017, and I2 = 64.51%, p < 0.001, respectively). Heterogeneity in mI was significantly moderated by medication status (p = 0.027), such that mI was significantly increased only in MDD subjects who were unmedicated. While medication status did not emerge as a significant moderator of choline (Cho), subgroup analyses did reveal that Cho was significantly increased only in MDD subjects who were unmedicated (p = 0.047).

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this was the first study to compare multiple key neurometabolites specifically in the ACC of people with MDD compared to healthy controls. Based on standard mean differences, our results suggest that subjects with MDD had significantly decreased levels of NAA and GABA, and significantly increased levels of Gln. Further, glutamate (Glu), glutamate + glutamine (Glx), and Cho seemed to be trending in clear directions, but failed to reach significance. There are a few potential explanations to consider regarding the significant differences found here. First, NAA is known to be a marker of neuronal health and density. Deficits in NAA in the ACC could suggest that this region has less neuronal integrity and/or density compared to that of healthy controls. Next, the observed deficits in GABA are consistent with the well-established GABAergic deficit hypothesis of MDD, which suggests that MDD is associated with decreased availability of GABAA receptors and thus reduced concentrations of GABA. Lastly, these results suggest significantly increased Gln, and decreased concentrations of Glu, though this is only significant in the dorsal ACC specifically. This could reflect a minor imbalance of the glutamate/glutamine cycle, which is the process of converting Glu to Gln once Glu enters an astrocyte, which would be in line with a proposed theory of astrocytic dysfunction as a piece of the pathology of MDD. There were several limitations of this analysis overall. First, data search and extraction was only completed by one reviewer, increasing the risk of personal biases influencing the systematic review, though a priori inclusion/exclusion criteria, preregistration of the study, and multiple statistical approaches for controlling for potential biases were implemented. Next, a majority of the studies included used scanners with a field strength of 3T or lower, which are not as efficient at collecting certain metabolic signals compared to 7T. A third limitation is that this study only considered four categorical and two continuous moderators. This is a compelling avenue for future research to uncover more explanations for between group heterogeneity. Taken in light of the aforementioned limitations, results of this study support the notion that MDD is associated with decreased NAA and GABA and increased Gln in the ACC.
