Introduction

Efficient language comprehension relies on the cognitive ability to generate probabilistic predictions about upcoming input.1 Current predictive coding frameworks propose a hierarchical neural architecture based on the dynamic interplay between domain-specific linguistic processes and domain-general executive resources.2,3 The Angular Gyrus (AG) is widely recognized as a key hub for contextual integration within the language network4, whereas the Dorsolateral Prefrontal Cortex (DLPFC) exerts cognitive control to maintain context under uncertainty5. Within this framework, the DLPFC is assumed to generate top-down predictions about upcoming linguistic content by maintaining discourse context, which are then integrated with incoming input at the level of the AG's semantic representations. To date, this dissociation of prediction generation (DLPFC) versus semantic buffering and integration (AG) remains untested. Hence, critical questions persist: Does the DLPFC actively drive linguistic prediction, or does it merely support working memory load? How relevant is the AG for semantic integration beyond semantic storage? Here, we used transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) to test the causal relevance of these nodes during naturalistic reading, hypothesizing that DLPFC disruption would impair prediction generation, whereas AG modulation would interfere with semantic integration.

Methods

This preregistered (https://doi.org/10.23668/psycharchives.16408) study used a within-subject, cross-over repeated measures design. Twenty-five healthy younger adults (M = 31.3, SD = 6.6, range = 21–41 years; 13 female) participated. Sample size was determined via a priori power analysis (90% power to detect Cohen's d = 0.564). All participants were right-handed, native German speakers with no contraindications to MRI or TMS. Neuropsychological profiles were assessed using the Trail Making Test, Digit Symbol Substitution Test, and Reading Span Test. Participants were blinded to stimulation conditions. The study was approved by Leipzig University's ethics committee and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. 

Participants completed three sessions (≥5 days apart): offline repetitive TMS (rTMS) over the AG, offline+online rTMS (AG + DLPFC during tasks), and sham stimulation (Fig. 1). In each session, participants performed a self-paced reading task and a dual task combining reading with a 2-back color-matching task. Both tasks used 150-word newspaper articles, which participants read word-by-word through pressing the space button with their left hand, with comprehension questions following each text. In the dual task, participants performed the 2-back task concurrently by pressing a button whenever the current word's color matched that two positions earlier.

Neuronavigated TMS was delivered using individual T1-weighted MRI scans. Low-frequency rTMS (1 Hz, 100% resting motor threshold (RMT), 25 min) was applied offline over the left AG prior to the experimental tasks. During the offline+online session, participants first received low-frequency rTMS (1 Hz, 25 min) before the tasks, followed by high-frequency rTMS (10 Hz, 100% RMT) over the left DLPFC during the reading and dual tasks. The online stimulation was time-locked to the word onset via 5-pulse bursts (200 ms post-onset, ≥5 s inter-burst interval). The sham session mimicked the offline+online session using a sham TMS coil over two midline sites. Task order and stimulation condition order were counterbalanced across participants.

Data were analyzed in R using linear mixed-effects models (LMMs). Separate models were fitted for log-transformed reading times, n-back sensitivity (d-prime), and comprehension accuracy. To account for individual and stimulus-specific variability, models included random intercepts for participants and texts, as well as by-participant random slopes for the stimulation condition. Fixed effects included the stimulation condition, information-theoretic metrics (surprisal, entropy), and lexical properties (word frequency, length). We additionally controlled for task-related and demographic confounds, including session, trial number, elapsed time since beginning of the task, previous word duration, TMS side effects, age, and education. Crucially, to examine whether TMS disrupted predictive processing mechanisms, the models assessed the interaction between stimulation condition and word surprisal, the interaction between stimulation condition and elapsed time, and finally the three-way interaction between stimulation condition, word surprisal, and elapsed time to account for potential task-specific build-up effects of TMS. Continuous predictors were mean-centered and categorical predictors were contrast coded using the simple coding scheme with sham stimulation as reference level.

Results

Analysis of reading times confirmed the validity of the naturalistic paradigm, with high surprisal significantly slowing reading speed across tasks (p < .001). Stimulation conditions produced distinct effects on reading dynamics. In the single reading task, the combined Offline AG + Online DLPFC condition significantly slowed reading times relative to both Offline AG alone and sham (p < .01), with this effect persisting throughout the task and resulting in overall slower reading performance. In contrast, both active stimulation conditions (Offline AG and Offline AG + Online DLPFC) in the dual task induced slower processing relative to sham (p < .01). Participants showed less acceleration over time compared to sham, indicating that concurrent cognitive load and stimulation interact to limit facilitation effects.

Regarding predictive processing, we observed a task-dependent dissociation. In the single reading task, disrupting the angular gyrus showed a trend towards increasing the processing cost of high-surprisal words (p = .08), a pattern absent when concurrent DLPFC stimulation was applied (p = .72). Under high cognitive load (dual task), this surprisal modulation disappeared; however, a distinct role for the DLPFC emerged. The combined Offline AG + Online DLPFC condition significantly modulated reading times during dual-task targets compared to sham (p = .018), whereas AG stimulation alone did not. This indicates that while AG disruption may subtly impair semantic integration during focused reading, concurrent DLPFC stimulation actively recruits executive resources to manage dual-task interference.

Stimulation effects on task performance were selective. Combined offline AG + online DLPFC stimulation significantly improved dual-task n-back performance compared to sham (p = .028), whereas offline AG showed no significant effect (p = .367). Comprehension accuracy was unaffected by stimulation (p > .18), though the dual-task condition reduced accuracy compared to reading-only performance.

Conclusion

This study provides causal evidence that prefrontal and parietal cortices shape the dynamics of predictive language processing. Offline AG stimulation tended to amplify the processing cost of surprising words during focused reading, consistent with a role in contextual semantic integration, whereas concurrent DLPFC stimulation altered overall reading speed and selectively modulated performance under dual-task demands. Together, these patterns suggest that the DLPFC contributes flexibly to managing interference and maintaining task performance when prediction and control demands are high, while the AG supports efficient integration of unexpected input. The preserved comprehension accuracy despite substantial changes in reading dynamics indicates that these regions primarily tune the efficiency and robustness of predictive processing, refining hierarchical models that posit a simple top–down (DLPFC) versus integration (AG) dissociation.
