Introduction

The human brain is anatomically diverse, yet white matter variability is frequently treated as methodological noise in group-level neuroimaging analyses. Recent evidence suggests that structural variability is a biologically meaningful feature that underpins individual differences in cognition and behavior [1]. However, a comprehensive characterization of normative white matter variability - integrating spatial, microstructural, and macrostructural features across the entire human lifespan - remains limited.

In this study, we aggregated over 2,800 high-quality diffusion MRI scans spanning from birth to late adulthood (0–100 years). We aimed to: (1) quantify the spatial gradient of white matter variability; (2) determine how inter-individual variability evolves across the lifespan; and (3) assess the extent to which structural variability explains individual differences in cognitive, motor, and emotional traits. We propose that neurovariability is not random but follows specific developmental and anatomical principles.

Methods

Dataset:

We pooled diffusion MRI data from four branches of the Human Connectome Project (HCP): the Baby Connectome Project, HCP-Development, HCP-Young Adult, and HCP-Aging. After quality assurance, the dataset comprised 2,854 imaging sessions (Age range: 1 week to 100 years).

 

Tractography and Features:

We utilized TractSeg [2] to delineate 64 long-range association, projection, and commissural pathways. For each pathway, we extracted three classes of features:

  1. Spatial: We generated cohort-specific fiber orientation distribution (FOD) templates and calculated voxel-wise displacement fields required to align individuals to the template, quantifying spatial consistency.
  1. Microstructural: We derived metrics from Diffusion Tensor Imaging [3] (FA, MD, RD, AD) and NODDI (ICVF, ISOVF, Orientation Dispersion) [4].
  1. Macrostructural: We calculated tract volumes, both unnormalized and normalized to Total Brain Volume (TBV).

 

Analysis:

We quantified variability across three scales: intra-individual (across the brain), inter-individual (across the population), and lifespan variability (developmental trajectories). To characterize the "shape" of variability, we analyzed the population standard deviation of metrics within 8 age cohorts. Finally, we employed multiple linear regression to link white matter features to 8 behavioral domains (including Working Memory, Processing Speed, and Episodic Memory), controlling for age and sex.

Results

Spatial Variability:

Spatial variability followed a robust deep-to-superficial gradient (Figure 1 a-c). Deep white matter structures (e.g., brainstem, internal capsule) showed high population consistency, while variability increased systematically toward the cortical periphery (Figure 1a,b). This gradient was observed across all age groups but evolved dynamically, with spatial variability increasing during childhood and adolescence (Figure 1c).

 

Lifespan Trajectories:

We observed a "convergence-divergence" pattern in microstructural variability (Figure 1d). In infancy, inter-individual variability decreased (convergence) as brains adhered to a developmental blueprint. However, from middle adulthood onward, inter-individual variability significantly increased (divergence), particularly for diffusivity measures. This suggests that aging is a period of growing anatomical heterogeneity.

 

Structure-Function Relationships:

Variability in white matter anatomy significantly predicted individual differences in cognition. Processing speed showed broad associations across multiple pathways, while working memory was linked to specific microstructural features. Notably, structure-function associations were strongest during development, suggesting that cognitive phenotypic variance is most tightly coupled to structural variance during periods of rapid neuroplasticity.

Conclusion

We present a multi-scale, lifespan-informed framework for understanding normative neuroanatomical variability. We demonstrate that variability is not noise, but a dimension of brain organization that is developmentally dynamic and pathway-specific.

We identified a spatial gradient of variability likely reflecting evolutionary hierarchies (conserved deep structures vs. variable superficial connections) [5]. Further, the observed divergence of white matter phenotypes in aging highlights the limitations of using a single "average" template for older populations. Finally, the strong link between structural variability and cognitive performance supports the utility of normative modeling to identify individual deviations in clinical contexts. These findings provide a foundation for precision neuroscience across development and aging.
