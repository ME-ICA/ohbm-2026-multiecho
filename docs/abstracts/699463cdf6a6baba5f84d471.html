Introduction

T1-weighted MRI is widely available in both clinical and research settings. Despite its accessibility, structural MRI has limited capability in capturing individual-level phenotypes such as cognition. Structural similarity mapping has been introduced to estimate an individual’s structural brain network from T1 MRI 1. However, the relevance and practical utility of the network in reflecting brain function and behavior remain unclear. In this study, by adapting the connectome-to-connectome (C2C) transformation modeling framework 2,3, we developed a cross-modal transformation model that predicts whole-brain functional connectomes (FCs) from T1-derived structural similarity networks, which can be further used to predict individual behaviors.  

Methods

We analyzed the MRI dataset from Yoo et al.2, which includes task-free and attention task-related (gradual onset continuous performance task, gradCPT) fMRI from 92 healthy participants. From T1-weighted MRI, we constructed volumetric similarity networks (VSNs) by computing z-transformed histogram correlation metrics between regional volumetric features defined by the Shen 268 atlas 4,5.  

We employed the connectome-to-connectome (C2C) transformation framework3 to develop a cross-modal model that predicts individual task-free functional connectomes (FCs) from T1-derived VSNs. The model first extracted latent structural subcomponents from VSNs and latent functional subcomponents from empirical task-free FCs using principal component analysis (PCA). Structural subcomponents were then transformed into functional subcomponents using partial least squares (PLS) regression, and whole-brain task-free FCs were reconstructed from the transformed features. To avoid arbitrariness in model specification, we implemented systematic parameter optimization for the number of principal components and latent dimensions in the PLS transformation. Model performance was evaluated by comparing predicted and empirical task-free FCs using spatial correlation (r), mean squared error (MSE), and predictive validity (q²). Brain-state specificity was further assessed using state fingerprinting, quantified by identification success rate (%), which tested whether each predicted task-free FC was more similar to its corresponding empirical task-free FC than to empirical task-related FCs. Lastly, behavioral relevance was assessed by connectome-based predictive modeling (CPM) to predict individual attention behavior (gradCPT and general attention) from their predicted task-free FCs, evaluated using correlation (r) and q2. All cross-modal transformation modeling and CPM analyses used 10-fold cross-validation, repeated 1,000 times.  

To assess generalizability, we conducted replication analyses using three independent clinical datasets: the ADHD200 sample from three different sites (New York University Child Study Center [NYU; n = 184], Oregon Health and Science University [OHSU; n = 65], and Peking University [PU; n = 219]), the UCLA consortium for Neuropsychiatric Phenomics (LA5c) dataset (n = 248), and an OpenNeuro Parkinson’s disease (PD) dataset (n = 51). The ADHD200 dataset included children and adolescent participants with ADHD and controls, while the UCLA dataset comprised adult participants across multiple psychiatric diagnoses, including ADHD, bipolar disorder, and schizophrenia. The PD dataset consisted of an aging population with Parkinson’s disease. For the PD dataset, model performance was evaluated using leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) for within-cohort prediction. For the ADHD200 and UCLA datasets, C2C models were trained exclusively on controls and applied to clinical samples without retraining to test out-of-sample transfer of normative VSN-FC mappings. In all replication datasets, predicted task-free FCs were further used for behavioral prediction, targeting attention in PD, inattention in ADHD200, and inattention and impulsivity in the UCLA dataset. 

Results

We observed high spatial correlation between predicted and empirical task-free FCs (r = 0.70; MSE = 0.02; q2 = 0.49), demonstrating the successful prediction of individual FCs from T1 MRI. The similarity between predicted and empirical task-free FCs was significantly higher than the similarity between empirical VSNs and empirical task-free FCs (r = 0.06) (p < 0.01), indicating that predicted FCs more closely resemble empirical FC patterns, and successfully capture cognitive-state specificity in functional connectomes achieving an identification success rate of 91.3%. Behavioral predictions using predicted task-free FCs (gradCPT: r = 0.28 [p < 0.01], q2 = 0.07;   general attention: r = 0.22 [p = 0.04], q2 = 0.05) significantly surpassed predictions using VSNs alone in gradCPT and general attention measures (gradCPT: r = 0.08 [p = 0.43], q2 = -0.13; general attention: r = 0.06 [p = 0.56], q2 = -0.13), and even exhibited comparable prediction performance to empirical task-free FCs (gradCPT: r = 0.39 [p < 0.01], q2 = 0.13;  general attention: r = 0.382 [p < 0.01], q2 = 0.12). 

Across the all replication datasets, the transformation model achieved consistent FC prediction performance, with spatial similarity between predicted and empirical task-free FCs at approximately r = 0.50 (NYU: r = 0.47, MSE = 0.08, q2 = 0.21; OHSU: r = 0.52, MSE = 0.10, q2 = 0.25; PU: r =0.55, MSE = 0.08, q2 = 0.30; UCLA: r = 0.53, MSE = 0.09, q2 = 0.28; PD: r = 0.48, MSE  = 0.09, q2 = -0.12). In the PD dataset, predicted FCs captured individual differences in attention (r = 0.19 [p = 0.18], q2 = -1.24), whereas behavioral prediction based on empirical task-free FCs yielded (r = 0.46 [p < 0.01], q2 = 0.21). In the ADHD200 dataset, predicted FCs yielded a reliable prediction of inattention across sites (NYU: r = 0.20 [p = 0.05], q2 = 0.01; OHSU: r = 0.36 [p = 0.05], q2 = 0.04; PU: r = 0.20 [p = 0.06], q2 = -0.01). In contrast, predictions using empirical task-free FCs were weaker or variable across sites (NYU: r = -0.038 [p = 0.71], q2 = -7.35; OHSU: r = 0.47 [p = 0.01], q2 = -10.05; PU: r = -0.03 [p = 0.77], q2 = -10.54). In the UCLA dataset, predicted FCs showed predictive relationships with both inattention (r = 0.14 [p = 0.10], q2 = 0.06) and impulsivity (r = 0.15 [p = 0.08], q2 =  0.03) across diagnostic groups, including ADHD, bipolar disorder, and schizophrenia. Corresponding predictions based on empirical task-free FCs were reduced or inconsistent (inattention: r = 0.13 [p = 0.15], q2 = -0.88; impulsivity: r = 0.04 [p = 0.62], q2 = -0.89). 

Across both main and replication datasets, VSNs-based predicted FCs demonstrated stable FC-behavior relationships, whereas behavioral prediction based on empirical FCs was more variable across cohorts.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that individual functional connectomes (FCs) can be predicted directly from T1-weighted MRI using a cross-modal transformation model. The proposed model successfully predicted task-free FCs from T1-derived volumetric similarity networks (VSNs), and the predicted FCs consistently outperformed empirical VSNs in predicting individual attentional behaviors, achieving performance comparable to predictions based on empirical FCs in the healthy adult population. Importantly, these findings generalized to independent clinical datasets spanning adolescents to aging populations, despite substantial heterogeneity across sites, diagnoses, and imaging conditions. Together, these results suggest that T1-based volumetric similarity networks encode functionally relevant information that can be reliably transformed into behaviorally meaningful functional representations across the lifespan. When combined with the cross-modal transformation model, this approach offers a scalable and practical alternative for studying brain–behavior relationships in settings where high-quality functional MRI data are limited or unavailable.
