Introduction

The pathophysiological cascade of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is characterized by the accumulation of amyloid-β (Aβ), followed by the spread of aggregated tau pathology, ensuing neurodegeneration and cognitive decline. Therefore, Aβ-associated tau accumulation is considered a key driver of dementia development. Thus, it is critical to understand the mechanisms by which Aβ triggers tau spreading, to identify novel therapeutic targets to attenuate Aβ-related tau accumulation.

Pre-clinical studies and clinical neuroimaging studies have established a tau progression model, in which tau spreads trans-synaptically, using brain networks as conduits to propagate through the brain^1. 

Furthermore, recent studies have shown an activity-dependent synaptic release of hyperphosphorylated tau (p-tau) seeds in AD that may drive the trans-synaptic spread of tau in dependency of Aβ pathology^2. Based on these findings, our key next goal is to understand how Aβ initiates and drives tau spread across connected brain regions. 

Aβ is a potent driver of neuronal hyper-excitability and hyper-synchronicity in animal models of AD and is associated with increased epileptiform activity and seizures in early-stage AD^3. Importantly, we recently demonstrated that Aβ promotes functional hyperconnectivity - a fMRI-assessed proxy of synchronous neuronal activity - thereby facilitating the connectivity-based spreading of tau pathology^4. Together these findings suggest neuronal hyperactivity and hyper-synchronicity to be a crucial mechanism linking Aβ deposition and tau spreading in AD.

In the current study we hypothesized that Aβ provokes a regional excitatory shift in neuronal activity, resulting in an accelerated trans-neuronal spread of tau pathology. Crucially, the identification of neuronal hyperexcitability as a novel mechanistic link between the two hallmark pathologies of Alzheimer’s disease potentially opens new avenues for therapeutic intervention.

Methods

We first assessed the effect of Aβ on neuronal hyperactivity by using a previously established novel algorithm^5 to estimate the excitatory to inhibitory (E/I) ratio from resting-state fMRI in 104 amyloid-negative controls (centiloid <10) and 204 early-stage AD subjects (centiloid 10 to 70) from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative, who also underwent amyloid-PET. Second, we used glucose metabolism (FDG-PET) as a proxy of neuronal activity in 638 amyloid-positive AD spectrum patients from the ADNI cohort, with a subset (n=215) of them having tau-PET at a later timepoint. Lastly, we analysed post-mortem data of 5 AD patients and 4 controls from the Munich Brain Bank stained for c-Fos as a marker of ante-mortem neuronal activity.

Results

Resting-state fMRI-based E/I-ratio assessment in Aβ negative controls showed biologically plausible stronger patterns with high ratio in motor cortices and low E/I-ratio in association cortices (Fig.1A). With increasing centiloid levels, we found an increase of estimated E/I-ratio, indicating a hyper-excitatory shift of neuronal activity with disease progression (Fig.1A,C). When investigating the ROI-wise association between regional centiloid values and E/I-ratio contrast (E/I-ratio difference between early-stage AD patients and low centiloid controls) across early-stage AD patients, we found an overall positive association between centiloid levels and E/I-ratio contrast (CI 95% [0.05,0.08], p < 0.001). Second, when looking at FDG-PET as a proxy of neuronal activity, we found that within subjects higher regional amyloid-PET was linked to higher FDG-PET (z-scored) (correlationamyloid-PET vs. FDG-PET: 95% CI [0.08,0.32] p-value <0.01), suggesting higher neuronal activity in Aβ-harbouring regions (Fig. 2A). Similarly, we found post-mortem elevated neuronal c-Fos expression in AD brain tissue vs. controls, indicating higher ante-mortem neuronal activity (Fig.3G - not dipicted in the abstract). Finally, we found that amyloid-PET-based prediction of subject-level future tau accumulation is improved when including regional FDG-PET (Fig.2B) and that FDG-PET-assessed hypermetabolism mediates subject-level effects of Aβ on subsequent tau accumulation in preclinical stages of AD (Fig.2C).

Conclusion

We employed three complementary approaches—estimation of the excitation–inhibition (E–I) balance from functional connectivity data, assessment of cerebral glucose metabolism using FDG-PET, and quantification of neuronal activity via c-fos immunostaining in post-mortem tissue—to robustly demonstrate a shift toward neuronal hyper-excitability in the early stages of Alzheimer’s disease. This hyper-excitatory state was consistently associated with accelerated tau propagation. Collectively, these findings indicate that amyloid-β–associated neuronal hyperexcitability represents a key mechanistic contributor to the coupling of amyloid and tau pathologies and highlight neuronal hyperexcitability as a promising therapeutic target for attenuating the Aβ–tau pathological axis in Alzheimer’s disease.
