Introduction

Despite decades of use in both clinical and preclinical research, the precise nature of large-scale functional connectivity (FC) and resting-state network (RSN) activity remains a subject of debate. Recent studies have proposed that FC, as measured by fMRI, is shaped by traveling waves coupled to fluctuations in arousal1,2, thereby constraining whole-brain activity to a low-dimensional manifold embedded within an arousal-related space3. However, the relative contributions of arousal-linked components and localized standing dynamics to the organization and dynamics of FC remain unclear. Here, we address this question by combining transcranial functional ultrasound imaging (fUSI) with pupillometry in the mouse4.

Methods

We leveraged the minimally invasive nature of fUSI to measure cerebral blood volume (CBV) signals through the intact skull of lightly sedated mice, while simultaneously recording pupil diameter as an index of arousal. We used a novel sedative combination encompassing the use of low-dose isoflurane with chlorprothixene, which preserves spontaneous pupil dynamics4. To dissect traveling-wave and standing-wave activity, we extended the co-activation pattern (CAP) framework5 to explicitly incorporate temporal structure. Specifically, we identified stereotyped, phase-locked activation sequences relative to a reference signal (including global or regional CBV fluctuations and pupil dynamics). We term these phase-locked sequences: dynamic CAPs (dCAPs). This approach, together with conventional phase-difference analyses, enabled systematic investigation of the coexistence of stationary and propagating wave dynamics in fUSI timeseries.

Results

Using fUSI, we identified large-scale FC networks whose spatial organization closely recapitulates canonical RSNs previously described with fMRI in the mouse brain5. Despite the use of light sedation, videometric analysis revealed unexpectedly rich pupil dynamics, characterized by brief and rapid dilations, indicative of pronounced fluctuations in arousal. CAP analysis demonstrated that spontaneous whole-brain activity recurrently occupies three dominant co-activation states, which act as attractors of the dynamic within a low-dimensional state-transition space. Modeling state transitions while excluding self-transitions revealed that these states are not visited in a strictly cyclic manner, but instead with varying periods. Given that dCAPs are also based on non-regular temporal resampling, this analysis further supported the presence of pseudoperiodic cycles in the hemodynamic signals. We further examined this dynamic organization of whole-brain fUSI and identified robust infraslow (0.04–0.1 Hz) stationary hemodynamic waves (referenced to intrinsic CBV activity), superimposed on slower (<0.03 Hz), arousal-linked traveling waves (referenced to pupil dynamics). Stationary wave-like activity preferentially engaged homotopic and anatomically interconnected regions, yielding spatial patterns consistent with established RSN architecture. In contrast, arousal-related traveling waves recruited a distributed anatomical trajectory encompassing subcortical structures, including the superior colliculus, thalamus, and dorsal striatum, and propagated toward cortical regions, initiating in retrosplenial cortex and extending to lateral and visual cortices. Notably, phase-difference analyses indicated that static FC patterns are more accurately explained by fast, intermittent in-phase coupling between regions than by fixed temporal delays along the trajectory of the slower traveling waves.

Conclusion

Collectively, these findings support the notion that intrinsic network activity arises from weakly coupled pseudo-oscillators, enabling the coexistence and interaction of local standing waves and arousal-driven traveling waves across distinct temporal scales. Our data also suggest that under low-arousal conditions, the dimensionality of brain dynamics is increased by the independent contribution of standing hemodynamic waves, rather than being compressed into a single arousal-embedded manifold. The dCAPs framework we introduced here provides a new way for interrogating the spatiotemporal organization of both standing and traveling hemodynamic activity underlying FC.
