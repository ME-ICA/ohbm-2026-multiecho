Introduction

Adolescents with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) often report sensory symptoms, such as hypersensitivity to bright light. Sensory symptoms likely result from a different processing of sensory information. Sensory processing is modulated by the subcortical locus coeruleus-norepinephrine (LC-NE) system1 that filters sensory input based on attributed salience and reward2, called sensory selectivity. Despite high prevalences, the neurophysiology of sensory symptoms remains elusive. In this study, with a pre-registered analysis (https://osf.io/9qcz8), we investigated the effects of experimentally manipulated salience and reward on the LC-NE system and on cortical sensory processing in a group comparison between autism, healthy controls and other mental health conditions.

Methods

The sample included 48 autistic adolescents (ASD), 53 healthy controls (CON) and 42 non-autistic adolescents with other mental health conditions (MHC). The MHC group (anxiety, depression, ADHD) allowed us to describe sensory differences specific to ASD. Participants performed an active visual oddball task (figure 1) with infrequent (20%), and thus salient, oddballs among frequent standards. The counterbalanced task design included independent manipulations of stimulus salience and reward. Simultaneous pupillometry and electroencephalography (EEG) during the oddball task provided stimulus-evoked pupillary response (SEPR) as an index of subcortical LC-NE activity3, and the event-related potential P3 as a cortical marker of attention-driven sensory processing4. Manipulation effects and group differences were explored with linear mixed-effects models on aggregated and trial level in R.

Results

Main effects of stimulus on SEPR (F(1, 980) = 3582.12, p < 0.001) and P3 amplitude (F(1, 889) = 430.23, p < 0.001) indicated higher SEPR (β = 1.6 [1.55; 1.65]) and higher P3 amplitude (β = 1.65 [0.59; 0.71]) to oddballs than standards. Groups differed in these stimulus effects (fig 2) on SEPR (F(2, 980) = 20.02, p < 0.001) and P3 amplitude (F(2, 889) = 6.28, p < 0.01). ASD (β = 0.39 [0.30; 0.48]) exhibited a smaller stimulus effect on SEPR compared to both CON (β = 0.79 [0.70; 0.87]) and MHC (β = 0.63 [0.53; 0.73]), while MHC showed a smaller effect than CON. On P3 amplitude, the stimulus effect was also smaller in ASD (β = 0.60 [0.49; 0.70]) and MHC (β = 0.55 [0.44; 0.67]) compared to CON (β = 0.79 [0.70; 0.89]), with no difference between ASD and MHC. Stimulus salience modulated the stimulus effects on SEPR (F(1, 980) = 112.78, p < 0.001) and P3 amplitude (F(1, 889) = 27.59, p < 0.001). The increased SEPR to oddballs versus standards was stronger at high (β = 1.73 [1.65; 1.80]) versus low (β = 1.48 [1.40; 1.55]) salience. Similarly, the stimulus effect on P3 amplitude was larger at high (β = 0.78 [0.69; 0.86]) versus low salience (β = 0.52 [0.43; 0.60]). Trial level analyses additionally revealed an interaction with group on SEPR (F(2, 73644) = 8.24, p < 0.001). Salience emphasized the stimulus effect most strongly in ASD (β = 0.47 [0.42; 0.52]) compared to CON (β = 0.33 [0.28; 0.38]) and MHC (β = 0.37 [0.31; 0.43]). The effect of salience on P3 amplitude did not differ by group (p > 0.7). Reward modulated the stimulus effects on SEPR (F(1, 980) = 21.92, p < 0.001) and P3 amplitude (F(1, 889) = 17.86, p < 0.001). SEPR was relatively increased to oddballs than standards at high (β = 0.73 [0.65; 0.80]) versus low reward (β = 0.48 [0.40; 0.55]). Similarly, the P3 stimulus effect was emphasized at high (β = 0.78 [0.69; 0.86]) versus low reward (β = 0.52 [0.43; 0.60]). Trial level analyses additionally showed an interaction with group on SEPR (F(2, 73642) = 11.50, p < 0.001) and P3 amplitude (F(2, 63581) = 5.07, p < 0.01). Reward emphasized the stimulus effect on SEPR more strongly in both ASD (β = 0.22 [0.17; 0.28]) and MHC (β = 0.25 [0.20; 0.31]) than in CON (β = 0.08 [0.03; 0.14]). In contrast, on P3 amplitude, reward had the largest effect in CON (β = 0.21 [0.16; 0.27]) compared to ASD (β = 0.12 [0.05; 0.18]) and MHC (β = 0.09 [0.02; 0.15]).

Conclusion

The Oddball Task induced sensory selectivity across groups. Sensory selectivity in the LC-NE system was lowest in ASD, but attention-driven sensory processing on the cortical level was as much reduced in other mental disorders. Salience and reward enhanced sensory selectivity specific for clinical groups only in the LC-NE system. Particularly ASD profited from both manipulations whereas MHC showed increased sensory selectivity only in response to higher reward. Our findings suggest that sensory symptoms in autism originate from attenuated subcortical LC-NE signaling for salient stimuli that impair sensory selectivity rather than from altered cortical evaluation of salience or reward.
