Introduction

Motor-related cortical potentials (MRCPs) reflect cortical processes underlying movement preparation and execution1,2. Their amplitude and temporal dynamics are influenced by several task-related factors, including perceived effort3. Previous work has demonstrated that vertical arm movements are planned to optimally exploit gravity, leading to directional asymmetries in kinematics and muscle activation that minimize effort. Upward and downward movements differ systematically in their temporal organization and in the phasic activation of antigravity muscles, reflecting predictive motor strategies that integrate gravitational torque4,5.

While gravity-related motor optimization is well documented at kinematic and electromyographic levels, its neurophysiological correlates during motor planning remain poorly understood. Neuroimaging studies have implicated vestibular and insular regions in gravity perception, but their limited temporal resolution prevents disentangling planning-related from execution-related processes. MRCPs offer a unique opportunity to address this issue, as they capture slow cortical activity preceding movement onset.

Here, we investigated whether MRCPs encode gravity-related motor optimization during the planning of vertical arm movements. We compared kinematic, EMG, and EEG signatures of upward and downward reaching movements and tested the hypothesis that directional differences in effort minimization are reflected in distinct MRCP amplitudes and temporal profiles across cortical regions.

Methods

Participants

Twenty-height right-handed & healthy young adults were recruited (mean age = 25.6 ± 4.7 years; 10 women). All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, and reported no neurological, musculoskeletal, or pain-related disorders affecting the upper limbs. All participants gave informed oral consent prior to participation.

Experimental setup and task

Participants were seated upright on a stool with feet flat on the floor (Figure1). Three circular targets (3 cm diameter) were vertically aligned on a tripod. The central target (T0) was positioned at shoulder height, directly in front of the participant, at a distance of 2 cm from the fingertip when the arm was extended. The upper (T1) and lower (T2) targets were positioned symmetrically above and below T0, corresponding to a 20° shoulder rotation. Two light-emitting diodes placed above and below T0 served as visual cues. The spatial arrangement of the diodes and targets was designed to allow task execution without eye movements, such that participants maintained fixation on T0 throughout each trial. 

Each trial began with participants pointing to T0 with their right arm and maintaining a static posture. After approximately 5 s, one diode illuminated (S1), indicating the upcoming movement direction (upward or downward). Two seconds later, the diode turned off (S2), serving as the imperative signal to initiate movement. Participants then executed a fast, single-degree-of-freedom vertical arm movement toward T1 or T2 in the parasagittal plane. Reaction time was not emphasized. After reaching the target, participants maintained the final posture for ~2 s before relaxing. Control trials involving horizontal movements were included to prevent directional anticipation.

Participants completed 30 upward, 30 downward, 10 rightward, and 10 leftward trials (80 trials total), presented in randomized order. Trials were separated by ~10 s rest periods, with a 1-min break after 40 trials.

Data acquisition

Kinematic data were recorded at 100 Hz using a six-camera optoelectronic motion capture system (Vicon, Oxford, UK). Reflective markers were placed on the acromion, arm, lateral epicondyle, wrist, and index finger.

Surface EMG signals were recorded bilaterally from the anterior and posterior deltoid using bipolar electrodes (sampling rate: 1,000 Hz).

Continuous EEG was recorded using a 64-channel Biosemi ActiveTwo system (sampling rate: 2,048 Hz), with electrodes positioned according to the international 10–20 system. Electrooculographic activity was monitored using electrodes placed at the outer canthi and below the left eye. Mastoid electrodes were used for offline re-referencing.

Data processing

Kinematic data were low-pass filtered (Butterworth, 5 Hz). Movement onset and offset were defined using a 10% peak velocity threshold. Extracted variables included movement duration, amplitude, and relative timing of peak acceleration and peak velocity.

EMG signals were band-pass filtered (30–300 Hz), rectified, and low-pass filtered (20 Hz). Phasic EMG activity was estimated using a classical subtraction method4,5. Negative phases of phasic EMG were then quantified using a negativity index reflecting amplitude and duration.

EEG data were downsampled to 512 Hz, band-pass filtered (0.05–35 Hz), and corrected for ocular artifacts using independent component analysis. Epochs were time-locked to movement onset and baseline-corrected. MRCPs were quantified across predefined temporal windows (Resting Potential: -2.5s to -2s; Early Potential: -2s to -1.5s; Between Potential: -1.5s to -0.5s; Late Potential: 0.5s to movement onset; Monitoring Potential: during movement) and regions of interest encompassing motor, sensorimotor, and visual cortices.

Statistical analysis

We used Paired-sample t-tests to compare upward and downward movements and deviation from zero potential. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Holm-Bonferroni correction was applied.

Results

Kinematics

Movement amplitude did not differ between upward and downward conditions (upward: 25.3 ± 5.4°; downward: 24.8 ± 4.7°), indicating comparable spatial accuracy across directions. In contrast, clear directional asymmetries were observed in the temporal organization of movements, as reported by previous studies4,5. Downward movements exhibited significantly longer relative times to peak acceleration and peak velocity compared to upward movements (t(27)>4.00, P<.001, Cohen’s d>0.76), reflecting later occurrence of these kinematic landmarks within the movement duration. Conversely, overall movement duration was slightly longer for upward than downward movements. These results confirm the presence of direction-dependent motor strategies consistent with gravity-related optimization.

Electromyography

Phasic EMG analysis revealed characteristic direction-dependent patterns in antigravity muscle activity. During upward movements, a negative phase in the phasic component of the anterior deltoid emerged predominantly during the deceleration phase, whereas during downward movements, negativity occurred earlier, during the acceleration phase. These negative phases indicate periods in which muscle activation fell below the tonic level required to compensate for gravity, reflecting active exploitation of gravitational torque. These EMG results confirm that participants systematically minimized muscular effort by adapting motor commands to movement direction.

Electroencephalography

MRCP analyses revealed robust and widespread directional effects (Figure2). Across all regions of interest and all time windows except the resting baseline, MRCP amplitudes differed significantly between upward and downward movements (t(27)>3.58, P<.001, Cohen’s d>0.68). Directional differences emerged early, shortly after the preparatory cue, and persisted through late preparation and movement execution.

In motor and sensorimotor regions, downward movements were associated with more negative MRCP amplitudes during the late preparatory period and execution phase, whereas upward movements showed reduced negativity or positive amplitudes. These effects were present on both hemispheres, despite movements being performed with the dominant arm. One-sample tests confirmed that MRCPs associated with downward movements significantly deviated from zero in most motor and sensorimotor regions (t(27)>4.77, P<.001, Cohen’s d>0.90), consistent with classical readiness potential dynamics.

In contrast, visual regions exhibited a distinct pattern. MRCPs in these areas remained predominantly positive across all time windows for both directions, although their amplitudes still differed significantly between upward and downward movements. Notably, no progressive negativity characteristic of motor preparation was observed in visual regions, supporting a functional dissociation between visual processing and motor planning components as measured by the MRCP.

Together, these results demonstrate that gravity-related directional asymmetries are reflected not only in kinematic and muscular signatures of effort minimization but also in the temporal dynamics and spatial distribution of preparatory cortical activity.

Conclusion

The present study aimed to explore whether gravity-related motor optimization during vertical arm movements is reflected in movement-related cortical potentials. By combining kinematic, electromyographic, and electroencephalographic recordings within a temporally controlled paradigm, we sought to determine whether directional differences in effort minimization are associated with distinct patterns of preparatory cortical activity.

At the behavioral and muscular levels, our results replicate well-established findings: upward and downward movements exhibit robust directional asymmetries in temporal organization and in the phasic activity of antigravity muscles, consistent with predictive motor strategies that exploit gravitational torque to reduce effort4,5. These observations confirm that the experimental paradigm successfully elicited gravity-related optimization processes.

At the cortical level, MRCP analyses revealed systematic differences between upward and downward movements across multiple preparatory time windows and cortical regions. These differences emerged early, shortly after the preparatory cue, and persisted through movement onset and execution. Importantly, such early modulations suggest the involvement of anticipatory processes rather than purely feedback-driven mechanisms. The presence of direction-dependent MRCPs in motor and sensorimotor regions, together with distinct patterns observed in visual areas, is compatible with the hypothesis that gravity is integrated into motor planning through distributed cortical processes.

However, these results must be interpreted with caution. This study represents a first exploratory attempt to investigate gravity-related motor optimization using EEG. MRCP analyses remain relatively coarse and simplistic by contemporary standards. While MRCPs offer the advantage of direct comparability with a large body of literature on effort, motor preparation, and contingent negative variation1,2,3, they provide only limited specificity and do not allow direct inference about underlying neural generators or network interactions. Moreover, directional differences in MRCPs cannot be unambiguously attributed to gravity-related effort minimization alone, as they may also reflect visual, attentional, or expectancy-related processes induced by the preparatory cue. The present findings therefore do not demonstrate a definitive cortical mechanism of gravity integration, but rather suggest that gravity-related motor optimization may be reflected in slow cortical potentials during movement planning. 

This initial step provides a necessary foundation for future investigations using more refined EEG approaches. Time–frequency analyses, source reconstruction, and functional connectivity measures will be required to disentangle feedforward and feedback components, identify task-specific neural oscillations, and characterize the network dynamics underlying gravity-efficient motor control. By beginning with simple and well-characterized measures, the present study lays the groundwork for a more comprehensive understanding of how the human brain integrates gravitational constraints into motor planning.
