Introduction

Generalizable machine learning models for personalized first-line treatment selection of psychiatric disorders have gained much attention over the last decade. However, the development of such models has been increasingly challenging, especially for heterogeneous populations such as patients with major depressive disorder (MDD). As a first-line treatment option, antidepressants – such as serotonin-reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) - are often used, to which approximately 35-50% of patients respond.1 The remaining patients who do not respond to initial treatment have a wide range of options available in a stepped care approach. However, each additional treatment phase is met with increased treatment resistance.2 Generalizable machine learning models may be needed to meaningfully inform treatment selection, not only to help patients towards remission more quickly, but also to lower the burden for family, caregivers, and health care professionals and the cost to society. 

Resting-state EEG has shown promising results as a biomarker to predict treatment response to SSRIs. One study stood out with an extraordinary balanced accuracy of 79.2% (sensitivity 67.3%; specificity 92%) in predicting escitalopram outcome with baseline EEG features.3 However, the study may have suffered from data leakage: their method selected EEG features using the whole dataset, leading to inflated performance.4  

In this current study, we aimed to replicate findings by Zhdanov et al. using baseline features. The results show that (1) performance was indeed inflated by non-independent feature selection compared to the (correct) model with feature selection on the training set only, and (2) that performance could be improved using data harmonization across sites.

Methods

This prognostic replication study used baseline EEG data from the CAN-BIND-1 study. This study was approved by the ethics review board. All patients provided written informed consent. Treatment outcome was evaluated using the Montgomery-Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) after 8 weeks of treatment. Responders were classified as having more than 50% symptom reduction. As an intention-to-treat analysis, we also included patients (n = 8) who dropped out after 2 weeks but had provided a MADRS score. These patients were classified as non-responders.     

Eight-minute resting-state eyes-closed EEGs were recorded at four sites. Data were harmonized to the same sensors (n=58) for all sites, including several preprocessing steps. Closely following the procedures of Zhdanov et al., additional preprocessing was performed using an in-house built automatic MATLAB pipeline including: 1) removing line noise (60Hz), 2) removal of bad channels, 3) band-pass filtering (1-80 Hz for power, source-localized, and multi-scale entropy (MSE) features and 1-30 Hz for microstate features), 4) ICA to remove ocular artifacts, 5) Automatic Artifact Rejection tool (AAR) to remove electromyographic artifacts, and 6) interpolation of bad channels. 

As did Zhdanov et al., we calculated log-transformed absolute power spectral densities, source-localized power, multi-scale entropy (MSE), and microstates.3 Power features were calculated in seven conventional frequency bands: delta (1-3.5Hz), theta (4-8Hz), low alpha (8.5-10Hz), high alpha (10.5-12Hz), low beta (12.5-18Hz), middle beta (18.5-21Hz), and high beta (21.5-30Hz). We extracted absolute power for the frequency bands in the anterior cingulate cortex [ACC], rostral ACC, and medial orbitofrontal cortex, computed with the Python package MNE. MSE was calculated by extracting Sample entropy over multiple time scales (i.e., 1-70), of which the latter refers to averaging the signal over different window lengths. Four distinct EEG microstates were calculated with pycrostates. Hemispheric asymmetry was calculated per matching sensor pairs (e.g., Fp1/Fp2, etc.), excluding midline electrodes (i.e., Cz, Fz, etc.).

We added additional features to increase spectral resolution (i.e., log absolute and relative power values per sensor between 1 and 40 Hz, divided in 1 Hz bins) and cordance per conventional frequency band and channel. Cordance combines absolute and relative power and has been suggested as a relevant feature in depression treatment outcome prediction. 

We first (1) replicated the findings of Zhdanov et al. with their described methodology using support vector machines with radial basis function kernel and 10-fold cross-validation and feature selection on the whole dataset to predict treatment outcome. We then (2) performed feature selection on the training set only, (3) aimed to improve the analysis by regressing out site effects, and (4) added additional informative features. For the fourth analysis, patients from the two smallest sites were excluded. Additionally, we performed (5) sex prediction to benchmark antidepressant prediction performance. For all analyses, vote-based feature selection was employed, as in the original paper. For 100 iterations, samples of 80% responders and 80% non-responders were randomly selected and used in t-tests on all features. Each time a feature reached significance (p < 0.05), a score was assigned (i.e., between 0 and 100). A higher score indicated predictive power of features with respect to treatment outcome. Features were included in analyses if they had a score above a pre-specified threshold (i.e., T = {50, 60, 70, 80}). 

Results

Of the 121 included patients, 55 were responders (45.5%) after 8 weeks of treatment. Patients had a median age of 33 years [interquartile range 23], with 44 females (36.4%). The median MADRS was 29.0 (standard deviation 8.0) in the total sample.

Balanced accuracies, sensitivity, and specificity for different analyses and vote thresholds are provided in Figure 1. Results showed that performance dropped considerably when features were selected using the training sample only. At a vote threshold of T = 60, the maximum balanced accuracy we obtained was 74.7%, in contrast to the 79.2% reported by Zhdanov et al. Balanced accuracy was greatly reduced from 74.7% (sensitivity, 81.7%; specificity, 67.7%) to 51.1% (sensitivity, 50.0%; specificity, 52.1%) at T = 60, which the authors claimed to be the best trade-off. With independent feature selection, the best balanced accuracy was 57.1% (sensitivity, 55.3%; specificity, 59.6%) at T = 50. After data harmonization through regressing out site-effects, the highest balanced accuracy was 58.1% (sensitivity, 60.3%; specificity, 56.0%) at T = 50. In their work, Zhdanov et al. report that, with increasing vote thresholds (i.e., decreasing number of selected relevant features), balanced accuracy drops. We also noticed this trend in our balanced accuracies across vote thresholds. 

Whereas antidepressant response prediction was poor with independent feature selection, sex prediction remained acceptable with balanced accuracy of 72.8% (sensitivity, 54.5%; specificity, 91.1%), which is comparable to previous studies.5 

Conclusion

We replicated the high balanced accuracies reported by Zhdanov et al. when incorrectly using feature selection on the whole dataset. When features were selected using the training set only, performance was greatly reduced. These results highlight the necessity to avoid data leakage to obtain more accurate estimates of model performance. Data harmonization and additional features slightly increased performance, suggesting that EEG may have the potential to serve as a biomarker for treatment prediction. However, balanced accuracies were still too low to implement these models to aid in clinical decision-making.
