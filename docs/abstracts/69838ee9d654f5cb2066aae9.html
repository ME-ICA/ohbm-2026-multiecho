Introduction

Major depressive disorder is a devastating psychiatric condition that frequently proves refractory to conventional treatments, such as antidepressant medications and psychotherapy. Neuromodulatory interventions including deep brain stimulation (DBS) have been studied for some time as potential management tools for treatment-resistant depression (TRD), with the subcallosal cingulate area (SCC) constituting the most-studied DBS target for this purpose.1  Decades of research indicate that high-frequency SCC-DBS is able to bring about clinically meaningful and durable clinical improvement in roughly 60% of treated patients.1 However, substantial response variability persists, and recent work has highlighted the need for brain-based biomarkers that could enable better personalization and optimization of SCC-DBS therapy. Our group previously acquired resting state functional MRI (fMRI) in fully implanted SCC-DBS patients during active and inactive stimulation conditions, identifying brain areas that exhibited alterations in basal BOLD activity within minutes of stimulation onset.2 In particular, active SCC-DBS decreased activity within the dorsal ACC, posterior cingulate cortex, and precuneus. Changes in these areas appeared to relate to cingulum bundle engagement and correlated with both long-term clinical improvement and intra-session mood fluctuations.

This study interrogated the impact of SCC-DBS on functional connectivity within the brain using fMRI. Specifically, we aimed to better understand how stimulation i) modulates the functional connectivity profile of the SCC target itself; and ii) affects distributed resting state networks (RSNs), which reflect synchronized activity between noncontiguous brain areas and offer insights into the brain’s intrinsic functional architecture.3 Given previous evidence implicating expansion of the salience network in major depressive disorder, we specifically hypothesized that active SCC-DBS may reduce the footprint of this RSN. 

Methods

As previously described,2 Toronto Western Hospital patients who were actively using implanted SCC-DBS devices were recruited for fMRI scanning under the umbrella of institutional REB approval (UHN: #14-8255). Only those with specific Medtronic DBS hardware were considered as per prior MRI safety work. Participants underwent a single 3 Tesla MRI (General Electric Signa HDxt) scanning session, during which one high-resolution T1-weighted structural scan (TR: 8 ms, TE: 3 ms, BW: 31.3 kHz; FA: 20°, matrix: 256×256 voxels) and two 6.5 minute-long rsfMRI scans (TR: 2151-3010 ms, TE: 30 ms, BW: 62.5 kHz; FA: 76-84°, matrix: 64×64 voxels) were acquired. One rsfMRI scan was obtained with the participant’s DBS device turned off (DBS-OFF), the other with their device turned on at clinically determined optimal settings (DBS-ON). The order of rsfMRI scan acquisition was varied between participants. A 5-min washout period was observed between each DBS setting change and the initiation of the related rsfMRI scan. Demographic and clinical outcome data (i.e., baseline and 12-months post-operative HAMD-17 scores) were collected; as in prior work, ≥50% HAMD-17 reduction was deemed the cut-off for “response”.1 For comparative purposes, we also obtained T1-weighted structural and rsfMRI scans from an equal number of age- and sex-matched healthy controls, sourced from the publicly available ABIDE (https://fcon_1000.projects.nitrc.org/indi/abide/) and PPMI (https://www.ppmi-info.org/) databases. 

Acquired fMRI data were preprocessed using BRANT (http://brant.brainnetome.org/), a Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM)-based rsfMRI toolbox.4 Briefly, this involved i) discarding the first 10 volumes of each fMRI sequence; ii) correcting for head motion using volume realignment; iii) coregistering the fMRI and corresponding structural scans; iv) normalizing all images to MNI space; v) resampling the fMRI data to 3×3×3 mm3 and denoising for nuisance variables using a multiple regression model; vi) filtering the resulting fMRI data with a temporal bandpass filter (0.01–0.08 Hz) and smoothing them with a 6 mm full width at half maximum Gaussian kernel.

Two seed-based functional connectivity analyses were performed to understand the potential impact of SCC-DBS on functional brain architecture. The first analysis focused on how SCC-DBS alters the connectivity profile of the SCC target itself, using that region as the seed. The second analysis, examining stimulation-related changes in canonical RSNs, used nodes of the salience network and other neighbouring RSNs (default mode network, executive network, and somatomotor network), defined as per Yeo et al.’s 7-network parcellation.3 The nodes of each network were eroded by 1 voxel and then used as a collective seed to study the overall connectivity of other brain voxels to that network. In each case, Pearson correlations between the average BOLD timecourse of the seed region and the BOLD timecourse of all other voxels in the brain were computed for each rsfMRI scan, yielding a whole-brain connectivity r-map (subsequently subjected to Fisher r-to-z transformation) denoting voxel-wise connectedness to the seed. In the case of the SCC target analysis, these maps were used for voxel-wise mass-univariate paired t-tests – corrected for multiple comparisons using threshold-free cluster enhancement (TFCE; pTFCE < 0.001) – contrasting brain connectivity between participants’ DBS-ON and DBS-OFF conditions. In the case of the canonical RSN analysis, the four RSN connectivity z-maps generated for rsfMRI scan were compared in order to identify the relative size/footprint of the RSNs.5 A “winner-takes-all” method was utilized to assign each voxel to the “most connected” RSN; the resulting RSN-affiliated voxel maps were then converted to cortical vertices and RSN network size was gauged by calculating the proportion of all frontal lobe vertices affiliated with said RSN (Figure 1A). 

Results

A total of 16 SCC-DBS patients were scanned with 3T fMRI under active and inactive stimulation conditions (age: 42.9±12.9 years; sex: 94% female; disease duration: 17.7±8.3 years; surgery-to-scan interval: 7.3±4.7 years; proportion of responders: 69%). Data from 16 age- and sex-matched controls were also obtained from publicly available databases (age: 42.3±13.2 years; sex: 81% female). Voxel-wise comparison of SCC-DBS participants’ DBS-ON and DBS-OFF scans revealed that active stimulation was associated with rapid changes in functional connectivity between the targeted SCC region and a variety of brain areas (pTFCE < 0.001). Specifically, participants tended to exhibit increased connectivity between SCC and rostral ACC, orbitofrontal cortex, insula, and frontal operculum in the DBS-ON state, while showing decreased connectivity between SCC and right middle frontal gyrus and superior and inferior parietal lobule (Figure 2).

Interrogation of SCC-DBS’ impact on the size of canonical frontal lobe RSNs did not reveal any significant change in network size between DBS-ON and DBS-OFF conditions when considering all SCC-DBS participants together. However, DBS responders and non-responders did significantly differ with respect to the extent of stimulation-linked salience network change (t = -2.35, p = 0.034), with responders (mean relative salience network change from DBS-OFF: -13.7±25.1%) but not non-responders (mean relative salience network change from DBS-OFF: 24.7±42.9%) generally exhibiting salience network contraction with active stimulation. During active stimulation, responders’ salience network size moved towards that of healthy controls. In addition, the extent of reduction in salience network size was associated with both relative HAMD-17 reduction from baseline (R = -0.52, R2 = 0.27, p = 0.039) and HAMD-17 score itself (R = 0.57, R2 = 0.32, p =0.022) at 12 months follow-up; that is, patients who exhibited greater reduction in salience network size during active stimulation tended to be those with better clinical outcomes (Figure 1B,C). Change in the size of other frontal lobe RSNs did not relate to clinical outcome.

Conclusion

These 3T fMRI results serve to clarify the way in which SCC-DBS modulates the resting functional architecture of the brain in patients with severe depression, identifying rapid stimulation-related changes both in the connectivity profile of the focal brain area being targeted and in the functional footprint of canonical RSNs within the frontal lobe. In particular, active stimulation appeared to generally strengthen the connectedness of the SCC target with nearby regions such as rostral ACC and orbitofrontal cortex while decoupling SCC from more superficial cortical areas like right middle frontal gyrus (encompassing dorsolateral prefrontal cortex) and superior and inferior parietal lobule. Active SCC-DBS also corresponded to contraction – and potentially normalization – of the salience network in responders, with the extent of salience network reduction correlating with long-term clinical outcomes. The salience network has previously been shown to be aberrantly enlarged in depression.5

Building on previous fMRI work that characterized basal BOLD changes related to SCC-DBS, these findings expand our understanding of the mechanism of action of SCC-DBS and identify a novel putative fMRI-based biomarker of successful treatment that might have applications in personalized therapy (e.g., guiding post-operative selection of stimulation parameters). Future work that seeks to validate these findings in larger, multi-centre cohorts is warranted, as are studies that further explore the relationships between structural and functional connectivity and between short-term and long-term brain changes in SCC-DBS.
