Introduction

Resting-state brain networks (RSNs) are frequently disrupted in neuropsychiatric disorders, promoting the development of fMRI-based RSN atlases for clinical research. Despite the utility of fMRI in mapping brain function, it remains limited by its reliance on hemodynamic changes (neurovascular coupling) rather than direct neuronal activity, as well as susceptibility to motion artefacts and low signal-to-noise ratios. In contrast, [18F]FDG-PET offers a more direct measure of neural activity via metabolic coupling, exhibiting higher signal-to-noise ratios, reproducibility, and replicability. While simultaneous fMRI/PET studies have identified both overlapping and modality-specific networks, the specific value of metabolic connectivity necessitates a dedicated [18F]FDG-PET RSN atlas. Consequently, this study aimed to develop a robust [18F]FDG-PET-based RSN atlas comprised of replicable and reproducible networks to serve as a modality-specific adjunct to fMRI-based templates.

Methods

To assess replicability, we analyzed data of 122 cognitively healthy, amyloid-β–negative subjects from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI), divided into two age- and sex-matched groups (Group 1: n=61, mean age=67.0±3.0; Group 2: n=61, mean age=67.3±2.5). To assess (test-retest) reliability, data of 61 healthy individuals scanned in our institution twice were included (mean age=56.2±4.0) Static [¹⁸F]FDG-PET images underwent standardized preprocessing including spatial normalization and smoothing. Independent Component Analysis (ICA) was performed using the GIFT toolbox (Infomax algorithm) to decompose the data into 30 independent components. Significant voxels were identified using a normal–gamma–gamma mixture model (threshold: μ + 2.5σ). Components were visually inspected to exclude those dominated by white matter or CSF. Replicability and reproducibility of extracted RSNs was assessed using the Dice similarity coefficient (DSC). Components with at least fair overlap (DSC > 0.20) in both metrics were included in the atlas. 

Results

We identified multiple canonical RSNs with fair-to-high consistency, including cerebellar (replicability=0.86; reproducibility=0.89), primary visual (0.60; 0.81), sensorimotor (0.47; 0.54), posterior default mode (0.36; 0.84), central executive (0.58; 0.61), and basal ganglia networks (0.59; 0.53). Furthermore, we found RSNs not typically seen in fMRI: medial prefrontal (0.74; 0.55), parieto-occipital (0.23; 0.55), and inferior temporal (0.48; 0.36). Certain RSNs, such as the auditory and anterior default mode, were consistent across subgroups but lacked sufficient consistency. Thus, the final [18F]FDG-PET atlas comprises the cerebellar, primary visual, sensorimotor, posterior default mode, central executive, basal ganglia, medial prefrontal, parieto-occipital, and inferior temporal networks.

Conclusion

We developed the first RSN atlas based on [18F]FDG-PET images, demonstrating that metabolic connectivity patterns are both replicable across independent cohorts and reproducible across scanning sessions. The atlas includes well-established RSNs alongside networks specific to metabolic imaging, suggesting that [18F]FDG-PET captures aspects of large-scale brain organization not fully represented by fMRI. Given that functional, structural, and molecular architectures do not fully overlap, this PET-based atlas provides a critical modality-specific reference. This atlas, therefore, serves as an essential resource for reducing variability and advancing the study of the brain’s metabolic architecture.
