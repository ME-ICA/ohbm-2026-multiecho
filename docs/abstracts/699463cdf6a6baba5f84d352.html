Introduction

Recent scientific effort has started to delineate how ovarian hormones, which regularly fluctuate during the menstrual cycle, influence neural and cerebrovascular tissue. This may underlie the relationships between later hormonal decline and neurogenerative disease risk 1. Animal models have demonstrated that ovarian hormones lead to vasodilation 2 and increased reactivity 3. However, this remains under-researched in human endocrine states. Previous research has demonstrated a significant influence of oestradiol and progesterone on baseline vascular function, such as resting perfusion (cerebral blood flow; CBF), arterial arrival time, and vessel density 4. To fully profile how these hormones interact with vascular function, it is vital to also consider dynamic aspects that support neural activity.

It is essential that the cerebrovascular system not only delivers sufficient blood at rest, but can also adjust to local tissue demands. This is a key indicator of cerebrovascular reserve capacity and health. Cerebrovascular reactivity (CVR) is the ability of blood vessels to respond to a vasodilating stimulus, such as CO2, and increase CBF in response. Another useful metric to interrogate this is the haemodynamic response function (HRF). Using blood oxygen level dependant (BOLD) MRI, the temporal timecourse of the BOLD signal is measured in response to a stimulus designed to evoke a strong but brief neural response. The final HRF shape contains information about oxygen consumption due to neural activation, cerebrovascular coupling mechanisms, and local CBF. While it is not possible to detangle specific contributions, the HRF provides a useful overview.

This study uses hypercapnic CVR and visually-evoked HRF to investigate the contribution of menstrual-related changes in oestradiol and progesterone to these dynamic aspects of the cerebrovascular system.

Methods

21 healthy, menstruating females (age mean[SD]=22.9[3.91] years; visual acuity mean[SD]=-0.20[0.22] logMAR) completed a 3T MRI (Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma) scanning session during the early follicular (EFP; day 1-4; N=17), late follicular (LFP; day 10-12; N=17), and mid-luteal (MLP; day 20-22; N=18) phase of their menstrual cycle (14 completed all three). All sessions took place at the same time of day, after fasting for 4-6 hours. Blood samples were taken to measure circulating hormones.

Simultaneous BOLD-CVR and CBF-CVR data were collected using a pseudocontinuous arterial spin labelling (pCASL) acquisition during periods of hypercapnia (5% CO2) with PETCO2 monitoring. The parameters were: maximum repetition time [TR]=3600ms, echo time [TE] 1=10ms, TE2=30ms, in-plane resolution=3.4x3.4, slice thickness=6.5mm, GRAPPA acceleration factor=3. The HRF was estimated using a whole brain echo planar imaging (EPI) scan during stimulus presentation. The parameters were: TR=2s, TE=30ms, resolution=2mm3, multi-band acceleration factor=4). A 100% contrast alternating radial checkerboard was presented for 2 seconds, followed by 20 seconds of mean grey luminance (ten repeats). Finally, field map and T1 structural scans were acquired for distortion correction and registration respectively.

The HRF timeseries was averaged across repeats and a gamma variate HRF model fit. Three metrics were extracted to describe the HRF shape – peak amplitude (PA), time-to-peak (TTP), and full-width-half-max (FWHM). PETCO2 traces were used in modelling CVR maps. BOLD-CVR (∆%BOLD/mmHgCO2) and CBF-CVR values (∆ml/100g/min/mmHgCO2) were extracted across the whole brain, while HRF metrics were extracted from visually responsive regions. Mixed linear models investigated the contribution of hormones to vascular outcomes across phases, with participant included as a random effect. Progesterone was regressed against oestradiol to allow investigation of independent variation (hereby called resProgesterone).

Results

The average max-min of oestradiol concentration across a cycle in this sample was 0.51nmol/L, whereas the resProgesterone max-min was 20.43nmol/L. Oestradiol was found to explain a significant amount of global CVR variance, both for CBF-CVR (χ2(1)=50.92; p<0.001; fixed effect estimate[SE]=0.002[0.0003]; average variation attributed to oestradiol across a cycle in this sample=1.01∆ml/100g/min/mmHgCO2) and for BOLD-CVR (χ2(1)=59.14; p<0.001; fixed effect estimate[SE]=0.0002[0.00003]; average variation attributed to oestradiol across a cycle in this sample=0.1∆%BOLD/mmHgCO2). ResProgesterone however, was only significantly associated with global CBF-CVR (χ2(1)=26.71; p<0.001; fixed effect estimate[SE]=0.027[0.0053]; average variation attributed to resProgesterone across a cycle in this sample=0.6∆ml/100g/min/mmHgCO2).

In terms of HRF shape, increasing oestradiol was associated with slightly higher PA (χ2(1)=20.55; p<0.001; fixed effect estimate[SE]=0.0002[0.00004]; average variation attributed to oestradiol across a cycle in this sample=0.1%BOLD) and earlier TTP (χ2(1)=10.036; p=0.002; fixed effect estimate[SE]=-0.0008[0.0003]; average variation attributed to oestradiol across a cycle in this sample=0.4 seconds). Increasing resProgesterone was associated with narrower FWHM (χ2(1)=28.71; p<0.001; fixed effect estimate[SE]=-0.026[0.005]; average variation attributed to resProgesterone across a cycle in this sample=0.5 seconds). No significant regional effects were found in either CVR or HRF.

Assumptions about the HRF shape are baked into standard fMRI analysis pipelines (e.g., for signal convolution), raising questions about how systematic alterations in HRF may impact fMRI outcomes if not considered. To start to answer this question, surface-based population receptive field (pRF; estimate of the size of receptive fields of all functional neurons within a voxel) mapping of the visual cortex was completed. A moving-bars retinotopic stimulus (0-7° eccentricity) was displayed during EPI scanning (TR=2s, TE=30ms, resolution=2mm3). pRF modelling was completed using SamSrf software (v.10.1; https://github.com/samsrf/samsrf) and a 2D Gaussian model, using either a canonical or individually measured HRF. Preliminary results across phases (N=10) suggest that neither oestradiol nor resProgesterone are associated with pRF size when modelled using a canonical HRF (both p>0.05). However, a significant association with oestradiol and slightly larger pRF sizes was discovered when using individually measured HRFs (χ2(1)=6.75; p=0.009; fixed effect estimate[SE]=0.0007° [0.0003]; average variation attributed to oestradiol across a cycle in this sample=0.4°).

Conclusion

This study found evidence that dynamic cerebrovascular functions are sensitive to menstrual-related ovarian hormones. These effects were small but notable within the context of the regular menstrual cycle and raise questions about larger hormonal variations, such as within pregnancy or menopause. This may be a potential mechanism underlying menstrual symptomatology and has implications for fMRI studies that assume intact neurovascular coupling processes in women, regardless of menstrual staging, to make inferences about neural activity. As suggested by these preliminary retinotopy data, this added noise may be obscuring neuroendocrine-related effects.
