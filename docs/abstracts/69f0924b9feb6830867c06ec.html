Introduction

Hemodynamic response function (HRF) translates neuronal activity, e.g., interictal epileptiform discharges (IEDs), into functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) responses. Studies in healthy subjects have shown that HRF is anatomically dependent and hypothesized that it may be altered by disease1. Here, we investigate how HRF variability relates to anatomical location and pathology in the epileptic brain.

Methods

We comprehensively characterized the temporal and spatial features of HRF in the epileptic brain, using a large and well-defined clinical dataset comprising 132 IED related fMRI response maps collected in pharmaco-resistant patients with epilepsy who underwent presurgical EEG-fMRI. All patients subsequently had surgical resection with histopathological diagnosis. First, we built a data-driven HRF library specific to the epileptic brain using regularized HRF deconvolution2 and temporal decomposition3 methods. Second, we mapped HRF features throughout the epileptic brain and investigated their associations with anatomical location using data from regions outside the cavities resected during subsequent surgeries. Third, we evaluated whether HRF features are associated with pathology using data from regions that were subsequently resected and histologically analyzed. To obtain accurate and reliable estimation, we developed a novel hierarchical Bayesian model that performs partial pooling of HRF features following the brain’s anatomical hierarchy, enabling estimated effects to be informed by both local and global homogeneity.

Results

We identified 18 representative HRFs, grouped into four shape categories: no undershoot, low undershoot, moderate early undershoot, and large late undershoot (Fig.2d). HRF features showed structured spatial organization along the anatomical hierarchy. Peak and undershoot amplitudes were larger in primary sensorimotor cortex (Fig.2ab), which also predominantly exhibited HRF groups with earlier peaks and larger undershoots (Fig.2c). Amplitudes and HRF groups showed clear left-right symmetry across cortical and subcortical regions, consistent with prior studies in healthy subjects. The above observations are consistent with recent studies of healthy subjects1,2,4.

HRF features varied systematically across pathologies. Peak amplitude increased from hippocampal sclerosis/dual pathology and cortical malformation, to gliosis and encephalitis. The most probable HRF shape group followed the same trend across pathologies. Model comparison showed that the model including pathology and location achieved the best fit accuracy, generalizability, and parsimony than models excluding pathology. To further demonstrate this association, pathology classification using HRF features alone achieved good performance (e.g., κ = 0.74), although this performance may be partially inflated by associations between pathology and anatomical location.

Conclusion

This study provides the first systematic analysis of HRF variability across the epileptic brain. We identified representative HRF shapes and further mapped their spatial distribution throughout cortical and subcortical regions, revealing structured rather than random variability. Our restuls suggest that HRF variability is associated with pathology, in addition to its dependence on anatomical location, and emphasize the limitations of traditional approaches based on canonical or multiple predefined HRFs. This study establishes a foundation for HRF modeling of EEG-fMRI analysis in epilepsy, motivating region- and pathology-based HRF modulation in epilepsy studies. These insights may refine presurgical evaluation using EEG-fMRI, improve localization of epileptogenic zones, and inform broader understanding of neurovascular dynamics in neurological disease.
