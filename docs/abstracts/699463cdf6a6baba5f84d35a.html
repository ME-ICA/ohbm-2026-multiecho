Introduction

Over the past decades, brain dynamics have been characterized as a metastable and chaotic system, in which large-scale networks exhibit a continuous flow of interactions and transitions among quasi-stable configurations over time. Yet how these dynamics vary across populations, the lifespan, and clinical conditions remain poorly understood. Existing approaches typically quantify brain states by clustering hand-crafted dynamic connectivity features, such as LEiDA and EiDA. However, these clustering pipelines lack any explicit supervisory signal and estimate state centroids directly from the empirical distribution of each cohort, rendering the resulting clusters highly sensitive to demographic, scanner, and site-related distributional differences. In this study, we introduce NeuroLex, a vector-quantization-based autoencoder (VQ-VAE) framework that learns a shared embedding manifold through reconstruction-driven representation learning and performs discrete state quantization within this aligned latent space. Using fMRI data from 10,620 participants across 97 scanners, we identify 12 robust brain-state tokens that are shared across cohorts. Experiments show that our Neurolex outperforms several baselines in both clustering quality and half-split reproducibility. We then fit normative models to fractional occupancy and dwell time to chart lifespan trajectories and quantify heterogeneous deviations in psychiatric cohorts relative to age and sex. Our results establish a scalable framework for discrete brain-state modeling and enable population-level analyses of lifespan variation and disease heterogeneity within a unified token space. The models and norms are transferable to unseen datasets and can be used for disease heterogeneity mapping.

Methods

Participants and datasets. We aggregated fMRI data from 97 scanning sites across multiple publicly available studies, yielding a total of 10,620 participants (7,721 healthy or non-diagnosed individuals, 57.48% female; 2,899 individuals with clinical conditions) aged 6-88 years, as is shown in Fig. 1a. The data were collected from ABIDE, CAMCAN, CORR, FCON1000, HBN, NKIRS, SALD, SLIM, SRPBS-Open, SRPBS-1600, and several subsets of OpenNeuro (ds000030, ds001408, ds001747, ds001796, ds002330, ds002785, ds002790, ds003037, ds003346, ds003469, ds003831, ds003974, ds003988, ds004144, ds004169, ds004469, ds004636, ds004648, ds004697, ds004718). Resting-state scans with TR ≤ 2 s and total acquisition time ≥ 240 s were retained. For clinical analyses, we included five clinical cohorts: attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), autism spectrum disorder (ASD), major depressive disorder (MDD), schizophrenia (SZ), and anxiety disorders (ANX). All datasets were preprocessed with fMRIPrep, including slice-timing correction, motion correction, susceptibility-distortion correction, and spatial normalization to MNI space. ICA-AROMA was applied for motion-related denoising followed by nuisance regression and band-pass filtering (0.01-0.1 Hz). To ensure temporal consistency across datasets, we downsampled all scans with TRs in the 2.0-2.05 s range using a 5% tolerance.

NeuroLex. For brain state quantization, we developed NeuroLex, a vector-quantized graph Transformer framework designed to discretize fMRI brain dynamics, as illustrated in Fig. 1b. NeuroLex takes as input phase-locking matrices derived from the instantaneous phases of regional fMRI time series. In this study, we used the Yeo-7 atlas for network mapping, resulting in seven functional sub-networks. Each phase-locking matrix at a given time point is treated as a graph in which nodes represent networks and edges represent the strength of phase-locking between them. The encoder is a graph-aware Transformer1 equipped with learnable positional embeddings and multi-head self-attention layers. It produces a latent vector that summarizes the instantaneous topology of the functional network. These latent representations are then discretized using a vector quantizer that contains a finite codebook of learnable embedding vectors. For each latent vector, the quantizer assigns the closest codebook entry, effectively mapping continuous representations to discrete brain-state tokens. The codebook is initialized with K-means on the first batch and then updated continuously through exponential moving averages. Quantization is performed using a straight-through estimator so that gradients can be propagated during training. A commitment loss encourages latent vectors to remain close to their assigned codebook entries and prevents instability in the learned representation space. The decoder maps each token back into the graph domain and reconstructs the corresponding phase-locking matrix. The full training objective combines reconstruction quality with the vector-quantization loss, ensuring that both the embedding space and the codebook evolve in a stable and meaningful manner.

Normative modeling. As shown in Fig. 1c, we further applied normative modeling2,3 to the resulting discrete tokens to quantify dynamic brain organization through fractional occupancy and dwell time. Normative models were estimated using Generalized Additive Models for Location, Scale and Shape (GAMLSS), with fractional occupancy and dwell time as the response variables, smooth functions of age and sex as predictors, and scanning site included as a random effect. Subject-level normative deviations, expressed as z-scores, were derived from the fitted distributions. Extreme deviations were defined as absolute z-scores greater than 1.96, which correspond to abnormally increased or decreased expression of specific brain-state tokens relative to the reference population.

Results

In this study, NeuroLex quantizes brain dynamics across populations into 12 brain-state tokens. Fig 2a shows the corresponding phase-lock matrices and global phase-locking strength (absolute summation of the connectivity) of these tokens. The number of brain state tokes are decided by Silhouette score, adjusted ranking index (ARI), and R2 consistency of the embeddings across runs, whereas is shown in Fig 2b, the overall performances perform the best when token number is 12. Tokens organize into three modes: whole-brain synchrony (BST1–BST4, progressively reduced global coupling strength), single-network decoupling (BST5–BST9), and hybrid transitional states (BST10–BST12).

Brain-state characterization. To evaluate the quality of the brain state representations, we assessed clustering performance (Silhouette score) and reliability (half-split correlation of FO across tokens) on the test set. Across the ten largest test sites (Fig. 2c), NeuroLex consistently achieved the highest Silhouette scores (0.48 avg.), indicating that the derived brain-state clusters were more compact and better separated. Half-split reliability analysis further demonstrated that NeuroLex produced the strongest cross-split consistency (r = 0.81, Fig. 2b). EiDA exhibited comparable reliability but weaker clustering, while LEiDA showed stronger clustering with reduced reliability, suggesting instability of eigenvector-based clusters in heterogeneous datasets. These results indicate that NeuroLex yields more robust and generalizable brain-state tokens, enabling reliable population-level comparisons at scale.

Lifespan trajectories and clinical heterogeneity. Based on these tokens, we further constructed normative models of FO and DT to characterize lifespan trajectories and subsequently referenced multiple clinical cohorts against these norms to identify condition-specific alterations in temporal brain dynamics. Normative models fitting FO and DT revealed systematic age-related changes in several tokens (Fig. 2d-e), where the most significant three brain state tokens in the first two modes with aging (P < 0.05) are displayed. The globally synchronized state (BST1) and visual-network decoupling (BST5) both declined with age, consistent with reduced large-scale integration and sensory-driven dynamics across adulthood. In contrast, limbic decoupling (BST7) increased after age 20. Age-related changes in dwell time were generally modest. Notable patterns included clearer shifts in BST2 and BST9 before age 20, and a nonlinear trajectory in BST7, which showed an initial decline followed by a later increase.

Based on the derived norms and lifespan trajectories, we applied the normative models to five clinical cohorts to quantify z-score deviations in FO and DT. At the individual level, the model provides a way to identify which brain-state tokens show atypical engagement. As illustrated in Fig. 2f, a healthy control individual shows only minor deviations across both FO and DT, whereas an SCZ individual exhibits a high positive deviation in BST1 and extreme negative deviations in other states. These individualized deviation profiles allow personal quantification of atypical brain dynamics. We further examined group-level differences using both z-score and extreme deviations (|z| > 1.96). To assess which features most systematically contribute to disorder-related variation, we performed a CCA analysis on FO and DT across 12 brain state tokens. The results reveal that ANX and ADHD show strong deviation biases in DT, indicating shortened and less stable engagement of multiple states; in contrast, MDD displays prominent alterations in FO, reflecting shifts in the proportional occupancy of specific states. These patterns are consistent with the notion that anxiety- and attention-related disorders may involve increased instability of temporal dynamics, whereas depressive disorders may strongly affect the overall distribution and balance of state engagement4,5.

Conclusion

In this study, we introduced NeuroLex for brain state quantization and used normative modeling to characterize how large-scale brain dynamics vary across the lifespan and across five clinical cohorts. Our experiments demonstrate that the method provides a stable and robust approach for identifying discrete brain states, with superior performance in both clustering structure and test-retest reliability. Through normative modeling, we revealed lifespan patterns in dynamic engagement, where fractional occupancy showed clear age-related variation while dwell time displayed more modest changes. By comparing clinical cohorts to the normative reference, we further uncovered heterogeneous alterations across disorders. Anxiety and ADHD cohorts showed shortened dwell times relative to age-matched norms, while the MDD cohort exhibited more pronounced changes in the organization of brain states. Overall, NeuroLex combined with normative modeling offers a scalable and population-level framework for analyzing brain dynamics. The approach provides a unified token space for charting typical developmental trajectories and for quantifying disorder-specific deviations in large and diverse cohorts.
