Introduction

Emotion plays a central role in perception and memory, and emotionally salient events are typically encoded more vividly and remembered more accurately. Yet it remains unclear whether the emotional response experienced during recall resembles the affective state originally evoked during perception. Emotional reactions at encoding may be reconstructed or altered in memory, making it essential to examine how similar the neural representation of emotion is across perception and recall.

fMRI studies show that viewing and recalling the same event can elicit similar but transformed neural patterns in high-level associative regions1, suggesting that memory involves reconstruction rather than simple replay. Because emotional evaluation and regulation rely on prefrontal systems—including dorsolateral, ventrolateral, and ventromedial/orbitofrontal areas—the prefrontal cortex is a key region for tracking affective states across time2.

Most previous work relies on fMRI, but its physical constraints limit ecological validity, especially when measuring naturalistic emotion and spoken recall. Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) offers a more flexible alternative: it tolerates movement and speech, allows testing in realistic environments, and provides quiet, portable measurement suitable for emotionally engaging tasks3.

The present study uses fNIRS to investigate whether emotional experiences during viewing of positive and negative video stimuli are neurally reinstated during recall. We focus on prefrontal activity, predicting differences between positive and negative affect as well as lateralization effects. By examining within-participant similarity across viewing and recall, and between-participant similarity during recall of the same stimuli, we aim to clarify how emotional memories are preserved or transformed under naturalistic conditions.

Methods

Participants

Thirty-six individuals participated (7 male, 29 female). Due to acquisition issues, four datasets were removed, leaving 32 participants (6 male, 26 female; ages 19–26). 

Stimuli and apparatus

A pilot study (N = 7) was conducted to select video stimuli. Thirty-eight YouTube videos were rated on a 7-point scale across 15 emotion adjectives. For each emotional condition, the three videos with the highest mean scores (e.g., average of “sad,” “anxious,” “gloomy” for negative/low arousal) were selected. In the main experiment, participants viewed three short clips per condition (29–79 s).

Brain activity was measured with NIRSIT LITE (OBELAB Inc.), a validated portable fNIRS device. The system includes 15 prefrontal channels. Signals were converted to oxyhemoglobin using MBLL and filtered to reduce low-frequency physiological noise, and motion was corrected via the built-in IMU. Channels were mapped to IFG, MFG, dlPFC, and mPFC using MNI coordinates and the AAL atlas, covering regions linked to emotional evaluation, inhibition, working memory, and autobiographical processing.

 Procedure 

Participants were asked to avoid alcohol and excessive forehead makeup. Upon arrival, they provided consent and were tested in a controlled environment with minimized ambient light. Sensors were carefully attached, with hair parted and skin cleaned when necessary.

A block design was used. The experiment consisted of a watching session and a recall session. In the watching session, 12 videos (4 conditions × 3 clips) were shown in pseudorandomized order. After a short break (~3 min), the recall session followed. Each trial began with a still frame cue, and participants verbally recalled the scene for 1 minute. Natural movement was allowed but minimized to preserve signal quality. The entire experiment lasted ~50 minutes.

Preprocessing and Model Specification

fNIRS data were preprocessed by removing channels with poor or unstable signals and interpolating brief invalid segments. Remaining signals were converted to optical density, corrected for motion, and transformed into hemoglobin concentrations. Missing channels were replaced using neighboring-channel averages. A GLM was then applied to estimate condition-specific hemodynamic responses. Each condition was modeled with its own regressor, and noise was reduced using motion and short-separation signals as nuisance terms. Models were fit separately for HbO, HbR, and HbT, and contrast estimates were averaged within ROIs. Trial-level activation patterns were standardized for multivariate analyses.

Statistical analyses

To visualize representational structure across the 24 trials, a participant-level 24×24 correlation matrix was computed from activation patterns and averaged across participants. Classical five-dimensional MDS was applied, followed by Procrustes rotation to align the configuration with theoretical contrasts related to modality-general and modality-specific affect4.

Support vector machines were used to classify valence, arousal, and emotion category. Cross-modal classification used leave-one-subject-out cross-validation, trained on one session (e.g., watching) and tested on the other (e.g., recall). Successful decoding indicates modality-general emotional representations shared between perception and memory. 

Results

MDS

Affective video stimuli from both the watching and recall sessions were represented within a five-dimensional solution derived from MDS (Fig. 1). Pearson correlation analyses were conducted to examine the correspondence between each dimension of the Procrustes-rotated MDS solution and the theoretical design matrix.

The first dimension, corresponding to modality, was significant (r = .945, p < .001), indicating substantial differences in fNIRS activation patterns between the watching and recall sessions. The second dimension, reflecting modality-general valence, was also significant (r = .424, p = .039), suggesting a consistent distinction between positive and negative videos across both sessions. The third dimension, representing modality-general arousal, was not significant (r = .198, p = .354), indicating unclear separation between high- and low-arousal stimuli independent of modality. The fourth dimension, associated with modality-specific valence, was significant (r = .78, p < .001), suggesting that the representation of valence differed systematically between watching and recall. Finally, the fifth dimension, reflecting modality-specific arousal, was not significant (r = .331, p = .114), indicating that arousal-related representations were not modality general nor modality specific.

These results suggest that while core affective valence is consistently encoded across perceptual and mnemonic experiences, there also exist modality-specific representations, indicating partial transformation of emotional information between watching and recall.

Classifications

Cross-participant cross-modal classification analyses were conducted to determine whether affective properties of the video stimuli could be significantly predicted from fNIRS data within and across the watching and recall sessions. In the cross-modal classification, where training and testing were performed across different modalities (i.e., watching vs. recall), significant decoding was observed for valence (M=.534, p=.028) and emotion categories (M=.296, p=.002), but not significant for arousal (M=.501, p=.457). The significant cross-modal classification of valence and emotion categories suggests that these affective representations are preserved across perceptual and mnemonic states, supporting the presence of modality-general encoding. 

Conclusion

This study examined how emotional experiences are represented in the brain during both perception and recall of affective video stimuli. Using fNIRS in a naturalistic setting, we tested whether valence and arousal could be decoded from prefrontal activation patterns and whether these representations generalize across watching and recalling. Through MDS and decoding analyses, we assessed the stability of affective representations across individuals and across perceptual versus mnemonic states. Overall, the findings indicate that emotional memory involves both shared and distinct prefrontal representational structures.

MDS revealed significant modality-general and modality-specific structures for valence but not for arousal. Although modality-general structure suggests shared emotional coding across perception and recall, modality-specific structure indicates representational differences across the two tasks. Rather than a contradiction, this pattern aligns with the idea that emotional memory includes both an invariant core and a context-dependent component. Prior work similarly shows that perception and recall share high-level representational patterns while undergoing systematic transformations during memory reconstruction1. A parsimonious interpretation is a hierarchical architecture: a modality-general valence code that preserves affective gist, followed by modality-specific adjustments during recall.

MDS and decoding also offered complementary insights. Valence showed both shared and specific representational organization, whereas arousal lacked consistent structure. Classification succeeded only for modality-general valence, further supporting the view that valence is encoded with greater cross-modal stability than arousal. MDS captured similarity geometry across stimuli, revealing nuanced structure, while decoding tested discriminability at the trial level. The convergence of these methods suggests that emotional memory contains a stable cross-modal valence signal embedded within a richer, context-sensitive representational space.

Region-level analysis supported this interpretation. Prefrontal subregions measured in this study—including IFG, MFG, dlPFC, and mPFC—showed patterns consistent with roles in emotional evaluation, attention, and autobiographical recall. These findings complement models proposing that emotional representations are distributed across cortical hubs that collectively track affective meaning5.

In sum, this study identifies a cross-modal core of emotional valence that remains stable across perception and recall, alongside modality-specific refinements that shape the memory reconstruction process. These findings advance a model in which emotional memory reflects both preserved affective gist and flexible task-dependent elaboration.
