Introduction

People with Alzheimer’s disease (AD) may experience neuropsychiatric alterations leading to worse cognitive and functional decline and greater caregivers’ burden. Efficient management of these symptoms, therefore, is of primary importance. Neurotransmitter dysfunctions have been implicated in different mental health conditions, including behavioural alterations in AD.1,2 To date, however, most neuroimaging studies on neuropsychiatric symptoms in AD have not assessed the role of neurotransmitters. The aim of this study, therefore, was to investigate the neurotransmitter-related functional connectivity (FC) alterations underpinning mood symptoms in AD.

Methods

The sample recruited for this study included 160 older adults in three groups: 55 cognitively unimpaired (CU; age = 67.1±9.7) and 75 patients with prodromal to mild AD with (PT-PS; age = 70.3±9.2) and 30 patients without neuropsychiatric symptoms (PT-NP; age = 69.0±8.1). All participants completed a comprehensive neuropsychological, neuropsychiatric (i.e., NPI, GAD-7 and PHQ-9) and MRI assessment. Resting-state functional MRI scans were pre-processed with CONN (steps included: realignment, slice-timing, motion-correction, denoising, band filtering and smoothing) and, subsequently, with the REACT pipeline3 to extract 13 whole-brain FC maps weighted by the distribution of receptors/transporters of the following neurotransmitters: serotonin (5HT1a, 5HT1b, 5HT2a, 5HT4, 5HT6, 5HTT) , dopamine (D1, D2, DAT), noradrenaline (NAT) and acetylcholine (M1, α4β2, VAChT). Voxel-based analyses were carried out in SPM12 to compare FC maps across the three groups (t-tests) and investigate associations between severity of global and affective neuropsychiatric symptoms and FC alterations (multiple regression). Confirmatory analyses were carried out by comparing FC maps of depressed and anxious patients (based on NPI results) with PT-NP participants. A cluster-level FWE-corrected p-value of 0.05 was used as significance threshold.

Results

The PT-PS had lower FC associated with the 5HT2a receptor than the PT-NP group in the right middle temporal gyrus. No other FC alterations were found. In the PT-PS group, global NPI and GAD-7 scores were positively associated with 5HT1b-, 5HT2a- and 5HT4-related FC in left occipito-temporal regions and in the hypothalamus. In the whole patient sample, a negative association also emerged between severity of depressive symptoms and both 5HT4- and 5HTT-related FC in the right inferior and middle temporal gyri. In confirmatory analyses, anxiety was associated with stronger fronto-parietal and weaker occipital FC associated with several serotonin receptors, while depression was associated with higher 5HT1b- and 5HT2a-relaed FC in the left superior frontal gyrus.

Conclusion

Both anxiety and depression in AD were associated with functional alterations influenced by the serotoninergic system, although with distinct patterns, in line with the serotoninergic hypothesis of mood disorders.4 Both self-reported and caregiver-reported affective symptoms were associated with serotonin-related FC. However, different methods of assessment led to the identification of distinct patterns of alterations. Pharmacological interventions targeting the serotoninergic system may offer a strategy for managing affective dysregulation in AD. Further research is needed to determine the most effective receptor targets also by means of sensitive neuroimaging outcome measures, such as neurotransmitter-related FC.5
