Introduction

White matter degradation is common in aging and Alzheimer’s disease (AD), yet its contribution to preserved or exceptional late-life cognition remains poorly understood. Prior work on “successful aging” and “super-aging” has focused primarily on macrostructural cortical features, but associations between regional thickness or volume and cognition are generally modest and often inconsistent, particularly in the oldest adults. Emerging evidence has suggested that later-life cognitive performance may be more strongly related to white matter integrity than to cortical macrostructure.1 Preliminary analyses from our group in the same large cohort examined here indicate that greater cortical thickness in primary somatomotor and sensory regions is associated with better cognitive performance, but that these relationships weaken with advancing age, consistent with the hypothesis that white matter integrity plays an increasingly important role in cognitive resilience. The present study investigates how WM integrity supports superior cognition from midlife into late life, specifically, whether superior cognitive functioning is supported by resistance to WM decline, compensatory changes, or absence of preclinical AD-related WM patterns.

Methods

Cross-sectional diffusion-weighted MRI data from 1,127 participants (aged 36–90+; 57.5% female) in the Human Connectome Project in Aging (HCP-A)/Adult Aging Brain Connectome (AABC) cohort were analyzed. All participants were cognitively healthy at baseline and were assessed at four acquisition sites using matched neuroimaging and characterization protocols.2,3 Participants were grouped into three age strata: midlife, young-old, and older adult. Three cognitive factor scores representing memory, fluid intelligence (FIQ), and crystallized intelligence (CIQ) were adjusted for age and sex and used to classify participants into high-, middle-, and low-performance groups (top 25%, middle 50%, bottom 25%). Tract-based spatial statistics (TBSS) was used to generate voxel-wise fractional anisotropy (FA) maps. Regional FA values were then extracted for statistical analysis using regions of interest defined by the Johns Hopkins University white matter atlas and registered to the MNI152 average T1 brain.

Results

Higher cognitive performance was associated with greater FA across the adult lifespan, with cognitive domain–specific patterns. Memory-related FA differences between high- and low-performers increased with age, whereas FIQ/CIQ associations weakened in later life (Figure 1). Across all ages, high performers showed elevated FA in rostral medial frontal and superior parietal white matter (Cohen d=0.44, p<.01), thalamic radiation (Cohen d=0.30, p<.01), and anterior limb of internal capsule (Cohen d=0.26, p<.01). Memory and FIQ were also linked with higher FA in corpus callosum and hippocampal regions. Among the older adults (80+), memory differences between high and low performers were driven primarily by more preserved white matter in frontal and parietal lobes, the corpus callosum, and periventricular regions in the high-performing group relative to typical performers (middle 50%). For FIQ and CIQ, both high and low performers diverged from typical performers, but with largely nonoverlapping spatial patterns.

Conclusion

White matter integrity is robustly associated with cognitive resilience, yet the spatial patterns vary by domain and age. In contrast to the AD-vulnerable tracts typically emphasized in disease-focused work, resilience-related white matter in our sample extends into broader sensorimotor and interhemispheric pathways, although tracts commonly affected in AD are also implicated. Strengthening memory/FIQ-white matter links in the periventricular regions in older age may suggest compensatory mechanisms, while CIQ-white matter links remain relatively stable over the adult lifespan. These cross-sectional results must be interpreted with caution as cognitive and neural trajectories are likely more informative to capture baseline differences and track neurocognitive change. Future longitudinal analysis in the HCP-A/AABC cohort will clarify whether white matter integrity predicts sustained resilience.
