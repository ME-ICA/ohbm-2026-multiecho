Introduction

Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD) is a neuropsychiatric condition characterized by obsessions and/or compulsions that significantly affect the quality of life of those who suffer from it. Obsessions refer to recurrent and persistent thoughts, impulses, or images that are intrusive; while compulsions correspond to repetitive behaviours or mental acts carried out in response to obsessions. This symptomatology is often time-consuming and causes clinically significant distress and impairment in various areas of the individual's life.

OCD is a serious disorder due to several factors, including the complexity of its symptoms, which often overlap with other clinical disorders, and the difficulty to establishing its treatment. Furthermore, symptom remission is infrequent. Therefore, advances in understanding its pathophysiology could improve diagnosis and clinical care, as well as the development of neurobiological models to explain it. However, early research in this field, using neuroimaging techniques, focused on studying corticostriatal regions and gray matter abnormalities, while the study of white matter tracts received less attention, and the resulting findings have been contradictory. Furthermore, most research has relied on diffusion tensor imaging, a technique suitable for studying large white matter tracts but limited in mapping complex fiber configurations, such as the presence of crossed fibers within a single voxel. Alternative analyses have emerged to address the limitations of diffusion tensor imaging, such as those based on spherical deconvolution, which offer better anatomical correspondence. Using these analyses in a complementary way would allow for the identification of distinct white matter characteristics relevant to the analysis of this psychopathology.

The objective of the research was to compare the white matter of participants with OCD to that of control individuals using two Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) analysis techniques:TBSS and FBA.

Methods

The research data were collected at the Magnetic Resonance Imaging Unit of the National Autonomous University of Mexico in collaboration with the Querétaro State Mental Health Center. The sample consisted of 28 control participants and 33 participants with OCD.

18 of the participants with OCD were women, with a mean age of 30.39 years (SD: 8.74), a mean age of symptom onset of 14.47 years (SD: 5.70), and a mean duration of symptoms of 15.27 years (SD: 8.4).

Most participants with OCD presented cleaning (7) and checking (10) compulsions, accompanied by contamination and aggression obsessions, respectively.

On the other hand, 20 of the control participants were women, with a mean age of 30.29 years (SD: 8.46).

Participant assessment included, among others, the Obsessive-Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS) to evaluate OCD symptoms, the Hamilton Anxiety and Depression Scales, the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS-IV), and the MINI International Neuropsychiatric Interview.

MRI Data Acquisition

A 3.0 Tesla MRI scanner with a 32-channel cranial coil, model Discovery MR750, manufactured by General Electric, was used for MRI acquisition. A sequence with a 128 x 128 mm matrix, a 25.6 cm FOV, and a voxel size of 1 x 1 x 4 mm was used. Sixty directions were acquired with a b = 1000 s/mm² and 3 volumes b=0, with a Repetition Time (RT) of 7000 ms and an Echo Time (TE) of 60 ms. 

Analysis of Data

Tract-Based Spatial Statistics (TBSS) analysis included preprocessing (denoising and eddy current correction), tensor fitting, brain extraction, and four processing steps for removing potential outliers from the tensor fit, nonlinear registration, fusion and creation of a fractional anisotropy skeleton, and statistical calculation. Threshold-Free Cluster Enhancement (TFCE) with multiple comparisons correction (Family-Wise Error, 5000 permutations) and a 99% confidence level (p < 0.01) was used to calculate Fractional Anisotropy (FA), Axial Diffusivity (AD), radial diffusivity (RD), and Mean Diffusivity (MD). Data were analyzed using FSL software.

The Fixel-Based Analysis (FBA) included preprocessing (denoising, eddy current correction, bias field correction, and global intensity normalization), as well as 17 additional analysis steps. These included estimating the fiber orientation distribution, creating a study template, calculating the response function, estimating fixels, and calculating metrics and statistics. Connectivity-based Fixel Enhancement (CFE) and non-parametric permutation tests (5000 permutations) were used, with a 95% confidence level (p<0.05), to calculate fibre density (FD), fibre cross-section (FC), and combined effect (FDC). Data were analyzed using Mrtrix software.

Results

TBSS

Higher levels of FA were found in the left hemisphere white matter of participants with OCD compared to control participants, considering a 99% confidence level (p<0.01).

Furthermore, lower levels of AD, RD, and MD were generally found in the white matter of participants with OCD compared to control participants; these differences were statistically significant, considering a 99% confidence level (p<0.01).

FBA

Greater FD was found in the cerebellum (superior and inferior semilunar regions) of control participants compared to participants with OCD.

In contrast, greater FD was found in participants with OCD in the thalamus, anterior cingulate cortex, cerebellar peduncle, corticospinal tract, and caudate nucleus; greater FC in the arcuate and temporo-occipital fasciculi, corona radiata, corticospinal tract, and midbrain; and greater FDC in the thalamus, corticospinal tract, arcuate fasciculus, parahippocampal region, cerebellar peduncle, and corona radiata.

Conclusion

Both methodologies revealed changes in the white matter of the thalamus, cingulate cortex, cerebellum, hippocampal areas, and corticospinal tract in participants with OCD, compared to control participants.

The findings of the FA associated with the laterality of white matter alterations in participants with OCD have been previously reported in other studies [1,2,3] and have been attributed to various factors, including compensatory mechanisms and the presence of comorbidities.

However, the findings corresponding to AD, RD, and MD contradict what has been previously reported in other studies. This may be due to various factors related to the characteristics of the sample of individuals used, including the influence of the various therapeutic interventions the participants have undergone throughout the history of their symptoms, the use of various medications and different psychological therapies, and the age of onset of symptoms. Several of the results agree with what has been previously found in pediatric participants with obsessive-compulsive symptoms [4]. In fact, it is important to highlight that the reported mean age of onset is 19.5 years; however, the mean age of onset of symptoms in the participants of this study was approximately 14 years.

Regarding the results obtained using FBA, there is a scarcity of research that uses this analysis technique as a tool for studying the white matter of individuals with OCD. In a study by [5], a greater density and cross-sectional area of fibers were found in participants with OCD, with slightly higher credibility in the subgroup of participants with early-onset OCD. This strengthens the hypothesis regarding the influence of age of symptom onset on the findings of this study.

In conclusion, the effects reported in this research could be associated with the age of symptom onset in the participants. Regarding the complementary use of both analysis techniques, the calculated metrics provided a broader perspective on the pathophysiology of the disorder. This leads to the conclusion, along with other research, that the changes in white matter found could be associated with an increase in neuronal activity and/or processing speed, which would ultimately also be reflected in changes in white matter anatomy.
