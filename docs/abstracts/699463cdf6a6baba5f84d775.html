Introduction

Intermittent theta burst stimulation (iTBS) of the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) has emerged as an effective treatment for depression. Interleaved TMS-fMRI enables direct observation of immediate neural effects during stimulation, yet standard analysis approaches assume uniform BOLD responses across the stimulation session. Recent work demonstrated feasibility of full clinical iTBS protocols in an interleaved TMS-fMRI paradigm.¹ Preliminary observations suggested cumulative build-up of activation at the stimulated DLPFC across trial blocks, raising questions about temporal non-uniformity of iTBS effects. Understanding these temporal dynamics is crucial for interpreting TMS-fMRI findings and optimizing treatment protocols and spacing. Here, we applied a trial-wise generalized linear model (GLM) approach to extract BOLD responses during iTBS, examining whether neural responses remain stable or show systematic changes across a single stimulation session. 

Methods

Eighteen healthy right-handed adults (8 females, ages 21-36 years) completed three within-subject interleaved TMS-fMRI sessions: left DLPFC stimulation at 80% resting motor threshold (RMT), left DLPFC at 40% RMT, and left primary motor cortex (M1) at 80% RMT. Sessions were conducted at least one week apart using a 3T Siemens Prisma scanner with an integrated TMS-fMRI setup comprising two 7-channel surface RF coils and an MR-compatible TMS system (MagVenture). Each session delivered standard clinical iTBS (600 pulses over 190 seconds: 50 Hz triplets repeated at 5 Hz for 2 seconds, followed by 8-second inter-train intervals, 20 trains total). Functional images were acquired continuously using EPI sequences (TR=2000ms, TE=30ms). The left DLPFC target was determined using MNI coordinates (-38, 44, 26) with neuronavigation (Localite). We applied least-squares-all beta series analysis to extract trial-by-trial BOLD responses, treating each of the 20 iTBS trains as individual regressors. ROI analyses examined five pre-specified regions: left DLPFC (stimulation target), right DLPFC, left M1, right M1, and left subgenual anterior cingulate cortex (sgACC). Statistical comparisons between early and late responses used paired t-tests with false discovery rate correction across ROIs.

Results

Trial-by-trial analysis revealed significant temporal variation of effects during iTBS, with effects most pronounced at network-connected rather than directly stimulated regions. During left DLPFC stimulation at 80% RMT, the DLPFC showed significant variance between early and late stimulation periods (Δ=-0.53, p=0.014 FDR-corrected). During M1 stimulation at 80% RMT, BOLD responses in the left M1 ROI declined similarly (Δ=-0.58, p=0.026 FDR-corrected), exhibiting a linear trend across the session (r=-0.97, p=0.005). Seed-based connectivity analysis of concatenated rest periods between iTBS trains revealed condition-specific network patterns, with DLPFC 80% showing connectivity increases to bilateral prefrontal regions and sgACC. Temporal windowing (early, middle, late phases) demonstrated that significant connectivity changes from the DLPFC seed emerged mainly during the middle phase of stimulation. 

Preliminary analysis of pre-to-post resting-state connectivity changes showed that DLPFC 80% stimulation produced a reduction in DLPFC-sgACC connectivity (Δ=-0.16, p=0.030), which correlated with immediate online sgACC network engagement during stimulation (r=0.50, p=0.035)

Conclusion

Our analysis reveals significant temporal non-uniformity in BOLD responses during clinical iTBS protocols, with the strongest effects observed at network-connected regions rather than direct stimulation targets and a significant temporal variation in local stimulation response that might show signs of habituation commonly seen in motor evoked potential studies and repeated sensory stimulation paradigms.5 This pattern is consistent with neurophysiological habituation mechanisms, where repeated stimulation leads to progressively diminished neural responses. 

If BOLD responses habituate substantially within brief sessions, this raises important questions about accelerated protocols delivering multiple sessions per day. The correlation between immediate network engagement and subsequent connectivity reorganization suggests that early-session network responses may be particularly relevant for understanding therapeutic mechanisms. Understanding the neurophysiological basis of habituation may inform optimal inter-session intervals. Limitations include the relatively modest sample size, healthy participant sample and single iTBS protocol. Future studies should examine whether habituation patterns differ in clinical populations and across stimulation parameters. In conclusion, trial-by-trial TMS-fMRI analysis provides insights into iTBS temporal mechanisms not visible with conventional approaches.
