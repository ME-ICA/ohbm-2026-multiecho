Introduction

Tinnitus is increasingly recognized as a disorder of large-scale neural organization rather than a purely auditory condition. Structural and functional MRI studies indicate that the chronic phantom percept emerges from abnormal interactions between auditory, limbic, and attentional networks. Early voxel-based morphometry (VBM) findings demonstrated gray matter alterations in Heschl’s gyrus and superior temporal cortex, alongside changes in the anterior cingulate cortex, hippocampus, and amygdala—regions mediating affective and mnemonic processing 1. These findings support the view that tinnitus arises from maladaptive auditory–limbic coupling and impaired top-down inhibition 2. Beyond the auditory system, surface-based morphometry (SBM) studies have reported dissociable effects on cortical thickness and surface area across orbitofrontal, occipital, and parietal regions, suggesting widespread cortical reorganisation 3. Subcortical analyses further implicate the thalamus, basal ganglia, and limbic nuclei, highlighting the contribution of sensory gating and emotional regulation mechanisms 4. Yet, it remains unclear how these distributed alterations align with the brain’s macroscale organization and hierarchical connectivity structure.

In this study, we leveraged a large cohort to combine VBM, SBM, subcortical region-of-interest, and gradient-based analyses, enabling a comprehensive examination of tinnitus-related structural reorganization across multiple spatial scales. We identify both shared and individual-specific deviations in cortical and subcortical morphology, which systematically align with principal cortical gradients. These results indicate that tinnitus involves hierarchical reorganization along sensory-to-transmodal axes and underscore the importance of individualized, network-level models for understanding its pathophysiology.

Methods

We analyzed structural MRI data from five independent cohorts, including 153 participants with chronic tinnitus and 133 matched controls. All participants provided informed consent, and each study was approved by the respective local ethics committee. Groups were matched for age, sex, hearing thresholds, and acquisition site using propensity-score matching. T1-weighted images were acquired on 1.5T and 3T scanners (Philips, Siemens, GE) using standard 3D MPRAGE or TFE sequences with 1 mm isotropic resolution. Structural MRI data were processed using complementary voxel-based and surface-based morphometric pipelines. For VBM, T1-weighted images were brain-extracted, segmented into gray matter, nonlinearly registered to MNI152 space, modulated, and smoothed (σ = 3 mm). Group comparisons were performed using general linear models with permutation-based nonparametric testing and threshold-free cluster enhancement. Age, sex, total intracranial volume (TIV), hearing thresholds (PTA), and scanner site were included as covariates. Analyses of subcortical regions were conducted to specifically target structures previously implicated in tinnitus. Surface-based morphometry (SBM) was conducted with FreeSurfer (v8.0). Cortical surfaces were reconstructed and registered to the fsaverage template. Cortical thickness and surface area maps were smoothed (10 mm FWHM) and analyzed using vertex-wise GLMs controlling for age, sex, TIV, PTA, and scanning site. Cluster-wise Monte Carlo correction (10,000 iterations; p < 0.05) was applied. Subcortical region-of-interest (ROI) analyses used FreeSurfer’s probabilistic segmentations of hippocampal, amygdalar, thalamic, and brainstem nuclei based on ultra-high-resolution atlases. Group comparisons were performed using GLMs with identical covariates and corrected across ROIs. Macro-scale cortical organisation was assessed using the Morphometric Inverse Divergence (MIND) framework 5 to derive subject-specific structural similarity matrices from five morphometric features (thickness, area, volume, curvature, sulcal depth) parcellated with the 1000-region Schaefer atlas. Matrices were z-transformed and thresholded to retain the top 10% of connections per region. Diffusion map embedding (α = 0.5) identified principal gradients of cortical organization. Individual gradients were aligned to a group template via Procrustes rotation, and deviations from the control template quantified interindividual variability. Multidimensional scaling summarized between-subject differences in gradient topology.

Results

Voxel- and surface-based morphometry revealed widespread but anatomically dissociable gray-matter (GM) alterations in chronic tinnitus. VBM analyses showed reduced GM volume in the subcallosal region (Fig. 1a, upper panel) and increased bilateral putaminal volume in the tinnitus group (Fig. 1a, lower panel), consistent with impaired limbic–auditory gating and compensatory striatal plasticity. SBM demonstrated cortical thinning in dorsolateral prefrontal, posterior parietal, and occipital cortices (Fig. 1b, upper panel), accompanied by focal increases in thickness within the right insula and superior temporal cortex (Fig. 1b, lower panel). Surface-area differences were observed primarily in orbitofrontal, anterior cingulate, and temporal regions, indicating heterogeneous cortical remodeling across sensory-association and salience networks.

Subcortical analyses identified significant group differences within several amygdalar nuclei (accessory basal, paralaminar, central–cortical, and lateral), with tinnitus participants showing larger volumes relative to controls. Additional increases were detected in left thalamic lateral posterior and pulvinar nuclei. These findings suggest that subcortical structures implicated in emotional salience, multisensory integration, and sensory gating play a more prominent role in tinnitus pathophysiology than previously recognized. Although prior studies have reported macroscopic alterations in limbic regions such as the amygdala, hippocampus, and anterior cingulate cortex, this is, to our knowledge, the first report of structural differences specifically within the accessory basal amygdala—a subnucleus involved in evaluating the emotional significance of sensory input and modulating attention to aversive stimuli. Its engagement is consistent with the heightened distress, hypervigilance, and affective responses commonly associated with tinnitus. Similarly, the observed volumetric increases in the pulvinar and lateral posterior thalamic nuclei represent novel findings. These higher-order thalamic structures mediate thalamo-cortical communication, multisensory integration, and allocation of attention to salient stimuli. Dysfunction within these nuclei may contribute to persistent awareness of the tinnitus percept by failing to filter behaviorally irrelevant sensations. Together, these results support models positing that tinnitus reflects disruptions in cortico-limbic-thalamic circuits, including the “noise-cancellation” hypothesis, which attributes phantom auditory perception to impaired limbic gating of aberrant auditory signals.

At the macroscale level, diffusion-map embedding of MIND-derived structural similarity networks yielded three principal gradients explaining 69% of variance in cortical organization. Gradient 1, spanning the sensory-to-transmodal axis, revealed a clearer separation between the auditory network and the default mode network (DMN) in tinnitus compared to controls, suggesting altered large-scale hierarchical organization (Fig. 2a). Normative modeling of individual gradients showed marked heterogeneity among tinnitus participants, with many individuals exhibiting substantial deviations from the normative distribution. Despite this variability, consistent group-level differences emerged, with tinnitus participants forming a partially separable cluster from controls while still displaying wide dispersion across the gradient space (Fig. 2b–d). These subject-specific alterations highlight the heterogeneous neuroplastic responses associated with tinnitus and underscore the importance of individualized, network-level analyses for characterizing its biological diversity.

Conclusion

Our multimodal morphometric and gradient analyses reveal converging evidence for large-scale cortical and subcortical reorganization in chronic tinnitus, characterized by reduced subcallosal and increased putaminal volume reflecting impaired limbic–striatal gating, cortical thickness alterations in prefrontal, insular, and temporal regions indicative of compensatory sensory–emotional plasticity, and enlargement of amygdalar and thalamic nuclei pointing to abnormal salience and attentional modulation. At the network level, marked individual deviations from normative structure, underscore both shared and highly heterogeneous organizational patterns among tinnitus patients. A major strength of this work lies in applying normative modeling to individual gradient profiles, which revealed distinct neuroanatomical subgroups that would remain obscured in traditional group-based analyses; likewise, the use of MIND-derived structural similarity networks provides a novel perspective on cortical organization beyond conventional structural or functional connectivity. Nevertheless, several limitations warrant consideration: the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, the multi-cohort sample—though large for tinnitus research—may not capture the full spectrum of heterogeneity, and MIND gradients require further validation against functional and diffusion-based measures. Future longitudinal and multimodal studies integrating structure, function, and clinical phenotype will be essential to clarify the temporal dynamics and mechanistic role of these gradients. Overall, our findings suggest that tinnitus arises from distributed disruptions across sensory, limbic, and associative systems rather than a single auditory locus, and they highlight the promise of individualized, gradient-informed neuroimaging frameworks for developing precise biomarkers and guiding personalized interventions.
