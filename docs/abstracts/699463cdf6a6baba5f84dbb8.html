Introduction

Accurate mapping of long-range axonal pathways is essential for understanding primate brain organization and for validating structural connectivity estimates derived from diffusion MRI (dMRI).¹ Anatomic tracer studies in non-human primates (NHPs) provide the gold-standard representation of fiber trajectories, offering cellular-level detail unmatched by any in vivo method and crucial for assessing the accuracy of human dMRI tractography.² However, reconstructing fiber pathways from tracer histology requires labor-intensive manual annotation, severely limiting scalability and hindering the construction of connectivity atlases. 

Machine-learning–based segmentation offers a promising route toward automation,³ but progress has been constrained by the scarcity of annotated tracer images and variability in tracer appearance across brains, injection sites, and histological preparations. Synthetic training data have improved robustness and generalization in related biomedical imaging tasks,⁴,⁵ suggesting a way to mitigate limited real annotations. Domain randomization addresses this challenge by generating training data that span a broad range of appearance, geometry, and noise characteristics, encouraging models to learn features that generalize beyond specific datasets. Despite their success elsewhere, such approaches have not yet been applied to tracer injection histology. 

Here, we present a synthetic-data–augmented framework for automated fiber bundle segmentation in macaque anatomic tracing data. Using dMRI tractography as a generative prior, we create synthetic image patches that capture realistic local fiber texture while avoiding the encoding of global anatomical connectivity, which dMRI cannot reliably provide. Integrating these synthetic patches with a small number of annotated sections aims to improve fiber bundle detection and enable scalable, reproducible analysis of tracer datasets for dMRI validation. 

Methods

Real Histological Data. We used high-resolution coronal histological sections from macaque brains with bidirectional tracer injections at multiple cortical sites. Sections (50 μm thick) were digitized at 0.4 μm in-plane resolution; every eighth section was processed to visualize a certain tracer, yielding an effective 400 μm inter-slice spacing. Prior to sectioning, the blockface was photographed using a fixed camera setup to provide an undistorted reference for registration. Blockface images were acquired at 16–21 μm in-plane resolution. Expert neuroanatomists annotated three classes of fiber bundles—dense, moderate, and sparse—defined as groups of axons traveling in close proximity from the injection site to one or more terminal fields. Because the original histological images were extremely large, all sections were downsampled by a factor of 16 prior to training and evaluation. Manual annotation is labor-intensive and feasible only for a limited subset of sections, resulting in a small amount of labeled real data. 

Diffusion MRI Data. Whole-brain diffusion MRI was available for a single ex vivo macaque brain imaged on a 4.7T preclinical scanner using a 3D echo-planar sequence (.5mm isotropic resolution, 2 segments, TR = 500ms, TE = 48ms, δ = 15ms, Δ = 19ms, 515 gradient directions on a Cartesian grid in q-space, bmax = 40K s/mm²). After standard preprocessing to mitigate noise and correct for eddy-current distortions and bias fields, multi-shell constrained spherical deconvolution was used to estimate fiber orientation distributions. 

Data synthesis. To address the scarcity and variability of labeled tracer sections, we generated synthetic training data using a domain-randomization framework, leveraging a single macaque case for which histology, blockface images, and whole-brain dMRI were available in the same brain. Background textures were sampled from blockface volumes, while foreground fiber bundles were synthesized from dMRI tractography. Streamlines were registered into histological space with diffeomorphic transformations computed using Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs). Tractography was used solely to provide realistic local fiber geometry; synthetic patches do not encode global connectivity, and the model does not learn anatomical connection patterns.  

To generate fiber bundles with realistic density at microscopic resolution, probabilistic tractography was seeded 10,000 times in a single dMRI voxel near the gray-white matter interface. Streamlines were then transformed from dMRI to blockface space, and synthetic image patches were generated at the microscopic resolution of the blockface volume, using the streamlines and blockface volume as foreground and background, respectively. Seed location, fiber intensity, bundle density, and morphology were randomly varied to produce diverse synthetic images that captured key anatomical and signal characteristics observed in real tracer data. Ground truth labels (fiber bundle vs. background) were generated automatically via connected-components analysis applied to densified streamlines, with thresholds chosen to avoid over-segmentation into individual axons. Patch-based synthesis was used to enable efficient generation without constructing full-resolution synthetic volumes. 

Training. A 2D U-Net was trained exclusively on synthetic patches. Transfer to real histological sections was evaluated under three settings: (1) zero-shot inference without fine-tuning, (2) one-shot fine-tuning using a single annotated section from dataset M1, and (3) few-shot fine-tuning using five annotated sections from M1. For comparison, baseline U-Nets were trained on the same one-shot and few-shot real data without synthetic pretraining, isolating the contribution of synthetic data to segmentation performance. 

Results

All models were evaluated on real tracer histology data. Data from two macaques, namely M1 and M2 were used. Dataset M1 was used for one-shot and few-shot training; dataset M2 served as a held-out test set. Segmentation performance was quantified using: 

- False Discovery Rate (FDR): proportion of predicted positives that are false positives, with lower values indicating improved precision. 

- True Positive Rate (TPR): sensitivity, defined as the proportion of ground-truth fiber bundles correctly detected. Although the model outputs a binary segmentation mask without differentiating bundle types, we report TPR separately for dense, moderate, and sparse bundles to better characterize performance as a function of fiber density populations. 

- Average true and false positives per section (TPavg, FPavg): TPavg measures the average number of predicted bundles that overlap with at least one ground-truth bundle, while FPavg measures the average number of predictions that do not overlap with any ground-truth bundle. Both metrics are computed by dividing total counts by the number of test sections. 

Zero-shot evaluation showed that synthetic data alone were insufficient for direct deployment on real sections. Dense bundles were detected moderately well (TPR_dense = 0.83), but performance dropped for moderate and sparse bundles (TPR_moderate = 0.30; TPR_sparse = 0.19). The model also produced many false positives (FPavg = 23.63; FDR = 0.96). These results indicate that real-data fine-tuning is essential to adapt the appearance statistics of synthetic fiber bundles to those observed in histology. 

In the one-shot setting, synthetic pretraining provided clear advantages over a model trained only on a single real section. Pretraining reduced the number of false detections nearly two-fold (FPavg 7.37 vs. 13.50) while maintaining similar TPR for dense (≈0.83) and sparse bundles (≈0.42). However, both the baseline and pretrained one-shot models systematically misclassified terminal fields as fiber bundles (Figure 2), a failure mode consistent with our synthetic labels, which do not include terminals. This limitation highlights the need for additional real-data supervision beyond a single annotated section. 

Few-shot fine-tuning (five real sections) substantially improved performance across all bundle classes and corrected the terminal-field error seen in one-shot training. Synthetic pretraining further enhanced these gains: TPR increased for dense (0.91 vs. 0.89), moderate (0.80 vs. 0.71), and sparse bundles (0.49 vs. 0.39). Although FPavg was slightly higher after pretraining (1.66 vs. 1.00), this reflected increased sensitivity (TPavg 2.63 vs. 2.29) rather than reduced precision. 

Together, these results demonstrate that domain-randomized synthetic data consistently improve generalization, reduce false positives in low-supervision regimes, and significantly boost detection of sparse and moderate fiber bundles when combined with even limited real-data fine-tuning.

Conclusion

In this work, we introduced a synthetic-data–augmented framework for automated fiber bundle segmentation in macaque anatomic tracer datasets. By training on a combination of domain-randomized synthetic patches with a small number of manually annotated sections, our approach addresses the long-standing bottleneck of labor-intensive manual annotation that has constrained scalable connectivity mapping from microscopy data. A key novelty of this framework is the use of dMRI tractography to generate anatomically informed synthetic fibers, allowing us to capture realistic local fiber geometry and produce synthetic appearances far more representative of true axonal trajectories than purely simulated textures. Although segmentation performance is not yet optimal, synthetic pretraining consistently improved results across all training regimes, with particularly strong benefits in the few-shot setting. These findings highlight the potential of domain-randomized synthetic data for mitigating limited real annotations and enabling more robust detection of axonal pathways. 

There are, however, important limitations to consider. Zero-shot transfer was largely unsuccessful, with high false discovery rates (FDR = 0.96) indicating a substantial domain gap between the synthetic patches and real tracer histology. Much of this mismatch arises from imperfections in our synthetic label generation: automated detection occasionally produced labels corresponding to individual axons rather than coherent bundles, diverging from the semantic target of the segmentation task. In addition, all models, including those fine-tuned on real data, showed systematic errors near terminal regions. We trace this to inaccuracies in the registration between blockface images and dMRI space, which can misplace synthetic streamlines into regions inconsistent with true fiber trajectories, such as grey matter. These spatial misalignments propagate into the synthetic patches and complicate the model’s ability to learn realistic bundle–terminal distinctions. 

Despite these challenges, the one-shot and few-shot experiments clearly demonstrate the advantages of synthetic pretraining. Across both settings, synthetic initialization reduced false positives and substantially improved detection of moderate/sparse-density bundles. While performance remains below what could be achieved with extensive real-data supervision, the observed gains underscore the promise of the approach and validate the role of synthetic data in improving generalizability. 

Future work will focus on further close the synthetic–real gap. Incorporating white-matter masks during patch generation would prevent synthetic bundles from appearing in anatomically implausible locations. Improving streamline-based labels and enriching the domain randomization process with additional variability should also enhance realism. Finally, incorporating these, along with the generation of larger, more diverse synthetic datasets, we expect performance to approach that of models trained on substantially more real histological data. 

Overall, the proposed approach offers a practical foundation for scaling fiber bundle segmentation in anatomic tracer datasets. By leveraging dMRI tractography as a generative prior for tracer-like synthetic microscopy images, and with continued improvements to synthetic data quality, this approach opens a new path toward large-scale segmentation of anatomic tracer data. This is essential for the development of standardized primate connectivity atlases and, ultimately, for the validation of dMRI tractography.
