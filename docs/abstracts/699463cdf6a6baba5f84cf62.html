Introduction

Brain age gap was introduced as a promising imaging-derived biomarker of brain health.1 However, despite more than a decade-long development it has not yet lived up to its initial promises of becoming a personalized biomarker of brain health. In addition to the lack of standardization and benchmarking of the extant models, various (methodological) concerns have been raised about the brain age prediction (e.g., ref 2). In contrast, normative models in neuroscience offer an alternative approach to brain age estimation by identifying deviations in brain properties from those of the reference population. This is achieved by modelling age as a covariate within the approach, instead of relying on age prediction. By mapping deviations from the population at an individual level, normative models promise an alternative to the case-control study design. 3,4 Here, we compare the predictive accuracy of conversion to Alzheimer’s disease, based on the outputs of both BrainAGE model and a voxel-wise normative model.

Methods

Sample

BrainAGE and normative models were trained on a large healthy reference sample composed of openly available images from IXI (n=562, 48.12±16.61 years, 55% women) OASIS-3 (n=549, 69.53±8.86 years, 62% women), Cam-CAN (n=567, 54.30±18.59 years, 51% women), SALD (n=494, 45.18±17.44 years, 62% women), Enhanced NKI (n=1322, 38.74±21.27 years, 63% women), NKI-RS (n=188, 37.43±18.71 years, 39.08% women) Dallas Lifespan Brain Study (n=315, 54.62±20.09 years, 62.86% women), and WAYNE10/11/EF (n=412, 53.63±17.27 years, 66.48% women) databases. 

Trained models were later applied to 1172 T1w images from Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI), including healthy control subjects (n = 496, 72.8±5.98 years, 55% women), subjects who progressed to MCI (n = 42, 78.1±4.26 years, 45% women), subjects with stable MCI (n = 370, 72.7±7.55 years, 40% women), subjects who progressed from MCI to Alzheimer’s dementia (n = 112, 75.2±6.99 years, 40% women), and AD patients (n = 152, 75.5±7.57 years, 46% women).

Data processing and model estimation

The datasets were processed using SPM’s CAT12.9 toolbox running in MATLAB 2024. Affine-registered grey (GM) matter tissue segments were resampled to 8 mm resolution and smoothed with the 8mm FWHM kernel, and were subsequently used for the estimation of brain age and normative modelling.

The cross-sectional global BrainAGE model using Gaussian process regression was trained on the reference sample and applied to the ADNI sample (MAE = 4.51, r = 0.72). An age-bias correction was used as described in ref 5.

We modelled the voxel-wise GM volume as a function of age using the Generalized Additive Models for Location, Scale and Shape (GAMLSS) framework from mgcv R package. GAMLSS models using Gaussian distribution were applied only to voxels, where the average value was larger than 0.05 and at least 80% of the data were non-zero. The resulting voxel-wise z-scores were mapped to the brain surface and averaged for the comparison with global BrainAGE value.

To compare the predictive performance of the two model types, we treated progression from cognitively unimpaired to MCI and from MCI to AD as reference outcomes and constructed receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for each model. We then quantified their ability to distinguish between stable states and progression by calculating the area under the curve (AUC), and statistically compared the AUCs using DeLong’s test from the pROC R package. Apart from the brain age estimation, which was employed in MATLAB, the GAMLSS model estimation and ROC analyses were performed in RStudio (version 2024.12.1+563).

Results

Average z-scores maps for different groups of ADNI participants indicated differences between the groups predominantly in temporal area, particularly in those who progressed from MCI to dementia and for the AD patients.

The AUCs for predicting the conversion to MCI were 0.525 and 0.607 for global BrainAGE and the average z-score from the normative model, respectively. Nonparametric DeLong’s test for comparison of the AUC showed no statistically significant differences between the AUCs (Z = -1.86, p = 0.063, 95% CI: [-0.169,  0.004].

For predicting the conversion to AD,  AUC for global BrainAGE resulted in 0.627 and 0.678 for the average z-score from the normative model, however, differences in AUCs were not statistically different either (Z = -1.63, p = 0.103, 95% CI: [-0.112, 0.010]).

Conclusion

We have compared two widely adopted methods of comparing (structural) brain deviations from the reference group; the brain age gap estimation model and the normative model. Our results demonstrated that the outputs of both models were similarly effective at distinguishing between the stable and progressive stages of cognitive impairment and neurodegeneration. Nevertheless, when separating between people without impairment and AD patients,  averaged deviation scores from the normative model showed a greater ability to discriminate between these groups than global BrainAGE scores. 

However, the present study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design does not account for cohort effects or secular trends in the data. Second, we compared a global brain age gap value and averaged voxel-wise patterns. These do not convey equivalent information and, at least for the normative models, are not the typical application of such a method. Additionally, the BrainAGE model that we used was defined across the entire age range of the training datasets (6-95), making it less sensitive to neurodegeneration, which typically occurs only in older age. Finally, we only compared the models with regard to their application to dementia, disregarding other neurodegenerative and psychiatric disorders.

Nevertheless, our results may indicate a need to transition from brain-age models to normative models when studying individual brain deviations from the reference population. Further studies are needed to compare different brain age models and normative models, and to compare these methods across various populations.
