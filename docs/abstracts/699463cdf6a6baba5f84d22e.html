Introduction

Stroke is a leading cause of death and acquired disability worldwide. In mild-moderate stroke, initial impairment strongly predicts long-term recovery. However, patient outcomes become increasingly heterogenous with greater severity. Neuroimaging biomarkers offer non-invasive insight into the mechanisms underlying post-stroke and remain predictive at all levels of severity1. Recent work has shown that DWI-derived measures such as lesion volume and regional lesion load, can accurately predict acute stroke severity2. Although promising, these approaches require expert manual lesion delineation and substantial preprocessing, limiting clinical scalability. Deep learning (DL) 3D convolutional neural networks (CNNs) learn from MRI directly with minimal preprocessing but their performance depends on large, diverse training datasets3. While public MRI repositories have fueled DL in Alzheimer’s disease and aging, comparable resources for stroke outcome prediction remain limited2. 

To address this, we investigate transfer learning by fine-tuning pretrained models of healthy brain age3 and dementia diagnosis3 to classify acute stroke severity, measured by the modified Rankin Scale (mRS). Models are trained on T1-weighted (T1w) MRI and impairment data from the publicly available Stroke Outcome Optimisation Project (SOOP)2. We assess the value of pretraining by comparing these models with an identical architecture trained from scratch. For reference, we also train an XGBoost model on the same patients using conventional DWI-derived biomarkers, including lesion volume and regional lesion loads, which are highly sensitive to acute stroke. Research using machine learning (ML) and T1w biomarkers has linked higher brain age gap (BAG = predicted – chronological age) to worse motor outcomes in chronic stroke4. Here, we evaluated BAG from the T1w Healthy Brain Age SFCN as a biomarker for acute stroke severity, hypothesizing that higher BAG would correspond to greater impairment (mRS).

Methods

We used a subset of the Stroke Outcome Optimisation Project (SOOP)2, a public retrospective dataset of 1106 patients with acute ischaemic stroke (AIS) containing clinical, demographic and multimodal MRI data. T1w MRI was acquired within 30 days of admission (majority <48h). Acute stroke severity was defined using the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) with mRS > 2 labelled as severe. After applying inclusion and quality-control criteria (confirmed AIS, no prior stroke, available mRS and T1w MRI with minimum spatial dimension 100x100x60), 565 patients remained (52% male; mean age 63±14.6). MRI quality control (QC) included automated checks for preprocessing failures and visual checks for motion artefacts, removing 21 patients. The same QC process was applied to T1w MRI from 563 healthy controls in the IXI dataset (55% female; mean age 49±16.5) with none being removed.

T1w MRI preprocessing matched the original SFCN pipeline3: brain extraction (Freesurfer autorecon), affine registration to MNI152 (FSL FLIRT, 6 dof), voxel intensity normalisation (NumPy) and left-hemisphere lesion flipping for alignment (fslswapdim). Lesion volume and regional lesion load measures were available for all patients in the SOOP repository with a visible lesion. For patients without a visible lesion, lesion volume and loads were set to 0. Full DWI preprocessing is described by Absher et al.2. Briefly, DWI lesion masks were manually delineated, registered to template space, and used to compute whole-brain lesion volume and vascular atlas-derived regional lesion loads2, stored in a tabular format with clinical and demographic variables for modelling with XGBoost.

All models were trained with 4-fold stratified cross validation. For SFCNs, training folds were further split into 80/20 for validation, with a fixed held-out test fold. To capture noise in model performance due to data partitioning, the entire procedure was repeated ten times with different partitions. Partitions were predefined using set seeds to allow paired model comparisons across identical data partitions. Three SFCN variants were trained for acute stroke severity classification (binary: mRS > 2): two using publicly available pretrained weights (Healthy Brain Age, Dementia Diagnosis)3 and one trained from scratch. All shared the same VGG-like 3D CNN architecture (~3M parameters) and identical hyperparameters. An XGBoost classifier using regional lesion loads served as a reference model, trained with matching data partitions and repetitions. A second XGBoost model was trained identically using Healthy Brain Age SFCN embeddings of T1w MRI extracted from the 3D global average pooling layer.

The SFCN models were implemented using code adapted from Leonardsen et al.3, in Keras/TensorFlow 2.11 and trained on an NVIDIA A100 GPU (80 GB VRAM) for up to 160 epochs (batch size 10, with early stopping (patience 5), weight decay 0.09, dropout 0.6 and initial learning rate (LR) 0.01. The LR was reduced by a factor of 3 after epochs 20, 40 and 60. The best validation checkpoint per fold was used for testing. Performance was evaluated using F1 Score, with sensitivity, specificity and balanced accuracy also reported. Test predictions were concatenated across folds and metrics were summarised as median values across ten repetitions.

Results

When predicting chronological age (years) in acute stroke patients the SFCN healthy brain age model achieved an R2 of 0.74 (r = 0.86, p < 0.05, MAE = 5.4). This was compared with an R2 of 0.88 (r = 0.94, p < 0.05, MAE = 4.2) in healthy controls from the IXI dataset, indicating that the brain age model generalised well to acute stroke patients. However, contrary to our hypothesis, correlational analysis did not reveal a significant positive association between BAG and acute mRS values (r = -0.10, p < 0.05). Results of linear probing using XGBoost also showed that SFCN brain age features were a poor predictor of stroke severity, achieving the lowest performance among our models with a median F1 score of 0.45 (ranging 0.40-0.48) across 10 repetitions (Fig. 2a, 2e).

Next, we evaluated how well pretrained SFCNs transfer to the domain of acute stroke severity classification through finetuning. Distributions of SFCN performance across 10 repeats of cross-validation showed that the Finetuned Dementia SFCN achieved the highest performance across all our metrics with a median balanced accuracy of 0.66 (ranging 0.65-0.68) and median F1 score of 0.59 (ranging 0.58-0.62) (Fig. 2a, 2e). The median accuracy for severe mRS (sensitivity) was 0.56 (ranging 0.54-0.61) while the median precision was 0.62 (ranging 0.61-0.66) indicating that the model was only able to identify 56% of severely impaired patients and that when a severe prediction was made, the model was only correct 62% of the time. Paired t-test comparing model performance across repeats showed that Finetuned Dementia SFCNs had significantly higher F1 scores (M = 0.59, SD=0.01) than SFCNs trained from scratch (M = 0.51, SD = 0.07) (t(9) = 3.87, p = 0.004). In contrast, while Finetuned Healthy Brain Age SFCNs did perform better than SFCNs trained from scratch, there was not a significant difference in F1 scores (M = 0.54, SD=0.05) (t(9) = 1.30, p = 0.23). As our best performing model, Finetuned SFCN-Dementia was selected for further analysis and comparison against conventional machine learning (XGBoost) using DWI biomarkers. To identify the sources of performance limitations in the Finetuned Dementia SFCN, we analysed the median classification accuracy for each individual mRS score. The resulting bar plot shows that accuracy was notably lower for mRS scores of 3 and 4, indicating that the model struggles with intermediate levels of disability (Fig. 2c).

We then compared our best performing whole brain T1w SFCN model (Finetuned Dementia) against an XGBoost classifier trained on DWI-derived regional lesion loads. Across 10 repetitions, XGBoost achieved the highest performance among all our models with a median F1 score of 0.64 (ranging 0.62-0.66) and a balanced accuracy of 0.69 (ranging 0.68-0.71) (Fig. 2a, 2e). XGBoost also achieved higher sensitivity (M=0.62, ranging 0.60-0.65) and precision (M=0.65, ranging 0.63-0.68) indicating that the model was better able to identify severely impaired patients relative to the best performing SFCN model. Paired t-tests confirmed this, revealing that XGBoost with DWI lesion load features significantly outperformed the Finetuned Dementia SFCN based on F1 Score (t(9) = 8.7, p < 0.05) and balanced accuracy (t(9) = 7.3, p < 0.05).

Conclusion

Our analysis showed that the best performing SFCN model was the Finetuned Dementia SFCN, which achieved moderate performance with a median F1 Score of 0.59 (ranging 0.58-0.62) and balanced accuracy of 66% (ranging 65-68). Contrary to our hypothesis, SFCN-based brain age gap (BAG) was not predictive of acute mRS scores and showed a slight negative correlation with mRS. This may reflect the age distribution of the SOOP dataset, which skews older than the healthy controls, combined with a known bias of brain age models to overestimate younger participants’ age and underestimate older participants4. Linear probing indicated that pretrained Brain Age features were not directly useful for classifying acute stroke severity. However, finetuning the brain age SFCN substantially improved performance. The highest accuracy was achieved using a clinical dementia model that had itself been finetuned from the Healthy Brain Age SFCN, suggesting that further gains may be possible through pretraining on more closely related domains, such as brain cancer patient outcomes. Despite these improvements, XGBoost models using regional vascular lesion load outperformed all DL approaches, achieving higher accuracy and better identifying patients with severe impairment, a critical factor for clinical use. This likely reflects the higher sensitivity of diffusion weighted MRI (used for lesion load) to acute tissue abnormalities compared to T1w MRI used by the SFCN. Our finetuned dementia SFCN also struggled to classify patients with intermediate severity (mRS 3-4) (Fig. 2c), and most false negatives occurred in this range (Fig. 2b). This may be due to the coarse nature of the mRS scoring system and potential inter-rater variability leading to ambiguity in patient assessments. Future work will explore curriculum learning, starting with easier-to-classify samples before incorporating intermediate cases. Additionally, a key limitation of this study is the arbitrary selection of hyperparameters which were kept identical across models. Optimising these parameters for each model via grid search as performed in previous SFCN studies is likely to increase performance and further work will include this step. The strong performance of the lesion load XGBoost model underscores the importance of lesion visibility at the acute stages for identifying severely impaired and at-risk patients. Future work will investigate finetuning with more sensitive modalities. We will transfer the pretrained T1w SFCN models to T2 FLAIR MRI, which is more sensitive to brain abnormalities and is available in SOOP for the same participants and at the same resolution.
