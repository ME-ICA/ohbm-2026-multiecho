Introduction

Epilepsy is increasingly recognized as a network disorder, in which distributed alterations in brain organization shape clinical presentation beyond the presumed seizure onset zone1. Network science could provide a framework to relate macroscale brain connectivity to the widespread pathophysiology. Initiatives such as the Human Connectome Project (HCP) provide normative structural and functional connectivity information from a large sample of healthy individuals. The ENIGMA Epilepsy consortium reported that atrophy in temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) colocalized with highly interconnected cortical hub regions2. 

N-acetylaspartate (NAA), often viewed as a metabolic marker of neuronal integrity, has been most consistently reported to be reduced in the foci as well as propagation zones of temporal lobe epilepsy3. Recent advances in ultrafast MRSI have enabled whole brain mapping of NAA at 3 millimeter resolution within clinically acceptable scan time4, which presents an opportunity to investigate the spatial distribution of NAA reduction in TLE and the underlying network mechanisms that shape its pattern.

This study is designed to elucidate the spatial architecture of neuronal metabolic vulnerability in TLE. We first derived a covariate-adjusted neuronal metabolic vulnerability map using NAA/Cr with patient hemispheres mathematically flipped to align the epileptogenic side. We then rigorously tested the alignment of this metabolic vulnerability pattern with connectome topology by examining two key network metrics: (i) its spatial association with functional and structural hubness (degree centrality) and (ii) epicenter like coupling between regional connectivity fingerprints and the vulnerability map, to identify candidate functional and structural epicenters driving the distributed metabolic abnormality in TLE.

Methods

Data acquisition:

This IRB-approved study involved 74 patients with drug-resistant temporal lobe epilepsy (LTLE: 42; RTLE: 32; age 9–71 years), undergoing MRSI on a 3.0 T MRI (Biograph mMR: Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany). All patients were verified to have been seizure-free for at least 24 hours prior to scanning, underwent a 4 to 6 hour fast before the MR examination, and were instructed to stay awake with their eyes closed throughout the imaging session to promote consistency across measurements.

The MR imaging protocol included high-resolution MRSI scans performed with SPICE (2.0 × 3.1 × 2.7 mm3, FOV= 240 × 240 × 96 mm³, or 2.0 × 3.1 × 3.8 mm3, FOV = 240 × 240 × 160 mm³, TR/TE = 160/1.6 ms). T1-weighted anatomical images were obtained using MPRAGE (0.5 × 0.5 × 1.0 mm³, TR/TE = 1900/2.44 ms, matrix size =256 × 256). The acquisition of MRSI data for healthy controls (N=42) followed the same protocol as that used for patients.

Network modeling followed the established framework2, leveraging independent SC and FC reference connectomes. Both templates were derived from the averaged healthy control matrix of the ENIGMA dataset sourced from the Human Connectome Project (HCP).

Data processing and analysis:

Targeted spatiospectral functions were reconstructed from the MRSI data using a union-of-subspaces model incorporating pre-learned spectral basis functions, as previously described4, yielding metabolite concentration maps including NAA, Cho, mIn, and Cr. In parallel, T1-weighted MPRAGE images were processed with the FreeSurfer image analysis suite to obtain brain segmentations. Mean ROI-level metabolite ratios, including NAA/Cr, Cho/Cr, and mIn/Cr, were then computed. For patients with right-sided seizure focus, we performed a left–right hemisphere flip (i.e., swapping left and right ROIs) to align the focus to the same hemisphere across subjects. Following this procedure, measures were reported as ipsilateral and contralateral relative to the seizure focus. 

The neuronal metabolic vulnerability was defined as the negative t-statistic of the group term (TLE vs HC) from ROI-wise GLMs adjusting for age and sex, such that higher neuronal metabolic vulnerability indicates stronger TLE-related reduction in NAA/Cr.

Network Modeling: 

Resting-state functional connectivity (FC) and diffusion-weighted imaging-derived structural connectivity (SC) were used to construct whole-brain connectomes. Following the established network-based atrophy modeling approach2, we quantified:

Hub susceptibility: Calculated as the spatial correlation between the regional neuronal metabolic vulnerability map and the degree centrality for both cortical and subcortical networks. This metric assesses whether metabolically vulnerable regions tend to be network hubs.

Epicenter mapping: The spatial correlation between the pattern of regional metabolic features and the functional/structural connectivity profiles from every single node (seed) to the rest of the brain, identifying potential metabolic epicenters of network disruption.

Statistical Analysis: To ensure statistical rigor and control for the non-independence of brain regions, the significance of correlations between spatial maps was assessed using a non-parametric spatial spin permutation test and this test was performed for 10,000 permutations. Corrections for multiple comparisons (e.g., FDR) were applied to the epicenter statistical tests.

Results

An NAA/Cr–based regional neuronal metabolic vulnerability map was constructed to quantify the extent of metabolic impairment in the patient group relative to healthy controls. As shown in Fig. 2a, patients exhibited significant neuronal metabolic deficits across multiple key regions compared with controls. These reductions were primarily concentrated in the ipsilateral hippocampus, temporal cortical areas, and parts of the limbic and subcortical nuclei, suggesting focal neuronal dysfunction and/or loss within these territories.

To evaluate whether metabolically vulnerable regions are preferentially aligned with network hubs, we examined the spatial association between the neuronal metabolic vulnerability map and hubness. We found significant negative spatial correlations between neuronal metabolic vulnerability and functional degree centrality in both cortical and subcortical networks after spin test (pspin < 0.05 for cortical networks, pshuffle < 0.05 for subcortical networks), indicating that nodes playing more central roles in functional communication are more susceptible to neuronal metabolic impairment. In contrast, we did not observe significant spatial correlations between neuronal metabolic vulnerability and structural degree centrality after spin test.

Epicenter analysis was then used to further probe the spatial coupling between the regional neuronal metabolic vulnerability pattern and functional connectivity fingerprints (Fig. 2c). We observed a predominantly negative coupling across the brain, indicating a positive correspondence between vulnerability map and FC connectivity profiles. Notably, this coupling was not focal but rather widely distributed along the cortex, exhibiting a clear spatial gradient: stronger negative coupling was observed in lateral associative cortices, particularly within fronto-parietal and temporo-parietal territories. Importantly, several subcortical and limbic nuclei showed especially strong coupling when used as seeds—most prominently the hippocampus (p =0.031), amygdala (p = 0.012), and thalamus (p = 0.002).

In contrast to the widespread functional coupling, structural epicenter mapping (Fig. 2d) revealed a more restricted pattern. After FDR correction, only three cortical seeds showed significant negative structure–metabolism coupling (p < 0.05): the left paracentral lobule (r =−0.518), the left postcentral gyrus (r = −0.509), and the right caudal anterior cingulate cortex (r =−0.471). 

Conclusion

In this study, we constructed an NAA/Cr–based neuronal metabolic vulnerability map and integrated it with connectome topological organization, thereby demonstrating that metabolic abnormalities in TLE follow a network-constrained spatial distribution. A central finding was the significant negative association between neuronal metabolic vulnerability and functional hubness (degree centrality): regions that occupy more central positions in functional networks exhibit greater impairment in neuronal metabolic integrity.

Epicenter analyses further indicated that functional network coupling plays a dominant role in shaping the spatial pattern of neuronal metabolic vulnerability. Functional epicenter mapping revealed a broadly distributed, predominantly negative coupling between the vulnerability map and functional connectivity fingerprints. This non-focal, network-wide coupling suggests that neuronal metabolic abnormalities in epilepsy are not confined to the canonical epileptogenic focus, but rather reflect a network-level pathological process that may propagate or evolve along circuits of functional synchrony. By contrast, structural epicenter mapping identified significant negative coupling in only a limited set of cortical ROIs. 

Finally, functional epicenter mapping highlighted limbic and subcortical nuclei—including the hippocampus, amygdala, and thalamus—as seeds exhibiting particularly strong negative coupling. This finding suggests that these structures, long implicated in seizure generation and propagation, may act as metabolic epicenters that anchor and potentially drive the cortical spatial architecture of metabolic vulnerability. Note that although hippocampus sclerosis is known as the most common pathology for mesial temporal lobe epilepsy, it is weaker than thalamus on the epicenter map. Further investigation is needed to understand whether the epicenter characterization may capture heterogeneities across individual subjects who might be stratified towards different therapies.
