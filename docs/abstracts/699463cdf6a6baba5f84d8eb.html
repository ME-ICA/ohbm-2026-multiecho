Introduction

Perivascular spaces (PVS) are cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)-filled spaces surrounding cerebral blood vessels which support waste clearance, solute delivery, and CSF-interstitial fluid exchange. Considering the metabolic demands of neurodevelopment, PVS may play a critical role in supporting structural neuroplasticity. Although PVS morphology varies widely across individuals, the biological sources of this variation remain poorly understood. Emerging evidence in adults suggests a genetic component to PVS structure and function (Duperron et al., 2023; Ran et al., 2025), however no study to date has examined the contribution of genetics to PVS structure during childhood, a critical period when neurovascular systems undergo rapid maturation. The Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study provides a unique opportunity to investigate genetic influences on PVS morphology during childhood using harmonized MRI and whole-genome sequencing across a large, diverse cohort.  Here, we utilize genomics and a cutting-edge PVS segmentation pipeline to test whether 45 SNPs identified by Duperron et al. (2023) and Ran et al. (2025), as well as variants of the aquaporin-4 (AQP4) gene that have been implicated in PVS flow and brain-CSF homeostasis (Rainey-Smith et al., 2018), are associated with PVS count and volume fraction (VF) at 9-10 years old. Count models assess whether genetic variation relates to having a greater number of MRI-visible PVS, whereas VF models evaluate associations with overall PVS burden, reflecting potential dilation independent of count. We hypothesize that SNPs near genes involved in astrocyte structure and function (AQP4, LAMC1, SLC13A3, GFAP) and those regulating cellular migration and angiogenesis (WNT7A, EFEMP1, SEMA6A, GNA12, GIT1) will be associated with PVS burden in the frontal and parietal white matter, as PVS in the white matter of these regions are most prevalent at this age. 

Methods

The current study leveraged cross-sectional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)  and genetic data from 6,600 participants (48.5% female) enrolled in the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study. Data used in this study was collected at the baseline study visit, when participants were 9-10 years-old. Genetic data for the ABCD Study were generated from saliva and blood samples and processed by Rutgers University Cell and DNA Repository. Quality-controlled (QC) genotypes were imputed using the TOPMED reference panel and population structure was characterized using PC-AiR (GENESIS), producing 32 ancestry principal components (PCs). For the current study, we used imputed SNPs, excluding variants with call rate <98% or minor allele frequency <5%, and individuals with extreme heterozygosity. Data were converted to PLINK format and predefined SNPs of interest were extracted. Raw T1w and T2w images passing ABCD and FreeSurfer QC, and those without clinical findings, were downloaded and processed. Images were registered using the Human Connectome Project (HCP) minimal preprocessing pipeline and upsampled to 0.7mm3 to enhance contrast. Preprocessed T1w and T2w images were denoised using adaptive non-local mean filtering, and an enhanced PVS contrast (EPC) image was created by dividing filtered T1w/T2w images (Sepehrband et al., 2019). EPC images then underwent manual QC to remove cases with noise or incidental findings. PVS were segmented using the Weakly Supervised Perivascular Spaces Segmentation method (Lan et al., 2023). White matter was parcellated using the Desikan-Killiany atlas and grouped into six regions of interest: frontal, temporal, parietal, occipital, cingulate, and centrum semiovale (CSO). PVS count and volume were extracted, and volume fraction (VF) was computed by dividing PVS volume by regional white matter volume. Covariates were selected using prior literature and directed acyclic graphs. The following variables were included in analyses: age, sex, household income, scanner/headcoil. Genetic PCs 1–5 were also included as covariates to capture population structure, consistent with prior ABCD genetic analyses. Regional white matter volume was included in count-based analyses to control for differences in white matter volume, and site was included as a random effect in all models to control for the hierarchical nature of the ABCD Study and to capture scanner-related clustering. Analyses were conducted in R. Linear mixed-effects (LME) models were used to test associations between each SNP (N = 45) and PVS count and VF in each of the six regions. While PVS count is formally discrete, the observed distributions were sufficiently continuous and well-behaved to justify Gaussian mixed-effects models. FDR correction was applied across all SNP-region tests within each outcome, with PFDR < 0.05 considered statistically significant. 

Results

Across regions, we observed several significant associations between SNPs identified by Duperron et al. (2023) and Rainey-Smith et al. (2018) (Figure 1). Two SNPs near the WNT7A locus (chr3:13780942:G:C and chr3:13791114:A:G) showed associations across multiple regions. The first variant was linked to temporal, occipital, CSO (pFDR < 0.05), and parietal (pFDR < 0.01) VF, while the second showed associations with temporal, parietal, and CSO VF (pFDR < .01), as well as temporal count and occipital VF (pFDR < 0.05). Two AQP4 SNPs (chr18:26850565:G:A and chr18:26869139:T:G) demonstrated associations with temporal and parietal VF (pFDR < 0.05). Several SNPs near the SLC13A3 locus exhibited the strongest and most widespread associations. Three variants (chr20:46629653:C:T, chr20:46641228:C:T, and chr20:46685796:T:C) were associated with temporal (pFDR< 0.01 - 0 .001), parietal (pFDR < 0.01 - 0.001), occipital (pFDR < 0.05 - 0.001), frontal (PFDR < 0.05 - 0.001), CSO (pFDR < 0.05 - 0.001), and cingulate VF (pFDR < 0.001), as well as temporal and parietal count (pFDR < 0.05 - 0.01). A fourth SLC13A3 variant (chr20:46626979:A:G) showed associations with temporal and occipital VF (pFDR< 0.05).

Finally, variants at the ITGB5/UMP3 (chr3:124799515:G:A), the C6ORF195 (chr6:26168888:C:A), the PDE8A (chr15:85143096:A:G), and the ISYNA1/ELL/LRRC25 loci demonstrated region-specific effects, including associations with temporal, parietal, frontal, and CSO VF (pFDR < 0.05 - 0.01). The LPAR1 variant (chr9:110896391:G:A) was associated with parietal VF (pFDR < 0.01), while SNPs annotated to FADD/PPFIA (chr11:70243594:C:T) showed significant associations with temporal, parietal, frontal, and CSO VF, and temporal count (pFDR  < 0.05 - 0.01).

Overall, the strongest and most region-generalizable associations were observed for variants in WNT7A, FADD/PPFIA, and SLC13A3, while loci near AQP4 and other genes showed more region-specific effects.

Conclusion

We replicated several significant associations between SNPs and PVS burden during preadolescence, reinforcing findings from adult studies which have demonstrated that PVS are influenced by genetics implicated in biological pathways related to fluid homeostasis, neurovascular development, and glial metabolism. Associations were more consistently observed for VF than count, suggesting that genetic variation may preferentially relate to PVS dilation, or overall burden, rather than the number of discrete, MRI-visible PVS. Among the strongest associations were SNPs near the WNT7A, SLC13A3, and FADD/PPFIA1 genes, all of which have established relevance to brain development. SNPs near the WNT7A locus were associated with white matter PVS in the temporal, parietal, and occipital lobes, and the CSO. WNT7A encodes for the Wnt protein, which has established roles in neurodevelopment, including neural progenitor proliferation, axon guidance, and vascular development in the white matter. Although the associated SNPs  are located upstream of WNT7A, they may reflect regulatory effects on gene expression, which may in turn influence the development and patterning of the neurovascular unit. SNPs at the SLC13A3 locus demonstrated some of the strongest and most widespread associations. SLC13A3 encodes a sodium-dependent dicarboxylate transporter expressed in astrocytes. Altered transporter activity may influence metabolic support for astrocytes or waste clearance within the PVS, providing a plausible mechanistic link to PVS burden. Mutations at this locus have also been associated with Acute Reversible Leukoencephalopathy, which is characterized by extensive white matter damage. We also identified associations near the FADD/PPFIA1 locus. FADD encodes the Fas-Associated Death Domain protein, which is involved in programmed cell death, inflammatory signaling, cell proliferation, and embryonic development; PPFIA1 (liprin-α1) is involved in synaptic organization, cell adhesion, migration, and development. Both genes are expressed in neural and vascular-associated cell types and have been implicated in neural scaffolding and cellular communication. Hence, genetic variation in this region may influence neurovascular integrity or development and organization of white-matter microstructure, which may in turn affect perivascular architecture. 

SNPs near AQP4 showed associations with PVS VF in the parietal and temporal white matter. AQP4 encodes the aquaporin-4 protein, which is the primary water channel located on astrocyte endfeet, where it facilitates perivascular fluid exchange and glymphatic clearance. Therefore, genetic variation in the AQP4 locus may influence CSF and interstitial fluid dynamics, which has been linked to PVS burden. Additional SNPs, including those near LPAR1, PDE8A, ITGB5/UMP3, and ISYNA1/ELL/LRRC25 showed region-specific associations. These genes are implicated in cell and lipid signaling, cyclic nucleotide metabolism, inflammation, and cell–matrix interactions and may exert indirect effects on glial or vascular function. 

Overall, these findings support that genes involved in astrocyte function, neurovascular development, metabolic regulation, and cellular signaling may contribute to differences in PVS burden in childhood. Because analyses were cross-sectional at baseline, these findings cannot yet distinguish developmental timing or trajectories of genetic influence on PVS morphology. Integration with animal models, single-cell transcriptomics, and longitudinal imaging will be critical for clarifying the mechanisms linking genetic variation to PVS structure across the lifespan.
