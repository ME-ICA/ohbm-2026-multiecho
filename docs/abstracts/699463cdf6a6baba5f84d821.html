Introduction

The conventional neuroscientific perspective highlights the amygdala's essential role in emotional processing^1, regarding its activation patterns as potential biomarkers for understanding and addressing mood dysregulation disorders, including anxiety and depression. However, current emotion provocation tasks mainly capture group-averaged effects and demonstrate limited inter-individual reproducibility^2. Despite employing Multi-Voxel Pattern Analysis (MVPA), the reliability of amygdala activation patterns in predicting individual responses to emotional stimuli remains a topic of debate. In this study, we utilize an emotional picture rating paradigm^3, paired with both 3T and 7T functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), to investigate whether the amygdala can represent distinct emotional states.

Methods

Participants

This study integrates two sets of experimental data: 3T MRI data from 36 participants (22 females, mean age = 20.34 ± 2.15 years) and 7T MRI data from 28 participants (15 females, mean age = 22.82 ± 3.04 years). All procedures were approved by the institutional review board, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Data acquisition 

Structural and functional MRI data were acquired using both 3T and 7T scanners. T1-weighted images were obtained for anatomical reference, while functional data during the emotion task were collected using T2*-weighted EPI sequences. Acquisition parameters differed between the scanners: for the 3T scanner, voxel size was 2.5 × 2.5 × 2.5 mm³, and repetition time (TR) was 1000 ms; for the 7T scanner, voxel size was 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.9 mm³, and TR was 3800 ms. The higher spatial resolution of the 7T scanner enabled fine-grained analysis of amygdala subregions.

Image pre-processing

 Identical pre-processing procedures were applied to the 3T and 7T datasets.Functional images datasets were slice-time corrected and realigned to correct for head motion. Structural images were segmented into gray matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and co-registered to the mean functional image. Bias-field correction and brain extraction were applied, and both structural and functional images were normalized to standard space. Finally, functional image intensities were standardized, without spatial smoothing.

Experiment paradigm

A classic emotional picture-rating paradigm was employed, featuring 40 pictures per valence category (neutral, negative, positive) selected from the International Affective Picture System (IAPS). In each trial, a picture was presented for 2 seconds, during which participants viewed the image and pressed a button to report its emotional valence (positive/neutral/negative). Only trials with valid responses were included in subsequent multi-voxel pattern analysis (MVPA).

Multi-voxel pattern analysis

To accurately estimate amygdala activation patterns for each trial, the least-squares separate (LSS) method was employed^4. This method models individual trials while controlling for the combined effects of other trials, yielding a feature matrix based on trial-wise t-values. A linear-kernel support vector machine (SVM) was then trained to classify individual trials into one of the three valence categories. To minimize random bias, a repeated stratified cross-validation scheme was applied (10 repetitions, 8-fold). Classification performance was assessed using balanced accuracy and confusion matrices. Additionally, 1000 permutation tests were performed for each participant to assess statistical significance.

Whole-amygdala MVPA was initially conducted on the 3T data (voxel count: 250–360), which served as the foundation for subsequent analyses in this study. Following this, the analysis was replicated using the higher-resolution 7T data, which had a substantially larger voxel count (>8000). To prevent overfitting, a two-step feature reduction process was applied: (1) selection of the top 50% most activated voxels based on univariate t-tests, and (2) iterative Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) to reduce the final feature set to fewer than 500 voxels.

Subsequently, finer-scale representations were examined by subdividing the amygdala into 7 subregions per hemisphere (14 total) based on a published segmentation protocol ^5. MVPA was performed within each subregion (voxel count: 200–1000). For these analyses, the number of features was systematically tuned: starting with 50 voxels, and increasing in steps of 50, selecting the top-k most activated voxels across trials to identify the optimal feature set for each classifier.

To verify the robustness of the findings, control analyses were performed with different kernel functions and feature selection methods. These variations did not affect the main conclusion.

Results

MVPA Analysis of the Entire Amygdala Based on 3T fMRI Data revealed that the average classification accuracy for participants was 39.71% (SD = 4.05%, range: 32%–48%; Figure 1.a), slightly above the chance level (33%). To further assess statistical significance at the individual level, we compared each participant's classification accuracy with the null distribution generated by 1000 permutation tests. After applying Bonferroni correction (corrected α = 0.05/36 = 0.0014), only 5 out of 36 participants (13.89%) showed significantly higher-than-chance classification accuracy (p < 0.001). This result suggests that the activity pattern of the entire amygdala cannot reliably predict emotional responses at the individual level.

This finding was further validated using 7T fMRI data. The MVPA analysis of the entire amygdala at 7T revealed that the average classification accuracy for all participants was 31.22% (SD = 7.37%; Figure 1.b), even slightly lower than the theoretical chance level, further supporting the lack of stable emotional valence decoding ability in the entire amygdala at the individual level.

To explore whether decoding signals exist at a finer spatial scale, we divided the bilateral amygdala into 14 subregions in the 7T data for analysis (Figure 1.c). The average classification accuracy of each subregion ranged from 34.1% to 36.0% (Figure 1.d). Although these accuracies were slightly higher than chance, no participant showed significantly higher classification accuracy in any subregion after Bonferroni correction (α = 0.05/28 = 0.0018) compared to the null distribution generated by the permutation tests.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that amygdala activity patterns do not reliably decode emotional valence at the individual level. This null result, consistent across 3T and 7T fMRI data and across both whole-amygdala and subregion analyses, challenges the notion of the amygdala as a source of robust, individualized emotion biomarkers.

Rather than indicating an absence of emotion-related processing, these findings suggest that emotional valence may not be the primary dimension encoded in amygdala activity patterns, which may instead be more sensitive to factors such as emotional salience or threat relevance. In addition, the lack of reliable individual-level decoding highlights an important distinction between group-level effects and person-specific neural markers, motivating future work to move beyond local amygdala signals toward network-level or multidimensional affective representations.
