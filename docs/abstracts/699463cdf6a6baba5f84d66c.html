Introduction

Hypersexuality (HY) is a clinically significant phenomenon that occurs across a wide spectrum of neurological conditions and is associated with distress and impairment for patients and families1. Despite its growing visibility in clinical practice, HY is often overlooked, partly due to the absence of ad hoc assessment approaches, the stigma surrounding sexuality, and the difficulties patients and clinicians face in openly discussing such behaviours2. When recognised, clinicians may encounter challenges in selecting and implementing effective management strategies, as available interventions are heterogeneous, rarely standardised, and often require multiple attempts before achieving success.

The HY construct itself is controversial. Classifications range from compulsive sexual behaviour disorder (CSBD) in the ICD-11 to diagnostic proposals that were considered but not implemented in the DSM-5. A consistent limitation of these frameworks is their exclusion of cases secondary to acquired or iatrogenic causes, despite clear evidence that HY can arise from lesions, dopaminergic therapies, neuromodulation. While epidemiological data remain fragmented, HY has been described across both younger and older populations, with marked heterogeneity in phenomenology. Neurobiological studies further implicate front-striatal and limbic dysfunction and dopaminergic dysregulation, yet findings are inconsistent.

We synthesised evidence from 394 studies characterising aetiologies, clinical presentations, assessment methods, and management of HY in neurological disorders. Our goal is to provide clinicians with a structured framework for recognition and intervention and to highlight gaps that must be addressed to advance toward a future taxonomy and evidence-based treatment strategies. The clinical question guiding this review is: How should hypersexuality in neurological disorders be recognised, assessed, and managed in clinical practice?

Methods

The study protocol was preregistered on PROSPERO (CRD42023446088) and uploaded to OSF (https://osf.io/e9tqf). The review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA3 and SWiM4 guidelines.

We searched PubMed, PsycINFO, Web of Science, Scopus, CINAHL, and the China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) from database inception to February 26, 2025. Grey-literature sources recommended in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions and citation-tracking from a recent dissertation on the topic1 were also employed. The most comprehensive version of duplicates or overlapping reports were included.

In accordance with the PROSPERO protocol, eligibility criteria followed the PICO framework. The population (P) comprised adults (≥18 years) with neurological conditions associated with hypersexual or paraphilic behaviours. Eligible interventions (I) included both pharmacological and surgical treatments, as well as non-pharmacological approaches such as psychological, behavioural, or rehabilitative programmes. Studies were eligible regardless of whether they included a comparison group (C), and single-case reports were considered when sufficient clinical information was provided. The primary outcome (O) of interest was any measure or description of sexual behaviour or disinhibition. Studies of paedophilia were excluded given the availability of recent dedicated reviews, as were reviews, meta-analyses, editorials, and conference abstracts. Articles were eligible if published in English, Italian, Spanish, or Chinese. Given the marked heterogeneity of the literature, the PICO framework could not be applied uniformly across all studies but was used as a guiding structure for inclusion. 

Three raters independently screened records and extracted data, with disagreements resolved by consensus. Extracted variables included study design, sample characteristics, neurological diagnosis, clinical presentation of HY, assessment methods, interventions, and outcomes. We extracted participant sex (male/female) as reported in source articles and treated these labels as sex because gender identity was rarely reported and therefore could not be analysed.

Quality appraisal used validated tools chosen to match study design, allowing a tailored assessment of key bias domains. Two raters independently conducted the assessment, with disagreements resolved by a third rater. The Murad et al. scale was used for case reports and case series; the National Institutes of Health (NIH) quality assessment tools for cross-sectional and observational cohort studies; the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used for cohort and case–control studies; the Cochrane Risk-of-Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool for randomized controlled trials; and the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklists for qualitative research. Because these instruments use different items and summary approaches, and to facilitate clear presentation across diverse designs, we converted each study’s item-level responses into a harmonised overall rating with three categories: good (>75% “Yes” responses), fair (50–75% “Yes” responses), and poor (<50% “Yes” responses). For this mapping the denominator comprised the count of affirmative (Yes) plus negative (No) responses for the tool items; responses of “Cannot determine”, “Not reported”, or “Not applicable” were excluded from the denominator. Low study quality was not used as an exclusion criterion a priori; however, methodological limitations were explicitly considered when interpreting findings and formulating recommendations.

Given the heterogeneity in study designs, populations, outcomes, and reporting practices, we did not attempt a formal meta-analysis. Instead, we followed the SWiM guidelines to provide a structured narrative synthesis. Findings were grouped by aetiology, neurological disorder, clinical presentation, assessment method, and intervention type.

Results

The systematic search identified 1,660 records. After duplicate removal and title/abstract screening, 649 full-text articles were assessed, yielding 394 studies for inclusion.

The total sample comprised 46,516 participants, with a median sample size of seven per study (range: 1–3,090). Studies were conducted in 50 countries, most frequently in the United States (n = 116), Italy (n = 48), and France (n = 22), with further contributions from Australia, Germany, Spain, and the United Kingdom, and limited representation from low- and middle-income regions, suggesting geographical bias.

Case reports and case series accounted for most studies (n = 215, 54.6%), followed by cross-sectional (n = 105, 26.6%), observational cohort (n = 35, 8.9%), case–control (n = 28, 7.1%), cohort (n = 6, 1.5%), qualitative (n = 3, 0.8%), and randomised controlled trial designs (n = 2, 0.5%). Study quality varied by design. Overall, 57.4% of studies were rated fair, 24.6% good, and 18.1% poor.

Participant age ranged widely from young adulthood to very old age, but distributions were skewed toward late adulthood, particularly in neurodegenerative and movement-disorder cohorts. Younger adults were more commonly represented in single-case reports, especially following traumatic brain injury or focal lesions. 

Across studies providing sex-disaggregated data, hypersexuality was markedly more frequent in males. Male presentations were typically characterised by overt behavioural disinhibition and compulsive sexual behaviours, whereas female cases were rarer and more often involved sexual preoccupation or verbal/relational disinhibition.

MMSE scores were reported in 103 studies (26.1%). Of these, 64.1% reported mean or group scores ≥24, 27.2% <24, and 13.6% mixed or heterogeneous values, including longitudinal change. These findings indicate that hypersexuality occurs both with preserved cognition and in the context of cognitive decline.

Assessment methods were highly heterogeneous. Nearly half of studies (43.6%) relied solely on unstructured clinical history. Others used self-report instruments (16.0%), single clinical interviews or examinations (5.8%), combined history and examination (8.4%), or multimethod approaches (21.1%). Structured or semi-structured interviews were uncommon and typically embedded within multimethod assessments. Informant reports were explicitly included in 8.6% of studies. 

Hypersexuality was attributed to iatrogenic causes in 61.2% of studies, acquired neurological pathology or surgery in 25.4%, DBS-related mechanisms in 4.3%, and unclear or mixed aetiology in 9.1%.

Iatrogenic hypersexuality was most commonly associated with Parkinson’s disease (82% of pharmacological cases) and was strongly linked to dopaminergic therapies (87.1%). Symptoms typically emerged following medication initiation or adjustment and were characterised by sexual disinhibition and increased drive, often alongside other impulse-control symptoms. Management primarily involved medication reduction or discontinuation, frequently resulting in partial or complete remission; adjunctive pharmacological and supportive non-pharmacological measures were also reported.

Acquired hypersexuality most often occurred in dementia syndromes and traumatic brain injury, with onset typically years after disease onset. Presentations involved marked behavioural disinhibition and loss of social boundaries. Management targeted both the underlying neurological condition and hypersexual symptoms, using pharmacological and behavioural approaches, with mixed but often favourable individual outcomes.

DBS was reported both as a trigger of hypersexuality and as an indirect facilitator of improvement through dopaminergic dose reduction. Most cases involved subthalamic nucleus stimulation in Parkinson’s disease. Symptom improvement was reported following DBS reprogramming, medication adjustment, or targeted psychopharmacological treatment, though some cases required long-term management.

Conclusion

In this systematic review we investigated how HY in neurological disorders should be recognised, assessed, and managed. Across 394 studies, HY arose through multiple, sometimes interacting, pathways.

Overall, HY appears aetiologically and phenomenologically heterogeneous yet mechanistically convergent on dysfunction within a fronto-striatal-limbic network that governs reward valuation, inhibition, and social behaviour5. Parkinson’s disease contributed the largest share of cases, though this may reflect either true susceptibility or ascertainment bias given intensive research on impulse-control disorders in PD.

Iatrogenic HY typically shows an abrupt temporal relationship to drug initiation or dose escalation, frequently co-occurs with other impulse-control disorders, and is often reversible after dose reduction or withdrawal. This pattern aligns with models in which excessive D2/D3 stimulation in ventral striatal circuitry increases reward salience and impulsive approach behaviour while cortical inhibitory control is only partially preserved. 

In contrast, acquired HY associated with focal lesions, traumatic brain injury, or neurodegenerative disease reflects chronic disruption of orbitofrontal, ventromedial, and/or temporal components of fronto-striatal-limbic circuits. These presentations tend to be more persistent and are often marked by behavioural disinhibition, sometimes including paraphilic or coercive acts, and typically require combined disease-directed and symptom-focused interventions.

DBS occupies a distinct bidirectional position. Subthalamic nucleus stimulation -especially in interaction with residual dopaminergic tone- may precipitate or worsen HY and impulsivity, while reprogramming and medication tapering can restore behavioural equilibrium. This duality complicates perioperative risk-benefit evaluation but underscores the potential reversibility of stimulation-related behavioural effects via parameter optimisation.

Acute inflammatory or metabolic causes can produce rapid-onset HY tightly coupled to neurological decompensation and often resolving with immunotherapy or metabolic correction, illustrating how transient limbic excitability disturbances can mimic more persistent disinhibitory syndromes.

Across these aetiologies, presentations appear to converge on two complementary processes: reduced top-down executive control from prefrontal systems and hyperactivation of mesolimbic reward pathways, yielding impaired inhibitory gating over reward-seeking drives5. Clinically, this may support a disexecutive model in which diminished prefrontal restraint interacts with heightened subcortical reward responsivity. 

Demographic and clinical moderators shape risk and phenotype2. HY is commonly reported in older adults but also occurs in younger adults after TBI or focal lesions, indicating age is not protective. A pronounced male predominance likely reflects a mixture of disease prevalence, biological sensitivity, and ascertainment bias, with probable under-reporting in women and older adults. Cognitive status is heterogeneous: HY can occur with preserved global cognition, suggesting executive-salience network dysfunction may be more relevant than global decline. Standardised reporting of sex/gender, age, cognitive and executive measures, and exposure metrics is essential for refined risk models.

Assessment practices were often limited: roughly half relied on unstructured notes, risking under-ascertainment and misclassification. No brief generic tool currently integrates detection, first-pass aetiological triage, and guidance for definitive assessment. 

Future work should prioritise consensus definitions and validated instruments, large longitudinal cohorts, controlled treatment studies, mechanistic neuroimaging/computational modelling, and caregiver/system outcomes, supported by harmonised registries and international data sharing.
