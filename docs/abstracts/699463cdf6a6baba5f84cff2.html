Introduction

Measurement of functional connectivity (FC) via fMRI currently dominates among non-invasive techniques for characterizing functional brain organization in humans. Standard methods estimate the FC between two brain regions of interest (ROIs) by computing the Pearson's correlation coefficient between their corresponding time series. Higher resting state functional connectivity (rsFC) indicates membership in the same functional network, and computing all pairwise rsFC estimates across the brain constructs an FC map that enables identifying intrinsic brain networks.

Motion effects in fMRI data can compromise FC estimates and consequently, conclusions from downstream FC analyses. Extensive studies have demonstrated that these effects survive regression-based denoising approaches and have recommended combining methods that regress out motion effects and methods that censor motion-degraded volumes.1 The latter category includes scrubbing, which identifies and deletes motion-corrupted volumes according to some motion (such as framewise displacement) or signal change threshold. 

In any study, scrubbing intensity differs across subjects regardless of the scrubbing method employed due to varying levels of motion contamination. Therefore, a multi-subject FC analysis on scrubbed data compares sample correlations computed from different numbers of time points. Fewer time points implies greater uncertainty, and equalizing the contributions of FC estimates with varying levels of uncertainty can attenuate power or induce bias in statistical analyses. 

Recently, Afyouni et al. developed xDF, a method that enables calibrating FC estimates by computing their standard errors and accommodates both autocorrelation between time points in the same time series or induced cross-correlation between time points in different time series.2 However, xDF does not recognize non-contiguous time points, so we extended their work to encompass the missing data setting.

Methods

We used data from two harmonized sub-studies of the Human Connectome Project: HCP-D and HCP-YA.3,4 We obtained ICA-FIX-denoised volumetric resting state time series from the 100 Unrelated Subjects dataset provided by the HCP (1200 volumes each). We additionally performed global signal regression (GSR) and parcellated the data using the refinement of Yeo’s 17-networks parcellation comprising 114 cortical ROIs. 

We preprocessed resting state fMRI data from HCP-D subjects using fMRIPrep and postprocessed the fMRIPrep outputs using XCP-D, specifying the 4S parcellations. Our final HCP-D subset comprised 625 subjects (478 volumes each). In our scrubbing experiments on HCP-D data, we censored volumes exceeding a framewise displacement (FD) threshold of 0.2 mm. 

The original xDF method (hereafter referred to as scrubbing-naïve xDF) derives a formula for the standard error of an FC estimate in terms of the estimated auto- and cross-correlation matrices of the time series. We borrowed its theoretical framework and incorporated additional steps that estimate lagged auto-and correlation coefficients in time series with missing time points. Our approach, hereafter referred to as scrubbing-aware xDF, is computationally comparable to scrubbing-naïve xDF.

We first compared the type I error control of scrubbing-naïve and scrubbing-aware xDF standard errors by simulating pairs of uncorrelated AR(1) time series (where the true FC is 0, so any discovery constitutes a false positive) and randomly downsampling them to different numbers of retained time points. 

We also compared the type I error control of scrubbing-naïve and scrubbing-aware xDF standard errors on the 100 Unrelated Subjects subgroup from HCP-YA by adapting the inter-subject scrambling experiment described in Afyouni et al.2 Since we do not expect any dependency between time series derived from unrelated subjects, we assumed all significant inter-subject sample correlation coefficients to be false positives. 

We conducted additional experiments testing for associations between xDF squared standard errors and age and sex in our 625 HCP-D subjects to probe the effects of these covariates on intra-subject rsFC variability in early development. We justified these experiments by observing that an xDF squared standard error estimates the variability in the correlation coefficient if we repeatedly acquire time series while maintaining the conditions that determine the temporal auto- and cross-correlation structures. Under the assumption of static functional connectivity, this approximates intra-subject scan-rescan rsFC reliability. 

For each of our 625 HCP-D subjects, we estimated the rsFC between all pairs of ROIs in the 4S-Schaefer 1000 parcellation, before and after scrubbing, the standard errors of the rsFC estimates from unscrubbed time series using scrubbing-naïve xDF, and the standard errors of the rsFC estimates from scrubbed time series using scrubbing-aware xDF. First, for each pair of regions in the 4S-Schaefer 1000 parcellation, we regressed the (scrubbing-naïve) xDF squared standard errors of the unscrubbed rsFC estimates from the 625 HCP-D subjects against their age, sex, and mean FD (of all 478 volumes). Then, we repeated the same regressions on scrubbed data, where we specified the scrubbing-aware xDF squared standard errors of the scrubbed rsFC estimates multiplied by the proportion of retained volumes as the dependent variable and the mean FD of the retained volumes as the motion covariate.

Results

The results of our experiments on type I error control corroborate our hypothesis that overestimation of standard errors by scrubbing-naïve xDF induces spurious rsFC signals in settings where none should exist, such as between simulated, uncorrelated time series or time series from different subjects. Scrubbing-aware xDF maintains lower type I error rate than scrubbing-naïve xDF across different levels of scrubbing intensity in our simulations involving uncorrelated AR(1) processes. As expected, this difference manifests more dramatically at higher levels of scrubbing. For example, when we retain only 15% (300) of the time points, scrubbing-aware xDF achieves a 0.056 type I error rate while scrubbing-naïve xDF produces 0.072. 

On scrambled HCP-YA data, the type I error rate decreases for both scrubbing-naïve and scrubbing-aware xDF as the scrubbing intensity decreases, with the former exhibiting higher type I error rates across all intensities. For example, when we retain only 100 volumes, their type I error rates equal 0.070 and 0.062, respectively. When we retain 1100 volumes, their type I error rates converge at approximately 0.061. Thus, scrubbing-aware xDF exhibits remarkable robustness and stabilizes the type I error rate at approximately 0.06 even when fewer than 10% of time points remain. This suggests that scrubbing-aware xDF, unlike scrubbing-naïve xDF, guarantees adequate type I error control, barring a level of scrubbing beyond which overly sparse data precludes accurately estimating auto- and cross-correlation structure. 

For our association tests, among the 338 4S-Schaefer 1000 atlas regions in the left hemisphere with available rs-fMRI data for all 625 HCP-D subjects, our analyses on unscrubbed data (Figure 1(a)) reveal age effects on intra-subject rsFC reliability throughout the brain (6.5% of brain region pairs), in addition to a large block of positive effect in the visual network (34.7% positive) and a smaller block of negative effect in the somatomotor network (14.3% negative). On scrubbed data, the global signal diminishes while the two blocks of signal in the visual and, to a lesser extent, somatomotor networks persist (Figure 1(b)). We hypothesize that the scattered, bidirectional signal pervading Figure 1(a) constitute primarily false discoveries because they do not delineate reasonable brain network structure patterns. This suggests that scrubbing effectively reduces the false discovery rate, removing spurious signal while preserving true signal. These latter observations suggest that in youth and adolescence, intra-subject scan-rescan reliability in within-network connectivity increases in the visual network but decreases in the somatomotor network. The sharpening effect of scrubbing-aware xDF in Figure 1 supports its potential for parsing trajectories in functional connectivity during development and aging. 

Our association tests on unscrubbed data reveal global sex effects on intra-subject rsFC variability, detecting higher intra-subject rsFC reliability in males for 29.2% of brain region pairs. However, on scrubbed data, fewer than 0.1% of brain region pairs survive FDR thresholding. The dramatic difference between the results of these two sets of association tests highlights the contribution of image quality and motion to intra-subject rsFC reliability and the need to carefully formulate analyses to disentangle these effects from studies probing biological factors and rsFC.

Conclusion

We have extended the work of Afyouni et al. by developing scrubbing-aware xDF, which enables estimating the standard error of a sample correlation coefficient in the presence of missing data (in addition to non-trivial auto- and cross-correlation structures).2 Given the impact of motion corruption on fMRI data, this contribution bridges an important theoretical gap by accepting scrubbed time series. In addition, it is straightforward to implement and parallelizable, which ensures efficient computation.

In this paper, we have demonstrated that scrubbing-aware xDF controls the type I error rate more effectively than scrubbing-naïve xDF, which inflates estimates of autocorrelation coefficients for scrubbed time series with non-contiguous time points.

Moreover, the squared standard error estimated by xDF encodes interpretable information for neuroscientific research. Previous works have utilized scan-rescan data to characterize intra-subject variability in rsFC estimates across the brain, and xDF extracts something similar from cross-sectional studies.5 The results of our association tests in this paper indicate that standard errors for rsFC estimates computed from scrubbed time series can illuminate interesting findings that might be obscured by unscrubbed, motion-contaminated fMRI data. This suggests that scrubbing-aware xDF contributes a valuable ingredient for continued work on understanding intra-subject reliability in functional connectivity without requiring scan-rescan data.
