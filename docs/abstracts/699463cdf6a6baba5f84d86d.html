Introduction

Pain imagination—the mental simulation of pain without sensory input—engages many of the same cortical systems involved in nociception and affective processing. Imagination shapes human behavior, enhancing empathy, planning, and recovery, but can also intensify pain vulnerability through internally generated sensory predictions. Previous studies have shown partial overlap between imagined and real pain, yet none have mapped this relationship at the fine-grained, body site–specific level. Using precision functional MRI and a deep-phenotyping design (>7 hours scanning per participant; N = 9), we examined how imagined and experienced pain differ in body site representation and multivariate structure across nociceptive and transmodal brain systems.

Methods

DESIGN. Multiple N-of-1 within-subject design, featuring three conditions: 1. Hot-Stimulation (~46-49°C), 2. Imagined Hot-Stimulation and 3. Warm Stimulation (<44°C). Stimulation trials were repeated 8 times per run, and imagination trials were repeated 12 times per run. Each run focused on one of 8 pseudorandomized body sites (left/right face, left/right arm, left/right leg, chest, and abdomen). Temperatures for each body site were calibrated per participant prior to scanning.

PARTICIPANTS. Participants were 9 subjects (5 male, 4 female). Each subject underwent at least 4 runs per session, and returned for at least 10 session days, totaling at least 40 runs per subject.

Results

Imagined pain recruits a distributed network that mimics—but does not replicate—nociceptive somatotopy. This dissociation reveals how the brain simulates pain through abstract, transmodal systems, clarifying neural mechanisms of empathy and pain vulnerability.

Despite a small-n design, reliable-within-subject maps can produce group-level results that uncover important relationships between verum experiences of pain and mental simulations of and/or recollections of pain. Precision functional mapping revealed that imagined pain engaged distributed transmodal and affective-control systems without reactivating body site–specific nociceptive codes. 

•Overlap but distinction: Both imagined and experienced pain activated canonical pain regions (insula, SMA, anterior cingulate), but only experienced pain showed body site–specific somatotopy in S1/S2 and dpIns.

•Distributed similarity: Representational similarity analyses revealed shared multivariate structure across widespread cortical and subcortical regions, including dorsomedial and lateral PFC, hippocampus, and thalamus.

•Signature dissociation: The Neurologic Pain Signature (NPS) responded robustly to experienced pain, whereas the Stimulus-Independent Pain Signature (SIIPS-1) was expressed during imagination, reflecting affective-cognitive rather than nociceptive processing.

•Neurotransmitter mapping: Experienced pain correlated with dopaminergic, serotonergic, and opioid receptor maps; imagined pain showed weaker dopaminergic and cholinergic coupling and lacked histaminergic involvement.

•Principal components: PC1 captured a somatosensory nociceptive axis (hot > warm), while PC2 reflected cognitive-control and linguistic elaboration (imagine > warm).

Imagination evokes distributed pain-like activity across transmodal networks but fails to reinstate body site–specific nociceptive maps. This dissociation—between nociceptive somatotopy and cognitive-affective simulation—clarifies why imagined pain feels less vivid and more effortful than real pain.

Conclusion

 Findings suggest that pain imagination draws on abstract, integrative systems for simulation and empathy, rather than direct sensory reactivation, bridging sensory neuroscience with cognitive and social models of pain. This sensory–cognitive dissociation clarifies how the brain simulates pain—illuminating mechanisms of empathy, mental imagery, and vulnerability to chronic pain. This study provides the first evidence that body site specificity discriminating hot from warm stimulation is found along all regions of the pain-pathway, and these regions along the pathway do not recapitulate that mapping when the pain is imagined.
