Introduction

Reward responsiveness, the capacity to respond emotionally to rewards, forms one of three positive valence systems defined by the National Institute of Mental Health Research Domain Criteria. Many psychiatric disorders involve blunted or maladapted reward responsiveness as a central transdiagnostic component, often linked to differences in reward-related activation of the ventral striatum.1 Given that many of these disorders emerge before reaching adulthood,2 there is a need to identify early environmental factors that influence developmental trajectories of neural and behavioural reward responsiveness in adolescence. Experiencing adversities in childhood, such as abuse, witnessing violence, and poor-quality caregiver relationships, shapes brain development3 and significantly increases the risk of psychopathology in later life.4 In the present study, we examined associations between data-driven dimensions of adversity previously identified in the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) study,5 potential resilience factors and reward responsiveness across early adolescence. We hypothesised that deprivation-related adversity factors would be negatively associated with reward responsiveness. We also hypothesised that resilience factors (prosocial behaviour and school positivity) would buffer against the reward blunting effects of adversity.

Methods

Using three timepoints from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) study (n=11868), we used longitudinal mixed effects models to examine associations of 10 adversity factors with self-reported behavioural and neural reward responsiveness across ages 9-14. Models included fixed effects for age, sex, and race-ethnicity. We operationalised early adversity using 10 previously derived factors of co-occurring adversity,5 which use 60 items across parent and child report surveys. Behavioural reward responsiveness was measured using the behavioural inhibition/behavioural activation (BIS/BAS) “Reward Responsiveness” subscale. Neural reward responsiveness was measured using data from an fMRI monetary incentive delay task (Figure 1), collected repeatedly at the same waves as BIS/BAS, focusing on three a priori striatal subregions: the nucleus accumbens, caudate nucleus and putamen. We used two contrasts from this task: large reward vs no reward during the anticipatory phase, and during the consummatory phase. The resilience factors “prosocial behaviour” and “positive school environment” were measured at baseline using the prosocial behaviour scale from the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire, and PhenX School Risk and Protective Factors Survey, respectively. For the BIS/BAS models, two random effects were included to capture clustering within individuals and families. For the neural models, an additional random effect was included for data collection site, to account for variability between MRI scanners. Additional individual-level random slopes were tested to model individual variation in change over time. These improved model fit for the BIS/BAS model (p<0.001), but were omitted from the neural model as their inclusion prevented model convergence. To investigate whether potential resilience factors of prosocial behaviour and protective school environment may buffer the association between adversity and reward responsiveness, we tested interactions between these variables and adversity factors that were negatively associated with reward responsiveness. In order to assess whether the impact of adversity factors changed over age, we also tested interactions between reward-blunting adversity factors with age and age squared.

Results

Several early adversity factors were associated with BIS/BAS reward responsiveness. Three deprivation-related adversity factors were negatively associated with BIS/BAS reward responsiveness: lack of supervision (β=-0.104, p<0.001), lack of support from primary caregiver (β=-0.076, p<0.001), and lack of support from secondary caregiver (β=-0.205, p<0.001). One threat-related factor, youth report of family conflict, was positively associated with reward responsiveness (β=0.167, p<0.001). Of the adversity factors negatively associated with reward responsiveness, there was marginal evidence for an interaction between age 9/10 prosocial behaviour and lack of supervision (β=0.033,  p=0.05) such that the association was weaker when the prosocial behaviour scores were higher. There was no other evidence of an interaction between resilience factors and reward-blunting adversities. Lack of supervision was also negatively associated with neural anticipatory reward responsiveness in the nucleus accumbens (Figure 2) (β=-0.010, p=0.005) and putamen (β=-0.005, p=0.03). The threat-related factor family aggression was positively associated with anticipatory reward responsiveness in the nucleus accumbens (β=0.008, p=0.01). Lack of support from secondary caregiver was positively associated with consummatory reward activation of the putamen (β=0.008, p=0.01). We tested interactions for the deprivation-related adversity factors lack of supervision and primary caregiver lack of support with age in years (and age squared). For both factors, we observed a significant interaction (p<0.001), which suggests the associations with BIS/BAS attenuate with age for primary caregiver lack of support, and strengthen with age for lack of supervision.

Conclusion

We provide the first large-scale evidence that early co-occurring deprivation adversities are associated with blunted neural and behavioural reward responsiveness in early adolescence, and that early co-occurring threat adversities are associated with the opposite. We found limited evidence of an interaction between resilience factors, prosocial behaviour or school positivity, and early deprivation. Age-adversity interactions suggest that the effects of different forms of early deprivation on reward responsiveness are not equal.
