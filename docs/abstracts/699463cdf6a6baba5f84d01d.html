Introduction

Emerging evidence suggests that the autistic brain is pharmacologically atypical. We have previously shown that acute drug challenges on multiple neurosignalling systems, including the serotonin system, produce differential effects on functional brain connectivity in autistic and non-autistic individuals1. This has implications for the use of serotonergic compounds in autism, e.g. for mental health conditions, such as depression, which are highly prevalent but sub-optimally managed in the autistic population2. Psilocybin is a classic serotonergic psychedelic that has demonstrated considerable potential in the field of psychiatry. It has been proposed that psilocybin exerts its (beneficial) behavioural effects through the 5-HT2A receptor and by modulating whole-brain functional connectivity3,4. However, we have previously shown not only that brain responsivity to serotonergic modulation is distinct1 but also that there are differences specifically in 5-HT2A receptor density in autism5. Therefore, we hypothesised that autistic and non-autistic individuals would respond differently to a pharmacological challenge using the 5-HT2A receptor agonist psilocybin.

Methods

We tested this hypothesis in a sample of 30 non-autistic (15 females) and 37 autistic (18 females) participants. We employed a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, crossover study design to investigate the differential group effects of two acute low doses of psilocybin (2 mg and 5 mg COMP360 psilocybin, a proprietary synthetic formulation of crystalline psilocybin owned by Compass Pathways plc, London, United Kingdom) on weighted degree centrality (wDC), a network-level measure of resting-state functional connectivity. Statistical analysis was conducted on a voxel-wise level. The main effects of group and drug as well as the effect of their interaction on wDC were estimated in linear mixed effects models (LMM) using restricted maximum likelihood, with a random intercept for each subject. Another set of LMM’s was fitted to investigate within-group drug effects (separately in each group). To improve cluster inference, the resulting whole-brain, raw t-statistic images (from each comparison) were passed through probabilistic threshold-free cluster enhancement to produce voxel-wise Z- and p-value estimates representing the amount of cluster-like local spatial support. The ‘enhanced’ voxel-wise p-values were then corrected for multiple comparisons using FWER Gaussian Random Field Theory-based maximum height thresholding (corrected a = 0.05). Any contiguous group of voxels that remained statistically significant after correction was considered a cluster.

Results

We observed group by drug interactions at both doses, characterised by relative wDC increases in the autistic group in DMN regions (at 2 mg and 5 mg) as well as broader, multi-network increases (at 5 mg). Post-hoc, within-group analyses revealed psilocybin elevated wDC of sensorimotor and attention regions (2 mg), and DMN regions (5 mg) in the neurotypical group. Following psilocybin administration, autistic individuals had increases in DMN wDC even with the lower 2 mg dose, and increases at the higher 5 mg dose across multiple networks (predominantly DMN and fronto-parietal), including marked increases in sub-cortical (thalamus, striatum) and cerebellar wDC.

Conclusion

Our findings add to a growing body of literature suggesting that the autistic brain has a distinct pharmacological responsivity profile. Our work does not speak directly to clinical applications but may well have implications for dose choice in the context of depression.
