Introduction

Cough is a defence mechanism that protects the airways and lungs often in response to airway irritation. It is usually preceded by an urge-to-cough (UTC), which is a sensory experience 1,2. Persistent cough and an insatiable UTC can have an adverse effect on an individual’s quality of life. Previous neuroanatomical and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies in animals and humans have identified sensory and motor networks associated with cough and UTC 3. In addition, studies in humans have investigated placebo-related modulation of UTC and cough and associated regional brain responses 4. Notably, the outcomes of these studies indicate commonalities between pain and cough in the neuroanatomical regions involved in modulation of the two sensations. This commonality of functional neuroanatomy provides an impetus to investigate other pain-modulating processes in the context of responses to tussive stimuli. One prospective form of airway afferent modulation to investigate is conditioned pain modulation (CPM). CPM is a mechanism whereby pain in one part of body inhibits pain in another region of the body 5. In this study, we investigated the behavioural and regional brain responses during inhalation of tussive stimuli and pain-conditioning in healthy participants.

Methods

Sixteen healthy participants (mean age mean ± 24.94 ± SD 4.39 years) inhaled graduated levels of the tussive stimulus capsaicin to determine the concentration that elicited two spontaneous coughs (recorded as the C2 threshold). Pressure was applied in increments of 0.79 kg/cm2 to the left thumbnail to identify the stimulus level that elicited a moderate rate of pain intensity (rating of 5 on an 11-point numerical pain rating scale). The CPM effect was determined by the contrast of UTC ratings on an 11-point numerical rating scale and counts of audible coughs in response to inhalations of capsaicin at C2 and C2+2 (two doubling concentration levels above C2) during the application of moderately painful stimuli or the absence of thumbnail pressure. The maximum capsaicin concentration (Smax) that could be inhaled without coughing was recorded. The Smax was used to evoke an UTC and the recorded moderate painful stimulus was used to elicit a CPM effect during the fMRI session. UTC ratings were recorded under both contingencies. Statistical analysis using SPSS (ANOVA & Paired t-test) was performed to determine whether painful stimuli had an effect on UTC ratings and cough frequencies. During the fMRI session, brain responses were obtained for capsaicin inhalation without painful stimulation (CN) and during the application of painful stimuli (CP). Participants also inhaled saline with no pain (SN) and saline with pain (SP). Planned contrasts of CN>SN and CN>CP were intended to identify the network of responses associated with tussive stimulation, and the influence of pain-conditioning on these responses.

Results

Pain-conditioning significantly reduced both UTC ratings (P<0.001) and cough frequency (P<0.001). The average reduction for UTC ratings was 16%, and the number of coughs reduced by 44% across the two doses of capsaicin. fMRI results showed that capsaicin activations (CN>SN) were observed within previously known regions of the brain (figure 2 “CN>SN”). From the defined regional activations for CN>SN, painful conditioning (CN>CP) was associated with widespread changes in regional brain responses to capsaicin inhalation (attached figure). 

Conclusion

The novel outcomes of this study indicate that painful conditioning stimuli reduces UTC and cough frequency in response to inhalation of capsaicin. In addition, this reduction is associated with widespread changes in regional brain responses to tussive stimulation. These results point toward down regulation of airway afferent inputs into the brain by nociceptive stimuli. These outcomes may have implications for the development of therapies to reduce unwanted coughing.
