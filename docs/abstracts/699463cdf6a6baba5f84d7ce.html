Introduction

The central amygdala (CeA) is a major convergence point for emotional, interoceptive, and autonomic signals; however, its structural wiring is not yet fully defined. To clarify its anatomical organization, we mapped the structural connectivity of the CeA in humans using high-resolution diffusion MRI and in mice using anterograde tracing data from the Allen Mouse Connectivity Atlas.

Methods

Diffusion MRI scans from 730 participants in the Human Connectome Project (Van Essen et al., 2012) were analyzed using multi-shell BEDPOST modeling (Jbabdi et al., 2012; Jenkinson et al., 2012), and probabilistic tractography was performed with PROBTRACKX (Behrens et al., 2007). 

The CeA seed region was defined using the CIT168 atlas (Pauli et al., 2018), non-linearly registered to each subject’s diffusion space, and the resulting left and right native-space seeds were used as inputs for subject-specific probabilistic tractography.  Subject-level tractography maps were subsequently transformed to MNI space, where fixed-effect group connectivity probability maps were generated. Anatomical assignment of group-level connectivity was performed using the HCP XTRACT, Harvard–Oxford, and Brainstem Navigator atlases.

For the mouse dataset, the ten CeA injections with the largest volumes, as determined by the Allen Mouse Connectivity Atlas (Kuan et al., 2015), were aggregated to construct 3D projection-density maps of efferent pathways. Projection densities were quantified and visualized using the Allen API.

Results

The CeA exhibited widespread structural connectivity across cortical, subcortical, brainstem, and cerebellar regions. White matter tracts most strongly associated included the fornix (7.21), optic radiation (6.34), and inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (5.15), supporting CeA’s role in multisensory and cognitive integration. Cortical connectivity was highest with subcallosal cortex (0.64), anterior cingulate (0.52), parahippocampal gyrus (1.47), and occipital regions, suggesting integration of emotion, memory, and visual inputs. Sensorimotor associations showed moderate probabilities with the primary motor (M1: 0.22) and somatosensory cortex (S1: 0.14), reflecting involvement in motor-autonomic integration. Subcortical connections were strongest to the thalamus (5.95), hippocampus (3.20), and amygdala (3.06), aligning with limbic circuitry. Cerebellar projections were sparse but present, with low-probability connections to Crus II and Lobule IX, reflecting minor cerebellar integration. Brainstem nuclei exhibited strong overlap with key autonomic and affective centers, including the Parvicellular reticular nucleus, alpha part (PCRtA) (65.2%), lateral parabrachial nucleus LPB (61.4%), dorsal raphe DR (60.5%), Nucleus raphe magnus RMg (56.7%), periaqueductal gray PAG (31.9%), and Locus Coeruleus LC (28.6%), supporting CeA’s role in arousal, pain, and visceral regulation. The structural connectivity maps in the mouse also show widespread projection patterns to different cortical and subcortical areas. 

Conclusion

Our findings reveal that the CeA exhibits extensive structural connectivity with cortical, subcortical, and brainstem regions, consistent with its known role as an integrative hub for emotional, autonomic, and interoceptive processing. Notably, high-probability connections with the PAG, PBN, NTS, DMV, and LC support previous tracer-based studies highlighting CeA’s involvement in regulating visceral pain, arousal, and parasympathetic control (Becker and Carrasquillo, 2019; Saper, 2002; Tovote et al., 2015). CeA’s Structural links to the brainstem vagal complex (NTS, DMV) and sensorimotor cortex may functionally associate with its ability to modulate gut motility, receive visceral feedback, and shape behavior through internal bodily states (Han et al., 2018; Rinaman, 2007). In the mouse brain, anterograde tracer data reveal similarly broad projection patterns.  A key limitation of diffusion tractography is that it provides an indirect measure of water diffusion along fiber pathways, which does not reflect actual axonal projections with the accuracy of tracer studies and is therefore susceptible to false positives and anatomical misinterpretation.
