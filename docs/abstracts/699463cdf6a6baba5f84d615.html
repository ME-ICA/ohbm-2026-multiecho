Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) exhibits substantial age-related heterogeneity in clinical presentation, treatment response, and disease trajectory, with adolescent-onset MDD associated with poorer antidepressant response and a more severe long-term course than adult-onset MDD 1. Neuroimaging studies have revealed both shared and age-specific abnormalities in functional and structural brain networks across developmental stages; however, prior structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) research has largely focused on regional morphology within single age groups, overlooking interregional covariance and age-related network interactions 2. Individualized structural covariance networks (iSCNs) provide a framework for characterizing gray matter connectome organization and inter-individual heterogeneity, and have revealed disrupted connectivity and topology associated with clinical symptoms and transcriptional patterns in MDD. Yet, whether adolescent and adult MDD share convergent or exhibit distinct patterns of iSCN disruption remains unclear. Addressing this gap is essential for understanding the neurodevelopmental mechanisms underlying age-related heterogeneity in MDD and for informing early-stage interventions.

Methods

Structural MRI data were obtained from 1,057 participants, including 504 first-episode, drug-naïve MDD patients and 553 healthy controls (HCs), recruited from three independent datasets across two sites. The sample comprised adolescents (12-18 years) and adults (19-64 years). Depressive symptom severity of patients was evaluated using the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale. High-resolution T1-weighted images were preprocessed using standard procedures and gray matter volume (GMV) maps were segmented. ComBat harmonization was applied to minimize site-related effects. Individualized structural covariance networks were constructed by parcellating the brain into 246 regions using the Brainnetome atlas. For each region, GMV probability distributions were estimated using probability density functions (PDFs) with a Gaussian kernel. Pairwise similarity between the PDFs was quantified using the Kullback-Leibler divergence, which was then transformed into Kullback-Leibler similarity scores ranging from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater similarity in the probability distributions of GMV between pairs of regions 3. Thus, an undirected 246 × 246 weighted iSCN was constructed for each participant. The similarity values were defined as network edges. Group differences in iSCN connectivity were identified using the network-based statistic, and the anatomical distribution of altered subnetworks was characterized by quantifying intra- and inter-lobar connectivity patterns. Raw edge counts (Nraw) were defined as the total number of edges within and between lobes, while normalized counts (Nnorm) were calculated as the proportion of edges relative to the total possible number of connections within or between the corresponding lobes, thereby accounting for differences in lobe size. To determine whether the altered connectivity patterns were accompanied by regional topological abnormalities, graph-theoretical analyses were conducted to assess nodal topological properties, including degree and nodal efficiency. Between-group differences were evaluated using nonparametric permutation tests (5,000 iterations), controlling for age, gender, and total intracranial volume. To examine age-related effects, pooled analyses assessed the main effects of age group, MDD diagnosis, and their interaction on connectivity and nodal topology using analysis of covariance models. Age was additionally modeled as a continuous variable using generalized linear regression to characterize linear age-related trajectories within MDD and HC groups. Functional annotation of altered subnetworks was performed based on Neurosynth-derived functional mappings, and the clinical relevance of altered connectivity was examined using partial correlation analyses with depressive symptom severity. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 after false discovery rate correction.

Results

Two aberrant subnetworks were identified in adolescent MDD patients relative to adolescent HCs. One subnetwork comprised 190 increased edges among 95 nodes, distributed primarily within subcortical regions (Nraw = 22; Nnorm = 0.035) and between subcortical regions and other lobes, particularly the frontal (Nraw = 43; Nnorm = 0.018) and temporal (Nraw = 24; Nnorm = 0.012) lobes. After adjusting for differences in lobe size, increased connectivity was most prominent within the insular lobe (Nnorm = 0.061; Nraw = 4), followed by connectivity between the subcortical and limbic lobes (Nnorm = 0.040; Nraw = 20). The other subnetwork included 198 decreased edges among 77 nodes, mainly within the temporal lobe (Nraw = 20; Nnorm = 0.013), between the temporal and parietal lobes (Nraw = 19; Nnorm = 0.009), and between the temporal lobe and subcortical regions (Nraw = 19; Nnorm = 0.009). Normalized counts further highlighted decreased connectivity within the limbic lobe (Nnorm = 0.022; Nraw = 2) and subcortical regions (Nnorm = 0.022; Nraw = 14) (Figure 2A). Adolescent MDD patients also showed significant nodal topological alterations, characterized by reduced degree and efficiency in the left parahippocampal gyrus and right postcentral gyrus, alongside increased nodal efficiency in the orbital gyrus and thalamic regions (p < 0.05, FDR corrected) (Figure 2B). In adults with MDD, a single subnetwork with increased connectivity (75 edges among 52 nodes) was identified, predominantly involving insular connections with occipital (Nraw = 13; Nnorm = 0.042), subcortical (Nraw = 11; Nnorm = 0.030), and frontal (Nraw = 10; Nnorm = 0.012) regions, as well as increased intra-insular connectivity (Nnorm = 0.030; Nraw = 2) (Figure 2A). Although widespread nodal alterations were observed at an uncorrected level, no nodal topological differences survived FDR correction (Figure 2B). Importantly, significant diagnosis × age group interactions were identified in connectivity linking subcortical regions with frontal and temporal lobes, within the temporal lobe, and within insular and insular-limbic circuits. These interactions were characterized by decreased connectivity in adolescent MDD patients but increased connectivity in adult MDD patients relative to HCs, driven mainly by pronounced reductions in adolescents (Cohen’s d = −0.449, p < 0.001, FDR corrected) (Figure 2C). Consistently, when age was modeled as a continuous variable, MDD patients exhibited increasing connectivity with age (β = 0.0003, p = 0.004), whereas HCs showed a gradual decline (β = −0.0002, p = 0.022). Diagnosis × age group interactions on nodal degree and efficiency were observed in the orbital gyrus, postcentral gyrus, insula, and thalamus (p < 0.05, FDR corrected), with opposite developmental patterns between adolescent and adult MDD patients. Functional annotation indicated that increased connectivity in adolescent MDD was predominantly associated with emotional face processing, social cognition, and perceptual functions (p < 0.05, FDR corrected), whereas increased connectivity in adult MDD was linked to action observation (p < 0.05, uncorrected). In adolescents, depressive symptom severity was negatively correlated with connectivity involving the dorsal granular insula, cuneus, superior frontal gyrus, and nucleus accumbens, whereas no significant symptom-connectivity associations were observed in adults (Figure 2D).

Conclusion

Our study delineates both shared and age-specific alterations in gray matter structural covariance networks in drug-naïve first-episode MDD, extending prior reports of age-related functional network abnormalities and providing convergent evidence for a neurodevelopmentally modulated structural connectome pathology 4. Across adolescents and adults, we identified a common pattern of excessive frontal-insular-subcortical covariance, with the medial prefrontal cortex, insula, and thalamus emerging as convergent hubs. This shared hypercovariance likely represents a core structural substrate of MDD independent of age stage, reflecting abnormal synchronization of gray matter maturation within circuits critical for integrating interoceptive, emotional, executive, and motivational processes. Additionally, adolescent MDD was characterized by age-specific reductions in temporoparietal-limbic-subcortical covariance and accompanied by nodal topological disruptions. These findings suggest a disruption or delay in the maturation of associative and limbic networks during this critical developmental window, supporting the delayed or altered brain maturation hypothesis of adolescent depression 5. Reduced nodal centrality in regions implicated in memory encoding and somatosensory integration, alongside increased centrality in orbitofrontal and thalamic regions involved in affective and executive regulation, indicates an imbalance between network integration and segregation. This topological reconfiguration may underlie the heightened emotional reactivity, impaired cognitive control, and symptom severity observed in adolescent MDD, as further supported by symptom-related reductions in insula-cuneus and frontal-nucleus accumbens connectivity. Diagnosis-by-age interaction analyses further revealed divergent developmental trajectories of structural covariance and network topology in MDD. Adolescents exhibited attenuated or reduced covariance relative to controls, whereas adults showed relative increases, with continuous-age analyses demonstrating increasing covariance with age in MDD but a normative decline in healthy individuals. These opposing trajectories suggest that MDD distorts normal age-related reorganization of structural networks, potentially transitioning from delayed or weakened structural coordination in adolescence to maladaptive over-synchronization and network rigidity in adulthood. Functional annotation highlighted age-dependent differences in the cognitive relevance of altered subnetworks: adolescent-specific covariance changes were mainly linked to emotional face processing, social cognition, and perception, whereas alterations in adult patients were associated with action observation. These findings indicate that while frontal-insular-subcortical hypercovariance constitutes a shared anatomical substrate of MDD, its functional embedding and clinical significance differ across developmental stages. Together, these findings enhance our understanding of the structural substrates of MDD across different stages and highlight adolescent MDD-specific structural biomarkers that may offer potential windows for early intervention. The observed interplay between diagnosis and age group further underscores the importance of considering age-related heterogeneity in both research and clinical practice of MDD.
