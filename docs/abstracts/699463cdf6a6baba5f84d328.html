Introduction

Making decisions for others is socially significant in daily life. Among these, decisions involving potential losses carry particularly important consequences, both personally and interpersonally. However, neurocognitive research on self-other differences in risky decision-making has yielded mixed findings1. This prompts a further investigation into the underlying value computation processes between self and others. One plausible mechanism concerns how the self is represented and engaged during personal and interpersonal decision-making. Extensive evidence shows that self-related information receives prioritized processing across perceptual, attentional, and evaluative domains2, reflecting an automatic self-related processing architecture. When decisions are made for others, this self-related machinery may still serve as a reference point, but its recruitment is likely weaker or more variable. Such graded engagement of self-related processing offers a principled account of why loss aversion sometimes generalizes from self to other and sometimes diminishes: The extent to which self-related processing is engaged during evaluation may explain variability in other-related risky decisions, reflecting an egocentric reference point when making decisions for others. Thus, in the present study, we attempted to answer the following research questions: (1) How are gains and losses evaluated differently when people make decisions for themselves versus for others? (2) More importantly, how does self-related neural processing contribute to self-other differences in loss aversion?

Methods

We scanned participants (n = 27, 11 females, age range 20 to 27, M = 23.11, SD = 2.22) using fMRI while they completed a mixed-gamble task3 examining loss aversion involving self and another person as recipients (Fig. 1A) and a self-association task2 aimed at probing the neural signature of self-related processing (Fig. 1B). In the mixed-gamble task, participants were asked to choose one ID from three randomly picked IDs of other participants at the beginning of the task. When “OTHER” was presented on the screen, the decision outcome at this trial would be attributed to another participant with this chosen ID. The “R” in the first frame of Fig. 1A represented the response button (the right button) to indicate acceptance. Otherwise, “L” would be presented on the screen. The “+” and “-” signs suggested the potential gains and losses, with the corresponding magnitudes presented below these signs. The “G” and “NG” in the fifth frame of Fig. 1A indicated the participant’s response, with “G” (gamble) representing acceptance and “NG” (no gamble) representing rejection. In the self-association task, participants were asked to first link three geometrical shapes with three labels, as shown in the left panel of Fig. 1B. These pairings were randomly shuffled across participants. Then they had to judge whether a series of shape-label pairings was matched or mismatched in the scanner.

For the behavioral analysis, we formalized mixed-gamble decisions as evidence accumulation processes where participants weighted gains against losses (Fig. 1C)4. Participants’ choices and reaction times were fitted to drift diffusion models (DDMs). The valuation of gains and losses was then quantified as the effects of one unit gain or loss on the drift rate (vG and vL in Fig. 1C). We examined the potential self-other differences in the valuation of gains and losses. For the fMRI analysis, brain activities from two tasks were linked together using multivariate pattern expression analysis5, as shown in Fig. 1D. Specifically, we used principal component analysis (PCA) to select the most relevant features from the large voxel space and trained a logistic regression classifier with L2 penalization on the retained principal components, aiming to distinguish self-related processing (e.g., You-Square in Fig. 1D) from other-related processing (e.g., Friend-Triangle). The training process was embedded within a nested Leave-One-Out-Cross-Validation (LOOCV) procedure to avoid overfitting. The resulting neural signature of self-prioritization was then combined with trial-level beta maps from the mixed gamble task through dot products, leading to trial-wise pattern expression (PE) values. The greater values indicate that the current brain activity is increasingly reliant on, or performing, the self-prioritization. We examined the PE values across two recipients and tested whether trial-by-trial PEs predicted evidence accumulation dynamics by incorporating the values into the regression model of drift rate. We also conducted multilevel mediation analysis to test whether self-other differences in loss valuation could be explained by these PE values. At the first level, each participant’s parameters in the mediation model were estimated by fitting a DDM. At the second level, the statistical significance of each parameter estimate was tested using a bootstrapping procedure. All the computational models and regression models, except the multilevel mediation models, were implemented using a hierarchical Bayesian approach. We reported posterior means and 95% Highest Density Intervals (HDIs). If the 95% HDI of a specific parameter estimate does not include zero, the parameter estimate is considered statistically significant.

Results

At the behavioral level, DDM results indicated that deciding for others was linked to reduced drift rate in evidence accumulation for both potential gains (self > other, vG:SelfOther = 0.13, 95% HDI [0.04, 0.22]) and losses (self > other, vL:SelfOther = 0.41, 95% HDI [0.17, 0.65]), as shown in Fig. 2A. Such drops in drift rate parameters indicate more pronounced signal-to-noise ratios for loss valuation than gain valuation, vL:SelfOther - vG:SelfOther = 0.28, 95% HDI [0.02, 0.55]. No self-other difference was observed for the fixed utility bias (v0), vSelfOther = -0.03, 95% HDI [-0.20, 0.16]. These computational modeling results suggest that participants weighed self-related gains and losses more than other-related financial outcomes, leading to higher signal-to-noise ratios when gathering evidence for oneself than for others. 

At the neural level, the analysis of self-related processing (Fig. 2B) PE values revealed that deciding for oneself relied more on self-prioritization than deciding for others (self > other, b = 0.12, 95% HDI [0.03, 0.20], Fig. 2C). We then conducted neurocognitive modeling analysis by re-estimating the DDM and including the trial-wise PE values as an additional regressor, allowing it to explain the variability of drift rate parameters. The neurocognitive model revealed a positive association between the PE values and vL (vL:PE = 0.22, 95% HDI [0.11, 0.33]), indicating the heightened signal-to-noise ratio for loss valuation when the concurrent evaluation-related brain activity increasingly resembled self-related processing (Fig. 2D). We also observed a weakly positive link between the PE values and vG (vG:PE = 0.04, 95% HDI [-0.00, 0.08], with 96.9% posterior probability mass above zero), and a positive slope of the PE values on the fixed utility bias v0 (vPE = 0.11, 95% HDI [0.04, 0.19]). By contrasting the effect of PE values on loss valuation with the same effect on gain valuation, we found a more pronounced effect on loss valuation than gain valuation, vL:PE - vG:PE = 0.18, 95% HDI [0.07, 0.30]. These findings suggest that greater self-related processing PEs were associated with an enhanced evidence accumulation signal for decision-related information. No self-other difference was observed for these effects (vL:SelfOther:PE = -0.12, 95% HDI [-0.35, 0.11]; vG:SelfOther:PE = 0.00, 95% HDI [-0.08, 0.08]; vSelfOther:PE = -0.11, 95% HDI [-0.27, 0.05]). 

Notably, the self-other difference in loss valuation and PEs, as well as the positive relationship between PEs and loss valuation, implied the potential mediation pathway. To confirm this, we formally tested the putative mediating effect by conducting multilevel mediation analysis. The mediation model revealed a significant indirect effect from recipients (deciding for oneself vs deciding for others) through self-related processing PEs to loss valuation, as shown in Fig. 2E, where all path coefficients were reported as means and 95% CIs. This result suggests that the self-other difference in loss valuation was partially mediated by self-related neural processing. In contrast, the same mediation effect on gain valuation was not statistically significant (indirect effect = -0.01, p = 0.557, 95% CI [-0.04, 0.01]).

Conclusion

In conclusion, using a combination of drift-diffusion modelling and multivariate pattern expression analyses, we demonstrate that deciding for others was associated with reduced signal-to-noise ratios in gathering evidence for potential gains and losses, suggesting the devaluation of other-related financial outcomes. Critically, we clarified the neurocognitive pathway that shapes both personal and interpersonal risky decisions. We found that self-other differences in loss valuation, but not gain valuation, were partially mediated by the degree to which evaluation-related brain activity recruited the neural signature of self-related processing, indicating prioritized loss valuation for self-related decisions. This mediation pathway also implies that the self serves as an anchor in interpersonal decisions: Those engaged more self-related neural processing when making decisions for others would demonstrate less self-other discrepancies in loss valuation, delineating the neural implementation of “Golden Rule”, i.e., treating others’ losses as if they were one’s own. Our findings underscore the pivotal role of self-related neural processing in understanding self-other decision-making and demonstrate the utility of neurocognitive modelling in pinpointing the channels that regulate social decision-making. This study offers novel insights into how the brain makes loss-averse decisions for oneself and others, with important implications for understanding both self-biased behaviors in decision-making and surrogate loss-averse choices in financial contexts.
