Introduction

Functional organization is strongly distance dependent: nearby regions interact more than distant ones.1 This principle shapes how functions emerge throughout the cortex, with surface geometry acting as a scaffold for cortical organization. As a result, many functional properties have a smooth spatial distribution.

Functional gradients nicely capture the spatial dependence of functional organization.2 They are global axes  of smooth cortical differentiation which are thought to arise from the spatial layout of cortical regions. The first gradient spans from sensory to association regions, the second from visual to somatomotor areas, and the third from the frontoparietal to the default mode network. These axes constitute a functional space that describes intrinsic cortical organization.

Neural activity evoked by different cognitive processes occupies distinct positions within the gradient space, reflecting the contents of the task at hand.2 For example, sensory-motor tasks evoke activity at the lower end of the first gradient, while higher-level cognition is associated with the opposite end. Thus, as the layout of regions in gradient space varies across individuals, task-evoked activity can be expected to vary accordingly.

One likely source of gradient variability is cortical geometry. Functional gradients are derived from functional connectivity, which is highly spatially autocorrelated,3 suggesting an association between individual geometry and functional organization.

In this work we investigate how differences in gradient distribution relate to individual task-evoked activity, focusing specifically on geometry-dependent gradient variability. Our analyses first determine a part of individual gradient variability linked to the spatial layout of regions on the cortex. We then show that this represents a relevant predictor of task-related activity across different cognitive domains.

Methods

We aimed to identify elements of functional organization linked to individual geometry, and test their association with variability in task-evoked activity. We summarized cortical organization using the first three functional gradients, the dominant latent components of functional connectivity (Fig. 1A). Next, we embedded individual surfaces into a shared geometric space, which captures variability in vertex position across individual cortices (Fig. 1B). We defined geometry-dependent functional variation by modeling locally-centered gradients based on vertex location in the geometric embedding (Fig. 1C). The output of these models was finally used to predict cortical activity across participants in eight different tasks (Fig. 1D,E).

From the Human connectome project, we analyzed multimodal MRI data from 960 healthy young adults (age=22-35; F=515)4 divided into a train (719) and a test set (241). We used cortical surfaces, four resting-state runs, and eight task contrasts per subject. The data was preprocessed using the minimal HCP pipelines, and downsampled to 10,000 vertices per hemisphere to reduce computational costs while preserving surface geometry. The resting-state timeseries further smoothed (6mm FWHM) prior to downsampling. 

To compute functional gradients we generated subject-level functional connectivity matrices based on the concatenated resting-state timeseries. The matrices were then thresholded to the 5% strongest connections and decomposed using a joint embedding approach. This method decomposes simultaneously individual matrices and a group reference to ensure comparability of the extracted gradients across subjects. In line with classic literature, the first three gradients reflected the differentiation between sensory and association regions, visual and somatomotor systems, and fronto-parietal and default-mode network. We used these functional axes as summary metrics of individual cortical organization.

To estimate variability in cortical geometry, we used a similar approach, embedding vertex-wise geodesic distance matrices measured on individual surfaces (Fig. 1A). Distance was converted to affinity using a Cauchy kernel with a 50 mm scale, emphasizing the known autocorrelation range of functional connectivity.3 Adapting diffeomorphic mapping methods, Laplacian eigenmaps were then applied jointly to the individual and group average affinity matrices. The first three extracted components were adopted as a shared set of coordinates to compare the cortical location of vertices across subjects.

Next, we modeled functional gradients fitting spatial models within non-overlapping searchlights defined in the geometric embedding (Fig. 1C). The searchlights were defined by binning the geometric embedding, and they grouped all vertices that, across all individuals, fell within their borders.  Gradients were centered on subject means within the searchlight, which helped control for geometry-independent confounds, as well as  differences in macroscale gradient distribution. Searchlight-wise spatial models were fitted on the train set participants to predict centered gradients based on vertex location in the geometric embedding. These predictions capture aspects of cortical organization that vary with surface geometry across individuals.

Finally, we tested the association between the spatial model’s predictions and eight different task-evoked activity maps involving movement, memory, language, reward, and emotion (Fig. 1E). For each searchlight and task, we fitted a linear model  including the individual gradient means and the predicted centered gradients as independent terms (Fig. 1D). We then tested if the centered gradients contributed significantly to task predictions in the train set using nested F-tests, comparing the full model with a means-only model. To assess the generalizability of the model, we also compared the prediction error of the two models when applied to the hold-out test set.

Results

Our analyses revealed that geometry-related gradient variability is associated with task activity across all eight tasks, albeit with varying degrees of strength. The nested F-tests showed that predicted centered gradients explain additional variance beyond individual gradient means. The proportion of significant tests, however, varied vastly across tasks, as well as the magnitude of the prediction improvement. The language and cued movement tasks showed the most widespread effects, with the full model surpassing the means-only model in over 75% of the searchlights (Fig. 2A). These findings largely extended to task predictions in the test set. Adding the centered-gradients to the model systematically reduced the mean absolute prediction error also in new participants (Fig.2B,C). Thus, task-related activity shows a systematic association with geometry-predicted gradients, beyond what individual means already account for. 

The relative contribution of the three gradients also varied across tasks, reflecting their cognitive content and the functional systems they recruit (Fig. 2A). For example, the math and story sections of the language task engage executive and semantic systems respectively. Consistent with this, gradients one and three yield the largest average coefficients in the full model for this contrast. The cued movement task, instead, showed a relatively weaker association with gradient three and a stronger association with gradient two, in line with the higher involvement of sensorimotor systems in this task.

In line with this finding, performance improvement was localized to different regions of the functional gradients, resulting in distinct improvement profiles for each task. These profiles also reflected the cognitive nature of the task and the distribution of positive and negative activation regions in gradient space (Fig. 2D).

For instance, the math versus story contrast showed larger error reduction near the association end of gradient one and near the frontoparietal end of gradient three. This pattern aligns with the task map differentiating between regions involved in narrative comprehension—negative z-scores—and arithmetic processing—positive z-scores (Fig. 1E). In gradient space, these activation regions markedly differ along gradient one and three, but largely overlap along gradient two (Fig. 2D). Similarly, the difference between cue presentation and average movement vastly differed along the first and second gradient. The visual cortex is mostly recruited during cue presentation, while the somatomotor and association cortex are more active during movement. Consistent with this, we observe the greatest error reductions at the two extremes of the first gradients and at the visual end of gradient two. Further, across most tasks, error reductions were more pronounced in regions with extreme z-scores. Thus, the full model provides the most additional information precisely in the regions most active during each task.

Together, this evidence indicates that the functional organization variability linked to cortical geometry is associated with individual differences in task activation maps. This suggests that, by modulating local functional organization, cortical geometry indirectly affects the extent to which a vertex is recruited by different cognitive processes.

Conclusion

Our findings show that geometry-dependent variation in local functional organization helps explain individual differences in task-evoked activity. We have found that predicted centered gradients improve predictions of task-related activation beyond subject-level gradient means. This suggests that, modulating vertex layout in functional space, cortical geometry affects how strongly they engage in different cognitive processes.

This association displays task-specific characteristics, with different tasks being related to distinct sections of the gradients. Variability along the same functional axis may be important for one task but irrelevant for others, or relevant in certain regions but negligible elsewhere. These results suggest that the physical and functional space interact in a nuanced, context-dependent way to determine cortical recruitment during cognition.

Our results align with recent evidence that cortical geometry shapes the spatial layout of task-related activity within the individual cortex.5 Here we extend this work showing that such geometrical constraints partly translate into interindividual differences in functional topography. Specifically, vertex affinity to distinct functional systems varies systematically across subjects based on its location on the cortical sheet. This affects the local structure of the functional space that hosts task-evoked activity, leading to individualized cortical representation of cognitive tasks. In other terms, cortical geometry may contribute to differences in task-related activity by subtly warping the layout of the functional space within which they unfold.
