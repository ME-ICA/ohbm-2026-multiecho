Introduction

Mal de Débarquement Syndrome (MdDS) is a chronic neuro-otological disorder characterized by a persistent perception of self-motion, including bobbing, rocking, swaying, and gravitational pulling sensations, while in rest. Symptoms alleviate temporarily when individuals are re-exposed to passive motion, such as a car ride, distinguishing MdDS from other vestibular disorders. In addition to a phantom motion-perception, patients experience secondary symptoms such as brain fog, migraines, visual sensitivity, mood disturbances, and fatigue, collectively contributing to substantial reductions in quality of life.Although originally described after sea travel, MdDS can also arise after non-motion events or spontaneously, supporting the classification into motion-triggered MdDS and spontaneous or other triggered MdDS. Conventional vestibular tests are typically normal, suggesting a central rather than peripheral origin. Current hypotheses attribute MdDS to maladaptation within the velocity-storage mechanism of the vestibulo-ocular reflex and entrainment of cerebellar–vestibular networks, impairing readaptation to a stable environment after passive motion exposure.Optokinetic stimulation (OKS) with head-roll movements is the most established treatment and is believed to recalibrate velocity-storage dynamics. Clinical studies have shown that OKS can reduce self-motion perception and improve functional outcomes in both MdDS subtypes1. Prior neuroimaging research has revealed alterations in functional connectivity, metabolic activity, and grey matter volume within limbic, visual, and multisensory networks, suggesting broad central network involvement2. Despite these insights, the neural mechanisms underlying OKS-induced symptom improvement remain insufficiently understood.Tis study therefore aimed to identify resting-state functional connectivity (rs-fMRI) alterations in MdDS and determine treatment-induced network reorganization following a standardized OKS protocol.

Methods

Thirty patients with a confirmed diagnosis of MdDS and twenty-four age- and sex-matched healthy controls were enrolled. MdDS diagnoses were established by specialists using SO STONED anamnesis3, adapted to Bárány Society diagnostic criteria4 to determine symptom onset, triggers, and motion-modulation characteristics. The study was approved by institutional ethics committees, and written informed consent was obtained.TreatmentPatients completed a standardized four-day OKS protocol based on the Dai paradigm5. Each day consisted of four four-minute sessions, two in the morning and two in the afternoon. Half of the patients were treated in an OKS booth and half using a virtual-reality application. The stimulus consisted of full-field moving stripes at 10°/s while a clinician simultaneously oscillated the patient’s head at 0.167 Hz, the resonance frequency of the vestibular system.Data acquisitionObjective postural stability was assessed using static posturography on a Wii balance board. Patients stood with arms crossed and eyes closed for one minute before and after each treatment session, totaling 24 measurements. The predicted ellipse area (PEA, cm²) was computed as the primary metric of center-of-pressure displacement, with larger values indicating reduced postural stability.Subjective symptom severity was measured with a 10-cm visual analog scale (VAS), where 0 indicated no self-motion perception and 10 maximal perceived motion. VAS ratings were collected before and after every two OKS sessions (16 measurements total).Resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) and structural MRI were acquired at 3T (Siemens Prisma for the first six patients, Siemens Vida for remaining patients and all controls), each equipped with a 64-channel head coil. T1-weighted images were obtained using an MPRAGE sequence (TR=2060 ms; TE=2.17 ms; voxel size 0.75 mm isotropic). rs-fMRI data were collected using a EPI sequence (TR=700 ms; TE=30 ms; voxel size 2.5 mm isotropic; multiband factor 6). Double-echo field maps were acquired for distortion correction.PreprocessingPreprocessing was performed using SPM12. Steps included slice-timing correction, VDM-based realignment, co-registration to T1 anatomy, normalization to MNI space, spatial smoothing with a 4-mm FWHM Gaussian kernel, and identification of outlier scans using ART (framewise displacement >0.9 mm or global BOLD fluctuations >5 SD). Anatomical segmentation was performed using CAT12. Denoising was implemented in CONN using CompCor (5 WM and 5 CSF components), motion parameters and derivatives (12 regressors), outlier scans, session effects, and linear trends. Band-pass filtering (0.008–0.09 Hz) and linear detrending were applied. Effective post-denoising degrees of freedom ranged from 70.6 to 71.6.Connectivity analysesTwo analyses were performed. First, intrinsic connectivity contrast (ICC) was used as a hypothesis-free, voxel-wise measure of whole-brain connectivity, computed as the mean Fisher z-transformed correlation between each voxel and all others, generating individual ICC maps. Second, seed-based connectivity (SBC) analyses were conducted using 10 predefined regions of interest from the Julich Brain atlas and a cortical vestibular atlas, selected based on prior MdDS findings involving the amygdala, entorhinal cortex, vestibular operculum, and multisensory hubs.Seed-to-voxel connectivity maps were generated for each subject and timepoint and transformed using Fisher’s r-to-z. Between-group differences (patients pre-treatment vs. controls) were analyzed using two-sample t-tests; pre- to post-treatment changes were assessed with paired t-tests. Age, sex, and scanner type were included as covariates. Voxel-level thresholding was set at p<0.001 (uncorrected), followed by cluster-level FDR correction at p<0.05. Multiple-comparison correction across ROIs was implemented using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Associations between FC changes and ΔVAS/ΔPEA were tested using Pearson correlations.

Results

What defines the MdDS brain: Comparison between healthy controls and MdDS patientsWe primarily investigated FC differences between the controls and MdDS patients at pre-treatment for different ROIs. A significantly less negative FC was found between the right amygdala and the right occipital pole (OP, area V1/V2) (p(FDR) = 0.008484) for the MdDS patients compared to the healthy controls. Secondly, a significant higher FC was found between the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) and the left orbitofrontal cortex (OFC, area Fo5) (p(FDR) = 0.018216) in the MdDS patients. Thirdly, a significant less negative FC was found between the uvula and the right lingual gyrus (LingG, area hOc4v) (p(FDR) = 0.010405) for the MdDS patients. Fourthly, a significant higher FC was found between the right operculum (OP2) and the right frontal operculum (FO, area OP8) (p(FDR) = 0.014870) for the MdDS patients. A stronger positive FC was found between the left entorhinal cortex (EC) as a seed region and the right superior temporal sulcus (STS, area STS1) (p(FDR) = 0.001835), the right inferior frontal sulcus (IFS, area IFS4) (p(FDR) = 0.049158), cerebellum (left Crus 1) (p(FDR) =0.034680), cerebellum (right Lobule VI) (p(FDR) < 0.00001), and the left fusiform gyrus (FusG, area FG3) (p(FDR) < 0.00001).Central adaptation to the OKS treatment: Comparison pre- to posttreatmentWe primarily investigated FC differences between the pre- and posttreatment scans of the MdDS patients. A significant stronger positive FC was found between the left amygdala and the lateral occipital complex (LOC) (right area hOc4Ia (p(FDR) = 0.012943), and left hOc5 (p(FDR) = 0.012943), posttreatment compared to pre-treatment. Additionally, a significant altered FC was found posttreatment between the left EC and the thalamus (left lateral geniculate nuclei (p(FDR) = 0.029036).

Conclusion

Baseline MdDS brain network alterationsAt baseline, MdDS patients exhibited widespread resting-state functional connectivity (FC) alterations relative to healthy controls, reflecting disruptions in sensory integration, emotion regulation, and cognitive processing. Seed-based analyses revealed several prominent differences:Right amygdala–right occipital pole (V1/V2): MdDS patients showed significantly reduced negative FC, suggesting disrupted emotional-visual integration. This weakened inhibitory coupling may underlie visual hypersensitivity and abnormal self-motion perception characteristic of MdDS. The amygdala’s role in processing emotional salience, particularly fear and anxiety, may be abnormally interacting with early visual areas, consistent with previous vestibular disorder findings.Left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC)–left orbitofrontal cortex (OFC, Fo5): FC was significantly increased, reflecting enhanced interaction between cognitive control and emotional regulation regions. This may represent a maladaptive strategy developed in response to persistent motion perception, potentially sustaining chronic symptoms.Cerebellar uvula–right lingual gyrus (hOc4v): Reduced negative FC was observed, indicating impaired cerebellar-visual integration, which may contribute to persistent self-motion perception and visual-vestibular mismatch.Right OP2–right frontal operculum (OP8): FC was significantly increased, reflecting altered vestibular-somatosensory coupling and heightened sensitivity to motion-related sensations, potentially intensifying symptom awareness.Left entorhinal cortex (EC) seed: Stronger positive FC was found with the right superior temporal sulcus (STS1), right inferior frontal sulcus (IFS4), left fusiform gyrus (FG3), cerebellar Crus I, and right Lobule VI, indicating broader network-level alterations involving multisensory integration, visual object processing, spatial navigation, and predictive motor control.These baseline patterns reveal that MdDS is characterized by functional disruption across distributed networks rather than a localized vestibular deficit, contributing to persistent motion perception, visual dependence, and altered emotional regulation.Treatment-induced FC changes following OKSLongitudinal comparison of pre- and post-treatment scans revealed targeted neuroplastic changes accompanied by clinical improvements in self-motion perception (VAS) and postural stability (PEA):Left amygdala–lateral occipital complex (LOC): FC increased bilaterally post-treatment (right hOc4Ia, left hOc5), suggesting strengthened interactions between emotional and visual-spatial processing regions. This rebalancing may reduce visual dependence and improve sensory integration.Left EC–left thalamic lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN): FC increased post-treatment, reflecting adaptive modulation of thalamo-limbic and thalamocortical pathways critical for sensory relay, visual-vestibular integration, and spatial orientation.Correlations between these FC changes and ΔVAS/ΔPEA indicate that network-level plasticity supports symptom reduction, particularly within visual-limbic and thalamo-cortical circuits. The observed connectivity changes suggest normalization or strengthening of distributed networks involved in multisensory integration, emotion regulation, and cognitive control, highlighting the brain’s capacity for functional reorganization following neuromodulatory interventions.Collectively, these results demonstrate that MdDS is a dynamic network disorder. Baseline FC disruptions reflect maladaptive integration across visual, vestibular, cerebellar, and cognitive-emotional systems, while OKS treatment induces neuroplastic recalibration within these pathways, providing mechanistic insight into symptom relief.
