Introduction

Cognitive decline is a key symptom that allows to disentangle healthy ageing from pathological conditions and that can lead eventually to different forms of dementia. In particular, mild cognitive decline (MCI) is the first recognized stage of neurodegenerative disease, and its diagnosis allows to start patient follow-up and intervention planning. Since identifying even earlier stages of potential neurodegeneration could be crucial to aid therapy and biomarker development, subject cognitive decline (SCD) has gained a lot of interest: SCD patients lack the objective impairment observable in MCI, but they report themselves a decrease in cognitive abilities, in particular memory-related ones. Because of this definition1, SCD groups tend to be very heterogeneous, potentially including both individuals closer to the healthy controls (HC) as well as prodromal patients experiencing very early stages of cognitive decline. In terms of neuroimaging-based findings, structural and functional differences have been reported in the literature, localised mainly in the hippocampus and the fronto-parietal areas2, despite limited agreement between studies because of the already mentioned intrinsic heterogeneity. A technique that could help identifying specific microstructural changes and therefore stratifying SCD patients with closer profiles to the well-characterised MCI patterns is diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI). While its historical applications are mainly in white matter (WM), multi-shell DWI acquisitions allow to fit more complex models and to characterise both WM and grey matter (GM). To explore this perspective, this work proposes a classification-based approach to SCD stratification, leveraging the use of DWI-based features of WM and GM in key structures for cognition already known to be affected in MCI patients.

Methods

Three cohorts of participants (90 HC, 82 SCD, 70 MCI; 146F/96M; mean age[SD]: 67.5[7.9]) underwent MRI scanning and neuropsychological assessment. The MRI protocol, acquired on a Siemens Prisma 3T, included a T1-weighted anatomical scan (spatial resolution: 1 mm isotropic) as well as multi-shell DWI (spatial resolution: 1.8 mm isotropic; 11 b=0 volumes; 8 vols. w/ b=300; 32 vols. w/ b=1000; 71 vols. w/ b=2500). The neuropsychological assessment included the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning test, specifically the immediate (RAVLT-I) and delayed recall (RAVLT-D) scores, the Story Recall Test score (SRT), and the Fluency test (FAS) score. Anatomical data were processed with FreeSurfer for tissue segmentation and cortical parcellation with the Desikan-Killiany (DK) atlas, from which the following ROIs were extracted: the hippocampus (HP), the precuneus (PC), the entorhinal gyrus (EN) and the parahippocampal gyrus (PH) – all bilateral. DWI data were pre-processed using QSIprep – which includes denoising, Gibbs unringing and distortion corrections. ROIs from the DK atlas were aligned to the pre-processed DWI data through a rigid transformation. After pre-processing, multi-shell multi-tissue constrained spherical deconvolution was fitted with MRtrix3 to the data, and the resulting fiber orientation distribution was processed using TractSeg3 to automatically segment anatomical white matter bundles, in particular the cingulum (CG), the inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (IFOF), the inferior longitudinal fasciculus (ILF), the uncinate fasciculus (UF), and the middle longitudinal fasciculus (MLF) – all bilateral. In parallel, DWI data was also leveraged to fit the mean signal diffusion kurtosis4 (MS-DKI) and the mean apparent propagator5 (MAP) models, both using DIPY. From these models, the following maps were obtained: from the MAP model, the mean squared displacement (MSD), the Q-space Inverse Variance (QIV), the Return-To-Origin Probability (RTOP), the Return-To-Axis Probability (RTAP) and the Return-To-Plane Probability (RTPP); from MS-DKI, the mean kurtosis (MSK). Sample maps from one participant are shown in figure 1. Median values for each map were extracted for all the GM and WM ROIs, resulting in 108 initial features – these were further reduced to 50 using recursive feature elimination. From this reduced set, a random forest (RF) classifier with 100 estimators was trained on the HC and MCI group data using the scikit-learn package, splitting the participants into training and test sets according to the 80-20 proportion and withholding the test set for final evaluation. After training and evaluation, the classifier was applied to the SCD group data to stratify them into healthy-like (H-SCD) and MCI-like (M-SCD) subgroups. The RAVLT-I, RAVLT-D, SRT and FAS scores were then compared between HC and the other groups (MCI, original SCD, H-SCD, M-SCD). To identify significant differences, the Welch t-test (p<0.05, corrected for multiple comparisons with the Bonferroni method) was used to take into account potential unbalance after subgroup classification.

Results

The trained RF classifier achieved on the test set an accuracy of 87.5%, a precision of 91.67% and a recall of 78.57% – the related confusion matrix is represented in figure 2a. To assess which features were driving the classification performances, we assessed their importances through the mean impurity decrease accumulation within each tree: as showed in figure 2b, the most important features were related to both GM and WM, in particular PC, PH, EN as well as CG, IFOF and ILF, with right areas tending towards higher scores. These results seem to indicate as expected that changes in the medial temporal lobe (MTL) and along the anterior-posterior (AP) axis are key to distinguish MCI from HC. In terms of specific measures, the most represented among the top-scoring features are QIV, MSD and RTAP, which altogether suggest that these changes are due to alterations in tissue composition and potentially neurites.

When applied in the SCD population, the stratification resulted in 61 H-SCD and 21 M-SCD. Given the discussed importance of specific features, this implies that the M-SCD group should be closer to MCI in terms of MTL and AP structures. The score comparison supports this implication and it is shown in figure 2c-f: as expected, MCI are significantly different from HC in every score, while SCD are only significantly different in the RAVLT-I score; the M-SCD group instead was significantly different from HC in the RAVLT-I, RAVLT-D and FAS scores, while not significantly different for the SRT score. Overall, these results seem to indicate that stratification based on WM and GM differences is also linked with neuropsychological stratification – so leveraging MTL- and AP-based features allows to identify patients with initial verbal memory deficits but also with verbal fluency issues. The SRT score as well as the general trends seem to point out in any case that cognitive impairment in M-SCD is still distinct from what observed in MCI patients.

Conclusion

In this work we proposed the use of microstructural indices from diffusion MRI, able to distinguish MCI from HC populations, to stratify SCD patients and potentially identify the ones with closer microstructural patterns to MCI. We used neuropsychological assessment to independently evaluate this stratification, observing that indeed the structural differences driving the classification seem to be able to isolate patients with objective cognitive problems.
