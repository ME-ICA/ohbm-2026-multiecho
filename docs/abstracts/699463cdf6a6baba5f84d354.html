Introduction

Rapid advances in neuroimaging and neurotechnology offer new scientific and clinical opportunities while also intensifying concerns about privacy, autonomy, and mental integrity. International debates surrounding “neurorights” suggest a need for new legal protections, yet the EU already provides a dense framework through fundamental rights and regulatory instruments such as the GDPR, the European Health Data Space (EHDS), and the AI Act. This study examines whether these existing regimes adequately safeguard individuals and how they shape, support, or constrain ethically responsible brain research.

Methods

We conducted a focused doctrinal analysis of EU fundamental rights and regulatory instruments, assessing how they govern neural data and neurotechnology. Two illustrative case studies - Malta’s Mental Health Act and Chile’s constitutional neurorights reform - were examined to explore the potential consequences of neuro-specific regulation. Our analysis centred on risk categorisation, safeguards for research, and the interplay between data protection, scientific practice, and technological development.

Results

EU law already offers substantial protections for neural data through rights to dignity, integrity, privacy, and freedom of thought, combined with risk-based obligations under the GDPR, EHDS, and AI Act. These frameworks steer researchers toward strong safeguards without assuming that all neural data are inherently sensitive. However, legal uncertainty, particularly around classification of whether neural data are “sensitive” by default, DPIA requirements, and cross-sector uses, can burden research and limit innovation. Comparative findings show that neuro-specific rules, while well-intentioned, may create unintended obstacles or depend on speculative risk claims.

Conclusion

Rather than creating new neurorights, clarifying and operationalising existing European frameworks may better protect individuals while enabling robust neuroimaging research. Priority areas include clearer guidance on neural data classification, proportional safeguards for high-risk uses, and regulation focused on harmful outcomes rather than data types alone. A balanced approach can uphold cognitive liberty while supporting scientific and technological progress.
