Introduction

The human subcortex contains densely interconnected hub regions that integrate diverse functional networks to support information processing, yet its organizational principles remain less understood than those of the cortex. Within the subcortex, the zona incerta (ZI) is implicated in regulating heterogeneous processes, including pain, locomotion, and fear-based learning. Recent diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) studies reveal topographically organized structural connectivity between the ZI and neocortex, suggesting a structural basis for its broad functional roles. Despite these advances, the subcortical connectivity of the ZI remains poorly characterized due to complex local anatomy and dense fibre crossings. 

To address this limitation, we apply a probabilistic tractography workflow previously developed in our lab (Structural Connectivity Applied To Targeted Regions; SCATTR1), shown to be reliable and reproducible using the Human Connectome Project test-retest data1,2. Using high-resolution 7T diffusion MRI (dMRI) of healthy participants, we examine the subcortical connectivity of the human ZI. We hypothesize that the ZI serves as a subcortical hub, characterized by diverse and topographically organized connections with key motor-related deep brain regions, offering insight into the organizational principles underlying the human subcortex. 

Methods

Diffusion-weighted imaging 

Healthy adults (n=33; M=21, F=12) were recruited for 7T diffusion-weighted imaging  using a single-shot spin-echo EPI sequence (1 b=0 s/mm2, 95 b=2000 s/mm2; TR/TE=5500/60.8 ms, flip angle=80°, resolution=1.5mm3 isotropic). The diffusion-weighted images were de-noised, and corrected for Gibbs ringing artifacts. EPI distortion correction, Eddy correction, and gradient correction were performed. The diffusion-weighted images were aligned with the T1-weighted anatomical images via rigid registration (ants v2.5.1). All procedures were approved by the Western University HSREB. 

SCATTR workflow 

Probabilistic tractography (MRtrix3 v3.0_RC3) was performed using MSMT-CSD with group-averaged response functions. Whole-brain seeding generated 20 million subcortical streamlines (step = 0.35 mm; angle = 45°), followed by SIFT2 weighting. Streamlines were retained only when terminating within 1.5 mm of target ROIs and excluded if intersecting other ROIs or exclusion masks. Exclusion masks included: (1) the cortical ribbon; (2) an inverted convex hull restricting streamlines to the subcortex. Inclusion ROIs included: (1) BigBrain2 subcortical nuclei (excluding the hippocampus); (2) our previously established in vivo ZI atlas from 7T MRI3, and; (3) Freesurfer-derived thalamic nuclei4. All atlases were registered to MNI152NLin6Asym and then to native space (affine and SyN) with visual QC.

Node-specific metrics

SIFT2-weighted streamline densities were log-transformed and controlled for ROI volumes and tract lengths. Graph-theoretical measures were computed to characterize ZI hubness within the subcortical network: degree centrality, nodal strength, betweenness centrality, closeness centrality, eigenvector centrality, and coreness. All of which have been used previously to characterize the hubness of a node. For each region, a composite hubness index was calculated by normalizing all metrics and averaging across subjects.

Results

We first evaluated the reliability of the SCATTR workflow using 7T diffusion MRI from healthy participants (n=33). Two independent raters generated connectivity matrices using identical parameters. The resulting SIFT2-weighted adjacency matrices showed high reproducibility (Spearman’s ρ = 0.92, p < 0.001) with no hemispheric differences or meaningful changes in streamline densities (Figure 1). 

Probabilistic tractography revealed that the ZI exhibits robust connections with basal ganglia structures (including SN, substantia nigra; GPi, globus pallidus internus;  GPe, globus pallidus externus). The ZI also formed robust connections with the motor (VLp, ventral lateral posterior nucleus; VLa, ventral lateral anterior nucleus), sensory (VA, ventral anterior nucleus) and intralaminar thalamic nuclei (Pf, parafascicular nucleus), aligned with previous tract-tracing studies, supporting the involvement of the ZI in sensorimotor and pain-related processing. These connections are predominantly ipsilateral and symmetric across the left and right hemisphere. 

Given its diverse connections, we then assessed whether the ZI functions as a subcortical hub.  After controlling for ROI volume and tract length, the ZI consistently ranked among the highest scoring subcortical regions for all graph-theoretical metrics, including degree, strength, betweenness, closeness, eigenvector centrality, coreness, and the composite hubness index, indicating that the ZI occupies a central role in the subcortical network (Figure 2). 

Conclusion

Our findings support the hypothesis that the ZI functions as a subcortical hub in the brain, as graph-theoretical analyses consistently placed it among the most central structures in the subcortex. In addition, we introduce a reproducible subcortical structural connectivity workflow (SCATTR) that demonstrates high reliability, which can be extended to investigate other subcortical regions of interest.
