Introduction

Bilingual code-switching relies on cognitive control systems that regulate language activation. These systems enhance access to the intended language during production while inhibiting competing activation from the unused language1. Bilingual language control is believed to depend partially on domain-general executive control, particularly cognitive flexibility – the capacity to shift between tasks, concepts, or strategies. Although language switching and cognitive flexibility appear to involve similar cognitive mechanisms, their precise relationship remains poorly understood. A recent meta-analysis examined both processes, measuring cognitive flexibility through task-switching paradigms. The findings revealed both shared and distinct neural patterns: language switching uniquely activated the left middle and inferior occipital gyri, while task switching was specifically associated with left middle frontal gyrus activation2. Cognitive flexibility, however, encompasses more than cued switching alone. It also includes rule-discovery switching, typically measured using the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test. These two types of cognitive flexibility—rule-retrieval and rule-discovery switching—engage different neural systems. Rule-discovery switching shows stronger activation in bilateral frontoparietal and subcortical regions, while rule-retrieval switching uniquely involves medial prefrontal areas3. Given this complexity, it remains unclear how language switching relates to different facets of cognitive switching. The present study therefore aims to refine the neural correlates of language switching in young bilingual adults and to compare it with the rule-retrieval (via task switching paradigm) and rule-discovery (via Wisconsin card sorting test) switching.

Methods

Two independent literature searches identified fMRI studies on cognitive flexibility and language switching across five databases (PubMed, Web of Science, BrainMap, Neurosynth, and PsycInfo) through October 2024.For cognitive flexibility, the search terms [("cognitive flexibility" OR "set-shifting" OR "task-switching") AND (fMRI)] returned 5,882 records. After removing duplicates, 3,502 articles underwent title and abstract screening. We excluded 2,988 articles for not using fMRI, not examining cognitive flexibility, lacking original data, or being single-case reports. The remaining 514 publications received full-text review. Studies were excluded if they: (1) were not in English; (2) did not involve human participants; (3) lacked whole-brain analysis; (4) did not use GLM-based fMRI; (5) lacked BOLD switch-control contrasts for the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test or task-switching paradigms; (6) did not report MNI/Talairach coordinates; or (7) included participants outside the 18–35 age range. Additional exclusions targeted tasks not examining cognitive flexibility or clinical samples without separable control data. The final dataset included 66 articles reporting 89 experiments (19 using the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test, 70 using task-switching paradigms), with 1,557 participants.

For language switching, the search terms [("language switch") AND (fMRI)] yielded 1,791 records, reduced to 1,663 after deduplication. Title and abstract screening excluded 1,612 records for reasons parallel to the cognitive flexibility search. Full-text assessment was conducted on 51 articles, with 35 excluded for: (a) not being in English; (b) lacking whole-brain analysis; (c) not using GLM-based analyses; (d) lacking language switching tasks or switch-versus-repeat contrasts; (e) not reporting coordinates; (f) including participants outside 18–35 years; or (g) studying early/simultaneous bilinguals. Seventeen studies (17 experiments; 389 participants) were retained.

Activation likelihood estimation (ALE; version 3.0.2) was used for quantitative evaluation of brain activation patterns4. We conducted separate ALE analyses for the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test (rule-discovery), task-switching paradigm (rule-retrieval), and language switching. For the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test and task-switching paradigm, significance was established at cluster-level FWE p < .05 with a cluster-forming threshold of p < .001. Due to the smaller and more heterogeneous language switching dataset, we applied a slightly more lenient cluster-forming threshold (p < .005) while maintaining cluster-level FWE correction at p < .05. To verify comparability across tasks, supplementary analyses used uniform thresholds (cluster-forming p < .005; cluster-level FWE p < .05) for all conditions. These supplementary results aligned with the main findings, confirming that task-specific thresholding was appropriate.

Contrast analyses identified task-specific neural correlates by comparing: (1) Wisconsin Card Sorting Test versus language switching, and (2) task-switching paradigm versus language switching. Conjunction analyses examined overlapping activations across tasks (task switching ∩ language switching; Wisconsin Card Sorting Test ∩ language switching; and all three combined). For both contrast and conjunction analyses, we set significance at p < .01 (10,000 permutations) with a minimum cluster size of 200 mm³.

Results

ALE meta-analyses of the Task switching paradigm (rule-retrieval), Wisconsin card sorting test (rule-discovery) and language switching

Task switching activated a predominantly left-lateralized fronto-parietal network, along with bilateral insula and posterior cingulate gyrus (Figure 1). The strongest activation likelihood was observed in the left superior and middle frontal gyri (BA 6, 9) and the intraparietal sulcus/inferior parietal lobule (BA 40). Wisconsin Card Sorting Test switching engaged bilateral fronto-parietal regions and subcortical structures, including the right thalamus, caudate, and lentiform nucleus (Figure 1). Peak activation likelihood occurred in bilateral insula (BA 13), right superior frontal gyrus (BA 6), left inferior frontal gyrus (BA 9), and right thalamus. Language switching primarily recruited left-lateralized fronto-parietal areas (Figure 1). The regions showing highest activation likelihood were the left superior frontal gyrus (BA 6), left intraparietal sulcus/inferior parietal lobule (BA 40), and left superior parietal cortex (BA 7).

Paired contrasts and conjunction analyses

Subtraction analyses between task switching and language switching revealed no suprathreshold clusters in either direction, indicating no task-specific differences. Conjunction analysis identified shared activations in the left superior frontal gyrus (pre-SMA; BA 6), left intraparietal sulcus/inferior parietal lobule (BA 40), and left superior parietal lobule extending into the precuneus (BA 7).

Rule-discovery switching (Wisconsin Card Sorting Test) showed greater activation than language switching in bilateral middle frontal gyrus (BA 9), insula (BA 13), precentral gyrus (BA 6, 9), and intraparietal sulcus/inferior parietal lobule (BA 40). Additional clusters emerged in the left frontopolar cortex (BA 10), right superior frontal gyrus (BA 6), right angular gyrus (BA 39), and subcortical regions including the thalamus and caudate nucleus. Conversely, language switching produced stronger activation in the left superior frontal gyrus, specifically the ACC/pre-SMA region. Shared activations between rule-discovery and language switching appeared in the left superior parietal lobule (BA 7), left pre-SMA (BA 6), and left intraparietal sulcus/inferior parietal lobule (BA 40).

Conjunction analysis of all three tasks revealed a common left-lateralized network encompassing the superior parietal lobule (BA 7), superior frontal gyrus/pre-SMA (BA 6), and intraparietal sulcus/inferior parietal lobule (BA 40; Table 6). These regions constitute a shared neural substrate supporting switching demands across cognitive and linguistic domains.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis examined the neural basis of rule-discovery, rule-retrieval, and language switching across 107 experiments. Despite differences in task demands, all three switching types converged on a left-lateralized network comprising the superior parietal lobule (BA 7), intraparietal sulcus/inferior parietal lobule (BA 40), and pre-supplementary motor area (pre-SMA; BA 6). These regions appear to support domain-general switching processes that operate independently of specific task contexts or content domains.

Language switching consistently engaged the pre-SMA/ACC complex and left parietal regions, consistent with prior meta-analyses, though with fewer additional frontal or subcortical activations than previously reported2. Differences from previous studies likely stem from our stricter inclusion criteria and use of corrected statistical thresholds. Language switching and task-switching paradigm activations overlapped completely: both involve cued, rule-retrieval switching and recruit identical executive control regions. This finding supports the hypothesis that language switching depends primarily on domain-general control mechanisms rather than language-specific processes.

Comparisons with rule-discovery switching (assessed via the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test) yielded the most pronounced dissociations. Rule-discovery switching activated broader bilateral networks including dorsolateral prefrontal and premotor cortex, parietal regions, insula, thalamus, and caudate – patterns typically associated with uncertainty management, feedback processing, and hypothesis testing5. Language switching, in contrast, showed stronger activation in pre-SMA/ACC, reflecting its reliance on cue-based monitoring and rapid resolution of competition between alternatives1. These differences align with previous findings contrasting the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test and task-switching paradigms, reinforcing the distinction between rule-retrieval and rule-discovery switching3.

Conjunction analysis of all three tasks identified three core regions: left superior parietal lobule, left intraparietal sulcus/inferior parietal lobule, and pre-supplementary motor area—areas critical for context-independent mental switching. Together, these regions constitute a fundamental neural architecture that enables flexible transitions between mental sets, irrespective of modality (verbal versus visuospatial) or uncertainty level (cued versus feedback-driven).
