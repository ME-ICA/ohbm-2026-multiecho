Introduction

Hubs occupy central positions in large-scale brain networks and integrate information within and across distributed systems1,2. Clinical neuroimaging studies have examined these regions in diverse disorders, documenting both increases and decreases in functional connectivity as well as shifts in their patterns of participation within modular network structure3. Although the body of findings is substantial, consistent principles have been difficult to establish, in part because the spatial distribution and polarity of alterations vary across conditions. This variability has left the nature of hub alterations unresolved. However, a central question remains whether hubs predominantly lose influence, as suggested by the hub vulnerability hypothesis4,5, or whether their roles are reassigned within the surrounding network architecture. To clarify the dominant mechanism of hub alteration across disorders, we analyzed resting-state fMRI data from 12 diagnostic groups and evaluated whether hub changes are better explained by selective weakening or by network-constrained reorganization.

Methods

We analyzed resting-state fMRI data from 2,163 patients and 2,011 matched controls drawn from 18 datasets spanning 12 diagnostic groups, using a harmonized structural and functional preprocessing pipeline. Analyses were organized around three hypotheses. First, to test selective hub degradation, we computed functional connectivity strength (FCS or weighted degree) per subject. We estimated patient-control differences at the parcel level using site-adjusted linear models including age, sex, and brain volume, and combined across datasets using random-effects meta-analysis. We defined hub architecture in controls using FCS, participation coefficient (PC), and within-module degree (WMD) z-score, each standardized within subjects3. Mixture modeling with bootstrap confidence intervals assigned hub status. We evaluated whether parcels classified as high-confidence hubs in controls showed disproportionate alteration in patients. Second, to assess how hub roles changed in patients, we created node-type transition matrices that tracked whether each region shifted between non-hub, provincial hub, connector hub, or kinless hub categories. These matrices showed how often regions changed type compared with controls and were summarized both regionally and by the total number of hubs in each disorder. Third, to assess whether alterations were constrained by large-scale network architecture, we quantified the segregation of FCS increases and decreases using a sign-based network segregation score (SNSS) and examined whether FCS differences and node-type transitions were concentrated within canonical resting-state networks through permutation-based enrichment and clustering tests.

Results

Selective hub degradation. FCS alterations were observed across cortical regions and included both increases and decreases. Reductions in FCS scaled with normative hubness (R² 0.08–0.18), whereas increases were concentrated in low-degree regions (R² 0.06–0.60). However, high-confidence hubs did not exhibit preferential alteration after correction for multiple comparisons, and the hub architecture derived in controls remained largely preserved in patients. These findings provided limited support for selective hub degradation as the dominant pattern, motivating our subsequent analyses of hub-role reorganization and network-level constraints.

Node-type reorganization of hub roles. Node-type transitions revealed a consistent reconfiguration of hub roles across diagnostic groups. Patients showed loss of hub status in a median of 8% of cortical hubs (IQR 4-14%), whereas gains were limited to about 1% of hubs (IQR 0-3%; Wilcoxon W = 78, p < .01). Despite these shifts, the overall number of hubs per brain changed minimally, indicating that hub roles were redistributed rather than globally lost.

Network-constrained organization of alterations. Network-level analyses indicated that these changes were constrained by the anatomy of resting-state networks. FCS increases and decreases were distributed across largely distinct network territories, with SNSS values of 0.90-0.94 in insomnia, schizophrenia, and obesity indicating pronounced segregation of FCS increases and decreases. Spatial mapping onto the Yeo 7 networks showed that both FCS alterations and hub-role transitions were non-uniformly distributed, with somatomotor and ventral attention networks emerging as recurrent loci of reorganization. Transition matrices were highly correlated across disorders (ρ = 0.80-0.98), consistent with a conserved pattern of network-constrained hub reconfiguration.

Conclusion

Our findings offer convergent evidence across diagnostic groups that selective hub degradation is not the dominant form of network disruption in brain disorders. High-confidence hubs did not show disproportionate alteration, despite systematic associations between normative hubness and the polarity of FCS changes. On the other hand, analyses of node-type transitions demonstrated a robust pattern of hub-role reorganization, marked by localized losses and gains that collectively preserved the overall prevalence of hubs. Finally, the organization of these alterations was constrained by large-scale network architecture. FCS increases and decreases occurred in distinct networks, and both FCS differences and hub-role transitions were focused within specific resting-state networks, particularly somatomotor and ventral attention systems. The high correspondence of transition patterns across disorders supports a conserved systems-level process. Thereby, these results indicate that brain disorders commonly reshape hub roles within existing network structure, reflecting a mechanism of network-constrained reconfiguration.
