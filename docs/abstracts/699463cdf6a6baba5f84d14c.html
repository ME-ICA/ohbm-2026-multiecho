Introduction

Humans acquire diverse motor skills in their daily lives; however, there are significant individual differences in how fast these skills are acquired. Even with the same amount of practice, some individuals master skills quickly, whereas others progress more slowly. Several studies have examined the neural basis of such inter-individual differences in motor learning, demonstrating that baseline brain structure can partially predict learning performance (Tomassini et al., 2011; Sampaio-Baptista et al., 2014; Lehmann et al., 2019). However, despite these findings, predictors of motor learning ability prior to training have not been fully established. In recent years, brain image analysis using deep learning has gained attention because of its ability to extract complex features that are difficult to capture using conventional statistical approaches. Therefore, this study investigated whether deep learning could predict subsequent motor learning abilities using brain images acquired prior to motor learning.

Methods

Participants were recruited based on a pre-screening questionnaire to ensure that they had no prior juggling experience and were right-handed. A total of 68 individuals (44 males and 24 females) participated in the study. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kochi University of Technology.

First, T1-weighted and diffusion-weighted images (DWI) were acquired using an MRI scanner (MAGNETOM Prisma 3T; SIEMENS). After the scans, participants performed a juggling task. The task consisted of two-ball one-handed juggling with the right hand. Each participant performed 250 trials; however, because one participant completed only 248 trials, the number of trials was standardized to 248 for all participants. The total number of successful catches was used as an index of motor learning ability, and based on this measure, participants were divided into a high-performing (n = 34) and a low-performing group (n = 34).

Brain image preprocessing was conducted using SPM12. Both T1-weighted images and DWI underwent spatial normalization and smoothing. For T1-weighted images, voxel-based morphometry (VBM) was used to extract gray matter images. Diffusion-tensor imaging (DTI) analysis was used to generate fractional anisotropy (FA) maps, and white matter regions were extracted using a white matter mask. For input into the deep learning model, axial slice images were extracted from 3D gray and white matter images to create a two-dimensional image dataset. ResNet50 was used as a 2D convolutional neural network (2D-CNN) deep learning model. Preprocessed 2D slices of gray or white matter images were used as input data to perform binary classification, predicting whether each participant belonged to the high or low motor learning ability group. Using the holdout method, 80% and 20% of the data were allocated for training and testing, respectively. To reduce sampling bias, the model procedure was repeated 20 times, and the mean classification accuracy across these trials was used to evaluate model performance.

Results

Using ResNet50, binary classification of participants with high versus low motor learning abilities was performed. The model trained on white matter images achieved an accuracy of 77.55 ± 1.85%, whereas the model trained on gray matter images achieved an accuracy of 92.56 ± 2.57%. One-sample t-tests comparing the accuracy of each model with the chance level (50%) revealed that both models had significantly higher accuracy (P<0.01). Furthermore, comparing the classification accuracy between the two models, the model using gray matter images achieved significantly higher accuracy (P < 0.01).

Conclusion

This study examined whether motor learning performance could be predicted from brain images acquired prior to motor skill acquisition using a deep learning approach. The results showed that motor learning ability could be predicted with high accuracy from both white and gray matter images, with particularly high classification accuracy observed when gray matter images were used. These findings suggest that the structural features of the brain before learning may reflect characteristics related to an individual's motor learning ability. Moreover, the higher accuracy obtained from gray matter images indicates that anatomical variability in the cerebral cortex may underlie individual differences in motor learning abilities.
