Introduction

Sleep regularity has emerged as a critical dimension of sleep health, yet its neurocognitive consequences in childhood remain poorly understood. Social jetlag (SJL)—the misalignment between biological and socially imposed sleep schedules1—is highly prevalent during late childhood and early adolescence and has been linked to mood, academic performance, and cognitive difficulties. However, existing studies often rely on self-reported sleep and rarely integrate behavioral and neuroimaging measures. Moreover, little is known about whether irregular sleep timing exerts domain-specific effects on cognition, and whether such effects are supported by identifiable neural pathways. To investigate this issue, in this study we use objective actigraphy and structural MRI data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) data to test whether social jetlag (SJL) relates to cognitive performance across multiple domains and whether cortical structure mediates these associations.

Methods

Participants were drawn from the ABCD 5.0 dataset. Objective sleep measures were obtained from Fitbit Charge 2 devices worn for three consecutive weeks. Nights with implausible time in bed were excluded, and participants with fewer than seven valid days or fewer than two weekend days were removed. Sleep variables included social jetlag (SJL; calculated as the difference in midsleep between workdays and free days), chronotype defined as the corrected mid-sleep point on free days (MSFsc), total sleep time (TST; calculated as mean sleep duration across the week), and weekend catch-up sleep (WCS; calculated as the difference between mean weekend and weekday sleep durations). For the present research, we used data from the two-year follow-up wave, which included 6,078 youth with available Fitbit-derived sleep recordings. After applying quality-control criteria for actigraphy validity, 4,371 participants had usable sleep data. They ranged in age from 10.6 to 13.7 years (M = 12.0, SD = 0.7) and were approximately balanced by sex (50.4% male).

Cognitive performance was assessed using the NIH Toolbox (age-corrected standard scores). Domains included Picture Vocabulary, Oral Reading Recognition, Picture Sequence Memory, Flanker Inhibitory Control, Pattern Comparison Processing Speed, and the Crystallized Composite. Linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) tested associations between SJL, TST, and cognitive outcomes, adjusting for chronotype, WCS, age, sex, family income, parental education, ethnicity, and with site and family as random intercepts.

High-resolution structural MRI data were processed using the ABCD FreeSurfer pipeline. Cortical volumes were extracted for 68 regions defined by the Desikan–Killiany atlas. An a priori bilateral language network was defined, comprising the transverse temporal, superior, middle, and inferior temporal cortices; inferior frontal subdivisions (pars opercularis, pars triangularis, and pars orbitalis); the precentral gyrus; the supramarginal and inferior parietal cortices; and the insula. A sensorimotor network served as a negative control. Linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) examined associations between social jetlag (SJL) and cortical network volumes, controlling for demographic covariates, intracranial volume, chronotype (MSFsc), total sleep time (TST), and weekend catch-up sleep (WCS). Mediation analyses using structural equation modeling (SEM) with 1,000 bootstrap samples further tested whether language network volume mediated the effects of SJL on vocabulary, reading, and crystallized cognition.

Results

After controlling for TST, social jetlag remained a significant negative predictor for several cognitive outcomes. Specifically, greater social jetlag was associated with lower scores in Picture Vocabulary (β = -0.061, p < .001, partialcR² = .003), Picture Sequence (β = -0.039, p = .032, partial R² = .0011), Oral Reading (β = -0.046, p = .012, partial R² = .0015), and the Crystallized Composite (β = -0.050, p = .006, partial R² = .002). Importantly, these associations remained significant even after adjusting for key demographic and socioeconomic covariates, including sex, age, ethnicity, family income, and parental education, underscoring the robustness of the relationship between social jetlag and cognitive outcomes. In contrast, TST showed no significant associations with these measures. No significant associations were observed for Flanker Inhibitory Control or Pattern Comparison Processing Speed.

Structural MRI results revealed a significant negative association between SJL and cortical volume within the language network. Specifically, greater social jetlag was associated with reduced language network volume (β = -0.026, p = .012, partial R² = 0.0013), whereas no significant association was observed with motor network volume. TST was not significantly related to either network volume. Mediation analyses demonstrated significant indirect effects for Picture Vocabulary (indirect effect β = -0.005, 95% CI [-0.008, -0.002]), Oral Reading (indirect effect β = -0.006, 95% CI [-0.007, -0.003]), and the Crystallized Composite (indirect effect β = -0.007, 95% CI [-0.009, -0.003]), indicating that greater social jetlag was associated with reduced cortical volume, which in turn predicted poorer language-related performance.

Conclusion

Across a large, objectively measured pediatric cohort, the present findings demonstrate that social jetlag, but not total sleep time, exerts domain-specific rather than global cognitive effects, disproportionately impairing language-based cognition and episodic memory while sparing inhibition and processing speed. Moreover, social jetlag was associated with reduced language network-specific cortical volume, and this reduction significantly mediated its effects on vocabulary, reading, and crystallized knowledge. These findings suggest that sleep timing regularity is a critical determinant of neurocognitive development. These structural findings align with theoretical models positing that circadian stability supports synaptic pruning, myelination, and cortical reorganization in regions essential for language learning. Together, these results offer converging behavioral and neuroanatomical evidence that irregular sleep timing alters both cognitive function and cortical structure in children. Promoting stable sleep–wake schedules during this sensitive developmental period may therefore support healthier brain maturation and yield downstream benefits for learning, language development, and long-term academic outcomes.
