Introduction

Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) is a non-invasive optical imaging modality that measures local cortical changes in oxygenated (HbO) and deoxygenated hemoglobin (HbR). In addition to the evoked hemodynamic response to tasks, spontaneous and periodic fluctuations in fNIRS signals have been consistently observed across a range of physiological frequencies, including cardiac (0.8 - 1.5 Hz), respiration (0.16 - 0.6 Hz), vasomotor fluctuations (0.04 - 0.15 Hz), low-frequency oscillations (0.01 – 0.04 Hz) and very low-frequency oscillations (less than 0.04 Hz)1. These frequency bands reflect a mixture of systemic physiological processes (e.g., heart rate, respiration, blood pressure variability) as well as cortical contributions from neurovascular processes. Near infrared tomography (NIROT) allows the reconstruction of three-dimensional images of hemodynamic activity along the cortical surface from non-invasive fNIRS measurements obtained using scalp sensors. Wagner et al, 20252 reported improved reproducibility of the localization of task-related activity across sessions and subjects when applying NIROT in comparison with classical channel-level analysis.

However, most NIROT approaches consider different variants of the minimum norm estimate (MNE) to solve the fNIRS reconstruction problem, a method that is not accurate in assessing the underlying spatial extent of the HbO/HbR responses3. Moreover, reconstructing ongoing resting state fluctuation/oscillations is even more challenging since we are dealing with low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) ongoing data. 


As our group has already demonstrated the accuracy of Maximum Entropy on the Mean (MEM) to perform NIROT when reconstructing high SNR task responses3, we are now proposing an adaptation of wavelet MEM (wMEM), a method recently validated for the reconstruction of low SNR resting state of electro- and magnetoencephalography oscillations4, specifically designed for NIROT. 

Methods

wMEM takes advantage of the discrete wavelet representation of the fNIRS signals to reconstruct resting state hemodynamic oscillations along the cortical surface at specific frequencies. We evaluated this method using simulated and real data, comparing it against two benchmark methods: (1) the widely used MNE method and (2) coherent Maximum Entropy on the Mean (cMEM)3, our group’s regular time-domain implementation of MEM.  

1. Simulation of task response 

To generate realistic simulated data, we applied our preprocessing pipeline5, to extract 22 minutes of awake motion-free resting state data from a healthy subject, acquired using 3 sources and 8 detectors targeting the left auditory cortex, according to our fNIRS optimal montage framework5. We simulated a task response (boxcar convolved with a canonical hemodynamic response function) for 28 different regions spanning the same cortical region. A fNIRS forward model was applied to generate simulated signals at the channel level, and data were corrupted with real resting state noise at different SNR. After reconstructing fNIRS oscillations along the cortex using either wMEM, cMEM, or MNE, we evaluated the 3D reconstruction accuracy using three metrics: (i) Distance Localization Error (DLE) measuring the Euclidean distance in millimeters from the maximum of the map to the closest vertex belonging to the ground truth, (ii) Spatial Dispersion (SD) assessing the spatial spread (in millimeters) of the localization around the Ground Truth, and (iii) Temporal correlation (TC) quantifying temporal accuracy by estimating the linear correlation between the simulated signal and the reconstructed signal. A two-way ANOVA was used to detect the impact of SNR and the NIROT method on the quality of the 3D reconstruction results. 

2. fNIRS reconstruction of task response (tapping, auditory)

To apply our method to real data, we estimated the hemodynamic response to two tasks. The first was a finger tapping task (1 subject) acquired using 3 sources, and 16 detectors covering the right motor cortex using our optimal montage framework, consisting in 20 repetitions of 10s finger tapping followed by 30 to 60s of rest, following the protocol described in Cai et al, 20233 The second dataset was an auditory task (3 subjects) acquired using high-density fNIRS (96 sources, 92 detectors) covering the bilateral auditory and visual cortices and consisted of 6 repetitions of 15s of listening to words followed by 15s of rest.

We compared two pipelines to estimate the hemodynamic response to the task:
(1) Localization of the averaged hemodynamic response to the task using cMEM. We applied an additional low-pass filter (0.1 Hz) to the data to remove the effects of physiological fluctuations (cardiac, respiration) before localizing the averaged hemodynamic response along the cortex using cMEM.
(2) Localization of the entire time course using MNE or wMEM before estimating the averaged hemodynamic response from NIROT reconstructions.  We first reconstructed the entire dataset along the cortex using either MNE or wMEM within the whole (0.002 Hz - 2 Hz) frequency band, then applied a low-pass filter (0.1 Hz) before averaging the response reconstructed on the cortical surface.

3. Simulation of oscillatory response

As for the task simulation, we simulated transient oscillatory activity at 0.1 Hz, modulated by a Gaussian envelope lasting around 100s. We simulated one repetition of the oscillations in the same 28 regions, and at 3 different SNR levels.   

4. fNIRS reconstruction of oscillatory patterns 

We finally evaluated the performance of wMEM when reconstructing ongoing resting state oscillations along the cortical surface, when applied to finger tapping data. After applying NIROT on the entire dataset, the time-frequency representation of the signal for each vertex of the motor cortex was obtained using Morse continuous wavelets. We then estimated the average power spectrum over time during the tapping and rest periods. 

Results

1. Simulation of task response

Figure 1B represents the distribution of the validation metrics (DLE, SD, and TC), obtained using cMEM, MNE, and wMEM for three SNR levels: –1 dB (red), 0 dB (green), and 0.25 dB (blue). DLE shows that across all SNR levels, MNE, cMEM, and wMEM achieved accurate localization (median DLE = 0), indicating high spatial precision for all three methods. For SD, we found that cMEM (applied to averaged data only) exhibited consistently lower SD when compared to MNE and wMEM (applied to the entire dataset before averaging). When considering all simulated regions, wMEM exhibited significantly lower SD than MNE for the high-SNR condition (0.25 dB). Temporal correlation showed consistently high values (correlation > 0.8) for all three methods,  indicating that MNE, cMEM, and wMEM were all able to recover the temporal dynamics of the task-related hemodynamic response with good temporal accuracy.

2. fNIRS reconstruction of task response (tapping, auditory)

When considering the finger tapping task, cMEM, MNE, and wMEM yielded comparable average time courses in the motor cortex (Figure 2A), featuring peak activation approximately 13 seconds after task onset, for both HbO and HbR.  For the listening words task, the location of the activation found by cMEM, MNE, and wMEM was consistent with fMRI results and in agreement with expected hearing and speech processes. Importantly, we found that cMEM and wMEM estimated a more spatially focal solution when compared to MNE for both tasks. 

3. Simulation of oscilatory response

Based on previous experimental results indicating that cMEM was not optimal for localizing low SNR oscillations, we only evaluated MNE and wMEM for their ability to reconstruct oscillatory activity. Figure 1B represents the distribution of the validation metrics (DLE, SD, and TC), obtained using MNE (red), and wMEM (blue) for three SNR levels: –1 dB (red), 0 dB (green), and 0.25 dB (blue). For DLE, wMEM demonstrated significantly lower DLE for both medium (0 dB) and high-SNR (0.25 Db) simulations. For SD, wMEM showed significantly lower SD for all SNR levels, demonstrating its ability to recover the spatial extent of the generators accurately when reconstructing ongoing oscillatory activity. TC further supports the superiority of wMEM to localize transient oscillations. Overall, wMEM outperformed MNE across spatial and temporal dimensions when localizing oscillations, especially under medium and high SNR conditions.

4. fNIRS reconstruction of oscillatory patterns 

Figure 2C compares the spectral content of the fNIRS signal across sensors and along the cortical surface after 3D reconstruction. Both power spectra revealed prominent power in physiological frequency bands, notably in the Mayer wave (~0.1 Hz) and cardiac (~1 Hz) ranges.  However, wMEM exhibited a stronger and more localized increase in power within the 0.016 – 0.1 Hz range during the task when compared to rest, indicating that wMEM more efficiently captured and preserved the task-related oscillatory components of the fNIRS signal. This peak in the power spectrum aligns with the expected frequency of the hemodynamic response to the tapping task. 

Conclusion

Our results demonstrate that wMEM enables accurate and robust localization of both evoked and spontaneous hemodynamic oscillations, even under low SNR conditions. We demonstrated that wMEM preserves the temporal structure of oscillatory responses more efficiently than MNE and yields more spatially focal reconstructions when analyzing ongoing activity.  Importantly, we demonstrated for the first time that MEM was robust across a variety of montages, including locally dense optimal montages and high-density fNIRS covering a large portion of the cortex.
