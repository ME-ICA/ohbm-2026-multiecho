Introduction

Researchers often use multiple MRI methods such as MRS to gain a more complete understanding of brain phenomena, but both MRI measures and multi method designs are prone to missing data. Researchers are usually advised to use full information maximum likelihood (FIML) or multiple imputation (MI) to handle missing MRI data (1). Unlike listwise deletion, which is valid only when data are missing completely at random, FIML and MI can produce accurate estimates when data are missing at random and can also lessen the impact of missing not at random data.

A key advantage of FIML and MI is the ability to include auxiliary variables, which are related to missingness or to variables that contain missing data and often have more complete data. Including them can reduce the impact of missing data. In MI, auxiliary variables can simply be added during imputation. In FIML, one can saturated correlated models presented in Figure 1a (2). We refer to using MI or FIML with auxiliary variables as the auxiliary variable approach.

Although the auxiliary variable approach is recommend, it may still be improved in multi method MRI studies. When missingness is concentrated in one measure, other measures are often used as auxiliary variables. However, using multi informant data, Chen et al. (3) showed that incorporating information from a more complete referent informant as indicators in a bifactor structural equation model (SEM) can further increase robustness to missing data. 

Following this reasoning, we propose that including other MRI measures as indicators in a revised bias correction SEM (see Figure 1b) should have similar benefits. In the model, the latent variable represents the MRI variable and other measures serve as the supporting indicators to stabilize estimation when they are missing data. We refer to this method as the bias correction model and simulations comparing it with other missing data methods using population models based on a real multi method MRI dataset.

Methods

he dataset was collected in Taiwan and contains measures from 62 participants on reward responsiveness and impulsivity, such as glutamate level (glu, modeled as Glutamate+Glutamine) and self-report data from a Chinese version of Behavioral Inhibition/Activation System (BIS/BAS) originally developed by Carver and White (4). After excluding 12 participants with excessive head motion, analyses were conducted on the 50 participants (age: M = 21.7, SD = 2.85; 11 males, 39 females). Glu data were acquired through MRS from the left and right dorsal striatum on a 3T Siemens Skyra using a 32-channel coil and a 30×20×20 mm³ VOI. A T1 MPRAGE scan guided VOI placement and tissue segmentation. PRESS (TR=2000 ms, TE=40 ms) was used, with shimming optimized via a 16-average pre-scan, followed by the 128-average water-suppressed scan and a non-water-suppressed scan for quantification. Spectra were analyzed in LCModel, calibrated to the water reference, corrected for GM/WM/CSF fractions and relaxation effects. Data with CRLB >20% were excluded. BIS/BAS data were collected after the scan.

We first calculated the correlations between Glu and all BIS/BAS items. The four items (3, 8, 9, 16) that were significantly correlated with Glu (p < 0.05) were considered auxiliary variables and included in both the auxiliary variable approach and the bias-correction model. We then fit the model to the data and used the standardized estimates as the parameters for our data generation model. Since Glu is often used as an outcome measure for group comparisons in brain imaging studies, we created a binary (dummy-coded) independent variable to predict the latent Glu variable with a target coefficient β in the model. Design factors

We fixed the sample size of each simulated dataset at 100 in the simulation, given it is close to the smallest sample size for which an SEM model can work. After that, we manipulated the following design factors. 

Magnitude of the target effect (β) We varied the population of β (cross-group mean difference) at four levels: 0 (no effect), 0.2 (small effect), 0.5 (medium effect), and 0.8 (large effect), which correspond to the conventional cutoffs of Cohen’s D.

Missing data rates We set up our missing data rates at 0% (complete data), 10% 20%, and 40% as Chen et al (2024). For simplicity, we call these conditions complete data and missing data conditions, respectively.

Missing data mechanisms. We considered all three missing data mechanisms in the simulation. In addition, given that MRI data are more likely to have missing data than self-report measures in many scenarios, we only imposed missing data on the glu. In MCAR conditions, participants were randomly selected to miss their glutamate data. For MNAR, referring to Chen et al (2024), we make the probability of failing to provide glutamate data determined by their own glutamate values. First, all participants’ simulated values on glu were ranked in order from smallest to largest. Second, the rank of a participant i (i.e., ranki) was to determine the probability of missingness (pi) on the glutamate variable. pi was calculated as 1 - ranki/n and compared to a random number j that follows a uniform (0, 1) distribution. If pi > j, then participant i would have missing data in the glu variable. The MAR data were generated similarly, except that the ranki is calculated based on the summation of the four BIS/BAS items.

Outcome variables and implementation of the methods

The design generated 40 conditions. We generated 1000 replications per condition. For each replication, the data were analyzed using three methods: the bias-correction model with FIML (Figure 1d), the auxiliary variable approach with FIML (Figure 1e), and regression with listwise deletion (Figure 1f). Each method’s performance was evaluated based on the estimated biases of β, as well as the type I error rates and the power of its Wald test.

Results

The simulation results are summarized in Figure 2. The type I error rates/power and the estimated biases of the methods are presented in the upper and lower panels of the figure, respectively. One can tell that the methods perform identically under complete-data conditions across all outcome variables. In addition, the three methods also performed similarly under MCAR conditions. They can all generate accurate point estimates and reasonably control type I error rates (< 0.1), although power rates of listwise deletion might be slightly lower than those of the two methods in conditions with high missing data rates (e.g., in 40% MCAR data)

In MAR conditions, however, the performance of listwise deletion was severely impacted. It not only produces substantially inflated Type I error rates (> 0.1), but also has the lowest power and most biased point estimates among the methods when more than 10% of the data are MAR. In contrast, the auxiliary variable approach and the bias-correction model can still generate accurate point estimates and reasonably control type I error rates. The two methods also have similar power rates, although the power of the bias-correction model tends to be slightly higher than that of the auxiliary variable approach

In MNAR conditions, the performance of all methods is affected. Their point estimates are all biased. In addition, when there are more than 10% of MNAR data, the Type I error rates of methods are all substantially inflated (> 0.1) as well. The power rates of the methods also decrease drastically as the MNAR data rate increases.

 However, the bias-correction model appeared to be more robust than the other two methods. It yields the least biased point estimates across all MNAR conditions. In addition, the bias-correction model also shows the smallest inflation in Type I error rates in conditions with more than 10% MNAR data. Last, it also generally outperformed the other two methods in terms of power. The differences in power of the bias correction model versus the auxiliary variable approach and listwise deletion are up to 0.128 and 0.328, respectively.

Conclusion

Our results show that, compared to the widely suggested auxiliary variable approach, adding other measures as indicators in the bias-correction model can improve the robustness of target analyses against missing data in multi-method MRI studies. When data are complete or MCAR, as the auxiliary variable approach, the bias correction model can generate identical results to typical regression analyses without introducing bias. In fact, the performances of the auxiliary variable approach and the bias correction model are also similar to each other when the data are MAR. 

However, when missing data are MNAR, the bias-correction model would yield the least biased estimates. Furthermore, it also has the lowest type I error rates inflation and the highest power, followed by the auxiliary variable approach. The listwise deletion has the worst performance in all outcome variables when the missing data are not MCAR.

In sum, our results indicate that the performance of the bias-correction model is better than, or at least comparable to, the typical auxiliary-variable approach. In empirical studies with substantial missing data, researchers often cannot fully identify the missing-data mechanisms (e.g., completely rule out the possibility of MNAR data). Furthermore, in theory, researchers can use the bias-correction model and auxiliary variables simultaneously. For instance, they can include some appropriate auxiliary variables as indicators in the model and treat others as typical auxiliary variables. Thus, we believe the bias-correction model proposed in this study should be useful for addressing missing-data problems in multi-method MRI studies. In contrast, listwise deletion should be avoided.
