Introduction

Human cerebral cortex is tiled by networks. Their boundaries and topography are essential for interpreting and contextualizing neuroimaging findings. Recent work has shown that network topography is highly individualized and relevant for clinical and basic science research. Precision functional mapping (PFM) is a data-driven framework that aims to reliably estimate macroscale functional brain networks, leveraging high volumes of fMRI data per individual1. A key component of PFM is community detection, grouping individual voxels into functional modules of the brain. One commonly used community detection algorithm is Infomap, which seeks partitions that maximally compress the trajectory of a random walker2. In virtually all PFM pipelines, only the “optimal” community partition is retained for further analysis. In doing so, the variability of all other solutions, including those only slightly worse-performing, are ignored. We hypothesize that this ensemble of high-quality partitions is informative, and that by embracing and characterizing variability in the solution landscape, we can reveal previously undisclosed properties of individual brain networks and better understand individual differences, improving the performance of PFM analysis pipelines3.

Methods

We analyzed resting-state fMRI data from the Midnight Scan Club (MSC) dataset1 (N=10 subjects, ~5 hours of resting-state fMRI data per subject). Functional connectivity matrices were computed using Pearson correlation, and graphs were constructed at three density thresholds: 0.1%, 0.5%,  and 1.0% of strongest connections, consistent with previous literature1,4. For each subject and density, we performed Infomap community detection with 100 random initializations to generate a solution space. To identify families of similar solutions, we computed pairwise Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) between all solution pairs and applied hierarchical clustering (Figure 1). The optimal number of families was determined using an elbow detection method on the dendrogram.

To facilitate network-level interpretation, we spatially matched each solution family to two standard network templates: 1.) Lynch 21-network probabilistic priors4, and 2.) WashU120 14-network binary parcellation1. This produced network-labeled parcellations for each solution family. We quantified vertex-level consistency across solution families at two levels. 1.) Pre-template matched communities: For each vertex, we calculated the proportion of pairwise family comparisons where the vertex was assigned to the same community 2.) Post-matched functional networks: After template matching, we calculated the proportion of comparisons where vertices maintained the same network label.

For each possible family pair within a subject, we constructed confusion matrices T where each entry T(i,j) represents the number of vertices assigned to network i in family 1 and network j in family 2. Matrices were row-normalized to account for different network sizes, providing transition percentages across families. Also, for each transition (e.g., DMN→Salience), we identified the specific vertices that made that transition and created spatial maps showing where on the cortical surface each type of transition occurred.

Results

Across the generated solutions, the Infomap algorithm produced 2-3 distinct solution families for most subjects, confirmed by clear divisions in the hierarchical clustering dendrograms. Within-family vertex consistency was high, while between-family consistency was significantly lower, validating family separability. Community-level consistency (pre-matching) showed strong spatial heterogeneity. Group-average maps revealed high-consistency regions (>80% agreement) primarily in primary sensory-motor cortices, posterior cingulate, and lateral temporal areas. Low-consistency "hotspots" (<50% agreement) were concentrated in prefrontal cortex, inferior parietal lobule, and temporal-parietal junction regions. (Figure 2). A significant correlation between network size and stability was seen, suggesting that across family differences are primarily affecting smaller association networks. We interpret this finding as larger networks exhibiting significantly greater stability by maintaining consistent boundaries across solution families, while smaller networks showed higher variability with ambiguous functional boundaries. These findings suggest that solution families represent alternative resolutions of boundary zones between stable large-scale networks.

Conclusion

We present a novel framework for understanding variability in individual estimates of functional brain networks through a solution family and transition analysis. Rather than seeking a single "best" network parcellation, our approach characterizes the structure of solution variability itself, revealing which brain regions have stable network assignments versus flexible boundaries across different valid alternative iterations. Our findings show that functional network boundaries are heterogeneous in their stability. Importantly, these patterns are consistent across subjects, suggesting that boundary flexibility reflects genuine regions that struggle to identify with a single network label rather than purely measurement noise. These results suggest that while templates provide useful network labels, substantial variability exists in boundary regions that may be functionally significant and limit our ability to assign a single network label or sharp boundary to specific higher-order brain regions.
