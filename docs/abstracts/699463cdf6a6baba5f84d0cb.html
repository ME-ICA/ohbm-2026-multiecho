Introduction

Retinotopic mapping is a defining feature of the early visual cortex, but accumulating evidence indicates that spatial representations of the visual field persist far beyond occipital areas.1-3 Visual field maps have been identified in dorsal attention and frontoparietal control networks, consistent with their role in spatial attention and eye movement planning. More unexpectedly, retinotopic structure has also been observed in regions traditionally considered transmodal, including parts of the default mode network (DMN).1-3 Notably, Silson and colleagues have shown that medial parietal and ventral DMN regions exhibit negative pRFs, where neural populations respond more strongly to regions of the visual field that are not currently stimulated. This signature is associated with memory-guided, internally directed visual processing. These findings challenge the conventional view that transmodal cortex is agnostic to visual space, and instead suggest that representations of visual field position remain embedded in heteromodal regions, potentially supporting visual scene construction and memory retrieval.1-4

While previous studies have documented the presence of retinotopic organisation in high-level association cortex, its functional relevance for task focus has remained largely unexplored. In this study, we address this gap by examining how large-scale patterns of retinotopic organisation—captured via principal component analysis (PCA)—relate to participants' ability to stay focussed during both externally-oriented tasks (e.g., reading) and internally-oriented tasks (e.g., autobiographical memory retrieval). By linking individual differences in sustained task focus to the spatial structure of retinotopy across cortex, we test the hypothesis that the embedding of visual field representations in high-level cortex constrains the allocation of attention, regardless of whether that attention is directed outward or inward.

Methods

Retinotopic mapping task: 30 participants were recruited for this task (24 females, mean age = 20 years, range = 18-23 years; one was excluded). The retinotopy scan used a sweeping bar contained a 100% contrast white noise stimulus. The bar moved in steps once for every TR length (1500 ms) in 16 steps for 8 bar directions (with four blank periods), updating at 2 Hz (see Figure 1A). Participants performed a button press when the fixation cross changed colour as an attentional task to aid fixation. Four scan repeats were carried out for each participant.

Comprehension and recall tasks: The same sample of participants conducted comprehension and recall tasks in fMRI (four participants were excluded). This study occurred across two consecutive days. On day 1, participants generated personal memories linked to autobiographical cue words (e.g., gift) outside the scanner. On day 2, participants recalled these memories from the cue word in the scanner and also read and listened to factual sentences in a comprehension task (Figure 1B). The experiment consisted of the following conditions (1) read sentences; (2) listen to sentences; (3) retrieve personal memories while ignoring written sentences; (4) retrieve personal memories while ignoring spoken sentences; (5) retrieve personal memories (no conflicting sentence presented). After each reading or memory recall trial, task focus ratings on a scale of 1 (i.e., not at all focused) to 4 (i.e., highly focused) were collected to index the extent to which participants were able to focus on the current task.

Population Receptive Field (pRF) Modelling: We ran the pRF model using a non-linear fitting algorithm in AFNI. The best explained pRF parameters for each voxel were selected by simultaneously using both Simplex and Powell optimization algorithms to minimize the least-squares error between the acquired retinotopy time series and the amplitude-scaled prediction. The estimated pRF parameters included the pRF position (x, y), size (sigma), amplitude (positive, negative). The pRF amplitude reflects the relative activation or deactivation to visual stimulation of its receptive field. Important for the current study, PRF coefficient of determination (R2) multiplied by the sign (positive or negative) of the estimated amplitude parameter, reflecting the quality of the model fit for both positive and negative pRFs.

Comprehension and recall tasks analysis: We performed a univariate analysis using a general linear model to identify the parametric effects of focus for these tasks. Ten Explanatory Variables (EV) of interest and four of no interest were modelled using a double-Gaussian hemodynamic response gamma function. The ten EVs of interest were as follows: (1) visual comprehension; (2) auditory comprehension; (3) recall while ignoring visual sentences; (4) recall while ignoring auditory sentences; (5) recall without distraction; (6-10) task focus effects for each of the five experimental conditions as parametric regressors. EVs of no interest were: (11) memory cue word, (12) task instruction, (13) fixation (the period before the rating question) and (14) rating decision period. The parametric EVs for task focus weighted by demeaned task focus ratings.

Neural association between pRFs and focus effects: PCA was performed to reduce the dimensionality of this retinotopic mapping allowing the identification of the principal patterns of variation in positive and negative R2 across the Schaefer 17 networks.5 The PCA scores were then entered as regressors in the group level (N = 25) general linear modal (GLM) analysis in comprehension and recall study. The design matrix consisted of a constant term representing the focus effects across all five conditions, along with additional columns corresponding to the PCA component scores for each condition. Group level maps were threshold with a FWE threshold of p < 0.05 and a z-statistic threshold of 2.6.

Results

The group-averaged whole-brain R2 map (N = 29) revealed that visual organization comprises both positive- and negative-amplitude pRFs distributed across brain regions (Figure 2 A). We used the Schaefer 400 parcels from the 7 network parcellation as ROIs to explore the distribution of R2 for +pRFs and −pRFs across networks (Figure 2B). Significant differences were found only in the visual network and the dorsal attention network, where R2 values were higher for +pRFs compared with −pRFs. Then, we performed pairwise linear mixed-effects models for network pairs adjacent along the principal gradient. The visual network exhibited significantly higher R2 than both the somatomotor and dorsal attention networks for both +pRFs and −pRFs. The dorsal attention network showed higher R2 for both +pRFs and −pRFs than the ventral attention and default networks, and showed higher R2 than the control networks only for +pRFs. The default network showed higher R2 than the limbic network.

We examined whether three retinotopic mapping patterns extracted from PCA predicted neural activity associated with participants' ability to stay focussed during reading and autobiographical memory tasks, using a group-level GLM with PCA scores as covariates (N = 25). Below, we describe these components in order of their functional relevance to the focus effect. The most relevant component was associated with positive retinotopic responses in default and limbic networks, and negative retinotopic response in somatomotor A. During both auditory and visual comprehension, this component was negatively associated with the focus effect in bilateral frontal lobe, anterior cingulate and paracingulate gyrus. Higher scores in this component were also associated with decreased focus effect in insula, right SPL and precuneus during visual comprehension. By examining the focus effect within these association regions, we found that activation in these regions decreased when participants reported higher focus during both auditory and visual comprehension. Accordingly, participants with higher scores on this component showed a greater decrease in activation in the anterior cingulate and paracingulate gyrus across visual and auditory comprehension, as well as in the additional bilateral frontal regions and the right precuneus during visual comprehension.

Another component related to positive retinotopic effects in attention and control networks. During recall without distraction, this component was negatively associated with the focus effect in bilateral lateral occipital cortex, ITG, MTG, parietal cortex, medial temporal regions, anterior cingulate gyrus and precuneus cortex, left insula, and right frontal lobe. The focus effect within the association map revealed that during focussed recall without distraction, participants with higher scores showed smaller increase in activation in bilateral parahippocampal gyrus, temporal fusiform cortex and right temporal pole, while showed greater decrease in activation in right SMG and frontal regions.

A third component captured the pattern of negative retinotopic responses in the attention and control networks, consistent with visual decoding. In auditory comprehension, it was negatively associated with the focus effect in left ITG/MTG, bilateral anterior cingulate and paracingulate cortex. The focus effect within the association map revealed a significant association between focus and activation in the left anterior cingulate gyrus. During visual comprehension, it was positively associated with the focus effect in bilateral frontal regions, insula, anterior cingulate and paracingulate cortex, where activation decreased as focus increased. It was negatively associated with the focus effect in the left anterior temporal lobe, where activation increased as focus increased.

Conclusion

In summary, we found that the retinotopic coding was distributed across whole brain, with strongest power of explanation in the visual and dorsal attention networks, especially for positive coding, while in other networks there was a similar distribution of positive and negative retinotopic coding. The PCA recognized three main patterns of retinotopic coding, and the results showed that these patterns modulated the relationship between brain activation and focus state during comprehension and recall tasks. We found that the positive retinotopic coding in default regions was negatively associated with the focus effect in anterior cingulate regions during comprehension, where it showed stronger suppression when participants were more focused on comprehension. The positive coding in attentional control regions was positively associated with focus effect in auditory comprehension, while it was negatively associated with the medial temporal region during recall without distraction. Negative retinotopic coding in attentional control regions was negatively associated with the lateral temporal region in both auditory and visual comprehension, while it was negatively associated with the anterior cingulate in auditory comprehension and positively associated in visual comprehension. Our results indicated that the retinotopic coding extended to higher-order cortical regions, such as the DMN and attentional control networks, and that individual differences in retinotopic coding within the default and attentional control networks modulated participants' neural sensitivity to changes in focus during comprehension and recall tasks.
