Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is increasingly understood as a disorder of distributed brain networks rather than isolated dysfunction of brain regions. Functional neuroimaging studies have demonstrated altered interactions among large-scale intrinsic networks. However, most network-level analyses rely on group-level atlases, assuming consistent functional topography across individuals. Personalized functional networks (pFNs) derived from spatially-regularized nonnegative matrix factorization (SR-NMF) [1] addresses this limitation. pFNs yield subject-specific, probabilistic spatial maps and their corresponding functional time series. pFNs have an overarching common structure that is adapted at the subject level. This approach allows functional network organization to be examined at the level of individual subjects while ensuring comparability between subjects’ networks

Additionally, network neuroscience provides a way to quantify properties of functional networks. Representing the brain as a graph enables characterization of network topology and community (modular) structure, reflecting how networks segregate into cohesive subsystems. This characterization is especially relevant for MDD, where pathology may affect not only functional connectivity between networks, but also how those networks are spatially embedded and organized.

Here, we tested two related hypotheses: (i) that MDD shows dissociable alterations in functional connectivity versus spatial overlap network organization, with functional differences emerging primarily at node and modular scales, and (ii) functional modular organization in MDD is scale and state-dependent. To evaluate these hypotheses, we analyzed resting-state fMRI from the large multi-site COORDINATE-MDD dataset [2]. We examined topology and modular organization of functional connectivity and spatial overlap networks using static and dynamic analyses across global, node-level, and modular network properties.

Methods

Resting-state fMRI data were obtained from the multi-site COORDINATE-MDD consortium. The sample included 467 healthy controls (HC), 610 individuals with current MDD, and 153 individuals with past MDD. Data were collected across multiple scanners and sites. Functional MRI data were preprocessed using fMRIPrep [3], including skull stripping, motion correction, susceptibility distortion correction when available, co-registration to T1-weighted anatomy, and normalization to MNI space. Post-processing was performed using XCP-D [4], including nuisance regression (motion parameters, white matter and CSF signals), temporal filtering, and censoring of high-motion frames. Subjects failing quality-control thresholds were excluded. To mitigate site-related effects, harmonization was applied separately to spatial and functional features. Personalized network spatial maps were harmonized using ComBat-GAM, with site treated as a batch effect and age and sex as covariates. Static and dynamic functional connectivity matrices were harmonized using FC-CovBat [5], applied separately to each dynamic window.

Personalized functional networks (pFNs) were estimated for each subject using spatially regularized nonnegative matrix factorization (SR-NMF).Using this approach, preprocessed resting-state data were decomposed into 17 subject-specific probabilistic spatial maps and their corresponding time series, allowing functional network boundaries to vary across individuals while maintaining cross-subject correspondence.

Three complementary network representations were constructed for each subject: (1) Functional connectivity networks were defined by computing Pearson correlations between pFN time series, yielding weighted, signed 17×17 adjacency matrices. (2) Spatial overlap networks were defined by computing pairwise continuous Dice coefficients between pFN spatial maps, yielding weighted, unsigned 17×17 adjacency matrices that quantify spatial embedding and overlap independently of temporal coupling (3) Dynamic functional connectivity networks, defined by computing sliding-window Pearson correlations between pFN time series (60-second window, 3-second step), yielding time-resolved sequences of weighted, signed adjacency matrices.

Graph-theoretic measures were computed separately for functional connectivity and spatial overlap networks. Global and node-level metrics included weighted degree, clustering coefficient, path length, closeness centrality, and global and local efficiency. Small-world properties were assessed relative to density-matched random graphs.  Group differences in global network measures were assessed using pairwise Welch’s two-sample t-tests between diagnostic groups, with false discovery rate (FDR) correction applied across metrics. Node-level metric distributions were compared between groups using two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests, with FDR correction applied across networks.

Community structure was examined using Louvain modularity maximization across a range of resolution parameters (γ = 0.5–3.0). For each γ, modularity and number of modules were computed at the subject level for both functional and spatial networks. Group differences in modularity and number of modules were assessed using pairwise Welch’s two-sample t-tests at each γ value, with FDR correction applied across resolution parameters.

Dynamic functional connectivity was estimated using a sliding-window Pearson correlation approach applied to pFN time series. Louvain community detection was applied to each window to assess time-resolved modular organization across the same range of γ values. Subject-level summaries of dynamic modular organization, including mean modularity, modularity variance, and participation coefficient were compared between groups using pairwise Welch’s two-sample t-tests, with FDR correction applied across γ values.

Results

Global graph-theoretic analyses showed a dissociation between spatial and functional network organization. Global functional connectivity measures, including efficiency, clustering, path length, and small-worldness, did not differ significantly between diagnostic groups after FDR correction, indicating preserved large-scale functional topology (all pFDR > 0.10). By contrast, global properties of spatial overlap networks differed across diagnostic groups. Current MDD exhibited reduced clustering (HC vs current MDD: t(1015) = −3.77, pFDR = 0.010, d = −0.23) and reduced small-worldness (σ; t(992) = −3.60, pFDR = 0.010, d = −0.22), along with altered spatial path length (HC vs past MDD: t(312) = −2.77, pFDR = 0.043, d = −0.23; past vs current MDD: t(278) = 3.31, pFDR = 0.016, d = 0.27) altered closeness centrality (HC vs past MDD: t(292) = 2.79, pFDR = 0.043, d = 0.24; past vs current MDD: t(266) = −3.23, pFDR = 0.017, d = −0.27, and alterd global efficiency (HC vs past MDD: t(282) = 2.65, pFDR = 0.047, d = 0.24; past vs current MDD: t(259) = −2.68, pFDR = 0.046, d = −0.22).

Node-level analyses showed a clearer separation between the two network representations. Functional connectivity networks showed widespread distributional differences across degree, clustering, closeness centrality, and local efficiency, with particularly strong effects for local efficiency (e.g., HC vs current MDD: KS D = 0.104, pFDR = 1.5 × 10⁻⁴; past vs current MDD: D = 0.102, pFDR = 3.0 × 10⁻⁸). In contrast, spatial node-level differences were more limited and primarily driven by clustering (HC vs current MDD: D = 0.057, pFDR = 2.3 × 10⁻¹¹), with no other spatial node-level metrics surviving correction.

Static modular analyses indicated largely preserved spatial modular organization alongside altered functional modular structure. Spatial overlap networks showed highly overlapping modularity and module counts across groups at all resolution parameters (γ = 0.5-3.0), with no HC-current MDD differences surviving FDR correction (Fig. 2A-B) (all pFDR > 0.05). Functional connectivity networks, however, exhibited consistently higher modularity in current MDD across a broad range of γ values, with 72.7% of γ values surviving FDR correction for the HC vs current MDD contrast (Fig. 2C)  (e.g., γ = 1.5: t(995) = 3.42, pFDR = 0.015, d = 0.21). Functional module number decreased with increasing γ, with modest group differences that did not consistently survive correction (Fig. 2D). Past MDD showed intermediate functional modularity values that did not differ significantly from either group after FDR correction.

Dynamic functional connectivity analyses showed state- and scale-dependent differences in how functional modules reconfigured over time.. Mean dynamic modularity increased with γ in all groups but diverged at higher resolutions, with current MDD showing significantly elevated mean modularity relative to healthy controls at γ = 2.0 (Fig. 2E-F) (t(988) = 3.91, pFDR = 0.0007, d = 0.25). The strongest effects were observed for temporal variability of modular organization. Modularity variance was significantly higher in current MDD at multiple γ values (Fig. 2G) (e.g., γ = 1.75: t(1002) = 5.01, pFDR = 3.9 × 10⁻⁵, d = 0.32), indicating increased instability of functional community structure. Mean participation coefficient also differed in a resolution-dependent manner, with reduced participation in current MDD at γ = 1.0 (Fig. 2H)  (t(996) = −3.87, pFDR = 0.009, d = −0.24) and increased participation at higher γ values, consistent with scale-dependent reconfiguration of cross-module communication.

Conclusion

In this study, we examined large-scale brain network organization in MDD using complementary representations of personalized functional networks, including static functional connectivity, spatial overlap between networks, and dynamic functional connectivity. Across these analyses, we observed a clear dissociation between spatial overlap and functional connectivity organization of pFNs in MDD, indicating that these representations capture distinct aspects of network-level pathology.

At global and node-level scales, functional connectivity networks showed relatively preserved global topology but widespread node-level alterations, whereas spatial overlap networks exhibited significant global disruptions in select measures with more limited node-level effects. Together, these results indicate that MDD-related network abnormalities extend beyond altered functional connectivity and also involve changes in the spatial organization of large-scale networks. Importantly, these effects were detectable only when spatial overlap and functional connectivity were examined separately using personalized funcxtional networks.

Analyses of community structure further demonstrated that alterations in MDD are most pronounced at the level of functional modular organization. Static functional networks in current MDD showed increased modularity and reduced module number across multiple community scales, whereas spatial modular organization was largely preserved. Dynamic analyses revealed the strongest group differences: individuals with current MDD exhibited increased temporal variability of modular organization and scale-dependent alterations in participation coefficient, reflecting greater instability of functional community structure over time. Individuals with past MDD generally showed intermediate patterns, suggesting partial normalization of functional network dynamics with remission.

 Notably, global functional topology was largely preserved despite pronounced node-level and modular alterations. This pattern suggests that MDD may preferentially affect how networks segregate, integrate, and reconfigure over time rather than overall connectivity strength. Increased functional modularity and reduced participation in static networks point to greater segregation of functional systems, while elevated modular variability in dynamic analyses suggests unstable coordination among communities across time. These patterns are consistent with a model in which MDD reflects dysregulated coordination and flexibility of distributed brain systems, and they highlight modular organization and temporal reconfiguration as particularly sensitive markers of disease state.

A limitation of the present study is that network organization was examined at the level of interactions between 17 large-scale networks, rather than within-network topology. While this network-to-network approach provides a summary of brain organization, it may overlook finer grained patterns of local coordination within individual networks. Future work could extend these methods to capture within network topology more directly.

Future work should investigate the heterogeneity of MDD using individual-level pFN modularity metrics, to determine if there are subtpes of MDD that can be discovered through network metrics such as modularity. Identifying network-based subtypes of MDD remains an important direction for future work. Additionally, longitudinal designs and symptom-dimensional analyses using network measures could help determine whether changes in modularity track clinical trajectories or treatment response.
