Introduction

    The Allen Mouse Brain Connectivity Atlas (AMBCA) is often used as the gold standard for mouse brain structural connectivity.^1 The AMBCA consists of serial two-photon microscopy experiments, in which a given brain region is injected with a fluorescent adeno-associated virus tracer that specifically infects the soma of neurons, before ex-vivo imaging of whole-brain coronal slices that visualizes projections to the infected neurons. Nearly 500 experiments in C57BL/6 mice injected in diverse brain regions were used to build whole-brain connectomes, which can be used to model structural connectivity and probe the connectomic architecture in animal models.^1,2 

Despite the AMBCA’s importance to the neuroscience community, we observed many tracer images with off-target injections, diffuse projections, unrealistically small injections and projections, and anatomical misalignments (Figure 1). These failures may result in the over- or under-estimation of connectivity to the injected region. Here, we show that this problem affects two common, publicly available connectomes: the regional connectome (“homogeneous model”) in Oh et al., and the voxel-level connectome (“voxel model”) in Knox et al.^1,2 The homogeneous model takes the injection volume from each experiment and assigns the projection to the injected brain region(s) based on the size of the injection, regardless of the injected location within each region. The voxel model assigns the projection volume to the centroid of the injected region and infers connectivity to neighboring voxels using a distance-weighted average. Both models apply quality control (QC), but we identify additional failures beyond the QC applied in each study. 

We apply a detailed QC procedure to the n=427 experiments that survived QC in Knox et al., 2018. We then rebuild both connectomes using the n=375 remaining experiments and characterize the impact of using our quality-controlled connectome on structural connectivity and disease modelling.

Methods

Overview

The AMBCA consists of 2D two-photon microscopy images that are stacked and aligned to form the Allen Institute’s Common Coordinate Framework (CCF) version 3 ("CCFv3") template (via autofluorescence channel images), along with templated-aligned 3D injection and projection images (fluorescence channel images). These images are then resampled into 50 μm voxels to define voxel-level metrics. We QCed the “injection density” and “projection density” metrics used in Knox et al.^2 We downloaded data from the Allen API for all included (n=427) and excluded (n=63) experiments from Knox et al. We QCed injection values over 0.5 and projection values over 0.1 to ensure that our QC identified experimental failures instead of noise.

Our analysis consists of three phases: automated quality control (QC), manual QC, and characterizing rebuilt whole-brain connectomes (Figure 1). As a case study on the impacts of our QC, we model the network spreading of neurodegeneration from a caudoputamen (CP) epicenter using the Susceptible-Infected-Removed (SIR) model.^3,4

Automated QC

Our first automated QC criteria excludes experiments with the largest numbers of out-of-brain and ventricular projection voxels, indicating registration failures to the CCFv3 template. We did not consider voxels into the third ventricle and cerebral aqueduct as failures due to partial volume effects in these thin regions. 

Our next QC criteria excludes experiments with the largest and smallest injection and projection voxel counts, viewing these extrema as biologically implausible. We defined upper outliers as being over four standard deviations above the mean voxel count for a given QC metric, and lower outliers for voxel counts as the bottom four values. 

Manual QC

We manually QCed all n=427 experiments’ injection and projection densities to detect nuanced experimental failures. For standardized inspection, we generated ten equidistant slice images along each dimension, overlaid atop the CCFv3 template. We then QCed all images using PyQC (see https://github.com/CoBrALab/ for make_slice_images and PyQC ).

We rated each image using the guidelines below.

  • “Pass”: meets none of the failure criteria.

  • “Partial failure”: some evidence of failure, but not severe enough for removal.

  • “Failure”: 

    • For injections:

      • A large or nonspecific injection spanning multiple major brain divisions

      • Subcortical injections with substantial cortical leakage, or a cortical injection infecting subcortical areas

    • For projections:

      • Nonspecific tracer spreading to off-target regions in multiple major divisions

      • Little or no long-distance projections

      • Misalignment to the anatomical template

We removed all failed experiments before rebuilding the Knox and Oh connectomes. Two independent raters (V.N, S.T.) evaluated all images and reviewed any discrepancies before deciding consensus ratings. The final QC ratings determined which experiments were excluded from further analysis.

Rebuilding Connectomes

To rebuild the regionalized voxel model (291 gray matter regions) and homogeneous model (211 regions), we reran the algorithms from the Allen Institute’s mouse_connectivity_models package. We kept all parameters the same except the list of experiments excluded, resulting in maps of normalized connection strengths.

SIR Model

To assess downstream effects of the connectome changes, we ran the SIR model of the network-spreading hypothesis for neurodegenerative diseases, synucleinopathies specifically, based on MRI-derived atrophies.^3,4 This model predicts alpha synuclein (αSyn)-mediated neurodegeneration through the spreading of infectious agents, taking as inputs the connectome and the spatial expression of the SNCA gene encoding αSyn. Previous work has shown that SIR accurately predicts atrophies from a CP epicenter developed by injecting toxic aSyn fibrils into an M83 mouse model.^3,4 We kept all parameters the same except the structural connectome and evaluated the changes in simulated atrophy.

Results

Automated QC

We identified several experiments as significant outliers (> 4 s.d. from the mean) for the number of projection voxels outside the brain (n=3) and within the ventricles (n=5). Further inspection of the projection patterns of the top out-of-brain experiments revealed misalignments to the underlying anatomy (Figure 2A).

We also identified extrema for injection and projection voxel sizes (Fig. 2B) and compared them to “failed” QC experiments in Knox et al. Importantly, we find that our outliers for largest projections, largest injections, and smallest injections are more extreme than those identified in Knox et al, and that our excluded experiments for smallest projections are within 100 voxels of those excluded by Knox et al. 

In total, after these automated QC steps, we removed n=18 experiments. However, we also see that the experiments removed by Knox et al. show a diverse range of injection and projection voxel counts, indicating more nuanced experimental failures (Fig. 2B). Thus, manual QC was necessary to assess the remainder of our failure modes (Figure 1).

Overall QC Experiments Removed

Our manual QC identified 46 failed experiments, of which n=12 were previously excluded by automated methods above, resulting in a total of n=52 excluded experiments. Our removed experiments show trends across major brain divisions and failure modalities (Figure 2C). For example, all n=4 failed cerebellar experiments had small projections. Additionally, for the n=19 failed hypothalamic and midbrain experiments, all but two of the experiments had off-target injections. Nonetheless, for the hippocampal formation, we also observed a mix of connectivity failures, including out-of-brain projections (n=1), cortical leaking injections (n=3) and small projections (n=5).

To more precisely examine the locations of the lost connectivity, we calculated the ratio of removed projections per voxel to the total across all n=427 experiments. As expected, we lose 100% of projection voxels outside of the brain and in the third ventricle, but also lose ~75% of projections to hypothalamic areas (Fig. 2D). 

Connectomic Changes

After QC, we rebuilt both connectomes. We show changes in the average connectivity percentile aggregated across each major brain division (Figure 2E/F). In the regionalized voxel model (Figure 2E), we see the strongest losses in the connectivity of the hypothalamus to the contralateral cortical subplate (-9.50%) and the hippocampus to ipsilateral medulla (-8.08%). Additionally, we see widespread losses in cerebellum-forebrain connectivity (isocortex, olfactory bulb, hippocampal formation, corticular subplate), but a strong gain in connectivity to hindbrain divisions (pons, medulla). We also see a general loss of connectivity to the hypothalamus, especially to the cortical subplate (-3.89%). These changes are consistent with the changes in the homogeneous model (Figure 2F), especially the strong losses in hippocampus-cerebellum (-4.79%) and hypothalamus-cortical subplate connectivity (-2.19%). Nonetheless, each model shows distinct gains in connectivity; namely cerebellum-pons in the voxel model (+3.40%) and midbrain-cortical subplate in the homogeneous model (+2.96%).

Impacts on CP Epicentre Atrophies in SIR Model

Using the same SIR model parameters as Tullo et al., 2025, but changing the connectome from the original Oh connectome to the rebuilt Oh connectome with n=375 experiments, we observe a slight reduction in the correlation of the simulated vs. empirical atrophy (⍴=0.64 vs. 0.60) (Figure 2G). We see similar correlations with the original and rebuilt Knox connectome (⍴=0.60). Finally, we observe a strong correlation between the simulated atrophies using the Oh connectome before vs. after QC (⍴ = 0.94), but observe the largest differences in the dentate gyrus, anterior cingulate cortex, and subparafascicular nucleus of the thalamus.

Conclusion

 Our quality control procedure identifies a set of n=375 experiments that satisfy the credit assignment problem for building whole-brain connectomes; namely, projections are properly aligned and neither too small nor too diffuse to meaningfully represent axonal tracts, and injections are confined to a single brain region. After rebuilding the connectomes on this cleaner subset of the data, we observe large decreases in connection strength percentiles across major divisions. While we only show changes in major division connectivity patterns, these changes arise from stronger changes in regional and voxel-level connectivity, which are particularly relevant to researchers examining specific connections to a single voxel or region of interest.

We interpret our post-QC percentile changes as losses in spurious connectivity patterns. Follow-up analyses, solely examining the top 20% of connection strengths in the old versus new connectomes, reveal a 100% loss in previous cerebellum-hippocampus and hippocampus-medulla connections in the regionalized voxel model. To our knowledge, these connections between distal forebrain and hindbrain regions have not been reported in previous tractography studies; therefore, we view them as implausible. On the other hand, we also see gains in previously-known connectivity (hippocampus-pallidum; hypothalamus-cerebellum) as a result of downweighting these implausible connections, further confirming our approach.

    In light of these findings, we encourage the community to use our rebuilt connectomes as a more reliable ground truth estimate of structural connectivity in the mouse brain. We also anticipate that our rebuilt connectomes will alter downstream modelling applications, such as the SIR model presented in this abstract. Moreover, since the AMBCA experiments were used to build the CCFv3 template, future work needs to assess the effects of excluding misaligned experiments on the anatomy of this commonly-used template. Finally, we hope that future work will extend the application of our QC criteria to the entire AMBCA, consisting of n=2,995 experiments in C57BL/6 mice and other transgenic mouse lines that visualize specific cell types of interest.
