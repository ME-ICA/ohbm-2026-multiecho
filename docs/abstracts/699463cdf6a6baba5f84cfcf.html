Introduction

In recent years, cerebrovascular co-pathology has become a major focus in Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) research, being now explicitly incorporated into the revised AT(N) framework.1 However, the relationship between canonical AD pathology and vascular brain changes remains poorly understood, with competing theories proposing either independent mechanisms or shared vulnerability pathways driven by impaired perfusion, disrupted clearance of amyloid-β (Aβ), and neurovascular dysfunction.2 Associative cortices, particularly those within the default mode network, are among the regions most prone to early Aβ accumulation. These areas are also characterized by high metabolic demand and tightly regulated neurovascular coupling.3 Although atrophic changes inevitably lead to reduced cortical perfusion in advanced AD stages, the picture is considerably more complex in the earliest phases, with conflicting evidence of both hyperperfusion and hypoperfusion.2 In the present study, by combining Aβ positron emission tomography (PET) with arterial spin labeling (ASL) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) cerebral blood flow (CBF), we investigated whether early Aβ deposition patterns are linked to cortical perfusion in a cohort of patients with subjective cognitive decline (SCD).

Methods

We identified 230 participants with SCD from the longitudinal Fundació ACE Healthy Brain Initiative (FACEHBI) study (Ace Alzheimer Center, Barcelona, Spain; 2014-2024). PET/CT scans (Siemens Biograph) were acquired with [18F]-Florbetaben as a tracer for all participants, with manufacturer-recommended dosing. Images were acquired on a 1.5T MRI scanner (Siemens Aera), and included FAIR QUIPSS II pulsed ASL (3D GRASE PASL with background suppression, 4x4x4mm3 voxel size, TR/TE=3400/16.3ms, TI=1900ms, bolus duration=700ms), an anatomical 3D T1w MPRAGE (1x1x1mm3 resolution, TR/TE=2200/2.7ms, TI=900ms) and a 3D FLAIR (1x1x1mm3 resolution, TR/TE=5000/334ms, TI=1800ms). 

PET images were linearly registered to each participant’s 3D T1w and non-linearly registered to the MNI template. Standardized uptake value ratio (SUVr) maps were computed using the whole cerebellum as a reference, and global burden quantified using Centiloid (CL) within the cortical meta-region of interest (ROI) defined by the Global Alzheimer’s Association Interactive Network (GAAIN).4 The PET scans closest in time to ASL were used for cortical SUVr estimation.

Aβ-status was defined for each time point based on CL. Participants were classified as negative (consistently <12CL across the follow-up); gray-zone (one time-point over 12CL and no time-points over 30CL), or positive (at least one time-point >30CL). 

We generated ROIs based on the spatial distribution of amyloid. First, we generated a cortico-limbic gray matter (GM) binary mask by combining Desikan-Killiany (DK) atlas ROIs (all cortical ROIs, hippocampus, and amygdala). Second, we generated voxel-wise Z-score PET maps for each participant using the mean and standard deviation derived from the Aβ-negative group. Visual inspection of the mean Z-score map of the Aβ-positive and gray-zone group showed a spatial gradient consistent with the expected anatomical pattern of amyloid (Figure 1). Third, we masked this mean Z-score map with the cortico-limbic mask, and then we subdivided it into ROIs of low (<1 SD), intermediate (1-2 SD), and high (>2 SD) propensity to Aβ-accumulation (Figure 1). 

ASL images were quality-controlled, and scans showing motion or severe arterial transit artefacts were excluded. ASL data were processed in ExploreASL, and CBF was quantified with a single-compartment model.  A pseudo-M0 image was generated by recovering the background-suppressed mean control image. After masking vascular voxels, we extracted total cerebral GM CBF and ROI-specific CBF from the amyloid-based atlas and the DK parcellation.

Statistical Analyses

1) We examined CBF correlation with age and sex with an SPM voxelwise analysis using a multiple regression design (general linear model, GLM), within the cortico-limbic mask. The analysis was repeated with global mean scaling and with an ANCOVA correction using the participant’s total cerebral GM CBF. CBF maps were smoothed with an 8mm Gaussian kernel. The significance threshold was set at the voxel-level as p<0.05 FDR-corrected, and a 150-voxel cluster size. 2) We compared global CBF across Aβ-status groups with an ANCOVA model adjusting for age and sex. 3) Using the amyloid-based atlas, we tested whether regional CBF differed across low, intermediate, and high regions with a linear mixed model (LMM) adjusting for age, sex, global CBF, with a subject-level random intercept. 4) To provide alternative evidence of a correspondence between amyloid and perfusion spatial gradients, we explored their spatial relationship within the DK atlas ROIs: 4.1) we ranked regions within each participant by both SUVr and CBF, and tested the correlation between the SUVr and CBF mean ranks; 4.2) we tested the correlation between continuous SUVr and CBF with an LMM adjusting for age, sex, global CBF, and Aβ-status, with subject and region as random factors, allowing SUVr slopes to vary by region.

Results

After quality control, 156/230 participants (age at baseline 64.9±7.0 years; 109 F) had CBF images of acceptable quality and at least one amyloid-PET acquisition. Their follow-up was 54.2±14.4 months. Based on follow-up CL adjudication, 109 (70%) were classified as Aβ-negative (76 F; 63.6±6.9 years), 25 (16%) as gray-zone (19 F; 65.5±6.7 years), and 22 (14%) as Aβ-positive (14 F; 70.6±5.0 years).

1) In the SPM voxelwise analysis, sex showed a widespread global effect in the majority of the voxels included in the cortico-limbic mask. In contrast to the widespread impact of sex, age-related CBF decline was more restricted to the precuneus (p<0.001, pFDR=0.012, T=4.90), posterior cingulate (p<0.001, pFDR=0.012, T=4.66), and lateral temporo-occipital cortices (p<0.001, pFDR<0.021, T>3.49). In the ANCOVA model adjusted for total GM CBF, the effect of sex was reduced in all regions. At the same time, the regional associations between age and CBF did not change with total GM CBF adjustment, suggesting that the impact of age on CBF is not only global. 

2) In the ANCOVA model, global CBF was not significantly different across Aβ-status groups (p=0.912, η²=0.001) (Figure 2A).

3) Across the whole sample, CBF was higher in regions with greater propensity for Aβ accumulation, independent of age, sex, and global CBF. Specifically, post-hoc comparisons showed that CBF was higher in the intermediate than in the low region (B=14.59, pFDR< 0.001) and in the high than intermediate region (B=7.12, pFDR<0.001) (Figure 2B).

4.1) The ranking analyses within the DK parcellation confirmed a correlation between regions with high SUVr and regions with high CBF (Pearson’s r=0.342, permutation p=0.049; Figure 2C). We identified the postcentral region as a high-leverage point, the only one with a Cook’s distance exceeding the conventional threshold of 4/n. Excluding this region did not alter the sign or statistical significance of the association, indicating robustness of the inference (r=0.413, permutation p=0.019). 

4.2) In the LMM, SUVr showed a positive relationship with CBF (β=27.453, p<0.001). The ANOVA Omnibus F-test for Aβ-status was not significant (p=0.262). However, there was a significant Aβ-status×SUVr interaction (p<0.001): Aβ-negative and gray-zone participants exhibited similar CBF-SUVr slopes, whereas Aβ-positive individuals showed a significant negative deviation in the CBF-SUVr relationship (β=-33.699, p<0.001; Figure 2D).

Conclusion

Our results demonstrate that Aβ accumulates preferentially in regions with intrinsically higher CBF, supporting a vascular-metabolic model for susceptibility to amyloid pathology. At the same time, in the voxelwise analysis, our findings show that the associative cortices, particularly the posterior ones (parietal, occipital, and posterior temporal lobes), present steeper age-related CBF decline. Such age-related perfusion decreases could, in turn, create conditions that favor amyloid deposition in precisely those regions that initially rely most strongly on adequate blood supply.

The Aβ-status×SUVr interaction indicated that amyloid-positive participants show a negative deviation of the CBF-SUVr distribution across the brain, whereas amyloid-negative and gray-zone individuals displayed almost comparable slopes. This deviation suggests that, as AD pathology progresses, the coupling between perfusion and amyloid becomes increasingly disrupted. In other words, regions that initially exhibit high CBF and high amyloid uptake gradually lose this concordance, a result consistent with emerging neurodegenerative changes, reduced metabolic activity, atrophy, and consequent perfusion decrease, paralleling ongoing amyloid accumulation.

These results align with a previous study by Meier et al., which compared perfusion patterns in young adults with amyloid deposition patterns in older adults.5 

The major novelty of the present study is that we directly assess perfusion and amyloid in the same individuals, at an early stage, using ASL MRI and amyloid-PET data, and we generate an amyloid-based atlas that captures a pattern of regional vulnerability to amyloid pathology specific to our cohort.

Several mechanistic pathways could explain these observations. One possibility is that aging-related reductions in CBF in high-demand regions3 may progressively weaken perivascular and glymphatic drainage, setting in motion a self-reinforcing loop of vascular damage and amyloid deposition.
Although speculative, an intriguing hypothesis is that aging-related vascular efficiency impairment arising from multifactorial systemic fragility may preferentially affect distal vascular territories that require finely tuned perfusion regulation to sustain high metabolism and synaptic activity.3 Under such conditions, Aβ deposition could result from cumulative vascular pathology in regions that are intrinsically more fragile from a vascular aging perspective, and energetically demanding.

Some limitations should be acknowledged. First, the absence of a healthy control group without cognitive concerns restricts conclusions regarding whether the observed perfusion changes are specific to SCD. Second, the ASL sequence is sensitive to prolonged arterial transit times in aging populations, although we partially address this limitation by excluding scans with vascular artefacts during quality control. Finally, the cross-sectional nature of the analysis precludes causal inference. Given the nature of the present findings, we cannot disentangle the temporal sequence of events in the CBF-amyloid relationship. At this stage, no causal direction can be inferred, and future longitudinal ASL-MRI analyses will be needed to clarify whether alterations in perfusion precede, follow, or reciprocally interact with amyloid deposition. 

To conclude, our results show regional associations between perfusion and amyloid burden. These findings underscore the importance of vascular mechanisms in shaping early amyloid pathology and support the need for dedicated studies targeting vascular contributions to AD.
