Introduction

Individual variability in brain functional organization has emerged as a key feature of human neurophysiology. The concept of connectome fingerprinting refers to the ability to identify individuals based on unique and stable patterns of functional connectivity, reflecting trait-like characteristics of large-scale brain networks. This framework was originally introduced in functional MRI (fMRI), where it was shown that inter-individual variability in whole-brain connectivity profiles exceeds intra-individual variability across repeated measurements, enabling reliable subject identification and longitudinal tracking of brain organization.^1 Subsequent work formalized fingerprinting metrics, providing quantitative measures of subject identifiability and test–retest reliability in functional connectomes.^2 These approaches have highlighted that individual-specific network features are not confined to fMRI but may represent a general property of brain functional organization across imaging modalities.^3 Electroencephalography (EEG) offers a complementary perspective on brain fingerprinting by capturing neural dynamics at high temporal resolution. However, EEG-based fingerprinting remains relatively underexplored, particularly at the source level, where functional connectivity can be analyzed within anatomically defined brain networks. A systematic evaluation across connectivity metrics, frequency bands, and resting-state conditions is still lacking. In this study, we apply a comprehensive EEG fingerprinting framework to a large cohort of healthy participants. By combining source-reconstructed EEG, multiple functional connectivity measures, and established fingerprinting indices, we aim to identify the methodological configuration that maximizes individual identifiability and reliability in resting-state EEG networks.

Methods

Resting-state EEG data from healthy participants were obtained from the publicly available LEMON dataset.^4 From the available data, 202 preprocessed EEG recordings were selected, consisting of 61-channel data acquired during eyes-open (EO) and eyes-closed (EC) conditions. For each condition, 400 s of continuous data were analyzed and split into two non-overlapping segments of 200 s to assess test-retest reliability. Each segment was further subdivided into consecutive non-overlapping 10 s windows, which were used to compute cross-spectral density (CSD) matrices. CSD estimates were averaged across windows and frequencies within five frequency bands (delta: 1–4 Hz, theta: 4–8 Hz, alpha: 8–13 Hz, beta: 13–30 Hz, gamma: 30–40 Hz) to obtain a single band-limited CSD matrix per segment. Source reconstruction was performed with exact low-resolution electromagnetic tomography (eLORETA) on a template head model with 5 mm spatial resolution computed using the Boundary Element Method (BEM). Cortical source activity was parcellated according to the Schaefer 100-region atlas. Functional connectivity was estimated at the source level for each segment using seven metrics: debiased weighted phase lag index (wPLI), pairwise phase consistency (PPC), weighted PPC (wPPC), phase locking value (PLV), coherence (COH), imaginary coherence (ImCoh), and absolute imaginary coherence (AbsImCoh),^5 Fingerprinting performance was quantified following the framework proposed in,^2 computing within-subject similarity (Iself), between-subject similarity (Iothers), and their difference (Idiff). The optimal configuration was identified as the combination maximizing the group-level mean Idiff. All analyses were conducted in MATLAB using EEG analysis toolboxes, including FieldTrip and EEGLAB.

Results

EEG-based connectivity fingerprinting can be reliably applied to healthy subjects who were individually identified with high accuracy based solely on within-scan connectivity patterns, both during EO and EC conditions. Importantly, Idiff differences can be noted, EO and EC conditions displayed comparable success rate values, indicating that the individuality of brain connectivity profiles is preserved irrespective of the condition. Across all frequency bands and connectivity measures, imaginary coherence achieved the highest fingerprinting performance, outperforming all other methods in both EC and EO conditions (Figure 1). This dominance was observed in every frequency band, indicating a robust advantage of imaginary coherence that was not band-specific. The highest Idiff overall was found for ImCoh in the alpha band during EC (Idiff = 0.66), followed by alpha band EO (Idiff = 0.56), driven by high Iself and low Iothers.

Conclusion

These findings suggest that resting-state EEG functional connectivity contains individual-specific signatures at the source level. In particular, ImCoh consistently provides the most reliable and discriminative fingerprints, especially in the alpha band during EC at rest. This work highlights the importance of selecting connectivity measures that preserve inter-individual variability and supports the use of phase-based, non-zero-lag metrics for EEG connectome fingerprinting. Together, these results could provide a methodological framework for EEG-based identification and open new perspectives for clinical applications.
