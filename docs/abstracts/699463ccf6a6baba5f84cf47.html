Introduction

The behavioral variant of frontotemporal dementia (bvFTD) is marked by profound socioemotional and executive disturbances that frequently manifest as disinhibited actions1 and, in a substantial subset, criminal behavior (CB). Converging evidence indicates that disinhibition—failure to suppress prepotent responses and adhere to social norms—together with impaired emotional processing, constitutes a necessary condition for CB in bvFTD. Yet, the neurobiological mechanisms that specifically link disinhibition to offending remain insufficiently defined. Prior work emphasizes anterior temporal lobe degeneration, often with a right-hemispheric localization, and implicate frontal and temporal brain regions2, however, the lateralization, regional specificity, and network-level pathways through which disinhibition promotes CB are unresolved. Moreover, it is unclear whether disinhibition explains CB above and beyond other hallmark bvFTD features, such as apathy and broader executive dysfunction.

To address these gaps, we use magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) within the German Consortium for Frontotemporal Lobar Degeneration (FTLD) to investigate structural substrates of CB in bvFTD. Using voxel- and surface-based morphometry, we test the hypothesis that CB in bvFTD primarily reflects disinhibition-related degeneration within anterior temporal systems. By coupling imaging with the Frontal Systems Behavior Scale, we aim to establish behavioral specificity, disentangling disinhibition from co-occurring syndromic features and clarifying the role of frontal and temporal brain regions including the anterior superior temporal gyrus. Elucidating disinhibition-related mechanisms in bvFTD is crucial for refining phenotyping, forecasting risk for harmful behaviors, and informing targeted interventions that restore inhibitory control and social comportment.

Methods

A total of 475 patients in the multicenter German FTLD consortium met the diagnostic criteria for bvFTD1.  To investigate frontal behavioral syndromes with an emphasis on disinhibition, we administered a modified informant-based Frontal Systems Behavior Scale (FrSBe). This version quantifies both frequency and distress for three domains—apathy, disinhibition, and executive dysfunction—yielding domain-specific subscale scores and a total score. Our primary behavioral variables were the disinhibition frequency and disinhibition-related distress subscales. Apathy and executive dysfunction were examined as complementary measures.

High-resolution structural MRI and FrSBe scores were available for 66 bvFTD patients, of whom 13 showed CB (5 female; age 67.7 ± 8.8 years) and 53 showed no CB (27 female; age 62.9 ± 9.3 years). T1-weighted images were acquired using an MP-RAGE sequence. Images were processed with voxel-based morphometry (VBM) and surface-based cortical thickness estimation using the Computational Anatomy Toolbox (CAT12, rev. 12.8.2) running in Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM12, rev. 7771) under Matlab R2022b. Standard CAT12 pipelines were applied, including tissue segmentation, normalization to MNI space, modulation to derive gray matter density (GMD) maps, and smoothing according to toolbox defaults. Surface reconstructions were generated to obtain vertex-wise cortical thickness.

To test for a link between brain structure and behavioral impairment, whole-brain analysis was performed with SPM12 using a general linear model with a full-factorial design implementing an interaction between the factors ‘group’ (bvFTD patients with and without CB) and ‘behavior’ using all FrSBe subscales. Additional nuisance covariates (age, sex, and TIV) were included. Contrast images were then computed to assess associations between brain structure (gray matter density and cortical thickness) and FrSBe scores within both subgroups of patients with and without CB. Interaction contrasts between the factors ‘group’ and ‘behavior’ were also specified to test whether structure–behavior correlations differed between patients with and without CB across all FrSBe subscales. Significant results were obtained using a voxel/vertex threshold of p < 0.001 and a cluster extent k > 500. Multiple-comparisons control used family-wise error (FWE) correction at the cluster level (pFWE < 0.05; Friston et al., 1994). To further limit false positives, clusters were only reported if their peak voxel/vertex survived peak-level FWE correction at pFWE < 0.05. All tests were whole-brain, correcting across the entire search volume.

To complement voxel- and vertex-wise tests, we ran region-based analyses in CAT12 using the same SPM design matrices (SPM.mat) using the LONI probabilistic brain atlas and the Desikan–Killiany atlas, respectively. We tested correlations between regional GMD/cortical thickness and FrSBe subscales within CB-bvFTD and NoCB-bvFTD groups as well as group-by-behavior interactions. Multiple comparisons were controlled using false discovery rate (FDR q = 0.05).

Results

Using the diagnostic criteria for the behavioral variant of frontotemporal dementia1, patients with bvFTD and CB exhibited significantly greater disinhibition—particularly socially inappropriate behavior and impulsive, rash, or careless actions—and less apathy than patients with bvFTD without CB.

Using voxel-wise analyses with an FWE correction, we found no significant positive or negative correlations between GMD and any of the six FrSBe subscales in either patients with CB or those without CB. Likewise, no voxel-wise interactions between any FrSBe subscale and the group factor were detected. In contrast, region-based analyses revealed a significant negative correlation between GMD and distress associated with disinhibition in the subgroup of patients with CB (Fig. 1A), with the strongest effect in the left superior temporal gyrus (STG). Additional significant negative associations were observed across the left temporal lobe and in the precentral gyrus, fusiform gyrus, hippocampus, and putamen (Fig. 1A). No significant positive or negative correlations were observed in the subgroup without CB (Fig. 1B). Finally, region-based interaction analyses showed a significant interaction between group and distress of disinhibition, indicating that the relationship between GMD and distress of disinhibition differed between bvFTD patients with and without CB. This effect was widespread, encompassing regions in the left temporal and frontal lobes and bilateral subcortical structures, including the putamen and caudate (Fig. 1C).

Consistent with our region-based GMD findings, cortical-thickness analyses linked distress of disinhibition to regional atrophy. Vertex-wise analysis showed a significant negative correlation between disinhibition-related distress and cortical thickness in the left anterior STG specifically in patients showing CB (Fig. 2A), and no such association was observed in patients without CB (Fig. 2B). A significant interaction between the factors ‘group’ and ‘distress of disinhibition’ confirmed that this thickness–disinhibition relationship was specific to patients showing bvFTD with CB (Fig. 2C). Note that in all vertex-wise analyses within the bvFTD subgroups, only the FrSBe subscale ‘distress of disinhibition’ showed a significant association with cortical thickness. No other positive or negative vertex-wise correlations were observed in either patients with or without CB, and no significant interactions were detected between any other FrSBe covariate and the group factor.

In line with the vertex-wise results, region-based analyses using the Desikan–Killiany atlas revealed a significant negative correlation between cortical thickness and FrSBe disinhibition-distress scores. This effect was strongest in bvFTD with CB, peaking in the left STG within the left temporal lobe, and extended to additional regions in the frontal and parietal cortices. In contrast, patients without CB did not show any significant correlations. A significant interaction between ‘group’ and ‘distress of disinhibition’ confirmed that the negative correlation is specific to bvFTD with CB.

Conclusion

In bvFTD with CB, disinhibition correlated significantly with brain structure in both voxel- and vertex-wise analysis. No such relationship was observed in bvFTD without CB. In addition, there was a significant interaction between the factors ‘group’ (bvFTD with vs without CB) and ‘distress of disinhibition’, indicating that the disinhibition–structure association differs between groups. Clinically, within bvFTD, CB was associated with greater disinhibition, higher verbal fluency, and less apathy. Together, these findings suggest that disinhibition is the primary driver of CB in our bvFTD cohort.

In contrast to relate CB to brain structure alterations in the right temporal lobe, our data show disinhibition as a general temporal-lobe phenomenon in bvFTD with CB. Rather than a strictly right-lateralized process, we observed an interhemispheric imbalance of temporal atrophy that engages both hemispheres. This aligns with prior work linking disinhibition to structures in both left and right temporal cortex3,4. In bvFTD, disinhibition scales negatively with gray-matter density across multiple temporal regions and limbic structures, including the hippocampus and amygdala4. In our cohort, the strongest structure–behavior association localized to the left anterior STG, overlapping the region identified by Paholpak and colleagues5 in a mixed-dementia sample, where disinhibition mapped to the left anterior STG and was less pronounced in Alzheimer’s disease than in bvFTD. In line, metabolic imaging similarly ties disinhibition to temporal-lobe involvement and shows higher disinhibition scores in FTD than Alzheimer’s disease3.

In sum, the stronger association between disinhibition and left anterior STG atrophy in bvFTD with CB suggests that heightened disinhibition in the context of left temporal degeneration contributes to CB, highlighting left anterior temporal systems within a bilateral network as a key substrate of disinhibition. Future work should employ longitudinal, multimodal, and pathologically confirmed cohorts to test causal pathways and lateralization and to identify modifiable network targets.
