Introduction

People’s ability to plan is remarkably efficient in the face of complex everyday environments. As the number of multiple multi-step trajectories in a decision tree increases, so too does the intractability of representing all aspects of the problem under limited cognitive resources1. Take, for example, navigating between Camden Market and Buckingham Palace: one must consider all possible tube stations along the route to plan a route. Yet, humans are normally capable of solving such problems with ease. How do individuals construct plans under such constraints? 

One promising approach to modelling how individuals efficiently plan is the idea that people build simplified mental representations of the problem space. The value-guided construal (VGC) model assumes that people select the appropriate level of information required to successfully complete a task, in a resource-rational fashion2. According to the VGC model, a cognitively limited decision-maker selects a subset of information, termed a task representation, over which they plan. The information included in mental representations balances both utility and complexity. In our example, the VGC model would predict that an individual would plan only along the relevant tube lines to Camden Market, rather than considering all possible routes. The model proposes that efficient planning involves two nested optimization loops: an outer loop of construal, and an inner loop that plans conditional on a particular task representation2. To validate the VGC model, participants reported their awareness of obstacles in a maze navigation paradigm (Fig 1a).

Two key limitations of the previous work remain unaddressed. First, participants reported their mental representations on a scale which is several steps removed from the construal process. Second, the previous work does not shed light on the temporal dynamics of how individuals construct simplified mental representations. 

Methods

To explore the temporal dynamics of how individuals construct simplified mental representations we relied on a maze navigation paradigm with simultaneous recordings of magnetoencephalography (MEG). We conducted a feasibility study to determine whether we could track neural dynamics of simplified mental representations using Rapid Invisible Frequency Tagging (RIFT)3.

Participants (N=6) took part in a two-part experiment. In the first behavioural session, participants completed the maze navigation paradigm. Participants returned after a delay of ~3-7 days and performed a similar maze navigation task while simultaneous MEG was recorded. The two tasks were identical except that participants did not navigate maze stimuli on a third of the trials during the MEG scan. This ensured that scan times remained under 2 hours for all participants.

Participants were asked to move an avatar from a starting location to the goal location using the arrow keys (Fig 1a). Each maze consisted of an 11x11 grid with 6 blue obstacles and black central walls in the shape of a fixation cross which participants needed to navigate around. All trials began with a fixation cross, after which participants were prompted to navigate the maze, and report their awareness of all obstacles presented on that trial. Participants’ awareness reports served as a proxy for their simplified mental representation.

We fitted the VGC model to our maze stimuli to derive normative predictions for what aspects of the maze people should be aware of while planning. The VGC model assumes that an optimal decision-maker combines a subset of cause-and-effect relationships to represent their environment in the aid of making planning more efficient. The optimal construal maximizes the value of representation (VOR), defined for each possible construal as: VOR(c) = U(πc) − C(c), where U(πc) is the utility of a construed plan πc, and C(c) represents the cost of keeping that information in mind2.

We modelled the awareness of each obstacle in the maze as the marginalized probability of each obstacle ibeing included within a construal2. This marginalized probability, P(Obstaclei), is used for every obstacle in each maze to predict participants’ awareness reports. Obstacles with P(Obstaclei) larger than 0.5 were deemed task relevant, while obstacles with P(Obstaclei), less than 0.5 were deemed task irrelevant.

Participants navigated 24 maze stimuli, 12 of which had task-relevant obstacles (as predicted by the VGC model), lateralized to a single hemifield4. Our previous work demonstrated that participants’ simplified mental representations for lateralized maze stimuli were more aligned with the VGC model.

We relied on RIFT to track the neural processing of task-relevant and irrelevant stimuli3. Here, stimuli were shown to participants using the Propixx projector at 480Hz. We frequency tagged task-relevant obstacles and task-irrelevant obstacles at 60 and 68.5 Hz, in a counterbalanced fashion across blocks. Frequency tagging was achieved by modulating the RGB values of the obstacles such that they oscillated between white and blue, with participants perceiving a steady image of a light blue obstacle (Fig. 1a). All participants reported being unaware of the frequency tagging. The tagging response of task-relevant and irrelevant stimuli was monitored using two photodiodes placed on the outer corners of the screen.

Coherence between brain activity recorded over occipital sensors and the photodiodes was computed using the weighted phase lag index (wPLI), as implemented in Brainstorm. We computed time-resolved wPLI using the Hilbert transform using 0.5-second sliding windows with 90% overlap. 

Instantaneous alpha power (8-12Hz) was computed using the Hilbert transform. Alpha power was baseline corrected between -750ms to 0ms prior to the onset of the obstacles. The alpha lateralization index (ALI)5 was computed as the ratio of alpha power as follows: ALI=  (⍺right - ⍺left )/ (⍺right  +  ⍺left)

Results

First, we sought to test whether the sVGC model captured participants' simplified mental representations. We ran hierarchical linear regression models to predict participants’ awareness reports from the sVGC model predictions. Our behavioural results corroborate that individuals build simplified mental representations: the sVGC model significantly predicted participants’ awareness reports (β1 = 0.43, SE = 0.01, 95% CI [0.41, 0.46]; Fig. 1b), replicating previous work2,4. Note that the task-relevance of obstacles is obtained from the VGC model predictions.

Our previous behavioural work demonstrated that mental representations are constrained by inductive attentional biases4—we showed that the spatial context in which information is presented affects participants' simplified mental representations. To reproduce these findings in the present study, we generated two sets of maze stimuli, one whose model predictions were lateralized to a single hemi-field (i.e., lateralized) and one that were not (i.e., non-lateralized). We hypothesized that participants would select task-relevant information with greater ease – constructing task representations more closely aligned with the sVGC model –when task-relevant information is spatially confined to a visual hemifield (i.e., presented unilaterally). We make this prediction based on previous work on visual crowding effects and the attentional spotlight. This is precisely what we observed (moderation: β = 0.05, SE = 0.01, 95% CI [0.02, 0.08]; Fig. 1b).

Concerning our neural data, we tested whether we observed a neural response to the RIFT at both targeted frequencies (i.e., 60 and 68.5 Hz) by computing the percent change in coherence between the stimulus and brain activity from baseline. We collapsed across blocks, task-relevance, and maze type, to compute the RIFT response. Our results indicated an increased coherence in the desired frequency bands (i.e., 60 and 68 Hz) relative to baseline, validating our methodology (Fig. 2a). The RIFT response was stronger (i.e., higher percent change in coherence) for stimuli tagged at 60Hz. These findings suggest that our RIFT manipulation was successful.

We then tested whether the RIFT response differed between task-relevant and irrelevant stimuli throughout planning. A difference in coherence of task-relevant and irrelevant stimuli would indicate that participants are preferentially processing one type of information over the other, and therefore corroborate the neural time course of simplified mental representations. We observed little evidence for a difference in coherence generated by task-relevant and irrelevant stimuli when collapsing across all maze types.

We then explored whether maze type (i.e., lateralized vs non-lateralized) significantly impacted the coherence of task-relevant and irrelevant stimuli. Here, we observed a difference in the coherence of task-relevant and irrelevant stimuli at 200 ms, for both tagged frequencies, specifically for lateralized maze stimuli (Fig. 2b). These findings corroborate our behavioural findings and suggest that participants are able to rapidly form simplified mental representations more closely aligned to the sVGC model for lateralized maze stimuli.

Last, we explored the neural dynamics of alpha power lateralization across the posterior cortex as a proxy measure of attention. To do so, we computed an awareness lateralization index (ALI) for each trial, which reflects the tendency of participants to represent information preferentially in a single hemifield. We then rank-ordered trials based on participants’ ALI reports into three bins: those with significant left lateralization, right, and non-lateralized trials. We observed that alpha power in the posterior cortex was lateralized accordingly to participants’ self-reports around 700 ms after maze onset, which dovetails prior work on spatial attention (Fig. 2c).

Conclusion

We assessed the feasibility of tracking the neural dynamics of simplified mental representations using RIFT in a pilot study. Here, we relied on a previous maze navigation paradigm to test the neural dynamics of how participants construct simplified mental representations. We validated our RIFT methodology and demonstrated that participants showed a significant tagging response. Our findings showed that people’s simplified mental representations are built relatively early on in planning. We found neural evidence for simplified mental representations specifically for maze stimuli in which the model predictions of task relevance are lateralized to a single hemifield. These neural findings are aligned with our behavioural results and our previous work4, emphasizing that individuals’ have an easier time forming simplified mental representations when they are lateralized to one or other hemifield. Alpha lateralization similarly tracked how participants oriented their attention during planning. Taken together, our pilot study demonstrates the feasibility of using pioneering neuroimaging techniques to track how people build simplified mental representations across time. We anticipate that our findings will lay the foundations for further investigations into the neural dynamics of efficient planning.
