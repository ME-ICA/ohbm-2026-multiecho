Introduction

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) is an effective treatment for Parkinson’s disease (PD) but its neural mechanisms remain poorly understood. A mechanistic understanding requires precise characterization of functional responses to various stimulation conditions within the same individual. However, neuromodulation therapies face two major challenges: the limited understanding of the biological effects of stimulation and the need for personalized treatment strategies due to significant individual variability in brain functional organization. 

Precision neuroimaging with longitudinal densely sampling data for each individual shows great potentials to reveal the large-scale circuit responses to DBS, using high-field MRI (3.0T). However, MRI compatibility issues with implanted DBS hardware—especially concerns about radiofrequency heating, scanning length, and field strength limitations —have historically restricted data collection, especially the precision neuroimaging data. The recent development of 3T MRI-compatible DBS systems has mitigated these challenges, enabling more comprehensive and longitudinal fMRI data collection under different stimulation conditions at the individual level.

Methods

Here, we used 3T MRI-compatible DBS and precision neuroimaging to collect extensive data from 14 PD patients implanted with DBS.1 Across five timepoints spanning one year (preoperative, 1, 3, 6, and 12-month after DBS implantation), each patient underwent up to 11.7-hour fMRI under seven stimulation conditions (30-172 minutes/session), including interleaved stimulation ON-OFF block-design and continuous resting-state paradigms with high-frequency (130 Hz), low-frequency (60 Hz), and variable-frequency (alternating between 130 and 60 Hz) DBS frequency settings. In addition, up to 2.2 hours of structural MRI (26 min/session) and 1.3 hours of diffusion MRI (16 min/session) were acquired per patient (Fig. 1). Behavioral outcomes were independently evaluated by two neurologists at each stimulation condition. Imaging data were also collected from 27 healthy participants. 

This uniquely rich and high-resolution dataset—integrating longitudinal, multimodal imaging dimensions with various stimulation conditions—enabled reliable mapping of DBS-modulated functional circuits at both individual and group levels. We have released the dataset, including all raw and preprocessed data, at https://doi.org/10.57760/sciencedb.26302.

Results

We identified two distinct large-scale functional circuits responding to STN-DBS: an activated globus pallidus (GP) circuit, including GP, thalamus and posterior cerebellum; and a deactivated primary motor cortex (M1) circuit, comprising M1, posterior putamen, and anterior cerebellum. The activation of the GP circuit was frequency-dependent, showing maximal activation under high-frequency stimulation (130 Hz), whereas the deactivation of the M1 circuit  was time-dependent, gradually increasing with longer stimulation, with strongest deactivation at 12 months after DBS (Fig. 2). 

Focusing on the M1, we found that the recently identified somato-cognitive action network (SCAN)2 exhibited significantly stronger DBS-induced responses than effector motor regions (foot, hand, and mouth motor regions). The stimulated STN sites were most strongly connected to the SCAN among all cortical functional networks, likely reflecting engagement of the hyperdirect pathway.3 A key finding was the divergent effect of DBS—it normalized functional connectivity within the SCAN but de-normalized it in effector networks.

Conclusion

Neuroimaging is one of the most suitable recording tools to investigate the circuit mechanism of DBS, but has been hindered by MRI safety concerns. This densely-sampled precision neuroimaging dataset of DBS provided reliable, individual-specific functional measures and revealed the large-scale functional circuit responses to DBS. The unique and rich dataset is shared with the community to accelerate research into DBS mechanisms and improve personalized treatment strategies.
