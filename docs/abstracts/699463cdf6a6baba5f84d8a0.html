Introduction

Music listening engages distributed brain networks beyond the primary auditory cortex, and acoustic and music-structural features can explain brain responses across large-scale systems (Alluri et al., 2012; Toiviainen et al., 2014). Converging evidence also shows that strongly affective and pleasurable music recruits limbic/paralimbic and reward-related areas, consistent with coordinated auditory-affective-evaluation processing (Blood & Zatorre, 2001; Zatorre & Salimpoor, 2013). A central challenge is to disentangle which stimulus aspects drive functional coupling by separating acoustic structure from higher-level emotion descriptors. We test a feature-specific modular pipeline of how music-evoked emotions are implemented in functional brain networks during naturalistic listening. Building on a hierarchical model from sound to emotion, we ask whether a reproducible “connectivity fingerprint” can dissociate acoustic/music contributions from emotion-related contributions within the same stimuli.

To address this question, we developed BOSS (Brain Orchestra Synchronization Study), a modular MATLAB toolbox for feature-loading connectivity analysis. BOSS is designed as a configurable framework rather than a single fixed pipeline: users can flexibly choose source/target definitions (atlas-based, ROI-to-ROI, or whole-brain searchlights), swap synchronization metrics (e.g., correlation, PCA-assisted multivariate coupling, CCA), and plug in alternative feature sets and models. Here, we report one instantiation of the toolbox by using a CCA synchronization metric deployed through searchlight while comparing a full model including both acoustic and emotion predictors and residual models where a feature family is regressed out. Percentage drops in canonical correlation between full and residual models yield a domain-contribution metric that can be mapped across the brain to reveal feature-specific connectivity fingerprints.

Methods

Dataset and regions of interest. The framework is applied to an fMRI dataset (Williams et al., 2022) in which 25 participants listened to approximately 40 minutes of continuous music; runs are concatenated per participant. Source regions of interest (ROIs) are defined using the Glasser/HCP parcellation and include primary auditory cortex (A1), the amygdala, the temporo-parieto-occipital junction (TPOJ), and the anterior cingulate cortex area a24 (ACC). Connectivity is estimated between each source ROI and distributed target spheres across the brain. Analyses are conducted separately for left and right audio channels using contralateral hemispheric seeds to respect channel-specific stimulus structure.

Toolbox modularity (BOSS). BOSS implements feature-loading connectivity as a modular, two-stage pipeline. In a group-level preparation step, data across participants are combined to create a common mask/reference image and to fit model parameters (e.g., canonical weights) that define a shared representational space; in a subject-level step, these group-derived parameters are applied to each participant to generate feature-specific connectivity maps, later aggregated for inference and exported in NIfTI format. The toolbox exposes interchangeable “target models” that factor the synchronization metric (e.g., mean correlation; CCA; CCA after dimensionality reduction, such as PCA) and the target summarization/iteration strategy (e.g., ROI-to-ROI, iterate all atlas regions, or whole-brain searchlight with configurable radius/padding). New models can be integrated via the object-oriented architecture, including non-linear/dynamic extensions discussed in the toolbox design. 

Feature construction and selection. Acoustic predictors are derived from the audio waveforms using MIRToolbox and Essentia descriptors (spectral, temporal, timbral, rhythmic, and music features), together with deep VGGish embeddings. Features are aligned to the BOLD signal (sampling rate 1 Hz), and reduced via PCA to a compact set of components. Emotion predictors are obtained from an online behavioral study with continuous ratings of 10 basic emotion categories, complemented by outputs from pretrained emotion-classification models. Measures are projected into a shared PCA space capturing latent affective dimensions. Feature redundancy is quantified using Partial Variance Analysis.

BOSS connectivity model and “residual drop” logic. For each seed-target sphere pair, we estimate multivariate coupling with CCA. Canonical weights are fit on group-averaged data and then applied to individual participants to extract canonical variates and correlations in a common space. We compute full-model synchrony (all predictors) and residual-model synchrony (after regressing out one feature family from voxel time series). Domain contribution is quantified as the percentage drop in canonical correlation between full and residual models.

Inference and correction. At the group level, full versus residual maps are compared using paired t-tests on differences in canonical correlations across participants. Family-wise error is controlled with permutation-based max-statistic correction across searchlights and canonical components. Residual estimates are stabilized using synchronized permutations of feature regressors, ensuring that the drop metric reflects domain-specific stimulus structure rather than unsystematic variability. 

Results

Feature structure. Feature analyses show that acoustic and emotion predictors capture complementary aspects of the stimulus. MIR features display relatively low redundancy with emotion regressors, with components dominated by loudness and MFCC, while emotion components are dominated by valence/arousal-like axes and Scare, Tension affective categories.

A1-centered networks reveal a subcortical emotion-linked pathway. For both acoustic and emotion models, A1 shows strong bilateral auditory-cortex synchronization (belt/parabelt complexes). The emotion feature set produces a robust mapping of the inferior colliculus (IC) and related ascending auditory structures, indicating that emotion-regressor removal significantly reduces A1 coupling with midbrain auditory relays. The medial geniculate body (MGN) also shows significant drops for both feature domains, with an especially robust ipsilateral effect reported for the right hemisphere (peak drop ~7.29% in an MGN cluster). Across A1 results, peak drop values span from mid-single digits to larger effects in specific cortical clusters.

Amygdala-centered networks emphasize memory/default-mode coupling with domain differences. Connectivity drops linked to amygdala synchronization appear in temporal regions and limbic structures, including bilateral hippocampus, temporal gyri, and perirhinal-ectorhinal cortex. The retrosplenial cortex (RSC) is highlighted as showing feature-dependent cluster distributions, with emotion-related clusters partially overlapping with acoustic-driven ones but differing in spatial extent and laterality.

ACC (a24) and TPJ-related patterns suggest integrative hubs with asymmetric channel effects. The a24 seed shows prominent coupling with precuneus and retrosplenial regions for both feature sets, but with strong channel asymmetries: right-channel results (left-hemisphere seed) are markedly more extensive. Emotion features elicit strong drops in contralateral hippocampal connectivity and reveal subcortical involvement of the globus pallidus externus, consistent with an auditory-basal-ganglia loop supporting affective aspects of music. Acoustic features, by contrast, yield broader large-cluster effects in posterior medial regions for the right channel. TPOJ results include clusters spanning auditory-adjacent cortex and temporal regions (with reported peak drop values into the ~10-15% range in some clusters), consistent with a role in higher-order integration during naturalistic listening.

Meta-analytic decoding supports domain selectivity of the results. Meta-analytic decoding with Neurosynth is used to assess the construct validity of the connectivity fingerprints. Emotion-driven clusters show greater overlap with literature tagged by emotion-related terms, whereas acoustic-driven clusters are more strongly associated with auditory topics. A chi-square test confirms a significant difference in domain association between feature sets (χ² = 12.56, p < 0.05), supporting feature-specific selectivity of the identified networks. Behavioral data indicate that participants were generally healthy, had low levels of formal musical training, and experienced the task as engaging but not intensely emotional, suggesting that the observed networks primarily reflect recognized rather than intensely felt emotion.

Conclusion

This study presents a feature-loading approach to functional connectivity that quantifies how stimulus domains shape multivariate synchronization during naturalistic music listening. By contrasting full connectivity with residual connectivity models (after regressing out acoustic or emotion predictors) and by using group-trained CCA weights to define cross-participant inference, the framework identifies separable connectivity fingerprints: acoustic features predominantly modulate auditory-cortical and broadly sensorimotor/associative couplings, whereas emotion features make distinctive contributions to limbic circuitry and subcortical auditory relays such as the inferior colliculus and thalamic auditory nuclei. Meta-analytic decoding further confirms that these networks differentially align with emotion versus auditory-related literature currently available. Overall, the findings support the thesis that music-evoked emotional content is instantiated in distinct functional pathways within the auditory system and interactions with affective and integrative hubs.
