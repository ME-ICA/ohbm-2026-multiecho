Introduction

With more than 250 million axons, the corpus callosum (CC) is the largest commissural bridge of white-matter bundles connecting the left and right hemispheres in the human brain 1. Variation in CC architecture has been associated with high-level cognitive functions, including executive and attentional functions to memory, language, and socio-emotional abilities 2. Moreover, alteration in CC architecture have been consistently reported in neurodevelopmental and neuropsychiatric disorders, including autism spectrum disorder and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder.

The formation of the corpus callosum depends on tightly regulated axonal guidance mechanisms occurring during early prenatal development. Among the key contributors are NRP1 and DCC, which guide axons toward and across the midline, ROBO1, which prevents inappropriate ventral misrouting, and IQCJ-SCHIP1, which supports intracellular axonal organisation and growth 3. Although these molecular mechanisms operate during a relatively restricted prenatal window, they lay the foundation for subsequent callosal development.

Diffusion MRI (dMRI) provides in-vivo markers of white-matter microstructure that are sensitive to developmental, genetic and cognitive differences. However, averaging these measures across entire tracts limits the ability to detect focal changes or spatially specific variations along its course.

To move beyond tract-averaged approaches, this study aimed to provide a fine-grained characterisation of spatial variation in callosal microstructure and to determine its developmental, genetic and cognitive correlates. More specifically, leveraging multimodal data from the Philadelphia Neurodevelopmental Cohort (PNC 4) (ages 8–21) and a functional data analysis framework, we characterised the developmental trajectory of spatial variation in callosal microstructure and assessed their associations with selected genetic variants and behavioural outcomes.

Methods

This study used diffusion-weighted imaging, cognitive and genetic data from 1342 participants (ages 8–21) of the PNC. 

dMRI scans were acquired at University of Pennsylvania on a 3-T Siemens TIM Trio scanner (Siemens Medical Solutions), acquired using a twice-refocused spin-echo single-shot echo-planar imaging sequence sequence (FoV 240 × 240 mm; matrix 128 × 128 × 70; 64 gradient directions; b = 1000 s/mm2; voxel size 1.875 × 1.875 × 2 mm) [61]. Single-shell diffusion-weighted images were preprocessed using QSIPrep (v0.19.1), which integrates tools from Nipype, MRtrix3, and FSL. Two diffusion-weighted series were concatenated after individual preprocessing, with volumes below 100 s/mm² treated as b0 images. Data were denoised using MP-PCA, intensity-normalized across runs, and corrected for B1 field inhomogeneities using the N4 algorithm. Head motion and eddy current distortions were corrected using FSL’s eddy with slice-wise outlier detection and replacement, followed by resampling to ACPC space at 1.875 mm isotropic resolution; framewise displacement was computed as a motion metric. Diffusion tensor imaging was applied to derive whole-brain FA and MD maps. Multi-tissue response functions were estimated using the Dhollander algorithm, and fibre orientation densities were reconstructed using single-shell optimized multi-tissue constrained spherical deconvolution, followed by intensity normalization. Corpus callosum white-matter bundles were segmented into seven subdivisions using TractSeg, a convolutional neural network–based approach operating on voxelwise FOD peaks. Each tract was divided into 100 anatomically corresponding segments using an assignment mapping approach, enabling consistent along-tract correspondence across participants. Segment-wise FA values were then extracted, yielding 98 reliable along-tract measures per tract after excluding endpoints.

Cognitive functions were assessed via the Penn computerized neurocognitive battery spanning 12 computerised tests. A factor analysis was performed on the efficiency scores (accuracy + reaction time) guided by previous findings supporting a four-factor structure. The four neurocognitive factors correspond to four domains: executive functioning, episodic memory, complex cognition, and social cognition. 

Genotype data from PNC participants with MRI were obtained from dbGaP (phs000607.v2.p2) and generated using seven Affymetrix and Illumina arrays across eight batches. Quality control was conducted per batch using PLINK, including the removal of individuals with >5% missing genotypes, SNPs with >5% missingness, minor allele frequency <1%, or Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium p-value <1e-6.  Allele coding was standardised, genome builds harmonised to GRCh37 using liftOver (hg18ToHg19.over.chain.gz, accessed June 2025), and strand alignment and allele normalisation performed with bcftools version 1.21 using 1000 Genomes Phase 3 references. Genotype imputation was performed chromosome-wise using IMPUTE5 leveraging the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 reference panel and GRCh37 genetic maps. Ambiguous and multiallelic SNPs were removed, and shared variants were iteratively merged into a final dataset. LD-pruned PCA was used to derive ancestry components, with ten PCs retained as covariates in statistical models describe below. Candidate gene regions for NRP1, DCC, ROBO1 and IQCJ-SCHIP1 were defined using Ensembl GRCh37 annotations and extended by ±10 kb.

All statistical analyses were conducted using a two-stage functional data analysis framework, that models both global (penalised functional regression, PFR) and local (function-on-scalar regression, FOSR) effects of spatial variation in callosal tract metrics, using the R refund package. This approach allowed to examine associations with age, selected genetic variants, and behavioural outcomes (post-hoc analyses). 

Results

Along-tract developmental effects: Based on AIC comparisons, the best-fitting PFR models revealed significant global associations between age and along-tract FA across all seven callosal tracts (FDR-corrected p ≤ .002, Figure 2). The proportion of variance explained by age ranged from modest to moderate across tracts (adjusted R² = 0.03–0.20), with the strongest associations observed in the anterior midbody (adjusted R² = 0.20), genu (0.15), rostral body (0.14), and isthmus (0.13). Smaller but significant effects were found in the rostrum, posterior midbody, and splenium (adjusted R² = 0.03–0.06). FOSR analyses further demonstrated heterogeneous local age effects along each tract, indicating that age–FA associations vary spatially along callosal pathways. The rostrum showed relatively stable positive age effects across segments, whereas the genu, rostral body, anterior midbody, and isthmus exhibited marked segment-specific variability, with the largest positive effects emerging toward distal tract portions. In contrast, the posterior midbody and splenium showed more uniform along-tract FA patterns, with overall small effect sizes and a mix of positive and minor negative local associations.

Along-tract association with selected genetics variants: We examined along-tract associations for a total of 1370 genetic variants, including 160 SNPs in NRP1, 519 SNPs in DCC, 329 SNPs in ROBO1, and 362 SNPs in IQCJ-SCHIP1. PFR models revealed significant global associations between along-tract FA pattern and selected genetic variants for all seven callosal tracts. The along-tract FA of all seven subdivisions were significantly associated with several DCC genetics variants (FDR-corrected p = 0.002 to p = 0.02). The proportion of variance explained by the models ranged from weak to strong across the seven callosal tracts (adjusted R² = 0.08 - 0.74). Similarly, IQCJ-SCHIP1 and ROBO1 showed significant association with along-tract FA of all seven subdivisions but with the proportion of variance explained by the models ranging from modest to weak (FDR-corrected p < 0.001 to p = 0.04; adjusted R² = 0.05 - 0.44). Finally, NRP1 showed significant associations only with along-tract FA of the rostrum, rostral midbody, isthmus and splenium with a proportion of variance ranging from modest to weak (FDR-corrected p < 0.001 to p = 0.04; adjusted R² = 0.06 - 0.26). FOSR analyses further indicated heterogeneous segment-level FA–gene associations, with pronounced along-tract variability for DCC, ROBO1, IQCJ-SCHIP1, and NRP1 across the seven callosal subdivisions.

Behavioural outcomes and its relationship with along-tract FA: PFR models revealed significant global associations between along-tract callosal FA and all four behavioural factors. Complex cognition was associated with spatial FA variation across all callosal tracts except the anterior midbody, while memory and executive functioning showed significant FA associations across all seven tracts (FDR-corrected p ≤ 0.01). Social cognition showed along-tract FA associations, limited to the rostral body and posterior midbody. FOSR analyses further indicated heterogeneous segment-level FA–behaviour associations, with greater spatial variability for complex cognition and executive functioning than for memory and social cognition.

Conclusion

Considering the importance of the corpus callosum in human brain organisation and aiming to overcome the limitations of tract-averaged diffusion measures, we conducted a fine-grained characterisation of spatial variation along callosal tracts and examined how these patterns relate to developmental, genetic, and behavioural processes.

Consistent with prior work using tract-averaged metrics, we observed overall increases in callosal FA with age across childhood and adolescence. Extending beyond averaged measures, our results demonstrate that callosal maturation follows finely differentiated spatial patterns, with each callosal subdivision exhibiting a distinct along-tract profile of age-related effects. Among frontal callosal tracts, the rostrum showed the most spatially stable age association, suggesting a relatively homogeneous maturation pattern despite ongoing orbitofrontal development. In contrast, tracts connecting frontal and parietal regions (genu, rostral body, anterior midbody, isthmus) showed pronounced spatial heterogeneity, with the largest FA increases toward distal segments near cortical terminations. Interpreted as reflecting myelination, these patterns align with evidence that cortical myelination is prolonged and spatially non-uniform in heteromodal and unimodal association cortices, remaining dynamic well into adulthood 5. By comparison, callosal fibres connecting sensorimotor and occipital cortices (posterior midbody and splenium) showed weaker and more uniform age associations, consistent with earlier maturation and greater stability of these systems. Overall, these findings indicate that typical callosal maturation is spatially differentiated, varying both across subdivisions and along tract length.

Such spatial variability in callosal microstructure across development suggests these patterns may still manifest organisational constraints imposed by early axon-guidance processes. We therefore assessed associations with genetic variants implicated in callosal axon guidance. Consistent with this hypothese, we observed robust associations between along-tract FA patterns and genetic variants implicated in axon guidance. Variants in DCC, ROBO1, and IQCJ-SCHIP1 were associated with along-tract FA across all callosal subdivisions, whereas NRP1 showed more regionally restricted effects. Segment-level analyses further revealed pronounced spatial heterogeneity, indicating that genetic influences on callosal microstructure are not uniform but vary systematically along tract length.

After characterising developmental and genetic correlates of along-tract FA, we assessed whether these spatial patterns were behaviourally meaningful. Across cognitive domains, spatial variation in callosal FA showed significant and domain-specific associations with behavioural performance. Complex cognition and executive functioning displayed widespread and spatially heterogeneous associations, consistent with their reliance on distributed interhemispheric integration. In contrast, memory and social cognition showed more circumscribed and homogeneous patterns of association. Contrary to expectations, memory-related effects were observed in anterior callosal tracts rather than temporal pathways such as the isthmus. This may be explained by the efficiency-based nature of the memory measure, which likely recruits executive control processes. Associations with social cognition were also more circumscribed than anticipated based on prior work. 

Overall, leveraging multimodal data and a functional data analyses approach, our findings revealed pronounced spatial heterogeneity in callosal microstructure measured along tract that varied with age across childhood and adolescence. Furthermore, spatial variation in callosal microstructure was associated with genetic variants involved in callosal axon guidance and contributed to individual differences in behavioural outcomes.
