Introduction

Huntington’s Disease (HD) is an autosomal dominant genetic disorder marked by progressive neurodegeneration, which results in significant impairment of behavioral, cognitive, and motor functions. Studies of the morphological effects of HD have identified widespread changes throughout the brain, including decreased volume in the striatum and cerebral white matter and increased cortical volume in preclinical cases, which may affect development.^1

Disease burden in HD is often computed as a product of the cytosine-adenine-guanine (CAG) load and chronological age in the form of the CAP (CAG-age-product) score. Recent advances in the study of brain aging have adopted a neuroimaging-first approach to age-based neurological development, using methods that fit machine learning models to features derived from structural imaging data, with some initial findings in HD suggesting that neuroimaging-based brain age may help better characterise disease progression^2. 

In this work, we continue this novel line of work, applying brain age modeling to HD; however, instead of training brain age models directly on disease populations that can be biased by disease-specific trends, we take a normative modeling approach in which large models are first trained on a large (N=18,084) healthy populations and then applied on a subset (N=129) of HD participants from the PREDICT and PREVENT HD studies in order to quantify deviations from normative brain aging trends. 

Methods

We develop a large normative brain-age model using a sample of N = 18,084 non-affected individuals from the UK Biobank, Human Connectome Project (HCP), HCP-Aging, and HCP-Development datasets. T1 images from these studies were warped to MNI space, normalized, segmented for gray and white matter maps in SPM12 and then smoothed with a gaussian kernel at 6 FWHM. The smoothed gray matter maps from the normative population were then used to train a 3D Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)  based on the architecture from Feng  et al. 2020^3 using 5-fold cross-validation with a fixed seed to avoid model initialization bias across folds.

For inference on HD, 129 participants from the PREDICT and PREVENT HD studies were selected based on the availability of T1 scans and associated demographic and cognitive variables. Baseline T1 scans for the HD cohort were preprocessed identically to the normative cohort, and then the trained 3D-CNNs were applied to these scans to provide brain age estimates. From all 5 models, brain age estimates were computed individually and then averaged to provide an ensemble estimate across all normative folds and model initializations. 

The brain age delta for each participant was then computed as the difference between the predicted brain age and the participant’s chronological age, and bias-corrected^4 prior to evaluation against target variables.

Results

Our normative model fits well on the healthy population, achieving a mean absolute error (MAE) of 3.30782 +- 0.07 and a Pearson’s Correlation Coefficient of 0.98 +- 0.001 on validation data. We found that a GLM controlling for sex, age and total motor score while modulating the brain age delta by chronological age identified strong significant associations with CAG (p_FDR = 0.0079, effect size = 0.77), CAP (p_FDR = 0.021, effect size = 0.68) as well as with single digit modalities test score (p_FDR = 0.033, effect size = -0.6613), trail making test duration A (p_FDR=0.0063, effect size=0.84), and trail making test duration B (p_FDR = 0.00040, effect size = 1.017).  Our integrated gradient attributions show high model attribution in the striatum, as well as in the frontal cortex, cerebellum, and other areas throughout the brain, suggesting that the model leverages widespread neuroanatomical differences, with a focus on regions commonly associated with neurodegeneration in HD.

Conclusion

We present a normative model for structural brain age at baseline and demonstrate its efficacy in an application to cross-sectional differences in HD. Our model identifies significant associations with CAG and CAP scores even without age adjustment; however, when modulating by age, we observe more potent effects and associations with multiple cognitive and behavioral scores, suggesting that larger brain age caps in younger cohorts, for example, are associated with greater genetic load and symptom severity. As our study is currently limited to a small disease cohort at baseline, in future work, we aim to apply this method to the longitudinal study of HD and to larger cohorts, while also exploring new architectures that improve normative brain age estimates.
