Introduction

Sodium ions (Na+) play an important role in cell homeostasis and signal propagation in brain. Sodium (23Na) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the living human brain could therefore provide a wealth of new information leading to a better understanding of brain physiology in both health and disease1. It is however much more difficult to generate clear sodium images compared to conventional hydrogen (proton, 1H) MRI, due to their low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) since the 23Na signal is about 20,000 times lower than the 1H signal in brain1 and very fast decaying due to quadrupolar relaxation dynamics from its spin 3/2. 

A technique called MR fingerprinting (MRF)2 was developed in the last decade to provide quantitative maps of 1H properties (proton density [PD] and relaxation times) in one single acquisition. However, images from both standard 1H MRI or MRF mostly reflect structural changes and cannot directly assess metabolic/physiological processes in tissues. We therefore recently developed three techniques based on 23Na imaging to assess this new physiological information: (1) simultaneous 1H MRF/23Na MRI that can generate maps of tissue sodium concentration (TSC) within the same acquisition of 1H MRF3; (2) correlation-weighted 23Na MRF that can provide TSC and relaxation times in a single acquisition4; (3) a super-resolution (SR) algorithm that can increase the spatial "low resolution" (LR) of the 23Na MR data to match the "high resolution" (HR) of the 1H MR data.

We combined these three methods into a final technique called multinuclear fingerprinting (MNF) that consists of a simultaneous 1H/23Na MRF acquisition followed by the application of the SR algorithm to generate seven exactly co-registered maps of structural 1H MR data (PD, T1, T2) and 23Na (TSC, T1, T2,short, T2,long) with the same spatial and temporal resolution in brain. MNF was tested on the whole brain of seven heatlhy subject at 7 T.

Methods

Imaging protocol: Experiments were performed at 7 T (MAGNETOM, Siemens) using a 16-channel transmit/receive (8 1H channels, 8 23Na channels) radiofrequency (RF) head coil developed in-house. The scanner was modified to allow simultaneous acquisition of both 1H and 23Na signals by inserting an external frequency generator in the RF cabinet of the system to demodulate the 23Na signal with a proper local oscillator3. MNF brain images were acquired on seven healthy volunteers (4 females, mean age = 27±3 years) after IRB-approved informed consent. 

RF pulse sequence: A new 3D simultaneous 1H/23Na MRF pulse sequence was developed to generate 23Na LR maps and 1H HR maps in the whole brain. See Figure 1 for a diagram of the sequence. Nuclear spins are sequentially excited every inter-pulse delay τ for 1H, and every 2τ for 23Na, using non-selective RF pulses followed by one simultaneous readout for both nuclei. The partition phase-encoding gradient moments were distributed such that images from both nuclei had the same slice thickness. The frequency-encoding gradient moments were distributed such that a full radial trajectory for 1H and a center-out radial trajectory for 23Na were obtained in k-space, leading to a ratio of approximately 1.9 for in-plane resolution between the 1H and 23Na images, which corresponds to half of the ratio of their respective gyromagnetic ratios. Both RF pulse trains for 1H (500 pulses) and 23Na MRF (22 pulses) were optimized separately as described in more details in Yu et al.3 and O'donnell et al.4, respectively.

Acquisition parameters: Field-of-view = 240×240×168 mm3, in-plane sagittal resolution = 1.5×1.5 mm2 for 1H and 2.85×2.85 mm3 for 23Na, inter-pulse delay τ = 7.5 ms for 1H and 2τ = 15 ms for 23Na, echo time = 2 ms for 1H and 1.2 ms for 23Na, 1 slab of 56 slices, sagittal slice thickness = 5 mm for both 1H and 23Na, 3 shots per slab, 2 acquisitions averaged together, total scan time = 21 min. 

1H MRF data reconstruction: All individual images acquired after the 275 non-zero RF pulses of the pulse train were reconstructed using conjugate gradient sensitivity encoding (CG-SENSE) to reduce the radial artifacts, with a 11-acquisition sliding window. The 1H fingerprint dictionary was computed using Bloch equations and the extended phase graph formalism including parameters T1, T2 and additional B1+ (RF transmit correction factor). Matching between the acquired signal and the fingerprint dictionary was performed voxel by voxel using the maximum correlation (matrix product). 

23Na MRF data reconstruction: All individual images acquired after the 22 RF pulses were reconstructed using compressed-sensing (CS) using total-generalized-variation regularization to maximize SNR and minimize radial artifacts, and with a 5-acquisition sliding window. The spin 3/2 dynamics simulation to generate the 23Na fingerprint dictionary was performed using the irreducible spherical tensor operators framework3 including paremeters T1, T2,long, T2,short, ΔB1+ factor (applied as a correction factor to the RF amplitude), and Δf0 (frequency offset correction factor). Matching between the acquired signal and the fingerprint dictionary was performed voxel by voxel using Pearson correlation. 

PD and TSC quantification: PD and TSC were calculated voxelwise by as the mean value of the division between the signal evolution and the optimal matched fingerprint, then normalized using the mean reference signal in vitreous humor (eyes). Normalized PD was then divided by 0.98 (water content in vitreous humor). Normalized TSC was divided by 0.98 and multiplied by 145 mM (TSC in vitreous humor).

Super-resolution: A super-resolution algorithm based on partial least square (PLS) regression5 between the 1H HR maps and the 23Na LR maps was applied to generate 23Na HR maps with the same resolution as the 1H HR maps, generating all seven final maps of PD, TSC and 1H and 23Na relaxation times with the same resolution (1.5×1.5×5 mm3). 

Results

MNF maps: Examples of the seven 3D HR maps from MNF in the whole brain on a healthy volunteer can be seen in Figure 2, all acquired simultaneously and therefore naturally co-registered in both space and time, all with a final spatial resolution of 1.5×1.5×5 mm3.

Measurements in brain: Mean value measurements were also performed in four regions-of-interest in each subjects (grey matter [GM], white matter [WM], cerebrospinal fluid [CSF] and vitreous humor [EYE]), and then averaged over all subjects. Note that for 23Na MRF data, T2,short = T2,long in EYE and CSF (monoexponential T2 in fluids or quasi-fluids). Results were as follows:

For 1H HR maps: 

- PD in EYE = 0.99 ± 0.00, in CSF = 0.82 ± 0.09, in GM = 0.74 ± 0.08, in WM = 0.55±0.06

- T1 in EYE = 3914 ± 274 ms, in CSF = 3296 ± 145 ms, in GM = 1689 ± 53 ms, in WM = 1027 ± 13 ms

- T2 in EYE = 123 ± 31 ms, in CSF = 145 ± 17 ms, in GM = 55 ± 4 ms, in WM = 38 ± 3 ms

For 23Na HR maps (after SR):

- TSC in EYE = 144.4 ± 0.8 mM, in CSF = 136.6 ± 24.1 mM, in GM = 89.9 ± 16.7 mM, in WM = 60.7 ± 13.1 mM 

- T1 in EYE = 77.0 ± 11.5 ms, in CSF = 76.5 ± 7.7 ms, in GM = 43.9 ± 4.2 ms, in WM = 31.3 ± 3.0 ms 

- T2,short in EYE = 58.4 ± 8.5 ms, in CSF = 58.6 ± 6.2 ms, in GM = 11.6 ± 4.6 ms, in WM = 8.7 ± 2.5 ms

- T2,long in EYE = 58.4 ± 8.5 ms, in CSF = 58.6 ± 6.2 ms, in GM = 27.9 ± 6.5 ms, in WM = 20.2 ± 4.3 ms 

For 23Na LR maps (before SR):

- TSC in EYE = 144.0 ± 1.0 mM, in CSF = 105.2 ± 29.8 mM, in GM = 62.0 ± 10.2 mM, in WM = 51.4 ± 10.8 mM 

- T1 in EYE = 60.2 ± 5.8 ms, in CSF = 62.0 ± 6.3 ms, in GM = 37.9 ± 4.6 ms, in WM = 38.2 ± 4.2 ms 

- T2,short in EYE = 50.3 ± 5.4 ms, in CSF = 51.9 ± 6.1 ms, in GM = 8.5 ± 4.7 ms, in WM = 9.6 ± 4.1 ms

- T2,long in EYE = 50.3 ± 5.4 ms, in CSF = 51.9 ± 6.1 ms, in GM = 25.3 ± 6.8 ms, in WM = 25.7 ± 6.2 ms 

Conclusion

In this proof-of-concept study, we demonstrated that with MNF, we can generate seven naturally co-registered maps from 1H and 23Na MRF acquired simultaneously in brain that can provide conjoint structural information (PD, T1 and T2 from 1H MRF) and physiological information (TSC, T1, T2,short, T2,long from 23Na) with the exact same spatial and temporal resolutions.

Preliminary results in seven healthy subjects showed that the mean values measured in EYE, CSF, GM and WM where within the ranges of values from the literature, except for: (1) PD in CSF, GM and WM, that were slightly underestimated; and (2) TSC in CSF, GM and WM that were slightly over-estimated. These deviations are most probably due to ineffective corrections of the coil sensitivies (multichannel RF receptions) and of the residual image bias field for both 1H and 23Na in the post-processing pipeline. Both coil sensitivity and bias field corrections are well known problems in the quantitative MRI research field that affect PD and TSC quantification, and while many methods were tested on our data, these were still not sufficient, and slight residual inhomogeneities could not be corrected. More work is currently under progress to improve the quality of the PD and TSC maps from MNF. However, since we mostly intend to use MNF for longitudinal studies, where the same patient is scanned under different circumstances over time (performs tasks, monitor therapy), changes in PD and TSC values between measurements will be of upmost interest compared to the absolute values themselves. A similar reasoning can be applied to 1H and 23Na relaxation times, where dynamic changes over multiple acquisitions in the same individuals will provide more useful information related to changes in brain morphology and metabolism. 

Since all 1H/23Na MRF data is acquired simultaneously, all images have the exact same position and orientation, and therefore undergo identical B0 inhomogeneities, as well as the same head motion and blood and cerebrospinal fluid pulsations. This could proved to be useful for future dynamic or functional studies to assess both local or global changes in water and sodium concentrations, and their respective relaxation times, altogether during tasks or interventions in brain.

The possibility to provide new 23Na-based physiological maps alongside structural 1H-based maps with MNF will therefore enable scientists and clinicians to directly assess ion homeostasis linked to cellular energy processes and related morphological tissue changes, bridge the gap in resolution that has held back our ability to study metabolism in the living human brain, and help understand brain pathologies or monitor therapies.
