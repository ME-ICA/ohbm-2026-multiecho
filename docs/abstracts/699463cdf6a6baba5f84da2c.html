Introduction

Brain function biomarkers remain scarce for many brain diseases and disorders. Identifying objective indicators of dysfunction is challenging because these conditions often lie on a continuous spectrum, patient groups are heterogeneous, and large cohorts are often difficult to recruit. Recently, normative models have gained popularity in neuroimaging because they address these challenges by utilizing large cohorts of healthy subjects to characterize the normal variation of measurements given demographic covariates such as sex and age. Potential pathology is indicated by deviations from the norm that are greater than would be expected from change alone.

While most applications have focused on structural and functional MRI, normative modeling of MEG/EEG data remains limited and faces additional challenges due to the oscillatory nature of the signals. However, the recent emergence of large, high-quality MEG/EEG datasets paves the way for normative models that exploit the high temporal resolution and capture subject-level properties in both the time and frequency domains. 

Most previous normative models in MEG/EEG have assessed power spectral density, magnitude, or peak frequency within bands, while only a few have investigated functional connectivity (FC) measures such as phase synchronization index or amplitude envelope correlation (AEC)1,2. These models have all been spatially localized, assessing one brain region of interest (ROI) or sensor at a time or, in the case of FC, individual pairs. Such localized approaches overlook systemic effects3, despite growing evidence that many disease phenotypes arise not only from focal lesions but also from network-level consequences of localized pathology4. Here, we propose a whole-brain normative modeling framework of MEG FC, which can characterize a broad range of FC measures, thereby leveraging the richness of electrophysiological data while moving from the level of individual connections to the whole brain.

Methods

We recently proposed FUNCOIN (Functional Connectivity Integrative Normative Modeling) as a whole-brain normative modeling framework in fMRI, which revealed pathology not identifiable with localized models3. With FUNCOIN, which is based on a recently developed covariance regression method5, dimensionality reduction and regression are done simultaneously. This ensures that the information most relevant for the model covariates is kept for analysis, which, in turn, allows for identifying network-level sex- and age-dependent trajectories of functional connectivity. Here, we extend the FUNCOIN framework to applications in MEG/EEG, enabling whole-brain normative modeling of a broad range of functional connectivity metrics in the time and frequency domains. The models thereby reveal sex- and age-associated changes not spatially confined to individual pairs of ROIs and not limited to time domain correlations. Using FUNCOIN and source-reconstructed resting-state MEG data from the CamCAN dataset, we created network-level normative models that characterize (i) co-modulation of networks within frequency bands as quantified with AEC, (ii) coupling in the frequency domain assessed with spectral coherence, and (iii) covariance in the time domain while accounting for potential shifts in phase across the brain via time-delay embedding (TDE) (Figure 1A-B). For AEC, we first band-pass filtered the signal to the desired frequency band, then calculated the analytic signal for each ROI with the Hilbert transform. The AEC matrix was determined as the Pearson correlation matrix of the absolute values of the analytic signals (Figure 1A, column 1). Spectral coherence was calculated from the ROI-wise power spectral densities (PSDs) (Figure 1A, column 2). PSDs were calculated using Welch's method by averaging across segments of 500 time points (2 s) with an overlap of 250 time points (1 s). TDE was performed by creating, for each ROI, 15 time-shifted copies of the signal spanning offsets from −7 to +7 time points, resulting in a total delay window of 60 ms (Figure 1A, column 3). FC was then calculated as the covariance matrix of the first 208 principal components. All models were trained on 500 subjects, validated with 10-fold cross-validation, and tested on 112 out-of-sample test subjects.

Results

We identified components of the three functional connectivity metrics, which showed strong association to age (see Figure 2A) - for coherence and AEC, which are frequency band-specific, the strongest age effects were found in alpha and beta (only alpha is shown here). Careful model validation showed reliable model fits with normally distributed, homogeneous residuals, which are core requirements for clinical applications of normative models. The framework was shown to be scalable in both data dimensionality and number of subjects. The variances explained in these models exceed previous normative models of electrophysiological FC, with R2 values between 0.18-0.22 obtained from 10-fold cross-validation for the examples shown in Figure 2A.

The identified components captured non-redundant information about the aging brain as seen from their non-overlapping spatial profiles (Figure 2B). The age-dependent component of alpha band AEC indicates a complex auditory network in the left hemisphere involving medial and lateral temporal cortex, auditory association cortex, early auditory cortex, and temporo-parieto-occipital junction, as well as right prefrontal cortex. On the other hand, the strongest age-dependent component of alpha band spectral coherence involved left inferior parietal cortex and intraparietal sulcus, right posterior cingulate cortex, as well as bilateral MT+ complex and right superior somatosensory and motor cortex. Correlations after TDE revealed a motor network consisting of bilateral supplementary motor cortex, left premotor cortex, left cingulate motor areas, as well as right cingulate and medial prefrontal cortex.

To further assess the explanatory power of the three FC metrics and their combinations, we tested how well age was predicted from a linear regression model with the component values, sex, and component-sex interaction as covariates and age as the outcome (Figure 2C). When using only AEC, coherence, or TDE respectively, the age prediction model yielded R2 values between 0.21 and 0.27 for training data and between 0.15 and 0.23 for out-of-sample test data. Combining either two of them improved the fit, while the best prediction was found by combining all three measures. This resulted in R2 values of 0.41 and 0.37 for training and test data, respectively (Figure 2C). In all combinations of FC measures, the component values contributed significantly (coefficient p-values <0.0001, t-test), except when combining all three, in which case the AEC coefficient was not significantly different from zero (p=0.21, t-test).

Conclusion

FUNCOIN-MEG provides a consistent and scalable framework for whole-brain normative modeling of FC. The three different FC measures were shown to capture complementary information about age as seen from the non-overlapping spatial profiles of the identified components and from the improvement of age prediction when combining the FC measures. The comprehensive growth charts, capturing distinct aspects of electrophysiological data at the network level, pave the way for future normative modeling aimed at disease identification, characterization, and stratification into clinical subtypes. FUNCOIN-MEG is published as a ready-to-use, well-documented Python package installable with PyPI or directly from Github.
