Introduction

As the human brain matures, increasingly complex cognitive and behavioral functions depend on the coordinated activity of large-scale functional networks. This coordination includes both positive correlations—reflecting synchronous activation across networks—and anticorrelations, in which engagement of one network is accompanied by suppression of another.1 The neonatal period represents a critical window of rapid functional reorganization as brain networks adapt to the sensory-rich postnatal environment. While the early emergence of positively correlated network structure has been described in neonates,2 the developmental trajectory of anticorrelated network activity remains poorly understood.

In this study, we identify and characterize the spatial and functional properties of emerging anticorrelated networks from birth through 5 weeks of postnatal life using advanced functional MRI and network neuroscience approaches. Specifically, we map the presence of anticorrelated network pairs in the neonatal brain and test whether these paired networks exhibit coordinated developmental trajectories or mature independently during early postnatal development.

This work leverages data from the first public release of the HEALthy Brain and Child Development (HBCD) Study to provide a novel framework for understanding normative functional network maturation and its relevance to neurodevelopmental disorders. As additional data become available, we will extend these analyses across a broader age range and larger cohort to refine developmental trajectories of large-scale functional networks. By delineating normative patterns of early network organization, this work aims to establish a developmental reference framework that may aid in identifying atypical brain maturation, informing prognosis, and guiding the timing and optimization of early clinical interventions.

Methods

Data used in the preparation of this study were obtained from the HEALthy Brain and Child Development (HBCD) Study, release 1.0.3 Minimally preprocessed fMRI data were accessed through the NIH Brain Development Cohorts Data Sharing Platform with the following processes: initial preprocessing steps included correction for slice timing, head motion, and susceptibility-induced geometric distortions, followed by alignment of functional images to subject-specific high-resolution anatomical images. fMRI data were further preprocessed to remove global signal and nuisance signals—stemming from head motion parameters, white matter and cerebral spinal fluid. Temporal frequencies were retained at 0.01 to 0.08 and framewise motion metrics were computed for exclusion. Anatomical data were processed using infant-specific segmentation and surface reconstruction methods to generate subject-level cortical surfaces and subcortical volumetric representations. These procedures account for age-appropriate tissue contrast, cortical thickness, and folding patterns unique to early postnatal development. Individual anatomical images were nonlinearly registered to age-appropriate infant templates. Functional data were projected from native space onto subject-specific cortical surfaces and volumetric space and then resampled to a standardized infant grayordinate system.

Participants were stratified into four postnatal age groups: 0–1 weeks (n = 66; 27M/39F), 2 weeks (n = 69; 33M/36F), 3 weeks (n = 85; 45M/40F), and 4–5 weeks (n = 67; 28M/39F). For each age group, a group-representative functional MRI (fMRI) atlas was constructed using GroupSync Alignment, a data-driven framework that jointly aligns and temporally synchronizes fMRI data across subjects to generate an atlas representative of the collective functional dynamics within each group.4

Following atlas construction, individual fMRI time series were temporally synchronized to their corresponding group atlas using BrainSync,4 which applies an orthogonal transformation to spatially aligned fMRI time series to maximize temporal correspondence across subjects without altering signal variance. This procedure enhances sensitivity to shared network structure while accommodating inter-individual variability in neonatal functional organization. In addition, it enables direct comparison of functional time courses across individuals within each age group.

For each synchronized age-group dataset, Nadam-Accelerated SCAlable and Robust CP Decomposition (NASCAR)5 was applied to identify large-scale functional networks. NASCAR decomposes group-synchronized fMRI data into spatial and temporal components, yielding pairs of networks characterized by opposing (positive and negative) spatial weights that reflect anticorrelated functional organization. Homologous anticorrelated network pairs across age groups were identified by maximizing correlations between network time courses, enabling comparison of network development across postnatal ages (Figure 1).

Whole-brain functional connectivity (FC) was computed on each age group’s fMRI atlas using pairwise correlations of grayordinate time series. For each anticorrelated network-pair, individual networks were delineated based on the sign of their spatial weights (positive versus negative). Intra-network FC was quantified as the weighted mean of pairwise correlations among vertices and voxels within a single network, while inter-network FC was quantified as the weighted mean of correlations between vertices and voxels belonging to oppositely weighted networks. Spatial weights derived from NASCAR were used to weight vertex and voxel contributions, emphasizing regions most strongly associated with each network component. These measures enabled assessment of age-related changes in within-network coherence and between-network coupling across early postnatal development.

Results

We were able to identify 12 network-pairs for Age 0-1, 16 for Age 2, 19 for Age 3 and 17 for Age 4-5 and confirm homologous network-pairs across all groups (p<0.05). Figure 1 shows the progressive changes of the first 5 network-pairs on the cortex across all 4 groups where the positive (red) and negative (blue) spatial weights together indicate a network-pair (NP). We note that color polarity is arbitrary and reflects relative weighting within each pair rather than absolute functional sign and therefore hold no biological significance. Each pair represents anticorrelated time courses, providing a window into the rapid emergence of large-scale functional architecture during the first five weeks of life.

Networks aligned with known adult network architecture, suggesting that foundational large-scale systems emerges remarkably early. The most dominant network-pair, NP 1, is comprised of the sensorimotor network (blue) anticorrelated with a nascent default-mode network (DMN). Between 0 and 5 weeks of postnatal age, the sensorimotor network’s spatial weights become more concentrated along the central gyrus and the boundaries becomes sharper. Simultaneously, the DMN expands along the dorsolateral and medial prefrontal cortex, precuneus, the intraparietal/angular gyrus and anterior/middle temporal gyrus. The sensorimotor network expands along the bilateral thalamus and expands into the brainstem, cerebellar peduncles and finally to the cerebellum while inclusion of the caudate begins to show after Age 3 (not shown).

In Ages 0-2 weeks, we observed more diffuse networks with limited functional segregation. More prominent networks were observed along the sensorimotor regions with weaker anticorrelation in association regions. This organization reflects early reliance on sensorimotor systems and limited functional segregation, consistent with the predominance of reflexive behaviors in neonates. By five weeks, opposing components displayed robust and spatially distinct patterns with association networks emerging alongside sensory systems. NP4 and NP5, for example, revealed segregation of dorsal attention and language-related regions. This rapid trajectory—from diffuse sensorimotor dominance to organized large-scale networks within five weeks—underscores the dynamic nature of early functional brain development.

Figure 2a illustrate intra-network functional connectivity (FC) for 24 individual networks (left) and inter-network FC across 12 network pairs (right) across four postnatal age groups (0–1, 2, 3, and 4–5 weeks). While individual networks show variability, there is no systematic global trend of increasing or decreasing FC across age groups, and mean differences are not statistically significant. This suggests that overall FC strength does not uniformly scale with age during the first five weeks of life. Instead, connectivity changes appear to be network-specific rather than global.

Figure 2b show a strong positive correlation between intra-network FC of anticorrelated network-pairs (r=0.84; p<0.0001) that indicates that when one network in a pair becomes more internally coherent, its opposing network also strengthens internally. Next, a strong negative correlation between the intra- and inter-network FC between network pairs (r=-0.85; p<0.0001) show that as networks within a pair become more internally cohesive, their connectivity to each other decreases, reinforcing functional segregation.

Taken together, these findings suggest that network coherence in the neonatal brain is not driven by global increases in FC but by the dynamic relationship between anticorrelated network pairs. These findings suggest that the emergence of anticorrelated network-pairs are interdependent rather than following independent trajectories. The positive coupling of intra-network FC across anticorrelated networks may reflect shared maturational processes underlying network stabilization. 

Conclusion

This is the first study to demonstrate the emergence of anticorrelated brain networks in infancy. We were able to identify 12 pairs of anticorrelated functional networks within the first week of life that were consistent across all age groups. Additionally, we were able identify another 4&5 network-pairs that emerged during the 2nd and 3rd week of postnatal life and persisted in the next consecutive age groups.

In early postnatal life, we observed emergent functional network architecture displays signatures of both network integration and segregation, consistent with hierarchical models of brain development. For one, spatially we observed both patterns of expansion and refinement with development. For example, upon close examination of the first network-pair, we were able to demonstrate that the sensory-motor network was present at birth and was progressively refined across the first few weeks of life. In parallel, we observed the emergence and expansion of the DMN.1 Anti-correlated networks play an important role in cognitive behaviors as it allows efficient switching of task through inhibitory mechanisms. Altogether, the emergence of the DMN network may play a role as an inhibitory mechanism that drives the refinement of the sensorimotor network. Across all network pairs, there were no systematic patterns of FC changes within and between network-pairs indicating that the developmental trajectory of networks in early-life is heterogeneous across the entire brain and should be examined discreetly.

Across the 12 anticorrelated network-pairs persistent across all age groups, we observed a strong positive association between intra-network connectivity between anticorrelated network-pairs indicating coordinated strengthening of internal network cohesion across pairs. Furthermore, intra-network connectivity was inversely related to inter-network connectivity strength between anticorrelated network pairs such that networks with higher internal coherence exhibited more pronounced functional segregation from their paired anticorrelated counterpart. Furthermore, the observed inverse relationship between intra- and inter-network FC aligns with theoretical frameworks positing that increasing local integration supports the functional segregation of large-scale networks. The strong positive coupling of intra-network FC within pairs, combined with the negative relationship between intra- and inter-network FC, points to a mechanism where functional modularity is reinforced by antagonistic interactions between opposing networks. This pattern implies that early modular organization may emerge through competitive or inhibitory processes between anticorrelated systems, rather than uniform strengthening of connectivity across the cortex. We hypothesize that inhibitory processes between anti-correlated network pairs support network cohesion. This pattern parallels developmental trajectories reported in older infants and children2 suggesting that the balance of integration and segregation begins to organize very early in the human connectome.

This is the first study to demonstrate the emergence of anticorrelated brain network activity in neonates across discreet network-pairs throughout the brain. This work introduces a scalable, robust approach for mapping network development in large pediatric cohorts, offering critical insights into the emergence of functional architecture during early life. Our results illustrate that nascent functional systems already exist in neonates and begin to organize through coordinated, competitive functional architecture that underlies the mature brain’s modular organization.
