Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and primary lateral sclerosis (PLS) are motor neuron diseases that share overlapping clinical manifestations but differ in disease course and pathophysiology. ALS involves widespread degeneration of both upper and lower motor neurons and typically progresses rapidly, whereas PLS primarily affects upper motor neurons in a more focal and slowly evolving manner. Early differential diagnosis remains challenging, highlighting the need for objective neurophysiological biomarkers for early discrimination¹.

Although PLS has classically been considered restricted to upper motor neuron pathways, recent neuroimaging suggests degeneration may extend beyond the corticospinal tract and involve additional cortical regions¹. Resting-state EEG provides a non-invasive approach to assess large-scale network dysfunction. EEG microstates—transient, quasi-stable patterns of neural activity—represent the building blocks of spontaneous cognition²,³ and have been proposed as biomarkers in neurodegenerative diseases4. However, microstate analyses in PLS remain unexplored. This study presents preliminary evidence of resting-state EEG microstate alterations in PLS.

Methods

High-density (128-channel) resting-state EEG was recorded from from 81 HC, 97 ALS, and 38 PLS participants. HC and 18 PLS were recorded at St. James’s Hospital Clinical Research Facility, Dublin, Ireland and 20  at University Medical Center Utrecht, Netherlands. Participants maintained visual fixation on a central cross with eyes open during three 2-minute resting blocks (BioSemi ActiveTwo, 512 Hz).

Ethical approval was granted by the St. James’s Hospital / Tallaght University Hospital Joint Research Ethics Committee; written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

EEG preprocessing followed established pipelines¹,². Microstate computation utilized the EEGlab Microstate toolbox3 with polarity-invariant modified k-means clustering into four canonical classes, Gaussian-weighted smoothing, and labelling of each timepoint based on lowest global map dissimilarity.

Five microstate prototypes5 were derived from Global field power (GFP) peaks (1–30 Hz) in the combined HC, ALS and PLS group. All EEG timepoints were backfitted to these prototypes, and microstate metrics—duration, occurrence, and global explained variance (GEV)—were calculated. Group-level differences were assessed using Wilcoxon rank tests and Mann–Whitney U tests with FDR correction. Microstate parameters will be correlated with clinical measures in disease groups.

Results

Five canonical microstate classes were identified, explaining similar proportions of global variance across groups (HC: 44 ± 0.2%; ALS: 46 ± 0.2%; PLS: 46 ± 0.2%). ALS displayed reduced expression of microstates 2 and 5, with lower occurrence, duration, and coverage, and reduced GEV in microstate 2 (p < 0.04). PLS showed markedly increased microstate 5 expression (occurrence, duration, coverage; p < 0.01). Microstate 5 robustly differentiated ALS from PLS across all features (p < 0.01), whereas no ALS–PLS differences were observed for microstate 2. Overall, ALS exhibited reduced microstate dynamics, while PLS showed amplified microstate 5 activity that will be explored further in future work by looking at sources of the signal.

Conclusion

Resting-state EEG microstates reveal distinct large-scale network alterations in ALS and PLS. ALS is characterized by altered network features (microstate 2), while PLS shows pronounced amplification of microstate 5, which strongly discriminates the two conditions. EEG microstate analysis may therefore provide a sensitive, non-invasive biomarker for differential diagnosis and motivate longitudinal and multimodal studies to determine diagnostic and prognostic utility. These results warrant future interrogation of networks and communication that underpin the microstate 5 abnormalities and potential biomarker candidates might therefore reflect functionality/physiologically meaningful abnormalities.
