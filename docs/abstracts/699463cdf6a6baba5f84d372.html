Introduction

Episodic memory is critical to our understanding of the world. It relies on binding individual fragmented memories (i.e., item memory) to create contexts (i.e., relational memory) where episodes occur, attending to the associative, spatial, and temporal aspect of events1. Over development, while item memory is already well developed by middle childhood, relational memory continues to improve from middle childhood to adulthood and decays during senescence2.

Classically, memory research has focused on the hippocampus in the medial temporal lobe, and the prefrontal cortex (PFC). The hippocampus directly contributes to initial learning and later retrieval of episodic memories, while the lateral PFC allows for mnemonic control through mental representations and semantic strategies3, which can to some extent help to compensate for binding difficulties happening in healthy aging. However, another critical region in episodic memory, which has been scarcely studied in human cognitive neuroscience, is the thalamus. Indeed, the anterior thalamic nucleus has been proposed to be more critical for recollective processes while the mediodorsal nucleus of the thalamus has been thought to be more involved in familiarity-related processes4.

The present work investigates the development of episodic memory over the lifespan focusing on the following three objectives: 1) understand the role of the hippocampal subfields, PFC regions and thalamic nuclei in item and relational memory; 2) study the interaction between episodic and semantic memory by examining the benefit of verbalizable materials over abstract shapes and whether semantic regions respond differently to the type of materials; 3) examine to what extent these two effects can be found in a data-driven manner during brain spontaneous fluctuations.

Methods

One-hundred and thirty-four participants took part in the study and were divided into three age-groups: children (aged 9-14 years), younger adults (aged 18-35 years), and older adults (aged 60-80 years). High-resolution fMRI images were collected inside a 3 T Siemens Magnetom PRISMAfit MRI scanner while participants performed an episodic memory task comprising encoding and retrieving pairs of images that appeared sequentially on screen. Participants were instructed to attend to the identity of the images (i.e., item memory) as well as their associative, spatial, and temporal relationships (i.e., relational memory) (see Fig. 1A-B). While item memory merely requires familiarity, the three relational memory types require recollective processes regarding the relations between the items within each pair. The images forming the image pairs comprised either verbalizable items such as objects and animals, or abstract shapes (see Fig. 1C). There were a total of 24 encoding-retrieval cycles, each comprising an encoding phase of 10 pairs of images, a distractor phase intended to prevent selective rehearsal, and a retrieval phase. Here we present task-based functional results related to retrieval. In addition to the task-based fMRI, high-resolution images were also collected during resting-state, and participants carried out a behavioral battery comprising standardized vocabulary and fluid reasoning measurements (i.e., KBIT-II intelligence test).

After excluding data due to excessive head motion or incidental findings in the anatomical MRI images 112 subjects comprised the final sample of the study: 38 children (M = 11.98; SD = 1.59 years; 18 females), 37 younger adults (M = 26.37; SD = 4.62 years; 20 females) and 37 older adults (M = 65.87; SD = 4.07 years; 18 females). Before preprocessing, we employed NORDIC (NOise Reduction with DIstribution Corrected) to reduce the thermal noise and boost the signal-to-noise-ratio of our fMRI data. SPM12 preprocessing routines and analysis methods were employed.

For the task-based fMRI data we employed two different analytical pipelines for normalized and native spaces. In normalized space, all participants shared stereotactic space to conduct whole-brain comparisons (e.g., group level whole-brain contrasts). In native space, images were preprocessed maintaining the individual space of each participant and were intended for ROIs analyses (e.g., high resolution hippocampal subfields and thalamic nuclei) and pairwise functional connectivity (FC) analysis.

For the resting-state fMRI data we employed a framework that allows for the extraction of FC-traits from a set of functional connectomes, based on connICA5. Whole-brain functional parcellation based on 278 regions obtained by Shen and colleagues was used for the functional connectome construction, which were ordered according to 7 functional networks proposed by Yeo and colleagues, to which we added two additional functional networks corresponding to subcortical and cerebellar regions. The functional connectomes of all subjects were embedded into a matrix where each row contains the upper triangular of the functional connectome of each subject, without any a priori information or group stratification. With this matrix containing all functional connectomes, ICA decomposition was applied to obtain FC-traits which represent an independent pattern of FC and the corresponding weights of the FC-trait on each subject. The weights of each subject were then used to understand group differences in each trait and associations to other measures of the study.

Results

Behavioral results showed that while item memory performance was unaffected throughout the lifespan, there were age-related relational memory differences in line with an inverted u-shape function. At the functional level, older adults exhibited decreased hippocampal activation and decreased FC within the hippocampus when compared to children and younger adults. In contrast, within PFC regions older adults showed hyperactivation and hyperconnectivity compared to the other age groups. Regarding the involvement of thalamic nuclei, the expected pattern in line with Aggleton and Brown’s model4 was found in children and younger adults, suggesting that the anteroventral nucleus, different from the mediodorsal nucleus, is more critical for relational memory judgments than item memory judgments. Nevertheless, the older adults showed a different pattern, revealing similar anteroventral and mediodorsal thalamic nuclei involvement in item and relational memory. This lack of replication of Aggleton and Brow’s model4 in the older adults group was associated with both PFC hyperactivation and PFC hyperconnectivity, suggesting a compensatory mechanism taking place in older adulthood that involves not only PFC but also the thalamus.

In line with our second objective, all age groups showed a behavioral benefit of verbalizable over abstract materials, indicating support of semantic memory on episodic memory. This beneficial effect was more pronounced in both children and older adults than younger adults. At the functional level, the whole-brain activation contrast of the entire sample comparing verbalizable and abstract materials showed bilateral temporal pole, bilateral superior temporal gyrus, angular gyrus, and frontal superior medial cortex involvement (see Fig 2A). When considering the activation difference between verbalizable and abstract materials, we found an age pattern in both cortical (e.g., pars opercularis, superior temporal gyrus) and subcortical (e.g., hippocampus) regions. Namely, older adults did not distinguish between verbalizable and abstract materials functionally despite strongly benefiting from verbalizable materials behaviorally. Interestingly, results revealed a systematic association between the activation difference in abstract and verbalizable materials with the behavioral performance in abstract materials, only in older adults. Therefore, despite older adults not showing engagement differences between verbalizable and abstract materials overall, their stronger regional engagement of multiple regions was associated with performing better in abstract materials, an effect again not present in other age groups.

By using connICA in the resting-state fMRI data we found a FC-trait showing age group differences and that was explained by the scores on standardized vocabulary knowledge (KBIT-II). This FC-trait related to the semantic knowledge involved mainly regions in the lateral temporal pole, but also superior temporal gyrus, angular gyrus, and frontal superior medial cortex, resembling the whole-brain contrast independently obtained on task data when comparing verbalizable and abstract materials (see Fig 2B). When taking the FC-trait obtained in resting-state to the task to understand the activation differences in those regions obtained in a data-driven manner, we found that the activation during the task also correlated with the vocabulary scores globally and in the children group, which is expected to be the group with strongest development in semantic knowledge. Neither younger nor older adults showed a correlation between engagement of the regions obtained from the FC-trait and their vocabulary scores, but the older adults showed overall higher activation and higher vocabulary scores relative to the other groups, as expected by their accumulated semantic knowledge over the years.

Conclusion

Older adults exhibited an overall different pattern than younger adults and children. They showed decreased engagement and FC in the hippocampus, which could be compensated by their hyperactivation and hyperconnectivity of the PFC. Older adults also failed to replicate the expected pattern according to Aggleton & Brown’s model4 regarding thalamic nuclei which was related to the PFC hyperactivation and hyperconnectivity.

In addition, while children and older adults showed higher benefit of semantic memory in episodic memory behaviorally, their functional pattern differed. Older adults did not show differential activation between materials overall, but exhibited stronger overall engagement in abstract shapes when performing better in them, perhaps suggesting an additional effort needed to overcome their binding difficulties especially with this type of materials. Thus, the compensatory mechanisms happening in older adults are complex and involve brain network reconfigurations including regions along the Papez circuitry.

Finally, from the task and resting-state data we independently obtained a similar network of regions related to the acquisition of semantic knowledge over development. This finding highlights the potential of resting-state to pin down relevant functional traits that can predict task-related networks, as well as to inform neurobiological models on the development of semantic and episodic memories over the lifespan.

Overall, the present work revealed neurodevelopmental differences in episodic memory in terms of relational memory and of the semantic-episodic memory interactions. Older adults were repeatedly found to have the distinct functional dynamics relative to younger adults and children, and to exhibit compensatory reconfigurations across several neural systems that take place during healthy aging.
