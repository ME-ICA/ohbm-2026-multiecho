Introduction

The human brain is intrinsically organized into canonical functional networks with distinct spatial topographies1. These networks exhibit a balance between convergent (positive) and divergent (negative) interactions2, exemplified by antagonistic dynamics between the default mode and action-mode network. While precision functional mapping delineates individualized topographies of single networks3, the spatial coordination among networks and its developmental origin remains largely unknown. To address this gap, we introduce Functional Topography Covariance Analysis (FOCA), a framework for quantifying interactions among individualized network topographies. Leveraging dense-sampling data from the Midnight Scan Club, population-level data from the Human Connectome Project, and neonatal scans from the Developing Human Connectome Project, we map person-specific patterns of network topographic interaction and demonstrate how these interactions reverse in newborns and evolve across early development.

Methods

We generated individualized functional networks using a widely adopted template-matching (TM) approach 4. For each participant, we computed individualized topographic maps by correlating the mean time series of each network with all cortical vertices. We then constructed the FOCA matrix by calculating the correlation coefficient between the spatial topography maps of every network pair. We quantified the cross-subject consistency, inter-individual variability, and within-subject stability of the FOCA matrix, and applied hierarchical clustering to characterize its organizational structure. Using neonatal scans from the dHCP cohort, we tested whether adult-like FOCA organization emerges at birth and whether person-specific FOCA patterns track brain maturation during the early postnatal period (37–44 PMA weeks) and predicted neurodevelopmental outcomes at 18 months. Finally, we conducted three benchmarking analyses to evaluate whether FOCA outperforms conventional functional connectivity (FC).

Results

We generated individualized spatial topographies comprising 11 major functional networks and 20 subnetworks, including the recently proposed action-mode (AMN)2 and somato-cognitive-action  (SCAN)5 networks (Fig. 1a). Visual inspection revealed widespread coupling patterns, including prominent positive (convergent) interactions (e.g., FP and DAN) and negative (divergent) interactions (e.g., DMN and CO/Action-mode). We quantified these relationships using Functional Topography Covariance Analysis (FOCA), defined as spatial correlations between individualized network topographies (Fig. 1b). The resulting FOCA matrix exhibited high cross-subject consistency and strong within-subject stability (Fig. 1c). Hierarchical clustering revealed a clear multiscale organization, with six clusters further grouping into three hyper-clusters (Fig. 1d). Notably, higher-order clusters (Clusters 1, 2, and 6; hyper-clusters 1 and 3) showed pronounced negative couplings, whereas primary clusters (Clusters 3–5; hyper-cluster 2) showed predominantly positive couplings (Fig. 1e). These coupling motifs were strongly associated with cortical aerobic glycolysis (Fig. 1f). 

Neonatal FOCA organization was markedly “adult-inverted”: higher-order clusters displayed strong positive coupling with one another (Clusters 1–2 and 6), largely opposite to the adult pattern. In addition, the visual cluster (Cluster 3) coupled positively with the DMN cluster (Cluster 1) in neonates, rather than with the somatomotor cluster (Cluster 4) as observed in adults (Fig. 2a, b). Developmental effects were most evident in negative topographies: the DAN’s strongest negative topography shifted from primary motor cortex in neonates to ventromedial prefrontal cortex and precuneus in adults, while its positive topography remained comparatively stable (Fig. 2c). Individualized topographic alignment indexed brain maturity at birth (Fig. 2d) and predicted neurodevelopmental outcomes at 18 months (Fig. 2e). Finally, relative to conventional functional connectivity (FC), FOCA was more robust to global signal effects and more sensitive to the maturation of negative couplings (Fig. 2f).

We assessed the stability of our findings across three major dimensions. (i) cross-dataset replication: individualized topographies and FOCA matrices derived from an independent adult cohort (HCP) closely matched those from MSC at both group and individual levels, indicating high reproducibility of the hierarchical organization. (ii) global signal regression: FOCA was highly consistent with and without GSR in adults and neonates, for both topographies and inter-cluster coupling profiles, indicating its high robustness. (iii) scan-duration harmonization: truncating adult HCP scans into neonatal-length showed little influence on the neonate-adult differences of FOCA, supporting that our results were not driven by the short scan time in neonates. These validations converge to show that FOCA's internetwork architecture and its developmental inferences are relatively reliable to methodological variations 

Conclusion

In adults, functional topography is characterized by divergent couplings among higher-order networks and convergent couplings within primary networks. In neonates, higher-order networks instead exhibit strong positive coupling, and the visual system preferentially couples with the default mode network rather than with somatomotor regions—an organization opposite to that observed in adults. Across development, negative topographies undergo the most pronounced reorganization, indicating a key role in network maturation. By explicitly modeling spatial topographic interactions, FOCA provides a substantially more robust and developmentally sensitive characterization of negative coupling than conventional FC.
