Introduction

Deep learning (DL) has transformed medical imaging, achieving state-of-the-art performance in segmentation, classification, and disease progression prediction1–3. These successes have enabled discoveries in neuroimaging, from automated brain parcellation to early disease detection. However, a critical bottleneck limits broader impact: common DL requires hundreds of labeled examples per task. This constraint severely restricts research in three key scenarios facing the neuroimaging community. First, rare neurological conditions often have few available patients, making standard DL impractical. Second, multi-site consortia struggle with scanner/protocol variability that degrades model performance. Third, exploratory research cannot justify the time and expense of collecting hundreds of expert annotations before determining if an approach is promising.

Foundation models (FMs) offer a potential solution by pre-training on large unlabeled datasets and then fine-tuning with minimal labeled data2,3. However, until recently, no rigorous benchmarks existed to validate whether FMs actually deliver on this promise for brain MRI. The Self-Supervised Learning for 3D Medical Imaging Challenge (SSL3D) and FM Challenge for Brain MRI (FOMO25), both held at MICCAI 2025, created the first standardized evaluation framework4,5. These competitions assembled unprecedented datasets and tested submitted models across diverse tasks using only limited labeled data for fine-tuning.

Our team achieved first place in both challenges. Critically, these represent the only brain MRI FMs validated through open competition. We now share these pretrained models, complete training code, and Docker containers for plug-and-play use. Our goal is to enable the community to apply proven methods to their specific research questions. We invite the community to explore these models' capabilities and help shape future development toward interpretability, causal frameworks, and novel clinical applications.

Methods

Challenge Datasets and Evaluation Framework. SSL3D assembled 114,570 volumes from 34,191 subjects spanning over 800 heterogeneous datasets4, including multiple MRI contrasts (T1, T2, FLAIR, T1c, DWI, etc.) and timepoints. FOMO25 provided 60,529 volumes from 11,187 subjects with research and clinical-quality scans5. Both challenges evaluated the same pretrained models across multiple downstream tasks, testing genuine few-shot (employing very small labeled datasets) generalization rather than task-specific optimization. SSL3D organizers performed all fine-tuning from submitted pretrained models, while FOMO25 participants submitted fine-tuned models from a common pretrained checkpoint.

For SSL3D, fine-tuning used only 15% of available labeled data per task, translating to approximately 17-70 labeled cases per task depending on dataset size. Specific tasks included: MS lesion segmentation (50 fine-tune/285 prediction cases), glioblastoma segmentation (104/590 cases), pediatric brain tumors (44/250 cases), brain metastases (46/262 cases), glioblastoma vs lymphoma classification (17/95 cases), tumor recurrence vs radiation necrosis (16/88 cases), and stroke onset time prediction (75/423 cases). FOMO25 used even smaller labeled sets: only 21 fine-tuning cases for infarct detection (320 test cases), 23 cases for meningioma segmentation (160 test cases), and 200 cases for brain age estimation (800 test cases). Evaluation datasets were acquired at different sites with different scanner vendors and field strengths.

Our Approach: Anatomical Inductive Biases. While successful FM models from computer vision (DINO, JEPA, etc.) employ natural images without domain assumptions, we designed our models to explicitly leverage neuroimaging structure. We employed CNN-based U-Net architectures trained with masked autoencoders (MAE)2,3, implementing a core principle: disentangling subject-invariant anatomical representations from contrast-specific pathological features.

For SSL3D, we forced the learned representations (broadly speaking, the low-dimensional version of the images at the end of the CNN, Convolutional Neural Network, encoder) to follow two distributions: one constrained to match anatomical segmentations across all contrasts and timepoints of a subject, and another optimized to capture contrast-specific pathology for health status discrimination. For FOMO25, we structured the latent space similarly and implemented cross-contrast reconstruction by swapping representations between contrasts while maintaining shared anatomical features. This encourages the model to separate anatomy from acquisition-specific characteristics, preserving contrast-specific information for downstream tasks.

Computational Efficiency and Democratization. Our models contain approximately 20 million parameters, compared to 300+ million for comparable transformer approaches. Pre-training required approximately 36 GPU-hours on a single GPU with under 80GB VRAM. This contrasts sharply with transformer-based submissions requiring 100-1000 GPU-hours and vast multi-GPU setups1. Most research groups can therefore pretrain or easily fine-tune (1 GPU for around 2-6 hours) these models using hardware they already have, without requiring expensive computing clusters.

Community Access. All pretrained model weights, training code, and fine-tuning scripts are publicly available at github.com/jbanusco/BrainFM4Challenges. We provide Docker containers ensuring reproducibility and simplifying deployment. Researchers can fine-tune our models for their specific tasks following step-by-step instructions: download the container, provide their labeled dataset, and fine-tune within hours using standard hardware. This plug-and-play approach removes technical barriers that previously restricted FM use to well-resourced institutions.

Results

Overall Challenge Performance. We ranked first place on SSL3D CNN track (team: Pep3Crew) averaging best performance across seven downstream tasks. Similarly,  we ranked first place on FOMO25 methods track (team: FOMO2JOMO) across three downstream tasks. All results used minimal labeled training data per task: 17-104 cases for SSL3D tasks, 21-200 cases for FOMO25 tasks. Full details can be found on the challenges sites ssl3d-challenge.dkfz.de/leaderboard  and fomo25.github.io/results.

Challenge Ranking Methodology. SSL3D computed separate rankings for each track. For segmentation tasks, Dice score and Normalized Surface Distance (NSD, 1mm tolerance) were calculated per case and class, averaged across cases, then ranked across teams. For classification tasks, AUROC and Mean Balanced Accuracy (MBA) were ranked across teams. Each dataset received a rank (average of metric ranks), and the final team ranking averaged dataset ranks across all tasks. FOMO25 ranked teams separately for each task using task-specific metrics: AUROC for infarct detection, Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC) and NSD for meningioma segmentation, and correlation coefficient with mean absolute error for brain age regression. The overall ranking averaged task ranks across all three tasks. Out-of-Domain Generalization. All test datasets represented out-of-domain clinical data: different hospitals, scanner vendors (GE, Siemens, Philips), field strengths (1.5T, 3T), acquisition protocols, and patient populations compared to pre-training and fine-tuning data. Test data spanned clinical sites in Denmark, India, USA, and Germany. Despite this domain shift, models maintained consistent performance across MRI contrasts (T1, T2, FLAIR, T1c, DWI, ADC, SWI, T2*) and diverse pathologies.

FOMO25 Task-Specific Results. For acute infarct detection (21 training cases, 320 test cases), we achieved 78.2% AUCROC. For meningioma segmentation (23 training cases, 160 test cases), we achieved DSC=0.095 and NSD 0.071. For brain age estimation (200 training cases, 800 test cases), we achieved correlation 0.65 and mean absolute error 11.0 years.

SSL3D Segmentation Task Results. For MS lesion segmentation (50 training cases, 285 test cases), we achieved average DSC=0.85  and NSD=0.90 across lesion classes. For adult glioblastoma segmentation (104 training cases, 590 test cases from multi-center BraTS datasets), we achieved an average DSC=0.82 and NSD=0.85 performance across tumor subregions (edema, enhancing tumor, necrosis). For pediatric glioblastoma segmentation (44 training cases, 250 test cases), we obtained a DSC=0.70 and NSD=0.74 performance despite pediatric population differences from adult pre-training data. For brain metastases segmentation (46 training cases, 262 test cases from Yale-BrainMets multi-center data), our approach obtained DSC=0.85 and NSD=0.91 for both edema and contrast-enhancing tumor regions.

SSL3D Classification Task Results. For glioblastoma vs lymphoma discrimination (17 training cases, 95 out-of-domain test cases from Heidelberg University Hospital), we achieved MBA=0.71 and AUCROC=0.75. For tumor recurrence vs radiation necrosis classification (16 training cases, 88 test cases), we achieved MBA=0.57 and AUCROC=0.58. For stroke onset time prediction using DWI-FLAIR mismatch (75 training cases, 423 test cases from Berlin stroke centers), our models achieved MBA=0.56 and AUCROC=0.57.

Cross-Architecture Performance Comparison. Our CNN-based approach achieved top average ranking across all tasks in both challenges. Transformer-based submissions, despite using 300+ million parameters (vs our 20 million) and 10-30× longer training times, did not achieve first place on any individual task in either challenge. The performance gap was consistent across segmentation (average 2.5% higher DSC for CNN-based methods) and classification tasks (average 8% higher accuracy for CNNs).

Conclusion

Proven Foundation for Brain MRI Research. We provide the neuroimaging community with the only brain MRI FMs validated through open competition. These models achieved top performance across two independent challenges, demonstrating consistent few-shot generalization for diverse tasks. 

Our models achieved competitive performance with 17-200 training examples per task, whereas traditional supervised DL approaches typically tend to require ten to hundred times more labeled samples. The same pretrained model generalized across segmentation, classification, and regression, spanning different anatomical targets, pathologies, scanner types, and clinical populations across sites. This few-shot generalization represents sample sizes typical of rare genetic disorders, pilot biomarker studies, and exploratory research where large-scale annotation is impractical. FMs thus potentially enable research that was previously impossible due to the lack of data.

Why CNNs Outperform Transformers in Medical Imaging. Transformers revolutionized natural image analysis, yet in both challenges, no transformer submission matched our CNN performance, despite 10× more parameters and vastly greater computational requirements. This finding extends beyond our work: systematic reviews show CNN variants continue achieving state-of-the-art results on most 3D medical tasks benchmarks1. Three factors likely explain this pattern. First, transformers require substantially larger datasets to learn effective attention patterns; even 100,000+ medical volumes appear insufficient. Second, 3D tokenization creates severe computational bottlenecks. Third, adapting transformers to different tasks requires task-specific modifications that undermine FM universality. However, the critical question isn't whether CNNs are inherently superior, but what medical imaging teaches us about effective FMs. Our success came from principled exploitation of neuroimaging structure, rather than architectural novelty. This suggests that FM performance depends less on parameter scale or architectural trends than on matching inductive biases to domain structure.

The Community's Essential Role. Here, expertise becomes indispensable. Machine learning researchers optimize algorithms, but only the neuroimaging community knows what structure actually exists in brain data. What anatomical relationships truly generalize across individuals and diseases? Which pathological features reliably transfer? What biological constraints should guide architectures? 

Community feedback on where our models succeed and fail will guide biologically plausible, interpretable development. Researchers applying these models to specific diseases will reveal which anatomical priors transfer and which require disease-specific modifications. Longitudinal studies can test whether models capture meaningful trajectory information. Cognitive neuroscience applications can probe whether learned representations align with known brain-behavior relationships. Multi-site consortia will identify what drives cross-site generalization success or failure.

We share these models as validated starting points for community-driven improvement. Five directions where OHBM expertise is invaluable: (1) Interpretability: do model predictions align with clinical reasoning? (2) Causal frameworks: can we encode biological mechanisms rather than spurious correlations? (3) Disease specificity: how do models handle conditions absent from pre-training? (4) Multimodal integration: how should we combine structural, functional, diffusion, and metabolic imaging? (5) Longitudinal modeling: what representations best capture progression and treatment response?

Success requires collaborative investigation bridging machine learning technical expertise with OHBM domain knowledge, neither blind faith in DL nor reflexive skepticism, but honest evaluation of failures, principled incorporation of neuroscience understanding, and rigorous testing across applications.
