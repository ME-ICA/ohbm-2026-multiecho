Introduction

Stroke remains a major global health burden, resulting in high mortality and long-term disability despite advances in acute management 1. Accurate assessment of cerebral perfusion is critical for guiding reperfusion therapy and predicting functional outcome. Cerebral blood flow is normally regulated by neurovascular coupling; however, ischemia disrupts this regulation, leading to altered timing and dynamics of the hemodynamic response 2. Accordingly, dynamic perfusion metrics, including bolus arrival time, time to peak, wash-out rate, and response curvature, provide physiologically relevant markers of vascular and neurovascular coupling dysfunction. These temporal features improve discrimination between infarct core and salvageable penumbra and enhance prediction of tissue viability 3. In this study, we present a sensitive, non-invasive dynamic BOLD-DSC MRI approach 4 to track ischemic progression and identify tissue with preserved neurovascular responsiveness.

Methods

Transient Middle Cerebral Artery Occlusion (tMCAO) rat model: Sprague-Dawley Rat (n=5, 280-350g) underwent right MCA occlusion under 2-3% Isoflurane. A silicon-coated filament was advanced into the ICA to block the MCA, then was withdrawn after 120 minute.

BOLD acquisitions:   T2-w, DWI-MRI, DSC-MRI measurements were acquired baseline, during occlusion, and repeatedly up to 4h, 24h post-stroke under 1.5% Isoflurane (Fig. 1A). DSC-MRI: TR/TE = 1000/15 ms, FA = 54°, matrix = 100 × 50, FOV = 30 × 15 mm², slices = 18. DWI-MRI: TR/TE = 3500/20 ms, NA = 8, segments = 3, 5 b-values = 100-1000 s/mm², FA = 90°, matrix = 100 × 50, FOV = 30 × 15 mm², slices = 18.

Hypoxic stimulation: A block design was applied: 60s normoxia (40% O2/60% N2) followed by 5s hypoxia (0% O2/100% N2), repeated twice (Fig. 1A).

Data processing: The hypoxic-induced signal intensity change of each voxel was converted to the relaxation rate change by  = −hypoxia-induced relative change/TE, since it is linearly related with deoxyhemoglobin concentration Ct(t) as: Ct(t) = k. , where k is constant 5. CBV and CBF were estimated from the area under the curve and peak amplitude of the response, respectively. Dynamic metrics included time to peak (TTP; stimulus onset to peak) and wash-out times (WS-30%: peak to 30% decay), as illustrated in Fig. 1A.

Results

Spatiotemporal evolution of cerebral ischemia quantified by multi-parametric perfusion mapping

Multiparametric maps demonstrate a clear spatiotemporal progression of ischemia during occlusion and reperfusion (Fig. 1B). During occlusion, subcortical regions exhibit marked reductions in ADC, rCBF, and rCBV accompanied by pronounced prolongation of TTP and wash-out times, consistent with ischemic core formation. In contrast, cortical regions show reduced rCBF with preserved ADC and elevated rCBV, indicative of viable penumbra. These cortical regions display markedly delayed TTP and prolonged WS-30% during occlusion and early reperfusion (≤2 h), reflecting impaired but preserved neurovascular responsiveness and a heightened risk of core conversion. With continued reperfusion, perfusion timing parameters progressively normalize, whereas diffusion deficits remain confined to the subcortical core. 

Hierarchical classification of ischemic core and penumbra based on diffusion, perfusion, and hemodynamic timing

Ischemic tissue was first coarsely classified using combined ADC and CBF measurements to separate core and penumbra candidates, reflecting diffusion injury and perfusion deficit, respectively (Fig. 2A-C). This initial ADC–CBF split captures the classical perfusion–diffusion mismatch observed during occlusion. To further resolve physiological heterogeneity within these regions, dynamic perfusion timing metric, wash-out delay was then applied to refine each class (Fig. 2D-E).

Within the core candidate region, Core 1 represents irreversibly infarcted tissue characterized by severely reduced ADC, minimal perfusion, and blunted BOLD-DSC responses, consistent with complete vascular failure. Core 2 denotes an evolving core with diffusion restriction but residual perfusion and delayed hemodynamic responses, indicating ongoing tissue injury with limited vascular supply (Fig. 2F-G).

Within the penumbra candidate region, Penumbra 1 corresponds to hypoperfused yet viable tissue with preserved ADC but markedly prolonged wash-out and TTP, reflecting severely impaired and a high risk of infarct progression. In contrast, Penumbra 2 represents lower-risk penumbral tissue with preserved diffusion and relatively shorter delays, consistent with collateral-supported perfusion and greater stability (Fig. 2F-G).

Conclusion

In this study, we demonstrate that dynamic BOLD-DSC MRI provides a non-invasive and sensitive means to longitudinally characterize the evolution of ischemic tissue. By integrating diffusion injury with static and dynamic perfusion metrics, including CBV, CBF, wash-out kinetics, and time to peak, we stratified ischemic tissue into physiologically distinct core and penumbra subtypes and tracked voxel-level transitions during arterial occlusion and following reperfusion. These combined perfusion–diffusion signatures reflect the severity and temporal progression of vascular and neurovascular coupling dysfunction in stroke, capturing impaired vascular responsiveness and delayed hemodynamic recovery. Collectively, our findings support the feasibility of dynamic BOLD-DSC–based subtyping as a mechanistically informed approach to characterize ischemic progression and to aid real-time prediction of tissue fate.
