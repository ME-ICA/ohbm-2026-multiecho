Introduction

Understanding the neural mechanisms of chronic widespread pain has been challenging due to its distributed, dynamic, and highly individual nature. Emerging evidence suggests that spatiotemporal dynamics are critical for the integration of pain signals.1,2 Traditional fMRI approaches (e.g., seed- or ROI-based, ICA) often focus on static, averaged network features, which can obscure finer spatiotemporal patterns. Here, we introduce a dynamics-based framework that captures network- and region-level propagation and synchrony, providing a complementary perspective on pain-related brain activity. We assess synchrony using the Kuramoto order parameter to describe phase coherence in pain-related networks. In addition, we characterize traveling wave behavior using phase gradient directionality (PGD), a metric previously applied to 2-dimensional local field potential data3 which we extend to 3-dimensional fMRI data, allowing the detection of signal propagation within regions of interest. Our metrics are significantly associated with pain burden and reveal novel insights into mechanistic changes in pain circuitry.

Methods

Analyses were performed on preprocessed resting-state fMRI BOLD time series from the Acute to Chronic Pain Signatures (A2CPS) study from a cohort of participants scheduled for total knee arthroplasty (n=773). The fMRI data were parcellated using the 400-parcel Schaefer atlas (7 networks, 2mm resolution). Parcel-wise BOLD time series were transformed to analytic phase using the Hilbert transform. Phase synchrony was quantified using the Kuramoto order parameter (KOP), defined as the magnitude of the mean phase vector across parcels within a network. To compute PGD, we selected parcel subsets implicated in pain processing and estimated spatial phase gradients across neighboring parcels. A key innovation is that parcel adjacency was defined based on cortical geometry, restricting phase propagation to paths along the cortical surface. PGD at each time point was defined as the magnitude of the mean spatial gradient vector, normalized by the mean gradient magnitude. Time-averaged PGD was assessed within each parcel subset. Statistical analyses tested relationships between each metric and widespread pain (number of pain areas in Michigan Body Map) using linear models, adjusting for age, sex at birth, research site, and framewise displacement. Analyses focused on KOP within three networks (somatomotor, salience/ventral attention, and default mode), PGD in precuneus and posterior cingulate, and the ratio between PGDs in the cingulate and insula.

Results

We found a negative correlation between phase synchrony and number of pain areas across all three networks of interest: somatomotor (beta=-0.0043, p=0.0119), salience/ventral attention (beta=-0.00437, p=0.00769), and default mode (beta=-0.00349, p=0.0326). The decrease in synchrony within the default mode network supports previous findings showing reduced oscillatory activity in chronic pain.4 The PGD ratio between the cingulate and frontal-operculum-insula decreased with the number of pain areas (beta=-0.0179, p=0.0332), suggesting increased spatial propagation in the insula relative to the cingulate in individuals with more painful body sites. The PGD in parcels overlapping with the precuneus, an important hub of the default mode network, significantly increased with the number of pain areas (beta=0.000819, p=0.00685). This suggests that spatial coherence of phase propagation within the precuneus increases with higher pain burden, potentially reflecting enhanced integration in this region. This finding is consistent with prior work implicating the precuneus in integrating pain signals, and further provides spatiotemporal information at higher resolution that may help elucidate underlying mechanisms. In addition, we found that increased spatial propagation in the posterior cingulate cortex relates to widespread pain (beta=0.0009, p=0.0351). 

Conclusion

Our dynamics-based approach offers several advantages to traditional fMRI approaches. It is computationally efficient and captures key spatiotemporal features of brain activity, providing unique insights into mechanisms underlying pain processing. By framing neural activity in a dynamical context, this method provides an informative description of temporal and spatial patterns, which can complement approaches that summarize static pairwise correlations or decompose signals. Additionally, summarizing spatiotemporal behavior within a single framework can streamline statistical analyses and enhance power for detecting meaningful relationships with behavioral or clinical measures. Overall, our work supports the use of higher-resolution spatiotemporal features in understanding pain symptoms.
