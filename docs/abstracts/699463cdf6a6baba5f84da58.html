Introduction

Amodal completion occurs when partially obstructed stimuli are perceived, allowing missing information to be filled in within the perceptual representation of external input. In contrast, visual imagery refers to internally generated visual representations that arise in the absence of external sensory input. To investigate how top-down signals support amodal completion and visual imagery, we conducted a human 7T fMRI experiment with natural scene images combined with a visual occlusion paradigm that allows us to isolate top-down contextual signals in a non-stimulated region of V1 cortex. The sub-millimetre resolution available with 7T fMRI allowed us to compare the layer profiles of the two types of feedback signal: visual perception and imagery in the visual cortex, thus providing an important extension to our previous occlusion studies1. To differentiate the subtle nuances between these two streams of feedback signal, ultra-high-field imaging was considered essential due to the enhanced sensitivity afforded by the higher signal-to-noise ratio at increased field strength2.

Methods

We recruited 28 healthy human participants for our fMRI experiment, 22 of whom also attended a behavioural experiment. In the binocular rivalry behavioural experiment, we measured participants’ imagery abilities by measuring its impact on subsequent conscious perception of a binocular rivalry stimulus. There were two tasks in this paradigm: the eye dominance task and the imagery strength task. After determining each participant’s dominant eye, we presented them with red horizontal and green vertical gratings in a binocular rivalry task and measured how their perception was influenced by the colour we asked them to imagine prior to that. This gave us an indication of their imagery abilities.

In the first half[1]  of our fMRI experiment, over the course of 4 functional runs (204 volumes) participants viewed two types of trials presented in a random order. Amodal completion (AC) trials consisted of the scene images with a white occluder placed over the lower-right image quadrant. Occlusion imagery (OI) trials cued the participants to imagine the full version of the previously seen occluded image. The second half of fMRI experiment (4 functional runs, 204 volumes each) consisted of two different types of trials where participants either viewed the full scene image (full perception condition, FP), or imagined the full image (full imagery condition, FI). This design allowed us to separate imagery trials where participants imagined image content that they had seen previously, from imagery trials where they had to generate images of the missing scene information based on the available contextual information. We collected fMRI data with a 7T Siemens scanner and a 32-channel NOVA head coil at Imaging Centre of Excellence at Queen Elizabeth University Hospital in Glasgow. We performed one scanning session for each participant, which included 8 functional scans (0.8mm isotropic resolution with 2s repetition time) and a high-resolution anatomical scan (voxel size: 0.6 mm isotropic).

We used BrainVoyager for the data preprocessing: for all functional data, we performed slice scan time correction, 3D rigid body motion correction, high-pass temporal filtering using a Fourier basis set with 4 cycles, and linear detrending to further process the data. No spatial smoothing was performed to preserve the spatial specificity needed for laminar analysis and multivariate pattern analysis (MVPA). We transformed the raw anatomical data to standard ACPC orientation but otherwise preserving its native space.  For the transformation we manually located the anterior commissure and the posterior commissure. The functional data and the upsampled anatomy (0.4mm3) were co-registered using boundary-based registration. Finally, we created 4D volume-time-course (VTC) functional data, preserving the sub-millimetre resolution, which was then used in the MVPA analysis with open source (LIBSVM) and customised MATLAB code to conduct the leave-one-run-out cross validation to get the decoding accuracy. The borders of area V1 and the occluded region of interest within V1 were defined functionally with a polar angle retinotopic mapping run with black and white flashing checkerboard and localiser presented in the occluded section of the visual field. 

Results

The group mean imagery strength was 52.79% ± 8.9% after excluding three participants’ data because of their high mock responses. The single-trial MVPA decoding accuracies of AC and FI conditions across all occluded V1 were significantly higher than chance level after FDR correction (Fig. A B), which might indicate that the contextual feedback and memory-based imagery content propagated to non-stimulated visual areas in the primary visual cortex. In contrast, the decoding accuracy of OI condition in occluded V1 was not significantly different from chance level (the red plot in Fig. A), which could suggest that the imagery filling-in at the previously occluded area could not be effectively distinguished by the classifier or the white occluder was still present in their mental eyes when participants were doing the imagery. The failed cross-decoding between imagery (FI and OI) and perception (FP and AC) might show the different processing codes between these two  mental processes (Fig. B).Classification across the cortical depths in the occluded V1 region provided information about the laminar profiles of the feedback signal involved in each of the four conditions. Feedback information during AC and FI could be successfully decoded from the brain activity in the non-stimulated area (Fig. C), with a trend of increasing decoding accuracy from deep to superficial layers (Fig. D). However, this was not the case for OI, for which decoding accuracy was at chance level across all cortical depths (Fig. C). As expected, the decoding for the FP condition was highest and significant across all layers.

Conclusion

The successful decoding of scene information in the amodal completion condition from non-stimulated areas of V1 suggests  that the feedback signal utilised in amodal completion is informed by the surrounding context. The feedback information employed in imagery was informative enough to allow the classifier to successfully differentiate between the imagined scenes, which would suggest that this feedback signal  draws upon prior knowledge stored in the brain that can be updated by the feedforward information.
