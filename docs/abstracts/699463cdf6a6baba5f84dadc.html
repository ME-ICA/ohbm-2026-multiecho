Introduction

The neonatal period is characterized by rapid structural maturation. Brain maturation during this period is closely linked to later neurodevelopmental outcomes, cognitive function, and vulnerability to neurological disorders. Therefore, developing quantitative measures of neonatal brain maturation is important in neuroimaging research.

Across the perinatal period, brain maturation involves complex morphological changes, such as expansion of white matter and gray matter, increases in total brain volume, thickening of cortical layers, expansion of cortical surface area, and increased cortical folding. These changes emerge during late fetal development and continue rapidly throughout the neonatal period. To this end, structural MRI enables capturing these developmental patterns non-invasively.1

Brain age prediction has been proposed as an effective framework to summarize high-dimensional MRI features into a single biomarker reflecting normative brain development. The discrepancy between predicted brain age and chronological age has the potential to indicate atypical or delayed maturation. In neonatal neuroimaging, T2-weighted (T2w) images are often preferred over T1-weighted (T1w) images, as immature white matter exhibits more intuitive contrast on T2w.2

Recent advances in deep learning have shown the potential to uncover such complex patterns from data, as they offer a way to interpret models through post-hoc analysis. In particular, deep learning-based age prediction may help evaluate atypical or delayed maturation. However, while several studies in older age groups have investigated prediction performance and interpretability, research in neonates remains limited.3

In this study, we develop and evaluate deep learning-based brain age prediction models for the neonatal brain using T2w images. We assess both predictive performance and model interpretability through post-hoc visualization techniques, aiming to better understand the developmental patterns across ages.

Methods

2.1 Dataset

In this study, we utilized T2w images from 716 neonates from the publicly available Developing Human Connectome Project (dHCP, third release, http://www.developingconnectome.org), aged between 37 and 46 weeks.4 The T2w images were previously preprocessed using the dHCP structural pipeline (including bias field correction). To train and validate the age prediction model, 3D T2w images from subjects with radiology score (RS) of 1 or 2 were used. The total numbers of subjects for training and validation were 537 and 20, respectively. For testing, we utilized an additional 170  subjectss. The test set contains 10 subjects with RS 1, 20 subjects with RS 2, 150 subjects with RS higher than 3.

2.2 dHCP structural MRI protocol

T2w images were acquired using a 3T MRI scanner (Philips Achieva) equipped with a neonatal 32-channel phased-array head coil and a customized patient handling system (Rapid Biomedical GmbH, Rimpar, Germany). A fast spin echo sequence was used with the following parameters: repetition time = 12,000 ms, echo time = 156 ms, in-plane resolution = 0.8 mm, and slice thickness = 1.6 mm.

2.3 Preprocessing

    Figure 1A illustrates the preprocessing procedure. First, T2w images were skull-stripped using the HD-BET brain extraction tool. Next, the images were rigid-body registered to the dHCP neonatal brain standard space, followed by non-linear registration to the same space.

2.4 Network architecture

    For the brain age prediction, a simple fully convolutional network (SFCN) was used as a backbone network.5 SFCN consists of 7 convolutional blocks. The first 5 blocks consist of a 3x3x3 3D convolution layer followed by an instance normalization layer, a max pooling layer, and a ReLU activation layer. These blocks consistently increase the number of feature channels up to 256. A 6th convolutional block consists of a 1x1x1 3D convolution layer to reduce the channel dimension, followed by an instance normalization layer, and a ReLU activation layer. The final block includes an average pooling layer, a dropout layer, and a 1x1x1 3D convolution layer followed by a softmax layer, which outputs the probability distribution.

2.5 Training objectives

Since structural differences in MR images are minimal across narrow age ranges (e.g., 1–6 days), we discretized the age range and reformulated the regression task as a classification task (Fig. 1B), thereby reducing the model's sensitivity to minimal structural changes and allowing it to better capture age-related imaging features. The actual age range of 37–46 weeks was first extended to 34–48 weeks by adding about a 2.5-week margin to ensure complete Gaussian-shaped distributions, and then divided into 14 intervals of 1 week each. By using this range, the real age was converted into a normal distribution, with a standard deviation of 6. Then, a network was optimized to predict the distribution using the Kullback–Leibler divergence loss. 

2.6 Saliency map

To interpret brain age prediction results, we utilized a saliency map (SM), which is widely used for interpreting and visualizing the neural networks outputs. The SM was computed by approximating the nonlinear score function with a first-order Taylor expansion and calculating the absolute value of the voxel-wise gradient of the model output with respect to the input MRI volume (Fig. 1C).

2.7 Evaluation

Model performance was quantitatively evaluated using the mean absolute error (MAE) and the Pearson correlation coefficient (R). To evaluate the model’s ability to capture abnormal patterns, we calculated predicted age difference (PAD), defined as the difference between the predicted age and the actual age.

SMs were visualized to explore the differences according to age, sex, and RS. While visualizing the SM of sex-related analysis, 67 male and 57 female subjects were used since age information was missing for some subjects. Meanwhile, for age and RS analyses , all 159 test subjects were used.

Results

As a result of the model performance evaluation, for the validation set, the MAE was 0.75 weeks, and the R reached 0.92 (Fig. 2A). The scatter plot of predicted age stratified by RS in the test set shows that the predicted age tends to decrease as RS increases (Fig. 2B). Consistently, the PAD exhibited an increasing trend with higher RS values (Fig. 2C).

SM analysis shows that the model highlights the occipital lobe and basal ganglia more prominently for relatively younger ages, whereas the frontal lobe is relatively more emphasized for predictions at older ages, as shown in Fig. 2D.  SM differences stratified by RS indicate that, when predicting healthy subjects, the model places greater emphasis on the frontal lobe (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, SM differences stratified by age reveal sex-dependent patterns. For younger subjects, males exhibit stronger saliency signals in the frontal and occipital lobes as well as the right basal ganglia compared with females. As age increases, males show stronger signals in the occipital lobe than females. However, when predicting older subjects, the model shifts its focus from the occipital lobe toward the left hemisphere and basal ganglia (Fig. 2G).

Conclusion

In this study, we performed neonatal brain age prediction using T2w images. We evaluated the performance of the brain age prediction model and analyzed predicted age stratified by RS. In addition, we investigated the interpretability of the neonatal brain age prediction model through SM analysis. Group-level saliency analysis revealed age-dependent shifts in brain regions contributing to age prediction. Occipital and subcortical regions contributed more to younger age predictions, whereas frontal and left-hemispheric regions became more prominent for older age predictions. Furthermore, saliency patterns stratified by RS indicated distinct attention to frontal regions in healthy neonates, suggesting that the model may capture clinically meaningful markers of brain maturation and abnormalities.Sex- and age-stratified analyses revealed sex-specific differences in cortical and subcortical contributions to age prediction. These findings indicate that combining deep learning–based brain age prediction with post-hoc interpretability analysis may offer insights into neonatal brain developmental processes.
