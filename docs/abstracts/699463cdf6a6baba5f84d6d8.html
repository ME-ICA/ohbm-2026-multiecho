Introduction

Psychotic symptoms are a key feature of schizophrenia and may occur in other psychiatric disorders including bipolar disorder and major depressive disorder. Experience of psychosis is associated with changes in social functioning1, which leads to quality of life and economic losses. Social participation (SP) is linked with several environmental, cognitive and brain-related factors. Among environmental factors, SP is dependent on personal and community circumstances related to deprivation2 including access to commercial and recreational resources, degree of support from family or friends, access to independent and public transportation options, and neighbourhood user-friendliness. There is also evidence that social cognition and general cognitive measures such as processing speed, working memory and verbal learning may predict social network size in healthy individuals and those at risk for psychosis. Brain structural and functional properties are linked with social outcomes3. Parameters including structural variation in subcortical regions such as the amygdala as well as cortical structures such as the orbitofrontal cortex has been identified as associated with measures of SP. White matter microstructure in frontotemporal tracts, inferior longitudinal fasciculus and the corpus callosum are also implicated in social functioning. Furthermore, several resting state networks have been linked with social outcomes including the default mode, limbic, and frontoparietal networks.

We investigate the relationship between region-based material deprivation and SP in the UK Biobank and use structural equation modelling to investigate whether indirect relationships may be present, mediated through predetermined measures of brain structure and function, namely the social brain network (SBN), and a predicted latent measure of general intelligence. We further investigate whether experience of psychosis or genetic polygenic risk for schizophrenia has an impact on these relationships.

Methods

Figure 1 summarises methods. Data used here are from participants in the UK Biobank (UKB). We defined a general population cohort as individuals who have no diagnoses of neurodegenerative, demyelinating or neoplastic brain diseases, are not part of the psychosis cohort (defined next), and have valid imaging and social outcome data. We defined our psychosis cohort as any individual with an ICD summary diagnosis including schizophrenia, schizotypal or delusional disorders, individuals with bipolar disorder or major depressive disorder with a psychotic component or individuals reporting having ever been diagnosed by a professional with schizophrenia or any other type of psychosis or psychotic illness. Polygenic liability for schizophrenia (SCZ PGS) was also investigated as a moderating factor. Only individuals of white British ancestry were used in the analysis cohorts.

Social participation (SP) was indexed using two social support variables: Frequency of friend/family visits (p1031) and leisure/social activities (p6160). Frequency of friend/family visits was recoded from 0 – 5 based on frequency. Leisure/social activities were recoded 0 – 5 based on the number of social activities attended once a week or more often. Both scores were summed, resulting in a numerical scale ranging from 0 (lowest SP) to 10 (highest SP).

We used pre-processed brain imaging-derived phenotypes (IDP) from a workflow developed and run on behalf of UKB from the September 2022 (v1.9) release in this study. T1-weighted structural measures indexing volume of subcortical structures (FreeSurfer subcortical volumetric segmentation; p1102); volume of cortical structures (Freesurfer Destrieux (a2009s) parcellation; p197); Tract-Based Spatial Statistics (TBSS)-derived mean fractional anisotropy (FA) values from diffusion-weighted MRI  (p134), functional connectivity measures from 25-dimension partial correlations of ICA-derived resting-state functional MRI networks (p111) were used. 

Based on previous work by Casburn et al., we defined a social brain network (SBN) from IDPs statistically linked with SP in the UKB. Our general population cohort was split into a 25:75 ratio with 25% being used to determine components of the SBN. The remaining 75% referred to herein as the “Test cohort” was used to test our models combined with individuals with psychosis. Recursive feature elimination (RFE) was used to determine components of the SBN.  For each set of IDPs (cortical volume, subcortical volume, diffusion-derived FA and functional connectivity (FC) measures), scaled IDP values were assessed for multicollinearity and highly correlated (R2>0.8) sets of IDPs were represented using an arithmetic mean. RFE feature selection was performed on each brain measure-type set in the 25% subset and 10-fold cross-validation and 5 repeats were used to control for overfitting. Each feature selection process was iterated 100-fold and IDPs retained as predictors in ≥ 50% of iterations were considered stable components of the SBN. The mean of IDPs selected in each imaging subtype was used to represent the SBN measures: SBN mean cortical volume, SBN mean subcortical volume, SBN mean FA and SBN mean FC. Our set of SBN IDPs generated by Casburn et al. comprised of 29 cortical volume, 11 subcortical volume, 9 diffusion FA and 49 rfMRI FC measures in total.

A general intelligence latent factor, “g”, was established from four factrors: fluid intelligence (FL); matrix pattern completion (MAT); symbol-digit substitution (SDS); and alphanumeric trail making (TMT). Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was used to estimate and predict a latent factor of general intelligence in the individuals in the test cohort. 

Baseline differences in SP between psychosis and healthy control populations were assessed using t-test. The relationship between TDI and SP was assessed using linear regression with age and sex as covariates. Structural equation modelling (SEM) and CFA were performed using the lavaan R package.

Results

Our test cohort consisted of approximately 28.5k individuals, of whom 140 have experience of psychosis. Median age at imaging was 67 years old, and 54.4% of participants were female. Increased material deprivation was associated with decreased SP (Std.β=-0.051, p<2.2e-16). Individuals with psychosis showed significantly lower SP t(28455)=2.117, p<0.05 and higher deprivation t(28431)=-3.99, p<0.001 than those in the general population cohort. 

In the SEM model, mean social brain network (SBN) cortical volume was negatively associated with TDI (Std.β=-0.022; p<0.001) and positively associated with SP (Std.β=0.038; p<0.001). Similarly, mean SBN subcortical volume was negatively associated with TDI (Std.β=-0.029; p<0.001) and positively associated with SP (Std.β=0.035; p<0.001). Neither mean SBN FA nor mean SBN FC was significantly associated with TDI or SP.

Predicted general intelligence (g) had a significant positive partial correlation with all mean SBN measures, except mean SBN FC. Predicted g was negatively associated with TDI (Std.β=-0.061; p<2.2e-16) and was positively associated with SP (Std.β=0.028; p=3.63e-4).

In the mediation analyses, SBN mean cortical (Std.β=-0.00083; p<0.001) and subcortical (Std. β=-0.001; p<0.001) volume but not SBN mean FA (Std.β=0; p=NA) nor SBN mean FC (Std.β=0; p=NA) had a significant mediating effect on the relationship between TDI and SP. Predicted g mediated the relationship between TDI and SP (Std.β=-0.0017; p<0.001).

Models with significant mediation effects were examined for a moderation effect of diagnostic group. Psychosis status did not have a significant moderating effect on the relationship between TDI and SBN mean cortical or subcortical volume or on predicted g. However, SCZ PGS in healthy controls significantly moderated the relationship between TDI and SBN mean cortical (F(1,27695)=6.22; p<0.05) and subcortical (F(1,27694)=5.43; p<0.05) volume.

Psychosis experience significantly moderated the relationship between mean SBN cortical volume and SP (F(1,27877)=2.03; p<0.05) and between mean subcortical volume and SP (F(1,27876)=6.30; p<0.05). Psychosis experience also had a moderating role in the relationship between predicted intelligence and SP (F(1,19909)=6.20; p<0.05). Significant mediation models and moderating effects of psychosis experience and SCZ PGS are illustrated (Figure 2).

Conclusion

Greater material deprivation was significantly associated with decreased social participation. These results are in line with conventional theory: areas with fewer resources have a shortfall in social capital which lowers the capacity for social interactions. Better social functioning has been previously linked to both objective and perceived neighbourhood affluence. As well as being an important index of function and quality of life, some evidence has reinforced the direct importance of social disconnection in health outcomes, namely an increased risk of all-cause mortality4, comparable to known mortality factors such as smoking. In our mediation analysis, we saw a small but significant role for the mean volume of the social brain network but not mean anisotropy, a measure of white matter microstructural organization, or at-rest functional connectivity measures in mediating the relationship between deprivation and social participation. Deprivation during childhood is associated with cortical volume and thickness changes later in life, furthermore, total brain volume may mediate the relationship between deprivation and cognitive impairment and behavioural disorder outcomes. There are some previous examples of brain features mediating the relationship between deprivation stress and behaviour. In the context of social disorder, the impact of poverty exposure on conduct disorder symptomology is mediated by structural changes in the orbitofrontal cortex in a 25-year longitudinal study5. Furthermore, when looking at material deprivation and stress-induced cognitive and behavioural outcomes, there is evidence for a mediating role of prefrontal cortex structure.

Experience of psychosis was associated with lower social participation in our cohort. This suggests individuals with psychosis, on average, are less likely to participate in scenarios involving socialising and taking part in activities with a likely social component. Individuals with psychosis are more likely to face social exclusion and rejection, which could lead to them being involuntary inhibited from these participations. Impaired general and social cognitive abilities are key in psychosis and given the established role of these factors in psychosocial functioning, individuals with experience of psychosis may be at a disadvantage in navigating social environments. In our analysis, individuals with psychosis showed a significantly differential relationship between the social brain network structural volumes and social participation. This could suggest that psychosis-linked structural changes are also involved in social behaviour outcomes. Furthermore, the moderating effect of schizophrenia PGS between TDI and brain structural properties indicates a possible gene-by-environment interaction.   

In this work, we highlight that material deprivation may be linked directly and indirectly to changes in social function. Although the effect of brain measures and cognitive ability is small in this older sample, it highlights the possibility that deprivation may be influencing these measures which may in turn influence behaviour – however longitudinal analyses would be necessary to provide more insight into the directionality of these relationships. Furthermore, this may suggest fundamental differences in how brain structural measures and cognition influence social outcomes in those with psychosis and those with higher genetic liability for schizophrenia. Material deprivation is a modifiable life factor, through targeted economic and policy shift. This work, followed by extension of findings to a younger cohort with more plastic social behaviours, may highlight a role for economic and policy interventions aimed at improving psychosocial outcomes.
