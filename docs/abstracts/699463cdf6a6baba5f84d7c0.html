Introduction

Mathematical skills play a critical role in shaping academic paths and are closely linked to overall well-being. However, math anxiety is widespread and is characterized by fear, apprehension or other adverse emotional reactions elicited by mathematical or numerical activities. This is particularly concerning because research has shown that anxiety has a negative impact on cognitive performance. People with higher anxiety levels often do worse on fundamental academic competencies such as reading comprehension or mathematical tasks. High anxiety, especially in anxiety disorders, is linked to higher school dropout rates, leading to poorer socioeconomic outcomes. Importantly, math anxiety has been linked to reduced mathematical performance, with individuals experiencing high levels of math anxiety consistently achieving lower scores in mathematics.1 Although it is well established that stress and stress-inducing stimuli can trigger or worsen anxiety, as observed in both animal and human studies,2,3 previous research has examined these effects only indirectly, for example by correlating general math anxiety ratings with math performance measured inside an MR scanner. Such studies have shown that math anxiety is associated with both structural alterations, including reduced amygdala volume4 and functional changes such as increased activation in emotion-related regions including the amygdala, insula and prefrontal cortex.5 Consequently, the underlying mechanisms of math anxiety and in particular the influence of stress on mathematical performance, is still unclear. The aim of this project is to address this gap by investigating how typically developing children respond, both behaviorally and neurologically, to mathematical challenges under stress. To our knowledge, this is the first study to directly measure how stress affects mathematical performance, allowing for a more precise assessment of neural responses to stress during mathematical and numerical processing.

Methods

To address this gap, 20 typically developing children aged seven to nine years were assessed behaviorally for general cognitive ability, mathematical ability and both general and math-specific anxiety. They then completed an fMRI task in which they solved subtraction problems alongside a control visuospatial task requiring them to decide whether two square-like figures would form a complete square when overlaid. The data were acquired on a 3T General Electric Signa scanner using an 8-channel head coil. Whole-brain functional images were obtained with an interleaved gradient-echo EPI sequence and high-resolution T1-weighted anatomical images were acquired using a fast spoiled-gradient echo sequence. Both tasks were administered under non-stress and stress condition. Stress was induced via an elapsing time bar, while in the non-stress condition the time bar remained full. To induce additional stress, feedback was provided after each trial using a happy or sad smiley (see Figure 1). Tasks were self-paced with a maximum of six seconds per problem, identical across conditions. fMRI data were processed using Statistical Parametric Mapping 12 (SPM12) running under MATLAB. First-level analyses were conducted using a general linear model (GLM), and second-level group analyses were performed on contrast images derived from the first level.

Results

Interestingly, results have revealed that children showed improved performance under stress in the subtraction task, as indicated by paired-samples t-tests, characterized by significantly shorter reaction times (t(18)=3.778, p<.001) and higher accuracy (t(18)=-5.411, p=<.001) compared to the non-stress condition. In the visuospatial control task, stress similarly led to reduced reaction times, as analyzed using a paired samples t-test (t(19)= 4.756, p <.001), while accuracy remained stable according to a Wilcoxon signed-rank test (Z = -0.55, p=0.585). Neuropsychological assessments further revealed a significant positive correlation between general anxiety levels and math-specific anxiety (r =0.51, p=0.03).

The fMRI data in the non-stress subtraction condition, showed strongest activation in the bilateral inferior parietal lobules, posterior-medial frontal regions and occipital cortices (p < 0.005, cluster-extent corrected k > 34). This observed activation is consistent with the known functional roles of these regions in supporting numerical reasoning, cognitive control, and visual processing, all of which are critical for performing the subtraction task. Under stress, subtraction trials elicited additional activation in occipital, insular and frontal regions, suggesting heightened engagement of networks involved in emotional regulation as well as visual and evaluative processing. In the control task, stress specifically increased activation in the right midcingulate cortex and thalamic regions, indicating a potential involvement of the salience network when children were exposed to time pressure in a non-numerical context.

Conclusion

Thus, this study was able to measure the effects of stress on brain activity directly for the first time. The results indicate that in typically developing children, moderate stress appeared to act as a motivator rather than an interference. The experienced stress enhances engagement of fronto-parietal and additional neural networks leading to improved performance efficiency without loss of accuracy.

Future work will extend this paradigm beyond typically developing children to those with developmental dyscalculia, a specific learning disorder characterized by persistent difficulties in mathematics. Children with developmental dyscalculia are much more likely to experience math anxiety compared to their typically developing peers. In this population, stress is expected to impair rather than facilitate performance, providing insight into how heightened anxiety interacts with numerical processing in atypical development.
