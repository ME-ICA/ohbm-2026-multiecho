Introduction

Radiotherapy (RT) is widely used to treat brain tumors and metastases, yet many survivors develop long-term cognitive dysfunction. Although traditionally linked to delayed structural changes, evidence indicates central nervous system alterations and cognitive decline can emerge within hours of irradiation1. Early physiological disturbances may contribute to permanent impairment, making characterization of immediate RT effects critical for improving outcomes.

Building on our recent work showing that fNIRS detects immediate irradiation-induced changes in brain hemodynamics2, we extend this approach to examine alterations in brain pulsations across multiple physiological frequency bands, reflecting cerebrovascular dynamics and heart–brain interactions.

We use fNIRS-derived amplitude of physiological fluctuations to test the hypothesis that irradiation-induced tissue and endothelial stress modulates cerebral blood flow and solute transport. Frequency-specific changes during whole-brain radiotherapy are quantified and assessed for dependence on clinical and demographic factors, highlighting potential relevance for adaptive radiotherapy.

Methods

We analyzed fNIRS recordings from 32 patients receiving whole-brain radiotherapy (WBRT) in a previous study2, now focusing on distinct physiological frequency bands to probe cerebral hemodynamic responses. WBRT was delivered according to clinical protocol, either 20 Gy in five daily fractions or 30 Gy in ten daily fractions, using conformal static fields with forward intensity-modulated radiotherapy (FIMRT) via a medical linear accelerator (TrueBeam, TrueBeam Edge, or Clinac iX, Varian). Treatments combined right and left fields (90°/270°) irradiated sequentially. The study followed the Declaration of Helsinki and GDPR, received ethical approval (237/2018), and all participants provided informed consent.

fNIRS signals were recorded at 800 Hz using a frequency-encoded device, with optodes placed on the left and right forehead (2.7–3.6 cm source-detector distance). The device was positioned outside the radiation room, with only optical fibers entering the treatment chamber. Near-infrared light was delivered at 690, 810, 830, and 980 nm using high-power LEDs (Roithner). Measurement lasted the full treatment fraction. Further setup details are provided in our previous studies2,3.

Signals were preprocessed as in2 and converted to HbO, HbR, and water concentration changes using 690, 830, and 980 nm wavelengths via the Beer-Lambert law4. Recordings with low SNR (<1) or technical artifacts were excluded, leaving 90 fractions for analysis, and one high-quality channel per fraction was used.

Signals were filtered into very-low, respiratory, and cardiac frequency bands, in addition to the full-band signal. Five time segments were defined to assess irradiation effects: 20 s before the first radiation field (pre-RT), the first field (RT 1), mid-interval between fields (inter-RT), the second field (RT 2), and 9 s after the second field (post-RT). Radiation fields lasted on average 21 ± 4 s, and the post-RT segment was shorter due to limited recording time.

Fractional amplitude of physiological fluctuations (fAPF), a modification of fALFF, was calculated for very-low, respiratory, and cardiac frequency bands. fAPF is defined as the ratio of the average amplitude within a specific band to that of the full frequency range, and was computed only on band-pass filtered signals5.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that fNIRS-derived fAPF can capture immediate, irradiation-induced changes in brain physiological pulsations. Similar alterations have previously been associated with neurological pathologies and long-term RT complications, suggesting that real-time monitoring of fAPF could inform adaptive radiotherapy. Further studies are needed to link these immediate changes with long-term outcomes.
