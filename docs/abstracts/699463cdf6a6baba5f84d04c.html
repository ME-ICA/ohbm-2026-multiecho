Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a leading cause of global disability.1 Substantial evidence implicates low-level neuroinflammation as a pathophysiological mechanism underlying MDD.2 Regions such as the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and insula have been identified as sites of gliosis in positron emission tomography (PET) and post-mortem studies of MDD.2 Fractional amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (fALFF), measured using resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI), is a measure of spontaneous neural activity that may indicate the functional correlates of neuroinflammatory mechanisms in the brain.3 Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the effect of modulating neuroinflammation with a putative centrally acting anti-inflammatory, low-dose naltrexone (LDN), on ACC and insula fALFF in MDD.

Methods

Participants with moderate, medicated MDD were recruited and randomized to receive LDN (up to 4.5mg per day) or placebo for 12 weeks. Rs-fMRI data were acquired on a 3T Siemens Vida Fit scanner at baseline and 12 weeks. Whole-brain fALFF maps were computed in the whole frequency band (0.01 – 0.08 Hz) and the slow-4 frequency band (0.01 – 0.027 Hz) using the RESTPlus V1.31 toolbox.  Average fALFF metrics were extracted from four regions of interest (ROIs): the left and right ACC and the left and right insula. Treatment effects for fALFF were tested using linear mixed-effects models adjusted for baseline. For regions showing a significant effect of treatment, the relationship between change in fALFF and the change in depression severity (measured with the Montgomery Asberg Depression Rating Scale [MADRS]) and inflammation levels (indexed by high-sensitivity C-reactive protein [hsCRP]) was assessed with Pearson correlations.

Results

Baseline and 12-week rs-fMRI data were available for 29 participants (LDN = 13, placebo = 16). LDN increased slow-4 band fALFF in the left ACC (SMD = 0.244 p = 0.008). This increase was not correlated with the change in MADRS or log-transformed hsCRP. There was no significant effect of treatment for any of the four ROIs in the whole frequency band (all ROI p values  > 0.05).

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that LDN has a discernible effect on left ACC fALFF, although alterations were not related to depression severity or inflammation levels. Given that the ACC exhibits a high level of expression of opioid receptors, the observed effects may represent LDN's opioid receptor antagonism, which is thought to reduce immune cell proliferation by modulating the opioid growth factor (OGF) axis.4 This study was limited by a small sample size, and replication in larger samples is required to confirm these findings. Future studies should also seek to correlate the observed changes in fALFF with serum measures of β-endorphin and OGF to clarify the role of the OGF axis in mediating treatment effects of LDN on neuroinflammation.
