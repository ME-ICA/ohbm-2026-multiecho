Introduction

Post stroke aphasia is a common and debilitating complication, but objective assessment measures that reflect the brain neurophysiological state are still lacking. Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) is a non-invasive technique that enables real-time monitoring of cerebral hemodynamics by estimating changes in oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin concentrations. Several studies using fNIRS in post stroke aphasia patients have reported that increased activation in perilesional left frontal and temporal regions or contralesional homologues during language tasks, while other channels within the same networks show decreased or absent task-related responses. However, there are still controversial for using fNIRS as outcome measure tool for language function in post stroke aphasia. This study aims to identify brain activation during language tasks in post stroke aphasia patients using fNIRS and compare them with age- and sex- matched healthy controls (HC). We also investigate the relationship between brain activation levels and aphasia severity.

Methods

Eleven individuals with subacute left‑hemisphere post stroke aphasia patients and 11 age- and sex- matched healthy controls were enrolled for this study. Participants who 1) inability to provide informed consent, 2) pre-existing language or cognitive impairments, 3) persistent motion artifacts that precluded valid acquisition despite repositioning were excluded. 

The patients were evaluated cognitive language function using mini-mental status examination and PK-WAB (Paradise Korean version-Western Aphasia Battery). All participants were evaluated cerebral hemodynamic responses during two language tasks (auditory comprehension and picture naming tasks) using fNIRS. To establish a stable baseline signal, a 60-second eyes-open resting period was initiated where participants fixed on a blank white sheet. Therefore, each language task was implemented using a block design, consisting of three alternating cycles of 20-seconds of rest and 20-seconds of task performance (Figure 1). Cerebral hemodynamic data were acquired using the NIRSport2® system (NIRx Medical Technology, Berlin, Germany), an fNIRS device operating at two wavelengths (760 nm and 850 nm) with a sampling rate of 10.2 Hz. The system featured 8 LED light sources and 8 detectors, yielding 20 source–detector channels with a fixed 3‑cm source–detector distance. Optodes were mounted on a headcap configured according to the international 10–10 electrode placement system, targeting bilateral frontotemporal language areas with a symmetric channel layout. For precise anatomical localization and data interpretation, the position of each fNIRS channel was co-registered to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) coordinate system using the AtlasViewer and fOLD (fNIRS Optodes’ Location Decider) toolbox. Data processing was performed in MATLAB R2022b (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) using the HOMER3 toolbox (Huppert, Diamond, Franceschini, & Boas, 2009). Raw intensity data were band-pass filtered between 0.01 and 0.2 Hz to remove baseline noise and filter out potential respiratory (about 0.3 Hz) and heart rate (about 1.0–1.6 Hz) signals. No channels were excluded due to excessive noise or poor contact. We analyzed HbO as the primary signal and HbR, HbT as secondary signals. Channel-wise HbO concentrations were then calculated from the filtered data using the modified Beer–Lambert law. 

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality of continuous variables was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test and Q-Q plot inspection. Age- and sex- matched a priori, are presented descriptively as counts and percentages without hypothesis testing. Between-group contrasts in channel-wise hemodynamic responses employed independent-samples t tests; within-group rest–task contrasts, paired t tests.

Associations between K-WAB AQ and channel responses relied on Pearson or Spearman correlations, as appropriate. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant. No correction for multiple comparisons was applied. Accordingly, all statistical tests were treated as exploratory and hypothesis generating.

Results

Post stroke aphasia patients showed statistically significant reduced HbO concentration in the left frontotemporal language network compared to control group. However, during the picture naming task, Post stroke aphasia patients demonstrated significantly increased HbO concentration in the left superior, middle and inferior temporal regions compared to control group. In the auditory comprehension task, Post stroke aphasia patients also exhibited increased HbO concentration in the left frontal and temporal area (retrosubicular area, pars opecuralis, temporopolar area). No definite reduction in HbO concentration was observed in the post stroke aphasia patients during either picture naming and auditory comprehension tasks compared with control group. Notably, higher aphasia quotients scores that means good language function positively correlated with increased HbO concentration in the left frontotemporal area (temporopolar area, pars triangularis, retrosubicular area) in both picture naming and auditory comprehension tasks.  Whereas no significant correlation was found in homologous right hemisphere regions.

Conclusion

In post stroke aphasia, fNIRS can quantify task related hemodynamic response of left-hemisphere language network activation. Our preliminary findings that the significant association between hemodynamic responses and clinical language function support the potential use of fNIRS as objective biomarker of language function.
