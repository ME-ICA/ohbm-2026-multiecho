Introduction

Humans naturally tend to bond with others who are similar to themselves, a well-established phenomenon known as homophily. Growing evidence suggests that homophily also exists at the neural level, with socially closer dyads exhibit more similar neural responses (inter-subject correlation (ISC)) while viewing naturalistic stimuli1,2. However, prior studies have relied on a single imaging session, leaving it unclear how neural similarity and social proximity influence one another over time - do we become friends with those whose brains already process the world like ours, or do our neural responses become more alike as friendships develop?

The mental processes driving this neural similarity also remain an open question. The localization of this effect to brain regions supporting higher-order cognition imply that closer individuals interpret external events more similarly1,2. Yet this effect has held even after controlling for similarities in subjective ratings of the stimuli1,  suggesting that neural similarity reflect alignment in deeper psychological processes which are difficult to quantify. Although such latent processes cannot be directly measured, they may be probed indirectly by examining how the association between neural similarity and social proximity is affected by the emotional and cognitive demands elicited by the stimulus3.

Here, we explored how dyads’ neural similarity changes as relationships are formed. We examined whether neural similarity exists both before and after acquaintance, suggesting it may reflect social compatibility, or whether it emerges following acquaintance, reflecting social influence. We also investigated how this association is modulated by the stimulus content to which participants are exposed during scan. Last, we tested whether pre-acquaintance ISC patterns can be used to predict future social proximity, suggesting it may serve as a neural marker of the potential of two individuals to become friends.

Methods

33 undergraduate students underwent an fMRI session just before and after their first academic year (Figure 1A). Each imaging session included six movie-watching fMRI runs with socio-affective, academic, or neutral (non-affective and non-academic) content (two runs per content; 04:24 min:sec; 352 TRs each). Stimuli were selected to be engaging and relatively unfamiliar to participants, with each category designed to elicit distinct affective or cognitive processes. To avoid familiarity effects, different videos were used across the two imaging sessions. To minimize variability unrelated to stimulus content, videos were drawn from the same TV or lecture series whenever possible.

After each imaging session, participants filled a social interaction survey2 in which they reported their friends from their academic community. Participants’ social network was characterized at each timepoint based on these reports, with a connection between two students defined when at least one reported the other as a friend. Social distance was calculated between each pair of participants as the length of the shortest path between them: a distance of one for directly connected participants (i.e., friends), a distance of two if both were connected to a mutual friend (i.e., friends-of-friends), and so on, increasing with each additional step in the shortest path (Figure 1B).

To isolate the effect of social interaction on neural similarity, all analyses were performed on dyads who had no connection at the first timepoint. This resulted in a final sample of 98 dyads whose social distances at the end of the academic year were distributed as follows: 15 at social distance of 1; 32 at social distance of 2; and 51 at social distance of 3 or more. 

To calculate ISC patterns, we concatenated the timeseries of the movie-watching fMRI runs corresponding to the same stimulus category. Prior to concatenation, each vertex timeseries was shifted by 4 TRs to account for the hemodynamic delay and then normalized (z-scored). The normalized timeseries were then concatenated for each participant within each stimulus category. These data was parcellated into 100 parcels4, and vertex-wise timeseries were averaged within each parcel. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was then computed between the parcel-wise timeseries for each dyad (Figure 1C). This was repeated for each stimulus category at each timepoint. 

To observe differences in ISC across social distance levels, ISC values were z-scored across dyads within each brain region and then averaged within each level of social distance (following1,2). Additionally, to quantify regions’ sensitivity to social distance and account for similarities in behavioral covariates, we fitted a linear mixed effects model (LMEM)5 predicting ISC from social distance level and similarities in age, sex, dominant hand and academic major, treating participants as crossed random effects. Negative values of the estimate (beta) of social distance indicate the hypothesized decrease ISC with growing social distance, beyond the levels of abovementioned covariates. 

To examine how stimulus content modulates ISC sensitivity to social proximity, we identified regions showing a negative LMEM estimate for social distance, for each stimulus category, at each timepoint. 

Last, we tested whether dyads’ social proximity after a year of acquaintance could be predicted from their pre- and post-acquaintance neural similarity. Dyads’ social distances were classified based on their ISC values using a K-nearest neighbors (KNN) algorithm (Figure 1D). Model performance was evaluated using 10-fold cross-validation, calculating accuracy, balanced accuracy, F1-weighted, and F1-macro scores on the held-out test data in each fold and averaging them across folds. Statistical significance was assessed using a one-sided permutation test and the resulting p-values were FDR-corrected for two timepoints.

Results

We aimed to determine whether similarity in neural responses among friends reflects a pre-existing, stable dyadic property or emerges through social interaction. Regions were considered to show a trait-like effect if their ISC was highest among friends and decreased with increasing social distance both before and after acquaintance. Conversely, regions were considered to reflect an experience-dependent effect if neural similarity became associated with social proximity only after a year of acquaintance.

Neural sensitivity to social proximity was evident both before and after acquaintance in widespread regions, suggesting that neural similarity reflects a trait of social compatibility. This effect was mainly evident in the lateral prefrontal cortex bilaterally, particularly within the inferior and middle frontal gyri (significantly negative LMEM estimate of social distance at both timepoints; Psocial distance<0.05; Figure 2). The same effect, although weaker, was also observed in the medial frontal gyrus in both hemispheres as well as in the left tempo-parietal junction, but it didn’t reach statistical significance for both timepoints (Psocial distance>0.05). 

Interestingly, in other regions, neural sensitivity to social proximity emerged after acquaintance, suggesting it was modulated by social interaction and shared experience. This was observed in the bilateral superior parietal (right: Psocial distance<0.05; left: Psocial distance=0.051), right intra-parietal sulcus, anterior part of the left inferior parietal lobule, as well as in the right orbitofrontal cortex and associative visual regions bilaterally (Psocial distance<0.05). Moreover, we found this effect was driven by an increase in ISC following aquintance among socailly closer dyads, supporting the idea that inter-personal interaction shape neural responses to naturalistic stimuli.

To further understand the mental processes involved in the association of neural similarity and social proximity, we investigated how it is affected by different affective and cognitive demands induced by the stimulus. We hypothesized that neural similarity between friends reflects shared interpretations and emotional responses to external events. Accordingly, we expected this similarity to be most pronounced during socio-affective content designed to evoke subjective experiences and social judgments, compared to structured academic or emotionally neutral material. 

The association between neural similarity and social distance appeared across all stimulus categories, with widespread regions showing negative LMEM estimates both before and after acquaintance. Yet, as we hypothesized, the socio-affective condition showed the strongest effect, with 51 regions exhibiting negative estimates across sessions - more than twice as many as the academic (20) or neutral (24) conditions.

Interestingly, the specific regions involved differed across stimulus categories, indicating that ISC patterns and their sensitivity to social distance were modulated by stimulus content. Nonetheless, the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex was observed across all conditions, suggesting a central role in social cognition.

Last, we classified dyads’ social distance from their ISC patterns before and after acquaintance. Social distance was successfully classified based on post-acquaintance ISC (PFDR<0.05 for all evaluation metrics), replicating previous findings2. Notably, we demonstrate, for the first time, that social distance can also be predicted from ISC measured prior to acquaintance (PFDR<0.05 for all evaluation metrics). Moreover, classification performance based on pre-acquaintance ISC was not significantly inferior to classification based on post-acquaintance ISC, across evaluation metrics (P>0.05). These findings suggest that neural similarity captures dyads’ social compatibility independent of social experience and may serve as a marker of future social relationships.

Conclusion

We investigated the interaction between social proximity and neural response similarity as relationships are formed. We aimed to determine whether similarity in neural responses among friends reflects a stable, trait-like property that predicts future friendships, or rather it emerges through social experience. Our results provide evidence for both mechanisms. Socially closer dyads exhibited higher neural synchronization across widespread cortical regions even before meeting one another, and this pattern remained stable over a year of acquaintance. At the same time, in other regions, increased synchronization among friends was evident only after acquaintance, driven by friends becoming more similar in their neural responses compared to socially distant dyads. These findings suggest that in some regions, neural similarity may reflect a predisposition for forming social relationships, whereas in others it is shaped by shared experiences and repeated interactions.

We further showed that socially closer dyads exhibit greater neural similarity across stimulus categories that evoke different emotional and cognitive processes. Notably, this effect consistently engaged the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex. Yet, this effect was most extensive during the viewing of socio-affective content. These results suggest that neural similarity among closer dyads arises primarily from shared socio-affective processing, reflecting alignment in how friends perceive, interpret, and emotionally respond to socially meaningful experiences. Moreover, these findings highlight the importance of selecting stimuli that engage the psychological processes relevant to the trait under investigation. We propose that choosing stimuli that target the cognitive and affective mechanisms of interest may enhance sensitivity to inter-individual variability.

Lat, we demonstrated, for the first time, that ISC patterns measured prior to any interaction predicts future relationship status at the dyadic level. While previous studies have achieved such predictions using post-acquaintance ISC2, this is the first evidence that pre-existing neural similarity patterns are predictive of future social proximity. Remarkably, prediction accuracy was comparable to that derived from post-acquaintance ISC. These results support the idea that neural similarity encodes inherent trait of social compatibility, which is independent to exposure or experience, and may therefore serve as a neural marker of social relationships.
