Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a common neurodegenerative illness that affects over 10 million people world-wide. It is extremely variable in presentation and progression, and to date there is no cure or effective disease modifying therapy. One of the major issues is that PD is a syndrome composed of different molecular subtypes, meaning different pathophysiological mechanisms give rise to the disease.

Using a skin biopsy, it is possible to grow different types of neurons from an individual person via induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSC’s). These individual-specific in vitro models can then be used to investigate the mechanisms that drive neurodegeneration1. High content imaging (HCI) is one technique to map longitudinal changes in different intracellular features, such as mitochondrial and lysosomal characteristics. 

Here, we investigated the changes in intracellular dynamics in 38 cell lines, including healthy control (HC), sporadic Parkinson’s (SP), slow progressing familial Parkinson’s (SFP, LRRK2 ,PINK1) and fast progressing familial Parkinson’s (FFP, A53T SNCA mutations) across five timepoints, creating data for approximately 4.2M midbrain neurons. We created new deep-learning based pre-processing steps to clean this data, and then used deep learning (DL) and dynamic causal modelling (DCM)2  to stratify and help understand differences in Parkinson’s progression.

The resulting deep neural network was able to classify cells as either HC or FFP with 82% accuracy. We used this to map the relative distance of the different cohorts between these two extremes, and how this changes over time. We also use SHAP to show the key features influencing classification at each timepoint. Finally, we fitted the clean data to a DCM to simulate mechanistic function of individual neurons. We show clear difference between mitochondrial and lysosomal function in HC cells compared to FFP cells.

Methods

Skin biopsies were taken from 38 participants in the Parkison’s Progression Marker Initiative4 and converted to midbrain neurons. We followed the same culturing protocol described in D’Sa et al.1 This protocol takes 50 days to transform from fibroblasts to neurons and involves plating them in 96 or 384 well plates. The cells are then imaged weekly using an Opera Pheonix High Content Imaging, generating 1200 x 1200 x 5 image tile data. The Opera system has an automated pipeline for the detection of cell nuclei and other organelles, resulting in summary tabular data for each cell.

From the imaging we capture 42 features describing properties of the nucleus, membrane integrity, lysosomes and mitochondria, with physical properties, texture and dye intensities of the organelles. These features were normalised to healthy controls on each plate to account for plate-to-plate dye intensity variation. For labelling of Neuronal cells Map2 dyes were applied to the cells and a Sytox dye was applied to label dead cells.

The models were developed in Python 3.11 and built using Keras5, trained on Ubuntu operating system with a Nvidia laptop 3070 GPU for up to 200 epoch and early stopping with patience of eight. All models were trained with Adam optimizer, learning rate of 0.001 and categorical cross entropy loss function. Data was split into a 70/15/15, training, testing, validation split, with the test data being unseen in model training.

The DCM utilised a model structure to test the observed variables of mitochondrial intensity and lysosomal intensity against a latent variable of α-syn. This was programmed in MATLAB2024b using SPM2025. The model was first fitted to the cleaned data before parametric empirical bayes (PEB) was used to infer the most likely model structure based on HC and FFP data.

Results

The models in our cleaning pipeline show high accuracy with the neuronal classifier achieving 87.2% accuracy and the apoptosis classifier achieving 99.7% accuracy. The complete pipeline removes approximately 900K cells from the dataset. To validate this pipeline we trained a classifier to distinguish between HC and FFP cells on data with and without the cleaning pipeline showing a 12% increase in model classification accuracy. Our SHAP analysis shows that DAPI intensity is clearly the most important factor in determining if a cell is dead where-as a mixture of shape properties of the DAPI determine if it is neuronal.

B. HC vs FFP-PD Classification:

For classification between HC and FFP cells we train different classifiers of the same architecture, hyperparameters, and optimization, but make five separate models for each timepoint with no pre-training only random initialisation. The best accuracy was timepoint three with 85% accuracy with the lowest accuracy in timepoint 1 with 74% accuracy. 

Using the SHAP output the most influential features in the model change from primarily physical features in the mitochondria and lysosomes in timepoints one and three to texture properties of the mitochondria alone in timepoint five.

We then use these models to classify all cells in all lines as either HC or FFP and look at the proportion of each line classified as FFP at each timepoint. This clearly show SP cells being close to healthy control at timepoint one but becoming significantly closer to FFP cells by week five. 

C. Cellular Dynamic Causal Modelling:

For this work, we used a basic, fully connected, three compartment model consisting of two observed parameters (mitochondria and lysosomes) and one latent (alpha synuclein aggregation). Using parametric empirical bayes (PEB), we to show fast-progressing Parkinson’s due to SNCA A53T mutation was associated with impaired mitochondrial energy supply to the lysosomes and impaired α-synuclein clearance by the lysosomes while maintaining the toxic effect of α-synuclein on the other organelles when comparing HC cells to FFP. 

Conclusion

The objective for this work was to start to create a methodological framework to interrogate and understand changes in cellular dynamics in Parkinson’s disease, and other forms of neurodegeneration. This type of data poses some unique challenges. We present a novel pre-processing pipeline to help improve data quality for analysis. We also utilise these models to inform us of what organelle features are important in finding cells that have either not turned into neurons or are dead. In the case of dead neurons, it is clear that cell death is shown by the intensity of the DAPI dye, used to highlight cell nuclei.

Our classifier between HC and FFP becomes more accurate through each week, suggesting that PD cells become more distinct over time. It is also important to consider that not every cell from PD donors will develop PD pathology and the models results some HC cells display PD pathology likely due to the features identified in the SHAP. Using this model to stratify all of the lines and subtypes we show in timepoint one sporadic cases being close to control whereas in timepoint five they are closer to FFP lines. We believe we have shown progression in a dish in sporadic cases tending toward being PD cases at later timepoints. 

Finally, our cellular DCM shows clear mechanistic differences between HC and FFP cultures with large changes in model structure after utilising PEB. This model shows that α-syns toxic effects stunt both mitochondrial and lysosomal function in midbrain neurons. We plan to further develop this generative model to include more organelle compartments and integrate additional measures that were not available in this first experiment, such as changes in the volume, size and distribution of alpha-synuclein aggregates. Combined, our aim is to deliver a comprehensive ecosystem of tools to help disentangle the individual mechanisms that underpin disease heterogeneity, to help support the discovery and delivery of precision disease modifying therapy.
