Introduction

Introduction

Functional brain representations suffer from substantial idiosyncratic variability across individuals, which profoundly limits effective cross-participant comparison based on anatomical alignment alone. The Hyperalignment framework offers a powerful solution by computing transformations that project fine-grained functional patterns into a shared, high-dimensional common representation space [1]. Despite its efficacy, the widespread practical application is hindered by significant methodological ambiguity, specifically concerning the optimal choice among diverse alignment algorithms and the unclear requirements for the sample size needed to construct the common space reliably [2].

This study addresses these critical methodological gaps to provide essential guidance for the field. Our objectives were twofold: (1) to systematically compare the functional alignment performance of common spaces derived from different hyperalignment algorithms (based on response vs. connectivity) and aggregation strategies; and (2) to rigorously quantify how variations in the training sample size (both number of subjects and stimulus data length) influence the resulting alignment quality.

Methods

Methods

We utilized two publicly available movie-watching fMRI datasets: the Forrest dataset (15 participants) for comparing algorithms, and a subset of the HCP S1200 dataset (175 participants) for analyzing sample-size effects [3,4]. We evaluated alignment based on response hyperalignment (RHA) and connectivity hyperalignment (CHA) (tested across low, mid, and high-resolution sampling). Each method was combined with two common-space algorithms (Procrustes rotation, PR; principal component analysis, PCA) and two aggregation strategies (con vs. ucon), yielding 16 distinct common-space models. Performance was assessed using four metrics: inter-subject correlation (ISC) of response, connectivity, and representational geometry, and between-subject multivariate pattern classification (bsMVPC) accuracy. The Sample-Size Analysis (HCP) focused on the RHA-PR template, testing alignment performance across varying numbers of training subjects (up to 150) and increasing amounts of stimulus data (up to three movie runs) (Fig .1).

Results

Results

Across the 16 tested models, the RHA-PR-ucon model demonstrated superior performance, achieving the highest ISC of response and representational geometry, alongside the best bsMVPC accuracy, establishing it as the strongest alignment model for these critical benchmarks (Fig .2A). Notably, initial whole-brain screening showed that the CHA-low model (connectivity with low-resolution sampling) generally outperformed other CHA variants, confirming that sparse connectivity data are sufficient. Furthermore, alignment improvements were pervasive, observed consistently across all seven canonical functional networks (Fig .2B and Fig. 2C). This indicates that the benefits of hyperalignment are not localized but yield widespread functional registration enhancement across the entire cortex (Fig .2D).

We next examined the influence of training sample size. Increasing the amount of stimulus data used for common space construction (from one movie run to three) consistently improved alignment performance across all evaluated metrics. Crucially, as the number of training subjects increased, performance rose sharply but distinctly plateaued at approximately 50 subjects across all movie lengths (Fig .2E). This robust finding suggests clear diminishing marginal gains for hyperalignment beyond this moderate sample size.

Conclusion

Conclusion

Our comprehensive comparative evaluation provides crucial methodological recommendations for functional alignment studies. We identify the RHA-PR-ucon model as the optimal approach for achieving the strongest functional alignment across key response and decoding benchmarks, and confirm that low-resolution connectivity is adequate for CHA. Most significantly, we quantitatively demonstrate that the substantial benefits of hyperalignment are achieved widespread across the cortex, and that near-optimal alignment performance can be reached with a moderate training sample size of approximately 50 subjects, with gains sharply diminishing thereafter. These findings offer practical, evidence-based guidelines for optimizing resource allocation and enhancing the reliability of future hyperalignment applications.
