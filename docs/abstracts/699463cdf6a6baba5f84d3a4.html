Introduction

Fragile X syndrome (FrX) is a monogenic neurodevelopmental disorder showing widespread but regionally specific abnormalities of cortical morphology. Previous structural MRI studies have reported enlarged brain volumes and increased cortical thickness in FrX, while histological and molecular work has highlighted aberrant neuron morphology, with a potential relationship between the two found in aberrant synaptic pruning. MRI primarily captures macroscopic alterations in cortical structure, often prohibiting direct inferences on underlying cellular or molecular processes. Spatial correlation or "colocalization" analyses between MRI‑derived effect maps and independent molecular or transcriptomic reference maps offer a way to relate such macroscale alterations to underlying neurobiology. Here, we combine cortical thickness data from individuals with FrX and healthy controls with in vivo PET receptor maps and transcriptomic profiles of synaptic processes to investigate the potential pathobiology underlying brain-morphological alterations in FrX.

Methods

We analyze FreeSurfer cortical thickness estimates from a clinical FrX sample (FrX: n = 12; healthy controls (HC): n = 16)1,2. The small size of the sample renders this study exploratory in nature; however, we will employ a flexible analysis method to show that effects can be observed even in individual subjects. First, we perform classical parcel‑wise group comparisons (t-tests: FrX vs HC) on 168 parcels defined by the Destrieux parcellation. We then perform three spatial colocalization analyses using the NiSpace toolbox3 (https://github.com/leondlotter/nispace): (1) We retrieve parcellated nuclear imaging atlases for a panel of neurotransmitter receptors, transporters, and general molecular markers such as synaptic density and transcriptomic activity. We then compute Spearman correlations between a parcel-wise FrX-vs-HC effect size map and each PET map, with significance assessed by permutation of group labels. (2) We repeat this analysis but perform it for each individual FrX subject after standardizing their parcel-wise data on the HC mean and standard deviation4. The analysis tests if each individual-level FrX "deviation" map is associated to the nuclear imaging atlases, while significance is assessed for the mean spatial colocalization across the FrX group. As it captures individual variation, this method often proves more sensitive to smaller effects. (3) We follow up on the findings from step 1 and 2 by calculating a gene-set enrichment analysis testing if the FrX effect size map is associated to healthy adult gene expression of genes involved in specific synaptic functions (https://www.syngoportal.org/). Here, significance is evaluated using a spatial null model of the effect size map as recommended by Fulcher at al.5.

Results

Cortical thickness was generally increased in FrX relative to controls, with widely distributed peak effects in temporo-parietal, occipital and dorsal frontal regions (Fig. 1a). The FrX effect size pattern showed strong positive spatial colocalizations with serotonin 5HT6 and 5HT2a as well as the cholinergic M1 receptor distributions (Fig. 1b). Additionally, we found a positive association to the synaptic vesicle protein SV2A, marking general synaptic density. Findings showed tight correspondence between analyses 1 and 2, while the individual-level analysis (2), as expected, showed higher power to detect smaller yet robust effects. Specifically, the positive direction of the spatial colocalization was confirmed across subjects only for serotonin 5HT6 and SV2A distributions (Fig. 1b, right). The gene‑set enrichment analysis (3) indicated several adjusted significant synaptic gene sets, with an apparent dominance of sets associated to postsynaptic signaling and structure (Fig. 1c, note the null-standardized y axis to facilitate comparison between gene sets).

Conclusion

Cortical thickness alterations in FrX preferentially affect regions with high serotonergic receptor and synaptic marker density, aligning with transcriptomic signatures of postsynaptic signal transmission and structural synaptic architecture. These findings are consistent with models of a fundamental synaptic dysfunction in FrX and illustrate how spatial correlation analyses can link MRI‑based case–control differences to underlying molecular and synaptic features.
