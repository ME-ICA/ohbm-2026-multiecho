Introduction

White matter hyperintensities of presumed vascular origin (WMH) are key markers of cerebral small vessel disease. Very common in old age, they are usually unnoticed but nevertheless associated with an increased risk of stroke and cognitive decline1.
 While most studies focus on total WMH volume, the spatial location of WMH has been shown to correlate with different risk factors (e.g. vascular risk/amyloid pathology)2 and might relate to different outcomes (ischemic vs. hemorraghic stroke)3. 

Dimension reduction techniques like principal component analysis (PCA) allow to extract the main directions of spatial variation of WMH across a large number of subjects, thereby providing a way to define WMH subtypes that capture shared mechanisms. In the context of WMH, a major challenge is the definition of these spatial patterns to be consistent across cohorts with varying image acquisition, preprocessing parameters, and population characteristics.

Here, we investigated the consistency of WMH spatial patterns based on a Bullseye based parcellation of WMH in MNI standard space and PCA in three large community-based cohorts of middle-aged to older adults.

Methods

We characterized the spatial co-occurrence patterns of WMH in three large community-based cohorts of healthy middle-aged and older adults: a subsample of the UKB (N=4737, mean age= 63 y, range 46 – 81y, 2487 (52%) females), LIFE (N=1156, mean age=70 y, range 65 – 82 y, 518 (44%) females), and 3C (Dijon)(N=1659, mean age= 72 y, range 65 -85 y, 1005 (60%) females). The cohorts varied in MRI acquisition and WMH segmentation parameters. All individual-level WMH maps were transformed to MNI space. We quantified the spatial distribution of the MNI-transformed WMH maps from these cohorts by first creating a Bullseye white matter parcellation4 in MNI space by running the volumetric segmentation with FreeSurfer v. 7.4.1 on the MNI 1mm template and segmenting the white matter into frontal, temporal, parietal and occipital lobe for each hemisphere, and a bilateral basal ganglia mask. Then, a distance map from ventricle to cortex was calculated and separated into four equidistant shells. The final Bullseye parcellation was created as the intersection of the nine regions and four shells, yielding 36 regions (openly available here: https://gitlab.gwdg.de/frauke.beyer/ukbb_mni/). We extracted the regional volume of WMH in each subject of each cohort.
We then performed PCA on the regional volume data to extract spatial WMH patterns separately for each cohort, and for combination of cohorts. We used parallel analysis which compares the eigenvalues from the data to those of a random dataset of identical sample size to determine the optimal number of components5.
 We evaluated the agreement of the spatial patterns from individual and combined cohorts by calculating the correlation of the component loadings across the 36 regions for 1. the comparison of UKB-only PCA, LIFE-only PCA and UKB-LIFE combined PCA; 2. the comparison of UKB-only PCA, LIFE-only PCA and UKB-LIFE-3C-PCA.
 Additionally, we performed 10-fold cross-validation on the UKB-LIFE combined PCA to check its stability. We evaluated the optimal number of components for each training data set and the average correlation of component loadings between different folds.
 We also tested the importance of  total WMH volume and performed a median split based on total WMH volume across UKB and LIFE. We compared the components from the PCA of the lower and upper split samples using correlation of component loadings.

Results

The median total WMH volume in UKB/LIFE/3C was 2866/1570/5315 mm³, respectively. The distribution of regional volumes was similar between cohorts, but in 3C there were comparably more participants with WMH in deep WM regions.
We found four to be the optimal number of components for the UKB and LIFE sample separately, and combined, while for 3C seven was the optimal number, estimated by parallel analysis.
In Analysis 1, we therefore compared the similarity of the four component loadings derived from UKB-only and LIFE-only PCA. We found that the four components derived from the separate datasets were similar (min r>0.75) to each other, and to the components derived from the UKB -LIFE combined PCA (min r>0.75).
Component 1 (explaining 21% of variance) was characterised by parietal and occipital WMH, component 2 (explaining 30% of variance) showed frontal and temporal WMH. Component 3 (explaining 31% of variance) was focused on periventricular frontal and basal ganglia WMH and component 4 (explaining 18% of variance) by occipital and temporal WMH.
The PCA in the combined sample of UKB and LIFE was stable according to cross-validation analysis: the optimal number of components was 4 in all runs, and the average correlation of component loadings across all fold combinations was 0.99.
In the median split samples the resulting components were similar (min r>0.75) but in the lower split sample the frontal and temporal WMH component explained most variance (TC1/2/3/4: 65/13/10/12 %), while in the higher split, explained variance was more equally distributed (26/28/26/19 %).
When performing the PCA on all cohorts combined in analysis 2, the optimal number of components was still four, but the similarity of the UKB-LIFE-3C combined PCA to the components of UKB-only and LIFE-only PCA decreased (max r=0.65) and components could not be unambiguously assigned.

Conclusion

Using a newly derived Bullseye segmentation in MNI space, we generated spatial patterns of WMH which were consistent across cohorts. In LIFE and UKB, all four patterns were highly correlated and could also be reproduced in samples with relatively lower and higher total WMH volume. When comparing to 3C, a cohort scanned on a 1.5 Tesla scanner without FLAIR, the  patterns were less reproducible. Possibly the detection of more deep WMH (both true positives due to higher age and false positives due to differences in MRI acquisition and processing) led to more and different components estimated in 3C.
The four patterns agree with the literature and reflect previously published separations into anterior vs. posterior and periventricular vs deep WMH. Yet, the asymmetry of component 4 and the consistency of the components across different age ranges warrants more investigation.
Taken together, this 4 component decomposition could be useful to generate robust phenotypes of WMH spatial distribution for future studies.
