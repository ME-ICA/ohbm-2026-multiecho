Introduction

Hyperalignment (HA) is a functional alignment technique that maps individual brain activity patterns into a common, high-dimensional representational space, enabling more precise cross-subject comparisons than traditional anatomical alignment. Prior work in humans has demonstrated that HA1,2 enhances inter-subject decoding accuracy, uncovers shared representational structures across individuals, and improves the generalizability of neural models, especially in naturalistic paradigms such as movie viewing. Foundational work has established the functional topography and retinotopic organization of the monkey visual cortex3, however, robust functional alignment techniques are still underdeveloped in non-human primate (NHP). NHP studies are critical for advancing neuroscience, although to a lesser degree than in humans, analyses across individuals are limited by anatomical and functional variability. In this study, we applied HA to fMRI data from 12 rhesus monkeys while they watched the movie Monkey Kingdom. We built a template using the first half of the movie data from all 12 monkeys, and computed individualized transformation matrices via ridge regression. These matrices were then applied to the second half of the movie data for validation. HA significantly improved inter-subject correlation, particularly in the occipital, temporal, and lateral prefrontal cortices. Moreover, between-subject classification accuracy for decoding movie time points increased by ~80% compared to anatomical alignment. Lastly, HA enabled us to estimate idiosyncratic functional topographies using other monkeys’ movie and localizer data. These results demonstrate that HA enables robust cross-monkey comparisons of fine-grained idiosyncratic neural representations and reveals high specificity in population responses to naturalistic stimuli. As a comparative validation, we also applied the same analysis to human data from 24 participants watching the same movie and observed consistent results. 

Methods

Datasets & Preprocessing

12 monkeys were scanned at the KU Leuven using a 3T Siemens PRISMA scanner. 8 monkeys were scanned at a spatial resolution of 1.25*1.25*1.25mm³ with TR = 1s, while 4 monkeys were scanned at a spatial resolution of 0.8*0.8*0.8mm³ with TR = 2s. To enhance contrast-to-noise ratio, monkeys were administered 8-11 mg/kg monocrystalline iron oxide nanoparticles (MION, BioPAL) immediately prior to scanning. 24 humans were scanned at Dartmouth using a 3T Siemens scanner equipped with a 32-channel phased-array head coil. Functional BOLD images were acquired at a spatial resolution of 2.5*2.5*2.5mm³ with a volume size of 96*96*52 and TR = 1s. All subjects watched the movie Monkey Kingdom in five continuous clips (900s per clip; monkey data were downsampled to 450s). Monkeys viewed the movie an average of eight times in the scanner, whereas human participants watched it once outside the scanner in English and once inside the scanner in Spanish. Face- and body-selective localizer data were also collected for 4 of the 12 monkeys. Monkey fMRI data were preprocessed using in-house scripts (skull stripping, slice-timing correction, and motion correction) and mapped to mkavg-ico32 cortical surface template. Human fMRI data were preprocessed using fMRIPrep 20.2.7 and resampled to the onavg-ico32 cortical surface template4.

Response Hyperalignment (RHA)

We used RHA to model shared information by projecting individual brain responses into a common model space using movie data. Whole-brain templates for both monkeys and humans were built from the first 3 movie clips using a searchlight-based Procrustes algorithm5. There were 19,863 overlapping monkey searchlights (mean size: 167 vertices) and 19,341 human searchlights (mean size: 121 vertices). For each subject, a transformation matrix was estimated with ridge regression using the same three clips and applied to the 2 held-out clips to project data into the common space. To evaluate HA performance, we performed three analyses on anatomically aligned and hyperaligned data: inter-subject correlation (ISC) of response time-series, movie timepoint prediction, and functional map prediction.

Inter-subject Correlation (ISC)

For each vertex, we computed the Pearson correlation between the response time series of each subject and the average of the other subjects, where higher ISC indicates better performance. We then compared the ISC before and after HA.

Movie timepoint prediction

We used a leave-one-subject-out cross-validation scheme. In each fold, we first performed whole-brain prediction using a two-step procedure: (1) we applied Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to reduce dimensionality in the training subjects, and (2) we predicted each timepoint in the left-out subject as the nearest-neighbor mean response pattern in the training subjects. Across all training subjects, we identified the number of principal components that yielded the highest classification accuracy, and then used these components in the left-out test subject for prediction. To determine which brain regions contributed most to prediction performance, we also ran searchlight-based classification. Using the same searchlight definitions as in RHA, we classified each timepoint in each searchlight within each subject and generated whole-brain accuracy maps by averaging across subjects.

Functional map prediction

For each of the 4 monkeys with visual category localizer data, we computed pairwise HA transforms from each of the other 3 monkeys to the target monkey using all 5 movie clips. We then transformed the localizer data of the 3 source monkeys to generate predicted maps for the left-out monkey and averaged these maps. Prediction accuracy was quantified as the correlation between each monkey’s measured and predicted maps. To assess individual specificity, we compared this congruent correlation with the mean correlation between a monkey’s measured map and the predicted maps from the other monkeys (incongruent).

Results

Across all analysis, hyperalignment (HA) consistently enhanced cross-subject comparability of neural responses in both monkeys and humans.

HA significantly increases shared responses across both monkeys and humans

We first examined whether HA increased the similarity of movie-evoked responses across individuals. Group-average ISC was substantially higher after HA, with the largest gains in occipital, temporal, and lateral prefrontal cortices (Fig. 1a, 1b). Individual ISC values showed the same pattern. In monkeys, mean ISC nearly doubled from 0.253 to 0.496 (Fig. 1c). A comparable effect was seen in humans, where the mean ISC increased from 0.067 to 0.126 (Fig. 1d).

HA greatly improves between-subject movie time-point decoding

We then performed between-subject time-point classification using both whole-brain and searchlight patterns. In monkeys, HA increased the accuracy of whole-brain, between-subject classification of movie timepoints by roughly 80% (from 0.240 to 0.430; Fig. 1g). Chance accuracy was 1/900 (< 0.0012). Searchlight analyses showed even larger gains, with mean whole-brain accuracy increasing more than 4 times (from 0.005 to 0.028), and even greater improvements in IT cortex (Fig. 1e). These high levels of decoding accuracy after HA indicate that fine-grained response patterns in the monkey cortex to a naturalistic movie encode shared representations of fine distinctions.  Human data showed the same qualitative pattern: whole-brain accuracy rose from 0.041 to 0.145 after HA (Fig. 1f), while mean whole-brain searchlight accuracy increased from 0.001 to 0.003.  Chance accuracy was 1/1800 (<0.0006).

HA enables accurate prediction of individual idiosyncratic functional topographic maps

Finally, we tested whether HA could be used to derive individualized functional topographies from other monkeys’ data. For each monkey, we calculated t-score maps of  the face-versus-object and body-versus-object functional topographies. We then predicted each monkey’s t-map by projecting localizer data of other monkeys into the left-out monkey’s native cortical space using pairwise HA.

Different monkeys showed distinct, idiosyncratic topographies during the category-selective tests (Fig. 1i, left). The predicted maps recovered these subject-specific features, including face-patches such as ML/MF and AM (Fig. 1i, right). Quantitatively, congruent predictions—correlations between each monkey’s measured and predicted map—were consistently higher than incongruent predictions, defined as the mean correlation between a monkey’s measured map and the maps predicted for other monkeys, for both localizers and across all monkeys (Fig. 1j; face-versus-object shown for illustration). Together, these results show that HA enables accurate cross-subject estimation of category-selective topographies.

Conclusion

These findings show that hyperalignment (HA) provides an effective framework for aligning fine-grained neural representations across individual monkeys and humans during naturalistic viewing. It substantially increased inter-subject correlation, particularly in occipital, temporal, and lateral prefrontal cortices, and improved between-subject timepoint decoding by enhancing shared response structure. Pairwise HA also enabled the prediction of subject-specific functional topographies from other individuals’ data, indicating that it captures common representational structure and models idiosyncrasies of functional topography in non-human primates.
