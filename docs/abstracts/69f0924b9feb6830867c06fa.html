Introduction

Analytical flexibility poses a major challenge for the reproducibility of neuroimaging results. Different yet defensible preprocessing and analysis pipelines can yield divergent conclusions about brain-behaviour associations¹, creating uncertainty about the robustness of reported effects to defensible analytical variation. Multiverse analysis offers a solution by computing all defensible pipelines and reporting the resulting distribution of outcomes, thereby reporting analytical uncertainty². However, the large number of decision points and defensible combinations of options involved in processing, quantifying and analysing neuroimaging data can produce multiverses so large that exhaustive computation is infeasible3. When this occurs, the typical approach is to sample pipelines from the multiverse and assume that the sample represents the full analytical space. However, different sampling strategies applied across the literature, including random, stratified, and active learning sampling, differ in how they navigate high-dimensional structures and prioritise elements of the space. Random sampling emphasises uniform chance, stratified sampling prioritised balanced coverage of decision nodes, and active learning prioritises informative regions of the space. Consequently, different sampling methods may yield different robustness conclusions, introducing an additional and largely overlooked source of uncertainty, which we term ‘multiverse sampling uncertainty’4. If unreported, this uncertainty threatens the transparency that multiverse analysis aims to provide and undermines the interpretability of multiverse analyses in neuroimaging. We developed an open-source Python tool that compares the aforementioned sampling strategies in terms of representativeness of the full multiverse. Using an EEG multiverse as a use case, we provide the first systematic benchmark of the representativeness of sampling methods for large-scale neuroimaging multiverse analyses. 

Methods

In our multiverse use case, we examined the extent to which Late Positive Potential (LPP) amplitudes elicited by emotional facial expressions predicted individual differences in extraversion across different defensible LPP quantifications. The LPP is theoretically and empirically motivated: it is sensitive to emotional salience and plays a central role in personality theories that posit heightened LPP reactivity to emotionally arousing stimuli in more extraverted individuals, yet prior work has reported inconsistent associations. Raw EEG and questionnaire data were used in which 98 adults completed a dynamic facial expression emotion-classification task across six emotion conditions (anger, disgust, fear, happiness, sadness, and surprise) and provided self-reported extraversion scores. EEG was recorded from 42 channels at 1000 Hz (online bandpass 0.032–70 Hz), with AFz as the ground and a left mastoid reference; impedances were <5 kΩ. Participants viewed 798 trials (114 per emotion condition including two intensity levels), each consisting a 700 ms fixation and a 600 ms dynamic facial expression clip (200 ms movement, 400 ms static), followed by a self-paced response. Preprocessing consisted of resampling to 500 Hz, re-referencing to Cz, 1 Hz high-pass and 30 Hz low pass filtering, identifying and removing bad channels based on sustained channel-level deviations in standard deviation maps, ICA-based ocular artefact removal using an ICLabel probability threshold of 0.85, and spherical spline interpolation of rejected channels. Data were epoched from -200 to 800 ms around stimulus onset. Data quality checks that the workflow retained signal while effectively removing noise. 

To quantify the LPP, we defined a 528-pipeline multiverse using the Cartesian product of defensible alternative options across four decision nodes: two baseline durations, three reference schemes, 11 temporal windows, and eight electrode clusters (Figure 1). This multiverse size reflects realistic analytical flexibility in LPP quantification while remaining fully computable for benchmarking sample representativeness. For each resulting dataset, mean LPP amplitudes across emotion conditions were extracted and entered into a linear regression predicting extraversion. Model fit was quantified using R2, providing a continuous index of how LPP quantification choices influence the strength of the association. 

We evaluated the representativeness of three samples obtained using approaches that have previously been applied in neuroimaging multiverse analyses (random, stratified, and active learning). For active learning sampling5, we computed pairwise Euclidean distances between the LPP condition vectors of each pipeline for a subset of 20 participants, and projected these distances into two dimensions using t-distributed stochastic neighbour embedding. A Gaussian Process (GP) model was fitted to this space, and an upper confidence bound acquisition function iteratively selected pipelines predicted to improve coverage. For pipelines not directly sampled, the GP model predicted R2 values, estimating the full multiverse. We directly sampled 26 pipelines (5% of the full multiverse) for the main analyses. 

Representativeness was assessed by comparing each sampling method to the full multiverse computed on the test set (N = 50) and a held-out participant test subset (N = 28). We examined (i) alignment of R2 distributions via median comparison and Kolmogorov-Smirnov distances; (ii) selection of pipelines with the highest R2 values; (iii) spatial coverage in the low-dimensional embedding, including average nearest-neighbour distance; and (iv) specification-curve correspondence between sampled and full multiverses. Replicability was assessed using cross-validation, and reproducibility across pipeline sample sizes was examined. All procedures were implemented in our open-source Python tool. 

Results

The full 528-pipeline multiverse exhibited substantial variability in model fit (R2 range = 0.02 to 0.3; median 0.084), confirming that defensible LPP quantification choices can influence the estimated strength of the LPP-extraversion association. In terms of distributional alignment, stratified sampling most closely represented the median R2 of the full multiverse in the primary test subset, whereas active learning showed the closest representation in the smaller lock-box subset. Kolmogorov-Smirnov distances indicated that active learning best approximated the overall R2 distribution when only 5% of pipelines were directly sampled (KS = 0.104, p < .01), outperforming random (KS = 0.710) and stratified sampling (KS = 0.856), likely reflecting its ability to estimate unsampled regions of the analytical space. At larger sampling proportions (10% and 15%), stratified sampling provided the closest match to the full-multiverse across both participant subsets. Both stratified sampling and active learning represented the full range of multiverse variability more closely than random sampling. Active learning most accurately recovered the upper tail of the R2 distribution, consistent with its prioritisation of informative regions of the analytical space. Stratified sampling provided the most accurate representation of the interquartile range at moderate and larger sampling proportions. 

Only stratified and active learning samples selected any of the top ten pipelines that yielded the highest R2 values at the smallest sampling proportion (5%), selecting two and three pipelines, respectively. Random sampling did not capture any of the highest R2 pipelines at a sample size of 5% but did so at larger sample sizes, illustrating its vulnerability when the feasible sample size is a small proportion of the full multiverse. 

All three approaches sampled pipelines distributed across the low-dimensional embedded analytical space. Active learning achieved the greatest local representativeness, reflected by the smallest average nearest-neighbour distance (0.422), relative to random (0.442) and stratified (0.517) approaches, indicating better coverage of more densely clustered regions. Stratified sampling selected from more sparsely populated regions, producing larger local distances but broad coverage. These patterns were reproduced at larger sampling proportions. 

Specification curve correspondence (Figure 2) revealed systematic variation in R2 across temporal windows, reference schemes, and electrode clusters in the full multiverse. Active learning most clearly communicated these trends in the specification curve, whereas stratified sampling captured the general structure but with less resolution. Random sampling showed incomplete representation of these trends at small sample proportions but represented the trends at larger sample proportions. The relative ranking of sampling methods was stable across test and lock-box subsets, indicating that differences in representativeness were not artefacts of overfitting. Reproducibility across larger sampling proportions confirmed the main pattern: active learning excelled at small sample sizes, whereas stratified sampling became increasingly competitive as more pipelines could be sampled. 

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that sampling from a large neuroimaging multiverse introduces a previously unreported source of variability, multiverse sampling uncertainty, which can influence conclusions about the robustness of brain-behaviour associations. Even when all sampled pipelines are defensible, different sampling strategies select different portions of the analytical landscape, yield different estimates of model fit, and vary in their ability to detect influential preprocessing configurations. This underscores that sampling decisions are not analytically neutral and must be treated as an additional source of uncertainty when exhaustive computation is infeasible. Across all evaluations, stratified and active learning sampling outperformed random sampling, but in complementary ways. Active learning most effectively represented the overall multiverse structure when only a small proportion of pipelines could be sampled. Stratified sampling became increasingly representative as sample size grew, providing accurate estimates of central tendency and distributional shape. 

By providing an open-source Python tool that systematically evaluates sampling approaches, quantifies sampling uncertainty, and supports transparent reporting, we aim to improve the methodological rigour of sampled multiverse analyses in neuroimaging. The tool enables researchers to justify sampling decisions, apply data-driven parameter tuning of the sampling approaches, and explicitly report the uncertainty introduced by sampling, even when full computation is infeasible by reporting variability across samples. This facilitates more interpretable, reproducible, and transparent inferences about brain-behaviour relationships, particularly in modalities such as EEG where analytic flexibility is substantial and full multiverse computation is rarely feasible.

Together, these results emphasise that robust neuroimaging inference requires not only evaluating analytical variability, but also making multiverse sampling uncertainty transparent. Doing so can strengthen the credibility of robustness claims and advance best practices for large-scale neuroimaging multiverse analyses.
