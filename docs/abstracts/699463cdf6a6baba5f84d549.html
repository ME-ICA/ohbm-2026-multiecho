Introduction

The general factor of human cognitive ability (g) represents the strongest single predictor of societally relevant outcomes and evinces substantial heritability as well as a wide array of biological correlates.2 Moreover, structural and functional imaging evidence from multiple branches of modern neuroscience converges on the existence of a domain-general frontoparietal brain network associated with complex cognitive abilities such as reasoning and problem solving,.1,2 Although several components of this network have been directly linked to g, one of its core regions has received comparatively little attention: the insular cortex. The anterior insula (AI), a major hub region, has been identified as an important functional substrate of working memory – a cognitive function that itself correlates strongly with g.1

The triple network model of cognitive control (TNM)4 accounts for the AI’s broad involvement in cognitive processing by proposing a switching mechanism within the salience network (SN), through which activation of the central executive network (CEN) is facilitated while the default mode network (DMN) is concomitantly suppressed. Consistent with this role, the AI contains von Economo neurons – a cell type implicated in the rapid transmission of information over vast distances – which may support g-related neuronal processes influencing network-level interactions.1

Despite the plausibility of this conjecture, the insula’s specific contribution to g – particularly in the context of task-evoked processing demands – remains understudied. The present study aims to address this gap by conducting a fine-grained analysis of the functional connectivity (FC) between the insula and the cortical networks implicated in the TNM and by evaluating its relevance for g.

Methods

We performed a secondary analysis of the Human Connectome Project Young Adult dataset (HCP) using pre-processed task-fMRI derived FC matrices from Ooi and colleagues.5 The sample consisted of 753 adults (age range 22-37 yrs), selected based on data completeness and fMRI quality. All acquisition parameters, recruitment procedures, and preprocessing pipelines follow the HCP protocols and analysis regimen described in Ooi et al. (2022). 

Cognitive variables. g was estimated using a bifactor confirmatory factor model based on NIH Toolbox cognitive tests and Penn CNB measures. The bifactor model decomposes variance attributable to g and domain-specific variance while correcting for measurement error. 

FC variables. We used task-based FC from the N-back working-memory task (0- and 2-back). Insula subdivisions were defined using a three-step procedure: (i) name-based parcel selection from the Schaefer atlas (400 ROIs); (ii) proximity-based assignment to ventral AI, dorsal AI, and posterior insula (vAI, dAI, PI) using Euclidean distance to previously reported centroids within a 13 mm radius; and (iii) anatomical cross-checking against the Desikan atlas, yielding 21 insula parcels. TNM target networks were defined as Yeo-17 ControlA (CEN) and DefaultA (DMN). All insula-to-cortex FC (Pearson r) were considered (bilaterally); insula-insula FC were omitted. We included all insula-to-cortex edges and excluded insula-insula edges. As a first prefilter, we removed the 40% of edges with the lowest between-person standard deviation (SD) in Fisher-z space.

Predictive modelling. Behavioural and FC data were combined and split family-wise into training (N = 602) and validation sets (N = 151), ensuring independence by preventing both double dipping and family leakage. The bifactor measurement model (Latent Variable Analysis in R [lavaan], Multiple Linear Regression [MLR]) was fit in the training set and latent g scores were obtained. Linear predictive modelling was carried out separately for insula connections to CEN and DMN, restricted to insula-to-cortex edges that survived the SD prefilter. Within each network, we applied a two-step training-set screen: (1) univariate selection of edges with |r (g, FC) | ≥ .10; (2) structural equation modelling (SEM) in which g was regressed on the retained edges, followed by elastic-net regularised SEM (regSEM; regsem package, α = .50, BIC-based).3 Each insula-to-network SEM was bootstrapped 100 times in the training set. For each bootstrap, we refit the unpenalised SEM and ran cv_regsem; for every edge we recorded the proportion of successful bootstraps with a non-zero regression weight. Edges with non-zero paths in ≥75% were retained yielding a final set of FC edges most relevant for g. FC predictors were z-standardised using training-set means and SDs.

Hypothesis testing. H1 (network enrichment): using the pooled insula-CEN/DMN edges after SD prefiltering, we tested whether bootstrap-stable AI-CEN and AI-DMN edges were over-represented relative to their base rates (hypergeometric / Fisher’s exact tests, BH-FDR). PI-CEN and PI-DMN comparisons were computed analogously. 

H2 (sign consistency, training set): for each AI-CEN/AI-DMN edge which were stable across the bootstrap samples we quantified (i) the prevalence of the TNM-consistent FC sign (AI-CEN positive, AI-DMN negative) via one-sided binomial tests against 0.5, and (ii) whether mean FC differed from zero in the expected direction using one-sided t-tests (or Wilcoxon tests when non-normal). 

H3 (behavioural relevance, validation set): we fitted an unregularised SEM including the bifactor measurement model and all FC edges to obtain latent g scores (Full Information Maximum Likelihood [FIML]). For AI-CEN/AI-DMN edges meeting full H2 criteria (sign prevalence >0.5 and significant mean in the expected direction), we tested one-sided Spearman correlations between FC and g in the TNM-consistent direction (AI-CEN positive, AI-DMN negative).

Results

After SD-based prefiltering, 47,880 edges remained across the connectome. Within this set, 1,797 insula-cortex edges were identified, of which 172 insula-DMN and 162 insula-CEN edges served as candidates for predictive modelling. In the training set, 35 CEN edges and 46 DMN edges met the |r (g, FC) |≥.10 screen. Bootstrap-enhanced regSEM (100 iterations per network) identified 10 AI-CEN, 6 AI-DMN and 2 PI-DMN edges with non-zero regression weights in ≥75% of successful bootstraps, yielding 18 g-relevant edges.

H1 (network enrichment). AI-CEN and AI-DMN edges were strongly over-represented among the selected edges relative to their base rates within the insula-network connections. Enrichment was substantial for AI-CEN (7.29×, BH-FDR p < 0.001 and also significant for AI-DMN (4.61×, p = .001). No enrichment was observed for PI connections with CEN (0 edges selected; p = 1). PI-DMN showed only a nonsignificant trend (enrichment 4.75×, p = .13). These results support a selective role of the AI, but not the PI, in g-related TNM interactions.

H2 (sign consistency). Across training subjects, bootstrap-stable AI-CEN edges showed strong positive FC: median sign prevalence = .835, median Fisher-z mean = .115, and large effect sizes for both prevalence (median Cohen’s h = .736) and mean FC (median effect size = .690). AI-DMN edges showed consistent negative FC, though with smaller magnitudes (median prevalence = .688; median mean = −.059; h = .388; effect size = −.430).

H3 (behavioural relevance in validation). Validation-set SEM yielded latent g estimates used for edgewise tests. Eleven AI edges met full H2 criteria and were evaluated. TNM-consistent Spearman correlations were robust for multiple AI-CEN edges (e.g., ρ = .455, .442, .360; BH-FDR p’s < 10⁻⁴) and for several AI-DMN edges (e.g., −.398, −.271; BH-FDR p’s < .001). Group-level tests confirmed that AI-CEN correlations were reliably positive across edges (median ρₜₙₘ = .355; p = .0009), whereas AI-DMN edges showed a positive average TNM-recoded effect that approached significance (median ρₜₙₘ = .183; p = .06). The largest part of the AI-CEN edges included control-regions in the parietal lobe, particularly in the intraparietal sulcus (IPS), with little involvement of frontal parcels. The results indicate that both AI-CEN coupling and AI-DMN anticorrelation predict g, with the former showing the strongest and most consistent effects. The specificity of these results is underscored by fact that the PI’s network interactions turned out to be practically irrelevant for g.

Conclusion

In the present study we performed a fine-grained analysis of how task-evoked insula-network FC contributes to g. On the basis of a multi-stage modelling pipeline, our results converge on a selective and robust involvement of the AI in the network-related foundation of g across a large HCP sample. After variance-based prefiltering and bootstrap-enhanced regSEM, AI connections to parcels from both the CEN as well as the DMN, were strongly over-represented relative than would be expected by chance. Contributions of the PI on the other hand proved to be negligible. This enrichment pattern aligns with the TNM, which conceptualises the AI as the SN’s linchpin, initiating access to task-relevant control regions while downregulating internally oriented DMN activity. 

Importantly, our findings highlight the specific relevance of positive coupling between the (dorsal) AI and regions in or adjacent to the IPS. This result aligns with previous work indicating that the contribution of parietal areas to g increases under elevated task demands, particularly in the domain of visuospatial reasoning.2 Moreover, the observation that FC between the AI and a medial prefrontal cortex (MPFC) region shows one of the strongest negative correlations with g corresponds with growing evidence that efficient DMN suppression is crucial during cognitively demanding processing, thereby contributing to g.1 Future research should aim to replicate these results and extend them to regions of other networks, such as the SN. Given that the TNM emphasises the interaction between the AI and the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) within the SN4, it would be valuable to examine how the conjoint and disjoint activation of these areas relates to g, and how these patterns correspond to TNM predictions. While our design does not permit causal inference, future studies could use our findings as a foundation for causal approaches, including dynamic causal modelling (DCM) or Granger causality analysis, to evaluate whether insular activity influences downstream network regions and whether this relates to g.

Taken together, our results position the TNM as a viable framework for explaining individual differences in g and underscore the specific relevance of the AI.
