Introduction

Subjective tinnitus is a common and debilitating hearing disorder with no effective treatments. It affects approximately 740 million people worldwide and up to one-quarter of adults in the United States. Tinnitus is a heterogeneous condition with widely varying symptoms and severity, severely impairing quality of life in approximately 10% of affected individuals.1 Its etiology is often associated with insults to the auditory system, including noise exposure, hearing loss, ototoxic medications, and Ménière’s disease. Recently, non-auditory factors, including psychological stress and other major life events, have also been hypothesized to trigger the condition. However, a recent meta-analysis highlighted that, aside from hearing loss, most associations between otologic and non-otologic factors and tinnitus onset remain inconclusive due to limited data.2 This highlights the importance of observational research. Moreover, reliable imaging biomarkers remain elusive, and the existing treatment options show inconsistent and variable outcomes. These inconsistencies are likely due to a critical gap in our understanding of tinnitus as a heterogeneous disorder with multiple etiologies and neural organization signatures. Neuroimaging studies using resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) have revealed alterations in functional connectivity across several large-scale brain networks, including the limbic system, default mode network (DMN), and salience network.3-5 While some findings localize dysfunction to the primary auditory cortex, others suggest widespread changes involving frontal, insular, cingulate, and parietal regions. Moreover, although hearing loss is considered a significant risk factor for tinnitus, its neural correlates remain under-investigated. This study aims to investigate the main risk factors associated with tinnitus onset and the neural signature of the disorder in participants with and without hearing loss. 

Methods

Tinnitus risk factors

We screened our database of 232 individuals with tinnitus (52.4±13.8 years, 137 Males/95 Females) to identify potential risk factors that may have triggered their condition. We grouped risk factors into seven main categories: infection, injury, medication use, loud noise exposure, stressful life events, idiopathic trigger, and other factors. Among the subset of participants with available audiogram data (n = 32), a logistic regression analysis was conducted to examine whether the distribution of specific risk factors differed between individuals with hearing loss (HL_T+) and those with normal hearing (NH_T+). 

Brain imaging

Rs-fMRI data and high-resolution structural images were available in six tinnitus participants with (HL_T+) and seven participants without hearing loss (NH_T+), in addition to six normal hearing participants without tinnitus (NH_T-). Images were acquired on a 3T Siemens scanner using a 64-channel head coil. Functional data were preprocessed using a standard analytical pipeline in FSL and AFNI and then utilized as inputs to the MELODIC Independent Component Analysis tool in FSL to decompose the signal into different spatial and temporal components. All obtained components were visually examined, and those associated with the auditory, salience, and DMN networks were extracted for further group analysis using dual regression. Voxel-based inferential statistics (NH_T- vs. all tinnitus participants, NH_T+ vs. HL_T+) were computed using a two-sample t-test on the individual Z-value maps of the dual regression at P<0.025 and a minimum cluster size of 40 voxels.

Results

Tinnitus risk factors

When looking at the entire dataset, the self-reported risk factors were distributed as follows: 47% idiopathic trigger, 18% loud noise exposure, 13% infection, 6.5% stressful life events, 6.5% medication, and 11% other triggers. In the small cohort of participants with an hearing test, individuals reporting loud sound exposure (odds ratio [OR] = 1.76, 95% CI: 0.32–9.88) and medication use (OR = 1.20, 95% CI: 0.13–12.80) showed higher odds of being in the HL_T+ group, suggesting these factors may be more prevalent among individuals with hearing loss. In contrast, idiopathic onset (OR = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.18–3.96) and stress (OR = 0.80, 95% CI: 0.07–9.33) were associated with lower odds of hearing loss, indicating they may be more commonly reported in the NH_T+ group. 

Brain imaging

The comparison between all participants with tinnitus and NH_T- showed the expected pattern of decreased connectivity in the tinnitus population in the auditory and salience networks, and increased connectivity in the DMN. When directly investigating the effect of hearing loss on tinnitus, participants with both tinnitus and hearing loss exhibited greater connectivity than those without it in the auditory network, specifically in the right inferior temporal and left occipital gyri, and decreased connectivity within the salience network in the left middle frontal gyrus. We did not find any statistically significant differences between HL and NH tinnitus participants.

Conclusion

These findings raise the possibility that different underlying mechanisms may contribute to tinnitus in individuals with and without hearing loss and underscore the need for larger studies to confirm these distinct risk factor profiles. Collectively, the results support the view that alterations in the auditory and salience networks may be characteristic of hearing loss status, while involvement of the DMN may represent a shared neural substrate of tinnitus, regardless of the presence of peripheral hearing loss. The identified differential pattern of brain connectivity calls for risk factor-specific brain organization.
