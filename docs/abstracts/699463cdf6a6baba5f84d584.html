Introduction

Artificial neural networks (ANNs) were originally inspired by the hierarchical organization of the brain, yet how their layered representations relate to large-scale cortical organization remains poorly understood. Prior work has mainly focused on local sensory circuits, leaving brain-wide correspondence and, more importantly, the behavioral relevance of ANN-brain similarity largely unexplored. Recent studies reveal a principal gradient (PG) of the human brain from sensorimotor to default-mode regions (Margulies et al., 2016), shaped by infra-slow propagating global activity that has been linked to sensory encoding and memory function (Gu et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2024). Here, using human fMRI, we investigated whether ANN structure representations align with this hierarchical organization of the human brain, and whether such alignment is critically related to human behaviors.

Methods

We analyzed 7T fMRI data from the Natural Scenes Dataset (NSD) (Allen et al., 2022), in which eight participants viewed 10,000 captioned images (Fig. 1A). The identical stimuli were passed through the 20-layer VGG convolutional neural network (Simonyan and Zisserman, 2014). ANN–brain correspondence was assessed using two complementary approaches. (1), voxel–layer response similarity analysis (RSA): a linear model predicted voxel responses from PCA-reduced ANN layer activations, using R² as the similarity metric (Fig. 1B). Statistical significance was evaluated via null distributions generated by shuffling voxel responses. (2) region–layer representational similarity analysis (RSA): representation dissimilarity matrices (RDMs) were computed for each brain region and ANN layer, and their correlations provided population-level similarity estimates (Fig. 1F). Two-sample t-tests were used to compare similarity measures across behavioral conditions (e.g., successful vs. unsuccessful encoded trials). 

Results

We first established voxel-layer correspondence across the cortex using the voxel-layer RSA. In both human dorsal and ventral visual streams, we observed a robust hierarchical correspondence between ANN representations and neural responses, with early visual areas mapping to lower ANN layers, whereas higher visual areas to deeper layers (Fig. 1C). 

Across the entire cortex, voxels within the visual regions predominantly corresponded to early ANN layers, whereas voxels in higher-order brain regions, such as the frontoparietal network and the default mode network (DMN), were more aligned with later ANN layers (Fig. 1D). Interestingly, this voxel-to-layer assignment recapitulated a well-known principal gradient of cortical organization, transitioning from sensorimotor to transmodal regions (Fig. 1E). Consistent with our voxel-wise similarity analysis, the region-wise similarity analysis confirmed the hierarchical alignment between ANN layers and brain regions along the principal gradient (Fig. 1G).

To assess the cognitive relevance of this ANN-brain correspondence, we compared the similarity measure of brain-ANN alignment between successfully encoded and unsuccessfully encoded images, based on a subsequent memory recall performance (Fig. 2A). The ANN-brain response similarity is significantly higher for successfully encoded images (Fig. 2C). This effect is most pronounced in deep ANN layers (∼65% of brain regions for layers 11-20) compared to early layers (∼20% for layers 1-10) (Fig. 2C). The difference in the similarity enhancement between the DMN and VIS regions progressively increased from early to late ANN layers (Fig. 2D), showing a near-perfect linear relationship with layer number (r = 0.94) (Fig. 2E). Differences between successful and unsuccessful encoding were characterized by strong middle-to-middle and late-to-late correspondences between ANN layers and brain regions along the PG direction (Fig. 2F). 

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate a striking correspondence between ANN hierarchical representations and human fMRI responses to the same set of image stimuli, suggesting shared architectural principles across artificial and biological neural systems. Most importantly, they also suggest that abstract representations of visual information at the higher-order brain regions, mirroring those in deep ANN layers, are critical for successful sensory encoding and subsequent memory retrieval.
