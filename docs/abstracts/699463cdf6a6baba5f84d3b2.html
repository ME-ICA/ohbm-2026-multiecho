Introduction

The identification of consciousness markers during anesthesia is a key focus in clinical practice [1, 2]. At sedative levels, propofol attenuates posterior alpha power and increases frontal beta power [3]. Traditional spectral analysis is a combination of rhythmic oscillations (e.g., alpha, beta) and aperiodic components, which may carry complementary neurophysiological information [4]. This study aims to systematically isolate and investigate the dynamic changes of these two components during different stages of propofol anesthesia (baseline, mild sedation, deep sedation, recovery) and their relationship with behavioral responsiveness.

Methods

We utilized a publicly available dataset to record 91-channel resting-state EEG from 20 healthy participants during progressive propofol infusion [5]. The spectral data were decomposed into aperiodic and periodic components using the SpecParam algorithm [6]. A linear mixed-effects model was employed to analyze how the spatial topology of aperiodic components (exponent and offset) was modulated by anesthesia. Additionally, a double-Gaussian model was applied to fit the periodic alpha and beta oscillations in the occipital and frontal regions, extracting their peak frequencies, power, and bandwidth, as shown in Fig. 1.

Results

The exponent and offset values during deep sedation were significantly higher than those observed during mild sedation and recovery. Spatially, anesthesia significantly altered the pattern of exponent variation along the anterior-posterior axis and weakened its attenuation from the midline to the lateral regions, as shown in Table 1. The anterior-posterior distribution pattern of offset was also modulated by anesthesia. Furthermore, deep sedation specifically altered the characteristics of periodic oscillations, with increased alpha peak frequency, reduced power, and widened bandwidth. Beta peak power was higher (relative to baseline), with a significant increase in bandwidth. During deep sedation, both the aperiodic exponent and offset were correlated with reaction time and accuracy. The peak frequency and bandwidth of periodic alpha oscillations showed a negative correlation with behavioral accuracy (Fig. 2A). Finally, baseline (awake state) periodic beta peak frequency was a reliable predictor of individual behavioral performance during deep sedation (Fig. 2B).

Conclusion

The current study affirms the significance of both aperiodic and periodic components in monitoring anesthesia-induced consciousness. It deepens our understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying anesthetic consciousness and provides new directions for the development of individualized anesthetic depth monitoring tools that integrate multi-dimensional EEG features.
