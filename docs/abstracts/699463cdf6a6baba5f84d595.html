Introduction

Aversive social experiences critically influence affective well-being and are particularly impactful among individuals exposed to childhood trauma 1,2. Neuroimaging studies implicate fronto-limbic and temporo-parietal regions of the social brain in stress-related social-affective processing 3. However, it remains unclear how childhood trauma shapes moment-to-moment social experiences in daily life and how these dynamics relate to brain structure. We investigated social stress sensitivity as a real-life behavioral phenotype and its neuroanatomical correlates.

Methods

The main sample comprised 349 healthy participants (13–28 years), with structural MRI available for 287 individuals, drawn from a population-based epidemiological cohort. A behavioral replication sample included 87 healthy adults (18–64 years). Childhood trauma exposure was assessed using validated self-report measures (CTQ, CTS)4-5.

Participants completed a 7-day smartphone-based ecological momentary assessment capturing affective valence and social experiences using GPS-triggered e-diaries (9–20 prompts/day). Multilevel and non-parametric analyses examined associations between childhood trauma, momentary social aversion, and affective well-being.

Structural T1-weighted MRI data were processed using voxel-based morphometry (CAT12/SPM12; 8 mm FWHM smoothing). Whole-brain and ROI-based multiple regression analyses tested associations between EMA-derived social variables and gray matter volume (GMV), controlling for age, sex, and total intracranial volume (voxel-wise FWE-corrected p<.001).

Results

Higher childhood trauma exposure was associated with increased momentary social aversion in daily life (main sample p = .015; replication p = .036). Greater social aversion was linked to reduced affective well-being across samples (ps < .001), with stronger effects observed among individuals with higher trauma exposure (ps < .05).

At the neural level, greater daily social aversion was associated with lower GMV in medial prefrontal–cingulate regions and the middle frontal gyrus (whole-brain FWE-corrected ps < .05). Reduced medial prefrontal–cingulate volume was additionally related to higher affective social withdrawal (p = .004).

Conclusion

In sum, aversive social experiences carry immediate affective costs and are more frequent and more mood-impacting in individuals with childhood trauma exposure. Together with prefrontal–cingulate correlates, regions central to socio-emotional regulation, these findings delineate a maladaptive neurobehavioral social–affective profile characteristic of social anhedonia.
