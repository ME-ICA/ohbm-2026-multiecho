Introduction

The human brain undergoes a prolonged course of development, providing extended opportunities for neural systems to be shaped by experience. Crucially, the environments in which children grow up can exert a disproportionate influence on brain development and the cognitive processes these systems support 1. Supportive environments that are safe, cognitively stimulating, and characterized by strong social relationships are associated with healthier neurodevelopmental outcomes 2. In contrast, exposure to chronic stressors has been linked to disruptions in neurocognitive development 3. Despite strong evidence that environmental factors shape brain maturation, less is known about how these influences impact the shared experiences between youth at different developmental stages. That is, if environmental factors influence neurocognitive abilities, do children and adolescents from more similar environments have more similar experiences of the world?  Addressing this question requires a large cohort of youth exposed to the same complex stimuli and a consideration that environmental influences are not uniform resulting in nonlinear patterns of brain development from childhood to adolescence. The present study addresses this gap by examining how specific environmental factors, particularly neighborhood safety, parenting style, and exposure to negative life events, relate to experiences experience of naturalistic stimuli as reflected in changes in neural synchrony across childhood and adolescence. Leveraging functional MRI and comprehensive environmental assessments from the Healthy Brain Network, we will apply nonlinear modeling to provide a more nuanced characterization of how distinct environmental experiences shape shared patterns of functional brain development during a critical developmental window.

Methods

Neuroimaging and behavioral data were obtained from the Healthy Brain Network biobank (releases 1–8), an initiative of the Child Mind Institute. The final sample included 840 participants (327 females; mean age = 11.28 years, SD = 3.61; age range = 5–21 years). All imaging data were preprocessed using the Reproducible Brain Charts (RBC) pipeline, an open framework designed to support large-scale, reproducible studies of brain development and mental health. Participants completed T1-weighted anatomical and functional MRI while viewing a 10-minute clip from the animated film Despicable Me. This clip was chosen because it mimics many real-world experiences that requires the integration of various cognitive systems to understand the plot, follow character development, and interpret rich social interactions, providing a naturalistic context for investigating the neural similarity patterns.

Functional MRI data were parcellated using the Yeo 7-network4 cortical atlas. Shared neural responses were quantified using intersubject correlation analyses, computed as pairwise Pearson correlations between participants’ network-specific time series. This procedure yielded an 840 × 840 intersubject similarity matrix for each functional network, indexing the degree of synchronization in neural activity across individuals.

Environmental and adversity-related variables were derived from parent-reported measures, including the Negative Life Events Scale (NLES), the Alabama Parenting Questionnaire (APQ), and neighborhood safety as assessed by the PhenX Toolkit. Representational similarity analysis (RSA) embedded within generalized additive models (GAMs) was applied by first computing pairwise Euclidean distances between participant scores on each of the scales, and age, resulting in corresponding 840 × 840 intersubject similarity matrices for each environmental dimension. GAMs were used to examine nonlinear relationship between similarity scores across environmental factors and age, and shared functional network maturation (ISC), with a focus on the default mode network (DMN). Generalized Additive Models provide a robust and flexible statistical framework for modeling complex, nonlinear relationships, as they do not impose predefined functional forms between predictors and outcomes, unlike traditional regression approaches. Instead, these relationships are empirically derived from the data during model estimation, eliminating the need for manual refinement and variable selection while enhancing both efficiency and precision in large-scale analyses. All GAMs were fitted using Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) estimation as implemented in the mgcv package within R. To account for potential motion-related confounds, the euclidean distance of mean framewise displacement between participant pairs was included as a covariate. The analyses focused on estimating the nonlinear effects of each predictor through separate smooth functions, as well as the interactions between environmental measures and age. An example of the nonlinear difference modeling framework is provided below in R syntax:

Results

Using this framework, we examined the partial contributions of each environmental factor and age to intersubject correlation (ISC) by estimating the component effects of individual smooth terms, which collectively contribute to the model’s overall predictions. Across predictors, we identified four distinct, statistically significant nonlinear patterns (see Figure1a).

Differences in age between participant pairs showed a pronounced nonlinear association with ISC, characterized by a steep decrease up to approximately a 10-year age gap, followed by a small transient increase and a subsequent decline (edf= 8.159, F= 394.612, p-value < 2e-16). Neighborhood safety, as measured by the PhenX Toolkit, exhibited a relatively stable association with ISC for small differences (up to ~2 points), after which ISC declined sharply (edf = 8.867, F = 236.227, p-value < 2e-16). A similar pattern was observed for parenting behavior (APQ), with minimal effects at smaller differences and a marked reduction in ISC at larger discrepancies between individuals edf = 6.145, F = 24.137, p-value < 2e-16). In contrast, the results of the GAM for NLES have shown that it is almost constant during the NLES differences (edf = 4.473, F = 5.846, p-value =1.6e-05).

Interaction analyses also revealed significant nonlinear effects. Specifically, age showed a significant nonlinear interaction with parenting behavior (APQ; edf = 4.208 ,F = 2.524, p-value = 0.0258), neighborhood safety (PhenX; edf = 9.044, F = 16.039, p-value  <2e-16), and exposure to negative life events (NLES; edf = 6.388,F = 7.308, p-value   <2e-16), indicating that the influence of environmental factors on neural synchrony varies across developmental stages (Figure 1b).

Conclusion

The current study identified significant nonlinear effects of age and multiple environmental factors on intersubject synchrony within the default mode network (DMN). Prior work has established neural synchrony, particularly in the DMN network, as a marker of shared experiences during naturalistic cognition5. Our findings extend this literature by demonstrating that similarities in age and environmental context are associated with more synchronized DMN responses, whereas larger discrepancies in negative early-life experiences and contextual factors such as perceived neighbourhood safety and parenting style are linked to increasingly divergent neural trajectories. These results suggest that youth with comparable environments likely process real-world experiences in more similar ways, while greater differences in environmental measures correspond to more distinct experiences reflected by reduced neural synchrony during development.

The observed patterns indicate that environmental factors do not exert uniform influences on neural synchrony. Instead, parenting behaviors, neighborhood safety, and exposure to negative life events, showed a distinct nonlinear relationship with neural synchrony, emphasizing the importance of disaggregating environmental dimensions.

Additionally, differences in age between individuals showed a strong nonlinear association with neural synchrony, underscoring age-related changes in shared neural responses across childhood and adolescence. Specifically, larger age gaps were associated with progressively reduced synchrony within the default mode network.

In addition, significant nonlinear interactions between age and environmental factors indicate that the impact of context on neural synchrony is developmentally dependent, suggesting that sensitivity to environmental influences changes across childhood and adolescence. These findings highlight that environmental effects on brain development are not static but evolve across developmental stages.

Overall, this work underscores the importance of early-life environments in shaping functional brain organization across childhood and adolescence. Specifically, our findings highlight the nuanced and differential influence of environmental factors on shared neural responses, emphasizing that not all environmental dimensions contribute equally to brain maturation. By isolating the distinct effects of neighborhood safety, adversity, and age this work provides insight that may inform targeted interventions aimed at supporting healthy cognitive and neural development through improvements in children’s environments and family contexts.
