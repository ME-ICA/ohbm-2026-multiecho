Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is one of the leading causes of death worldwide. The prediction of conversion from mild cognitive impairment (MCI) to AD is of high clinical value, allowing clinicians to provide accurate diagnostic and prognostic information and enabling treatment stratification. Neuroimaging is a valuable tool that can be utilised in the prediction of MCI conversion. Brain changes precede clinical manifestations of AD, detecting these changes early could help to predict patients more likely to convert from MCI to AD.1 When researchers utilise neuroimaging derived features, such as cortical thickness measures or other atlas-reliant features, they must make a choice of which brain atlas to use. Prior research has show the choice of brain atlas used for predictive tasks can significantly affect results.2 Further, choices taken by researchers implementing machine learning (ML) pipelines can affect results with previous research showing the variability between researchers using the same data to test the same hypotheses.3 These factors can leave researchers unsure what the optimal ML pipeline and brain atlas they should use is. Further, the range of options can leave researchers making arbitrary choices in their pipeline design resulting in poor reproducibility and inconsistent results.

Automated Machine Learning (AutoML) seeks to reduce researcher variability by selecting pipelines using a data-driven approach. Here we introduce an expanded version of AutoML-Multiverse, Atlas-Aware AutoML-Multiverse, that incorporates different atlases into its search, allowing for optimal pipeline-atlas combinations to be found and eliminating the need to pre-select an atlas.

By testing the Atlas-Aware AutoML-Multiverse framework on two prediction tasks - cognitively normal (CN) vs AD and stable MCI (sMCI) vs progressive MCI (pMCI) across multiple brain atlases we hope to show the value in using this framework to reduce variability in results while not sacrificing performance.

Methods

AutoML-Multiverse is built from a "multiverse" of ML pipelines and searched through to find the optimal pipeline for the data at hand. In total, 19,000 pipelines incorporating a range of pre-processing techniques and model architectures are constructed into a high dimensional space and reduced to a latent space of 4-dimensions using multi-dimensional scaling. These pipelines are clustered based on their predictions based on 65 OpenML datasets and uses Bayesian optimisation to effectively and efficiently select possible high performing pipelines.4 Figure 1 details the methodology and construction of the AutoML-Multiverse framework.

In this work the 4-dimensional latent space of AutoML-Multiverse has been expanded by adding an additional dimension to represent different brain atlas choices. This expansion of the latent space allows for the optimisation of pipeline-atlas combinations to create Atlas-Aware AutoML-Multiverse.

All data is from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) with the same participants and scans across all atlases. Progressive MCI was determined by a volunteer receiving an AD diagnosis within three years of their scan while they were MCI. In total there were 685 CN scans and 368 AD scans in the CN vs AD task and 250 sMCI and 277 pMCI scans in the sMCI vs pMCI task. Scans were processed with FreeSurfer, FastSurfer and GIF. FreeSurfer processed scans used either the Desikan-Killiany (DK), Desikan-Killiany-Tourville (DKT) or Destrieux atlas. The DK atlas processed with FreeSurfer had 70 features covering cortical regions, the DKT processed with FreeSurfer had 64 features covering cortical regions and the Destriex processed with FreeSurfer had 150 features covering cortical regions. FastSurfer processed scans used the DKT atlas and covered 100 cortical and subcortical features. Geodesic Information Flows (GIF) processed scans had 162 features covering cortical and subcortical regions. No further features were added to the data and the standard outputs were taken from each processing method to provide fair comparison.

Two prediction tasks were evaluated here across all 5 atlases, CN vs AD and sMCI vs pMCI. To compare the results of the Atlas-Aware AutoML-Multiverse, AutoML-Multiverse was evaluated individually on each atlas along with 9 standard machine learning benchmarks that were also evaluated on the individual atlases. All models were evaluated using stratified 5-fold cross-validation with 5 repeats.

Results

In both CN vs AD and sMCI vs pMCI the Atlas-Aware Multiverse performs at the top level, selecting an optimal pipeline-atlas combination. Figure 2 shows the performance of the Atlas-Aware AutoML-Multiverse across both tasks along with the single-atlas AutoML-Multiverse and the best performing ML benchmark across 9 standard ML models.

In the CN vs AD prediction task, the Atlas-Aware AutoML-Multiverse framework achieves a balanced accuracy of 0.894 ±0.019 selecting the DKT atlas processed with FastSurfer. Atlas-Aware AutoML-Multiverse has up to 20% improved balanced accuracy over selecting a sub-optimal atlas and using AutoML-Multiverse. When running AutoML-Multiverse on a single atlas it can also be seen to out-perform the best performing ML benchmark.

In the sMCI vs pMCI task the Atlas-Aware AutoML-Multiverse framework achieves a balanced accuracy of 0.767 ±0.033 selecting the DKT atlas processed with FastSurfer. Atlas-Aware AutoML-Multiverse has up to 18% improvement over selecting a sub-optimal atlas while using AutoML-Multiverse. While the best performing standard ML benchmark sometimes achieves better accuracy than AutoML-Multiverse within the same atlas, the Atlas-Aware AutoML-Multiverse has the highest overall accuracy.

Conclusion

This work shows the significant effect the selection of brain atlas can have on predictive neuroimaging tasks. While often researchers may select an optimal atlas and pipeline for their task, be it through prior knowledge or expansive searching, the presented Atlas-Aware AutoML-Multiverse framework allows researchers to not have to make a choice and take a data-driven approach. Further, we have shown using the Atlas-Aware AutoML-Multiverse framework has higher performance than standard ML benchmark models, allowing this data-driven approach to be used while ensuring high-level performance.

The main limitation of Atlas-Aware AutoML-Multiverse currently is the need to initially process all the atlases due to the processing time required for each brain scan along with the select number of different atlases Atlas-Aware AutoML-Multiverse was utilised with. Future work planned is to add more brain atlases to this analysis along with validating this framework with additional datasets and neurodegenerative diseases to asses the wider generalisability of the Atlas-Aware AutoML-Multiverse framework.
