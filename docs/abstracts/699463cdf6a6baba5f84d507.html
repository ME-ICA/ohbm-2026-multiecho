Introduction

Introduction

The concept of "Cerebellar Cognitive Affective Syndrome" (CCAS) posits that cerebellar damage produces a distinct clinical entity characterized by executive, linguistic, visuospatial, and affective deficits1. While this framework has revolutionized our understanding of cerebellar function, it remains uncertain whether cognitive impairment following cerebellar stroke truly differs from general post-stroke cognitive impairment (PSCI) observed after supra-tentorial damage2. Resolving this issue is critical for defining appropriate screening and rehabilitation pathways. We aimed to: (1) compare the prevalence and phenotype of cognitive and behavioural outcomes 6 months after isolated cerebellar stroke versus mixed and extra-cerebellar strokes; and (2) map the cerebellar substrates of these deficits using lesion-symptom mapping. We hypothesized that if CCAS is a distinct syndrome, cerebellar patients should exhibit a specific cognitive signature.

Methods

Study Design and Population

We analyzed data from 375 patients prospectively enrolled in the GRECogVASC and IDEA3 cohorts3,4. Inclusion criteria were age 40-85, confirmed acute stroke, and ability to complete testing. Exclusion criteria included pre-stroke dementia, severe sensory deficits, or life expectancy <6 months. Patients were assessed 6 months post-stroke and classified into three groups based on high-resolution MRI lesion topography (Figure 1): Isolated Cerebellar stroke (n=18), Mixed Cerebellar stroke (n=67, cerebellar plus supra/infra-tentorial lesions), and Extra-Cerebellar stroke (n=290). Age- and education-adjusted score were interpreted using those of healthy control group (n=1003) from the GRECogVASC normative database.

Neuropsychological Assessment

A comprehensive harmonized battery was administered by neuropsychologists. Global cognition was assessed via MMSE and MoCA. Executive functions were evaluated using Trail Making Test (TMT) B and verbal Fluency tests. Language assessment included a shortened Boston Naming Test (BNT). Attention and processing speed were measured using Simple Reaction Time, TMT A, and Digit Symbol Coding. Memory was assessed with the Free and Cued Selective Reminding Test (RL/RI-16). Behavioral and affective changes were evaluated using the informant-rated Behavioural Dysexecutive Syndrome Inventory (BDSI), assessing 12 dimensions including apathy and hyperactivity, alongside the CES-D for depression and Goldberg scale for anxiety. Raw scores were converted to z-scores using the normative data. Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) was defined as domain score <5th percentile with preserved instrumental activities of daily living (IADL).

Imaging Acquisition and Analysis

High-resolution 3D T1-weighted, T2-FLAIR, and DWI sequences were acquired at 3T. Lesions were characterized according to STRIVE criteria. White matter hyperintensities (WMH) were quantified using the Lesion Segmentation Tool (SPM12). Automated cerebellar lobular segmentation was performed using the CERES algorithm on the volBrain platform, normalizing volumes for intracranial size. Lesion-symptom mapping was conducted on the combined cerebellar cohort (n=85) using t-tests to identify voxel clusters associated with cognitive deficits, thresholded at p<0.01 (FDR-corrected) with a minimum cluster size of 10 voxels.

Statistical Analysis

Group comparisons employed one-way ANOVA with post-hoc REGWQ tests for continuous variables and Chi-square tests for categorical ones. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analyses identified discriminant cognitive markers. Processing speed efficiency was analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVA across tasks of increasing complexity (Tapping → TMT-A → TMT-B → Fluency)5.

Results

Demographics and Clinical Features

The cohort (mean age 63.97±10.54; 61.9% male) showed no significant age or education differences across groups (p>0.05). Initial stroke severity was lower in isolated cerebellar stroke (National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale [NIHSS]: 1.94±2.57) compared to mixed (4.20±4.47) and extra-cerebellar (5.30±5.64) groups (p=0.026), yet 6-month functional outcomes (Rankin score, Barthel index) were comparable (p>0.28).

Cognitive Prevalence and Profiles

MCI prevalence at 6 months did not differ (p=0.188) across groups: 44.4% (isolated cerebellar), 43.3% (mixed), and 36.9% (extra-cerebellar). Cognitive profiles were strikingly similar, with no domain showing a cerebellar-specific pattern. ROC analysis revealed that a global cognitive score 3 domains (comprising executive, speed, and language domains) strongly discriminated isolated cerebellar patients from controls (AUC=0.855, 95% CI [0.778 ; 0.932], p<0.001).

Behavior

Informant-rated behavioral changes were rare. Although 42.9% of isolated cerebellar patients showed “activity reduction” this was inextricably linked to depression (100% concordance with CES-D >16), distinguishing it from the “affective flattening” proposed in CCAS. No significant differences were found for disinhibition, impulsivity, or social conduct disorders.

Language and Speed Analysis

In the naming task, semantic paraphasias constituted 50% of errors in isolated cerebellar patients, pointing to semantic network disruption rather than pure motor speech deficits. The processing speed analysis revealed a significant Group × Task Complexity interaction (p=0.001). While all stroke patients were slower than controls, the slope of reaction time slowing as a function of task complexity was identical between cerebellar and extra-cerebellar patients, indicating a shared non-specific deficit in recruiting neural resources under cognitive load.

Anatomical Substrates:

Cognitive scores correlated significantly with volumes of Crus I (r=0.160), Crus II (r=0.170), and Lobule VIIb (r=0.150) (all p<0.01), though these volumes explained only ~3% of variance. Lesion-symptom mapping (Figure 2) identified a critical cluster in the left posterolateral cerebellum (spanning Crus I/II and Lobules VI-VIII) where lesions were significantly associated with worse global cognitive performance (p=0.002).

Conclusion

Our results do support the contribution of the cerebellum to some cognitive function but provide evidence against the existence of a distinct CCAS in the vascular population. Despite rigorous phenotyping of isolated cerebellar strokes, we found (1) no difference in MCI prevalence compared to extra-cerebellar strokes; (2) a cognitive phenotype indistinguishable from general PSCI, characterized by dysexecutive syndrome, slowed processing speed, and semantic language deficits; and (3) an absence of specific neuropsychiatric features when controlling for depression.

The cerebellar cognitive profile likely reflects the disruption of distributed cerebro-cerebellar networks rather than intrinsic cerebellar computation failure. The observed semantic and executive deficits, associated with left posterolateral lesions (Crus I/II), support the theory of disconnexion of fronto-cerebellar networks. However, this network disruption produces a clinical outcome identical to direct supratentorial damage, questioning the clinical utility of the CCAS construct in stroke.

The overlapping symptomatology has immediate clinical relevance. Cerebellar stroke patients are at equal risk for cognitive impairment as other stroke survivors and should not be excluded from standard cognitive care. We recommend abandoning specific “cerebellar” screening tools in favor of systematic inclusion of cerebellar patients in standard PSCI screening and rehabilitation pathways. Future research should focus on functional connectivity changes to further elucidate the network mechanisms underlying these shared deficits.
