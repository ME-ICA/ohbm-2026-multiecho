Introduction

Focal cortical dysplasias (FCDs) are malformations of cortical development and represent a major cause of drug-resistant focal epilepsy. Surgical resection offers seizure freedom in a substantial proportion of patients, particularly when the lesion is identified preoperatively on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). However, FCDs are difficult to detect visually due to subtle and heterogeneous imaging features, resulting in high inter-rater variability and more than 30% of lesions being missed even by experienced neuroradiologists.1

Deep learning-based lesion detection models have emerged as promising tools to address this diagnostic gap. A recent multicenter study demonstrated that existing models can detect FCDs across MRI data from different scanners without retraining.2 Nevertheless, current performance estimates are derived from curated retrospective datasets, which may not reflect the heterogeneity of real-world clinical settings with respect to patient characteristics, acquisition protocols, and scanner hardware.

While MRI at 3 Tesla (3T MRI) remains the clinical standard, ultra-high-field MRI at 7T (7T MRI) is gaining clinical interest due to its higher signal-to-noise ratio and improved lesion conspicuity. These advantages, however, are accompanied by altered lesion appearance and specific image artifacts, making 7T MRI a challenging test case for evaluating the transferability of models previously evaluated on 3T MRI.

In this study, we prospectively evaluated a previously developed deep learning model for FCD detection in a presurgical epilepsy cohort on 3T and 7T MRI. Our objectives were to assess detection performance and robustness across imaging platforms and clinically relevant subgroups, and to evaluate the model’s translational potential in routine clinical practice.

Methods

We prospectively included all individuals with drug-resistant focal epilepsy eligible for MRI at the Department of Epileptology, University Hospital Bonn, over a two-year period (2023–2024), as well as ten healthy controls. All participants obtained T1-weighted (T1w) and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) scans at 3T and at 7T. We selected the 3D U-Net model proposed by Kersting et al.,2 as it demonstrated the highest F1-score among multi-centric validated publicly available FCD detection models operating on 3D MRI images.

Linearly co-registered and skull-stripped T1w and FLAIR MRI served as model input and the model outputs a binary three-dimensional image indicating lesional voxels. Connected components larger than 0.1 ml were considered as candidate lesions. We assessed congruence of predicted lesions between 3T and 7T MRI on a per-subject basis and evaluated detection performance against manually annotated lesion masks in clinically known FCD cases.

Predicted lesions on 3T and 7T MRI were considered congruent if they showed any spatial overlap (Dice similarity coefficient, DSC>0). To account for uncertainty in manual annotations, lesions were considered detected if the bounding boxes surrounding the predicted and annotated lesions overlapped (boxDSC>0). All model predictions were independently reviewed by two neuroradiologists and two epileptologists on both MRI datasets. Reviewers assessed radiological features and congruence with presurgical evaluation to determine whether predicted lesions represented previously overlooked FCDs.

Results

The model was evaluated on paired 3T and 7T MRI from 115 individuals with epilepsy (mean age ± standard deviation: 34 ± 11 years; 53 females) and ten healthy participants (25 ± 3 years; 5 females). The epilepsy cohort was subdivided into “clinically known FCD” (n = 11), “MRI-negative” (n = 64), and “other lesion” (n = 40) groups.

In the “clinically known FCD” subgroup, 71% (5/7) of candidate lesions were congruent between 3T and 7T MRI, compared with only 26% (8/31) in the combined “MRI-negative” and “other lesion” subgroups.

Sensitivity for detecting clinically known FCDs was 55% (6/11) on 3T MRI and 45% (5/11) on 7T MRI, with no statistically significant difference between field strengths. All detected lesions were confirmed as FCDs on both MRI modalities during independent expert review.

In the “MRI-negative” subgroup, the model predicted 17 candidate lesions on 3T MRI, of which seven were rated as FCD by experts (41%, 7/17). On 7T MRI, four candidate lesions were predicted, of which one was confirmed as FCD (25%, 1/4).

In the “other lesion” subgroup, 13 candidate lesions were predicted on 3T MRI across 13/40 individuals and five candidate lesions on 7T MRI across 5/40 individuals; none were confirmed as FCD upon expert review. No lesions were predicted in healthy participants on either MRI modality.

Conclusion

This study represents the first prospective evaluation of a deep learning-based FCD detection model on a paired multi-scanner dataset with systematic expert validation, specifically addressing clinical utility beyond curated retrospective datasets.

The model maintained previously reported performance for detecting clinically known FCDs and demonstrated comparable sensitivity across 3T and 7T MRI, indicating robustness to differences in magnetic field strength.

However, in the MRI-negative subgroup, the primary target population for automated lesion detection, the model generated more candidate lesions on 3T MRI than on 7T MRI, several of which were independently confirmed as FCDs. The lack of consistent detection across field strengths suggests limited robustness for subtle lesions, potentially reflecting underrepresentation of such cases and diagnostic uncertainty in the training data.

While no false positives were observed in healthy controls, the model incorrectly predicted FCDs in patients with other epileptogenic pathologies, indicating a lack of contextual awareness of non-FCD lesions. This underscores a fundamental limitation of single-disease supervised training when models are applied to heterogeneous clinical cohorts and suggests partial overfitting to the training distribution.

Progress toward reliable clinical translation of automated FCD detection will require large, diverse, and representative datasets. To support this effort, we plan to make our paired 3T-7T MRI dataset publicly available to facilitate training and validation of future models across the full clinical spectrum of epilepsy.
