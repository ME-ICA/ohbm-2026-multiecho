Introduction

Childhood is a critical period in human brain development, during which the brain undergoes rapid structural maturation alongside continuous functional reorganization, supporting the emergence of fundamental cognitive, social, and emotional abilities1,2. The remarkable plasticity of the developing brain also makes this period especially vulnerable to disruptions in typical development, and many neurodevelopmental disorders such as autistic individuals first emerge during childhood. Despite its importance, the primary neuropathological underpinnings of autism across the full spectrum of childhood remain ambiguous. 

Hemispheric lateralization is a defining feature of human brain organization, with asymmetries in structure, connectivity, and gene expression detectable as early as in fetuses and neonates, supporting efficient neural processing across cognitive and socio-communicative domains3. The integration of intra- and inter-hemispheric connections gives rise to a balanced connectivity profile, which is critical for the execution of sophisticated cognitive and behavioral functions4. Nevertheless, traditional approaches on functional hemispheric lateralization have predominantly focused on static connectivity, which assumes temporal stability and is inherently influenced by scanning duration of resting-state fMRI5. Investigating the dynamics of such lateralization patterns in autism may provide key insights into the neural mechanisms underlying their complex clinical phenotypes. However, how dynamic functional lateralization evolves across full spectrum childhood in autism, and the molecular mechanisms that shape these developmental patterns remain unknown. 

Methods

Participants

The discovery cohort included resting-state fMRI data and clinical measurements from 1227 autism individuals and 326 typically developing (TD) individuals, and these individuals were divided into two developmental groups: early childhood (1 ≤ age ≤ 6 years) and later childhood (6 < age ≤ 14 years). The early childhood group consisted of 929 autism individuals (768 males, mean age ± SD = 3.67 ± 1.13 years) and 147 TD individuals (68 males, mean age ± SD = 3.94 ± 1.37 years). The later childhood group consisted of 298 autism individuals (245 males, mean age ± SD = 7.93 ± 1.68 years) and 179 TD individuals (70 males, mean age ± SD = 8.72 ± 1.79 years). An independent cohort of later childhood participants (6 < age ≤ 14 years; 56 autism: mean age ± SD = 10.77 ± 1.82 years and 97 TD: mean age ± SD = 10.75 ± 1.82 years) was selected from six sites of Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange (ABIDE)-I and II as validation cohort. 

Estimation of dynamic connectome lateralization strength

Fig. 1a illustrates the pipeline employed for the dynamic connectome lateralization strength (DCLS) analysis. Firstly, we extracted the average blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signals from 246 brain regions defined in Human Brainnetome Atlas. Thereafter, dynamic conditional correlation (DCC) was applied to calculate the dynamic connectome matrixes for each time point, and the DCLS index was subsequently calculated to quantify the degree of lateralization at the hemispheric connectome-scale for each brain region. 

Heterogeneity measures in region-level and whole brain-level DCLS

To further investigate whether individuals with autism exhibit greater heterogeneity than control group in DCLS, we first established a childhood-stage-specific normative model for each regional DCLS feature to quantify the DCLS deviations of each autism individual relative to the TD group of corresponding childhood stage, and then utilized the log variability ratio (lnVR) to evaluate the relative heterogeneity between the autism and TD groups (Fig. 1b). Additionally, to examine whether whole-brain heterogeneity in DCLS deviation among autism individuals increases following age, we computed the mean Pearson’s correlation coefficients of the 246 regional DCLS deviation values between each autism individual and all other autism participants within the early-childhood and later-childhood groups, respectively.

Clinical Associations 

We performed a sparse canonical correlation analysis (sCCA, Fig. 1c) to delineate latent dimensions linking functional lateralization features with clinical measurements in early and later childhood autism groups, separately. 

Transcriptomic and neurotransmitter analysis

We extracted brain-wide gene expressions of six postmortem human brains from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA, Fig. 1d) transcriptomic dataset. Then, partial least squares (PLS) regression was conducted to investigate the association between transcriptome and regional DCLS abnormalities (i.e., regional Cohen’s d values of DCLS comparisons between autism and TD) in early and later childhood autism groups, respectively. We further investigated the spatial correlations between regional DCLS abnormalities and 9 distinct neurotransmitter systems, including dopamine, norepinephrine, serotonin, acetylcholine, glutamate, γ-aminobutyric acid, histamine, cannabinoid and opioid, which derived from previously reported PET images (Fig. 1d).

Results

Atypical DCLS in different autistic childhood stages and hemispheres

Compared with TD controls, autism individuals exhibited significantly greater DCLS valuesin 20 regions during early childhood (largest effect size: right superior temporal gyrus [STG] = 0.361, Fig. 2a), and 46 regions during later childhood (largest effect size: left precuneus = 0.476, Fig. 2b). At the network level, during early childhood group, increased DCLS was mainly observed in the left FPN and right DMN (Fig. 2a), whereas during later childhood group, higher DCLS was found in the left DMN, DAN, VAN, and FPN, as well as in the right DMN (Fig. 2b). Hemispheric comparison further revealed left-lateralized DCLS abnormalities in autism, which were significant during the later childhood (t(122)=4.05, PFDR =4.4e-05, Fig. 2b), but not during early childhood (t(122)=1.85,PFDR =0.067, Fig. 2a). In the validation sample (ABIDE cohort), both the effect size brain map and the left-lateralized DCLS abnormalities observed in later childhood were successfully reproduced, showing a high spatial correlation (r=0.421, p=5.2e-12) and significant left-lateralization (t(122)=5.14, p=1.0e-06).

Heterogenous DCLS in different autistic childhood stages and hemispheres

Significant group difference on lnVR was observed in only one brain region during early childhood (middle temporal gyrus, MTG, lnVR=0.252, 95%CI=[0.004, 0.499], Fig. 2c), where autism individuals showed greater DCLS variability than TD controls. During later childhood, this pattern became more widespread, with 14 regions showing higher (largest lnVR: left parahippocampal gyrus=0.414, 95%CI=[0.153, 0.680]) and 6 regions showing lower (smallest lnVR: right orbital gyrus=-0.337, 95%CI=[-0.600, -0.073]) DCLS variability in autism compared with TD individuals (Fig. 2d). At the network level, autism individuals showed comparable DCLS variability to TD individuals during early childhood (|mean lnVR values|<0.1, Fig. 2c), but exhibited greater variability during later childhood, particularly in the left hemisphere within the VAN (mean lnVR=0.175) and LIM (mean lnVR=0.183) (Fig. 2d). Hemispheric comparison in regional lnVR further revealed greater DCLS variability in the left hemisphere among autism individuals, significant during both early childhood (t(122)=5.89, PFDR =3.4e-08, Fig. 2c) and later childhood (t(122)=12.65, PFDR <1.0e-20, Fig. 2d). Moreover, the lnVR map showed a significant spatial correlation with the effect size map of case–control DCLS differences across the whole-brain during later childhood (r=0.026, PFDR =0.686, Fig. 2f), but not during early childhood (r=0.277, PFDR =2.0e-05, Fig. 2e). We further calculated the inter-subject similarity of whole-brain DCLS deviations among autism individuals, which increased significantly with age during later childhood (r=0.294, PFDR=4.9e-09, Fig. 2g), but not during early childhood (r=0.054, PFDR=0.10).

Linked clinical measurements and DCLS in autistic individuals

Sparse canonical correlation analysis revealed robust associations between DCLS and clinical phenotypes. In early childhood, DCLS features—primarily in the limbic network—correlated with social-emotional scores. Later childhood associations shifted towards the default mode network with a left-hemispheric trend, linking to core autism severity.

Transcriptomic and neurotransmitter decoding of atypical DCLS in autistic individuals

Transcriptomic decoding identified significant molecular substrates specifically in later childhood, with DCLS abnormalities spatially tracking genes enriched in GABAergic, dopaminergic, and glutamatergic neurons. Furthermore, DCLS alterations showed age-dependent spatial correlations with neurotransmitter receptor architectures. While early childhood displayed moderate associations, later childhood exhibited strong coupling with broad excitatory (dopamine, glutamate) and inhibitory (GABA) systems.


Conclusion

This study addresses a number of heterogeneity questions concerning the development of functional lateralization in autism across full spectrum of childhood, including associations with clinical symptoms, transcriptomic substrates, as well as chemoarchitecture of the brain. Specifically, leveraging advanced dynamic connectome estimation and the difference between inter- and intra-hemispheric connectome, we quantified the brain-wide dynamic connectome lateralization strength across age groups. Our analyses reveal that atypical lateralization in autism is a dynamic, age-dependent deviation that evolves from early to later childhood. We identified a critical developmental shift: while early childhood aberrations were predominantly localized to limbic circuits associated with basic socio-emotional processing, later childhood manifested widespread disruptions in high-order networks, particularly the default mode network, correlating with complex social communication deficits. This stage-dependent trajectory underscores the necessity of viewing autism through a developmental lens, where early sensorimotor and limbic dysfunctions may cascade into widespread network reorganization in later stages.

Collectively, these findings establish a unified mechanistic framework linking synaptic molecular signatures to system-level dynamic functional lateralization. Moving forward, this multi-scale characterization not only deepens our mechanistic understanding of neurodevelopmental deviations but also highlights potential age-specific windows for precision therapeutic interventions targeting specific neurotransmitter systems to restore the balance of hemispheric functional organization.
