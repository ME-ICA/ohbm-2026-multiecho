Introduction

In-vivo neurochemistry can be studied non-invasively using magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS). Investigating the neurochemical environment can provide information on the state of neural processes under different pathological conditions. However, an understanding of neurochemical processes in healthy cohorts is equally important as it provides a reference point for investigations in clinical populations.

Metabolites measured via MRS can provide insight into excitatory neurotransmission (glutamate + glutamine (Glx)), energy metabolism (creatine), neuronal health (N-Aspartyl Aspartate (NAA)), membrane turnover (choline), and neuroinflammation (myoinositol)1. Metabolite research in healthy adults has largely focused on cohort studies with restricted age ranges2 or comparisons between young adults and elderly groups3. A better understanding of metabolite trajectories across the entirety of healthy adulthood is needed.

Similarly, sex-differences in metabolite concentrations over the course of healthy aging is poorly characterized. Differences in metabolites according to menstrual cycle stage have been documented4 but whether metabolites vary between females who regularly cycle and those who do not is unknown.

This investigation sought to determine the effects of age and sex on metabolite concentrations (NAA, creatine, choline, myoinositol, Glx) in two brain regions in a cohort of healthy adults. Based on previous literature in adult age-cohort studies (i.e., comparing between age-groups), we expected NAA and Glx to decrease with age and creatine, choline and myoinositol to increase with age. Due to a lack of established literature investigating sex differences in metabolite concentration, no hypotheses were formulated. In an exploratory sub-group analysis, we examined whether menstrual status (regularly vs. not regularly menstruating) was associated with metabolite concentration in females.

Methods

Sixty healthy adults (18-65 years old, 43F/17M) were scanned using a 3T GE 750w scanner. T1-weighted anatomical imaging was used for voxel placement (Figure 1) and tissue segmentation. Short-echo time PRESS (TR/TE = 2000/35 ms, 64 water-suppressed averages, 8 water unsuppressed scan averages, number of data points = 4096, spectral width = 5000 Hz) was used to quantify total NAA (tNAA), Glx, total choline (tCho), total creatine (tCr) and myoinositol (mI). Voxels were placed in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and right sensorimotor cortex (SMC) and measured 3 x 3 x 3 cm3.

Data were processed using Osprey (version 2.96), an MRS analysis software built in MATLAB. Spectral appearance and the presence of artifacts determined data exclusion in addition to standard quality metrics. After fitting with LCModel within Osprey, neurochemical concentrations were tissue-corrected for tissue specific T1, T2 and proton density as well as the CSF voxel fraction. 

Menstrual status in females was determined by whether they reported regularly or not regularly menstruating. It is important to note that in this investigation, menstrual status is not dependent on age or menopausal status; several young females reported not regularly menstruating due to hormonal contraception.

Multiple linear mixed models were used to examine the relationship of age, sex, and brain region with each metabolite. Independent samples t-tests were used to determine whether menstrual status affected metabolite concentrations among females. The Bonferroni correction was used to account for multiple comparisons and any analyses noted as significant survived this correction unless otherwise stated.

Results

Glx (Figure 2): Sex (β = 1.60, 95%CI[0.52,2.67], p = .004) and region (β = 9.83, 95%CI[8.87,10.80], p < .001) had a significant effect on Glx concentration, with males and the ACC associated with higher concentrations. Age was not significantly related to Glx concentration (β = 0.02, 95%CI[-0.03,0.06], p = .495).

tCho: Region (β = .243, 95%CI[0.08,0.40], p = .003) and age (β = .018, 95%CI[0.01,0.03], p < .001) had a significant effect on tCho concentration with the ACC having higher concentrations than the SMC and tCho increasing with age. Sex was not significantly related to tCho concentration (β = -.013, 95%CI[-0.19,0.17], p = .886).

tNAA (Figure 2): Region (β = -3.25, 95%CI[-5.47,-1.02], p = .005) had a significant effect on tNAA concentration with the SMC associated with higher concentrations than the ACC. Age (β=.006, 95%CI[-0.03,0.04], p = .735) and sex (β=.592, 95%CI[-1.94,3.12], p = .643) were not significantly related to tNAA concentration. A significant interaction effect of region, sex and age was found (β=-.086, 95%CI[-0.17,-0.01], p = .033) but did not survive the Bonferroni correction. 

tCr: Region (β = -.724, 95%CI[-1.17,-0.27], p = .002) and age (β = .039, 95%CI[0.02,0.06], p < .001) had a significant effect on tCr concentration with the SMC showing higher concentration than the ACC and tCr increasing with age. Sex (β = .010, 95%CI[-0.49,0.51], p = .969) was not significantly related to tCr concentration.

mI: Region (β = 1.141, 95%CI[0.53,1.75], p < .001) and age (β = .050, 95%CI[0.02,0.08], p < .001) had a significant effect on mI concentration with the ACC having higher mI levels and mI increasing with age. Sex (β = .027, 95%CI[-0.65,0.71], p = .937) was not significantly related to mI concentration.

Menstrual status: None of the metabolites (tNAA, Glx, tCho, tCr, mI) in the ACC differed by menstrual status (p >.05). However, females not regularly menstruating had higher mI concentration in the SMC (p = .046) compared to those regularly menstruating, though this did not survive the Bonferroni correction. No other metabolite concentration (tNAA, Glx, tCr, tCho) was associated with menstrual status in the SMC.

Conclusion

In summary, using short-echo PRESS in a large sample spanning healthy adulthood, age, sex and brain region were shown to significantly affect metabolite concentration. This investigation provides important reference information for comparisons to clinical populations.

We did not find Glx age-associations in this adult cohort, contrary to expectations. However, our analysis encompassed healthy adulthood (18-65 years) while age-related differences previously reported in Glx are often in narrow age range cross-sectional analyses. It is not surprising that Glx was higher in the ACC, as Glx is often considered a marker of metabolic activity and the frontal lobe is a highly active brain region1.

In line with our hypothesis and previous work in young adults, age was associated with tCho concentration. tCho is a marker of membrane turnover and inflammation1 thus, increasing tCho with age likely reflects increasing inflammatory processes even with healthy aging. In this sample spanning healthy adulthood, the ACC was associated with higher tCho concentrations than the SMC. This may indicate the ACC is especially vulnerable to age-related neuroinflammation.

There was no relationship between age and tNAA concentration in this sample. Given NAA is considered a marker of neuronal viability and function1, the lack of negative association between age and NAA concentration is surprising. The majority of existing literature has found a negative association5. However, most of these studies included restricted age ranges and heterogenous regions, which make direct comparisons difficult. In this investigation, the SMC was associated with higher tNAA concentrations, which may reflect accelerated neuronal dysfunction in the ACC.

Similar to prior work, increased age was associated with higher tCr concentrations in this investigation. It may be the case that age-related energy decline leads to increased creatine production in the brain. Furthermore, in this sample of healthy adults, the SMC was associated with higher tCr concentration than the ACC. This is surprising considering tCr levels are believed to be related to the amount of activity in a given region and the frontal lobe is often considered highly active1.

Age was significantly related to mI concentration, consistent with both our hypothesis and existing literature. Myoinositol is considered a marker of osmotic stress and neurodegeneration1, so the increase in concentration with age seems logical. In this study, the ACC was associated with higher concentration of mI than the SMC. This finding fits with research investigating structural changes by region that suggest the frontal lobe may be especially vulnerable to age-related decline.

No sex differences in metabolite concentration were found for any of the metabolites, except Glx. Previous research has shown Glx to be higher in males2, which was replicated in this study. However, few studies have examined sex differences in metabolites across healthy adulthood. It is important for future studies of neurochemistry to examine sex differences in order to better understand why females are more susceptible to neurodegenerative diseases. 

 In our exploratory sub-group analysis, the impact of menstrual status (regularly vs. not regularly menstruating) showed mI in the SMC was higher in females who were not regularly menstruating compared to those that did regularly menstruate. However, this relationship did not survive the Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. It is important to note that in this sample, menstrual status was not necessarily related to age; there were several young females without regular menstrual cycles due to hormonal contraception. Given the sparsity of studies considering the menstrual cycle in neurochemical investigations, it is important for future research to be fully powered to investigate menstrual status and also consider how hormonal birth control, or hormone replacement may effect metabolites.
