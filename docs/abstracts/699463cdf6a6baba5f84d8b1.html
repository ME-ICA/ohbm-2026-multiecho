Introduction

Adolescence represents a pivotal stage the maturation and organization of the brain. These changes are linked to significant neurobiological mechanisms, influenced notably by pubertal hormones (estradiol, progesterone, testosterone) and the maturation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. The understanding of this critical period is crucial to elucidate biological foundations of sex-specific differences in behavior, cognition and predispositions to psychopathology during adolescence. A body of research has identified associations between these hormones and brain neurometabolites using magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS). However, no studies have assessed whole brain magnetic resonance spectroscopic imaging (3D-MRSI) data in a longitudinal cohort of adolescent from the general population. Building on previous work to perform voxel-based analyses (VBA) ^1 in 3D-MRSI, we analyzed maps of N-acetylaspartate (tNAA), myo-inositol (Ins), choline components (Cho), glutamine+glutamate (Glx) and creatine+phosphocreatine (tCr). Our analysis was conducted relative to pubertal and HPA hormones.

Methods

Adolescents from the general population (n=40, age 13-15, 57% females) were recruited for the Mindfulteen study in Geneva, Switzerland and were assessed at 2 or 3 different time points. Exclusion criteria included having any somatic or psychiatric disease, except for a current anxiety disorder and/or a past (>6 months) episode of major depressive disorder. 

All participants underwent brain scans (total n=99) with the 3D-MRSI customer sequence ^2 acquired on a 3T MAGNETOM Trio, Siemens Healthineers, Forcheim, Germany. The resulting metabolic maps were initially registered to each participant’s structural image, followed by a registration to a subject structural template and subsequently transformed into MNI standard space. Voxel-based analyses (VBA) were performed using the sandwich estimator (SwE) and a non-parametric cluster-based corrections (significant clusters: p < 0.05) as implemented in FSL SWE tool ^3 . Mean values of significant clusters were extracted and plotted using a Linear Mixed Effect model in R. 

Blood of participants was collected in the 7 days before or after each MRI, in the morning after an overnight fast. Hormones were measured in the serum by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS).

Results

Our findings reveal first age-relative tNAA increase within and between subjects in frontal and occipital white matter (WM). We also observed a widespread increase of Glx with age and an increase of tCr in a fronto-parietal cluster. These results, especially the tNAA and tCr increase, have already been found in previous MRS studies, and align with previous studies of neuronal and axonal maturation. 

We also observed important changes regarding hormones variation and neurometabolites. In the whole population, dehydroepiandrosterone suflate (DHEA-S), a hormone known for its important role as a precursor of sexual hormones, was negatively correlated with Cho within subjects in the parietal cortex (Fig1A) and positively correlated with Ins (Fig 1B) and tCr in widespread GM and WM between subjects. This could indicate maturational changes with a role of the HPA axis in membrane turnover and glial energy metabolism. Cortisol was found to be widely and strongly correlated with Ins and tCr levels between subjects, supporting the previous results relative to the change in glial energy metabolism.  

Relative to sex-specific hormones, we found an increase of Cho in WM and increase of tNAA in frontal GM (Fig.1D) relative to estradiol in females at the group level, indicating potential brain maturation with this hormone that has previously been highlighted as protective in psychiatric diseases. In males, Glx (Fig.1C) and tNAA was found widely correlated with testosterone within subject, indicating potential specific neuronal paths relative to testosterone levels at the subject level. Estradiol could therefore lead brain maturation relative to other females, but testosterone could be a marker of change relative to the subject and not the population.  

Conclusion

Taken together, these preliminary results show different paths in neurometabolites’ levels during adolescence relative to age, sexual hormones and HPA axis steroids. They also advocate for different brain changes relative to sex and hormonal maturation. These could help design sex-specific and biomarker specific interventions in adolescents, relative to their brain development and metabolism.
