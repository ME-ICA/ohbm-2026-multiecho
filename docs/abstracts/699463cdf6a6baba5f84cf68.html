Introduction

To what extent do time-series foundation models (FMs) learn representations that generalize to complex clinical populations, such as patients with disorders of consciousness (DoC)? Recent advances in time-series FMs have demonstrated impressive performance on standard metrics. However, these metrics fail to assess whether the learned embeddings preserve clinically relevant features. For DoC patients, a domain characterized by substantial clinical heterogeneity and pronounced signal noise, ensuring faithful representation learning is particularly crucial. To address this challenge, we propose a rigorous evaluation framework for DoC patients that comprises three complementary assessments.

First, we quantify temporal preservation of signal structure using time-resolved decoding to discriminate original from reconstructed trials. Second, we assess neurophysiological validity by measuring retention of established consciousness markers. Third, we evaluate diagnostic utility by training classifiers on original and reconstructed scalar markers to distinguish between consciousness diagnostic states.

We applied this evaluation framework to TOTEM, a pretrained time-series foundation model.1,2 The data encompassed patients with DoC and healthy controls during the Local-Global Paradigm (LG)3, plus resting-state data from controls. Our temporal analysis revealed that while reconstructed signals remained indiscriminable from originals in control subjects during resting state, they became temporally discriminable during the LG protocol in both controls and DoC patients, indicating the model fails to preserve signal structure during stimulus-locked paradigms. In contrast, topographic correlations were preserved for most consciousness markers except Evoked ones, with scalar reconstructed markers suggesting a retention of diagnostic information.

Methods

EEG data was from 161 patients with DoC, including patients in emergence from minimally conscious state (EMCS), minimally conscious state (MCS), unresponsive wakefulness syndrome (UWS), and coma. Data was collected during a LG auditory paradigm3, which evaluates the capacity to detect auditory irregularities. Each trial consists of presenting sequences of five auditory tones and lasts a total of 1.54 seconds. In addition to the DoC patients, we also considered control subjects, with 37 of them recorded during the Local-Global Protocol and 14 recorded during resting state. Data was acquired at 250 Hz from 256 EEG channels, preprocessed with standard filtering (0.5-45 Hz bandpass), artifact rejection, and baseline correction. 

For our implementation, we evaluated TOTEM, a FM based on a Vector Quantized Variational Autoencoder (VQVAE) architecture.1,2 The VQVAE comprises an encoder that maps continuous multivariate signals to a discrete latent space, a learned codebook of embedding vectors, and a decoder that reconstructs signals from these embeddings. We used a VQVAE codebook pretrained on weather, electricity and traffic datasets, and performed zero-shot evaluation with the aforementioned data. 

In our first assessment (Fig. 1a), we tested temporal preservation of signal structure using time-resolved decoding,4 extrapolating these tools from trial-type to reconstruction differentiation. For each subject we trained a logistic regression at every time point to distinguish original from reconstructed trials, using channel values as features. This resulted in a ROC AUC curve per subject, which can be interpreted as the temporal decoder's capacity to discriminate between the two types of signals. A high AUC value suggests that the reconstruction process systematically introduces or removes information making the reconstruction differentiable from the original signal. Conversely, when the AUC value approaches 0.5, we can say that both signal types contain non-distinguishable information content. 

In our second assessment (Fig. 1b), we evaluated preservation of established scalar and topographic consciousness biomarkers. These markers were computed using the Junifer feature extraction tool applied to EEG 5,6,7 and comprised four main categories: (1) Spectral, including Alpha and Delta Power Spectral Density and Spectral Entropy; (2) Connectivity; (3) Evoked, including Mismatch Negativity (MMN), P3b, and Contingent Negative Variation (CNV), which are specific to the LG paradigm; and (4) Information Theory markers, including Kolmogorov Complexity and Permutation Entropy.

In our third assessment (Fig. 1c), we evaluated diagnostic utility using a previously reported method to assess both signal types' capacity to distinguish UWS from MCS patients.7  We trained linear Support Vector Machine classifiers on the computed scalar markers, with hyperparameters optimized via grid search. We then evaluated four training-testing combinations: OO (original train and test), OR (original train, reconstructed test), RO (reconstructed train, original test), and RR (reconstructed train and test).

Results

Firstly, regarding the temporal decoder, the resulting AUC curves (Fig. 2a) reveal two distinct patterns corresponding to the recording protocol: LG or RS. During the LG paradigm, temporal discrimination remained above 0.55 AUC baseline, with all curves showing statistical significance against null distributions. Discrimination peaked prior to the first stimulus at -0.1 s, likely reflecting baseline artifacts from preprocessing. Notably, while EMCS, COMA, and control subjects exhibited additional peaks following each of the five stimuli, these temporal peaks were absent or markedly reduced in MCS and UWS patients. In contrast, control subjects during RS showed AUC values near 0.5 across all timepoints. These different results suggest the model fails to preserve stimulus-locked temporal dynamics intrinsic to the LG paradigm. However, in a simpler setting like RS, the model does not introduce systematic alterations detectable by the decoder. 

Regarding the topographic consciousness markers (Fig. 2b shows 8 of 21 computed topomarkers), analysis revealed that for all spectral, connectivity and information theory markers except spectral entropy, the topographies significantly correlated between original and reconstructed data. In contrast, regarding the evoked markers, 5 of the 11 did not correlate significantly between original and reconstructed ones. The results for evoked markers align with findings from the temporal decoder, which revealed systematic alterations to stimulus-locked responses during the LG paradigm. Overall, while the model introduces temporal distortions that compromise the reconstruction of evoked markers, which depend on precise stimulus timing, it successfully preserves the topographic structure of spectral, connectivity, and information theory biomarkers across the scalp.

Lastly, with regard to the clinical utility of scalar markers from both original and reconstructed signals (Fig. 2c), the ROC-AUC results show an AUC of 0.800 for models trained and tested on reconstructed signals (RR), 0.771 for models trained and tested on original signals (OO), 0.743 for models trained on original signals and tested on reconstructed signals (OR), and 0.729 for models trained on reconstructed signals and tested on original signals (RO). To investigate whether these differences reflected genuine information loss, we calculated the difference in predicted probabilities for each subject across test folds. Our analysis revealed no statistically significant difference (Wilcoxon ranked-sum test, two-sided) in model probability predictions when trained on one marker type and tested on both types, and when models were trained on different marker types but tested on the same type. 

Conclusion

Our comprehensive evaluation pipeline demonstrates that while the model introduces systematic temporal alterations during the Local-Global protocol, it still successfully preserves many key neurophysiological properties. Specifically, the reconstructions maintain the topographic structure of most spectral, connectivity, and information theory markers, and suggest a retention of diagnostic features sufficient for patient-level classification between consciousness states (UWS vs MCS). These results indicate that the current model differentially captures spontaneous versus stimulus-locked neural dynamics.

These findings open a dual path forward in the application of deep learning to clinical neuroscience: on one hand, they suggest the potential for leveraging such models to enhance our understanding of neurophysiological markers and possibly refine them through learned representations; on the other hand, the temporal distortions in event-related responses expose the urgent need for domain-specific validation frameworks. Our proposed pipeline, grounded in neuroscience principles and clinical relevance, provides one such framework. By systematically assessing temporal fidelity, topographic preservation, and diagnostic utility, it offers a generalizable methodology for evaluating foundation models before their deployment in clinical settings. Ultimately, this work establishes a template for rigorous model evaluation that can be extended to other time-series foundation models, recording modalities, and clinical populations.
