Introduction

Resolving neural activity across cortical layers enables the elucidation of feedforward and feedback neural processing in the sensory cortices. Methods using non-invasive measurements, including scalp EEG and MEG, have limited resolution due to the distance between neural current sources and sensors. Laminar probe 1,2 and high-resolution functional magnetic resonance imaging 3 can resolve neural and vascular responses across cortical depths. However, they are challenged by a limited spatial coverage and venous confound, respectively.

    In this study, we propose the method of source modeling of the stereo-electroencephalograph (SEEG), an ensemble of electrodes implanted in the brain of epilepsy patients, to estimate neural activity across cortical depths. Different from cortical-depth dependent fMRI, this method has no vascular reactivity confound. Compared to recordings using a depth-resolving electrode, the proposed SEEG source modeling can extend the neural current estimates across cortical depths over the whole brain and a broader group of patients. Here, we present the method of SEEG source modeling to estimate neural activity in the primary and secondary auditory cortices under simple audio, visual, and audiovisual stimuli to demonstrate the feasibility and advantage in neuroscience studies.

Methods

Data were acquired from SEEG measurements on refractory epilepsy patients. All patients gave written informed consent before participating in the experiment. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Taipei Veteran General Hospital. Sixteen patients were presented with auditory (A), visual (V), or audiovisual (AV) stimuli in a randomized order with an average inter-stimulus interval of 2 s. l (ISI; mean 2 s). We used an oddball design with visual and auditory target stimuli of 10% probability. In the A trials, the non-target stimulus was a 300-ms white-noise burst and the target stimulus an equally long 440 Hz pure tone. In V trials, the non-target stimuli consisted of static checkerboard stimuli (300 ms duration), and the target was the same checkerboard overlapped with a central black diamond pattern. In AV trials, the participant heard and saw the combinations of A and V stimuli; the AV target was the combination of a pure tone and a checkerboard with a black diamond. The onset of the V stimulus preceded the A onset by 48 ms. Two six-minute runs of data were collected from each patient with 100 A, 100 V, and 100 AV stimuli. 

SEEG electrodes were placed solely based on the clinical need of each patient to identify epileptogenic zones. The participants with epilepsy were implanted with from 6 to 17 electrodes (0.3 mm diameter, 5-mm spacing; 6, 8, or 10 contacts per electrode Ad-Tech, Racine, WI, USA). SEEG data were collected over contacts on electrodes (6 to 17 electrodes, 0.3 mm diameter, 5-mm spacing; 6, 8, or 10 contacts per electrode, Ad-Tech, Racine, WI, USA) at 2,048 Hz. Two sets of anatomical MRIs were obtained before and after the implantation surgery of each participant with epilepsy to localize the electrodes. SEEG preprocessing was described in our previous publication 4. 

SEEG source modeling was conducted using our recently published strategy 5. The locations of electrode contacts in the individual’s brain were identified from the post-surgery MRI using our in-house Matlab (Mathworks Inc., Natick MA, USA). The SEEG electrode contact locations were then registered to the pre-surgery MRI, which was used to build Boundary Element Models required for the lead field calculation and to define locations of potential neural current sources. The inner-skull, outer-skull, and outer-scalp surfaces for the BEMs were automatically reconstructed from the pre-surgery MRI by using FreeSurfer (http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu). The cortical source locations were created over 10 equi-distance surfaces between the pial and gray/white matter boundary using mris_expand in FreeSurfer. At each cortical depth, a source space was created to have source locations separated by approximately 5 mm using MNE toolbox (https://mne.tools/stable/index.html). Three orthogonal neural current dipoles in +x, +y, and +z directions were created at each source location. The lead fields were calculated by using the OpenMEEG package (https://openmeeg.github.io/)6,7 with the relative conductivity values for air, scalp, brain parenchyma, and skull being 0, 1, 1, and 0.0125, respectively. Current sources at each location and each depth were estimated by the minimum-norm estimate (MNE), where the statistical significance was evaluated by normalizing current estimates by the pre-stimulus interval to generate dynamic statistical parametric maps (dSPM). We specifically examined the neural currents at the primary (A1) and secondary (A2) auditory cortices with labels defined by the Human Connectome Project (HCP) multimodal parcellation (MMP1) atlas 8. Results

The left column in Figure 2 shows the estimated neural time courses in the primary (A1) and secondary (A2) auditory cortices across cortical depths. In A condition, these time courses aligned with each other in the early interval (< 100 ms) after the stimulation onset. At the peak of the evoked responses around 150 ms, discernible significances were found across depths, with the strongest one at the white/gray boundary and the weakest one at the pial surface, respectively. Intermediate (250 ms) and a later peak around 400 ms responses were more variable across cortical depths, with the largest and least significant current at the white/gray matter boundary and pial surface, respectively. Responses later than 500 ms were also variable across cortical depths; However, the most significant one became the pial surface neural current. Between A1 and A2, the time courses and their variability across cortical depths were similar. Comparing between A and AV conditions, A1 and A2 had less difference across cortical depths at early (150 ms) and intermediate (250 ms) peaks in AV condition than A condition. However, the response variability across cortical depths was larger after 500 ms in the AV than A condition.  The middle and right columns in Figure 2 show the zoomed-in spatial distributions of significance of the estimated neural current across cortical depths in A and AV conditions around the left and right auditory cortices at 150 ms, 250 ms and 700 ms, respectively.

Conclusion

We presented the method to estimate neural current across cortical depths using realistic high-resolution MRI. Our method still belongs to the family of the ill-posed inverse problem, because the SEEG macroelectrodes cannot resolve neural activity across cortical depths unambiguously. However, compared to MEG or EEG over the scalp, SEEG electrode contacts were close to the region of interest (auditory cortex in this study). This proximity and multiple measurements within the brain (in the range of 50 to 100 per patient) improved the sensitivity and reduced the ill-posedness of the inverse solution.

    The estimated neural waveforms exhibited more variable activities only after 150 ms, suggesting that 1) the differences in neural current estimates across cortical depths were not because of a systemic bias in the solution. If so, the difference across depths would persist for the whole post-stimulus interval, and 2) early feedforward currents were less divergent than late feedback re-entrant currents to various cortical depths 9, demonstrating the advantage of SEEG source modeling across cortical depths in elucidating laminar neurophysiology.

    MEG source modeling has been suggested to resolve neural activity between superficial and deep cortical surfaces from theoretical 10,11, device-dependent 12, and modeling 13,14 perspectives. Our approach complements the MEG ones by the sensitivity difference between electric potentials and magnetic fields and by the shorter distance between electrodes and neural source locations. Further analysis on the spatial resolution via simulations and localizing oscillatory neural current components across cortical depths will clarify the confidence of resolving neural activity across cortical depths using SEEG measurements.
