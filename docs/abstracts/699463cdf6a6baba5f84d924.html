Introduction

Gain control is a key property of cortical neurons and is associated with sensory and cognitive functions ranging from detection to selective attention. Deficits in gain control are related to psychiatric and neurophysiological disorders. Thus, understanding gain control provides key insights into cortical function, and the noninvasive modulation of gain has potential clinical implications. 

Previous research has shown that oscillatory neuromodulation via sensory stimulation can induce long-term changes in neural responses, akin to long-term potentiation, and improve visual acuity1. Moreover, entrained neural responses to repetitive visual stimulation propagate beyond the primary visual cortex2, suggesting changes in a large-scale cortical network3. These findings suggest that sensory entrainment may modulate cortical processing and behavior. 

We investigated the effects of visual entrainment to full-field sinusoidal flicker across a range of frequencies. We recorded neural activity using high-density electroencephalography (EEG) and quantified neural gain as the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of power at the flicker frequency or its 2nd harmonic relative to neighboring frequencies. Our findings show an increase in gain at electrode sites spanning dorsally from occipital-parietal to frontal locations, for frequencies in the 10 to 20 Hz range. Gain at frequencies outside this range either remained stable or decreased across trials.

Methods

We investigated the effectiveness of full-field repeated visual stimulation at different frequencies on inducing neural responses and changes in gain. Thirty-three participants attended multiple EEG sessions (257 channels sampled at 1000 Hz)  during which they passively fixated a display flickering sinusoidally at one of five different frequencies (1, 5, 10, 20, or 40 Hz) for 2 seconds per trial (52.07 ± 1.25 trials per flicker condition: mean ± se). An eyetracker monitored eye movements;  trials were terminated and excluded from further analysis if large eye movements occurred.

We followed the EEGPrep pipeline to preprocess the data, including detrending, line-noise filtering, detection of noisy channels, and robust average re-referencing. Epochs were taken from -0.15 to 2 s around the flicker onset, baselined to the average of the pre-flicker signal, and detrended. To focus on the steady-state response to visual flicker, we analyzed only the last 1.5 seconds of each epoch. We estimated power spectra per epoch and channel via multi-taper spectral analysis (N*W = 2, each frequency bin represents the average power within  [-W, W] Hz around its center, where 2W = ~2.67 Hz) at 0.1-Hz intervals. To quantify the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), we first calculated the noise background as the mean power spectrum within a 3.67-Hz window centered at each frequency, excluding the immediate 2.67-Hz window. SNR was defined as the ratio of the signal to the background noise. To determine the peak frequency associated with the stimulation, we used a peak-finding algorithm within a [-0.5, 0.5] Hz window around the stimulation frequency or its second harmonic. The SNRs of noisy channels were interpolated based on good channels, weighted by their inverse Euclidean distance squared.

Our primary hypothesis was that neuronal gain (here quantified as the SNR) increased with exposure to visual flicker and that this effect would depend on flicker frequency. We used linear mixed-effects models (LMM) to test this hypothesis for each electrode, excluding the online reference electrode Cz.

log10(SNR) ~ fstimulus* harmonic_no * trial_no + (1 | subject) + (1 | subject:session_date)

where fstimulus denotes the stimulus frequency. The fixed-effects terms were selected a priori in accordance with our hypotheses. The random-effects terms were chosen via a series of stepwise simulated likelihood-ratio tests (103 iterations), in which we fitted nested basic and alternative models to the average SNR across the central occipital electrodes. To control the family-wise error rate due to collinearity in the EEG signal, we used a threshold-free cluster enhancement (TFCE) procedure, comparing the observed t-statistics to a null distribution obtained from 104 permutations for each of the 16 fixed-effects coefficients in the model. 

Results

Figure 1 shows a topoplot of the strength of the neural response (rows 1 & 2) and the change in gain across the session (rows 3 & 4) for each flicker frequency (columns). The color coding represents the beta coefficients of the linear-mixed model. Disks highlight channels with significant coefficients (p < .05). Note that the LMM used reference-coding for categorical variables, with 5 Hz as the reference. As a consequence, the first column shows the effects at 5 Hz, while columns 2 to 4 show the additive effects specific to the stimulation frequency of that column.

The first row shows, not surprisingly, that each stimulus frequency successfully induced a following response at its fundamental frequency. The response was stronger for 10 and 20 Hz and most pronounced in the posterior electrodes. The peak response appears to travel dorsally as the stimulation frequency increases (compare fstim = 5 Hz peaking around Oz and fstim=40 Hz peaking around Pz). The second-harmonic response (second row) shows a global positive response at 10 Hz (2nd harmonic) during 5-Hz stimulation, but a reduction at 20, 40, and 80 Hz during 10-, 20-, and 40-Hz stimulation, respectively.

The third row shows the main effects of exposure to flicker throughout the session (i.e., the fixed effect of trial_no in the model and its interaction with stimulus frequency). SNR at the fundamental was stable at 5 Hz and 40 Hz, but increased at 10 Hz and 20 Hz. These increases in gain were significant in both the posterior and frontal electrode clusters.  The fourth row shows how exposure to one frequency affected the gain at the 2nd harmonic. Stimulation at 5 Hz led to a significant increase in gain at 10 Hz at the dorsal occipito-parietal and frontal electrodes. Stimulation at 20 Hz decreased gain at the second harmonic (40 Hz) for dorsal occipitoparietal and frontal electrodes. 

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that while visual flicker consistently entrains cortical activity across a wide range of frequencies, the modulation of neural gain across a session is frequency-dependent. We observed a significant increase in gain over time exclusively at 10 Hz and 20 Hz. Crucially, the enhancement at 10 Hz occurred regardless of whether this was the fundamental stimulation frequency or an internally generated second harmonic (i.e., the 5 Hz stimulation driving a growing 10 Hz response). 

This specificity supports a resonance account, in which an external drive that generates intrinsic alpha (10 Hz) or beta (20 Hz) rhythms elicits amplification of responses. The spatial propagation of these effects to occipitoparietal and frontal electrodes suggests that prolonged visual entrainment recruits and boosts cortical networks beyond the primary visual cortex.
