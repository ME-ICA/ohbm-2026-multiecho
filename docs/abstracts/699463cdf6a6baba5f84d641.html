Introduction

Working memory (WM) training has long been proposed as a promising route to enhance broad cognitive abilities, due to the central role of WM in attention, learning, and intelligence. Yet more than two decades of research has produced a striking paradox: despite WM’s domain-general importance, WM training rarely yields reliable transfer to untrained skills.^1

To resolve this, we propose a network-based model of transfer, centered on two key factors. First, effective transfer likely requires “bridging”—brief practice on the transfer task—to scaffold connections between strengthened domain-general networks and task-specific circuits. Second, diversity in WM training tasks may amplify engagement of domain-general networks and thereby increase the readiness of these systems for transfer. 

Here, we tested these predictions through a large-scale, ecologically grounded gamified intervention. Across three experiments with nearly 700  typically developing children, we implemented a hybrid remote-supervised protocol: motivation-calibrated school-based pre-testing, followed by at-home online WM training and bridging. Study 1 assessed whether diverse WM training combined with a single session of bridging facilitates transfer on a novel WM task, compared with equally engaging speed & perceptual (SP) training. Study 2 increased the number of bridging sessions and incorporated multiple active control groups to examine whether enhanced transfer extends to non-verbal reasoning (NVR). Study 3 systematically manipulated the degree of training diversity while keeping bridging constant, allowing us to test whether diversity within WM tasks specifically promotes transfer across attention, episodic memory, and reasoning. Together, these experiments provide strong support for the network-based framework, demonstrating that the combination of diverse WM training and post-training bridging produces reliable and broad far-transfer effects.

Methods

Overview. All three studies conducted with similar procedural frameworks but distinct training regimens and transfer assessments. Across all studies, training was delivered remotely via a scheduled online platform. All participants were primary school children (grades 3–5; approximate age range 9–11 years). Written informed consent was obtained online from parents or guardians prior to participation. The studies were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the State Key Laboratory of Cognitive Neuroscience and Learning, Beijing Normal University.

Study 1 

Participants. After applying inclusion criteria (≥75% training completion and completion of the bridging session), 177 participants were included in the analysis across two groups: active control (n=112) and WM training group (n=65).

Procedures. The training procedure for the training group included 24 sessions (30 minutes each) of WM training. The control group, on the other hand, underwent 24 matched SP training sessions. The participants were asked to complete three transfer tasks - two near-transfer (spatial span, symmetry span) and one far-transfer (digit 3-back)—at pre-test, intermediate-test, and post-test. Training was accessible for 6–8 weeks after the pre-test. Participants then had two weeks to sequentially complete the intermediate-test, bridging session, and post-test. 

Study 2

Participants. We included participants who completed over 75% of training and all bridging/additional sessions, yielding 341 participants. These participants belonged to four groups: WM training with bridging (WB, n=58), SP training with bridging (SB, n=95), WM training with additional WM sessions (WW, n=67), or SP training with additional SP sessions (SS, n=121).

Procedures. Study 2 followed the same general timeline and remote delivery as Study 1, with two key modifications. First, the bridging phase was extended to three sessions to be completed within two weeks. Second, the transfer battery included one near-transfer task: spatial span; two far-transfer tasks: adaptive digit n-back, NVR.   

Study 3

Participants. After applying inclusion criteria, 181 participants were included across four groups: active control (Control, n=50), single-task WM training (ST, n=49), single-domain WM training (SD, n=40), and multi-domain training (MD, n=42).

Procedures. Study 3 employed a similar phased design (pre-test → training → intermediate-test → bridging → post-test) but with several refinements. To reduce participant burden, the number of training sessions was decreased from 24 to 20. And transfer tasks included 3 far-transfer tasks: multiple object tracking (MOT), continuous recognition task (CRT), and NVR. 

Data Analyze

Outliers (>3×IQR) were excluded (max 2.1% removed), and missing data were not imputed but retained in the mixed‑model framework. Bonferroni correction was applied for each research question.

Transfer Effects. For each task, a mixed‑effects model was fitted with performance as the dependent variable, group, phase, gender, and age as fixed effects, and user ID and school as random intercepts: 

Index ~ Group * (Phase + Gender + Age) + (1|user_id) + (1|school).

Learning Rate. In Studies 2 and 3, learning rates were estimated by fitting individual learning curves to sequential test data (pre‑, intermediate‑, three bridging sessions, and post‑test) for NVR and adaptive n‑back tasks, then compared across groups.

Results

Study 1

To assess training-related transfer, we fitted linear mixed models for each of the three transfer tasks using data from the pre- and intermediate-test phases. The group by phase interaction served as our primary effect of interest, which indicates significant transfer effect. The result indicated that on both near-transfer tasks significant transfer effect emerged (both <0.001). But there was no significant transfer effect for the digit 3-back task (F (1,169.80) = 0.46, p = 0.50,  ηp2= 0.003). 

To test whether a bridging session can promote far transfer, we fitted linear mixed models for task performance across pre- and post-test phases. Supporting our hypothesis, there was a significant transfer effect for digit 3-back task after bridging (F(1,166.77) = 6.26, pcorr=0.04, ηp2=0.04). And there were still significant transfer for the near transfer tasks. 

Study 2

We first replicated the analysis of training‑induced transfer effects. We also found WM training induces a near transfer effect (F(1,302.11)=41.34, pcorr<0.001, ηp2=0.1). But no significant interaction between group and phase was found for the adaptive digit n-back task or the NVR task (both ps>0.1). 

We next analyzed transfer effects following the bridging phase. We found significant interaction between group and phase for both far transfer tasks (both ps=0.03), while bridging did not affect near-transfer effects. 

We also estimated the learning rate for every participant in NVR task. The result indicated that the WB group exhibited a significantly steeper learning rate in NVR (t(144.82)=2.32, pcorr=0.04).

Furthermore, the comparison between the WW and SB groups revealed a transfer effect only on the spatial span task (F(1,151.33)=14.05, pcorr<0.001, ηp2=0.08), but not on either far‑transfer task (both p > 0.89). And the comparison between the SB and SS groups did not yield a significant interaction on any of the three tasks (all ps>0.54).

Study 3

Comparisons of pre‑ and intermediate‑test performance between the ST, SD, MD groups and the control group revealed that all three training groups exhibited transfer effects on the CRT task (all ps<0.06), none showed transfer on the NVR task (all ps>0.10), and only the MD and SD groups displayed transfer on the MOT task (both ps<0.02).

We combined the SD and MD groups into a single diverse‑training group (MD+SD) and compared it with the ST group to test the effect of task diversity on transfer. Results revealed significant transfer effects for the diverse‑training group on both the MOT and CRT (both  ps≤ 0.09) tasks, while no significant interaction was observed for the NVR task (F(1,129) = 0.79, p = 0.37,  ηp2=0.006).

Next, we examined whether bridging would enhance transfer. For both MOT and CRT tasks, all three training groups showed significant transfer effects following bridging (all  ps≤ 0.05). But for the NVR task, only SD and MD groups (both  ps≤0.001) exhibited far transfer.

For both MOT and CRT tasks, all three training groups showed significant transfer effects following bridging (all  ps< 0.05), with post hoc tests confirming performance gains in each group (for MOT, all ps< 0.05; for CRT, all ps< 0.001)

Comparing the diverse‑training group (MD+SD) with the ST group on pre‑ to post‑test performance revealed a significant interaction for the NVR task (F (1,129) = 5.69, pcorr=0.05, ηp2=0.04), but not for the MOT or CRT tasks (both ps>0.27).

And we estimated the learning rate of Control, ST and MD+SD groups in NVR. One-way ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of group (F(2,178)=13.04, p<0.001, ηp2=0.13). Post hoc tests confirmed that the MD+SD group showed a steepest learning rate and the difference between ST and Control groups was not significant (t (178) = 2.12, pcorr=0.11).

Conclusion

Working memory is a cornerstone of general cognition, yet traditional training regimens yield inconsistent and limited transfer. We proposed a brain network-based transfer model, positing that robust far-transfer requires both enhancing the domain-general network through diverse training and rapidly integrating it with task-specific circuits via targeted bridging. Three large-scale, ecologically valid experiments confirmed this synergy: neither diversity nor bridging alone sufficed, but their combination produced reliable transfer across episodic memory, attention, executive control, and nonverbal reasoning.

By including several transfer tasks in three studies, we found a gradient of pre-bridging transfer effects across cognitive domains. Diverse WM training alone produced strong near-transfer to novel span tasks, moderate transfer to episodic recognition, weaker effects on visuospatial attention, minimal benefits for n-back updating, and no improvement in relational reasoning. This pattern aligns with our network-based transfer framework, which posits that transfer likelihood is inversely related to the complexity of reconfiguring the domain-general network with a transfer task's specific neural circuitry. 

Our findings establish bridging as a critical catalyst for far transfer, operating through two interconnected mechanisms: functional network integration and accelerated meta‑learning. From a network neuroscience perspective, bridging constructs targeted connections between strengthened domain‑general networks and the task‑specific circuits required for novel cognitive demands. This view aligns with and extends the flexible‑hub theory^2: while frontoparietal hubs dynamically reconfigure connectivity to meet task demands, bridging actively guides this reconfiguration, facilitating integration between domain‑general resources and domain‑specific requirements. This account is supported by neurophysiological evidence that brief, targeted learning can rapidly reshape coupling between multiple‑demand regions and domain‑specific networks, ^3 with high‑connectivity hubs showing the most pronounced changes. ^4 In essence, bridging supplies the specific cues needed to align the abstract control representations built during diverse training with the concrete demands of new tasks.

This network‑level mechanism is behaviorally reflected in the nature of the transfer effects. The observation that transfer is best characterized by steeper learning rates—rather than immediate performance gains—indicates that bridging unlocks a latent meta‑learning capacity, enabling participants to learn novel tasks more efficiently, consistent with reports that action video‑game training enhances perceptual learning rates.^5

Our findings also clarify the role of diversity in cognitive transfer. While task diversity is essential, its efficacy depends entirely on implementation level. Only paradigm‑level diversity within a domain (e.g., varied WM tasks) or domain‑level diversity across cognitive constructs (e.g., WM plus inhibition) produced broad transfer, especially when followed by bridging, ultimately extending to nonverbal reasoning. In contrast, stimulus‑level diversity—altering surface features but not task structure—yielded only limited, near‑transfer benefits. Notably, domain‑level diversity did not surpass paradigm‑level diversity, suggesting that depth of structural variation within a core domain can be as impactful as breadth across domains.
