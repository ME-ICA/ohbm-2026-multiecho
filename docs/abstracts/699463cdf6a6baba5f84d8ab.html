Introduction

Diffusion MRI (dMRI) tractography has established itself as a benchmark tool for representing white matter fibres and studying brain connections. However, it relies on an indirect model based on the diffusion of water molecules, making it essential to validate it using anatomical references. In this context, the use of preclinical models, such as the mouse brain, allows direct comparison with data obtained using invasive methods.

In order to validate tractography of the murine white matter, we have developed two complementary tools. The first, nf-mouse, is a diffusion data processing pipeline currently adapted to ex vivo mouse brains, derived from the TractoFlow^1 pipeline initially developed for humans. The second, m2m^2 allows the extraction and processing of projection maps from viral tracers acquired by serial two-photon tomography in the Allen Mouse Brain Atlas (AMBA)^3. The combination of these two tools makes it possible to compare streamlines generated by tractography with anatomical ground truth derived from viral injections, thus providing a robust methodological framework for validating white matter tractography in mice.

Methods

Nf-mouse is an open-source and standardised Nextflow-DSL2^4 pipeline specifically designed to address the current heterogeneity in processing dMRI data in rodent studies. It offers a robust, automated workflow that includes essential preprocessing steps such as denoising, eddy current and bias correction, brain segmentation, and the reconstruction of both diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) and q-ball imaging (Fig. 1). Importantly, it incorporates the AMBA to enable anatomically guided extraction of specific diffusion metrics and tractography bundles. Nf-mouse is highly adaptable to different dataset types, configurable via JSON, and optimised for reproducibility, transparency, and ease of use, making it a valuable tool for preclinical imaging research.

The Python-based m2m tool is a complementary suite of nf-mouse, developed to extend the analytical possibilities offered by the AMBA. It enables interoperability between Allen and user data spaces by leveraging the Allen Software Development Kit (AllenSDK) for data import and ANTsPyX for high-precision image registration. Using this framework, we compute a transformation matrix to map AMBA-derived projection density maps onto the native space of user dMRI data, facilitating direct visual and analytical comparison such as overlaying tractography with viral tracer data. Conversely, the inverse transformation allows researchers to trace any given white matter location in their own data back to its corresponding region and experimental context within the Allen Mouse Brain Common Coordinate Framework. 

As a proof-of-concept, we processed one ex vivo C57BL/6 mouse brain scanned at an isotropic resolution of 52 µm on a 7T Bruker Biospec 70/20 system preclinical scanner using a surface cryoprobe coil 4-element (2x2) for the signal reception. The dMRI multi-shot Spin Echo EPI sequence parameters were: TE= 23.4 ms; TR = 400 ms; flip angle = 90°, 5 b0 and 60 directions at b=3000 s/mm2. 

Results

Nf-mouse first provides the fractional anisotropy, mean, axial and radial diffusion maps (cf. examples in Fig. 2A.1). The precise registration of the AMBA in the diffusion space allows the extraction of these diffusion metric values for each AMBA anatomical label. It also enables anatomically constrained deterministic tractography based on Q-ball Imaging^5 using the AMBA fiber tracts labels as a seeding mask (10 seeds per voxel) and the entire brain as a tracking mask (Fig. 2A.2). Specific tracts are then automatically extracted from the whole brain tractogram (Fig. 2A.3).

In order to assess the anatomical relevance of the obtained whole brain tractogram, a comparison is made between reconstructed fibres and ground truth derived from neural tracing data (Fig. 2B). In the current example, a projection map obtained with a viral injection in the medial septal complex (MS) was imported using m2m (experiment 100141597) and then compared to streamlines passing through the fornix and hippocampal commissure. The analysis shows that 73% of the reconstructed fibres belong to the mask derived from the ground truth, indicating good anatomical concordance. These results support the feasibility and relevance of the proposed approach for general validation of tractography at the ex vivo mouse brain scale.

Conclusion

Together, nf-mouse and m2m offer a comprehensive and interoperable toolkit that significantly advances rodent neuroimaging research. By enabling standardised preprocessing and reproducible analysis workflows, along with interactive and bidirectional mapping between diffusion and tracer-based datasets, they open new opportunities for exploring structural connectivity. This integration not only enhances tractography interpretation but also supports its validation against tracer-derived ground truth, fostering deeper insights into white matter organisation in the mouse brain.
