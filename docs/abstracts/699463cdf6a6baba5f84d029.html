Introduction

Functional gradients of connectivity, which summarise the high-dimensional functional connectivity into low-dimensional maps, reflect fundamental principles of brain organisation.^1 In adults, the primary gradient, which captures the largest variation axis in functional connectivity, spans from transmodal association networks to the primary sensorimotor and visual networks, and is altered in individuals with neurodevelopmental conditions.^2 While well-characterised in early childhood and adults, the emergence and development of functional gradients in neonates remain largely unknown. Long-range connections, which are crucial for integrating distributed brain regions, are aligned with functional gradients in adults.^3 long-range connections preferentially link transmodal association regions, whereas sensorimotor regions are dominated by short-range connectivity. In this study, we used the developing Human Connectome Project (dHCP) dataset to characterise functional connectivity gradients in neonates and assess whether the perinatal emergence of long-range functional connections shapes functional gradients.

Methods

We used resting-state fMRI data from 394 neonates (325 term-born, 69 preterm-born) imaged at term-equivalent age (postmenstrual age at scan ≥ 37 weeks) from the dHCP. A whole-brain atlas was constructed by combining cortical regions from the Schaefer atlas (400 parcels), subcortical regions from the dHCP template, and cerebellar regions from the SUIT atlas. The atlas was transformed to the extended dHCP 40-week template space. Cortical parcels were assigned to the Yeo-7 sub-networks. Subcortical and cerebellar parcels were treated as separate sub-networks, resulting in 9 sub-networks in total. Functional connectivity was computed as the pairwise Pearson’s correlation of average timeseries between parcels, with only positive correlations retained. Proportional thresholding was applied at different network densities (5% to 50%, at 1% steps).

To derive neonatal functional gradients, we applied a 10% row-wise thresholding to the functional connectivity matrix and calculated cosine similarity between the functional connectivity profiles of each pair of nodes. Diffusion map embedding was used to reduce the high-dimensional similarity matrix to low-dimensional gradients. Individual gradients were aligned to a group template using Procrustes rotation. The group template was defined from the group-averaged functional connectivity matrix of the 23 oldest term-born neonates (PMA > 44 weeks). For each neonate, maturation scores were computed as the Spearman’s rank correlation between the individual gradient map and the group template gradients.

The first three neonatal gradients, explaining 39.6%, 15.4% and 12.1% of the variance respectively, were selected in this study. The third gradient showed a similar pattern to the primary gradient in adults.^1 To compare the third functional gradient in neonates with the primary functional gradient in adults, Spearman’s rank correlation was performed. Data for the primary functional gradient in adults were downloaded from Sydnor et al.^4 (see Figure 1).

Connection lengths were estimated using white matter distance, the shortest distance through the interior white matter volume, and categorised into short- (< 40 mm), middle- (40-80 mm), and long-range (> 80 mm) connections. For each node, normalised functional connectivity strength (normalised FCS) within each distance bin was computed as the sum of strength (i.e., the pairwise Pearson’s correlation coefficients) to all other nodes in that bin divided by the node’s total strength. Normalised FCS reflects the proportion of short-, middle-, and long-range connections for each node. At the network level, normalised FCS was obtained by averaging nodal values within each network.

First, we examined the development of gradient maturation scores and normalised FCS of short-, middle-, and long-range connections. Second, to examine how long- and short-range connections shape the development of neonatal functional gradients, we computed Spearman’s rank correlations between functional gradient maps and normalised FCS maps at both the individual and group levels. At the individual level, correlations were computed between individual normalised FCS maps and gradient maps. At the group level, correlations were calculated between the group-averaged normalised FCS map (23 oldest term-born neonates, PMA > 44 weeks) and the group template of functional gradient.

Results

The first three neonatal gradients (G1, G2, and G3), explaining 39.6%, 15.4% and 12.1% of the variance respectively, were selected (Figure 2a). The third gradient, spanning from the default-mode network (DMN) to the visual network, showed significant correspondence with the primary gradient in adults^1 (Spearman’s r = 0.656, pspin < 0.001, 1000 spin tests; Figure 2b). Maturation scores for G1, G2, and G3 significantly increased with PMA at scan (all p values < 0.001), after controlling for preterm birth status (term-/preterm-born), sex and in-scan head motion (FD outliers).

The proportion of long-range connections increased across most sub-networks, with significant increases in the DMN and frontoparietal sub-networks (FPN) (all p values < 0.05, FDR corrected across 9 sub-networks; Figure 1c). A significant decrease in the proportion of short-range connections was found in the visual sub-network (p = 0.045, FDR corrected across 9 sub-networks; Figure 2c).

The G1 template was negatively associated with the group-averaged normalised FCS map of long-range connections (Spearman’s r = -0.562, p < 0.001, pspin = 0.002, 1000 spin tests for cortical regions; Figure 2d), but positively associated with the group-averaged normalised FCS map of short-range connections (Spearman’s r = 0.535, p < 0.001, pspin < 0.001, 1000 spin tests for cortical regions; Figure 2d). At the individual level, Spearman’s rank correlations between G1 and normalised FCS maps were significantly negative for long-range connections and significantly positive for short-range connections (all p values < 0.001).

The G3 template was negatively associated with the group-averaged normalised FCS map of short-range connections (Spearman’s r = -0.408, p < 0.001, pspin = 0.005, 1000 spin tests for cortical regions; Figure 2d). At the individual level, Spearman’s rank correlations between G3 and normalised FCS map of short-range connections were significantly negative (p < 0.001). No significant association was found with long-range connections (p > 0.05).

No significant association was found between the G2 template and the group-averaged normalised FCS maps of long-, middle-, or short-range connections (all p values > 0.05).

Conclusion

We identified neonatal functional gradients and demonstrated how distance-dependent functional connectivity underpins their development. Among the few studies that have characterised functional gradients in neonates, the first gradient was reported to span from sensorimotor to visual networks and the second gradient from posterior to anterior regions.^5 Consistent with these findings, our first gradient also anchored at the sensorimotor network on one end, with the visual toward the other end. Importantly, we identified a third gradient (DMN-visual network) that shows significant correspondence to the primary gradient in adults, indicating the early emergence of macroscale brain organisation (primary-transmodal axis). During early development, the proportions of long-range connections increased, with notable increases in the default-mode and frontoparietal networks. Distance-dependent functional connectivity shapes the first and third gradients: regions with a higher proportion of short-range connections showed higher G1 scores, while regions with lower proportions of short-range connections (or a higher proportion of middle- and long-range connections) showed higher G3 scores. These findings suggest that the balance between short- and long-range functional connections shapes the early development of neonatal functional connectivity gradients. This likely reflects an intertwined mechanism: establishment of long-range connectivity shaping macroscale brain organisation and vice versa.
