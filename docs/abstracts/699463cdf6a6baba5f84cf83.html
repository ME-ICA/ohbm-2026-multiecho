Introduction

Diffusion weighted imaging (DWI) is widely used to characterize microstructural properties of the brain in health and in diseases including Alzheimer’s (AD) and Parkinson’s disease (PD). Despite its valuable insights, DWI serves as a proxy of underlying biological processes as it lacks biophysical specificity1. Elucidating the biological and pathophysiological underpinnings of DWI in healthy and disease states is crucial to unlock its potential as a non-invasive tool for the diagnosis and prognosis of neurological disorders. Validating DWI findings with gold-standard histology is achievable by applying both modalities on the same ex-vivo brain specimens. However, the direct application of DWI to ex-vivo is methodologically challenging as acquisition protocols and processing pipelines are developed for in-vivo use, and cannot be directly translated to ex-vivo settings. This challenge arises due to changes observed in ex-vivo DWI magnetic signals following brain extraction and fixation2. The development of optimised and reliable DWI is therefore a necessary step, enabling the comparison of in-vivo DWI with ex-vivo DWI as well as ex-vivo DWI with histology, all on the same brain. Such an approach permits the translation of findings from in-vivo DWI to histology with ex-vivo DWI as the intermediate step. This work sought to establish how well in-vivo DWI translates to the ex-vivo contexts by quantifying its long-term fixation-related changes, and comparing DWI protocols for ex-vivo use. To do so, we used specimens from the Douglas Brain Bank (DBB), the largest Canadian collection of donated human brains.

Methods

One hemisphere of each extracted brain was fixed by immersion in 10% formalin and placed in an MRI-compatible container. Postmortem imaging was performed on a Siemens Prisma Fit 3T clinical scanner at the Cerebral Imaging Center of the Douglas Research Centre, using a 64-channel head and neck coil and a cerebellum-first bore orientation. First, we assessed the effect of long-term fixation on the DWI signal and its metrics, mean diffusivity (MD) and fractional anisotropy (FA), using the harmonized Canadian Dementia Imaging Protocol (CDIP)3. The acquisition protocol was a 30-direction 2D DWI sequence with isotropic resolution, left–right phase encoding, b = 1000 s/mm², 2 mm³ isotropic, plus a reverse-phase b0. For a subset of cases, we additionally acquired a multishell high angular resolution (HARDI) protocol (64 directions per shell, b = 2000 and 5000 s/mm², 5 b0, 2 mm³ isotropic, LR encoding) and a 3D ex-vivo-optimized DWI acquisition4 with 8 b0 volumes interspersed between four shells of increasing angular sampling: 30 directions at b = 1000 s/mm², 32 directions at b = 2000 s/mm², 64 directions at b = 3000 s/mm², and 96 directions at b = 4000 s/mm². All 3D DWI scans were acquired with both LR and RL phase encodings. Given that the specimens were immersed in a formaldehyde fixative solution, we custom-designed and 3D-printed a rigid stabilizer grid with interlocking pegs to immobilize the brain during 15-hour acquisitions.

All T1w scans were denoised, bias-corrected, intensity-normalized, and registered to MNI-ICBM152. SynthSeg5 was used to derive WM, GM, and subcortical segmentations based on preprocessed and linearly registered T1w images. WM and GM were further parcellated into lobes using Hammer’s Atlas. To assess depth-related differences in diffusion, masks were generated every 2 mm up to 10 mm, yielding outer, middle, and inner WM depth bands. DWI data were preprocessed with MRtrix3 and FSL (denoising, ringing removal, susceptibility and motion correction, N4 bias correction). MD and FA were computed from the diffusion tensor, and also median signal intensity (SI) of diffusion-weighted volumes. We then extracted median values for all metrics in frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital GM and WM, and across 3 WM depths.

Using the CDIP protocol (N=102), we assessed the relationship between fixation duration and regional differences across varying depths of WM. First, MD and FA were residualized for age at death, sex, and diagnosis, and linear regression was used to assess the association between the metrics and fixation duration. Linear mixed-effects models were conducted separately for each metric (MD, FA, SI) with band depth (outside, middle, inside) included, and interaction coefficients with FDR-corrected p-values were extracted. For the other two acquisitions (N=8), we compared HARDI and 3D-DWI fibre orientation distribution functions (ODF). First, we fit a WM response function and extracted WM ODF via constrained spherical deconvolution for each subject. We then created a population template using linear and non-linear ODF-specific registrations, and reconstructed the corticospinal tracts (CST) with population probabilistic tractography. 

Results

Across all specimens, mean fixation duration was 6.82 ± 7.22 years, with range [0.00 – 22.72]. Linear regression analyses examining the relationships between fixation duration and residualized MD and FA revealed region-specific associations. For MD, significant negative correlations were found in white matter of the parietal (r = - 0.353, pFDR = 0.006) and occipital lobes (r = - 0.628, pFDR < 0.0001). In gray matter, MD in the occipital cortex was also negatively correlated with fixation duration (r = - 0.421, pFDR = 0.001). For FA, marginally significant fixation-related decreases were observed in white matter only, with negative correlations in the frontal (r = - 0.287, pFDR = 0.049) and temporal lobes (r = - 0.267, pFDR = 0.048).

No significant depth-wise differences were found for MD in any of the four lobes, suggesting that the relationship between fixation duration and MD is spatially uniform across WM ring depths. Similarly, pairwise contrasts comparing outer to middle and outer to inner rings all yielded non-significant FDR-corrected p-values (all pFDR ≥ 0.4655), suggesting a global effect of fixation on diffusivity across WM, in all lobes. FA, by contrast, showed significant depth-dependent effects in several lobes. Specifically, significant differences in the fixation time were observed between the outer and inner rings in the occipital (t = 3.141, pFDR = 0.0067), parietal (t = 2.989, pFDR = 0.0075), and frontal (t =3.645, pFDR = 0.0021) lobes, with stronger fixation-associated decreases in FA in deeper WM. This may indicate that the effect of fixation on anisotropic diffusion is not consistent, but rather it varies as a function of tissue depth in a region-specific manner. Stronger effects were observed in the inner versus outer ring in the temporal (t=3.014, pFDR=0.0052), parietal (t = 3.845, pFDR = 0.0005) and frontal (t = 5.051, pFDR < 0.0001) lobes, and between outer versus middle ring in the frontal (t = 3.045, pFDR = 0.0052). These results indicate that SI, like FA, exhibits regionally specific sensitivity to fixative diffusion, with more pronounced signal differences at deeper WM levels in certain cortical areas.

When comparing the HARDI versus 3D DWI, we found high agreement between median MD extracted from both protocols with mean correlation across specimens r= 0.9 (range =[0.76,0.97]). We also found that both produced CST reconstructions with high spatial agreement. While population-averaged ODFs derived from the two sequences were broadly consistent, the 3D-DWI template exhibited sharper WM–GM boundaries due to its higher resolution. The results also demonstrate close agreement in ODF shape and orientation, with both protocols producing highly consistent reconstructions of the corticospinal tracts when processed using identical parameters. This correspondence indicates that the 3D-DWI sequence preserves fiber architecture when compared to HARDI acquisition, in which local differences appear at the tract periphery. Overall, the 3D-DWI protocol preserves the fiber architecture and angular structure captured by HARDI, but at a higher voxel resolution.

Conclusion

In this work, we show spatially distributed and depth-specific differences in metrics extracted from ex-vivo DWI. This effect may be in part due to the time it takes the fixative agents to penetrate into the tissue, as well as the density of the tissue it acts on. The strongest and most consistent fixation-related effects were observed for MD, which was significantly lower in parietal and occipital WM and in occipital GM. These findings indicate that WM regions differ in their sensitivity to fixation, with posterior structures showing the greatest susceptibility. FA also declined with fixation time, but exclusively in WM in frontal and temporal lobes, likely reflecting region-specific differences in fixative penetration dynamics and microstructural composition. Overall, this shows that ex-vivo DWI is feasible as a means to assess tissue microstructure. Depth-specific analyses revealed that MD showed no depth effect in any lobe, indicating spatially uniform fixation effects across WM depths, whereas FA exhibited significantly stronger fixation-related decreases in deeper WM across frontal, parietal, and occipital lobes, suggesting a depth gradient in which central WM is disproportionately affected by fixative penetration. These depth-specific patterns for directional diffusion measures emphasize the importance of considering tissue depth when interpreting ex-vivo diffusion metrics.

Taking this into consideration, the acquisition comparison further demonstrates that diffusion sequence design substantially shapes the quality and stability of ex-vivo diffusivity and fibre assessments and reconstruction. HARDI and 3D-DWI MD and FA values were highly correlated, both at the voxel- and the region-wise levels. They both produced robust and spatially consistent CST reconstructions with high spatial agreement, confirming that major projection pathways can be reliably reconstructed across acquisition strategies. Population-averaged ODFs derived from each sequence were broadly consistent, preserving core fibre architecture and angular structure. Yet the 3D-DWI sequence, optimized for ex-vivo conditions with higher spatial resolution, yielded sharper WM–GM boundaries in the population-averaged template. These enhancements reflect the improved spatial fidelity of the 3D acquisition, particularly valuable given that fixation-related signal differences were most pronounced at deeper WM levels where tissue characterization is critical. Local differences between protocols appeared primarily at the tract periphery, while overall fibre architecture remained consistent when processed using identical parameters. The superior spatial resolution of 3D-DWI makes it a more suitable choice for template construction and detailed microstructural mapping. This is in line with the ISMRM recommendation for HARDI acquisition with two high-gradient strength acquisitions for ex-vivo DWI2. We further supplement their insight with a postmortem-tailored acquisition with higher resolution, sharper boundaries, and enhanced spatial fidelity.
