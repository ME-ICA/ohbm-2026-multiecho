Introduction

The human brain comprises anatomically distinct regions with unique structural, functional and biological properties, yet cognition arises from their interactions. Connectomics shifts focus from isolated areas to the network, represented as a graph with regions as nodes and structural or functional connections as edges. 

Conventional edge-weighted graphs infer node importance only from connections, treating nodes as homogeneous. Degree centrality (DC), for example, identifies hubs from the number or strength of edges. Such edge-focused approaches ignore regional variation in metabolism and signaling, which is crucial for understanding information integration and for explaining vulnerability in disorders like Alzheimer’s disease, where local metabolic activity is a key driver.^2

Recent network science addresses this gap by incorporating node attributes into graph models. Node-weighted centrality assigns weights to both nodes and edges, refining classical metrics by integrating intrinsic node properties. ^1 As in a subway system, a station with many connections but few passengers may be less central than a busy station with fewer links; likewise, a region’s integrative role depends not only on its degree but also on activity in its neighbors. 

We present a fully-weighted brain graph that combines connectivity with regional activity, providing an activity-informed representation of the connectome. Since most neuronal energy is devoted to synaptic signaling, regional glucose metabolism serves as a proxy for signaling activity. Using simultaneously acquired [18F]FDG-PET and fMRI, we derive a Metabolism-weighted Centrality (MwC) metric which summarizes the cumulative information processing of a target region, deduced by the signaling activities of all connected regions. High MwC identifies regions embedded in metabolically active neighborhoods with high integrative capacity, whereas low MwC indicates connections to less active neighbors and reduced functional impact.



Methods

Participants and data acquisition
We analyzed three independent datasets with simultaneous resting-state fMRI and [18F]FDG-PET acquired on integrated 3T PET/MR scanners at two sites (TUM University Hospital Munich; Medical University of Vienna). The main TUM dataset included 20 healthy adults (10 females, 34.5 ± 5 years), and two replication datasets comprised 10 participants each (TUM.rep: 5 females, 27 ± 5 years; Vienna.rep:5 females, 27 ± 7 years), all right-handed and without psychiatric or neurological history. Participants gave written informed consent and local ethics committees approved all protocols. During scans, participants rested with eyes open (TUM.main, Vienna) or closed (TUM.rep). T1-weighted structural images, diffusion-weighted images and resting-state fMRI were acquired together with FDG-PET to estimate the cerebral metabolic rate of glucose (CMRglc).

Preprocessing
 Structural MRI was skull-stripped, segmented and registered to MNI space. Resting-state fMRI preprocessing (C-PAC pipeline) included slice-timing and motion correction, nuisance regression of motion and tissue signals, intensity normalization, spatial smoothing and band-pass filtering. Diffusion data were denoised, corrected for motion and eddy currents, and used for probabilistic tractography (MRtrix3) to derive structural connectivity (SC). PET data were reconstructed, motion- and attenuation-corrected, converted to CMRglc using Patlak analysis with arterial input, partial-volume corrected and registered to the structural image. The cortex was parcellated into 360 regions using the HCP-MMP atlas and regional fMRI time series, CMRglc and SC were extracted in subject space.

MwC calculation
As illustrated in the Methods figure (panels a–f), we combined multimodal data (a) to build a fully-weighted brain graph. FDG-PET provided a regional metabolism vector E (b) and resting-state fMRI provided the functional connectivity (FC) matrix (c). FC between regions was estimated with Gaussian-copula mutual information and constrained by the binarized structural connectivity to yield a fully-weighted graph (d). For each target region t, all connections FC values were first normalized,
nFC(t,i) = FC(t,i) / Σⱼ FC(t,j),
so that each connection reflects the relative influence of neighbour i on t. This normalization prevents target regions with more or stronger raw FC values from yielding artificially inflated MwC scores. MwC was then defined as
MwC(t) = Σᵢ nFC(t,i) · E(i),
quantifying the weighted average metabolism of connected neighbours (i). MwC is high when a region is connected to neighbours with both strong FC and high CMRglc and low when neighbours are weakly connected and metabolically less active. MwC was computed for each subject and then averaged to obtain group-level maps in each dataset. For comparison, we also computed classical degree centrality (DC) from an edge-only graph (e). The toy example (f) demonstrates that two nodes with identical degree can differ in MwC

External maps and statistical analysis
 To relate MwC to independent biological markers, we parcellated group-level maps of synaptic density ([11C]UCB-J PET), amyloid burden (ADNI AV45 PET), transcriptomic profiles from the Allen Human Brain Atlas and cognitive meta-analytic maps from Neurosynth into the same HCP-MMP space. Spatial associations between MwC and these maps were assessed with Pearson correlation and non-parametric, spatial-autocorrelation–preserving permutation tests. All primary analyses were carried out in the TUM.main dataset and repeated in the two replication datasets to assess robustness to sample and acquisition differences. 

Results

We calculated the correlations of MwC and DC with CMRglc across brain regions to compare their performance. Note that the calculation of MwC for node t is independent of E(t), as it only uses the CMRglc from connected regions i. On the group level, CMRglc shows a significant positive correlation with MwC (r = 0.63, p-smash < 0.001; Fig. 2A, top) and a weaker correlation with DC (r = 0.30, p = 0.01; Fig. 2A, bottom). Steiger tests confirmed that the MwC–CMRglc correlation was significantly stronger than the DC–CMRglc correlation, and multiple regression showed that MwC accounted for unique variance in CMRglc beyond DC. These effects replicated in both additional datasets and remained stable across a wide range of structural and functional connectivity thresholds and target region sizes. The stronger MwC–CMRglc association compared to DC was also evident at the subject level.

Our network-level analyses reveal that MwC significantly increases from sensory (visual, VIS; sensory-motor, SOM) to higher cognitive networks, including the default mode network (DMN), salience/ventral attention (SAL), and control network (CON) (one-way ANOVA, F = 31.36, p < 0.001; post-hoc Tukey test: p < 0.05; Fig. 2B, top). In contrast, the average DC showed a less distinctive distribution across networks (F = 2.97, p = 0.01; Fig. 2B, bottom). We also compared the regional distribution of MwC and DC with the distribution of regional activity related to 24 major cognitive functions from the Neurosynth database (Fig. 2C). MwC effectively distinguishes between higher cognitive functions (represented in orange, such as inhibition and memory) and sensory-motor processing (blue). In contrast, DC did not clearly differentiate between these two categories; for example, tasks related to “visuospatial” and “eye movements” were more associated with regions of higher DC values rather than lower values.

To probe the biological basis of MwC, we related the group-level MwC map to cortical gene-expression data from the Allen Human Brain Atlas using partial least squares analysis. The first component showed a spatial pattern that closely matched MwC and captured a substantial fraction of MwC-related variance. Genes with positive weights were enriched for mitochondrial membrane and matrix components, axon and synapse-related compartments, and processes involved in pre- and postsynaptic signaling, vesicle transport and trans-synaptic regulation (Fig. 2D). Following the transcriptomics analysis, we explored independent imaging data on synaptic density to evaluate its relationship with MwC. Using a high-resolution [¹¹C]UCB-J SV2A PET atlas, MwC showed a significant positive correlation with regional synaptic density (r = 0.33, p = 10⁻⁹; Fig. 2E). MwC-related genes were additionally enriched for KEGG pathways linked to oxidative phosphorylation, calcium signaling, and neurodegenerative disorders; among nine significant pathways, six were associated with neurodegenerative diseases (Fig. 2F). This suggests that high-MwC regions are not only metabolically active but also particularly vulnerable to neurodegeneration.

To further validate this finding, we used an external dataset of amyloid-PET imaging, which visualizes amyloid plaques—a hallmark of Alzheimer’s disease—to determine whether high-MwC regions are especially prone to plaque accumulation. Consistent with the KEGG analysis, we found a significant positive correlation between MwC and amyloid load in both cognitively normal (r = 0.32, p < 0.001) and amyloid-β–positive (Ab.pos) MCI patients (r = 0.4, p < 0.001), an early stage of Alzheimer’s disease (Fig. 2G).

In summary, these findings reveal a cross-scale correspondence: regional energy consumption emerges as the organizing principle that links macroscale connectivity with microscale synaptic machinery. The same energy-intensive hubs that sustain cognitive integration appear most exposed to metabolic stress, bridging network theory and cellular vulnerability.



Conclusion

Brain graphs are common tools to characterize the signaling architecture of the brain. Traditionally, the graph topology is defined by the degree and strength of edges derived from functional or structural connectivity data. However, the level of signaling activity within individual nodes has not been considered so far.  We extend this approach by incorporating node-specific metabolic activity, using regional glucose metabolism to weight the influence of each connection. This yields a fully weighted brain graph and MwC, a measure of nodal signaling dominance that reflects the metabolic effort a region invests in information processing across its connections. Connections with equal FC strength can therefore contribute differently to network signaling depending on the metabolism of connected regions. Unlike hubs identified from FC or SC alone, MwC hubs are regions that engage in metabolically demanding communication. We validated MwC with transcriptomic data and in-vivo synaptic density imaging, and found that regions with high MwC overlap with areas vulnerable in several neurodegenerative disorders, shifting hubness from a purely topological concept to one grounded in signaling activity.

Energy metabolism is tightly coupled to signaling, so interpretations of connectivity strength as information transfer or integration should respect the metabolic demands of the regions involved. ^3 We found that MwC and DC together explain ~44% of the variance in CMRglc, with MwC accounting for most of this variance. Thus, relying solely on DC or on local metabolism captures only a partial view of a region’s signaling dominance. By combining edge information with nodal signaling activity, MwC provides a biologically grounded description of an activity-driven connectome.

Both FC and SC derived from MRI primarily capture the presence of a connection rather than the level of signaling along it. FC  identifies synchronized BOLD fluctuations between regions, but BOLD reflects only a fraction of total activity. As a result, two regions with low overall activity can appear strongly connected if their BOLD signals are similar, and centrality metrics may mislabel such nodes as hubs. MwC addresses this by scaling edges according to the absolute metabolic demand of connected regions, anchoring centrality estimates to energy-consuming signaling rather than to synchrony alone.

Hub identification in brain graphs has largely relied on classical edge-based metrics such as degree or participation coefficient, which are sensitive to modality, preprocessing and graph resolution, and whose high values can mainly reflect structural prominence ^4. Previous work incorporating nodal properties (for example neurotransmitter maps) usually used them to select subgraphs ^5, while hubness itself remained edge-defined. In contrast, our node-weighted approach integrates nodal metabolism directly into the centrality calculation. MwC segregates more clearly across functional networks and better matches the hierarchy of signaling and cognitive roles than DC: high-MwC hubs lie in association cortices with complex processing demands, whereas high-DC hubs are more diffusely distributed and less tightly linked to cognitive specialization.

Independent molecular and imaging data support the notion that MwC marks energy-intensive and vulnerable hubs. Genes upregulated in high-MwC regions are enriched for synaptic signaling and mitochondrial pathways, and SV2A PET shows higher synaptic density in these hubs. MwC also aligns with higher accumulation of amyloid-β. Together, these findings suggest that regions with high lifelong signaling and metabolic turnover are preferential targets of neurodegenerative pathology. In summary, MwC identifies brain regions of strong signaling dominance and integration within the entire brain connectome, highlighting the susceptibility of highly active network nodes to neurodegeneration.
