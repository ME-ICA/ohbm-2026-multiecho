Introduction

A fundamental challenge in treating Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) is the inability to distinguish drug efficacy from placebo effects, resulting in SSRI response rates below 50%. To move beyond one-size-fits-all and trial-and-error prescribing, identifying predictive biomarkers for SSRI response is essential. Crucially, such biomarkers must be specific to pharmacological mechanisms, distinct from placebo-driven improvement.

Consequently, research on brain-based biomarkers (neurobiomarkers) has intensified, yet prior studies face significant limitations. Many have been constrained by small sample sizes, conventional statistical approaches, or machine learning models prone to overfitting, leading to poor generalizability. Moreover, most rely on static functional connectivity measures, potentially overlooking the complex, nonlinear spatiotemporal dynamics of the brain.

To address these limitations, we implemented a transfer learning framework using a 4D spatiotemporal deep learning model pretrained on large-scale data. This approach captures intrinsic brain dynamics while mitigating overfitting in small clinical datasets. We hypothesized that a model fine-tuned on pre-treatment resting-state fMRI would accurately predict SSRI response but show no predictive power for placebo-driven improvement. This study validates whether capturing spatiotemporal dynamics can predict drug-specific responsiveness.

Methods

We utilized pre-treatment resting-state fMRI data from the EMBARC study,1 a multi-site randomized controlled trial comprising MDD patients randomized to Sertraline (SSRI; N=142, 66 responders) or Placebo (N=145, 56 responders). Treatment response was defined as ≥ 50% reduction in Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS-17) score after 8 weeks. Preprocessing utilized fMRIPrep, followed by nuisance regression of CompCor components and motion parameters, and spatial smoothing with a 6-mm FWHM Gaussian kernel.

To overcome the limitations of static connectivity methods and small clinical samples, we employed a 4D Spatiotemporal Deep Learning approach based on the SwiFT (Swin 4D fMRI Transformer) architecture,2 which captures brain dynamics directly from volumetric data. We utilized transfer learning framework by pretraining SwiFT model on large-scale resting-state fMRI datasets (UK Biobank and Human Connectome Project;∼42,000 subjects). Specifically, we compared supervised and various self-supervised strategies (including contrastive learning, random masking, temporal masking, and tube masking) to identify the optimal pretraining method. To determine the optimal transfer learning (fine-tuning) protocol, we compared linear probing and partial fine-tuning strategies.

For clinical application, we fine-tuned the pre-trained SwiFT variants on the EMBARC SSRI arm using stratified 5-fold cross-validation. We compared our approach against conventional machine learning (XGBoost) and deep learning baselines (BrainNetCNN, BrainGNN, ST-GCN) trained from scratch using ROI-based features. Performance was evaluated using AUROC, balanced accuracy, and F1-score. The best-performing model on the SSRI arm was then applied to the Placebo arm for inference only, hypothesizing chance-level performance. Finally, we employed Integrated Gradients to identify neurobiological features driving predictions in correctly classified subjects.

Results

Baseline models using ROI-based features and SwiFT trained from scratch showed limited predictive performance. Baseline models achieved AUROCs ranging from 0.47 to 0.59 (XGBoost: 0.59±0.03; BrainNetCNN: 0.58±0.16; ST-GCN: 0.56±0.08; BrainGNN: 0.47±0.05). SwiFT trained from scratch performed comparably (AUROC: 0.53±0.11, balanced accuracy: 0.54±0.05, F1-score: 0.51±0.08), indicating that deep learning alone offers no advantage over conventional approaches in small clinical datasets.

However, fine-tuning models pretrained on large-scale datasets substantially improved performance. The optimal configuration—Tube Masking with partial fine-tuning—achieved the highest performance on the SSRI arm (AUROC: 0.68±0.05, balanced accuracy: 0.64±0.04, F1-score: 0.62±0.05), representing a 15% improvement in AUROC over the best baseline (XGBoost: 0.59). For transfer learning (fine-tuning) protocol, partial fine-tuning outperformed linear probing (AUROC: 0.68 vs 0.61). For pretraining strategy, tube masking outperformed supervised pretraining (AUROC: 0.60). Other self-supervised approaches—contrastive learning, random masking, and temporal masking—showed limited effectiveness comparable to training from scratch.

The model demonstrated drug-specific predictive power. When applied to the Placebo arm, the performance of the best SSRI model dropped to chance level (AUROC: 0.53±0.02, balanced accuracy: 0.51±0.03, F1-score: 0.47±0.10), confirming that learned features were specific to pharmacological mechanisms rather than placebo effects.

Integrated Gradients analysis with Bonferroni-corrected t-tests revealed distinct neurobiological signatures. Response prediction was driven by cortical regions implicated in top-down emotional regulation: the left orbitofrontal cortex emerged as the strongest discriminator, followed by Salience Network nodes (frontal operculum, insula) and widespread Default Mode Network regions (temporal and prefrontal areas). Conversely, non-response prediction was predominantly associated with subcortical structures central to reward processing and arousal regulation: the thalamus—particularly dorsal posterior and ventral anterior nuclei—showed the strongest association, alongside core components of basal ganglia reward circuitry (nucleus accumbens, caudate, globus pallidus, putamen). Additionally, medial nodes of the Salience Network and extensive somatomotor regions contributed to non-response prediction. These findings highlight the integrity of cortico-striatal-thalamo-cortical loops as a potential neural substrate distinguishing SSRI responders from non-responders.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that 4D deep learning, enhanced by optimized transfer learning, can successfully distinguish antidepressant response patterns from placebo effects. The success of Tube Masking, which masks continuous spatiotemporal segments, provides important methodological insights. This approach compels the model to learn evolving neural trajectories, capturing spatiotemporal dynamics that are not readily accessible to static connectivity approaches.

The observed specificity of the predictive features—evidenced by their chance-level performance in the placebo group—confirms that SSRI response is driven by a distinct neurofunctional substrate. The model's reliance on cortical regions implicated in top-down emotional regulation (e.g., the orbitofrontal cortex and Default Mode Network regions) and key Salience Network nodes for predicting response suggests that preserved top-down emotional regulation capacity may be a prerequisite for serotonergic modulation to be effective. Conversely, the strong association of subcortical structures central to reward processing and arousal regulation, specifically the thalamus and basal ganglia reward circuitry, with non-response indicates that dysfunction or abnormality in these subcortical-driven reward/arousal circuits may render standard SSRI treatment insufficient.

Clinically, these results support a mechanism-based precision psychiatry framework. Evaluating cortico-striatal loop integrity prior to treatment may eventually inform treatment selection. Specifically, patients with intact cortical regulation may benefit from SSRIs, while those with prominent subcortical abnormalities may warrant consideration of alternative interventions targeting dopaminergic pathways or direct neuromodulation.
