Introduction

Human brain structure shows substantial variability across person to person. The variability becomes even more pronounced within neurological and neurodevelopmental conditions. To investigate neuroanatomical differences associated with brain disorder and to ensure that the findings are robust and not outliers, a large-scale dataset is required. Evidence from large multi-institutional datasets indicates that more than 20% of acquired scans are excluded during quality control due to motion or related artifacts1. Motion artifacts remain one of the most pervasive challenges as it degrades imaging quality and may lead to misinterpretation2. This results in a significant loss of research time and the increased funding cost associated with data acquisition, and processing3. Utilizing the lost or corrupted data is crucial in identifying the most subtle difference or patterns observed in cohorts with neurological disorder.

Numerous deep learning (DL) based techniques have been proposed to correct the motion artifacts. While prospective motion-correction approach is used to modify the acquisition process to minimize the artifacts at the source, image-to-image correction methods without altering the acquisition and the requirement re gaining attention as it can be applied to the previously collected dataset existing in the clinical and MRI repositories. These methods incorporate CNNs, encoder–decoder architectures, or generative models such as GANs, VAEs, and diffusion models, which have shown strong potential for producing high-quality, realistic corrections4.  So, this study aims to develop a generative framework using supervised diffusion model to generate more realistic and high-quality MRI scans by suppressing motion artifacts in the previously acquired low-quality scans. This methodology has the potential to substantially reduce the time and costs associated with reacquisition and quality-control procedures. 

Methods

Description of dataset: The dataset for this study is obtained from Province of Ontario Neurodevelopmental Disorders (POND) Network, a multicenter research network. Both ethics board approval and informed consent were obtained in compliance with ethical standards. We used structural MRI to obtain T1-weighted (T1w) images of whole-brain anatomy. A total of 137 patient cohorts with two separate scans: with motion artifacts (noisy) and without motion artifacts (clean) were considered. Quality control approved by both CIVET and MAGeT, supplemented by visual inspection is used as the clean data. A stratified split of 70/10/20 was performed at the patient level. The MRI volumes were affine-registered, intensity-normalized, and reoriented to standard anatomical space with 1 mm isotropic voxel spacing, resulting in a matrix size of 182 x 218 x 182.

 

Proposed methodology:  The proposed method uses a conditional denoising diffusion probabilistic mode (DDPM) to reconstruct clean MRI image from a noisy image as shown in the Figure 1. 5

Both noisy and the corresponding clean images were fed as input to the model. During training, the model learns to predict the noise ϵ given the noisy image at timestep and the original noisy image as a conditioning input. By using the conditional DDPM, the model learns to distinguish true anatomical structures and focuses on learning the noise pattern rather than generating images from random noise without any guidance. A conditional 2D UNet with self-attention and residual DoubleConv blocks were used with timestep embedding and skip connections. The model follows an encoder–bottleneck–decoder structure with three spatial down and up sampling stages.

Implementation and Training: The input images were down sampled to 64x64. The model was trained for 200 epochs with a batch size of 4 on approximately 16000 2D slices. For model evaluation, quantitative image quality metrics such as Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) and Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM) are used.

Results

Preliminary - The proposed model was evaluated on a test dataset consisting of 27 patient cohorts with approximately 4500 slices. The evaluation metrics were calculated based on patient-wise by including all the slices in the MRI volume.

Examples of noisy MRI scans and their corresponding predicted (denoised) MRI scans produced by the model, along with the associated quantitative metrics, are shown in Figure 2. The proposed model demonstrated a strong ability to suppress motion artifacts in the majority of the test cases, achieving a higher PSNR of 29 dB and a SSIM of 0.9. These results indicate that the denoised images closely resemble the ground truth in terms of both intensity and structural content. However, in some challenging cases, the motion artifacts were not completely removed, which led to lower quantitative scores, with a PSNR of 17 dB and SSIM of 0.5. Despite these outliers, the model achieved an overall average PSNR of 24 dB and SSIM of 0.7 across the held-out test dataset.

Conclusion

In this study, we propose a generative diffusion model for correcting motion artifacts in MRI data collected from multiple institutions and imaging scanners. As an initial step, the framework was trained using a 2D U-Net-based diffusion model on individual axial slices. While the 2D approach effectively removed motion artifacts, it exhibited loss of resolution and inconsistencies across adjacent slices. To overcome these limitations, we are currently developing a full 3D diffusion model, which can generate higher-fidelity, high-resolution MRI volumes. By learning volumetric motion patterns and spatial distortions, the 3D model is more robust to the types of motion artifacts commonly encountered in clinical workflows.

In conclusion, the images generated using our approach have the potential to support studies on brain development, neurodevelopment, and diseases affecting health and function. By leveraging 3D modeling, the method produces high-quality, reliable MRI images that are valuable both for research and for clinical applications.
