Introduction

Healthy cognitive ageing reflects complex interactions among neural, cognitive, vascular, lifestyle, and demographic factors. The Cambridge Centre for Ageing and Neuroscience (Cam-CAN) began in 2010 to address this challenge using a population-derived, evenly age-distributed cohort with deep multimodal phenotyping across adulthood.1 The full Cam-CAN programme (www.cam-can.org) now comprises five Phases, of which Phases 2, 3, and 5 form the core neuroimaging dataset.1,2 Phase 2 provides a rich cross-sectional sample (~750 adults, aged 18–87 years) that combines MRI, MEG, and cognitive measures.3 Phase 3 re-tested a subset (~280) with additional structural imaging and an expanded set of fMRI and MEG tasks.1 To overcome cohort and generational confounds inherent to cross-sectional inference, Cam-CAN has now implemented a ~12-year longitudinal follow-up (Phase 5), reacquiring MRI, MEG, and core cognitive tasks to enable individual-level estimates of neurocognitive change.2 Together, these phases transform Cam-CAN from a cross-sectional resource into a longitudinal, multimodal platform spanning more than a decade of adult life. Here we describe Phases 2, 3, and 5, all curated in Brain Imaging Data Structure (BIDS) format, enabling reproducible cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses. Much of the data is available to researchers through the Cam-CAN data portal  (https://opendata.mrc-cbu.cam.ac.uk/projects/camcan/). Here, we describe the nature of the data and phases involved, highlighting their value for ageing research and open science.

Methods

Design & Sampling. Cam-CAN recruited a population-based cohort through Cambridge GP registers (Phase 1; n=2681) and derived an evenly distributed lifespan imaging sample for Phase 2-Arm 1 (2011-2013) with ~100 participants per decade (18–87), screened for cognitive health and imaging suitability. Phase 3 (2013-2014) recalled ~280 participants from Phase 2-Arm 1 for additional tests. Phase 2-Arm 2 (2016-2018) screened an additional sample of 93 participants from Phase 1. Phase 5 (2023–2025) invited all eligible Phase 2 participants to repeat MRI and MEG and to complete online core tasks. Of these, ~154 have completed at least one Phase 5 session.

Phase 2 imaging & tasks. MRI data were collected on a 3T Siemens Tim Trio scanner with a 32‑channel coil and included T1, T2-SPACE, MTR, DWI, field map, and functional (resting-state, movie-watching, and sensorimotor task) scans. MEG data were collected on a 306-channel Elekta VectorView system and included resting-state, sensorimotor, and passive audiovisual tasks. The cognitive battery spanned attention, emotion, action, language, and memory. For full details, see Shafto et al.1 and https://www.cam-can.org/materials/.

Phase 3 imaging & tasks. MRI data were collected on the same system as in Phase 2 and included T1, T2-FLAIR, Arterial Spin Labelling (ASL), field map, and functional (resting-state, emotional expression recognition, emotional memory, emotional reactivity and regulation, fluid intelligence, free selection, picture naming, sentence comprehension, Stop-Signal/Go-No-Go, and visual short-term memory task) scans. MEG data were collected on the same system as in Phase 2 and included resting-state, incidental memory, multi-mismatch, picture naming, sentence comprehension, Stop-Signal/Go-No-Go, and word recognition tasks. In addition, participants had the Mini-Mental State Exam (MMSE) from Phase 1 repeated. For full details, see Shafto et al.1 and https://www.cam-can.org/materials/.

Phase 5 imaging & tasks. MRI data were collected on a 3T Siemens Prisma scanner with a 32‑channel coil and included T1, T2-FLAIR, MTR, DWI, ASL, and functional (resting-state, movie-watching, and sensorimotor task) scans. MEG data were collected on a 306-channel MEGIN Triux Neo system and included resting-state, movie-watching, sensorimotor, story listening, Stop-Signal/Go-No-Go, and Multi-Mismatch Negativity tasks. Cognitive tasks were administered online and matched to Phase 2 domains. For full details, see Demetriou et al.2 and https://www.cam-can.org/materials/.

Harmonisation & BIDS Implementation. Protocols were aligned across phases to maximise continuity despite scanner upgrades (Tim Trio to Prisma; Elekta to MEGIN). Sequence parameters were matched where possible (including EPI geometry) while expanding sensitivity to vascular and functional changes (ASL, updated movie stimuli, expanded MEG tasks). All MRI, MEG, and behavioural data are BIDS-compliant, with standardised folder structures, JSON metadata, and cross-phase identifiers, enabling automated analysis using BIDS Apps (e.g., fMRIPrep, QSIPrep, MNE-BIDS) and supporting FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable). Data from Phases 2 and 3 can be requested via Cam-CAN portal (https://opendata.mrc-cbu.cam.ac.uk/projects/camcan/), where data from Phase 5 are planned to be added later in 2026.

Results

Across Phases 2, 3, and 5, the Cam-CAN now constitutes one of the most comprehensive multimodal lifespan and longitudinal neuroimaging datasets available for healthy ageing research. Figure 1 overviews all Cam-CAN stages.

The main arm of Phase 2 (Arm 1) provides baseline MRI, MEG, and cognition for ~700 participants, spanning the full adult lifespan (18–87) with approximately even decade sampling. Cognitive-behavioural data from multiple tasks yield an extensive phenotypic characterisation across attention, emotion, action, language, and memory domains, complemented by demographic, lifestyle, and physiological measures obtained in Phase 1. These data have been widely used for methodological development and structure–function–cognition mapping.4

In a second arm (Arm 2), the same cognitive and imaging protocols were administered to another group of ~100 people from the original Phase 1, focusing on the “cognitively frail” who failed the standard MMSE cut-off for dementia. (An additional ~40 MCI patients were also scanned with the same protocols but are not currently available for sharing.)

Phase 3 adds depth to a large subset (~280 participants from Arm 1), providing repeat structural MRI scans alongside a substantially expanded set of fMRI and MEG tasks that probe emotion regulation, memory, language, cognitive control, and fluid intelligence. These sessions enrich the dataset with test–retest information, modality-specific reliability estimates, and multi-task functional signatures of ageing across distributed cognitive systems.

Phase 5 provides ∼12-year longitudinal follow-up for more than 150 returning participants (from both Phase 2 Arm 1 and Arm 2; now aged 35-96), with updated MRI and MEG paradigms, plus repeat cognitive testing. This enables estimation of within-person change in brain structure, brain function, and cognition across more than a decade of adult life.

Across phases, >2,000 imaging sessions have been curated into BIDS-compliant datasets. The harmonised release supports automated preprocessing pipelines, robust cross-modal integration, and longitudinal modelling without requiring user-led data curation. Validation confirms internal consistency of identifiers, session mappings, metadata completeness, and cross-phase protocol correspondence. 

Conclusion

The Cam-CAN Phase 2, 3, and 5 datasets constitute a uniquely rich, multimodal, population-derived neuroimaging resource for understanding healthy cognitive ageing. By harmonising MRI, MEG, and behavioural measures across a ~12-year interval and delivering all phases in BIDS format, Cam-CAN supports reproducible investigation of both cross-sectional variation and longitudinal change in brain structure, function, and neurovascular health. This resource enables powerful modelling of lifespan trajectories, identification of ageing biomarkers, and mechanistic links between cognition and multimodal brain dynamics. By adopting BIDS across all neuroimaging phases, Cam-CAN not only offers rare longitudinal multimodal data but also ensures maximum usability, transparency, and reproducibility. As Phase 5 becomes publicly available, Cam-CAN will offer one of the world’s most comprehensive longitudinal neuroimaging resources, supporting open, collaborative, theory-driven research on how and why cognitive abilities are maintained across adulthood. Integration with global initiatives such as LifeBrain and compatibility with BIDS Apps further amplify its impact for collaborative ageing research.
