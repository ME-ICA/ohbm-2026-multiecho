Introduction

The relationship between biological sex and its effect on the brain remains highly debated. Given substantial overall brain volume differences between males and females, the question arises whether reported differences reflect true sex-specific patterns or are driven by other covariates. The effect of sex on structural brain measures - such as grey-white matter ratios or regional volumes - may be reduced, disappear, or show inconsistent patterns across studies depending on methodology1. Others however remain2. Similar issues arise in functional neuroimaging: brain size is an important covariate when predicting sex in fMRI3. Yet, even though there is a growing amount of literature looking into sex-specific effects also in EEG/MEG activity it remains unclear whether brain size is acting as a confounding variable. Some literature suggests that it may play a role by controlling for it4,5. However, a systematic evaluation is still lacking. In this study, we address this gap by asking two central questions in the context of MEG data: First, does intracranial brain volume (ICV) have an effect on MEG-recorded brain activity? Second, are there functional MEG markers that improve sex classification beyond brain volume alone?

Methods

Using source-reconstructed MEG resting-state data from 350 participants (ages 18-88 years) we extracted a comprehensive set of approximately 6000 time-series features, including both traditional power spectrum-related features and novel descriptors using the hctsa toolbox. In order to explore whether brain size modulates power-spectrum-derived measures, we performed parcelwise Pearson correlation between ICV and different, traditionally used spectral features. Testing for significant effects was performed using cluster-based permutation testing. Further, we tested sex classification using (1) ICV alone and (2) ICV plus one changing time-series feature. For classification SVMs were trained using 10-fold age-balanced cross-validation, repeated 50 times. Significance was assessed using 10,000 label permutations. The 50 best-performing features were clustered, and Haufe-transformed weights were used for interpretation. Bootstrapped corrected t-tests assessed whether adding features improved accuracy beyond ICV alone.

Results

Exploring the effect of brain size on MEG spectral measures, every spectral measure exhibited notable correlations with ICV specific spatial variation. For instance, one occipital parcel showed a  correlation between ICV and the 1/f component of r = .38.  Although not all parcels showed strong effects, every spectral measure exhibited notable correlations with specific spatial cortical variation. Cluster-based permutation tests confirmed significant ICV-feature clusters for all measures after correction for multiple comparisons. Based on these findings, we first tested whether ICV alone could predict biological sex. ICV alone, with just one data point per participant, predicted sex with 75% accuracy. Including individual time-series features resulted in a small, not significant improvement up to 78%. Approximately one-third of the top-performing features were Fourier Spectrum-related, suggesting again a strong link between brain size and spectral characteristics. 

Conclusion

Although MEG research has expanded rapidly, the influence of individual differences in brain volume on MEG signals remains underexplored. Our work shows that brain size systematically affects MEG-derived measures, including band-limited power, peak frequency, and the aperiodic component, with non-uniform cortical effects that cannot be explained by signal-to-noise differences alone. Our findings demonstrate that brain size is not a nuisance variable and must be explicitly considered when studying sex effects in MEG. Because brain volume differs by sex, failing to account for it risks misattributing size-related variance to sex. In our data, MEG features added little predictive value beyond ICV alone. These results emphasize the importance of careful methodology and considering a nuanced approach to the effect of sex or gender in future MEG research.
