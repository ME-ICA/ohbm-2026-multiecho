Introduction

Regions of the central autonomous network (CAN) are known to monitor and regulate cardiovascular function, and the insula in particular is associated with interoceptive awareness1. However, their role in cardiovascular diseases remains largely unknown. Extrasystoles — premature heartbeats that violate expected rhythmic patterns — and their compensatory pauses represent naturally occurring disruptions to cardiac rhythm, providing an ecologically valid window into how interoceptive systems detect and process deviations from normal patterns. We leveraged naturally occurring cardiac extrasystoles (both, supraventricular and ventricular) as a probe of neural responses to irregular cardiac events during resting-state fMRI. 

Methods

Nine adults with frequent extrasystoles (54.1±16.3 years, 6M/3F) underwent 10-minute resting-state fMRI (3T, TR=1500ms, 2.3mm³ isotropic, MB=4). Structural heart disease and persistent arrhythmias were excluded by echocardiography and Holter ECG; all participants demonstrated ≥750 extrasystoles on prior 24-hour Holter monitoring. Concurrent ECG and photoplethysmography enabled manual identification of 458 extrasystole events (mean 50.9±41.6 per participant, range 10-119). Each extrasystole onset was modeled as an impulse event convolved with the canonical hemodynamic response function. Regular heartbeats served as implicit baseline. Group-level mixed-effects analysis (FSL FLAME 1+2) weighted participants by within-subject variance. Cluster-based thresholding applied whole-brain correction (Z>3.0, GRF-corrected p<0.05). Preprocessing included motion correction (MCFLIRT), 8mm FWHM spatial smoothing, and 200s high-pass temporal filtering. Anatomical regions were identified using the Harvard-Oxford probabilistic atlas at cluster centers of gravity.

Results

Extrasystoles produced significant deactivations across core interoceptive processing regions (2,352 voxels total). Three distinct clusters emerged: bilateral ACC (611 voxels, cluster-corrected p=0.009, peak Z=5.18 at MNI [2, 36, 12]), left insula extending into frontal orbital cortex (636 voxels, p=0.007, peak Z=5.15 at [-34, -4, -8]), and right insula (1,105 voxels, p=0.0004, peak Z=5.16 at [30, 18, -4]). Effect sizes were highly similar across regions (Z=5.15-5.18), indicating a coordinated network response to cardiac irregularity.

Conclusion

The observed deactivations likely reflect neural responses to both the premature beat and subsequent compensatory pause, which are difficult to dissociate given fMRI's temporal resolution. The consistent deactivation across bilateral insula and ACC suggests these regions function as an integrated network for tracking cardiac rhythm. Regular cardiac rhythm maintains tonic activity in interoceptive processing regions; extrasystoles disrupt this baseline state, revealing the brain's active monitoring of cardiac patterns2,3. Our fMRI findings are consistent with source reconstructions based on EEG recordings during extrasystoles4. Understanding neural responses to cardiac irregularities may inform our knowledge of individual differences in interoceptive sensitivity and their role in anxiety, depression, and affective disorders5.
