Introduction

Infants who sustain brain injury in the neonatal period are at increased risk for neurodevelopmental impairments later in life. Predicting specific impacts of brain injury can help clinicians tailor care to improve outcomes. There is substantial variability in the type and severity of deficits that follow neonatal brain injury. The type, size, and location of injury explain some of this variability, as in voxel-based lesion-symptom mapping. White matter injury (WMI) localized to a small region may affect the broader white matter tracts involved, and connectivity between brain regions linked by these tracts. Understanding the impact of lesions on specific connections and the brain network overall enhances the ability to predict outcomes of WMI.

White matter integrity and connectivity between brain regions can be assessed using tractography performed on diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

Software exists for estimating the extent of white matter impacted by a given brain injury, i.e. the disconnectome, using healthy adult control diffusion MRI [1], but such a tool is not yet available for the neonatal population. Compared to adults, neonatal brains are smaller, and have less mature white matter connectivity. Tractography on diffusion MRI is typically not performed clinically, necessitating long scan times and complex post-processing, whereas structural MRI is routinely clinically acquired and used to assess brain injury. Here, we present the Neonatal Disconnectome Toolkit. This software enables estimation of the disconnectome from an injury segmentation on a subject's structural MRI, leveraging neonatal diffusion MRI of health controls. It enables users to easily estimate the broader connectivity implications of white matter injury using age-appropriate neonatal control data, requiring only a structural MRI with an associated injury segmentation.

Methods

Here, we outline the approach implemented in the toolkit, which is illustrated in Figure 1. To estimate the extent of white matter impacted by brain injury, the toolkit uses diffusion MRI of healthy neonatal controls from the Developing Human Connectome Project (dHCP) [2]. Specifically, we used diffusion MRI sequences processed through the dHCP diffusion MRI pipeline [3], which includes motion- and distortion-correction, and quality assessment. We selected 179 dHCP diffusion MRI sequences which had no radiological findings, and which had mean "relative" and "absolute" framewise motion less than 2mm, as assessed by FSL EDDY.

We performed deterministic whole-brain tractography on the diffusion MRI scans using Diffusion Imaging in Python (DiPy, version 1.11.0) [4]. Streamlines were seeded from voxels within a white matter mask, with two seeds per voxel. Streamlines were propagated using the EuDX algorithm. Streamlines were excluded if they entered a cerebrospinal fluid mask, or if they did not terminate in a gray matter mask.

The input to the toolkit is a structural MRI image, with an associated lesion map indicating WMI voxels. The toolkit first warps the subject's structural image to a template image using Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTS) [5]. The dHCP includes template images at different ages in weekly increments from 28 to 44 weeks. The dHCP template closest in age to the lesion subject is used as the registration target. After the warp is complete, the user is prompted to manually assess alignment between the subject's warped brain image and the target template. Using this computed warp, and transformations included in the dHCP dataset, the subject's lesion map is then transformed from the subject's native brain space, to the template image space, and finally into the space of each of the control diffusion MRI sequences. The whole-brain tractogram of each control image is then filtered: streamlines intersecting the lesion map are retained, while all other streamlines are removed. Each filtered streamline density map is then binarized to obtain a visitation map, indicating which voxels in the control image are connected to the lesion by tractography streamlines. The visitation maps for all control subjects are then warped to the dHCP 40-week template space, averaged, and the mean is thresholded at 50%. In the resulting disconnectome map, the value at each voxel represents the proportion of control images which have streamlines intersecting the lesion, and can be interpreted as an estimate of the probability that white matter connectivity at that location is diminished due to the lesion.

To illustrate the application of the toolkit, we applied it to a subset of infants in a cohort of infants born very preterm. To demonstrate that widespread white matter regions may be linked to localized lesions, we examined only a subset of infants with injury in the posterior white matter. Infants were recruited across 3 neonatal intensive care units in Canada, and were scanned early in life once clinically stable. White matter injury was manually segmented on T1-weighted structural MRI. The toolkit was applied to 37 subjects in the cohort identified as having posterior white matter injury. After obtaining a disconnectome map for each subject, we computed the mean disconnectome across subjects. Further, to assess which specific connections in the brain were impacted by posterior WMI, we computed connectivity matrices based on the lesion-filtered tractograms of each control image, with regions defined in the CerebrA atlas as nodes. These connectivity matrices were then averaged across control images and across lesion subjects to obtain a group "disconnectivity matrix", in which each element can be interpreted as an estimate of the probability that a given connection in the brain was impacted by posterior WMI.

Results

The toolkit is packaged as a desktop application that runs on Windows, Mac, and Linux operating systems. The toolkit includes a graphical user interface (GUI) with user interaction at each step, and displays preview images of intermediate and final results. In addition, it also includes a command-line interface, which runs without user interaction, facilitating high-throughput analyses. The Neonatal Disconnectome Toolkit is currently fully functional, but is being updated to improve functionality and user experience, and tested to identify issues, before executables are released. It will be made freely available and is open-source (https://github.com/sufkes/neonatal_disconnectome).

An example output of Neonatal Disconnectome toolkit is shown in Figure 2. Although the injury of the subjects was restricted to the posterior white matter, the mean disconnectome is spread broadly across the posterior and anterior white matter. The group disconnectivity matrix indicates that the posterior WMI in this set of subjects occurs in tracts connecting a wide array of cortical and deep gray matter regions, and the brainstem. Specifically, the injury in this set of subjects highly overlaps tracts connected to the thalamus; brainstem; ventral diencephalon; and the superior frontal, precentral, and superior parietal gyri.

Conclusion

While lesion size and location can be used to estimate the impact of neonatal brain injury, lesions of similar size and location can lead to widely variable outcomes, due in part to the complex network of white matter tracts linking brain regions, which are susceptible to WMI. The Neonatal Disconnectome Toolkit presented here enables estimation of the extent of white matter impacted by a given brain injury, using high-quality neonatal healthy control data. The toolkit serves to enhance the interpretability of injury segmented on neonatal structural MRI by estimating the extent of impacted white matter, and identifying specific connections likely to be impacted. While methods such as voxel-based lesion-symptom mapping can be used to associate lesion size and location with outcomes, the sensitivity of such methods is restricted by the small size of lesions: even in a large sample of subjects with WMI, there may be relatively little overlap of lesions across subjects. The present method leverages the underlying complex white matter structure: lesions lying in different locations, but in the same white matter tracts, will have similar disconnectome maps. Conversely, lesions lying near each other, but in different white matter tracts, will have very different disconnectome maps. Using disconnectome maps in place of lesion maps in a voxel-based lesion-symptom mapping approach may thereby more sensitively detect associations between anatomically related brain injuries and outcomes.

Whereas assessment of structural connectivity and white matter integrity using tractography requires long MRI acquisitions and complex post-processing, the present method requires only labelling of routinely acquired structural MRI. As implemented in a desktop application that can run on a personal computer, the present method is highly accessible to clinicians and researchers.

A previously published toolkit enables estimation of disconnectome maps in adults [1]. The present toolkit adapts that approach to neonates, whose brains are very different in terms of size, shape, and white matter structure, compared to adults. The disconnectome approach requires accurate alignment between the lesion subject and the template brain images, such that the lesion is mapped to the same anatomical location in the template and control images as in the original subject. Over the neonatal period, the brain's shape changes dramatically as gyri and sulci form. Using age-matched templates, which are at similar stages of development as the lesion subject, enables the lesion to be mapped to the same anatomical location as the subject, with high fidelity. Use of tractography from high-quality neonatal diffusion MRI ensures that the generated disconnectome maps reflect white matter structure in the neonatal period.

There are limitations of the present method. The interpretability of neonatal disconnectome maps would be strengthened by direct evidence that a given disconnectome map is associated with diminished white matter integrity in the same regions. While the current method is highly accessible to clinicians and researchers, it still requires considerable expertise: accurate segmentation of lesions in the input image, and verification of successful input image alignment with the template image. User intervention using other software is required when alignment between the input and template image fails.

The Neonatal Disconnectome Toolkit is an accessible tool that enables users to estimate the extent of white matter impacted by neonatal brain injury, as well as specific connections between brain regions. Future studies examining relationships between neonatal brain injury and outcomes can use this tool to incorporate the complex white matter network involved in white matter injury.
