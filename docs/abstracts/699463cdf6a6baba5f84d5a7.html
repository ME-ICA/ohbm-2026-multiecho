Introduction

Visual perception is based on the integration of endogenous top-down with sensory bottom-up processes. Sensory bottom-up information travels from primary visual areas to higher-order sensory regions in the occipito-temporal cortex. Conversely, top-down influences, driven by prior experiences or expectations, originate from hierarchically higher frontal areas and serve as input to modify lower-level representations. While sensory processes in the occipito-temporal cortex during the visual perception of objects have been studied intensively throughout the last decades, the knowledge about neural correlates of top-down processes is sparse. In a previous functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) study, we used an illusory face detection task to study the neural mechanisms associated with the detection of a face1. We showed that illusory face perception activated (just as real faces) the core system (i.e., bilateral occipital face area (OFA) and fusiform face area (FFA)), albeit with atypical left-lateralization of the OFA. The core system was coupled with two distinct brain regions in the lateral prefrontal (inferior frontal gyrus, IFG) and medial orbitofrontal cortex (mOFC).

In the present study, we aimed to generalize these findings. Thus, we investigated the neural network and specifically the top-down mechanisms associated with the illusory detection of letters, as one specific example of non-face objects.

We hypothesized that the neural mechanisms underlying top-down modulations described in the neuroanatomical model of face detection1 is not specific to faces but constitutes a more general categorization mechanism.

Methods

We used functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI) and Psychophysiological Interaction (PPI) analysis2 to investigate which prefrontal brain area could mediate the top-down process of illusory letter perception and how it might be functionally interconnected with letter sensitive areas in the occipito-temporal cortex. Additionally, hemispheric lateralization was assessed with a lateralization index (LI toolbox version 1.3)3. 

To trigger illusory letter perception, 20 subjects (11 women), mean age 23.3 years (SD = 2.7 years), were presented with 800 random noise images, equally divided over two fMRI sessions. Subjects were led to believe, that letters would be present on half of the trials. After each stimulus, subjects indicated whether they had perceived a letter or not. Results

Although there was never a letter present, subjects detected a letter in 29.76 % (SD = 11.18 %) of trials. The illusory detection of letters activated a distributed network consisting of prefrontal, parietal and occipito-temporal regions (see Fig. 2; contrast “letter detected vs. no-letter detected”). In the occipito-temporal cortex, the network encompassed several letter-sensitive brain regions, namely the visual word form area (VWFA) and a more posterior located area termed occipital word form area (OWFA) as well as their right-hemispheric homologues. In the lateral prefrontal cortex, activation was found in the lateral prefrontal cortex (lPFC), the insula and the dorso-medial prefrontal cortex (dmPFC). In contrast, the mOFC was deactivated. 

Additionally, we analyzed the functional connectivity pattern between the occipito-temporal and prefrontal cortex. We calculated separate PPI analyses for each activated brain region in the occipito-temporal cortex as seed region (i.e., left VWFA and OWFA and their right-hemispheric homologues). All PPI analyses revealed similar results. They showed interactions with a large network encompassing parietal, occipito-temporal and prefrontal brain regions. 

Finally, illusory letter detection resulted in a significant shift of hemispheric lateralization to the right hemisphere compared to real letter perception (paired-t-test: t(19) = 4.20, p <0.001).

Conclusion

We were able to mostly generalize our previous findings on illusory face detection1 to another non-face object: letters. As in the illusory face detection paradigm we also found activation in a network including occipito-temporal and prefrontal areas for illusory letter detection. The occipito-temporal regions corresponded with letter-responsive areas such as the VWFA and the OWFA. Prefrontal activation for illusory letters followed a similar pattern to that observed in illusory face detection.

The PPI analysis revealed a systematic coupling between the mOFC and higher-order sensory regions (letters: OWFA and VWFA; faces: OFA and FFA), indicating an important role as top-down mediator in object detection. However, the lPFC was only coupled with face-sensitive but not letter-sensitive areas.

Top-down modulations as shown in this study are also crucial for the interpretation of social stimuli like facial expressions. Thus, the findings from this study provide a valuable basis for understanding aberrant top-down modulations of sensory inputs in psychiatric disorders like autism, affective disorders or schizophrenia.
