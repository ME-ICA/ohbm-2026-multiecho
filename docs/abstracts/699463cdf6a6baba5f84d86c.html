Introduction

Bipolar disorder (BD) is a severe affective disorder characterized by recurrent depressive and (hypo)manic episodes that cause substantial impairments in social and occupational functioning and markedly increased suicide risk. Beyond these clinical features, BD is increasingly conceptualized as a disorder of large-scale brain networks rather than isolated regional abnormalities. Structural and functional imaging studies consistently implicate fronto-limbic and default-mode circuits, showing altered integration and segregation in networks supporting emotion regulation, interoception, and cognitive control [1]. At the same time, BD is highly heritable and polygenic, and converging evidence points to dysregulated synaptic, mitochondrial, immune, and circadian pathways, suggesting that molecular disturbances may render specific networks particularly vulnerable to dysconnectivity. Imaging–transcriptomic approaches now offer the opportunity to link these levels by asking how polygenic risk and regional gene-expression profiles constrain the topology of BD-related connectome alterations, with the overarching goal of identifying biologically grounded targets for improved prognosis and treatment.

Methods

Participants were drawn from a subsample of the Marburg–Münster Affective Disorders Cohort Study [2] and included individuals with BD and healthy controls (HC). The final sample comprised 126 BD patients (mean age = 41.7 ± 12.2 years, range = 19 – 64, 53.1 % female) and 852 HCs (mean age = 33.9 ± 12.6 years, range = 18 – 65; 63.4 % female). All participants provided written informed consent, and fulfilled study-wide inclusion and exclusion criteria (e.g., verbal IQ > 80, no neurological disorders, no history of significant head trauma, no current benzodiazepine use).

High-resolution T1-weighted and diffusion-weighted MRI data were acquired at 3 T scanners in Marburg and Münster using harmonized protocols. Structural brain networks were derived by deterministic tractography using the Lausanne120/Cammoun subdivision of the Desikan–Killiany atlas in FreeSurfer, as implemented in the CATO toolbox, resulting in weighted connectivity matrices [3]. From these matrices, node-wise connectivity strength and graph-theoretical indices of network integration and segregation (number of connections, global efficiency, clustering coefficient, small-worldness) were computed. 

Genome-wide genotyping with imputation to the 1000 Genomes Phase 3 reference generated an unrelated genetic subsample, from which a single polygenic risk score for BD (PRSBD) was derived. Population structure was controlled using multidimensional scaling components [4].

For each of the 114 cortical regions, nodal connectivity served as the dependent variable in separate robust linear regression models. Diagnosis models tested differences in nodal connectivity between BD and HC while adjusting for age, sex, and scanner. PRSBD models tested the association between PRS and nodal connectivity across diagnostic groups. Resulting node-wise t-values were visualized on the cortical surface. 

In a planned extension, a complementary imaging–transcriptomics analysis will be conducted by integrating the regional connectome measures with gene expression profiles from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA). AHBA samples will be mapped to the cortical parcellation and expression data will be preprocessed and normalized across donors using the abagen toolbox, yielding a region-by-gene expression matrix aligned with the 114 cortical network nodes. Next, for each node, imaging-derived metrics (e.g., diagnosis-related and PRS-related t values) will be correlated across regions with gene-wise expression profiles to obtain gene–phenotype association maps, while controlling for spatial autocorrelation via generative null models or spin tests, analogous to the approach used by Ecker et al. [5]. Gene sets showing significant spatial coupling with the connectomic patterns will then be subjected to enrichment analyses (e.g., Gene Ontology, cell-type markers, neurotransmitter systems) to identify molecular pathways and cell classes underlying BD-related network alterations and PRS effects, thereby linking macroscopic connectivity signatures to underlying transcriptional architecture.

Results

The node-wise analyses revealed widespread alterations of structural connectivity in bipolar disorder and comparable effects of genetic risk. In the diagnosis model, BD patients showed reduced nodal connectivity predominantly in ventral and medial prefrontal, anterior cingulate, and temporal regions, with relative sparing in more dorsal and posterior cortices (Figure 1a). In the genetic model, higher PRSBD was likewise associated with lower nodal connectivity in a largely overlapping fronto-limbic and temporal pattern and with relatively increased connectivity in posterior regions (Figure 1b). 

Conclusion

The present work could support a model in which polygenic liability and regional transcriptional architecture jointly shape large-scale structural dysconnectivity, rather than acting as independent influences. In clinical terms, these findings could point to fronto-limbic network dysfunction as a promising systems-level target for stratification and intervention, and motivate the development of treatments that modulate synaptic, immune, or circadian pathways specifically within these vulnerable circuits.
