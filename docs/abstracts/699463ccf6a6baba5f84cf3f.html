Introduction

The brain exhibits heightened neuroplasticity during adolescence. Yet, vulnerability to mental health problems also rises sharply, with most major psychiatric disorders first emerging during this time window. Puberty offers a critical lens for studying the neurodevelopmental mechanisms underlying this paradox, as surges in sex hormones trigger widespread brain remodeling that supports cognition and emotion regulation1. One hallmark of brain maturation is efficient inter-regional communication, which can be indexed by structure-function coupling (SFC)—the correspondence between white matter connections and their functional manifestations. SFC evolves rapidly in adolescence and is linked to cognitive abilities2. Yet, how pubertal processes shape SFC and relate to psychopathology remains understudied. Importantly, puberty unfolds differently in girls and boys, both behaviorally and neurobiologically. For instance, girls show earlier cortical maturation, stronger prefrontal-limbic engagement, and higher internalizing symptoms, while boys exhibit greater white matter growth, parietal-sensorimotor activation, and more externalizing behaviors3. It remains unclear whether sex-specific SFC trajectories may contribute to these divergent mental health risks. This study examines whether pubertal timing, a marker reflecting individual variation in pubertal progression, is associated with SFC, and whether SFC mediates the link between pubertal timing and internalizing symptoms. Both sex-specific and between-sex effects are evaluated to clarify how pubertal development shapes emerging mental health.

Methods

Participants: N = 629 participants from HCPD release 1.0 with complete imaging and behavioral data were included (N = 342 females). Analyses were conducted in the full sample (ages 6.1–21.99 years) and in a subgroup restricted to the pubertal window (ages 9–17 years; N = 401) to assess relations between pubertal timing, brain SFC, and symptom development.

Pubertal Timing: Pubertal development was assessed using self-reported physical and secondary sexual characteristics, based on the Sexual Maturation Scale (SMS) and the Pubertal Development Scale (PDS). A composite pubertal stage score was calculated as the average of the SMS and PDS scores. Pubertal timing was operationalized as the effect of pubertal stage beyond chronological age4.

Psychopathology: Internalizing, anxiety, and depression symptoms were measured using the Achenbach System of Empirically Based Assessment (ASEBA). Each of these psychopathology symptom measures was included as an outcome variable in our statistical models to estimate associations with brain SFC and pubertal timing.

Preprocessing of Imaging Data: Multimodal neuroimaging data from the HCP-D Release 1.0 dataset, including T1- and T2-weighted (T1w, T2w) structural MRI, resting-state fMRI, and diffusion MRI, were used in this study. fMRI data were preprocessed using the fMRIPrep pipeline (version 23.1.4) for head motion correction, susceptibility distortion correction, co-registration to T1w images, and resampling to HCP grayordinates space5. Resulting CIFTI images were further processed with the XCP-D pipeline (version 0.10.6) to identify high-motion outlier volumes, perform confound regression, and apply interpolation and bandpass filtering6. Diffusion MRI data were preprocessed using QSIPrep (version 0.16.1), including generation of a T1w reference template, T1w segmentation, MP-PCA denoising, Gibbs unringing, correct B1 field inhomogeneity, head motion, Eddy current, and susceptibility distortion, followed by Jacobian interpolation7.

Structural and Functional Connectivity: Whole-brain white-matter tracts were reconstructed from preprocessed dMRI data using the Schaefer-400 atlas via QSIPrep. Structural connectivity was defined as the number of probabilistic streamlines between regions, normalized by the average volume of the region pair, producing a 400 × 400 matrix per participant. Functional connectivity was calculated as pairwise Pearson correlations of regional time series from the same atlas, using only low-motion frames identified during XCP-D denoising. Correlation coefficients were Fisher Z-transformed before analysis. 

Structure-Function Coupling: SFC was quantified as the Spearman rank correlation between structural and functional connectivity profiles across regions, excluding zeros, which yielded a 400 × 400 coupling matrix per participant2. Network-level SFC was calculated by averaging coupling coefficients within the 7-network Schaefer-400 parcellation.

Statistical Analysis: Generalized Additive Models (GAMs) were used to capture potential nonlinear 1) effects of pubertal timing on SFC, 2) associations between psychopathology symptoms and pubertal timing, and 3) potential mediator of SFC in the link between symptoms and pubertal timing. Given our interest in identifying sex-specific and sex-differentiated effects, we allowed the smooth functions for age and pubertal timing to vary by sex, effectively estimating separate smooths for each sex within the same model. We then compute the confidence intervals to assess significant between-sex differences. All models additionally included sex and study site as covariates to account for potential confounding effects. Multiple comparisons were corrected using the False Discovery Rate (FDR) method.

Results

Puberty and age effects on SFC: At the network level, we found no evidence for pubertal timing effects on brain coupling in any of the seven large-scale networks. In contrast, strong age effects emerged in the somato-motor, visual, and salience networks (F’s>5.5; Figure 1A). When pubertal stage scores were included in the model, the age effect remained significant only for visual network SFC (F=7.06), suggesting substantial shared variance between age and pubertal stage in explaining SFC strength for the other two networks.

Sex-specific and sex-differentiated age effects on SFC: In contrast to insignificant effects of pubertal timing, age was significantly associated with visual network SFC in both sexes (F’s>5.3). Significant age effects were also observed in the salience network for girls (F=24.83) and in the somato-motor network for boys (F=6.98). Between-sex differences were observed for visual and salience networks, where boys showed stronger positive age-related increases in visual network SFC between ages 6 and 9, while girls exhibited stronger negative age-related effects on salience network SFC throughout the entire age range (6-22) except for 12-15 (Figure 1B).

Subgroup Analyses on SFC: Among participants aged 7–19, we replicated the main age effects on somato-motor network SFC (F=11.41), as well as sex-specific age effects in boys for this network (F=16.5). We also replicated sex-specific age effects on salience network SFC in girls (F=8.49), who showed high positive SFC strength at younger ages that declined over time. In contrast, boys demonstrated an increasing trajectory of salience network SFC over time that did not reach statistical significance. These sex-specific trajectories were significantly different, consistent with the findings for the full sample. 

Pubertal Timing effects on Psychopathology: Pubertal timing significantly affected internalizing symptoms across participants (=13.89), and within each sex (all >9.1), with no between-sex differences (Figure 1C). Effects were also observed for depression (=10.0) and anxiety (=4.63) across participants. Sex-specific analyses showed pubertal timing influenced depression in both sexes (all >4.3), with girls exhibiting a significantly larger symptom increase over the pubertal window. In contrast, pubertal timing effects on anxiety were restricted to boys (=4.36), with no sex difference. 

Subsample Tests on Psychopathology: In the 9-17 subgroup, effects of pubertal timing on internalizing symptoms were replicated (=8.08), including within each sex (all >4.9). Only depression was affected (=8.29), and only in girls (=9.19). No sex differences were observed for any psychopathology symptoms in this subgroup.

SFC Mediation Effects: No evidence supported a mediating role of network-level SFC in any of the seven networks. Similar null results were observed in the 9–17-year-old subsample.

Conclusion

This study identifies a dissociation between age- and puberty-related influences on adolescent brain development and mental health. Using the HCP-D dataset, we show that chronological age, but not pubertal timing, predicts brain structure–function coupling strength, whereas pubertal timing predicts psychopathology symptoms, independent of brain coupling. Notably, sex-specific and sex-differentiated trajectories emerge in both brain maturation and psychopathology during this developmental stage. These findings suggest that psychopathology risk in puberty may not be mediated by the integration of brain structure and function, as indicated by brain SFC strength. Overall, the results provide empirical evidence that developmental changes in brain and psychopathology follow distinct, sex-dependent trajectories. Future studies may consider alternative neurodevelopmental mechanisms, incorporating sex effects, to further clarify the links among puberty, brain development, and psychopathology risk.
