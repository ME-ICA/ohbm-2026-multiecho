Introduction

The impact of early-onset organ dysfunction is rarely restricted to one organ and likely has a systemic impact on brain development. The focus here is Biliary Atresia (BA), the most common early-onset liver condition characterized by inflammatory and fibrotic obliteration of the biliary tree, leading to cholestasis and liver failure. Despite early intervention surgery, over half will require a liver transplantation (LT) within early childhood. Whilst medical advances have dramatically improved survival, attention has shifted towards long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes and quality of life. 

Growing evidence suggests that BA’s impact extends beyond liver dysfunction, with studies documenting delayed childhood milestones, lower cognitive abilities, and impaired motor outcomes, regardless of liver transplantation in early childhood (Rodijk et al., 2018; Talcott et al., 2019). Previous work also highlights an increased likelihood of neurodevelopmental conditions, including autism (Earl et al., 2025).  Yet, whilst these findings suggest a neurodevelopmental vulnerability, the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. Given that the onset of BA coincides with the most critical period of brain development, investigating structural brain morphology represents one approach to identify biomarkers for altered neurodevelopment. 

This study aims to compare cortical thickness and cortical volume in adolescents and young adults with BA and age- and sex-matched healthy controls using structural MRI; a first step towards linking brain structure with neurodevelopmental and mental health outcomes in this population. This work utilizes data from the 'Brain Maturation in Liver Disease Project' at King's College Hospital (Ehrler et al., 2025) and controls from the European Autism Interventions - A Multi-centre Study for Developing New Medications (EU-AIMS) Longitudinal European Autism Project (LEAP) dataset (Loth et al., 2017).

Methods

Thirty-five adolescents and young people with BA (22 females; mean age=18.42 years, SD = 3.38) and 35 age/sex-matched neurotypical controls (18 females; mean age = 19.65 years, SD = 3.34) underwent a brain MRI scan and cognitive IQ assessment using the Wechsler Abbreviated Scale of Intelligence (WASI-II). Of the BA group, 25 had undergone a liver transplantation, and 10 were surviving with their native liver. 

 All MRI data (for both cohorts) were acquired on a 3T GE MR750 scanner. High-resolution T1-weighted data were collected from each participant using a 3D MPRAGE pulse sequence (repetition time/echo time = 7310/3.02 ms, flip angle = 11°, 196 sagittal slices, resolution = 1.2 × 1.1 × 1.1 mm³). 

Structural T1 images were processed using FreeSurfer Version 6.0 using a recon-all pipeline which performs several preprocessing steps, including motion correction and averaging, skull stripping, Talairach transformation, surface tessellation of the grey matter-white matter boundary, and surface-based cortical reconstruction using default parameters. Segmentation outputs were then visually inspected by three independent raters who identified inaccuracies in white matter and pial surface delineation. Manual edits were used to correct surface reconstruction errors in approximately 30% of the total sample. Cortical thickness and volume values for both left and right hemispheres were regenerated and compared between groups using ANCOVAs controlling for age, sex, IQ, and intracranial volume (for cortical volume analyses only).

Results

Groups did not differ significantly in age, intracranial volume, or sex distribution, however, the typically developing group  had significantly higher WASI-II full-scale four IQ scores compared to the patients with BA (LEAP: M = 111.77, SD = 12.68; BMLD: M = 97.40, SD = 16.17; t = 4.14, p < .001).

As can be seen in figure 1, mean cortical volume and mean cortical thickness was higher in the typically developing group compared to the patients with BA for both hemispheres. Group comparisons for cortical volume, after adjusting for age, intracranial volume, IQ,  and sex, one-way ANCOVAs revealed significant group effects for both the left hemisphere, (F(1, 64) = 14.32, p < .001, ηp² = .18), and the right hemisphere, (F(1, 64) = 12.89, p < .001, ηp² = .17) . Across both hemispheres, age and intracranial volume were significant covariates (left: age F = 59.81, p < .001; ICV F = 161.29, p < .001; right: age F = 63.997, p < .001; ICV F = 162.90, p < .001), whereas IQ and sex were not. Similarly, for mean cortical thickness, after  controlling for age, IQ, and sex, ANCOVAs revealed significant group effects for both the left hemisphere, ( F(1, 65) = 8.99, p = .004, ηp² = .12), and the right hemisphere, (F(1, 65) = 10.46, p = .002, ηp² = .14). Age was a significant covariate in both models (p< .001), whereas IQ and sex were not.

Conclusion

Adolescents and young adults with BA have significantly lower cortical volume and cortical thickness in comparison to their peers. This study is, to our knowledge, the first to show structural brain alterations in a homogenous cohort of patients with BA. Thus, children with BA have both liver dysfunction and alterations in brain development which are present in adolescence and young adults. Ongoing work will examine whether these neuroanatomical differences are functionally relevant, i.e. associated with cognitive and/or neurodevelopmental outcomes (Ehrler & Earl et al., 2025). Further work is also needed to understand to what extent brain morphological differences in young people with BA arise due to early life liver dysfunction or whether BA and brain alterations are the result of common upstream causal influences.
