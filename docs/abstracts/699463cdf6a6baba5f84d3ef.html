Introduction

Deep neural networks have gained popularity as models for understanding the brain, as they offer controllable model systems for probing neural mechanisms and their development. A growing body of research has probed the similarity (or alignment) between deep neural networks and brain function through encoding models, which predict neural responses from network activations (reviewed by Oota et al.1). Such predictive ability may indicate functional correspondence between artificial and biological neural systems. Studies have examined this predictive ability by comparing networks with differing architecture, training data, and training objectives. More recently, researchers have begun comparing neural networks from different modalities2,3, highlighting for example unique contributions of audio-based models in explaining activity in the auditory cortex.

Despite these advances, it remains unclear whether and which brain regions are differentially predicted by diverse deep neural networks, as a systematic comparison of region-specific predictivity of neural networks from the same and across different modalities is lacking. Analyzing differences in regional predictivity across deep neural networks can help identify the factors, i.e both stimulus modality and deep neural network properties (architecture, training data, and training objective), that give rise to region-specific brain-like representations.

In the present study, we we aim to quantify: (1) the overall brain prediction performance and region-specific prediction patterns of diverse deep neural networks, (2) which brain region predictions improve with neural network training, and (3) which portions of brain variance are uniquely or jointly explained by deep neural networks from different modalities across cortical regions.

Methods

Dataset. We perform a systematic investigation of deep neural network based brain predictivity using a large-scale individual-level brain imaging dataset collected during naturalistic movie watching4. Participants (n=3) each viewed around 80 hours of video content while being scanned, including six seasons of the television series Friends along with one feature-length film (Hidden Figures), providing a rich, ecologically valid stimulus set that spans diverse audiovisual and linguistic content. This enables comparisons across neural networks with differing input modalities. We assess the brain encoding performance on out-of-distribution movie content to ensure the robustness of our findings.

Candidate neural networks. We fit multiple brain encoding models (n=11) based on carefully selected pretrained neural networks that span the available spectrum of auditory, visual, and language domains. We selected neural networks based on architecture (transformer-based models and convolutional neural networks, and number of parameters), training data (images, videos, audio, text, audio-text pairs), and learning objectives (e.g., classification, audio-to-text transcription, masked language modeling, contrastive learning, or self-supervised pre-training for masked language modeling or masked patch prediction). This diverse set was selected to enable relevant comparisons both across modalities and within them: for instance, between an audio neural network trained exclusively on audio data, and one optimized for audio-to-text conversion.

To capture meaningful variance in neural network representations (i.e. activations), we extracted features across layers, patches, and tokens using conditions that match their training and typical usage settings, including appropriate context lengths. We extracted representations at each MRI time point (TR) by presenting independent stimulus samples to the neural network. For audio, video, and language models, each sample included its temporal context.

Ridge regression based on neural network features. We separately reduced both the extracted internal representations from neural networks, and fMRI brain activity using Principal Component Analysis. We then used the representations concatenated across the 5th–7th TR prior to the MRI signal (to account for the hemodynamic delay) to predict the brain activity using ridge regression (Figure 1A). We estimated the model on Friends Seasons 1–5 with 5-fold cross validation, and predicted brain activity in the Hidden Figures test dataset. 

Model Performance Scores. We back-transformed both true and predicted brain activity from PCA to vertex space. We then quantified the prediction performance r using the Pearson correlation of the true and predicted brain activity time series at each vertex.

Significance testing. To assess the statistical significance of predictions from each neural network-based encoding model, we employed permutation approaches based on shuffling the predicted time series to derive a null distribution. 

Noise ceiling. We derived noise ceilings from the test-retest data in the movie-watching dataset to establish the theoretical upper bounds of prediction performance.

Stacked regression and variance decomposition. To combine predictions based on multiple neural networks (Llama-3-8B for language, Whisper-small for audio, and Slow-R50 for vision) we made use of stacked regression. To quantify variance contributions, we systematically compared stacked models with different modality combinations using a variance partitioning approach: We quantified unique contributions by comparing the full three-modality stacked model against models excluding each modality of interest. Pairwise shared contributions were computed by subtracting unique contributions from the total variance explained by two-modality combinations. The remaining contributions were quantified analogously.

Results

Overall, language- and audio-based encoding models performed best, with similar overall prediction scores for the three tested language models (Figure 1B). Among the auditory neural networks, whisper-small and whisper-large performed best. Predictions based on visual neural network embeddings had the lowest scores among the three modalities, though more recent models (CLIP-ViT, DinoV2, and slow_r50) slightly outperformed the older ResNet50.

We next examined whether neural network representations were predictive of activity in distinct brain regions by correlating vertex-wise prediction patterns (~59,000 elements) between all model pairs. High correlations indicate similar regional predictivity, while low correlations indicate differences in regional predictivity. Prediction performance of neural networks from the same modality showed remarkably consistent spatial patterns (r ≥ 0.96, Figure 1C), indicating they are predictive of the same brain regions but with varying predictive accuracy. This also holds true for the auditory modality, where neural networks had the largest differences in the global brain scores. This indicates that models from the same modality are predictive of the same brain regions, but vary in the degree of predictive accuracy of those regions. 

Examining the spatial patterns of predictions based on three modality-representative neural networks (Figure 2C-E), we found that predictions based on language neural networks (Llama-v3-8B) best explained activity in higher-order cortical regions, while predictions based on audio (whisper-small) and visual (slow_r50) neural networks showed peak performance in in their respective secondary sensory regions. Both language and audio network predictions captured activity around the superior temporal sulcus, with predictions based on audio networks substantially outperforming those based on language networks in this region. 

We then compared predictions based on trained vs untrained neural networks (Figure 2A-C, and Figure 1B) to test whether training leads to region-specific improvements in brain predictivity. Such improvements may indicate training-dependent functional specialization in neural networks. Most notably, we find that the predictions based on the trained language model outperform those based on its untrained counterpart in regions of the default mode network (DMN). In contrast, predictions based on trained audio and visual networks showed less regionally specific improvements over their untrained counterparts.

We next examined which portions of explained variance are attributable to a single modality versus shared across multiple modalities (Figure 2D-L). This gives an indication of the degree to which each brain region relies on modality-specific or shared representations. For that, we decomposed explained variance into unique and shared contributions (Figure 2D-L). We find substantial contributions by the audio neural network (whisper-small), with unique auditory variance (up to 17%) and audio-language shared variance (up to 19%) both peaking in auditory cortex, where they jointly explained most variance. Beyond auditory cortex, unique auditory contributions appeared in language-related regions (left IFSp/44/IFJa; ~5%) and weakly in higher-order visual regions (~5%). Predictions based on Large language models (LLMs) showed their strongest unique contributions (up to 13.5%) in motor and premotor regions (areas 4, 5L, 6mp) and language regions (right 44/45), with moderate contributions to temporal and parietal regions (Figure 4F,G,I). Unique visual contributions were much lower overall (up to 5.8%), concentrated in early visual (V1-V3), color/form (V4), scene-processing (PHA3), and motion-processing regions (MT, MST, DVT; Figure 4E).

Conclusion

The finding of close to identical explanatory patterns of deep neural networks (DNNs) from the same modalities suggests an important role for stimulus-driven self-organization in DNNs, whereby brain predictive representations emerge from the statistics of sensory input rather than specific network architecture. This is most pronounced in the case of language neural networks (LLMs), for which we also did not observe any marked differences in overall prediction performance, despite intentionally selecting a diverse range of state-of-the-art models (e.g. ranging from 1.7B to 8B in parameter size, with different training data composition, and coming from largely different actors). Similarly, Chen & Bonner5 showed that the most brain-aligned representations were universal across neural networks and thus independent of their specific characteristics. We surmise that these representations may emerge when trained on sufficiently diverse data. 

Although brain prediction performance has been linked with parameter number in language and audio neural networks (Antonello, 2023), we found no differences across our three language neural networks and observed the opposite effect for audio neural networks, where the smaller version of whisper reached top predictivity by a small margin.Our results echo studies showing that the parameter-to-performance relationship is complex, with inconsistent findings across parameter ranges, whereby training data size (which typically scales with parameter count) could act as a confound.

The increase of LLM brain predictivity in response to training fits well with the DMN's established role in interpreting social interaction, narrative understanding, and tracking semantic richness, as these are all functions central to extracting meaning from the linguistic content that LLMs are trained on. The DMN’s link to memory systems, and its role in integrating internal and external information over long timescales parallels how LLMs synthesize diverse information across extensive contexts. 

Beyond characterizing what functions DNNs capture and how they acquire them, these models can also serve as probes of brain organization. Our findings suggest widespread multimodal processing across cortical regions, with evidence of vision, audio, and language information present in primary sensory regions, including language information in V1. This is consistent with evidence that primary sensory cortex captures more than purely unimodal activity.

In the present work, we showed that neural networks from the same modality yield similar brain predictions, pointing to stimulus-driven self-organization; that training of language neural networks specifically increases their predictivity for the DMN, suggesting higher-order functions emerge through learning; and that explained variance overlaps across neural network modalities, revealing shared representations between sensory streams. Our work thus demonstrates that deep neural networks can serve as powerful tools for understanding functional specialization.
