Introduction

Over the last two decades, network science has proved foundational to cognitive neuroscience by revealing how functional connections within brain networks support and disrupt cognition1. Although functional connectivity in humans is primarily quantified with functional magnetic resonance imaging, neurophysiological methods, including electroencephalography (EEG) and magnetoencephalography (MEG) have also been used. Functional connectivity in M/EEG is measured through the statistical couplings of neural oscillations—when multiple brain regions are electrically oscillating in rhythmic synchrony2. However, recent work has demonstrated that EEG functional connectivity is rarely driven by rhythmic oscillations, instead it is dominated by the dynamics of a nonrhythmic, broadband component of neurophysiological signals, aperiodic activity3. Although traditionally dismissed as “1/f noise” or conflated with neural rhythms, aperiodic activity has garnered increasing attention in cognitive and translational neuroscience4. Aperiodic activity can fluctuate in time5, but the relevance of aperiodic dynamics to cognitive brain networks has not yet been explored. Here, I evaluate the utility of aperiodic activity as a basis for brain network connectivity in intracranial EEG (iEEG) recorded from eleven participants performing a visual target detection task.

Methods

Aperiodic connectivity was computed for eleven iEEG participants with intractable epilepsy, acquired from an archival dataset recorded between 2009-2012. Participants performed a visual target detection task in which participants identified targets (white circles) from a stream of images presented on a screen with a button press. Images were presented for 500ms with an inter-onset interval of 1800-2200ms and included grayscale faces, houses, tools, scrambled faces, and letter strings in addition to targets. Each patient performed the task at least once and up to four times.

iEEG signals from 128 implanted electrocorticography (ECoG) and stereoencephalography (sEEG) sensors were acquired at 500Hz while participants performed the task. Electrodes were placed solely upon clinical indication. iEEG data were visually inspected, bad electrodes were excluded, remaining electrodes were referenced to local averages (grid/strip/probe), and the data were down sampled to 250Hz. To compute time-resolved aperiodic activity, 1) a time-frequency representation (spectrogram) of the full recording was obtained using a 1-second sliding-window multi-taper method for each iEEG electrode (Fig. 1A-B). The spectrogram was decimated by a factor of ten, yielding a time resolution of 25 Hz. 2) The power spectrum at each time point was parameterized between 2-100 Hz using spectral parameterization (Fig. 1C). The aperiodic component was fit with a knee, and periodic components (peaks) were fit if they exceeded a threshold of 2.0 standard deviations, with maximum 6 peaks fit with bandwidths between 2-20 Hz. 3) The aperiodic exponent of each time point was extracted for each electrode, providing a time series of aperiodic exponent dynamics with a temporal resolution of 25 Hz (Fig. 1D).

Aperiodic connectivity matrices were computed by electrode-wise Pearson correlation over the entire duration of the task. To assess the reliability and consistency of aperiodic connectivity within participants, the full-task connectivity matrix for each run was correlated with all other runs for that participant using a Pearson correlation of the vectorized upper triangle of each connectivity matrix. The reported statistic (r) is the average coefficient of all participants across all included runs of the task.

Results

Aperiodic connectivity matrices were highly conserved across multiple runs of the target detection task within participants (Pearson correlation: mean(r) = 0.727, p < 0.001, n = 11). Despite heterogeneous electrode coverage between participants, aperiodic connectivity for the entire task duration was highly similar within participants and was dominated by positive correlations (Fig. 2).

Conclusion

Here, I employ a novel, time-resolved approach to investigate the utility of temporal dynamics in aperiodic neural activity to inform cognitive networks. Applying this approach to human iEEG recorded from eleven participants performing a visual target detection task reveals that aperiodic connectivity matrices are highly conserved within participants across multiple, independent runs of the task. These findings illuminate the potential of aperiodic connectivity to study brain networks involved in cognition. Further investigation will interrogate the relevance of aperiodic networks in i/M/EEG to specific functions in attention and cognition.
