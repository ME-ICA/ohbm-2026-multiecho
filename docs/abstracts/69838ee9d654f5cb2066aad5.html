Introduction

Preterm birth is one of the leading causes of child mortality worldwide and, among survivors, is associated with a wide range of long-term adverse outcomes. These disadvantages span multiple domains, including cognition, language development, motor function, and an increased risk for psychiatric conditions later in life. Understanding how prematurity alters early brain development is therefore critical, particularly during the neonatal period, which is characterized by heightened neuroplasticity and rapid network reorganization. Most neuroimaging studies of prematurity have relied on static graph-theoretical measures of the functional connectome^1, providing important but limited insights into early brain organization. More recently, dynamic functional connectivity approaches have been introduced, yet these typically remain restricted to relatively simple network metrics and have yielded findings that are inconsistent across studies.

A key limitation of both static and dynamic graph-theoretical approaches is that they overlook the hierarchical organization of brain networks. The brain can be conceptualized as a complex system operating near a critical regime, characterized by flexible transitions between integrated and segregated states across multiple spatial and temporal scales. Capturing these hierarchical and state-dependent properties is likely essential for understanding how prematurity perturbs typical developmental trajectories and how such perturbations may relate to later cognitive outcomes. In this study, we apply Nested Spectral Partitioning (NSP)^2 a hierarchical network analysis method, to characterize both static and dynamic properties of the neonatal functional connectome. We explored differences between preterm and full-term neonates and examine how early hierarchical network dynamics relate to cognitive, motor, and language development assessed at 18 months of age.

Methods

Data were drawn from the third release of the Developing Human Connectome Project (dHCP)^3, collected with ethical approval from the UK Health Research Authority (14/LO/1169) and written parental consent. The sample includes full-term (FT) and preterm (PT) neonates spanning 23–43 weeks gestational age (GA) at birth, with a longitudinal subset scanned twice between birth and term-equivalent age. Only datasets with complete structural and resting-state fMRI data and radiological quality scores ≤4 were included.

Neurodevelopmental outcomes were assessed at 18 months corrected age using the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development, Third Edition (Bayley-III). For each domain (Cognitive, Language, and Motor), raw sum scores were used to retain sensitivity to age-related differences in performance. These sum scores were used as behavioural measures in subsequent brain–behaviour association analyses.

Resting-state fMRI was acquired using a neonatal-optimized multiband EPI sequence (TR = 392 ms, TE = 38 ms, 2.15 mm isotropic resolution, 15 min). Preprocessing followed the dHCP functional pipeline^4, including slice-to-volume motion correction, susceptibility distortion correction using opposing phase-encoded spin-echo images, and ICA-based denoising (ICA-FIX). Cortical surfaces were generated using dHCP structural pipelines; updated deep-learning–based surface reconstruction will be used in subsequent analyses.

Analyses were performed in native cortical surface space. The Myers–Labonté neonatal parcellation was resampled from template to individual surfaces using Connectome Workbench with adaptive barycentric interpolation and surface-area correction. Motion-corrected functional data were projected to the cortical surface, and parcel-wise BOLD time series were obtained by averaging vertex-wise signals within each parcel. Parcels with >20% missing data were excluded. Subject-specific functional connectivity (FC) matrices were computed using Pearson correlations between parcel time series. 

Hierarchical network organization was quantified using Nested Spectral Partitioning (NSP)^1. Static FC matrices were submitted to eigenvector decomposition to identify a nested modular structure across hierarchical levels. At each level, hierarchical components were quantified as a function of eigenvalue magnitude, number of modules, and module size homogeneity. Whole-brain hierarchical integration was computed at the first level, while hierarchical segregation was computed as the summed contribution of all lower upcoming levels. Static hierarchical balance was defined as the difference between integration and segregation components.

Dynamic FC was estimated using a rolling window of 154 time points (62.26 s), yielding time-resolved hierarchical balance trajectories. From these, dynamic properties were derived, including mean deviation magnitude relative to static balance, proportion of time spent in integration- versus segregation-dominated states, and switching frequency, defined as transitions exceeding ±5% of static balance.

Group-level analyses compared FT and PT infants on static and dynamic hierarchical measures using mixed-effects models accounting for repeated scans, postmenstrual age at scan, and sex. Associations between hierarchical balance metrics and gestational age at birth were assessed using regression analyses. All analyses were implemented in Python 3.10 and R 4.4.0. 

Results

Dynamic NSP analyses revealed group differences in hierarchical brain organization. Full-term neonates showed significantly higher dynamic hierarchical integration strength compared to preterm neonates, indicating a greater propensity for globally coordinated network states, whereas preterm neonates exhibited significantly higher dynamic hierarchical segregation strength, reflecting a relative bias toward locally specialized and fragmented configurations. These group differences were corroborated using non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum (Mann–Whitney U) tests, which demonstrated a robust effect for integration strength (U = 23,301.00, p < .001, r = 0.588) and segregation strength (U = 86,426.00, p < .001, r = −0.528), corresponding to large effect sizes. In contrast, no significant group differences were observed for the temporal persistence of either integration or segregation states (integration time: U = 55,920.50, p = .806, r = 0.011; segregation time: U = 57,205.50, p = .806, r = −0.011), indicating comparable dwell times across birth groups. However, transition frequency differed significantly between groups (U = 83,059.50, p < .001, r = −0.468), suggesting altered flexibility in switching between hierarchical network states in preterm neonates relative to their full-term counterparts.

Hierarchical dynamic measures showed selective associations with neurodevelopmental outcomes assessed at 18 months of age. Across NSP–behaviour pairings, the strongest brain–behaviour relationships were modest in magnitude although statistically significant, indicating that individual differences in network dynamics tracked variance in later developmental performance. Specifically, higher transition frequency (flexibility) was negatively correlated with cognitive scores (r = −0.090, p = 0.0325), higher segregation strength was negatively correlated with language scores (r = −0.100, p = 0.0177), and higher integration strength was positively correlated with motor performance (r = 0.095, p = 0.0236).

Conclusion

This study provides converging evidence that prematurity is associated with alterations in the dynamic hierarchical organization of the neonatal functional connectome. Relative to full-term infants, preterm neonates show a pronounced shift toward reduced hierarchical integration and increased hierarchical segregation, reflecting an early imbalance in the capacity to flexibly transition between globally coordinated and locally specialized network states. Importantly, these group-level differences were not confined to static differences in network topology but extended to dynamic properties of hierarchical organization, highlighting a reconfiguration of how large-scale brain networks evolve over time in the context of prematurity.

By explicitly modelling hierarchical and time-resolved network dynamics, the NSP framework captures aspects of functional brain organization that are not accessible through conventional static graph-theoretical measures. Beyond identifying group differences, the observed associations between dynamic hierarchical measures and cognitive, language, and motor outcomes at 18 months suggest that early deviations in hierarchical network dynamics carry functional relevance for later neurodevelopment. Although these brain–behaviour relationships were modest in magnitude, their consistent directionality, where greater integration and flexibility, and lower segregation, aligned with more favourable developmental profiles, mirror the group differences between preterm and full-term infants in brain network organisation.

Together, these findings indicate that early hierarchical network dynamics constitute a sensitive marker of developmental vulnerability following preterm birth. More broadly, they underscore the importance of incorporating hierarchical and state-dependent properties of brain organization into models of early neurodevelopment, as such properties may help us to better understand early functional network alterations and their association with longer-term cognitive and behavioural outcomes.
