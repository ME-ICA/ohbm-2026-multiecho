Introduction

Longitudinal structural MRI research in adolescence aims to quantify developmental change, yet this requires the assumption that differences between sessions reflect true anatomical variation rather than measurement noise.^1 Because adolescent scans are particularly vulnerable to short-term behavioural and physiological fluctuations, such as momentary differences in head motion, sleep, and stress, these state-related influences can introduce variability that mimics or obscures true anatomical change. Distinguishing genuine developmental effects from such noise is therefore essential for valid interpretation.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the test–retest reliability of structural MRI measures in adolescents over a one-week interval and to determine whether day-to-day behavioural states contribute to session-specific noise. We hypothesised that reliability would be lowest for cortical thickness, folding metrics, and small subcortical nuclei, and that greater head motion, poorer sleep quality and higher stress would each be associated with increased session-specific noise in structural estimates.

Methods

In this study, 79 adolescents (16–17 years) underwent two T1-weighted scans exactly one week apart, acquired on the same scanner, same protocol, and same time of day, thereby minimising technical and circadian noise. Structural measures were extracted using FreeSurfer 7.4.1, including cortical morphometry, folding metrics, and subcortical volumes. Reliability was evaluated using ICC(2,1) at two spatial scales: (1) global, reflecting whole-brain averages, and (2) local, reflecting parcellated estimates across 68 cortical regions and predefined subcortical structures. ^2

To separate true structural variance from session-specific noise, we additionally applied structural equation modelling (SEM), which decomposes each measure into a latent “true” component and day-specific residual variance. ^3 Same-day behavioural states head motion, sleep quality, and stress were linked to these residuals to test whether behavioural fluctuations contribute to measurement noise.

Results

Consistent with prior work, structural MRI showed high global reliability, ^4 especially for surface area (ICC = .98) and cortical volume (ICC = .91). Global cortical thickness was the least reliable morphometric measure (ICC = .76). At the local level, reliability varied considerably: cortical thickness displayed the lowest regional stability, with pronounced reductions in the frontal and temporal poles, the insula, and the entorhinal cortex. Among folding measures, Gaussian curvature showed poor global reliability (ICC = .39), with the lowest regional ICCs observed in the right fusiform gyrus, posterior temporal regions, and parts of the parahippocampal cortex. Subcortical structures were generally reliable, yet small nuclei showed reduced stability, including the nucleus accumbens (ICC = .46) and optic chiasm (ICC = .27).

SEM revealed that behavioural states meaningfully contributed to session-specific noise, offering insight beyond what ICCs alone captures. Stress was the only behaviour showing whole-brain effects, whereas motion, sleep, and stress each predicted region-specific residual deviations across cortical and subcortical areas. Importantly, these effects were not uniformly directional: higher stress, poorer sleep, or greater motion did not simply produce “more error”, but rather shifted measurements upward or downward depending on region and metric. This indicates that behavioural states modulate how far estimates deviate from the latent anatomical value, rather than inflating instability in a single direction.

Conclusion

By combining classical reliability metrics with SEM-based modelling of behaviour-linked noise, this study not only characterises measurement precision but also provides deeper context for why reliability varies across regions and individuals, an analytical perspective rarely included in test–retest MRI studies. These noise estimates, together with the identification of sensitive regions (e.g., temporal/insular cortex, Gaussian curvature regions, small nuclei), can serve as benchmarks for longitudinal multi-wave studies using the same scanner and protocol. Such benchmarks may help researchers establish minimum detectable change thresholds, monitor behavioural states that influence scan quality, and interpret observed developmental effects with greater confidence that they reflect true anatomical change rather than measurement variability.
