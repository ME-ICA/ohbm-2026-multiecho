Introduction

Auditory processing is essential in daily life, helping us monitor our surroundings, recognize important events, and learn from sound patterns to anticipate future outcomes. Auditory processing involves a distributed network of cortical and subcortical regions, with primary auditory cortex playing a central role in the analysis of basic acoustic features such as frequency [1]. Well established secondary auditory areas include the superior temporal gyrus (STG), which further integrate and abstract these features to support higher-order auditory functions, including speech perception and language processing [2]. Despite a large body of literature focusing on primary and secondary auditory areas in the lateral temporal cortex, the contribution of additional brain regions such as the insula, fronto-parietal networks or medial temporal lobe structures including the hippocampus and amygdala remains unclear. Recent work in humans has shown that both the medial temporal lobe [3] and the insula [4][5] exhibit rich auditory responses, but their sensitivity to different facets of auditory processing, including pitch and predictive processing remain unclear. The aim of the current work is to characterize auditory evoked neurophysiological responses in these regions using intracranial electroencephalography (iEEG) data. We hypothesized that the human brain encodes auditory stimuli along an extended auditory network including primary and secondary auditory areas as well as medial temporal, fronto-parietal and insular lobes. Moreover, we hypothesized that a fine-grained encoding of auditory characteristics including pitch and stimulus complexity would be observed in areas that are low in this hierarchy, with higher areas encoding mostly abstract auditory features.

Methods

We recorded iEEG activity in patients undergoing presurgical monitoring for drug-resistant focal epilepsy (N = 20). Patients were implanted with depth electrodes and grid arrays. Electrode coverage was concentrated in the lateral temporal lobe, with additional sampling in parietal and frontal cortices, medial temporal structures and the insula. Patients were presented with sequences of auditory stimuli while fixating on a central cross. The stimuli comprised of pure tones, complex tones and pitch glides, as well as phonemes, words and sentences. For the context of the present study iEEG responses to pure and complex tones were analysed. Those consisted of four different frequencies (512, 1024, 2048 and 4096 Hz) and were presented in two different conditions: pattern (with the tones repeated in a specific order) and random (with the tones in a random order). We extracted event related potentials (iERPs) (1 to 40 Hz) and high-frequency activity (HFA) (80 to 150 Hz) responses to the sounds. We computed their onset and peak using permutation tests comparing the post-stimulus interval to a pre-stimulus baseline (p<0.05). Moreover, we performed a multivariate pattern analysis to decode the sound frequency from iEEG signals. To this aim we trained and tested single-patient logistic regression classifiers in a 5-fold cross validation for electrodes within pre-defined regions of interest. Last, we used a temporal generalization analysis by training and testing classifiers along all of the available data points.

Results

We analysed N = 1,987 iEEG electrodes across the patient cohort. Among the recorded electrodes, N = 349 showed a significant iERP and N = 92 an HFA response to the auditory stimuli. The earliest response onset was observed in the lateral temporal lobe (79.1 +/- 13.6 ms), followed by the insula (85.1 +/- 36.7 ms), parietal (123 +/- 29.1 ms), frontal (153.8 +/- 30.1 ms), and medial temporal lobe (158.8 +/- 35.4 ms). HFA responses were the fastest in insula electrodes (35.8 +/- 14.8 ms) followed by electrodes in the lateral temporal lobe (45.7 +/- 7.5 ms) and parietal lobe (95.1 +/- 59.1 ms). The frontal and medial temporal lobe showed only sparse HFA responses to the tones. We then investigated the sensitivity of each brain region to different facets of auditory processing, using multivariate pattern analysis. To this aim, we decoded the identity of the auditory stimuli in a 4-class classification. We obtained a high decoding performance in the insula and the lateral temporal lobe. In the insula, we found an above chance accuracy of 36.96% at 119 ms aftersound onset, while for the lateral temporal lobe an accuracy of 41.96%, peaking at 95.7 ms after sound onset (with theoretical 4-class chance at 0.25). Results were similar for complex tones. For the remaining regions, decoding performance was indistinguishable from chance. Last, we performed a temporal generalization analysis, which showed that iEEG patterns reflecting the identify of pure and complex tones in the lateral temporal lobe persisted for ~250 ms around the diagonal, suggesting a sustained representation of their identity. For the insula the temporal generalization analysis showed less generalizable patterns and overall lower decoding results.

Conclusion

Our results established a hierarchy of auditory processing which extends beyond the auditory cortex and includes parietal, frontal, insular, and medial temporal lobe structures. The lateral temporal lobe and the insula were the earliest regions in this hierarchy and the only ones encoding the frequency of the presented stimuli, with the lateral temporal lobe showing a stronger temporal generalization, suggesting the presence of longer-lasting, stable auditory patterns. Moreover, we highlight the role of the insula as a key auditory region, which not only responds to the auditory stimuli, but also shows sensitivity to their identity.
