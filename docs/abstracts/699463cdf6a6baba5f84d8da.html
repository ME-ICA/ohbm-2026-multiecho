Introduction

Ultra-High Field (UHF) fMRI enables the investigation of brain functional architecture with high spatial resolution and sensitivity. However, UHF imaging also introduces technical challenges, including magnetic field inhomogeneities, image distortions, and artifacts related to thermal and physiological noise, which can diminish these benefits. Ensuring robust quality control is therefore critical for reproducibility and standardization, especially in the context of the current reproducibility crisis in fMRI. Nevertheless, few studies have systematically addressed data quality at UHF, and even at lower field strengths, approaches to quality assessment remain heterogeneous and investigator-dependent.

Recent efforts have aimed to define benchmarks for resting-state fMRI quality assessment. A promising approach is the System Identifiability (SI) index introduced in Mahadevan et al.1, which quantifies how easily a community structure emerges from atlas-based Functional Connectivity (FC) matrices. This framework builds on the assumption that the macroscopic organization of resting state networks is highly consistent across subjects and datasets, making SI a robust tool for quality evaluation.

Here we introduce SyNR, a Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR)-based metric that quantifies average correlation strengths within and between resting-state networks to evaluate System Identifiability. SyNR was applied across three 7 T UHF-fMRI datasets, characterized by small voxel sizes and high-resolution FC matrices. We compared SyNR with other SI-based quality benchmarks1–3, demonstrating its interpretability and robustness. Finally, we examine the relationship between SyNR scores with six native acquisition metrics of the fMRI runs, showing that framewise displacement and temporal SNR are the most influential metrics for reliable identification of resting state networks. These findings support SyNR as a valid tool for evaluating data quality in UHF resting-state fMRI.

Methods

Datasets description and preprocessing

The UHF fMRI datasets used in this work are described as follows. Datasets B and C (Imago7 Foundation, Pisa, Italy) include 25 and 15 healthy subjects, respectively, each with one run. Dataset B is fully described in Acquafredda et al.4 From Dataset B we extracted one T1w anatomical scan (0.43×0.43×0.8 mm³), one functional scan (90 slices, 1.5 mm³, TR/TE = 3000/23 ms, 160 volumes), and one scan with reversed phase encoding. Dataset C includes one T1w scan (1.0 mm³), one functional scan (58 slices, 1.5x1.5x2.0 mm³, TR/TE = 3000/22 ms, 160 volumes), and one scan with reversed phase encoding. Dataset G provides 7T Rs-fMRI recordings relative to 22 healthy subjects (functional scan with 70 slices, 1.5 mm³ iso, TR/TE 3000/17 ms, 300 volumes). Full information for dataset G is available at:  https://doi.org/10.1038/sdata.2014.54.

A unified preprocessing pipeline was applied to all datasets (AFNI, FreeSurfer). Preprocessing steps included retrospective correction using respiratory and cardiac signals, despiking, blip correction, slice-time correction, volume registration using 6-parameter linear alignment, and nonlinear warping to the anatomical scan and the MNI 2009 Nonlinear Asymmetric template.

GLM analysis regressed out 4th-degree polynomials, 6 motion parameters and their derivatives, and mean white matter and CSF signals. Temporal band-pass filtering (0.01–0.1 Hz) and motion censoring (>0.2mm) was applied, with no spatial smoothing. Subjects were excluded only in case of acquisition failures in both fMRI or physiological recordings, retaining finally 25, 14 and 18 subjects for subsequent analysis from B,C and G datasets respectively.

Six quality metrics were extracted: mean framewise displacement (FD), maximal motion during the run (MM), remaining degrees of freedom (DF) after GLM, standardized DVARS after motion correction, intrinsic blur with full width at half maximum (FWHM), and temporal signal-to-noise ratio (tSNR), estimated on untreated runs (Figure 1A).

System-to-Noise Ratio Calculation

Cortical regions were defined using the 400-region Schaefer parcellation5 and assigned to seven resting-state networks5. Functional connectivity was estimated using Pearson correlation between regional time series, yielding a 400 × 400 symmetric FC matrix (Figure 1B-C). Given an FC matrix cij, and a membership matrix mij for the ROIs, we defined:

SyNR = 20 log10 (ρintra/ρinter) [1]

with,

ρintra={<|cij|> ∀ (i,j) | mij≠0}   ρinter={<|cij|> ∀ (i,j) | mij=0} [2,3]

Where SyNR quantifies the logarithmic ratio between the average absolute correlation strength within the regions of each resting state network, and outside those networks (Figure 1C).

Benchmark evaluation and comparison

We compared SyNR to other SI-related benchmarks: 

  • the mean of correlation values inside RS networks2 (“InCorr”)
  • the System Identifiability index given by Louvain community detection algorithm, as implemented in Mahadevan et al.1 (“SILouvain”)
  • the tFCC contrast, corresponding to the Z statistics for a t-test for the distinction of the distribution of Pearson correlations within and between RS networsk3 (“tFCC”)
  • the similarity of each FC matrix to the dataset average matrix, calculated with Pearson correlation of weights (“MeanCorr”)

We evaluated Pearson correlation between the scores obtained in the three UHF fMRI datasets and calculated the coefficient of variation. We compared the statistical distribution for each metric with Kruskal Wallis test (alpha=0.01) and Kolmogorov Smirnov test (alpha=0.01). We showed the best and worst subject data, according to SyNR, providing the FC matrix and including snapshots of correlation volumes for 3 seeds in Default Mode Network, Visual Network and Auditory Cortex respectively (AFNI). We finally calculated and showed the Pearson correlation between SyNR and the 6 fMRI acquisition metrics, testing each coefficient with significance to p<0.01.

Results

The SyNR showed a mean of 4.70, 4.22, and 4.55 (a.u.) for the G, C, and B datasets, respectively, with standard deviations of 1.20, 0.85, and 0.95. The coefficient of variation ranged between 0.20 and 0.25 across datasets. SILouvain showed mean values of 0.16, 0.22, and 0.18 (a.u.) and standard deviations between 0.07 and 0.09, with a coefficient of variation between 0.43 and 0.47.

tFCC, representing the Z score for the distinction of within vs between networks correlations showed means of 88, 75, and 87, with a standard deviation of 10; the maximal coefficient of variation was 0.16 in dataset C. MeanCorr, representing the Pearson correlation of each FC matrix weight to the group average, ranged from 0.64 to 0.70, with a standard deviation of 0.05 and a coefficient of variation of 0.06–0.07. Finally, InCorr averaged 0.42, 0.34, and 0.44 for the G, C, and B datasets, with standard deviations of 0.07 and a coefficient of variation ranging from 0.16 to 0.22.

The distributions of SyNR and SILouvain values showed no statistical differences between datasets, whereas InCorr, tFCC, and MeanCorr differed significantly (Kruskal-Wallis test, α = 0.01). All metric distributions were non-Gaussian (Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, p < 0.01).

Figure 2A depicts the Pearson correlation between SyNR and the other SI indices. Results show variable behavior across metrics. SyNR correlates strongly with SILouvain (R² = 0.67 and 0.70 for G and B, p < 0.01) and with tFCC (R² = 0.69, 0.69, and 0.63 for G, C, and B, p < 0.01). In contrast, InCorr shows a significant negative correlation with SyNR, SILouvain, and tFCC (R² = 0.67 and 0.70, p < 0.01 for G and B). MeanCorr shows no consistent correlations with any other index.

In the same figure, cases (a) and (b) in panel B illustrate the highest and lowest SyNR subjects, respectively. Their FC matrices show clear differences: (a) exhibits a well-defined block structure with high diagonal correlations and low background correlations, while (b) is cluttered, with widespread all-to-all correlations. These patterns produce opposite results in seed-based volumetric correlations: (a) shows clear volumes for the Default Mode and Visual networks, whereas (b) introduces spurious correlations. Both subjects underwent the same preprocessing, including motion and physiological corrections, however a more aggressive denoising or exclusion may be warranted for the second subject. Notably, this subject (b) also ranks highest for within-network correlation (InCorr, Figure 2A, first panel) and shows average data quality in group similarity (MeanGroup, Figure 2A, last panel).

Given the non-significant differences in SyNR distributions among the G, C, and B datasets, we finally calculated correlations between SyNR and six native fMRI quality metrics (FD, MM, DF, sDVARS, FWHM, and tSNR) across all 57 subjects. Significant correlations were observed only with Framewise Displacement (r = –0.44, p < 0.01) and tSNR (r = 0.43, p < 0.01).

Conclusion

In this work, we assessed System Identifiability using SyNR, an SNR-based metric that contrasts average within and between-network correlations in functional connectivity matrices. The availability of three 7T UHF datasets (with 1.5 mm³ voxels) allowed the use of the Schaefer 400 region parcellation5, producing high-resolution FC matrices (80’000 weights, Figure 1C) with clear network contrast.

The strong correlation observed between SyNR and both tFCC and SILouvain supports the reliability of the proposed framework in capturing resting-state network organization (Figure 2A). SyNR shows an intermediate coefficient of variation between SILouvain and tFCC which could suggest a balance between stability and information of the measure, although no reference for an optimal coefficient of variation exists. By contrast to SyNR and SILouvain, tFCC shows a significantly different distribution between datasets, limiting the feasibility of joint ensemble analyses. In this context, data harmonization strategies may be beneficial.

A key feature of the SyNR methodology is the interpretability of the index, as its values can be directly related to the structure of the Functional Connectivity matrices (Figure 2B). By contrast, SI-Louvain is derived from a heuristic community detection approach, and its values may depend on the specific convergence of the algorithm.

The SyNR index proves robust also in the presence of widespread spurious correlations in the FC matrix (as in Figure 2B case b), due to the normalization of within-to-between network correlations. By contrast, the InCorr increases under these conditions (Figure 2A first panel), suggesting that results based on within-network correlations alone should be interpreted with caution when assessing fMRI data quality.

In summary, SyNR may offer a balanced trade-off between interpretability, informativeness, and robustness for the quantification of System Identifiability in the datasets tested.

When compared to native fMRI acquisition metrics, SyNR correlates significantly only with framewise displacement and tSNR. This may evidence the central role of framewise motion in System Identifiability, whose effects persist despite motion regression. By contrast, the lack of association between Maximal Motion in run and SyNR is notable, since MM is still now a widely used criterion for subject inclusion in fMRI studies. This suggests that framewise measures of motion should rather be considered when defining inclusion criteria to Rs-fMRI studies.

Although this study adopts a fixed preprocessing pipeline based on retrospective correction, future work should evaluate SyNR across datasets and alternative denoising strategies. In this context, SyNR may serve as a benchmark to quantify quality improvements introduced by different preprocessing pipelines. The sample size (57 subjects in total) is relatively small, limiting the degree of statistical significance, though acquisition and preprocessing of UHF fMRI is challenging.

The validation of the different SI-based benchmarks in this work is performed only through cross-dataset mutual comparisons, as no reliable ground truth still exists for an optimal functional architecture in fMRI subjects. In this context, we believe that the main resting-state networks5 represent a widely accepted feature of resting-state fMRI and therefore provide a suitable reference for evaluating and comparing data quality across subjects.

In conclusion, we propose the SyNR index as a suitable benchmark to be included at the end of any resting-state fMRI preprocessing pipeline, enabling robust visual and quantitative assessment of data quality and guiding the exclusion or preprocessing of low-quality subjects, with expected improvements for subsequent group analyses in both clinical and research contexts.
