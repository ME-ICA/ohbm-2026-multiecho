Introduction

Cognitive therapeutic interventions can enhance attention by targeting and strengthening neural systems involved in focus, working memory, and cognitive control through repeated, structured practice of attention-demanding tasks. Such interventions are increasingly used as non-pharmacological strategies to support individuals with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), as improving attentional capacity may help compensate for cognitive deficits and slow overall functional decline. By reinforcing cognitive networks and promoting neuroplasticity, attention-focused training can also reduce risk factors associated with progression to Alzheimer’s disease, making it a potentially valuable preventative approach for individuals at high risk or in early stages of neurodegenerative conditions.

Our lab recently developed a closed-loop digital meditation intervention, MediTrain – inspired by focused-attention meditation (1). At its core, participants are asked to focus their attention on their breath for short periods of time (“trials”). The trials start at 20s and at the end of each trial, participants respond with whether they were able to maintain focus on their breath in the trial. The “yes” and “no” answers would increase or decrease trial length – informing the closed-loop portion of this intervention, with the eventual goal to cognitively train participants to lengthen the periods of time by which they could perform sustained focused attention. MediTrain has been shown to improve attention in young adults (1) and teens (2) further investigations are underway.

Methods

MediTrain was used as an intervention in two ongoing related clinical trials (NCT06633978 and NCT06633952) and was performed by participants at home for 30 minutes/day, 5 days/week, for 6 weeks. In the first study, we evaluated whether cognitive gains were greater in participants with MCI compared to cognitively healthy older adults. In the second study, we evaluated whether cognitive gains were accelerated via theta-frequency (6 Hz) transcranial alternating current stimulation (TACS) applied at home, while participants were simultaneously performing MediTrain cognitive training.

In the first study, 59 participants (27 male, 32 female, age ± sd = 70.0 ± 6.7), either MCI, (N = 18) or healthy (N = 36) were recruited to receive MediTrain for 6 weeks. In the second study, 44 participants (22 male, 22 female, age ± sd = 70.4 ± 5.1) with cognitive profiles consistent with MCI were recruited and randomized to receive MediTrain for 6 weeks but with either TACS or sham-TACS. Additional MRI-based scans were acquired including T1-MPRAGE, resting-state fMRI, task-based fMRI during the Montreal Imaging Stress Task, multishell diffusion weighted imaging, and single voxel magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) over the left anterior cingulate cortex – due to its role in attentional control. However, in both studies, EEG was recorded during resting-state and cognitive tasks (filter task and the continuous performance task (CPT)) – both before, and after the 6-week MediTrain training period. In the filter task, participants must attend to one or three red rectangles with or without two visual distractor rectangles, presented initially for 200ms. After 900ms, participants are presented with red rectangles in either the same or different location, and participants respond with same or different location. In the CPT, participants are presented with targets (squares on the top half of the screen), or non-targets (squares on the bottom half of the screen) at a ratio of 1 target: 4 non-targets. Participants are required to press a button whenever they see a target – thereby requiring sustained attention over a long period.

Behavioral analyses of the filter task and the CPT were performed to assess cognitive changes before and after training. We used sustained attention metrics (via the CPT) to investigate changes to reaction time variability and d-prime. Visual attention metrics (via the filter task) investigated filtering efficiency and d-prime metrics on pure and filter conditions.

EEG analysis was performed to observe resting-state changes to metrics including aperiodic offset, aperiodic slope, delta (1-4Hz), theta (4-8Hz), alpha (8-13Hz), beta (15-30Hz), and Lempel Ziv Complexity (LZC).

Diffusion tensor imaging analysis was performed using MRtrix3 and FSL. Region-of-interest analysis based on the JHU parcellation was analyzed looking at fractional anisotropy and mean diffusivity values to inspect potential changes to white matter integrity. MRS analyses was performed via GANNET software to observe changes to Glx/GABA ratios after MediTrain intervention. The Glx MRS peaks reflect glutamine and glutamate chemical concentrations and the Glx/GABA ratio reflects the neurochemical balance of excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmitters.

Results

In the first study, investigating cognitive gains in healthy versus MCI cohorts, an interaction effect was observed in the filter task’s three rectangle pure trial conditions (χ2 = 4.2, p = 0.04) where the d-prime of the MCI cohort significantly improved after the MediTrain intervention (t = 3.7, p = 0.0009) whereas the healthy cohort did not (t = 1.4, p = 0.16). However, when looking at the CPT, significant reductions in reaction time variability were not observed. Neural changes comparing between post-MediTrain and pre-MediTrain resting-state EEG did not show changes in the prototypical spectral bands. However, reduced mid-frontal LZC (electrode Fz, t = -2.3, p = 0.027), increased frontal aperiodic offset (electrode Fpz, t = 2.14, p = 0.039) and slope parameters (electrode FC2, t = 2.2, p = 0.0349) were observed. However, no significant interaction effects evaluating whether healthy or MCI participants exhibited greater EEG-related changes were observed.

In the second study, investigating whether cognitive gains could be accelerated via simultaneous TACS neurostimulation, a significant interaction effect between TACS and sham groups was observed in filtering efficiency (χ2 = 5.8, p = 0.02) (3); where the TACS group improved in filtering efficiency in the one rectangle condition, whereas the sham group did not. When looking at theCPT, the group that received TACS showed significant improvements to sustained attention reaction time variability (t = 2.6, p = 0.016) – whereas the group receiving sham-TACS did not (t = 1.7, p = 0.104). Neural changes compared between post-intervention and pre-intervention showed significant changes in DWI and MRS measures. Decreases in mean diffusivity in the right superior longitudinal temporal fasciculus were observed in the sham group (t = 2.25, p = 0.024), but not in the TACS group (t = 0.29, p = 0.66). Significant interaction effects were observed for the Glx/GABA ratio in the left anterior cingulate cortex (χ2 = 14.1, p < 0.001), with the TACS group reducing their Glx/GABA ratio but the sham group increasing their Glx/GABA ratio. This indicates that TACS alongside MediTrain cognitive training may reduce excitation/inhibition ratios.

Conclusion

Six weeks of cognitive training via MediTrain may improve directed attention in participants with MCI, compared to healthy older adults. Furthermore, in participants with MCI, TACS stimulation may accelerate cognitive gains as evidenced by improved filtering efficiency. With TACS, MediTrain also reduced reaction time variability compared to the sham-TACS group.

Neural changes potentially associated with MediTrain includes increased white-matter integrity (via mean diffusivity measures) and reduced excitation-inhibition imbalance (via the reduced Glx/GABA ratio). Resting-state neurophysiological changes include increased aperiodic slope, suggesting reduced excitation-inhibition ratios (4). This is in line with inhibitory-boosting benefits linked to more traditional forms of mindfulness meditation (5).
