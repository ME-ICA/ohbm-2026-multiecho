Introduction

Large neuroimaging consortia are becoming increasingly common, with several collecting scans from thousands to tens of thousands of individuals. While each consortium targets distinct aspects of health, they share the key strength of sampling breadth. For the first time, we have neuroimaging data from individuals with diverse demographics at a scale sufficient to robustly train, refine, and test clinically relevant predictive models. Unfortunately, that scale overwhelms existing approaches to quality control. This quality problem extends to derived images as well as raw ones. Here, we focus on derived images.

Most preprocessing pipelines fail on a subset of scans. For large-scale studies, failure rates of even a few percentage points can amount to hundreds of scans. Although there are automated ways of detecting some pipeline failures, the data used to train failure detection algorithms are limited, so manual review remains esstential1. However, it is currently difficult to perform manual quality control at the scale of modern consortia, leaving many failures difficult to detect and mitigate.

We encountered these challenges of scalable quality control while preprocessing data from the Acute to Chronic  Pain Signatures (A2CPS) project, which aims to collect neuroimaging data on over 2800 participants who will undergo an incident of acute pain (surgery). Several quality control tools have previously been developed2–4, but none of them were sufficiently comprehensive for the specific derivatives and scale of A2CPS. To address these needs, we developed DIRT.

DIRT consists of three related but distinct components. First, DIRT is a program that generates a comprehensive set of images that can be used for quality control. Second, it is an algorithm for organizing the reviews. Third, it is a front- and back-end platform that coordinates the review, both presenting images to solicit ratings and storing those ratings.

Methods

In this section, we describe the design principles that guided the development of DIRT and outline how they were realized.

First, we designed DIRT to enable quick but accurate reviews. Given the scale of new neuroimaging datasets, small reductions in the time needed to review a single image translate to large overall savings. For example, reviews of a single type of derivative in one comprehensive platform, Qrater, take between 20 – 60 seconds5. Given this range of durations, reviewing a single derivative in a cohort of 2000 participants can take between 10 to 34 hours. Therefore, enabling accurate reviews to be carried out rapidly can lead to substantial gains in resource efficiency. DIRT minimizes review time variability by basing interactions on simple judgements applied to individual (possibly animated) 2D images.

Second, DIRT was designed to maximize the informativeness of each review interaction. Many processing failures are severe and detectable at a glance (e.g., a grossly misaligned mask), whereas others are subtle and may only be noticeable after careful inspection of many slices of an image. To facilitate identification of major failures while still allowing for identification of subtle issues as time permits, DIRT implements a breadth-first approach review strategy. For each scan and each derivative to be reviewed, a set of informative images is created (e.g., for reviewing masks, a set of 5 informative slices are selected from each of the 3 orientations). During review sessions, images are presented sequentially, and the next image in the sequence is chosen from whichever scan has the fewest reviews, skipping images from scans already judged to have failed. In this way, priority is placed on reviewing all scans in a dataset, while allowing for reviews to deepen for individual scans as time allows.

Third, DIRT was designed to be straightforward to deploy and extend. Several existing quality control frameworks are based on a crowdsourcing model. For example, the powerful Swipes for Science framework is a general tool for gamifying quality control and has been successfully used to review multiple neuroimaging derivatives2. However, the crowdsourcing-first approach implies an inherent degree of complexity, including secure account management, web hosting costs, and privacy considerations when raw structural data is involved. Crowdsourcing is powerful, but having it as the only option can slow the development of new features and hinder community adoption. For this reason, while DIRT can be deployed on cloud infrastructure, it is also designed to be deployable on a single local machine as a containerized (e.g., Docker) application, thus lowering the barrier for use in a range of computing environments.

An overview of the DIRT interface is shown in Figure 1. DIRT receives as input a derived dataset that adheres to the Brain Imaging Database Structure (BIDS). It uses rules from BIDS to identify derivatives and then creates images that allow reviewers to determine whether the preprocessing that created the derivatives was successful. Once the images have been created, quality reviews are coordinated by a mobile-friendly, web-based application. The frontend of the application is built around the HTMX JavaScript library, and the backend is powered by the Django framework. During a review session, reviewers select a category of derivatives to rate (e.g., mask, spatial normalization, surface localization). The pre-generated images for that derivative are then served sequentially, with the order based on a breadth-first algorithm. Each review is stored in an SQL database, which enables multiple reviewers to collaborate on a dataset simultaneously. In addition to the reviews, a limited set of session information is logged to augment review summarization (e.g., reviewer identity, time of the review).

Results

We have applied DIRT to Release 2.0 of the A2CPS neuroimaging data. This release includes ~29 terabytes of data across ~2.2 million derivative files (in addition to raw data). The dataset includes structural MRI (T1w), diffusion MRI, and functional MRI (BOLD), processed with several standard pipelines (i.e., fMRIprep, CAT12, FreeSurfer, fsl_anat, QSIPrep, MRIQC, GIFT, FDT), as well as several custom pipelines. For review of the release 2.0 derivatives, we targeted five pre-processing targets: brain masks, spatial normalization, surface localization, bold-field map coregistration, and diffusion tensor fitting. 

For each derivative, preprocessing quality was determined with one of two kinds of review interaction. The first type is for derivatives that have an objective issue that can be identified in a specific location. For example, brain masks can include extraneous tissue (e.g., bone), or the white matter surface may stray into the gray matter. For these derivatives, reviewers are presented with an image and then asked to click directly on areas in the image that display a problem, potentially producing multiple clicks per image. These reviews provide collections of coordinates associated with each image. Overall, images with more clicks are more likely to have suffered from a preprocessing failure (that is, be of worse quality). However, it is worth noting that the failures indicated by clicks may be spatially constrained, meaning that some downstream analyses may be feasible (e.g., some regional volumes may be accurate and usable, even while others are not).

In the A2CPS dataset, the click-based review was used to assess brain masks, spatial normalization, and surface localization. The comprehensive review facilitated by DIRT proved useful for spatial normalization by revealing a bug in the preprocessing pipelines. Most individuals with some cortical atrophy exhibited atypically poor normalization of the gyri that were most atrophied. These failures were driven by a misconfiguration that was only revealed to the A2CPS analysis team after review of a sufficiently large number of images enabled identification of the systematic nature of the failures (e.g., in older adults).

The second type of review is used in circumstances where preprocessing failures are not necessarily localized to specific parts of the image. For example, a colorized fractional anisotropy map may appear globally noisy or may lack major tracts, or a coregistration may have failed to align two images. Here, reviewers rate derivatives by choosing among three categories: “pass”, “unsure”, and “fail”. Each of these ratings is associated with a numeric score (0, 1, 2), and scans with a higher score are considered lower quality. In some circumstances, the presence of a single “fail” rating may be sufficient to exclude a derivative from any downstream analysis.

These categorical judgements were used to rate coregistrations and diffusion tensor fitting. 3007 coregistrations were reviewed, and the median time spent on each scan was 6.6 seconds (multiple images per scan), totaling 7.96 hours reviewing. These reviews uncovered 126 coregistration failures (failure rate: 4.2%), and 1103 cases in which at least one slice indicated a possible issue (that is, not all slices were a “pass”). The diffusion tensor fitting was assessed with a dynamic image of the colorized fractional anisotropy maps that cycled through the entire volume. The median time spent rating each image was 15.6 seconds, the observed failure rate was 0.1%, and 2.1% of scans received a maximum rating of “unsure”.

Conclusion

We have presented a new application for the assessment of neuroimaging derivative quality control: DIRT. The application is built around fast micro-interactions (e.g., ratings of individual slices of a preprocessing target) and is designed to support review of large-scale datasets such as those collected by consortia. It has been incorporated into and tested in conjunction with the quality control procedures of the A2CPS project.

While there are many existing frameworks for quality control, we found it more effective to develop a new application tailored to the unique needs of the A2CPS project. In practice, the main barrier to quality control is not the absence of tools, but the difficulty of deploying a particular system on a particular dataset. Individual consortia differ in their available expertise, computing resources, and organizational infrastructures, and a QC workflow that fits one project well may be difficult to adapt to another. The larger the project, the more issues may arise. Difficulties are especially acute when a project needs access to highly customized features. At the same time, modern development tools (e.g., Django, HTMX, and, more recently, LLMs) have lowered the cost of implementing an application. With sufficient expertise in these tools, the overall cost of developing something from scratch can be drastically lower than attempting to adapt a framework that was designed for another context.

To see how the unique constraints of individual consortia impose different constraints on a quality control framework, consider how DIRT is deployed for A2CPS. For A2CPS, DIRT is hosted with Tapis Pods, which is a service for managing Kubernetes pods provided by the Texas Advanced Computing Center (TACC). All A2CPS data is hosted and preprocessed on TACC, and the Tapis Pods service was provided at no additional cost. However, while the compute infrastructure was free, the system needed to be configured manually. Some neuroimaging consortia may not have expertise available to manually configure backend and database resources, while they may instead have the monetary resources available to purchase cloud-based alternatives. As outlined in the introduction, DIRT comprises multiple components (a system for generating images, an algorithm for reviewing those images, and the application software). These components were built with the resources that were available to the A2CPS. Other projects may have different constellations of resources or needs, considering factors such as expertise with web backends, availability of research assistant hours, or a budget for hosting infrastructure.

In principle, the components of DIRT may be modular, allowing for different projects to swap in different components based on their need. As an example of modularity, consider that, while A2CPS deploys DIRT remotely, it may also be deployed both locally (e.g., on a laptop). When deployed remotely, the images and reviews are stored in a centralized PostgreSQL database. When deployed locally, the images and reviews are instead stored with SQLite, a file-based database that requires minimal setup. A local deployment may make it more difficult for multiple reviewers to contribute simultaneously, but DIRT remains a full-scale application that may be sufficient for many users---particularly when a single machine can be shared by all reviewers (e.g., a lab-computer that is accessible by many researchers, or a machine accessible via a shell such as a local compute cluster). Additional explorations of modularity will be left for future development.
