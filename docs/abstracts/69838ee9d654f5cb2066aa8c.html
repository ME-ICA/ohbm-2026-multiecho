Introduction

An individual's reading abilities fundamentally impact their socioeconomic outlook and literacy is typically acquired through extensive training during childhood. This training changes the brain and triggers the emergence of two functional subregions in the human ventral temporal cortex that respond selectively to text, which we refer to as mOTS-word and pOTS-words 1. These OTS-words subregions are critical for fluent reading and several theories of their emergence have been proposed including the neuronal recycling hypothesis, the theory of graded hemispheric specialization, and the theory of emergence (for reviews see 2). One commonality of all three theories is the notion that the OTS-words subregions emerge in cortical terrain previously unrelated to reading skill, as written text is too recent of a cultural invention for dedicated neuronal resources to have been laid out by human evolution. However, simpler visual symbols predate formal writing systems by over 50000 years3, which makes it plausible that the OTS-words subregions are selectively engaged in linguistic processing of symbols even prior to formal literacy training and that training is needed specifically only for text processing as text is highly abstract. To test this hypothesis, we conducted a longitudinal fMRI study assessing neuronal responses during linguistic processing of modern-day symbols (emojis) and neural text selectivity immediately before and after the first year of formal literacy training and in relation to individuals' emerging reading ability. 

Methods

17 children (10 male) participated in this longitudinal fMRI study that was embedded in a child-friendly space-themed narrative (Fig. 1A). fMRI and behavioral data were collected before the children attended first grade (mean age±SD = 6.18±0.39 years) and after first grade (mean age±SD = 7.12±0.33 years). Two fMRI experiments were collected from the children at both timepoints: Experiment 1 was used to track emerging text selectivity and to define regions of interest (fROIs). In this experiment children viewed blocks of text, limbs, faces, objects, and houses while performing an oddball task (Fig 1B). All fROIs (Fig 1C) were defined at the second time-point, i.e. after one year of formal literacy training: mOTS-words and pOTS-words were defined by higher responses to text than the other visual stimulus categories (T=3, voxel level, uncorrected) and a limb-selective control region (OTS-limbs) was defined by higher responses to limbs than the other visual stimulus categories (T=3, voxel level, uncorrected). Experiment 2 served to evaluate if the OTS-words subregions are engaged in linguistic processing of symbols prior to and/or after the first year of literacy training. In this experiment, children viewed pairs of common emojis that together formed German compound nouns (e.g. SUN-FLOWER) while performing one of two tasks that were matched in task difficulty: in the linguistic task the children judged whether the emoji pairs formed a meaningful compound or not and in the color task they judged whether the two emojis were presented in the same or a different color hue. A cue at the beginning of each trial indicated which task children should perform (Fig. 1D). At each time-point, and for each experiment, we computed selectivity indices (SIs) to quantify functional selectivity. SIs were computed in adherence with field-standards as:  SI = ((1 + beta_Condition) - (1 + beta_otherConditions)) / ((1 + beta_Condition) + (1 + beta_otherConditions)), where beta_Condition is the beta estimate for the target condition (e.g. text in Exp 1 and linguistic task in Exp 2), and beta_otherConditions is the average beta of all remaining conditions. Changes in visual stimulus selectivities (Exp 1) were tested using a repeated measures ANOVA with time-point and visual stimulus category as factors. Note that for the second time-point we implemented a split-half analysis to ensure independence of fROI definition and extracted betas. Changes in linguistic processing preferences (Exp 2) were tested using a repeated measures ANOVA with time-point and linguistic processing demands as factors. In addition, reading performance was assessed prior to and after first grade using the SLRT-II (Salzburger Lese- und Rechtschreibtest-II)). Reading performance was then correlated with SIs from both experiments, and SIs were correlated across experiments, using Pearson’s correlation.

Results

In Experiment 1, stimulus selectivity in both mOTS-words and pOTS-words showed a main effect of category (mOTS-words: F(4,11) = 9.81, p < 0.001, pOTS-words: F(4,11) = 9.27, p < 0.001) and a category × time interaction (mOTS-words: F(1,14) = 11.16, p < 0.001, pOTS-words: F(4,11) = 13.51, p < 0.001). In both mOTS-words sub-regions text selectivity increased significantly over the first year of schooling  (ps < 0.001, significant with Bonferroni-corrected threshold), while the other categories showed no significant changes over time (all ps > 0.04, non significant with Bonferroni-corrected threshold). For OTS-limbs, we observed a main effect of category (F(4,56) = 16.55, p < 0.001) which did not change over time (no category × time interaction, F(4,56) = 1.65, p = 0.17).  In Experiment 2, mOTS-words showed a main effect of linguistic processing  (F(1,16) = 21.19, p < 0.001), which was similar at both timepoints (no linguistic processing  × time interaction F(1,16) = 0.03, p = 0.87). No main effect of linguistic processing or any other effects were found in pOTS-words and OTS-limbs (all ps > 0.39). Interestingly, in mOTS-words, individual children’s increase in text selectivity in Experiment 1 during the first year of formal literacy training was correlated with their linguistic processing preferences in Experiment 2 both prior to (r2 = 0.27, p = 0.04) and after (r2 = 0.32, p = 0.03,) literacy training. In addition to the neural changes, children's reading performance (percentile rank) also increased during the first year of schooling (before first grade: mean percentile rank ±SE: 0.9±0.9; after first grade: mean percentile rank ±SE: 67.4±6.5, paired t-test: p < 0.0001). Critically, an individual child’s change in reading performance was correlated with the linguistic processing preference in mOTS-words in Experiment 2 both prior to (Fig. 2C, r2= 0.43, p = 0.01) and after (r = 0.62, p = 0.008, r2 = 0.39) the first year of formal literacy training. In contrast, a child’s increase in reading performance correlated with text selectivity in mOTS-words in Experiment 1 only after (r² = 0.42, p = 0.009), but not before (Fig. 2C, r² = 0.01, p = 0.68) the first year of schooling. Finally, the increase in a given child’s reading performance correlated with the increase in neural text selectivity in mOTS-words in Experiment 1 ( r² = 0.38, p = 0.003).

Conclusion

This longitudinal study reveals the intricate interplay between reading skill acquisition and functional properties of the OTS-words subregions. Consistent with previous work4,5, we find that formal literacy training leads not only to improved reading performance but also to an increase in neural text selectivity in the mOTS-words subregions. However, critically our findings show for the first time that the cortical territory in which mOTS-words is developing is functionally related to linguistic processing even prior to literacy training. This initial linguistic processing preference is functionally relevant and scaffolds future increases in reading performance and neural text selectivity on an individual level. Based on these findings, we propose a novel theory of the emergence of OTS-words, the theory of linguistic scaffolding. This novel theory postulates a two-step developmental sequence in mOTS-words in which an initial tuning for linguistic processing provides the foundation for training-induced tuning for text from familiar alphabets. The theory of linguistic scaffolding hence provides a novel mechanistic understanding of how reading acquisition shapes the brain as well as a potential novel biomarker for the early diagnosis of developmental learning disabilities.
