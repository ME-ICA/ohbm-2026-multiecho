Introduction

Sleep provides a natural model for examining transitions in consciousness, reflected by systematic changes in large-scale brain activity. Prior work using dynamic functional connectivity has shown that fluctuations in vigilance and sleep depth alter the stability and integration of brain networks. In particular, non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep is characterized by reduced connectivity strength and increased temporal instability, suggesting that sleep modulates not only the magnitude but also the organization of large-scale neural interactions [1-2]. State-based frameworks, which cluster whole-brain activation patterns at each fMRI time point, may therefore offer a more direct representation of how network configurations recur and shift across sleep-stages transitions. In this study, we applied a two-level modularity framework to identify dynamic recur of whole-brain states from fMRI data and investigated how their repertoire and transition structure reorganize across wakefulness (N0) and NREM sleep stages (N1 to N3).

Methods

Simultaneous EEG-fMRI data were collected from 29 healthy men (22.9 ± 2.5 years) during up to two hours of natural sleep inside a 3T Siemens Trio MRI scanner. EEG was scored offline in 30-second epochs into N0, N1, N2, or N3 stages, each corresponding to 12 fMRI volumes (TR = 2500 ms, TE = 30 ms, FA = 80°, FOV = 220 mm, 64×64 matrix, 35 slices, 3.4-mm thickness. Preprocessed fMRI volumes were demeaned, standardized, and converted into instantaneous whole-brain activation vectors. We implemented a two-level modularity framework adapted from Gu et al. [3]. At the individual level, inter-volume similarity matrices were computed and clustered using modularity optimization (γ = 1.04) to identify subject-specific whole-brain states. At the group level, all subject-derived states were concatenated and re-clustered using a second modularity step (γ = 1.06), resulting in a consensus set of 113 recurring group-level states. For each stage, we quantified the number of distinct states, their transitions patterns, and the spatial distributions of the dominant states.

Results

The modularity analysis identified 113 distinct whole-brain states. Distinct-state counts differed significantly across sleep stages (ANOVA p < 0.001) (Figure 1). N1 and N2 exhibited the richest repertoires, N0 showed an intermediate number of states, and N3 contained the fewest (N1 > N2 > N0 > N3). FDR-corrected post-hoc comparisons confirmed significantly reduced state counts in N3 relative to N0 (p = 0.047) and to both N1 and N2 (p < 0.001). Transition frequency showed a different ordering (ANOVA p < 0.001), peaking in N2, followed by N1 and N0, and reaching its minimum in N3 (N2 > N1 > N0 > N3). FDR-corrected post-hoc tests revealed significantly fewer transitions in N3 compared with N1 (p = 0.00012) and N2 (p < 0.001).

Given their highly skewed frequency distribution of brain states, we focused on the first seven states, which accounted for 87.7% of all fMRI frames. Beyond these dynamical differences, the composition of the dominant states exhibited a marked redistribution across sleep. Wakefulness (N0) and N1 were dominated by two high-level cortical motifs (State 1 and State 2), which together accounted for more than 60% of all frames. Their prevalence declined in N2 and dropped substantially in N3 (to 25.8% and 23.8%, respectively), while sensory-, limbic-, and thalamo-cortical states (States 3–6) increased and collectively became dominant. This pattern reflects a progressive shift from high-order cortical motifs toward lower-level sensory and subcortical configurations as sleep deepens.

Conclusion

These findings demonstrate that sleep stages differ not only in the richness and switching frequency of whole-brain states, but also in the underlying composition of dominant activation motifs. Light sleep preserves a diverse and flexible state repertoire, whereas deep sleep collapses into a small, stable, sensory-dominant configuration. The identification of seven dominant states accounting for nearly 88% of all frames provides a compact and physiologically meaningful framework for describing brain dynamics during transitions in arousal. These results extend prior observations [1-2, 4-5] by showing that sleep reconfigures not only network stability but also the fundamental activation motifs structuring spontaneous brain activity.
