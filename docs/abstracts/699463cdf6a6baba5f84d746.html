Introduction

Auditory verbal hallucinations (AVH) are a core symptom of schizophrenia disorder and have been associated with altered prefrontal function, impaired cognitive control, and abnormal activity within language-related brain networks.1 Traditional neurobiological models often describe AVH in terms of regional hypo- or hyperactivation, particularly within frontal cortex.2 However, an increasing number of studies suggest failure to dynamically regulate large-scale brain networks as environmental demands change. Previous task-based and resting-state fMRI studies have demonstrated altered switching between task-positive and task-negative networks, as well as abnormal intrinsic low-frequency fluctuations related to hallucination severity.3 Yet, how spectral properties of intrinsic brain activity and inter-network coordination jointly contribute to AVHs remains insufficiently understood. Here, we investigated frequency-specific alterations in whole-brain dynamics, expressed across independent components and large-scale functional networks.

Methods

Participants were sampled from the cohort of schizophrenia patients described by Sandøy et al. (2025). Patients experiencing mild to severe auditory verbal hallucinations and age- and sex-matched healthy controls underwent functional MRI at Haukeland University Hospital, Bergen, Norway. All participants provided written informed consent, and the study was approved by the Regional Committee for Medical Research Ethics in Western Norway (REK Vest #2016/800). MRI data were acquired on a GE Discovery 750 3T scanner using an 8-channel head coil. Structural images were obtained using a T1-weighted fast spoiled gradient-recalled echo sequence (TR = 6.8 ms, TE = 2.98 ms, 1 mm isotropic voxels). Functional volumes were acquired using gradient-echo echo-planar imaging (TR = 2500 ms, TE = 30 ms, voxel size = 1.72 × 1.72 × 3 mm, 36 slices). The fMRI task utilized flanker paradigm, consisting of visually presented arrow stimuli arranged in congruent and incongruent configurations. Participants were instructed to indicate, via button press, the direction of the centrally presented target arrow while ignoring flanking arrows.
Whole-brain fMRI time series were preprocessed using standard procedures including realignment, unwarping, spatial normalization to MNI space, smoothing (8mm kernel), motion regression (12 parameters), CSF and white matter signal removal and bandpass filtering (0.008 -0.09Hz). Spatially constrained independent component analysis was applied in a data-driven manner to extract independent components representing intrinsic brain networks using Neuromark template consisting of 105 spatial components grouped into functional networks.4 Group differences were assessed using two-sample t-test design in MANCOVA Gift toolbox, spatial maps were categorized into large-scale networks, including default mode, salience, and central executive networks, to examine inter-network organization using univariate testing. Voxel-wise group contrasts were FDR corrected and computed using statistical thresholding at p < 0.05. Component time series were analyzed in the frequency domain across the 0–0.2 Hz range.


Results

Analyses of spatial components revealed cortical differences between patients and controls, encompassing medial and lateral prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, insula, and temporal and parietal association areas. The results include both midline structures (medial prefrontal cortex and posterior cingulate/precuneus) and lateral frontoparietal network. The pattern of increases and decreases across these regions suggests that group differences are not confined to a single locus but instead reflect a reorganization of distributed cortical systems that support higher-order cognitive and affective functions.

Spectral analyses revealed clear and spatially widespread frequency-dependent group differences across multiple independent components. Patients showed lower power at slower frequencies (below approximately 0.08 Hz) and higher power at faster frequencies (around 0.1–0.2 Hz), with these alterations distributed across numerous components rather than confined to a single region or network. The pattern of effects suggests that group differences are not limited to isolated nodes but instead reflect more global changes in the temporal organization of activity across the system. At the systems level, the findings are consistent with altered patterns of interaction among default mode, salience, and central executive networks, pointing to disrupted coordination between task-positive and task-negative systems and a potential breakdown in the dynamic reallocation of resources required for flexible cognitive control.

Conclusion

These findings indicate that schizophrenia patients with AVHs show frequency-specific dysregulation of intrinsic functional brain dynamics and altered synchronization of large-scale functional networks. Rather than reflecting region-specific dysfunction, our results revealed impaired regulation of brain-states across frequencies and networks during task and rest periods. The reduced low-frequency power may reflect impaired sustained attention and cognitive control even when demands for task-processing were present. Disrupted coordination between default mode, salience, and cental executive networks further supports the hypothesis that AVHs involve failure in switching between internally and externally oriented cognitive states.5 The results highlight the importance of large-scale network interactions as potential targets for future biomarkers and interventions in schizophrenia.
