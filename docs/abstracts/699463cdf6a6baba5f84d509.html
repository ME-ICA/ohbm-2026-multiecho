Introduction

Turbulence, unlike chaos, is characterized by enhanced multi-scale mixing, a property that can emerge in non-linear systems such as the brain.1 While brain turbulence has emerged as a key dynamic regime for optimizing information transfer,2 its regional heterogeneity and the mechanisms modulating its spatiotemporal organization remain largely unexplored. Global cortical dynamics are supported by subcortical structures, such as the thalamus and cerebellum. Converging evidence increasingly emphasizes the role of the thalamus within cortico–thalamo–cerebellar (CTC) circuits.3 Thalamic control is architecturally distinct: 'core' nuclei send projections primarily to cortical layer 4 of sensory areas, whereas 'matrix' nuclei project to supragranular layers 1-3 of diverse cortical areas,4 also distinguished by their differential cerebellar inputs.3 Here, we investigate how this thalamic architecture orchestrates cortical and cerebellar turbulence.

Methods

The hallmark of turbulent dynamics is enhanced fluctuation in local synchronization (Figure 1A),2 potentially reflecting frequent transitions between functional integration and segregation. To quantify this, we calculated the local synchronization level using the Kuramoto local order parameter (Figure 1B-C).2 First, voxel-wise time-series were Hilbert-transformed to extract instantaneous phases (ϕn(t); Figure 1B). Next, for each voxel n, we computed the weighted sum of the unit complex values defined by the instantaneous phases of neighboring voxels (exp(iϕp(t))). The weights were defined to approximate structural connectivity using an exponential distance rule (Cnp=exp(-λ·dnp)) where dnp is the Euclidean distance between voxel n and p, and λ = 0.18 mm-1 is a decay parameter.2 The magnitude of this resulting complex sum is defined as the local order parameter, Rn(t), where higher values indicate greater phase coherence among local voxels (i.e., local synchrony). Finally, we calculated 'regional turbulence amplitude' for each ROI as the spatiotemporal standard deviation of the local synchrony values (Figure 1D). 

Results

We first investigated turbulence organization within the CTC system. Using the HCP S1200 resting-state fMRI dataset (N = 951), we calculated regional turbulence amplitudes of regions of interest (ROIs) in the cortex (Glasser atlas), cerebellum (Diedrichsen atlas), and thalamus (treated as a single ROI). Principal component analysis (PCA) on the regional amplitudes revealed that the first component of the cortex closely aligned with layer 4 gene expression (acquired from Allen Human Brain Atlas; R = 0.66, p < 0.001, spin test; Figure 2A). The second cortical component correlated with thalamocortical functional connectivity (FC) weighted by thalamic core-matrix cell-type expression4 (R = 0.71, p < 0.001, spin test; Figure 2B), suggesting that the primary axes of turbulence organization are anchored in thalamocortical architecture. A similar organization was observed in the cerebellum: the first PC aligned with mean thalamo-cerebellar FC (R = -0.82; Figure 2C) and the second with core-matrix weighted FC (R = 0.64; Figure 2D). To probe the dynamics of this CTC circuit, we extracted regional turbulence time-series (spatial variance of local synchrony at each time point). The temporal correlations between thalamic and cortical ROI, as well as thalamic and cerebellar ROI, turbulence time series (Figure 2E) showed strong spatial correspondence with the first PCs of cortical and cerebellar turbulence, respectively (R = −0.78 for both), highlighting the thalamic role in orchestrating turbulent dynamics. To assess directed temporal predictability between macroscopic turbulence modes, we performed Granger causality analysis on turbulence time-series projected onto their dominant organizational axes (cortex: layer-4-aligned axis; cerebellum: thalamo-coupled axis). This revealed significant bidirectional negative influences within cortico–thalamo–cerebellar circuits (p<1e-13; Figure 2F), consistent with a stabilizing feedback control mechanism acting at the systems level rather than fast neural causation. Given the importance of CTC circuits in the psychosis spectrum,5 we analyzed two independent datasets: HCP-EP (early psychosis [EP]: N=104 vs. healthy controls [HC]: N=57) and COBRE (schizophrenia [SZ]: N=77 vs. HC: N=90). We found a significant reduction in turbulence within sensorimotor areas (FDR<0.05; Figure 2G-H). Crucially, the spatial maps of these turbulence differences (i.e., t-value maps for patients - controls) significantly correlated with layer 1-3 gene expression for both groups (R>0.23, p<0.003). This indicates that cortical regions lacking robust supragranular architecture are susceptible to dynamic collapse from the onset of psychosis. 

Conclusion

This study establishes that brain turbulence is not random noise but structured dynamics, actively maintained by a homeostatic negative feedback loop within cortico-thalamo-cerebellar circuits. The dominant axes of turbulence aligning with thalamic organization highlights the thalamus as a central regulator of such dynamics. Crucially, the selective collapse of turbulence in regions lacking supragranular support suggests a top-down regulatory failure, establishing turbulent dynamics as a vital link between CTC circuits and macroscale dysfunctions in brain dynamics of psychosis.
