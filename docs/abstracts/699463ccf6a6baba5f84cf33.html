Introduction

Neurodevelopmental delay/disorders (NDD) are the most prevalent extracardiac conditions in people with congenital heart disease (CHD). As many genetic conditions include both CHD and NDD, genetic variation may contribute to this risk1. The structural connectome reflecting the underlying complex neurologic processes is a key marker for brain function in CHD2. The connectome structural core is more likely to be under the influence of genetics, whereas peripheral connectivity may be more influenced by environmental factors3. However, the specific impact of rare, damaging genetic variants on the connectome and its relationship to NDD in CHD remains poorly understood. 

Rare predicted loss-of-function (pLOF) variants are genetic variants expected to severely disrupt gene function, Fig1A. Genetically constrained genes are those that are depleted for pLOF variants in the general population, indicating that they are likely fundamental to a biological process. Many constrained genes are highly expressed during fetal development, and thus could have multiple effects on other biological pathways i.e. on both brain and heart development in CHD.

Our objective is to determine the association between rare, pLOF variants in constrained genes and the connectome structural core in a CHD cohort, and determine whether either is associated with executive function (EF), a largely impacted cognitive domain in CHD.

Methods

Participants and Genetics: Participants aged 8-42 years old were enrolled in the Pediatric Cardiac Genomics Consortium CHD Brain and Genes Study with complete exome sequencing, neuroimaging, and neuropsychological testing1. 

pLOF-variants in constrained genes were defined as high quality rare (allele frequency < 1x10-5 in the Genome Aggregation Database [gnomAD] v2.0) variants predicted to cause a frameshift, premature stop, or canonical splice site alteration in constrained genes (defined by probability of intolerance to loss score greater than 0.5 against a total of 4,543 genes from gnomAD v2.0). Participants were then stratified into two groups for connectome analysis based on the presence or absence of a pLOF-constrained variant, Fig 1B. Genes with pLOF-constrained variants were assessed for functional enrichment (R package clusterProfiler) to identify the underlying biological pathways they are associated with. To identify potential mechanisms that pLOF-variants may impact the brain, a list of connectome-associated genes from whole genome association studies in literature was also curated and functional enrichment analysis performed on the constrained genes containing pLOF-constrained variants together with connectome-associated genes. P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure.

MRI and Structural Networks: MRI data were collected across 5 sites on 3 T Siemens. T1-weighted acquisition was a 3D inversion prepared RF-spoiled GRE sequence, TR/TE = 2500/2.88ms, flip angle = 8°; FOV = 256 x 256mm2; voxel dimension = 1 x 1 x 1mm3. Data were processed with Freesurfer, and mapped to the distortion-corrected b0 space with the inverse of a linear transform between skull-stripped b0-to-T1w volumes. Diffusion-weighted data was acquired with a multiband EPI sequence, multislice acceleration = 3; TR/TE = 411/88ms; FOV = 240 x 240mm2; 81 axial slices; voxel dimension = 1.7 x 1.7 x 1.7mm3 voxel dimension, at b-values 0 (n=7), 500 (6 directions), 1000 (15); 2000 (15) and 3000 (60) s/mm-2, along with a spin-echo EPI fieldmap for b0 distortion correction1. Data were pre-processed according to the ABCD protocol, which included motion and susceptibility artifact correction using the additional EPI data acquired in the reverse phase-encoding direction with FSL topup. Constrained spherical deconvolution diffusion models were fitted and whole brain tractography performed, Fig 1C. Network nodes were 84 cortical-subcortical regions, and edges were the weighted sum (SIFT2 x μ) of streamlines. Global network measures of global and local (Elocal) efficiencies, characteristic path length, betweenness centralities (BCnode, BCedge), transitivity and communicability (Comm) were computed. Core-Periphery subnetworks were calculated. Only regions that were identified as a core node in every participant within a group (i.e participants with or without pLOF-constrained variants) were designated a “group core” node. Where appropriate, network measures (Strength, Elocal, BCnode, BCedge, and Comm) were averaged by subnetwork (Figure 1D). Equivalent measures were calculated normalised by 500 random networks.

Neuropsychological Evaluation: Given that executive dysfunction is common in CHD, 11 subtests in Verbal Fluency, Trail Making and Tower Test from the Delis-Kaplan Executive Function System (D-KEFS) battery were chosen. All participants completed subtests of the norm-referenced, examiner-administered D-KEFS. 

Statistical Analyses: Linear regression was then used to compare connectome measures or EF scores between groups using the following covariates: age at MRI, sex, maternal education level, household income, MRI acquisition site and CHD heart category (single- or bi-ventricular defect). Connectome measures were associated with EF subtests from D-KEFS, per group, with multiple regression analysis and the same covariates.

Results

Thirty-five participants had a pLOF-constrained variant, and the remaining 49 did not. There were no significant demographic (age and sex) and covariate (CHD heart category, maternal education, household income, and MRI site) differences between pLOF groups. There were also no significant group differences in global network measures. 

The Core Subnetwork: Sixteen bilaterally distributed regions made up the core in the group without a pLOF-constrained variant (Figure 2A). There were strong connections associated with and between frontal and pre-frontal regions. The core included strong connectivity between pre- and post-central regions and to subcortical areas such as the thalamus—representing sensorimotor pathways—as well as strong within parietal association regions. The group with pLOF-constrained variants had a larger core, comprising the same 16 regions in participants without a pLOF-constrained variant, plus an additional seven nodes (Figure 2B). These additional regions are largely in occipital areas and the cerebellum, with lower nodal strength compared to the 16 core regions in both groups.

Core-Periphery Subnetwork Analysis: All core network measures, with the exception of Comm, were significantly lower in the pLOF-constrained variant group versus those without a pLOF-constrained variant (p < 0.001). Observed measures of BCnode, BCedge, and Comm from non-core (i.e. feeder and periphery) subnetworks were also significantly lower in those with pLOF-constrained variant versus those without – however these did not survive normalization against random networks, nor adjustments for covariates. 

Neuropsychological Evaluation: There were no significant differences in executive function scores tested between the pLOF groups, although the PCGC cohort’s performance as a whole was comparable to population norms. There was no association between significant core traits and EF. 

Genetics: Twenty-seven constrained genes with pLOF-constrained variants in the cohort were enriched in functions related to heart and embryonic development, signal transduction, and cell differentiation. Cardiac conduction system development (p < 0.001, all p-values are adjusted for multiple comparisons) cardiac septum development (p < 0.001), and positive regulation of cardiac muscle cell proliferation (p < 0.01) were the most enriched ontology terms. Some of the enriched processes participate in the synaptic vesicle cycle, such as synaptic vesicle priming (p < 0.01) and maintenance of synapse structure (p < 0.05). In addition, neurodevelopmental processes such as regulation of glial cell proliferation and neurogenesis were also enriched in these constrained genes (p < 0.05) .

The combined set of genes with pLOF-constrained variants in the cohort and connectome genes from literature were jointly enriched in brain and heart processes such as regulation of atrioventricular node development (p < 0.0001) and neurogenesis (p < 0.01). Genes that participate in regulation of neurogenesis include constrained genes DBN1, HOXB3, LYN and NOTCH1 as well as connectome-associated genes BCAN, MAPT, SEMA3A and VCAN. Genes that participate in embryonic and heart developmental processes include constrained genes GATA6, NOTCH1, NOTCH2, TBX5 and connectome-associated gene HGF (Figure 2C). 

Conclusion

Individuals with a pLOF-constrained variant had a larger structural core that included bilateral occipital regions, the precuneus and cerebellum—creating a more distributed central network than those without such variants. Although additional group differences emerged when examining specific subnetworks, only core-related measures remained significant after adjusting for covariates. Despite these core differences, whole brain structural network metrics did not differ between groups. In core analysis, regions typically identified as integral for network communication were similarly found in both groups, across both hemispheres and all lobes. We did not find network measures correlating with EF. Within the pLOF-constrained group, implicated genes were enriched for both cardiac and neurodevelopmental functions. When these constrained genes were compared to connectome-related genes from the literature, the joint enrichment analysis revealed substantial overlap with early developmental processes

Structural core analysis in CHD has predominantly been in neonatal and pediatric populations. The core regions identified in pre-operative neonates with CHD4,5 typify the core found in other populations. These findings reinforce the concept of a structural backbone that forms early in development, serves as a fundamental scaffold for all individuals, and is under genetic control3. Our present study in children and adults with CHD is consistent with this view as it suggests that core differences in CHD may be genetically driven, and more evident when analysed in conjunction with genomics (versus with controls4,5).

Despite clear differences in the core, no group-level effects emerged in the non-core subnetworks. This pattern supports the idea that core organization is more strongly shaped by genetic factors, while peripheral network architecture is more influenced by environmental inputs – consistent with prior heritability analysis of the structural connectome3. Equally notable is the absence of group differences in neuropsychological performance, which raises the possibility that environmentally driven resilience may buffer functional outcomes despite underlying core alterations. Further, there were no differences in the prevalence of CHD heart categories between the two groups, so the specific nature of altered hemodynamics due to CHD is less likely to have played a role in the different cores. However, the difference in fetal cerebral oxygen delivery in different types of CHD (i.e. dextro-transposition of the great arteries; tetralogy of fallot; hypoplastic left heart syndrome etc.) could play a role in the lack of association between the core and executive function, or have a role in influencing peripheral connectivity

Our cohort may have limited power to detect genetically driven traits, as all participants have CHD and also exhibited a lower-than-expected prevalence of neurodevelopmental impairments often seen in CHD. It is important to acknowledge that the genetic, clinical and environmental factors addressed in our work all influence the structural connectome to varying degrees in both core and periphery connectivity.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that in vivo MRI–based measures of structural connectivity can sensitively detect alterations in core network architecture associated with genetic variants in individuals with CHD, even in the absence of observable functional or cognitive differences. Disentangling the contributions of genetic influences from compensatory mechanisms - whether driven by neuroplasticity or environmental support - will be essential for defining personalized, mechanism-based therapeutic strategies to improve outcomes in CHD.
