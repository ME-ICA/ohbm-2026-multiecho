Introduction

Visual cues can evoke vivid impressions of taste. These impressions help us decide what to order at a restaurant, and contribute to our surprise if we mistake a glass of sparkling water for still water. The mechanisms underlying the powerful phenomenon of taste memory reinstatement are not well understood. While the neural underpinnings of visual memory reinstatement have been studied in humans1, these processes have not been addressed when it comes to taste. It stands to reason that the insula, which is considered to be the primary gustatory cortex and is central to taste perception2, may play a role when we bring to mind the memory of taste. Here, we sought out to address this gap and study the insula’s role in the retrieval of taste memories, using a cross-modal paradigm.

We combine functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) with a cue-association task in humans. Participants first learn pairings between visual bottle cues and six distinct flavor stimuli (two sweet, two sour, two bitter), and later use the cues to recall the associated flavors. This approach allows us to test whether the insula is engaged during taste recall. Additionally, we test whether the insula activation patterns reflect flavor-specific (or taste-specific) information, contributing to the ongoing debate regarding the nature of the representation in the insula3. 

Methods

Experimental procedure

Thirty five healthy participants (19 females) between the ages of 18 and 34, performed a cue-association task while being scanned with fMRI, followed by a taste assessment outside the scanner. The task was designed to elicit taste memories. It included 2 blocks of flavor encoding (Fig 1A), in which participants were instructed to memorize pairs of flavors and images of bottles4, followed by 3 blocks of flavor retrieval (Fig. 1B), in which participants viewed the bottles without receiving any gustatory input. The flavor retrieval stage included two conditions: a flavor condition in which subjects were instructed to recall the associated flavor in order to respond to a question about its properties, and a visual condition in which subjects were instructed to retain the bottle’s appearance in mind in order to respond to a question about its visual properties. 

Stimuli:

Six flavors were used, with the intention of including flavors with similar, as well as different taste qualities: apple juice and watermelon juice (sweet), unsweetened lemon juice and cranberry juice (sour) and unsweetened grapefruit juice and black tea (bitter). Each flavor was randomly associated with 2 different bottle stimuli. During flavor encoding, each unique bottle-flavor pair was presented 6 times, such that each flavor was delivered a total of 12 times. Between each flavor-bottle pair, participants’ mouths were rinsed with water for a duration of 3s (repeating a total of 72 times). During flavor retrieval, each flavor was probed 15 times.

Tastants were delivered via a custom-built MRI-compatible system. Both visual stimuli presentation and tastant delivery were controlled and synchronized via a custom-built program developed in the PsychoPy3 environment.

Image acquisition:

fMRI data were collected at the MRI Platform at the Zuckerman Mind Brain Behavior Institute at Columbia University, using a 3T Siemens Priesma scanner and a 32-channel head coil. BOLD acquisition was done with spin-echo echoplanar imaging sequence (TE/TR = 30/1560 ms, flip angle = 65°, voxel size = 2 × 2 × 2 mm3, 72 axial slices). Before task scans, 1 min EPI scans were acquired with opposite phase encoding direction, which were used for correction of spatial distortion artifacts during preprocessing. An additional high resolution MPRAGE sequence was used to provide an anatomical reference for the fMRI analysis (TE = 2.98 ms, TR = 2300 ms, flip angle = 9°, voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm).

Analysis

Preprocessing was done using fMRIprep. The preprocessing steps include motion correction, field unwarping, spatial normalization to MNI-space, bias field correction, and brain extraction.

Univariate analysis was performed using FSL, in the framework of a general linear model (GLM). For flavor encoding, the model included separate regressors for the onsets of the 6 different flavors, as well as a regressor for the rinse period. For the flavor retrieval, the model included 9 task relevant regressors: 7 regressors for image onsets (6 for the flavor condition and 1 for the visual condition), as well as 2 regressors for the questions period (1 per condition). The GLMs also included 13 nuisance regressors estimated with fMRIprep, to account for confounds such as global signal change and movement. 

Multivariate similarity analysis was used to determine whether activation in the insula includes a representation of taste. First, GLMsingle was used to compute trial-wise GLM beta estimates. The beta estimates in the insula ROI were extracted and their between-trial similarity was computed using Pearson's correlation. Averaging across trials from the same condition, we derived a 6x6 matrix showing the spatial similarity in insula activation within and between each flavor’s memory retrieval. This matrix was compared to a perceptual similarity matrix constructed from each participant’s rating of the flavors after the scan. 

Results

First, we performed univariate analysis to test whether the insula is activated during taste encoding and retrieval. For flavor encoding we compared the learning period, in which participants are presented with the bottles-flavor pairs, to the rinse period, in which participants are presented with a droplet while receiving water. As expected, we found increased BOLD activation in the mid-anterior insula, as well the visual cortex, and several subcortical regions. For flavor retrieval, we compared the flavor trials, in which participants are presented with the bottles and are instructed to recall their associated flavors, to the visual trials, in which they view the bottle but are instructed to focus on their visual properties. Strikingly, we find that the BOLD signal in the mid-anterior insula increased during flavor retrieval, even in the absence of any sensory flavor input (Fig. 2A).

To determine whether the BOLD pattern during memory retrieval carries a representation of flavor identity, we performed a similarity analysis in the insula. We asked whether the BOLD pattern similarity between pairs of trials can be explained by the flavors identity, as estimated using individual perceptual similarity. We found that perceptually similar flavors elicited more similar activation patterns in the right insula, both during encoding (𝑝<.0001) and retrieval (𝑝=.001), suggesting the insula encodes aspects of flavor identity (Fig. 2B).

Conclusion

This work shows for the first time that the role of the insula in taste representation extends beyond perception, and into taste encoding and retrieval. Future work will use a searchlight analysis to explore the involvement of other brain regions during taste retrieval. The current analysis relies on subjects’ perceptual similarity of the flavors  to evaluate whether the insula encodes flavor identity. Unsurprisingly,  flavors from the same taste category were perceived as very similar, compared with flavors from different categories. Consequently, the RSA results may be driven by the insula’s representation of taste categories rather than of the individual flavors.   We will evaluate the decoding of similar flavors to determine whether the insula represents flavor identity during memory retrieval.

These results provide insights into the role of the insula during both encoding and retrieval of the sensory details of taste, advancing our understanding of sensory representation beyond visual and auditory experience. Finally, combined with existing findings showing the insula receives interoceptive signals (e.g. hunger signals), this work raises new questions about how our physiological state affects long-term representation of taste, and how this modulation may be supported by the insula.
