Introduction

A major challenge in neuroscience lifespan studies is to connect individual differences in grey and white matter morphologies with their corresponding electrophysiological signatures while ensuring that results are statistically robust and clinically useful. Ageing exerts systematic and widespread effects on neural oscillations, particularly during task-negative or resting-state.1,2 Recent work has identified and differentiated this global ageing map into six distinct oscillatory changes with large, yet heterogeneous spatial patterns and effect sizes.2 Although these changes are replicable across many datasets and robust to several covariates, the underlying anatomical specificity of these spectral changes and effects remains unclear. 

This work investigated the structural-functional organisation underlying these ageing signatures via performing an anatomically driven, modality-specific decomposition of the global age effects spanning across cortical, subcortical, and white-matter human brain compartments. Specifically, we isolated the dominant patterns of structural variation in terms of first Principal Components (PC)s of an overall 190 imaging derived phenotypes, these were derived independently from three separate anatomical domains that constitutes whole cerebral cortex's parcels, subcortical volumes and major white-matter tracts’ microstructure measures. We then examined each component’s relationships with neural oscillatory activity as measured by magnetoencephalography (MEG) spectral power. For each structural domain, General Linear Models (GLMs) were constructed to relate PC scores to spectral power across all sensor-frequency combinations. Each separate neuroanatomical domain revealed distinct spectral–spatial maps reflecting its unique underlying electrophysiological roles.

Methods

The Cambridge Centre for Ageing and Neuroscience’s (Cam-CAN) ageing cohort was selected in this work due to its multimodal nature and the availability of structural MRI and MEG data from a large, population-based sample (557 participants), providing a cross-sectional window into the adult lifespan (18-88).3

Structural MRI data were processed via micapipe,4 an open-source container that integrates a lot of well-established and standard tools and functions for processing structural and diffusion data. Within this pipeline, FreeSurfer's cortical parcellation was used to extract cortical thickness measurements from 148 regions from the Destrieux anatomical atlas.  Subcortical morphometric measures for left and right hemispheres were extracted via FMRIB's Integrated Registration and Segmentation Tool (FIRST) with normalised volumes are calculated as a percentage of the total intracranial brain volume. Lastly, we also used micapipe for estimating white matter tracts from whole-brain tractography. Weighted fractional anisotropy (FA) values were computed to obtain tract-specific microstructural measures across total of 28 major white matter tracts. These metrics were calculated by averaging voxel-wise FA values within each tract, weighted by tract density. Quality control procedures were implemented independently for each tissue class (cortical, subcortical, white matter). Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was then conducted for each neuroanatomical domain (cortical parcels, subcortical volumes, and white matter tracts) to identify latent patterns of structural covariation. Next, split-half cross-validation were used to assess the stability of principal component modes. The largest principal components with (PC1s) exceeded mean split-half correlations of 0.8 across all modalities. PCA component scores for each domain were sign-corrected by multiplying each component by the sign of its correlation with age to ensure an interpretable alignment with the ageing effect. 

Cam-CAN’s eye-closed resting-state MEG data were preprocessed and analysed via MNE-Python and OSL-Ephys pipeline, then transformed into sensor-space spectral power estimates (sensors by frequencies) for each participant. A group level GLM spectrum was conducted separately for neuroanatomical domain with the design matrix including an intercept term capturing the average spectral power across all participants and one of the primary regressors of interest i.e. the PC1 scores for the cortical, subcortical and white matter structural modalities. All these design matrices were checked for efficiency and collineralty. The resulting spectrum of regression coefficients identifies spatial and spectral regions where structural brain measures predict oscillatory dynamics based on each anatomical modality. 

The GLM parameters were estimated independently at each sensor-frequency combination using Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) estimation as implemented in the glmtools package.5 We also conducted one other separate GLM for testing the direct raw age effects on MEG power (without PC mediators). This "age-only" model included mean and age (z-scored) as regressors and its results were reported in parallel to PC-GLM ones, allowing or facilitating the comparison between structural mediation pathways and direct chronological effects. 

Next, we used maximum statistic permutation testing in which PC scores were shuffled randomly across participants in each permutation, and all models were then re-estimated with the permuted design matrix, after which the maximum absolute t-statistic across all sensor-frequency points were recorded to obtain a null distribution and detect the statistically significant relationships between neuroanatomical components and MEG power. Finally, using cluster-based analysis of significant effects, we summarised the spatial and spectral extent of significant imaging derived phenotypes-MEG associations. 

Results

We quantified age-related changes and examined global axes of major variance in the resting-state MEG frequency spectrum at the whole-head level and within three specific anatomical domains. The top panel in Figure 1 displays the canonical age map, which we generated via a separate, age-only model based analysis, to reproduce the global ageing effects and illustrate their spatial and spectral distributions.1  This result is computed without any anatomical covariates to establish a baseline to assess the unique contribution of domain’s related patterns beyond general age-related effects. Panels 2-4 represent frequency-by-space 2D maps for the first principal component of cortical parcels, subcortical volumes, and white-matter tracts, respectively. Accordingly, these anatomically driven signatures further differentiate the canonical age effect into similar 2D structures; specifically, each corresponding pixel in each map indicates the likely tissue origin of the age effect or anatomical source of the greatest contribution (whether attributed to a single anatomical modality or a combination of some or even all thereof). 

The cortical PC1 closely mirrors the global ageing signature, particularly in the low frequency bands, with high-alpha suppression, beta increases as well as an increase within gamma spectra powers with age. There are several distinctions between the cortical thickness effect and the ageing effect. The low alpha sub-band, the harmonic of alpha and the decrease with gamma are modulated by age but not by cortical thickness. 

In contrast, the subcortical map revealed a biphasic low alpha enhancement followed by high alpha suppression. The combination of these effects strongly relates to decreases in alpha peak frequency across age. In addition, subcortical parcels are related to the secondary alpha harmonic near 18 Hz which relates to the non-linear waveform shape of the alpha oscillation. Finally, the pronounced high gamma suppression with age is strongly associated with subcortical volumes. The low-frequency and low-gamma effects of age appear to be unrelated to change in subcortical volumes.

White-matter PC1 exhibited a more attenuated, low-contrast overall results: lower frequencies ranges, in particular, were least impacted or minimally affected by white matter architecture, followed by mild alpha enhancement, strong suppression in the high alpha sub-band range, compressed beta modulation, and near-neutral gamma activity.. 

Collectively, these unique patterns clearly reflect domain-specific spectral profiles that are based on or derived from their distinct anatomical compartments.

Conclusion

In this work, we examined the structural-functional organisation of neural activity across cortical, subcortical, and white matter compartments using principal component-based analyses of whole head frequency-resolved t-statistics over the adult lifespan. Each anatomical compartment had several components and revealed distinct spectral–spatial profiles reflecting their unique underlying electrophysiological roles, and demonstrating unique relationships between each pattern and resting-state MEG power spectra across the adult lifespan. Comparing the canonical ageing signature results to our neuroanatomical PC results allowed us to assess the degree to which the anatomical patterns of variability are distinct from, or overlapping with, age-related variance. 

Some functional responses were specific to a single anatomical modality, this was particularly evident for the low-frequency oscillations (<8 Hz), which correlated only with the cortical mantle and were dominated almost entirely by cortical parcel contributions, while subcortical and white-matter PCs exhibited little effect and minimal correlations respectively. A similar phenomenon was also observed at the sub-band level, specifically within the low-alpha band (8–10 Hz), which showed a nearly exclusive subcortical contribution, whereas cortical and white matter PCs exhibited almost negligible effects. This suggest that low alpha power generation depend on subcortical morphometry.

Other functional responses, however, particularly within the high alpha and beta ranges (10–20 Hz) were robustly correlated with all grey and white matter neuroanatomical features, reflecting their dependence on the entirety of the anatomical backgrounds and substrates. This also clearly suggests a combined multi-compartmental origin with all cortical, subcortical and white matter structures contributing substantially and almost equally. This notable convergence in the latter frequency ranges unequivocally suggests that these oscillations -and hence their associations with attentional control systems and essential cognitive functions- require, and could only emerge from, an integrated structural integrity and coordination across all three anatomical compartments. Together, these differences across tissues demonstrated that substantial part of the characteristic whole-head, full-frequency ageing effects’ patterns arise from three complementary yet anatomically distinct spectral modes. 

Overall, the current study offers a comprehensive, whole-brain framework integrating multiple structural tissue types with resting-state electrophysiological activity using Cam-CAN ageing cohort to understand the underlying organising principles of structure-function coupling. These multimodal profiles offer a window that characterises the magnitude, polarity, spatial distribution, and spectral specificity of lifespan neural dynamics.

This work has explored how anatomy-based variations -at macro and microstructural levels- could constrain and or enable patterns of ongoing neural activity across the adult lifespan. Indeed, the current findings help us understand and could provide a foundation for future investigations into how multimodal compartment-specific structural neural markers may better diagnose and predict a variety of neurocognitive disorder-related susceptibilities and atypicalities, differentiating clinical trajectories and assessing deviations from established normative measures across the lifespan.
