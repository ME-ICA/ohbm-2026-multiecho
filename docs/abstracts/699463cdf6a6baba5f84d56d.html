Introduction

Graph-theoretical analyses of dynamic functional connectivity (dFC) enable quantification of recruitment and integration using the recruitment coefficient (RC) and integration coefficient (IC).1 These metrics indicate how consistently regions engage with their local network over time and how they communicate across different functional systems, respectively. Together, they offer insights into network stability and plasticity, processes thought to be important for motor recovery. In our prior work in chronic spinal cord injury (SCI), we used a multilayer community detection framework2 with layers defined across subjects, revealing altered RC and IC within the sensorimotor network (SMN)1. That study also identified a segregation of the SMN into two functionally distinct subregions, SMN-1 (upper and lower body regions) and SMN-2 (orofacial regions) (Figure 1). Building on this earlier work, the current follow-up study examined how these network properties change within individuals following 4 weeks of cycling training, with or without functional electrical stimulation (FES). In this study, the multilayer community detection approach was performed with time points as layers, such that RC and IC quantify within-person temporal stability of community assignments. Based on the previously defined SMN subdivision, analyses were restricted to nodes within the predefined SMN-1 and SMN-2 subregions.

Methods

Twelve individuals with chronic SCI participated in the study, divided into two groups: seven in the FES cycling group and five in the passive cycling-only group. They completed baseline and post-intervention assessments, which included MRI scans and neurological assessments using the International Standards for Neurological Classification of Spinal Cord Injury (ISNCSCI), with the Lower Extremity Motor Score (LEMS) as the primary clinical measure. Over four weeks, participants underwent cycling training three sessions per week, each lasting one hour at 50 RPM with the FES group receiving electrical stimulation, while the passive group followed the identical protocol without stimulation. Stimulation settings were tailored individually to keep the cadence consistent. 

Resting-state fMRI data were acquired on a 3T Philips Achieva scanner using a 32-channel head coil and a multi-slice SENSE–EPI sequence (TR/TE = 2000/30 ms; flip angle = 75°; 37 axial slices; 3 mm isotropic resolution; 7 minutes). Regional time courses were extracted using the Schaefer–Yeo3 200-parcellation (7 networks), and dynamic functional connectivity was computed using sliding-window correlations (15 TR window, 50% overlap).

Multilayer networks were constructed with temporal windows treated as layers. Community structure was estimated using repeated GenLouvain modularity optimization (γ = 1.0, ω = 1.0; 100 repetitions), followed by consensus partitioning via agreement matrices4. Analyses were restricted to predefined sensorimotor network subregions (SMN-1 and SMN-2) identified in prior work, from which RC and IC were computed for each node and subject. Finally, group differences in ΔRC and ΔIC (post–pre) were assessed using exact permutation tests with false discovery rate correction, and within-group associations with ΔLEMS were evaluated using permutation-based Spearman correlations5 with group-stratified analyses.

Results

Group-level ΔRC and ΔIC were modest and did not reach statistical significance in either SMN-1 or SMN-2 (q > 0.05). In contrast, consistent brain–behavior relationships emerged at the nodal level within SMN-1, which comprises upper- and lower-body sensorimotor representations. Three SMN-1 nodes, the full set surviving FDR correction within the pre-specified SMN-1 region were identified. These primarily left-lateralized somatomotor regions showed strong positive correlations between ΔRC and ΔLEMS (ρ = 0.88-0.90, FDR-corrected q < 0.05), indicating that participants with greater motor improvements also exhibited larger increases in the temporal stability of SMN-1 community affiliation (Figure 2). Rank-order plots demonstrate that these associations were rank-consistent with respect to ΔLEMS and robust to leave-one-out (LOO) sensitivity analyses, indicating that the observed relationships were not driven by any single participant (Figure 2). 

These associations were observed only in the FES group. When stratified by intervention, FES participants demonstrated consistently strong ΔRC–ΔLEMS relationships, whereas no meaningful associations were observed in the passive cycling-only group. Although formal interaction tests did not reach statistical significance, which is consistent with limited statistical power, the observed group-specific pattern is compatible with an intervention-related coupling between SMN-1 network reorganization and motor recovery. In contrast, SMN-2, encompassing orofacial representations, showed no comparable associations for either RC or IC. 

Conclusion

Although there were no significant differences at the group level in ΔRC or ΔIC, the selective and robust ΔRC–ΔLEMS coupling within SMN-1 (upper/lower body representations) indicates a potentially meaningful neurobiological signal: early motor improvements were accompanied by increased temporal stability of sensorimotor community organization in individuals receiving FES. Because the multilayer framework used here quantifies temporal stability within individuals, the directionality of these Δmetrics should not be directly compared to that of cross-subject multilayer estimates. Overall, the dissociation between null group-mean effects and the strong subject-level brain–behavior coupling suggests that individual-specific network reconfiguration might be a more sensitive indicator of early recovery in SCI than average group changes. 

These findings should be considered in light of a small sample size, which limits the ability to detect group effects and interactions, and should be interpreted with caution. Although overall mean network changes were subtle, FES participants exhibited regionally specific increases in SMN-1 recruitment that closely tracked motor improvements. Together, these findings suggest that multilayer community metrics may be useful for tracking individual-level neurorehabilitation response.
