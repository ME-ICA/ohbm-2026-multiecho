Introduction

Accurate delineation of ischemic infarcts in acute MRI is critical for clinical decision-making. Automatic segmentation of acute ischemic stroke (AIS) lesions has therefore become an active area of research. Supported by expert-annotated, multicenter MRI datasets, deep learning (DL) has emerged as a powerful tool for AIS lesion segmentation.

Among proposed methods, U-Net variants dominate the field, with design variations including loss functions, deep supervision (DS), residual connections, and attention mechanisms. However, comparing approaches remains challenging, firstly because most implementations are not publicly accessible, and secondly because few have been evaluated on external data.1

Related to this latter challenge, domain adaptation has emerged as a promising strategy to enhance cross-site robustness of DL models in AIS lesion segmentation.2 Ensemble learning, which combines multiple independently trained models, can also improve generalization by reducing overfitting and has shown benefits in AIS lesion segmentation.3

In this study, we propose ISLA (Ischemic Stroke Lesion Analyzer), a new DL model for AIS lesion segmentation from acute diffusion MRI. The first ISLA variant corresponds to the best-performing U-Net–based model selected through internal validation, comparing different loss functions, DS, residual versus standard convolutional blocks, and attention mechanisms. We further developed two additional variants: one incorporating unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) and another employing ensemble learning across multiple base models.

Methods

This study includes data from two public databases containing diffusion-weighted images (DWI) with ischemic stroke lesion annotations—SOOP and ISLES 2022—which were used for training, as well as a multicenter private database of MR images acquired in routine clinical practice from the Hauts-de-France region. The private dataset was divided into an internal validation set (HDF-val) and an external test set (HDF-test). Both HDF-val and HDF-test included manually annotated DWIs for AIS lesions along with additional unannotated DWIs. Acquisition centers and lesion annotators were separated between the two sets.

All participants had ischemic stroke and underwent MRI in the acute or subacute phase. The sample sizes were as follows: 1266 cases for SOOP, 250 for ISLES 2022, 223 labeled cases and 627 unlabeled cases for HDF-val, and 100 labeled cases and 633 unlabeled cases for HDF-test.

The baseline ISLA model is a 3D U-Net architecture that takes trace DWI and apparent diffusion coefficient maps as inputs (Figure 1). We evaluated several variants to identify the most effective configuration. First, we compared two loss functions: a combined generalized Dice loss and binary cross-entropy (GDL-BCE) and Unified Focal Loss (UFL). Second, we compared standard versus residual convolutional blocks. Third, we added deep supervision at each decoder stage. Fourth, we implemented several spatial and channel attention mechanisms.

We ranked the models by segmentation accuracy on HDF-val using the ISLES 2022 case-level ranking method applied with the Dice similarity coefficient (DSC), absolute volume difference (AVC), lesion-wise F1-score (F1), absolute lesion count difference (ALD), and 95th percentile of Hausdorff distance (HD95). The top-ranked model was defined as ISLA-B.

Based on this ranking, we also implemented ensemble models that averaged output probability maps from the base models. We explored combinations of the top two models, top three models, and so on. ISLA-ENS was defined as the ensemble configuration achieving the best performance on HDF-val.

In addition, we used UDA to improve generalization on a target domain for which only unlabeled data were available. More specifically, we implemented a Mean Teacher (MT)–based framework. The weight of the MT consistency loss—applied to the unlabeled data—was optimized on the labeled HDF-val, and the resulting model was named ISLA-MT.

After defining the three variants, we retrained them on the SOOP, ISLES, and HDF-val datasets—and on unlabeled HDF-test data for ISLA-MT—and evaluated them on the labeled HDF-test dataset. We compared their performance with two publicly available SOTA models for AIS lesion segmentation: DAGMNet4 and DeepISLES.3


Results

In preliminary evaluations of models without attention mechanisms, GDL-BCE was excluded due to poorer performance compared with UFL on HDF-val. All models using UFL were therefore compared. DS consistently improved performance across most metrics, and the benefit was more pronounced when combined with attention mechanisms.

Validation results also showed slightly better segmentation performance with both our MT-based UDA framework and the ensemble models. All ensemble configurations outperformed the single best base model. ISLA-ENS, which includes the six best-ranked base models, achieved the highest performance.

Figure 2a shows the distributions of segmentation metrics on the full HDF-test and stratified by lesion size. Across the full test set, both SOTA approaches were outperformed by all ISLA variants across all metrics when comparing medians, and the ISLA models exhibited smaller interquartile ranges. Comparable patterns were observed within lesion-size strata, with one exception: DAGMNet achieved DSC values on small lesions similar to ISLA-MT, though ISLA-MT remained clearly superior on the other metrics. Overall, DAGMNet outperformed DeepISLES on DSC, AVD, and HD95 but was slightly worse on ALD and F1. While establishing a strict hierarchy among the three ISLA variants is difficult, ISLA-ENS showed the most consistent performance across metrics and lesion sizes.

Applying the case-level ranking across the five models identified ISLA-ENS as the top-performing method on average. It ranked first for all metrics except ALD, for which ISLA-MT achieved the highest score. ISLA-MT performed similarly to ISLA-B across all metrics. Both DAGMNet and DeepISLES performed worse than all ISLA variants in terms of median values and rankings. With the exception of ALD and F1, DeepISLES was the lowest-performing method.

Qualitative comparisons between DAGMNet and ISLA-ENS showed that ISLA-ENS was more robust to MR artifacts (inhomogeneity, magnetic susceptibility near the ears or nose, corticospinal tract anisotropy), whereas such artifacts often resulted in false positives for DAGMNet (Figure 2b).

Conclusion

We introduced ISLA, a 3D DL model for MRI-based AIS lesion segmentation, trained on three multicenter datasets totaling more than 1500 AIS participants. The model was developed through systematic experiments evaluating loss functions, convolutional architectures, deep supervision, attention mechanisms, domain adaptation, and ensemble learning. Evaluation on an external clinical test set, together with comparisons against two SOTA approaches, demonstrated ISLA's sensitivity and robustness to MR artifacts across lesion sizes and segmentation metrics.

On the validation set, UFL outperformed GDL-BCE, consistent with previous work showing UFL’s superior performance in MRI-based 3D brain tumor segmentation. Validation results also showed clear performance gains from DS. Previous studies reported that DS helps mitigate the vanishing-gradient problem, and attention mechanisms further improved segmentation accuracy.

The MT-based UDA strategy yielded slightly more accurate segmentations on both validation and test sets. However, given practical constraints on accessing labeled source data and unlabeled target data, these gains may be insufficient for real-world deployment. In contrast, previous work reported more substantial improvements using a similar strategy.5 A plausible explanation for this discrepancy is the modest size of the source-domain datasets used in those studies compared with ours.

Among the three ISLA variants, the ensemble approach (ISLA-ENS) was clearly the most performant on the external test set. Even on the validation set—where ISLA-B was selected as the top-performing base model, potentially underestimating its errors—all ensemble variants outperformed ISLA-B. This is consistent with previous work showing that majority-voting schemes can outperform top-ranked ISLES 2022 models.3

The ISLA variants clearly outperformed DAGMNet and DeepISLES across all metrics and lesion-size strata. Additional analyses showed that DAGMNet produced numerous false positives far from any true lesion, whereas ISLA-ENS did not, suggesting better clinical usability.

DeepISLES was the lowest-performing model overall, mainly due to low sensitivity. All three DeepISLES constituent models also performed poorly on our test set. The short onset-to-scan delay in HDF-test may partly explain this, as DeepISLES has been reported to be less accurate in the acute than in the subacute phase.3

In this study, the three proposed ISLA variants demonstrated strong and consistent performance on an clinical test set, outperforming two SOTA approaches across lesion sizes and metrics. Qualitative analyzes further showed high sensitivity and strong robustness to MR artifacts, reducing clinically irrelevant false positives. Future work should extend validation to additional clinical centers and imaging protocols to further assess generalizability and readiness for real-world deployment.
