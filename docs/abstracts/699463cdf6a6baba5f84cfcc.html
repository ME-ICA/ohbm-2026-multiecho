Introduction

Neuroplasticity is the ability of the nervous system to change its activity in response to stimuli by reorganizing its structure, functions, or connectivity. It is a complex biological phenomenon, spanning macro-scale volumetric to micro-structural changes, measurable through structural (T1-based) or diffusion MRI, as well as functional alterations captured by resting-state or task-based fMRI. Since neuroplasticity is crucial for our ability to learn and adapt, it is widely studied across these modalities. However, while most studies examine effects within a single MRI contrast, a comprehensive understanding of the biological process may require a broader, multimodal perspective. In recent years, multimodal MRI acquisitions have become increasingly common, providing multiple image contrasts and quantitative measures for analysis. These are often combined in pseudo-multivariate analyses, where each measure is analyzed separately or multiple measures are used as distinct features in predictive models of behavioral change1. Yet, despite the growing use of multimodal MRI, no true multivariate framework – integrating variance across modalities within a unified feature space – has been widely implemented in this field. Here we introduce a multivariate framework for the investigation of learning-related neuroplasticity. Rather than performing univariate comparisons for each measure separately, we apply principal component analysis (PCA) across both spatial (e.g., brain vertices) and measure (e.g., MRI modalities) dimensions, extracting components that represent linear combinations of contrast measures across vertices. We evaluate this framework using a longitudinal learning dataset, showing that the resulting components capture cross-modal patterns of change, representing measures more directly related to the underlying neuroplastic process. These multivariate features may offer a novel biomarker for skill acquisition and adaptive learning.

Methods

As part of a larger study2, 87 participants with no prior sign language knowledge completed a 4-week Israeli Sign Language (ISL) course, taught in four groups of 17-22 participants covering ISL vocabulary, grammar, and syntax. Each participant underwent MRI scanning before and after the course (mean interval 5.6 weeks). Twenty control participants were scanned at comparable intervals without receiving language training. Data from 83 learners and 18 controls were included after excluding poor-quality or incomplete datasets. All learning participants completed an ISL proficiency test after the course. Out of 83 learners, 58 returned to a follow-up assessment 6 months after course completion. The study was approved by the Sheba Medical Center IRB, and all participants provided written informed consent in accordance with ethical regulations.

MRI data (T1, T2, diffusion, task and resting-state fMRI) were acquired on a 3T Siemens Prisma scanner with a 64-channel coil at Tel Aviv University Strauss Center for Computational Neuroimaging and preprocessed using the standard HCP pipeline3. Sixteen cortical measures were derived across modalities: four T1-based (cortical thickness, curvature, sulcal depth, myelination), two diffusion-based (Fractional Anisotropy - FA, Mean Diffusivity – MD), six resting-state network maps (seed-to-voxel connectivity maps with six of Yeo’s functional networks4 as seeds: Visual, Somatomotor, Dorsal Attention, Salience, Fronto-Parietal, DMN), and four task-based contrasts (sentences > dance, learned > dance, learned > unlearned, dance > fixation). Task contrasts were selected from ten available ones using a Dice index to maximize coverage area, while including all five available task conditions and avoiding contrast triads (e.g. 1-2, 2-3, 3-1).

The 16 surface-projected measures were normalized across scans and vertices. These were then concatenated and subjected to PCA across spatial and measure dimensions together (Fig. 1), yielding 166 components (166 scans; 83 participants × 2 scans). Analyses focused on the first 50 components, assumed to capture more shared variance across modalities and regions. Differences in component scores between post- and pre-learning scans (ΔPC = post − pre) were correlated with ISL test scores, and loadings were examined across both spatial and measure dimensions to identify shared multivariate patterns.

PCA components were used to train classification and regression models, predicting session identity (pre/post learning) and ISL proficiency test scores (taken 6 months after learning). Feature selection employed cross-validated (CV) stepwise regression, with accuracy and MSE as performance metrics. CV scheme was grouped by participant, to avoid data leakage between two scans of the same individual. Classification models include KNN, logistic regression, and Random Forest classifier. Regression models include Ridge and Lasso regressions and were run using only data from the post-learning scan of each subject, as in the original ISL study2. To improve the stability of the PCA decomposition, 25 additional participants without behavioral test scores were included in the component extraction stage. Their data contributed to the estimation of the PCA space but were not used for model training or evaluation. 

To assess the contribution and stability of each modality, ablation and robustness analyses were conducted by removing or replacing sets of measures before PCA. For ablation, one modality type (e.g., structural, resting state) was excluded before component-extraction. For robustness, alternative measures from the same modality were substituted: diffusion eigenvalues (L1, L2, L3) replaced MD, and an alternate combination of four task contrasts replaced the original set. The six resting-state maps were replaced with 43 ICA-derived networks. 

Together, these analyses provide a comprehensive multivariate framework linking distributed MRI features to learning-related neuroplasticity.

Results

The resulting principal components include some learning-related components, and others that do not seem to change between scans, as evident when comparing PC1 and PC2 in Fig. 2. The second principal component (PC2), which exhibited the largest mean change between scans across the learning participants (both the mean of directional differences and the mean of absolute differences), remained stable in the control subjects. As such, it was the first feature selected by the stepwise regression classifiers, indicating its strong predictive relevance for learning-induced neuroplastic processes.

Two sets of components were found to correlate with behavioral scores: PC2 and four other components were found to significantly correlate in value with the behavioral test scores. Higher PC2 values were associated with greater post-course proficiency, consistent with its positive change following learning. Another set of three components, two of them overlapping with the previous set of components (not including PC2) correlate in differential value with the test scores.

Classifiers trained to predict session identity using all available measures and the first 50 components achieved accuracy scores of up to 0.969, well above the 0.5 chance level. Including the control subjects’ data and labeling both of their scans as ‘first scan’ (since no language learning was introduced and no change was expected) yielded an accuracy of 0.975, indicating that the model correctly classified both scans from the control subjects as the first scan. In the ablation runs, removing structural measures before component-extraction resulted in the same accuracy score, and removing resting-state measures lowered the accuracy to 0.906. However, without the task contrasts, model accuracy ranged from 0.406 to 0.625, depending on the model. Another demonstration of this result is the fact that with all measures, models’ AUC ranged from 0.97 to 0.99, while without task contrasts, it dropped to 0.44-0.54. Altogether, these findings indicate that task-based contrasts contribute most critically to the discriminability between pre- and post-learning scans. In the robustness runs, where measures of one modality were substituted for alternative measures, all configurations achieved at least one model with an accuracy score of 0.875 or higher. This indicates that the predictive framework is robust to reasonable variation in feature definitions.

Regression models were trained and tested only on second scans (post-learning) data, which means that the principal components extracted from the data were different from those in Fig. 2, and are not expected to contain pre- and post-learning variability. Here, we used only the first 30 components extracted, as some inner folds did not contain enough subjects to produce 50 components.

Regression models trained to predict 6-months delayed test performance from post-learning data, successfully predicted from all available measures (Pearson’s r=0.332, p=0.011), from robustness run of alternative task contrasts (Pearson’s r=0.315, p=0.016), from ablation runs without structural measures (Pearson’s r=0.327, p=0.013) or without task fMRI measures (Pearson’s r=0.303, p=0.021) and from the data of two best-performing task contrasts only, instead of the original 16 measures (Pearson’s r=0.359, p=0.006). Prediction was not signiﬁcantly successful from robustness run of alternative resting-state measures (Pearson’s r=0.234, p=0.075) and alternative diffusion measures (Pearson’s r=0.247, p=0.062), and from ablation run without resting-state measures (Pearson’s r=0.185, p=0.172).

Conclusion

This study introduces a unified multivariate framework for investigating learning-related neuroplasticity using multimodal MRI. By jointly analyzing structural, diffusion, and functional measures through PCA spanning both spatial (brain locations) and measure (MRI modalities) dimensions, we provide an integrative representation of neural change that transcends the boundaries of single-modality analyses. The resulting components captured meaningful shared variance across modalities, enabling the identification of distributed neural patterns associated with skill acquisition. Notably, the second principal component (PC2) showed a clear and consistent increase following sign language learning while remaining stable in control participants. This component was the strongest predictor of session identity and correlated with behavioral proficiency, highlighting its relevance as a marker of neuroplasticity related to language learning.

Importantly, the multivariate components extracted here were not only sensitive to neural change but also interpretable in their biological and functional context. Components showing behavioral correlations suggest that the learning process engages coordinated adaptations spanning cortical structure, connectivity, and activation. This supports the view that neuroplasticity reflects both localized and distributed adjustments across multiple scales of brain function, rather than modality-specific effects.

Task-based fMRI measures contributed most critically to classification between pre- and post-learning scans. Models excluding task contrasts performed substantially worse, whereas those using all modalities achieved near-perfect accuracy. This underscores that active, task-evoked responses provide the most direct neural indicators of learning-driven reorganization. Nevertheless, the framework proved robust: replacing measures within modalities or using alternate contrasts maintained high performance, underscoring both generalizability and flexibility across acquisition protocols. The resting-state alternative of 43 ICA components proved to be ineffective in the regression models, possibly due to the high number of features added.

Regression analyses showed that post-learning MRI data moderately but significantly predicted individual differences in delayed behavioral performance. This finding supports the idea that multivariate neural signatures extracted across modalities encode aspects of learning retention and consolidation. Together, these results demonstrate that the proposed framework not only detects learning-related neural change but also captures individual variability in learning outcomes.

Several methodological limitations must be considered. First, task-fMRI contrasts inherently exhibit broader spatial effects than structural or diffusion measures5, which typically change in smaller, localized regions. This imbalance may cause the PCA to overemphasize functional variance, though this likely reflects both methodological and biological realities of learning-related activation. Applying the same PCA-based framework regionally may offer a finer-grained and more balanced cross-modal integration. Second, the reliance on task-based measures constrains generalizability across learning paradigms. Ongoing work aims to predict task activation maps from resting-state data, potentially allowing the framework to extend to datasets lacking specific task-fMRI protocols.

In summary, this study demonstrates that a multivariate, multimodal framework can capture and predict learning-related brain changes. By jointly characterizing variance across structure, diffusion, and function, it offers a scalable and interpretable approach for studying human neuroplasticity. Addressing measure imbalance and task dependence will further enhance its utility for mapping distributed learning mechanisms and developing reliable MRI-based biomarkers of neuroplasticity.
