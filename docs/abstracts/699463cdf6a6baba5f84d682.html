Introduction

The primary motor cortex (M1) exhibits sophisticated functional organization, with recent evidence revealing a somato-cognitive action network (SCAN) that integrates motor execution with cognitive control.1 While aging is known to affect motor function, how aging impacts the intrinsic functional organization of M1 remains poorly understood. Furthermore, whether targeted interventions can restore this age-related functional decline is unclear. Functional gradients provide a continuous representation of neural organization2 that captures both effector-specific regions and inter-effector integration zones. Here, we investigated age-related changes in M1 functional gradients and examined training-induced plasticity using a novel exergaming paradigm that combines motor and cognitive demands. We hypothesized that aging would disrupt the hierarchical gradient organization of M1, and that exergame training would promote functional reorganization toward a younger pattern compared to cognitive training alone.

Methods

34 young adults (mean age: 20.66 ± 3.18 years) and 93 older adults (mean age: 67.68 ± 6.26 years) participated. Older adults were randomly assigned to three groups: exergame training group (ETG, n=31, dance mat-based motor-cognitive training), cognitive game training group (CTG, n=31, tablet-based cognitive training), or control group (CG, n=31, no intervention). Participants in ETG and CTG completed 24 training sessions over 8 weeks using FISHERMAN, a custom-designed cognitive control training game delivered either via a dance mat (exergame format) or on a tablet (cognitive game format).3 Resting-state fMRI data were collected pre-training (all participants) and post-training (older adults only). For functional gradient analysis, M1 functional connectivity matrices were first computed for each group. Local functional gradients within M1 were derived using diffusion map embedding (BrainSpace toolbox) with normalized angle kernel (Figure 1).4 The first three gradients were extracted, representing dominant axes of functional organization. We calculated variance explained by each gradient and gradient variability (standard deviation) as an index of functional differentiation.

Results

Young Adult Gradient Organization: The principal gradient (G1) captured 31.24% variance and displayed a hierarchical pattern from foot to hand to mouth effector regions along the precentral gyrus. The second gradient (G2, 17.94% variance) revealed organization from inter-effector to effector-specific regions. Gradient variability decreased systematically (G1: 0.0162; G2: 0.0092; G3: 0.0062), indicating well-differentiated functional architecture (Figure 2).

Age-Related Changes: Older adults showed altered gradient architecture. While G1 explained higher variance (34.16%), G2 and G3 were substantially weakened (10.60% and 8.8% respectively), suggesting over-consolidation of G1. Gradient variability showed a similar pattern: G1 was elevated (0.0198) while G2 (0.0061) and G3 (0.0050) were reduced, reflecting diminished secondary functional differentiation (Figure 2).

Training Effects: ETG demonstrated remarkable plasticity. Post-training, G1 of ETG participants presented a trend toward the young adult pattern. Variance explained by G1 increased from 17.3% to 21.1%. CTG and CG showed minimal changes. Spatially, ETG participants exhibited emergent inter-effector organization in G2 post-training, absent in CTG and CG groups (Figure 2).

Conclusion

This study reveals that aging fundamentally reorganizes M1 functional architecture, characterized by over-reliance on the primary gradient and degradation of secondary gradients that support inter-effector integration. Critically, functional gradients demonstrate substantial plasticity: exergame training incorporating both motor and cognitive demands improved age-related gradient reorganization of M1 gradient toward a younger pattern, while cognitive training alone produced minimal effects. These findings suggest that the combination of motor execution and cognitive control is essential for maintaining or restoring healthy M1 functional organization in aging. The divergent effects of motor-cognitive versus cognitive-only training point to distinct neural mechanisms, with potential implications for designing effective interventions for age-related decline. Future work should examine whether these gradient changes predict behavioral improvements and functional connectivity with broader motor-cognitive networks.
