Introduction

Neurodevelopment lays the foundation for lifelong brain health. Deviations therein underpin various neurological and psychiatric disorders. Thus, identifying and characterizing neurodevelopmental deviations early is a central goal of clinical neuroscience. It is well-established that intra-individual changes are a stronger predictor of behavioral and clinical outcomes, compared to inter-individual differences1. However, it is impractical to rely on multi-year longitudinal assessments to gauge an individual’s neurodevelopment, and effective prognosticating depends on predicting changes yet to come.

Presently, researchers rely on cross-sectional proxies such as normative modeling and brain-age prediction to estimate deviations from reference curves. While these approaches offer valuable snapshots of brain health, the highly dynamic period of adolescence makes cross-sectional inter-individual differences unstable and “deviation from the norm” can be significantly biased by the make-up of the reference cohort. Furthermore, recent studies have shown these measures are poor predictors of subsequent change1.

We propose a novel solution to infer neurodevelopmental trajectories from a single timepoint by framing the challenge as a longitudinal prediction problem. We hypothesized that there exists a learnable mapping from an individual’s baseline phenotype to their subsequent maturation. Furthermore, we hypothesized that high-dimensional supervised machine learning models can approximate this mapping by capturing dependencies in large longitudinal cohorts, thereby enabling generalization of learned developmental patterns to new individuals with only single-timepoint data. Here, we focused on cortical thickness, a well-established marker of neurodevelopmental processes that is also associated with psychiatric risk. Based on this, we aimed to predict follow-up cortical thickness from baseline imaging, questionnaire and genetic data.

Methods

Prediction of follow-up cortical thickness from baseline was formulated as a supervised regression task using the ABCD dataset2 (n = 2363, age at baseline 9.91 ± 0.61 years, age at follow-up 11.95 ± 0.64 years, F1.A). Inclusion criteria were baseline and follow-up T1w scans passing quality control3 and successful completion of Freesurfer (v7.2.0). Input features were the baseline cortical thickness of 400 Schaefer parcels, alongside the first three genetic principal components as well as demographic, biological and environmental characteristics selected to cover well established influences on neurodevelopment, namely sex, age, race/ethnicity, pubertal category, trauma exposure, area deprivation index, family environment scale, parental education, and parental monitoring. The prediction target was the follow-up cortical thickness of each region. Five regression model classes were evaluated spanning a broad range of model complexities: Elastic Net (EN), Support Vector Regression (SVR), Kernel Ridge Regression (KRR), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGB), and Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP).

A nested cross-validation (CV) framework was used for hyperparameter optimization and performance estimation to prevent data leakage4 (F1.B). Family identifiers were applied to group siblings, preventing them from appearing in both training and test folds. All preprocessing steps were confined to the training data, including scanner harmonization using ComBat (v0.3.3), outlier treatment (5th/95th winsorization), multicollinearity filtering (r > 0.9), and z-score scaling. For each outer fold, the inner loops evaluated candidate hyperparameter configurations using the coefficient of determination (R²). We additionally computed the generalization gap, defined as the difference between training and held-out Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), to quantify the stability of model generalization to unseen data.

Furthermore, we compared the prediction errors of models to those of a region-specific mean target null model, to quantify the extent to which each model reduced error relative to this baseline. Given the mean-prediction null model represents the best prediction achievable without using any feature information, superior performance relative to this benchmark indicates that the model captures meaningful predictive signal. Finally, feature contributions were quantified in the outer CV loops using Shapely Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values5.

For the analysis of regional differences, the nested CV R² values were projected on the cortical surface using BrainSpace (v0.1.20). To further investigate regional variability in predictability, Pearson correlations were computed between R² and the mean and standard deviation of cortical thickness at baseline, follow-up, and their longitudinal change. Additionally, we examined the relationship between R² and signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) to assess how imaging quality influences regional prediction difficulty. Lastly, we determined whether residuals from the prediction models (i.e., the differences between observed and predicted values) were correlated with demographic input features or image quality control metrics. To investigate individual specificity, for each individual i∈S we computed within and between subject correlations as riwithin= corr(yiobserved, yipredicted) and ribetween= (1/S−1)∑corr(ykobserved, yipredicted) for k∈S and k≠i, where S is the set of all subjects. Correlations were Fisher r-to-z transformed prior to averaging and statistical testing, with summary values reconverted to r for interpretation. Additionally, in a ranked based analysis, the position of riwithin in the list of all corr(ykobserved, yipredicted) values across all subjects was evaluated, where a rank of one for a given individual indicated that this individual’s prediction map exhibited the highest correlation with its own observed cortical thickness, relative to all other individuals.

Results

Our best machine learning model (EN) predicted cortical thickness at the follow-up with 0.01 ± 0.008 mean squared error (MSE) and explained on average 55% ± 0.09 of variance across regions fivefold higher than the mean target null model which yielded MSE of 0.05 ± 0.02. All models produced similar distributions of RMSE and R², except for poorer performance of the MLP (F1.C). EN stood out, however, by consistently achieving a small generalization gap, reflecting a close alignment of training and CV estimates indicating an absence of overfitting. The predicted cortical thickness at follow-up fell within one sample standard deviation of their observed values for approximately 90% of participants, highlighting the overall high performance of the model. Individual variability in predictability was not significantly associated with participant demographics (age, sex, race/ethnicity) and only minimally associated with T1 quality scores (β = - 0.002, p < 0.01), highlighting the minimal demographic bias of model prediction.

Regional predictability (using the EN model) was negatively associated with inter-individual variability in longitudinal cortical thickness change (r = -0.61, p < 0.01), suggesting the model performed better in regions with less idiosyncratic neurodevelopment. A moderate correlation was observed between regional prediction difficulty and SNR (r = 0.44, p < 0.01), indicating that regions with lower SNR tend to be harder to predict due to reduced measurement reliability and increased noise interference (F1.D).

SHAP-based feature attribution revealed that each target region’s baseline cortical thickness was consistently the strongest contributor to corresponding prediction (0.54 ± 0.06), highlighting that baseline measurement in a given region serves as a strong predictor of its thickness at follow-up. Since collinearity filtering with r > 0.9 threshold resulted in no feature removal across models, SHAP results were summarized across all regions. Several regions of the visual cortex, the genetic components, as well as age, duration between scans and sex consistently appeared among the top contributors (F2.A). This suggests that, on one hand signatures of neurodevelopmental trajectories may be encoded in early-maturing visual cortex and on the other hand that biological characteristics are, as expected, prominent modifiers of neurodevelopment.

Finally, we evaluated whether the EN model generated individual-specific predictions, such that each individual’s predicted cortical thickness pattern was more similar to their own observed pattern than to those of other participants. To assess this, we calculated within-subject and between-subject correlations for each participant’s cortical thickness estimates (F2.B). The results revealed statistically significant higher within-subject correlations (0.96 ± 0.02) than between-subject correlations (0.85 ± 0.01), indicating that the predicted cortical thickness patterns closely mirrored each individual’s observed follow-up map (t(2362) = 152.35, p < 0.01). The rank-based analysis showed that for 96.7% of individuals the correlation between model predictions and their own observed follow-up cortical thickness was higher than the correlation with any other individual’s observed values. This pronounced separation between within- and between-subject similarity provides compelling evidence that the model captures individuals’ neurodevelopmental trajectories, rather than merely reflecting population-level trends.

Conclusion

This study illustrates the potential to predict cortical thickness maturation across early adolescence from only a single timepoint of data. High accuracy predictions substantiate the existence of systematic, learnable dependencies, rather than random variation, in adolescent neurodevelopment.

We find a substantial regional heterogeneity in predictability. Areas with high inter-individual variability of longitudinal change, such as anterior temporal, insula and prefrontal areas, were the most challenging to forecast. A possible explanation is that differences in cognitive-emotional learning during early adolescence leads to diversification of fronto-temporal cortical maturation across the population. Incorporating cognitive and psychological measures, along with more detailed environmental phenotyping, could help account for these idiosyncratic patterns. Lower predictive accuracy in primary sensorimotor regions, on the other hand, may be a by-product of an early adolescent inflection point in cortical maturation. At the group level, these areas have been shown to stop thinning and may even increase in cortical thickness during this age range. Individual differences in the timing of this transition could drive the heightened variability in longitudinal change that we observe, making predictions more difficult. Future research would benefit from investigating the nature and underlying causes of these inflection points.

Overall, superior performance to the null model and the individual specificity of predictions suggests that our model can enrich baseline assessments by extracting latent signatures of neurodevelopment. By framing neurodevelopmental variability as a high-dimensional space with various directions of change, our model overcomes the common biases of reference-based approaches, such as normative modeling and brain-age prediction, as reflected by the lack of correlation between prediction accuracy and demographic factors. Together, these finding highlight the potential of our predictive model to gauge neurodevelopmental status from cross-sectional data, which represents a key step towards neurodevelopmentally-informed biomarkers for psychiatry.
