Introduction

The development of biologically resolved wiring diagrams is crucial to understanding brain function and dysfunction. Neurotransmitters, together with neurotransmitter receptors, modulate neuronal excitability and mediate the propagation of electrical activity along white matter tracts, ultimately shaping patterns of brain-wide communication. Furthermore, while the brain's structural network can mediate disease propagation, the vulnerability of brain regions is also influenced by their physiochemical makeup. However, structural networks abstract away the rich biological detail of individual brain regions. Here, we develop a framework for annotating structural network edges using chemoarchitectural features of glutamatergic neurotransmission to build a signaling-informed connectome. We contextualize the resulting neurotransmission connectomes against the brain’s functional hierarchy and test their ability to improve the mapping between interregional structural and functional connectivity. We then evaluate the ability of signaling potential to predict patterns of cortical atrophy across a range of neurological and psychiatric disorders. Finally, we explore the computational properties of this low-rank glutamatergic modulation of the connectome by instantiating neurotransmission connectomes as recurrent neural networks in a reservoir computing setting.

Methods

To build connectomes that account for the cortical patterning of neurotransmitter synthesis and neurotransmitter receptors, we use gene expression data from the Allen Human Brain Atlas for the rate-limiting enzyme associated with glutamate synthesis (GLS), as well as a receptor density atlas acquired from PET tracer images1 for 2 glutamate receptors (mGluR5 and NMDA). We normalize both the microarray values and the receptor density maps using a robust sigmoid function. We then weight each edge of a consensus structural brain network by the product of gene expression and receptor density in the respective sender and target regions to obtain the signaling potential along that edge (Fig. 1). The resulting neurotransmitter signaling networks provide a directed transmitter-receptor-resolved perspective on inter-regional synaptic signaling. 

To relate structure to function in neurotransmission connectomes, we use group-representative structural and functional brain networks reconstructed from diffusion-weighted and resting-state functional MRI data, respectively (n = 326; Human Connectome Project). Nodes are defined according to the 400 cortical regions of the Schaefer parcellation. First, we evaluate the relationship between glutamatergic directionality and hierarchical directionality using the principal gradient of functional connectivity.2 We also compute the directed participation coefficients of the neurotransmission connectomes to quantify regional connectivity diversity across the 7 Yeo networks. We contextualize the resulting participation maps by correlating them with meta-analytic probabilistic functional activation maps from the Neurosynth platform across 123 cognitive terms grouped into 11 domains. 

Next, to bridge sparse structural and neurotransmission networks with fully connected functional networks, we apply the mean first-passage time measure of diffusive communication. Structure-function relationships are evaluated by regressing functional connectivity against communication measures obtained from the structural connectome alone. Model performance is then compared to that of a multiple regression model in which the communication measures of the neurotransmission connectomes are added to the model. The analysis is performed at both the global (all connections) and local regional level. Significance is assessed using a spatial autocorrelation-preserving null model applied to the gene expression and receptor density maps prior to reconstruction of the neurotransmission connectomes. This null model is highly conservative as the underlying structural topology is maintained intact. 

To measure the accessibility of interregional topological shortest paths, we use search information. To allow comparisons between structural and neurotransmission connectomes while ruling out differences due to weight and strength distributions, we normalize interregional search information against null distributions obtained from strength-preserving randomized networks. 

We also assess the role of system-specific neurotransmission in disease spreading using node-neighbour atrophy analysis3 in a dataset of abnormal cortical thickness patterns from 10 different neurological, psychiatric, and neurodevelopmental diseases and disorders from the Enhancing Neuroimaging Genetics through Meta-Analysis (ENIGMA) consortium.4

Finally, following a modular connectome-based reservoir computing framework5, we measure inter-modular memory capacity and compare it between the structural and neurotransmission connectomes. Briefly, we instantiate connectomes as recurrent neural networks and measure their capacity to maintain an input signal in their internal representations. Performance is evaluated as the coefficient of determination obtained from a Ridge regression model trained on reservoir states to predict the past input signal.

Results

To relate signaling directionality to hierarchical directionality, we start by clustering edges by their Yeo network affiliation. For each network pair, we measure the difference in mean signaling in each direction. We find significant signaling asymmetry for almost all network pairs (Fig. 2a; pspin < 0.05, FDR-corrected). Of note, outgoing signaling from visual cortex to other functional networks is systematically stronger than incoming signaling. Next, for each structural edge, we compute the forward asymmetry along the principal gradient of functional connectivity. We find that unimodal-transmodal signaling is significantly stronger than transmodal-unimodal signaling for the MGluR5 (Fig. 2a, p < 10-32, two-tailed Wilcoxon signed-rank test, pspin < 0.001) but not the NMDA connectome. To further characterize directed mGluR5 signaling, we compute the participation coefficient across Yeo networks of each region for incoming and outgoing connections separately. We then quantify the change in participation coefficient ranking of each region in contrast to the one obtained from structural connectivity weights. We find significant positive associations between increased functional diversity of a region’s incoming connections and the meta-analytic activation maps corresponding to “reasoning and decision making” processes (Fig. 2a, p < 0.05, two-sided permutation test, FDR-corrected). Conversely, increased diversity of outgoing connections is significantly associated with “perception”. This suggests that mGluR5 signaling directionality shapes the topography of cognitive segregation and integration.

Next, we evaluate the ability of neurotransmission-informed connectomes to improve the mapping between structural and functional connectivity. We find that adding diffusive communication measures derived from neurotransmission connectomes in addition to those derived from structural connectomes significantly improves functional connectivity prediction (Fig. 2b, left, pspin = 0.03), including in individual regions (Fig. 2b, right, pspin < 0.05). Performance improvement is significantly positively correlated with activation maps associated with “reasoning and decision making” and “learning and memory” (p < 0.05, two-sided permutation tests, FDR-corrected). Additionally, neurotransmission connectomes require significantly less search information than the structural connectome, which indicates that their optimal communication pathways are more accessible (Fig. 2c, p < 10-22, two-tailed Wilcoxon signed-rank test). More specifically, communication pathways where accessibility is most improved originate or terminate in visual or limbic regions. 

To test the potential of neurotransmission connectomes to explain connectome-mediated propagation of cortical atrophy, we perform a node-neighbour atrophy analysis across 10 neurological and psychiatric disorders. Across numerous diseases, we find that cortical thickness abnormalities correlate strongly with abnormalities in neighbouring regions weighted by outgoing glutamatergic signaling potential. Notably, significant relationships are observed for all forms of epilepsy using the neurotransmission connectomes, but not the structural connectome (Fig. 2d, pspin < 0.05, FDR-corrected). 

Finally, to explore potential differences in the computational properties of structural and neurotransmission connectomes, we instantiate them as recurrent neural networks to perform an inter-modular memory capacity task. Across all networks, we find that memory capacity is optimal in a stable dynamical regime close to the edge of chaos. In this regime, we find that both neurotransmission connectomes show improved inter-modular memory capacity in contrast to the structural connectome and spatial autocorrelation-preserving null models (Fig. 2e, p < 0.0001, common-language effect size (CLES) ≥ 66.62% for all two-tailed, Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney two-sample rank-sum tests).

Conclusion

In the present report, we develop a method for annotating macroscale connectomes with chemoarchitectural features of glutamatergic neurotransmission. We find that neurotransmission directionality recapitulates the brain’s functional hierarchy with stronger feedforward than feedback signaling. These results echo previous empirical evidence from tract-tracing studies in macaque visual cortex, as well as findings from network control theory indicating that forward transitions along the hierarchy are more energetically efficient.  

In addition to the alignment of signaling potential with the brain’s functional hierarchy, we find that signaling annotations can improve the mapping between brain structure and function. We use diffusive communication measures because of their greater biological plausibility. Nevertheless, we find that signaling annotations carve more efficient communication pathways, leading to a better approximation of optimal routing by diffusive processes. 

Additionally, we find that glutamatergic signaling predicts cortical vulnerability in epilepsy, in line with the excitatory role of glutamate in seizure propagation. Instantiating these networks as recurrent neural networks further reveals computational advantages, with signaling annotations improving inter-modular memory capacity under physiologically relevant dynamical regimes.

More broadly, our framework bridges molecular, systems, and computational levels of analysis by embedding neurotransmitter-specific annotations into macroscale structural brain networks. While we focus on cortical glutamatergic signaling, this approach could be extended to other neurotransmitter systems at the whole-brain level, providing a general pathway toward biologically grounded connectomes that better capture how brain structure gives rise to function, computation, and vulnerability to disease.
