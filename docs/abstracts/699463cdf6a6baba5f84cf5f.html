Introduction

Brain aging is a critical determinant of individual health trajectories, where deviations from normative neural patterns serve as potent biological predictors for neurodegeneration and cognitive decline. The "Brain Age Gap" (BAG)—the deviation between neuroimaging-derived biological age and chronological age—has emerged as a leading biomarker for characterizing this variability1. However, the prevailing prediction-based paradigm suffers from a methodological flaw we term the "Perfection Paradox" (Fig. 1A). Because standard BAG is calculated as a prediction residual, improvements in model accuracy inevitably drive this residual toward zero.

To resolve this, we advocate a paradigm shift: redefining BAG not as a point-wise residual, but as the distributional divergence between an individual’s inferred latent brain age posteriors, i.e., inference-based BAG (Fig. 1B). We further introduce BriDGE (Brain-age inference-based Distributional Gap Estimation), a probabilistic framework that treats brain age as a latent quantity to be inferred rather than a fixed target to be predicted. 

Through variational inference, BriDGE derives the individualized posterior distribution of brain age in a neurobiology-informed manner. This approach resolves the "Perfection Paradox" by inferring brain age posteriors directly, enabling an uncertainty-aware measurement of BAG via distributional divergence. Crucially, conventional prediction models can be seamlessly incorporated into the BriDGE framework via additional generative training, allowing the use of legacy models to the maximal extent.

We validated this novel estimation method using multiple neuroimaging cohorts enriched with diverse biological and cognitive information. Our analysis reveals that inference-based BAG yields superior biological interpretability compared to conventional BAG, demonstrating stronger correlations with both cognitive function and multi-omics biomarkers of aging.

Methods

Datasets and Preprocessing

We aggregated high-resolution multimodal neuroimaging data from the Human Connectome Project, merging the Young Adult (HCP-YA) and Aging (HCP-A) cohorts to span the adult lifespan (22–90+ years). For external validation, we independently curated two Japanese cohorts characterized by deep biological and cognitive phenotyping (Fig. 1C). All MRI data underwent a uniform, standardized preprocessing pipeline to ensure cross-cohort spatial consistency. We parcellated the preprocessed images using the multimodal HCP-MMP1.0 atlas (360 regions), extracting three distinct surface-based morphological features per region: cortical thickness, surface area, and T1w/T2w myelin maps.

The BriDGE Framework and Inference-Based BAG 

We introduce BriDGE, a probabilistic framework that reformulates Brain Age Gap (BAG) via variational inference. Departing from conventional discriminative paradigms that treat brain age as a fixed regression target, BriDGE models aging as a latent variable driving the generative process of observed neuroimaging features.

BriDGE infers the posterior distribution of brain age through two complementary channels:

  1. Neurobiology-Informed Posterior: The subject’s brain age distribution inferred exclusively from neuroimaging features, unconstrained by chronological time.
  2. Chronology-Informed Posterior: The brain age distribution conditioned solely on chronological age.

By characterizing brain age as full probability distributions rather than point estimates, we replace the conventional fixed-gap metric (predicted minus chronological age) with a distributional divergence. Specifically, the magnitude of the inference-based BAG is quantified as the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the neurobiology-informed and the chronology-informed posteriors.

Benchmark Models to Derive Prediction-Based BAG

To rigorously benchmark BriDGE against the prevailing prediction-based paradigm, we implemented four established supervised learning algorithms: (1) Lasso Regression, (2) Gaussian Process Regression (GPR), (3) Random Forest (RF), and (4) Deep Neural Networks (DNN). All models underwent hyperparameter optimization via nested cross-validation to ensure unbiased performance estimation.

Multi-Level Comparative Analysis

We performed a comprehensive validation structured across three levels to evaluate the BAG-level superiority, cognitive interpretability, and biological relevance of inference-based BAG against the conventional prediction-based BAG estimates across three morphological features (Fig. 1C):

  1. Model-Level (Bias and Reliability): We assessed the intrinsic properties of the estimators, specifically focusing on the estimated BAG statistics. We quantified the dependency of BAG estimates on chronological age (ADC value) and mean BAG to evaluate the age bias in BAG estimates. 
  2. Cognitive-Level (Behavioral Relevance): We correlated two BAG estimates with a broad battery of cognitive and behavioral scores (e.g., fluid intelligence, processing speed, episodic memory) derived from our cognitive test batteries. We hypothesized that the inference-based BAG, would show stronger associations with cognitive performance than prediction-based BAG estimates 2.
  3. Biological-Level (Biological Relevance): To ground our explanation in biology, we examined associations between BAG and various biological markers in available cohorts. This included (i) DNA Methylation, assessing the correlation between neuroimaging-derived BAG and epigenetic clocks (e.g., Horvath’s mAge and GrimAge) 3, (ii) Proteomic measures, evaluating the possible correlation between BAG estimates and aging-related protein measures (e.g., ADM, B2M and GDF15 factors), and (iii) Brain venous flow speed, a hemodynamic marker of vascular health. 

Results

BAG-Level Assessment

We first evaluated the properties of inference-based BAG estimates (derived from our BriDGE framework) against conventional BAG estimates (derived from one of age predictors, e.g., Lasso). As illustrated in Fig. 2A (left panel), prediction-based methods systematically failed to yield close-to-0 mean BAG irrespective of the approaches. Moreover, the conventional prediction-based BAG estimates exhibited a strong negative ADC value, suggesting prediction-based BAG estimates were largely biased by the provided chronological ages. 

In stark contrast, the BriDGE framework successfully rectified this age bias. The inference-based BAG (Fig. 2A, right panel) displayed an orthogonal relationship with chronological age, effectively adhering to the desired target of zero correlation with age while preserving the variance attributable to biological heterogeneity. Quantitative metrics confirmed this rectification: BriDGE achieved the lowest ADC values across all morphological features (Surface Area: ADC=-0.13; Cortical Thickness: ADC=-0.21; Myelination: ADC=-0.28) compared to the substantial biases observed in prediction-based models (e.g., Surface Area ADC=-0.82). Crucially, this independence was achieved without commonly conducted post-hoc bias correction, demonstrating the potential of prediction-based BAG in combating with age related bias.

Cognitive-Level Assessment

To validate the functional relevance of these metrics, we assessed subject-wise correlations with the Go/No-Go task, a robust probe of inhibitory control and executive integrity. We hypothesized that if BriDGE-derived inference-based BAG captures true biological degradation, it should track functional decline more accurately than prediction-based BAG estimates (Fig. 2B).

Results confirmed that BriDGE-derived inference-based BAG estimate exhibited significantly stronger associations with cognitive performance compared to its prediction-based alternative. As shown in Fig. 2B, BriDGE (highlighted in yellow) consistently yielded higher correlation magnitudes across all three morphological. Specifically, higher inference-based BAG was strongly associated with lower correct response rates (GNcorrect) and increased commission errors (GNMiss), particularly when derived from cortical thickness and T1w/T2w myelination maps (p < 0.001). Conventional prediction-based BAG estimates showed attenuated or inconsistent correlations, often hovering near zero. 

Biological-Level Assessment

The most profound validation emerged from the alignment of macroscopic neuroimaging with microscopic molecular aging. We benchmarked BAG estimates against a comprehensive battery of epigenetic clocks, proteomic markers, and hemodynamic indicators (Fig. 2C).

Remarkably, BriDGE-derived inference-based BAG demonstrated superior convergence with DNA methylation age, including GrimAge: a premier predictor of mortality risk. While conventional models yielded weak correlations (Fig. 2C, dark circles), BriDGE revealed robust positive associations (bright circles), effectively bridging the "body-brain" gap. This convergence extended to proteomic markers of inflammation and cellular growth (DNAmADM, DNAmB2M, DNAmGDF15), suggesting that BriDGE captures a holistic signature of systemic aging.

Furthermore, in the vascular domain, BriDGE showed the strongest correlation with venous flow speed (Phase_diff in Fig. 2C), a hemodynamic marker of cerebral health. This unique sensitivity suggests that inference-based BAG captures the subtle neurovascular underpinnings of senescence.

Conclusion

Our proposed inference-based BAG marks a departure from the conventional prediction-based BAG estimation. We demonstrate that the "perfection paradox" is not merely a statistical nuisance but a flaw in supervised paradigms: as age prediction performance approaches perfection, the prediction-based BAG is mathematically forced to zero. By reframing the inference-based BAG as a distributional divergence, we also propose a computational framework: BriDGE for correcting inferring the brain age distributions through provided neuroimaging-derived features and chronological ages.  

The biological implications of this BAG paradigm shift are substantial. While prediction-based BAG estimates often failed to correlate with non-imaging markers, BriDGE-derived inference-based BAG estimates revealed robust convergence with various aging biomarkers. The strong alignment with DNA methylation clocks and various proteomic markers suggests that the estimated inference-based BAGs may have deep connections with other biomarkers. Furthermore, the novel association with venous flow speed highlights inference-based BAG’s sensitivity to the neurovascular underpinnings of senescence, i.e., a physiological dimension largely invisible to conventional prediction-based BAG estimation.

Despite these advances, we acknowledge limitations in current inference-based BAG and BriDGE framework. First, our generative framework BriDGE, while statistically robust, incurs a higher computational cost compared to conventional models (e.g., Lasso), requiring careful hyperparameter tuning for variational inference. Second, the current validation primarily relies on cross-sectional cohorts. While BriDGE effectively infers aging deviations from inter-subject variability, it fundamentally approximates temporal dynamics from static snapshots. 

For this, future works will extend the application of BriDGE-derived inference-based BAG estimates to longitudinal datasets (e.g., UK Biobank and ADNI). By modeling the inference-based BAG rate over time, rather than a static BAG measure, we aim to disentangle "state" (current biological age) from "rate" (pace of aging). We anticipate that applying BriDGE-derived inference-based BAG to longitudinal data will allow us to map brain aging related acceleration patterns preceding cognitive decline.
