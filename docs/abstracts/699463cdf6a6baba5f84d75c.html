Introduction

Avoidance of negative outcomes is a fundamental component of adaptive behavior and relies on the evaluation of aversive outcomes, the experience of relief or safety when negative outcomes are prevented, and the motivational significance of outcome feedback. In affective disorders such as obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD), anxiety, and mood disorders, these processes are often disrupted, manifesting as heightened sensitivity to potential losses, exaggerated threat responding, and maladaptive avoidance behaviors that persist even when negative outcomes are avoided.Neuroimaging studies have implicated a distributed set of regions, including the ventral striatum (VS), anterior insula, amygdala, subthalamic nucleus (STN), bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNST), dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, orbitofrontal cortex, and ventromedial prefrontal cortex in the evaluation of outcome valence, motivational salience, and affective significance of feedback.1 However, these studies have focused on neural responses to receiving rewards or experiencing losses, often using gain–loss contrasts. While informative, this approach mainly captures responses to rewarding or aversive outcomes and overlooks feedback indicating that a loss was avoided. Such loss-avoidance feedback is linked to relief or safety and may be processed atypically in affective disorders, especially during adolescence and young adulthood, contributing to persistent threat sensitivity and maladaptive avoidance. The present study uses task-based fMRI during a probabilistic reinforcement-learning paradigm to examine functional connectivity during feedback indicating successful loss avoidance. We focus on the VS, STN, and BNST as key nodes within affective and reward networks and assess their connectivity with regions involved in affect, reward, and cognition. We further test whether loss-avoidance–related connectivity varies with individual differences in anxiety, depression, and anhedonia.

Methods

Participants

Data were drawn from 116 participants (age 16–25) from the SMARTSCAN study,2 recruited from the general population and mental health services. Participants were divided into a high-symptom (HS) group, with (sub)clinical depressive symptoms (MADRS ≥ 10) but no current psychiatric treatment or significant care needs, and a healthy control (HC) group, with low depressive symptoms (MADRS < 10), no current or lifetime psychiatric diagnosis, and no history of psychological or psychiatric treatment.

Study design
SMARTSCAN is a randomized controlled trial conducted at Maastricht University Medical Centre (Dutch Trial Register: NTR3808) examining psychological treatment effects on subclinical psychiatric symptoms in youth. Assessments included clinical interviews, MRI, and behavioral measures at baseline, post-treatment, and follow-up. The current study focused on baseline task-based fMRI data from the probabilistic reward learning (RL) task and symptom measures of depression (Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale; MADRS), anxiety (State–Trait Anxiety Inventory; STAI), and anhedonia (Snaith–Hamilton Pleasure Scale; SHAPS). Additional procedural details are reported elsewhere.2

Reward-learning task
The RL task was adapted from Gold et al.3 and comprised three stimulus pairs (gain, loss, neutral), each presented for 80 trials. Participants learned which stimulus within each pair yielded a more favorable outcome based on probabilistic feedback (70/30 contingency). Gain trials provided a €0.20 reward or no reward, loss trials involved avoiding or incurring a €0.20 loss, and neutral trials presented “Correct/Incorrect” feedback. Stimuli were presented side-by-side with counterbalanced positions. Across four pseudo-randomized blocks of 60 trials, participants completed 240 trials. 

MRI acquisition and preprocessing
MRI data were acquired on a 3T Siemens Magnetom Prisma scanner (Scannexus Maastricht). High-resolution T1-weighted images were collected with an MPRAGE sequence (1-mm isotropic; TR = 2250 ms; TE = 2.21 ms; flip angle = 9°; FOV = 256 × 256 × 192 mm; GRAPPA = 2). Functional images were acquired with a T2*-weighted EPI sequence (3-mm isotropic; TR = 2450 ms; TE = 28 ms; flip angle = 75°; FOV = 216 mm; GRAPPA = 3). Preprocessing in SPM12 included slice-timing correction, realignment, co-registration to structural images, normalization to 2-mm MNI space, and 4-mm FWHM spatial smoothing.

Data analysis
Analyses focused on task-evoked fMRI responses for the gain and loss conditions. A generalized psychophysiological interaction (gPPI) analysis with hemodynamic deconvolution was performed using the TMFC Toolbox (version 1.8.3).4 In total, 48 ROIs were analyzed, with the bilateral nucleus accumbens (NAcc), STN, and BNST designated as regions of special interest within the broader network. For each participant, representative ROI time series (first eigenvariate) were extracted following nuisance regression and temporal filtering. Neural signals were estimated using SPM’s parametric empirical Bayes deconvolution. Condition-specific interaction terms were computed by multiplying deconvolved neural activity with the psychological task regressors and reconvolving the product with the canonical hemodynamic response function. Subject-level GLMs included physiological, psychological, and interaction regressors for contrasts of interest (no-loss > loss, no-loss > gain, and no-loss).

At the group level, connectivity estimates were entered into second-level models that included clinical covariates (MADRS, STAI-State, STAI-Trait, and SHAPS scores) and group (HC, HS). These covariates were included to assess how individual differences in symptom severity and group status modulated task-related functional connectivity. Group-level statistical inference was performed using one-sample or between-group t-tests, with false-discovery-rate correction (p < 0.05).

Results

Loss avoidance versus loss receipt (no-loss > loss)

For task-related connectivity effects alone, 12 uncorrected connections (α = .01) were observed, primarily reflecting increased coupling between a left superior frontal gyrus (SFG) hub and multiple right inferior frontal gyrus (IFG) and middle frontal gyrus (MFG) nodes. However, none of these connections involved the predefined regions of special interest (NAcc, STN, or BNST). At the FDR-corrected threshold (α = .05), no connections survived. Similarly, when modelling individual differences in anxiety, depression, or anhedonia, no significant connectivity effects emerged at the corrected threshold. 

Avoidance versus reward (no-loss > gain)

When examining task effects alone, two uncorrected connections (α = .01) were identified, linking the right IFG with the left SFG. Again, this did not include any of the seed-regions. No connections survived FDR correction. In the symptom-modulated models, a significant association was detected, showing that higher state anxiety (STAI-State) was related to increased connectivity between the left BNST and the right globus pallidus (FDR-corrected α = .05). No additional symptom-related effects were found for depression or anhedonia. 

Main effects of loss avoidance (no-loss)

No connections survived FDR correction or the α = .01 uncorrected threshold. 

Conclusion

This study examined task-based functional connectivity during loss avoidance in young individuals with affective symptoms. 

Across all gPPI analyses, task-induced connectivity effects were modest and showed limited involvement of the regions of special interest (NAcc, STN, BNST). Although several uncorrected fronto-frontal connections emerged during no-loss > loss, none of these involved the highlighted subcortical regions, and all failed to survive FDR correction. This suggests that, within this sample of young individuals with (sub)clinical affective symptoms, loss-avoidance processing may modulate broader prefrontal functional interactions without strongly engaging the targeted reward and relief-related subcortical hubs at the connectivity level.

The no-loss > gain comparison showed similarly weak task-related effects, with only two uncorrected prefrontal connections detected. Notably, the only robust finding across all analyses was a symptom-dependent effect, that is higher state anxiety was associated with increased connectivity between the left BNST and right globus pallidus. This transient anxiety-related coupling may indicate that individuals experiencing greater anxiety at the time of scanning exhibit heightened sensitivity or vigilance toward avoidance (safety) signals compared to rewarding outcomes. The BNST is known for its role in sustained anticipatory anxiety,5 while the globus pallidus contributes to motivational and motor processes, suggesting this connectivity might reflect an acute, state-dependent modulation of avoidance-related neural circuits. However, as state anxiety captures temporary affective states rather than stable traits, this finding may represent a dynamic neural response tied to participants’ current emotional context rather than a persistent neurobiological characteristic.

Lastly, the absence of significant main effects for no-loss feedback further indicates that non-rewarded safety outcomes did not consistently alter connectivity across the sampled networks. Taken together, these results suggest that, in contrast to hypotheses emphasizing strong subcortical involvement, loss avoidance and reward-related decision processes in subclinical youth are primarily reflected in distributed prefrontal coupling rather than robustly in NAcc-, STN-, or BNST-centered circuits. Although limited in magnitude, the anxiety-related BNST–globus pallidus effect highlights the potential relevance of basal ganglia–extended amygdala pathways for individual differences in negative affect. Future analyses with larger samples, complementary task designs, or model-based fMRI approaches may help clarify whether these circuits reveal stronger modulation under conditions of greater uncertainty, motivational salience, or clinical severity.
