Introduction

Perceptual systems change in response to the statistical structure of sensory environments. In audition, listeners implicitly learn the probability distributions of sounds, and this learning can alter perceptual sensitivity even when those statistics are task-irrelevant (Agus et al., 2010). Adapting a venerable tone-in-noise detection paradigm where tone frequency is task-irrelevant, Luthra et al. (2024) showed that detection improved slightly for high-probability frequencies, but declined considerably for low-probability frequencies, with detection of frequencies close-by to the high-probability one rescued to some degree.  

These results show that short-term sensory statistics profoundly shape auditory perception, and suggest an interplay of perceptual enhancement and, particularly, suppression. However, the neural dynamics and underlying mechanisms of these probability-dependent effects remain mysterious, as does the relative contribution and interaction between auditory- and auditory-associated areas. Here we investigate probability-dependent effects in tonotopically organized auditory cortex using high-resolution fMRI.

Methods

Multi-session data have been collected from two participants at a 3T Siemens Prisma scanner, with additional participants currently being recruited. Functional images are acquired using a CMRR multiband EPI sequence (2.0 mm isotropic resolution, TR = 1 s, TE = 35 ms, multiband factor = 4). Auditory stimuli are delivered via high-fidelity MR-compatible earphones and a carefully calibrated audio system to ensure reliable presentation of near-threshold sounds over scanner noise. White noise is filtered to a flat spectrum and presented at ~80 dB SPL to mask scanner noise.

Participants perform an auditory detection task where low- and high-frequency pure tones are embedded in noise near individual detection thresholds (~79% correct). Tone frequencies (534 Hz and 2489 Hz) awere selected to avoid overlap with prominent scanner noise frequencies, and to stimulate distinct different tonotopic subregions. Tones are brief (250 ms), and participants are instructed to detect tones without mention of frequency.

Within each run, tone probabilities begin with one frequency high probability and the other low (e.g., 80% 2489 Hz, 20% 534 Hz/20);  these probabilities are reversed within a run. Each run lasts ~20 minutes and includes 60 trials; two runs are completed per session, with data collected across three sessions per participant. Interstimulus intervals vary from 7–11 s (mean 9 s), allowing for event-related response estimation. In each trial, participants hold a response button and release it either upon tone detection, or when a visual stop signal (red circle) appears.

Tonotopic regions of interest in the early auditory cortex are defined independently using above-threshold pure- tone tonotopic sweeps. Event-related fMRI responses are estimated separately for low- and high-frequency tones under high- and low-probability conditions. 

Results

Behaviorally, we replicated prior findings in the scanner environment (that includeding EPI acoustic noise): detection accuracy for near-threshold tones decreased as stimulus probability decreased following probability reversals within runs, confirming the paradigm’s sensitivity to subtle probability manipulations.

Neurally, early tonotopic regions exhibited robust event-related responses to brief (250 ms), near-threshold auditory stimuli. At least in these first two participants, event-related responses were systematically lower for high-probability tones relative to low-probability tones, despite identical acoustic properties. This probability-dependent dampening was observed for both low- and high-frequency tones and was spatially specific to their corresponding tonotopic regions. The effect was consistent across these two participants, runs, and sessions, providing preliminary evidence for a stable neural signature of the speed of learned stimulus probabilities. Data collection is ongoing to quantify effect sizes and further assess reliability in a larger cohort.

Conclusion

Our findings replicate and extend prior demonstrations of probability-dependent modulation in auditory statistical learning by linking behavioral sensitivity changes to neural responses in the early auditory cortex. Even brief, near-threshold pure tone stimuli elicit robust responses in tonotopically organized regions, which are systematically dampened for highly predictable events. Upcoming data collection will further clarify potential regional differences in the relationship with learning rate, and the potential modulatory role of auditory-related regions.
