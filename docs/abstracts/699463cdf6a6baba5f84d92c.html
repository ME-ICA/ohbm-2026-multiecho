Introduction

Hyperactivity within the cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical (CSTC) network has been hypothesized to mediate obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD).1 Deep brain stimulation (DBS) has emerged as a treatment for severe, refractory OCD with a number of promising therapeutic targets. However, response rates across targets remain limited to approximately 60%.2 One potential contributor to this variability in outcomes is the lack of engagement of the CSTC circuit during stimulation. 

At UCSF, we are conducting an ongoing study using invasive brain mapping to identify stimulation targets that acutely reduce OCD symptoms, with the goal of developing a personalized DBS strategy. Stereoelectroencephalography (sEEG) electrodes were placed within candidate targets such as the ventral capsule/nucleus accumbens (VC/NAc), ventral capsule/bed nucleus of stria terminalis (VC/BNST), subthalamic nucleus (STN), orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), and dorsal cingulate cortex (DCC). Stimulation mapping was performed to identify sites eliciting a rapid decrease in OCD symptoms while concurrently recording intracranial electrophysiological activity. Structural and directed functional connectivity of acutely therapeutic and non-therapeutic sites was characterized using diffusion tractography and single pulse evoked potential (EP) responses. These mapping results were then used to develop a personalized four-lead DBS implantation strategy. Participants subsequently underwent chronic DBS implantation followed by systematic programming, with longitudinal assessments to monitor improvement in OCD symptoms.

Methods

Participants. Three individuals with severe, refractory OCD (20F, 28M, 64F) gave written informed consent to participate in the SEEG-guided DBS study (NCT06347978).  

Electrode Implantation. Twelve-16 contact sEEG electrodes (PMT Corporation) were implanted bilaterally targeting: VC/NAc, VC/BNST, anterior medial subthalamic nucleus/zona incerta (amSTN/ZI), OFC, ACC, and DCC. Subcortical leads used 2.5mm center-to-center contact spacing and cortical leads used 3.5mm spacing for subject 1, while later subjects were implanted with 3mm center-to-center spaced leads for all targets. Surgical targeting combined anatomic landmarks and diffusion tractography in Brainlab iPlan. Post-operative CT was registered to pre-operative MRI to verify contact locations, confirmed by neuroradiologist review. Recording and stimulation procedures occurred over 10 days before electrode explantation. 

Diffusion Tractography. Pre-surgical 3T MRI included T1-weighted BRAVO (1mm isotropic) anatomical and HARDI diffusion imaging (b=2000s/mm², 83 directions). Diffusion data underwent noise reduction, distortion correction (TOPUP/Eddy), and multi-tissue constrained spherical deconvolution (MRtrix3, Dhollander algorithm). Stimulation contact coordinates were estimated from post-operative T1 MRI and CT co-registration (LeGUI, Rolston lab) and were transformed to diffusion space via rigid registration. Spherical 2mm-radius ROIs were created at each electrode coordinate using the MarsBar toolbox in SPM. Probabilistic tractography (iFOD2, 50,000 streamlines) was filtered to only those streamlines passing through combined ROIs composed of the ROIs for each electrode in a given therapeutic configuration. Structural connectivity to therapeutic contacts was estimated from the filtered streamline sets to support our electrophysiological findings. 

EP Mapping. Single-pulse electrical stimulation (bipolar, 3mA, 1Hz, 20 trials per site) was delivered to adjacent contact pairs across all regions. sEEG was recorded at 10kHz using a 256-channel Nihon Kohden system. EP responses were quantified by z-scoring voltage against 50ms pre-stimulus baseline, then measuring root-mean-square power of trial-averaged waveforms in the N1 window (10-50ms post-stimulus). EP magnitudes exceeding threshold (z>1) defined functional connectivity between stimulation sites and recording contacts. 

High-Frequency Activity (HFA) and Aperiodic Component Analyses. HFA and aperiodic spectral features were analyzed from continuous sEEG recorded during stimulation blocks (20-minute duration, 100 Hz, bipolar, 100 µs pulse width, 1.0 to 6.0 mA). Data were downsampled to 512 Hz, notch filtered at 60 Hz and harmonics, and bipolar re-referenced. HFA (30 to 90 Hz) was extracted via bandpass filtering, with power spectra computed at 5 second resolution. HFA power was z-scored to a 30 second pre stimulation baseline and averaged across the frequency band. Change in HFA was quantified by comparing average power 30 seconds pre stimulation to 5 minutes post stimulation onset in ipsilateral OFC contacts. Power spectra from the same contacts were decomposed into periodic and aperiodic components using the Fitting Oscillations and One Over F (FOOOF) algorithm. Spectra from 1 to 90 Hz were fit using a fixed aperiodic mode with peak width limits of 0.5 to 30 Hz and a minimum peak height of 0.01. Aperiodic change was quantified comparing average aperiodic exponent 30 seconds pre stimulation to 5 minutes post stimulation onset. Statistical significance was assessed via permutation testing (n = 10,000), comparing therapeutic and sham recordings. 

Clinical Assessment. OCD symptoms were assessed via visual analog scales (VAS, 0-100) collected continuously through REDCap measuring obsessions, compulsions, OCD-related distress, anxiety, depression, and energy. 

Results

Across all three participants, we consistently identified three ventral capsule (VC) sites near the globus pallidus that acutely relieved OCD symptoms and were not associated with significant adverse effects. Stimulation at acutely therapeutic VC sites significantly suppressed broadband HFA (30–90 Hz) within ipsilateral OFC compared to sham (7 of 9 sites; p<0.05, permutation test). Stimulation at acutely therapeutic VC sites also elicited a significant increase in the aperiodic exponent in ipsilateral OFC compared to sham (7 of 9 sites; p<0.01, permutation test), indicating a reliable steepening of the aperiodic 1/f slope, often interpreted as focal slowing during therapeutic stimulation.  

Therapeutic sites additionally elicited short-latency single pulse stimulation N1 (10–50 ms) EPs within ipsilateral OFC. Diffusion imaging revealed that across subjects, acutely therapeutic VC stimulation sites were highly connected to the OFC and other parts of the CSTC circuit.

Conclusion

Here, we describe an invasive brain mapping paradigm involving extensive stimulation testing to identify acutely therapeutic stimulation sites to guide multi-site DBS to treat severe, refractory OCD. Across three subjects, we found that stimulation at VC sites located near the globus pallidus led to acute reductions in OCD symptoms. Therapeutic VC stimulation produced two convergent electrophysiological effects consistent with suppression of ipsilateral OFC activity: suppression of broadband HFA and an increase in the aperiodic exponent. Broadband HFA in intracranial recordings is widely interpreted as an index of local neuronal population firing rates and aggregate synaptic activity,3 such that its suppression during effective stimulation suggests reduced local excitatory drive within OFC circuitry. The observed increase in the aperiodic exponent reflects a steepening of the broadband 1/f slope of the power spectrum and has been linked to decreases in excitation-inhibition balance.4 Single pulse stimulation within therapeutic VC sites additionally elicited short-latency EPs, demonstrating strong functional connectivity with the OFC. Diffusion tractography further confirmed that acutely therapeutic VC sites have strong structural connectivity with the OFC. 

Together, these electrophysiological and imaging findings support a model in which therapeutic VC stimulation blocks communication through white matter tracts, thereby suppressing input to and activity within OFC. In this way, decreases in HFA and focal slowing within the OFC during stimulation may indicate effective suppression of excessive CSTC network activity in OCD, resulting in rapid symptom improvement. These results also provide a preliminary framework for a clinical protocol integrating diffusion tractography, EP mapping, electrophysiological recordings during stimulation, and assessments of acute clinical response to optimize outcomes in patients receiving VC DBS for severe, refractory OCD.
