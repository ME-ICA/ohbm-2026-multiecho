Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic disease of the central nervous system in which focal inflammation and diffuse neurodegeneration jointly contribute to long-term disability. Beyond disease duration and relapse frequency, chronological age has emerged as a major determinant of clinical outcome, with older individuals showing a higher risk of progressive disability and brain tissue loss. As patients age, MS pathology increasingly shifts toward diffuse atrophy of gray and white matter, reflecting mechanisms that extend beyond focal lesion formation.1 

Accumulating evidence indicates that MS is associated with features of accelerated biological aging, even at early disease stages.2  Chronic low-grade inflammation, often described as “inflammaging”, and early cellular senescence are thought to promote neuroaxonal damage, structural brain changes, and reduced treatment responsiveness, underscoring the need for sensitive markers of neurodegeneration beyond conventional lesion metrics.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) enables in vivo assessment of structural brain changes. An emerging alternative to traditional volumetric measures, which ideally require long-term longitudinal data, is the brain-age paradigm, which uses machine learning models trained on healthy individuals to estimate brain-predicted age from a single structural MRI scan. The difference between predicted and chronological age, termed the brain age gap estimation (BrainAGE), has been linked to both baseline disability and future disease progression in MS.3  

Alongside imaging, serum biomarkers such as neurofilament light chain (sNfL) and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) offer information on inflammation-related axonal injury and chronic glial pathology, respectively. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between BrainAGE, clinical disability, and serum biomarkers in MS, to determine whether imaging-based brain age estimates reflect overlapping or distinct aspects of MS pathophysiology.

Methods

Baseline data were included from three prospective, single-center observational studies conducted at the local department of neurology (SATURATE, 360PMS, SAFEGUIDE-MS). Only patients with relapsing multiple sclerosis were included in this analysis. Healthy controls without neurological disease were drawn from a local imaging study and served as a reference group. Neurological disability was assessed using the Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS). Functional performance was evaluated using components of the Multiple Sclerosis Functional Composite (MSFC), including the Timed 25-Foot Walk and the 9-Hole Peg Test for dominant and non-dominant hands.

All participants underwent structural MRI on a 3T Siemens Trio scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) using a 3D T1-weighted MPRAGE sequence (TR=1.9s, TE=3.34ms, TI=900ms, flip angle=9°, FoV=256×256mm, sagittal plane, slices=192, voxel size=1mm³). Brain age was estimated using the best-performing model from More et al. (2023)4, a Gaussian process regression model trained on MRI data from 2,953 healthy individuals (18-88 years). Gray matter features were derived using standardized CAT12 preprocessing, smoothing with a 4 mm full-width-half-maximum kernel, resampling to 4 mm spatial resolution, and finally dimensionality reduction by principal component analysis. BrainAGE was calculated by subtracting the chronological age from the predicted brain age.

Baseline blood samples were collected, processed, and stored according to standardized protocols. sNfL and GFAP concentrations were quantified using electrochemiluminescence immunoassays on a Roche Cobas platform (Roche Diagnostics, Rotkreuz, Switzerland), with laboratory personnel blinded to clinical data. Biomarker values were converted to age-adjusted z-scores using established reference methods.5

Statistical analyses included non-parametric tests for group comparisons and trend analyses across EDSS categories, as well as correlation analyses to assess associations between BrainAGE, clinical measures, and serum biomarkers. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All procedures were approved by the local ethics committee, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Results

The analysis included 82 patients with relapsing multiple sclerosis (10.2 ± 10.5 years since diagnosis) and 30 healthy controls. Patients and controls did not differ significantly in age (patients: 39.24 ± 12.0 years; controls: 40.93 ± 15.21 years; p = 0.78) or sex distribution (68% vs. 77% female; Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.49). 92.7% patients were receiving disease-modifying therapy at the time of assessment.

Mean BrainAGE was significantly higher in patients with MS (6.48 ± 6.83 years) compared with healthy controls (0.69 ± 6.5 years), corresponding to an average difference of 5.8 years (p = 0.0002). BrainAGE increased stepwise across groups, from controls to patients with EDSS < 3 and EDSS ≥ 3 (Jonckheere–Terpstra, one-sided, increasing, p < 0.0001, Figure 1A). Overall group differences were confirmed by Kruskal–Wallis testing (p < 0.0001), with post-hoc Dunn’s tests with Bonferroni correction showing significantly differences in BrainAGE in both patient groups compared with controls (Control vs. EDSS <3: p = 0.015; Control vs. EDSS ≥3: p < 0.001), as well as in patients with EDSS ≥ 3 compared with EDSS < 3 (p = 0.040). BrainAGE showed a weak positive association with years since diagnosis (ρ = 0.22, p = 0.047), but not with years since symptom onset (ρ = 0.10, p = 0.37).

Higher BrainAGE was associated with worse motor performance. Specifically, longer completion times on the 9-Hole Peg Test correlated with higher BrainAGE for both the dominant (ρ = 0.34, p = 0.002) and non-dominant hand (ρ = 0.33, p < 0.001). Similarly, slower Timed 25-Foot Walk performance was associated with higher BrainAGE (ρ = 0.23, p = 0.043). Corresponding z-scores for both tests showed consistent inverse relationships (9-Hole Peg Test: ρ = -0.40, p < 0.001; Timed 25-Foot Walk: ρ = -0.24, p = 0.032, Figure 1B-C).

No significant associations were observed between BrainAGE and sNfL or GFAP, either for age-adjusted z-scores (Figure 1D-E) or absolute biomarker concentrations (all p > 0.3).

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that BrainAGE is closely associated with clinical disability in relapsing multiple sclerosis, while showing no relationship with serum biomarkers of neuroaxonal and glial injury. These findings suggest that imaging-based brain age captures clinically relevant aspects of disease burden that are not reflected by circulating biomarkers, supporting the concept that these measures provide complementary information of MS pathology.

Consistent with prior work, BrainAGE revealed a pronounced effect of MS on apparent brain aging3 and increased stepwise with disability severity. In our cohort, restricted to relapsing MS, higher BrainAGE was associated with worse motor function, as reflected by both upper- and lower-extremity performance measures. In contrast, BrainAGE was not associated with sNfL or GFAP, which, so far, has not been systematically evaluated under standardized conditions. This likely reflects fundamental differences in the biological processes captured by these measures. sNfL is primarily sensitive to acute, inflammation-related axonal injury and is strongly influenced by recent disease activity and treatment effects. The absence of an association in our cohort, which consisted of clinically stable patients largely receiving high-efficacy therapies, suggests that BrainAGE reflects cumulative and diffuse structural changes rather than short-term inflammatory damage. Similarly, the lack of correlation with GFAP may indicate that astroglial pathology and microstructural white matter changes, which GFAP is thought to reflect, especially in patients with progressive MS, are not captured by gray matter–based brain age models. This underscores the value of BrainAGE as a complementary, sensitive cross-sectional MRI-derived marker that integrates distributed structural changes into a single, age-adjusted metric.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. BrainAGE was derived using a single algorithm and scanner, and generalizability across models and imaging platforms remains to be established. No contrast-enhanced MRI was performed, precluding assessment of subclinical inflammatory activity. As noted, most patients were clinically stable and treated with high-efficacy therapies, which likely reduced variability in serum biomarker levels. Future studies should therefore investigate longitudinal BrainAGE changes, include progressive MS phenotypes, and evaluate multimodal models integrating imaging and fluid biomarkers.

In summary, BrainAGE emerges as a robust imaging marker of disease severity in MS that complements, rather than overlaps with, established serum biomarkers. Integrating brain age estimates for longer-term neurodegenerative consequences with fluid biomarkers for monitoring inflammatory activity and treatment response may improve the characterization of silent disease progression and enhance monitoring of MS-related neurodegeneration.
