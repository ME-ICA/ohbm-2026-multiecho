Introduction

Artificial neural networks (ANNs) have been found to reliably predict sensory-evoked brain activity and have emerged as potential models of brain processes. These advances have motivated the concept of brain alignment, in which a network’s internal representations are optimized to match brain dynamics, with the dual goal of improving encoding models of complex, multimodal brain processes and enhancing performance on downstream AI tasks1. While this concept has gained support in the visual domain, we investigate here the feasibility of creating auditory artificial neural models directly aligned with individual brain activity. This challenge is substantial, as models have to be trained directly with brain data, which are limited in scale compared to datasets typically used in AI. Here, we investigate whether aligning an auditory ANN to individual fMRI activity can (1) improve brain encoding for novel, previously unseen stimuli and (2) yield generalisable auditory representations that transfer to a wide range of complex auditory tasks. To address these questions, we leverage large-scale naturalistic fMRI data from the Courtois NeuroMod dataset2 and evaluate task-level generalisation using the HEAR benchmark3.

Methods

fMRI dataset

We used Friends, an fMRI dataset from 2022-alpha release of the Courtois Neuromod Dataset2. In this dataset, 6 subjects (3 men / 3 women, age between 31 and 47) have been recorded while watching the first 4 seasons of the TV show Friends, for a total of 38 hours of  fMRI data per subject. Details regarding the acquisition and the pre-processing of the data with fMRIPrep can be found in our article4. We used a parcellation of the preprocessed data called MIST ROI, divided into 210 regions of interest (ROI), to evaluate the encoding capacity of the models on the whole brain. 

Training the encoding models 

To assess the impact of brain alignment, we used a shared backbone based on a pretrained convolutional network, SoundNet5, processing the audio from Friends, followed by a convolutional encoding layer, to convert the internal representations extracted from Soundnet into a predicted fMRI signal. For each subject, we compared two models using different  training strategies:

  • Baseline models used transfer learning, where only the encoding layer is trained using the output of a pretrained SoundNet. The weights of the network remain untouched.

  • Brain-aligned models are fine-tuned, as part of SoundNet is retrained, as well as the encoding layer, to optimize fMRI prediction; results shown here correspond to fine-tuning up to an intermediate convolutional layer (Conv4; see Fig. 1). 

To evaluate the prediction capability of our models, we compute the r² score between the real parcellation and the prediction. All models were trained on audio from the first three seasons of Friends (21 h training, 7 h validation per subject) and evaluated on held-out data from season 4 (9 h). 

Statistical analysis

We compared the encoding performance of baseline and brain-aligned models against a null model with randomly initialized weights, to verify if both models were able to encode cerebral activity. For each cortical region, significance of r² differences across all episodes of Friends season 4 was assessed using Wilcoxon tests (α = 0.05). To account for multiple comparisons across 210 regions, p-values were corrected using a False Discovery Rate procedure (Benjamini–Yekutieli), and only regions with q < 0.05 were considered significant. The same statistical analysis was applied to compare baseline and brain-aligned models, in order to quantify the effect of fine-tuning SoundNet on brain encoding performance.

Evaluating the models on HEAR

To evaluate the impact of brain alignment on SoundNet transfer performance, we tested all baseline and brain-aligned models on the Holistic Evaluation of Audio Representations (HEAR) benchmark3. HEAR assesses the ability of models audio representations to generalise across diverse auditory tasks, such as speech detection, sound event recognition, and music classification. Following the HEAR evaluation protocol, features from baseline and brain-aligned models were used as fixed inputs to train a downstream multi-layer perceptron (MLP). For each task of the benchmark, we ranked the performance of our baseline and brain-aligned models amongst all models in HEAR leaderboard, and quantified the change in ranking for the SoundNet model before versus after fine-tuning with brain data. We assessed overall gains or losses in ranking using Wilcoxon tests.

Results

Improved brain encoding

Both baseline and brain-aligned models achieved good performance in predicting brain activity (r² up to 0.37). Across most subjects, the highest encoding accuracy was observed in the auditory cortex, particularly the medial and posterior superior temporal gyrus (STGm/STGp). In addition to the auditory cortex, both model types also captured fMRI responses in non-auditory regions, such as the visual network. 

We found that brain-aligned models perform slightly better than their baseline counterparts in the auditory cortex and/or in the visual cortex, depending on the subject. We also noticed that regions with lower baseline performance (r² between 0.05–0.15) tended to exhibit larger relative gains than highly predictive regions (see Fig.2, left panel). Overall, fine-tuning improved brain encoding performance, with substantial inter-subject variability in both the magnitude and spatial distribution of gains.

Improved performance in downstream tasks

Evaluation on the HEAR benchmark revealed that baseline SoundNet performed poorly on many tasks, frequently ranking among the lowest-performing models. Brain-aligned models achieved significantly higher rankings than baseline SoundNet in 12 of the 19 tasks (p < 0.05), while showing reduced performance in only two tasks (DCASE 2016 and VoxLingua107 Top-10; see Fig.2, right panel). Performance gains were not confined to a specific task category, with improvements observed across a broad range of auditory domains. 

Notably, brain alignment produced large rank improvements in tasks with limited training data, including Gunshot Triangulation, Beijing Opera, and NSynth Pitch. For example, in Gunshot Triangulation, brain-aligned models surpassed up to 18 competing models despite having only two minutes of data to train the MLP. These results collectively indicate that brain alignment enhances the generalisation capacity of SoundNet, particularly in low-data regimes.

Conclusion

We investigated the effects of aligning a pretrained auditory neural network (SoundNet) to individual fMRI activity on both brain encoding and downstream task performance. Brain alignment substantially improved the generalisation of encoding to previously unseen stimuli, with gains in the auditory cortex as well as the visual cortex. Importantly, aligned models also showed enhanced performance across a wide range of auditory tasks, as assessed through a standardized benchmark. These benefits were particularly pronounced for tasks with limited dataset available, highlighting the potential of brain alignment in the context of few-shot learning. Further work is required to identify which features of brain activity and task structure most strongly contribute to the benefits of brain alignment. These findings open many avenues for future research, ranging from studying inter-individual variations to testing brain alignment for various model architectures, types of training data and types of downstream tasks.
