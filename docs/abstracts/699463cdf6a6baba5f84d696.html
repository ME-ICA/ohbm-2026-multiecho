Introduction

Schizophrenia is a neuropsychiatric disorder characterized by a wide range of symptoms, classified into three categories: positive (e.g. psychosis), negative (e.g. flattened affect), and cognitive (e.g. memory deficits).1 While the disorder is commonly diagnosed during early adulthood, symptoms remain for life and represent a heavy burden on quality of life.1 Medication can alleviate symptom severity, but it is not yet understood how its action at the neuronal and circuit level shapes macroscale brain dynamics. Since spontaneous dynamics are known to both reflect and shape behavior,2 it is thus essential to understand how microscale circuit disruptions can alter spontaneous cortical dynamics and how these disruptions can be counteracted. On one hand, neuronal and circuit level hypotheses, such as deficits in NMDA receptors, have been advanced to explain the pathology of schizophrenia.1 On the other hand, studies show large-scale patterns of dysconnectivity3 or dynamic abnormalities.4 However, the connection between the two levels of organization remains largely unknown. For this reason, we aimed to investigate spontaneous spatiotemporal dynamics in schizophrenia patients using Leading Eigenvector Decomposition Analysis5 (LEiDA) and to reproduce these dynamics in whole-brain models through disruptions to local circuit dynamics and large-scale structural connectivity.

Methods

We analyzed 5-minute runs of resting-state BOLD fMRI data from 81 healthy control and 63 schizophrenia patients from the publicly available COBRE dataset (https://fcon_1000.projects.nitrc.org/indi/retro/cobre.html). Motion parameters and signal intensity of white matter and cerebrospinal fluid were regressed out from the time series, which were then bandpass filtered between 0.01 and 0.1 Hz and motion scrubbed. Data was then parcellated into 114 cortical regions using the Lausanne100 atlas. First, for each subject, we computed static functional connectivity (FC) matrices. Then, to extract a low dimensional representation of spatiotemporal dynamics, we used LEiDA5 (Fig. 1a). The results of k-means clustering with different numbers of clusters were evaluated using the silhouette score and Dunn’s index to choose an appropriate number of clusters. We then compute the occupancy of each state (i.e. percentage of time the state was present in the timeseries) for all subjects and compare them between groups. To ensure the robustness of findings, group comparisons were performed across k-means runs with different numbers of clusters and the resulting p-values were corrected for multiple comparisons. In addition, we extracted meta-analytic maps associated with 115 cognitive traits from Neurosynth (https://www.neurosynth.org/) and computed the percentage of areas in the cognitive maps that were contained by each LEiDA state. 

To simulate spontaneous BOLD signals, we made use of the Hopf-bifurcation model, where local dynamics can be controlled by the bifurcation parameter (a), which moves the system between a stable fixed point and sustained oscillations (Fig. 1a). In the large-scale model, the value of ai for each cortical area i was defined as ai = abottom + hi * (atop - abottom), where hi is a value between 0 and 1, representing the position of the area along the gradient of cortical hierarchy, estimated from a normative map of T1/T2w ratio in the cortex (Fig. 1b). Cortical areas were connected through long-range white-matter tracts, obtained from our sample through DTI imaging and multiplied by a scaling factor, K, formally called global coupling (Fig. 1b). For each model, we simulated 60 minutes of BOLD signals and used the k-means model trained on empirical data to extract the LEiDA state occupancies. Then, we computed the model’s fit to empirical data as 1 minus the mean difference between empirical and simulated state occupancies, in percentage (Fig 1b). This value was used as the goal of a Bayesian optimization algorithm to adjust the model’s hyperparameters (K, abottom, and atop) (Fig. 1b). We optimized separated models based on structural data from healthy controls and patients to the state occupancies from controls and patients, respectively, and compared the resulting hyperparameters to infer the main circuit disruptions behind the main differences in large-scale dynamics. 

For all group comparisons, we used the Mann-Whitney U-test and corrected p-values for multiple comparisons using the FDR correction.

Results

From the results of k-means clustering, a local maximum in the Dunn-index was found for N=7 states (Fig. 2a). The resulting spatiotemporal patterns include a “global” state, with widespread synchronization, and states overlapping with the canonical resting-state networks (RSNs) (Fig. 2a). When comparing healthy controls (HC) and schizophrenia patients (SCZ), we found significant differences in the occupancy of “global” (HC: 36.3±14.7%; SCZ: 29.6±16.3%; p = 0.033), “limbic” (HC: 8.8±7.5%; SCZ: 13.7±9.5%; p = 0.005) and “frontoparietal” states (HC: 11.7±6.8%; SCZ: 8.1±4.8%; p = 0.008) (Fig 2b). Importantly, these differences were very robust to changes in the number of k-means clusters (Fig. 2b). When comparing states with the meta-analytic cognitive maps, we found that the “Limbic” state included areas associated with negative emotions, face recognition and language, all of which relate to known positive and negative symptoms of schizophrenia (Fig 2c). In turn, the “frontoparietal” state encompasses areas involved in several functions, including emotional regulation, verbal fluency, cognitive control, and working memory, associated with the negative and cognitive symptoms (Fig 2c). To improve the interpretability of the patterns associated with LEiDA states, we propose that states can be interpreted as instances in time where specific RSNs are segregated from the rest of the brain. Our results confirmed this interpretation by showing a significant negative correlation between the occupancy of specific LEiDA states and RSN-Brain FC for the visual, somatomotor, frontoparietal, default-mode and limbic networks (Fig 2d). That said, the RSN-Brain FC of limbic and frontoparietal RSNs was not significantly different between the HC and SCZ groups (Fig 2d), as opposed to the occupancy of the associated LEiDA states. These results suggest that, even though static FC reflects dynamics, the statistics of transient synchrony patterns contain unique information in schizophrenia patients that is not evident from FC analysis alone. 

To test which disruptions at the level of local circuits and structural connectivity could lead to the observed changes in spatiotemporal dynamics, we implemented a large-scale model of the cortex, which was then fit to the state occupancies from the HC and SCZ groups (Fig 1b). By comparing the optimized model parameters, we found two main disruptions associated with schizophrenia. First, a significant decrease in the overall strength of structural connectivity (i.e. decreased K) (Fig 2E). Second, a significant increase in the bifurcation parameter at the bottom of the cortical gradient (Fig. 2E, abottom), leading to a flattening of the slope of a (Fig. 2E, Δa). To test the relative contribution of each disruption, we took the model optimized to patient data and substituted either the global coupling (K) or bifurcation parameters (ai) for the respective values in the healthy model (Fig 2F).  Both interventions significantly reduced the fit of the model to empirical SCZ data (Fig 2F, top). However, while a decrease in K was necessary to reproduce disruptions in the global and limbic states, decreases in the occupancy of the frontoparietal state were instead related to the flattening of bifurcation gradients (Fig 2F, bottom). 

Conclusion

Our results demonstrate that there are specific abnormalities in large-scale cortical dynamics in schizophrenia patients. First, we found decreased occupancy of a state of widespread synchrony and a state associated with frontoparietal networks. While the former has been associated with general cognitive performance,5 the latter contains cortical areas associated with cognitive functions known to be disrupted in schizophrenia, suggesting that these disruptions could be related to the ethology of cognitive symptoms.1 In addition, we also found increased occupancy of a state encompassing areas involved in emotional regulation, face recognition, and language, which are also associated with symptoms found in schizophrenia patients.1 Importantly, differences between patients and controls were only evident through dynamic analysis, indicative of the relationship between spontaneous dynamics and behavior2 and suggesting that, while FC does reveal general patterns of interaction between cortical areas, pathological states might be more related how these interactions fluctuate dynamically. In addition, preliminary modeling results indicate that the observed large-scale disruptions can be a consequence of a widespread decrease in structural connectivity3 and a flattening of the bifurcation gradient, which, in biophysical terms, can be roughly understood as the “excitability” of cortical circuits. Importantly, changes in the dynamics of the global and limbic states related to structural hypoconnectivity3, while the flattening of cortical gradients more strongly affects the dynamics of the frontoparietal state.
