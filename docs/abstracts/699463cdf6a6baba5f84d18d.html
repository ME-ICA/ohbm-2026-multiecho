Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a major public health issue and an established contributor to long-term cognitive decline and dementia. Injuries range from mild concussion to penetrating trauma, arising most frequently from falls, road traffic accidents, sports-related impacts and assault. Growing evidence links TBI to accelerated neurodegeneration and elevated risk of Alzheimer’s Disease (AD), vascular dementia (VD) and chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE).1 Neuroimaging studies have revealed widespread structural differences following TBI. Diffusion MRI shows lower fractional anisotropy and higher mean diffusivity across major white matter tracts, signatures of worse microstructural integrity that correlate with worse cognitive performance.2 Grey matter alterations, including cortical thinning and subcortical atrophy, have also been documented.3 though often in very small clinical samples (N<50). Although the link between TBI and later‑life cognitive impairment is becoming increasingly established, clinical trajectories are highly heterogeneous, reflecting individual differences in injury severity, age at exposure, post-injury care and underlying brain differences. 

Moreover, the later life neurostructural hallmarks of TBI in large-scale, non-clinical populations are incompletely understood. Large-scale datasets such as UK Biobank enable the detection of subtle yet robust effects in the general population. The present study combines high-dimensional vertex-wise cortical morphometry with connectome-based white matter analyses to provide an integrated, brain-wide characterisation of neurostructural differences in those with historic TBI. By comparing these patterns to those observed in individuals with dementia, we aim to delineate to what extent the brain differences associated with TBI overlap with those associated with dementia, potentially contributing to early neuroimaging markers of vulnerability to cognitive decline. 

Methods

Participants 

Data was leveraged from UK Biobank, a large prospective study with over 500,000 generally healthy, community-dwelling participants of middle- and old-age (40-69 years). We used N~35k with processed brain MRI data for the TBI-related analyses, following exclusion of those with self-reported or hospital recorded diagnoses of neurological disease or cancer. Of these, N~1000 fit our TBI definition (Fig1A). 

TBI definitions 

History of TBI was obtained through: (i) self-reported head injury at the time of imaging (Nmax=113) and (ii) primary and secondary ICD-10 diagnoses on participant hospital inpatient records as derived previously.2 ICD-10 codes were classified into two definitions: a broad-band TBI group (1798 codes related to head injury, Nmax=211) and a narrow-band group (51 codes specific to TBI and similar terms, Nmax=992) (more details at2). Participants who met criteria for more than one TBI definition were included in both groups to maximise power. Each TBI definition was treated as a binary predictor against true controls (negative for all three TBI definitions).  

Neuroimaging acquisition and processing 

Acquisition of T1-weighted and diffusion brain MRI sequences are detailed in UK Biobank’s open-access protocols. Cortical reconstruction, volumetric segmentation and manual QC were carried out locally prior to the present study using FreeSurfer.4 This produced vertex-wise surface data for volume, thickness and surface area at 298,790 positions across the cortical surface. White matter structural networks were also constructed locally using diffusion MRI and probabilistic tractography as described elsewhere.5 This produced five water diffusion weightings (mean diffusivity, MD; fractional anisotropy, FA; isotropic volume fraction, ISOVF; intracellular volume fraction, ICVF; and orientation dispersion, OD) for 85 brain regions and 1071 connections between them. Five graph theory metrics (efficiency, strength, clustering coefficient, mean edge weight and degree centrality) were used to estimate microstructural differences. 

Cortical vertex-wise morphometric associations 

Associations between each definition of historic TBI (predictors) and vertex-wise cortical morphometry (volume, thickness, surface area; outcomes) were examined at 298,790 vertices using linear regression (Fig1B). All models were adjusted for age at imaging, sex, site, ICV and head position on the scanner. Results were visualised on the average cortical surface and raw p-values were FDR-corrected for multiple comparisons across the cortical surface. 

Network-based connectivity associations 

We examined associations between each definition of historic TBI and measures of global or regional network-level white matter microstructure (Fig1C). All network metrics were standardised prior to analysis and computed separately for connectomes weighted by the five diffusion-derived metrics (FA, MD, ISOVF, ICVF, OD). For connection-specific analyses, linear regressions were conducted across 1071 connections using MD as the edge weight. All models were adjusted for age at imaging, sex, site and head position on the scanner. For all models, standardised beta coefficients, standard errors, p-values and FDR-corrected q-values were calculated. 

Similarity to dementia-related patterns 

We repeated the above analysis pipelines, this time using data from individuals who had a formal dementia diagnosis, and calculated brain‑wide Pearson correlations between the TBI‑ and dementia‑related differences. This allowed assessment of how closely the spatial patterns seen in historic TBI matched those observed in dementia (Fig 1D). To control for the strong spatial autocorrelation inherent in vertex‑wise maps, we applied spin‑test permutations, which adjust the p‑values to appropriately account for spatial dependency. 

Results

Vertex‑wise morphometry revealed a modest but significant difference in surface area of the bilateral orbitofrontal cortex (meanβ = ‑0.017, FDR‑q <0.05, Fig1A) associated with historic narrow-band TBI. No differences in cortical volume or thickness were observed in any TBI group versus controls (Fig1A-C). Narrow‑ and broad‑band TBI definitions yielded similar morphometric patterns (r = 0.50–0.64, pspin<0.001), whereas the self‑reported TBI group showed no similarity to either (r = ‑0.0013 to –0.13, pspin>0.05). Dementia was associated with lower cortical volume, thickness, and surface area (Fig1D); though, spatial overlap with TBI maps was minimal (r=0.04–0.26, pspin>0.05), except for a modest correlation between broad‑band TBI thickness and dementia (r=0.26, pspin< 0.05) and between self‑reported TBI volume and dementia (r =0.23, pspin< 0.05).

Global network‑level white‑matter analysis showed that narrow‑band TBI had robust positive associations with MD‑ and ISOVF‑weighted clustering coefficient, efficiency, and mean edge weight (β=0.14–0.18, q<0.02) indicating higher diffusivity; broad‑band effects were similar but smaller (β=0.06–0.08, q<0.05). No significant global connectome differences were found for self‑reported TBI. Locally, MD‑weighted efficiency was higher in 67% (narrow-band) and 72% (broad-band) of regions (Fig1E), with strongest effects in the lateral and medial OFC (β≈0.20, q<0.01) for narrow‑band, and in the paracentral cortex and thalamus for broad‑band (β≈0.10, q<0.01). Dementia also showed differences across the brain, with larger effect sizes (Fig1E, bottom). Edge‑wise analyses identified 13.5 % (narrow-band, Fig1F) and 29.6 % (broad-band, Fig1G) of connections with higher MD compared to controls, primarily in left‑dominant fronto‑temporal‑parietal networks. Dementia showed extensive white‑matter differences (66 % of connections, mean β = 0.395, Fig1H); overlap with broad‑band TBI was moderate (r=0.53, p<0.001) but minimal for narrow‑band (r = ‑0.05,p>0.05) and self‑reported TBI (r = 0.12).

Conclusion

We present the most comprehensive neurostructural signature of historic TBI to date, linking past injury to later-life cortical surface and whole-brain white matter network differences in otherwise healthy individuals. While TBI‑related differences overlap modestly with dementia patterns, the TBI profile remains mostly distinct. The orbitofrontal cortex shows lower surface area, reflecting its anatomical vulnerability to contre‑coup forces during rapid head acceleration‑deceleration. In contrast, white‑matter microstructure shows broader spatial concordance with dementia. Detecting these effects in a large, generally healthy cohort with heterogeneous causes of injury shows the robustness of the association and suggests disruption of large‑scale networks implicated in cognitive decline. Future work should replicate these findings in samples with detailed TBI frequency, severity and additional covariates (e.g., smoking, socioeconomic status) to refine risk modelling.
