Introduction

White matter hyperintensities (WMH) and enlarged perivascular spaces (ePVS) are common markers of cerebral small vessel disease in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). While both markers tend to accumulate with advancing age, past research has even discovered that higher global WMH volume is associated with higher ePVS burden.1 However, they likely arise from different underlying biological processes. While WMH are typically linked to ischemia in small vessels, ePVS are increasingly viewed as signs of disrupted glymphatic function.1 

An increased occurrence of WMH has been demonstrated to be particularly associated with a higher risk of cognitive dysfunction and the development of dementia. Furthermore, research has identified cardiovascular factors as the highest risk factors for the development of WMH in older ages.1 The extent to which ePVS contribute to this dynamic interaction between risk factors, WMH and cognitive dysfunction, however, remains largely unexplained. One potential explanation might be the glymphatic dysfunction, which contributes to structural damage in brain white matter, leading  to the development of WMH, thus correlating with decline in cognitive performance.1 In addition to that, cognitive dysfunction is frequently attributed to an interplay of cardiovascular risk factors and lifestyle-related determinants. This raises the question of whether lifestyle factors might significantly contribute to the development of WMH and ePVS, eventually resulting in cognitive impairments. To quantify lifestyle-related risk, we applied the Lifestyle for Brain Health 2 (LIBRA2)2  score. The present study sought to examine the intricate interplay among risk factors (i.e., cardiovascular and lifestyle), WMH and ePVS, as well as domain-specific cognitive performance, utilizing structural equation models (SEM) in a large population-based cohort of older adults, 1000BRAINS. 

Methods

The study sample consisted of 620 older adults ranging from 55 to 85 years (mean age: 66 years ± 7 SD; 332 males, 288 females). For lifestyle-related dementia risk, we used the LIBRA2 score, a composite index based on 15 modifiable risk and protective factors. Complete data required for LIBRA2 calculation were available for 261 participants (mean age: 66 ± 6 years; 148 males, 113 females), who were included in the corresponding subsample analyses. T1, T2 and T2-fluid attenuated inversion recovery (T2-FLAIR) images were acquired on a 3T MR scanner (Tim Trio, Erlangen, Germany) to quantify WMH volume and ePVS burden. WMH burden was measured using the widely used automatic segmentation tool Brain Intensity AbNormality Classification Algorithm (BIANCA) from the FSL library. Whole brain ePVS volume was extracted using the semi-automated approach presented by Jung et al.3. Three latent cognitive factors were calculated based on 16 neuropsychological tests in line with Jockwitz et al.4 : visuospatial & executive functions (VSE), fluency & memory (FM), and verbal memory (VM). Cardiovascular risk was assessed using cardiovascular age5 (CV-age), while the LIBRA2 score was calculated to estimate combined cardiovascular and lifestyle-related risk. Three SEM were constructed in lavaan version 0.6-19, each with an identical three-level architecture enabling simultaneous estimation of direct and indirect effects between risk factors, WMH and ePVS, and cognitive performance. Every SEM included (1) predictors (i.e., CV-age and/or LIBRA2) and covariates (chronological age, sex, and education; allowed to covary); (2) the endogenous WMH and ePVS whole brain volume (log-transformed and z-scored); and (3) the cognitive outcome, represented by the three latent variables VSE, FM and VM. We first examined CV-age effects in the total sample (n=620) and in the subsample (n=261) for validation, then examined LIBRA2 effects alone (n=261) (Fig.1), and finally both, CV-age and LIBRA2, simultaneously, to partition shared and unique variance (n=261) (Fig. 2).

Results

The CV-age model demonstrated an excellent fit in the total sample (CFI=0.97, TLI=0.95, RMSEA=0.04, SRMR=0.04). CV-age emerged as a significant predictor for both, ePVS (β=.13, p≤.05) and WMH burden (β=.11, p≤.05), while its effect on visuospatial & executive functions was mediated by WMH (β= -.01, p≤.05). Critically, WMH and PVS showed differential effects on cognition: more WMH were significantly associated with worse performance in  visuospatial & executive functions (β=-.15, p≤.05), explaining 38.6% of variance in this factor, while showing no significant effects on figural memory or verbal memory. In contrast, PVS showed no direct effects on any cognitive domain. Remarkably, ePVS showed a strong predictive value for WMH (β=.29, p≤.001). There was no significant direct effect of CV-age on cognition. All observed effects of the total-sample CV-age model could be reproduced in the smaller subsample of 261 participants with consistent effects.

In the LIBRA2-only subsample model, which demonstrated a good fit (CFI=0.94, TLI=0.92, RMSEA=0.05, SRMR=0.05), the LIBRA2 Score specifically predicted ePVS burden (β=.147, p≤.05), but not WMH. ePVS showed a strong predictive value for WMH (β=.31, p≤.001). The WMH effect on visuospatial & executive functions was replicated and even more pronounced (β=-.15, p≤.05).  

The final model, incorporating both CV-age and LIBRA2 as predictors, demonstrated a good fit (CFI=0.95, TLI=0.93, RMSEA=0.04, SRMR=0.05). Here, no significant direct effect of LIBRA2 on any factor was observed. At the same time, CV-age remained a reliable predictor of WMH (β=.172, p≤.05) and ePVS (β=.174, p≤.05) burden. Given the observed covariance between LIBRA2 and CV-age (r=.34, p<.001), the two predictors were not statistically independent in this model. Importantly, neither CV-age nor LIBRA2 showed significant direct effects on cognition. The predictive effects of ePVS on WMH (β=.29, p≤.001) and the effect of WMH on visuospatial & executive functions (β=-.11, p≤.05) remained consistent.

Conclusion

The present findings demonstrate the complex interplay between cardiovascular and lifestyle risk as assessed via CV-age and LIBRA2, WMH and ePVS, and domain-specific cognitive performance. First, we identified a domain specific rather than a global pattern in the association between WMH and cognition, with higher global WMH volume relating specifically to impaired visuospatial and executive functions. In addition, we were able to confirm the hypothesis that ePVS mediate the relationship between risk factors and WMH, thereby exerting an indirect effect on cognitive performance in a cognitively healthy older adult sample. Although CV-age and LIBRA2 cover partly overlapping domains of risk, they also capture distinct aspects of vascular and lifestyle-related influences, which were reflected in our models. CV-age, representing cumulative vascular burden, showed broad associations with both ePVS and WMH, consistent with the notion that vascular ageing impairs perivascular function and contributes to white matter damage.1 LIBRA2, by contrast, was not directly related to WMH but showed a robust association with CV-age and ePVS, contributing to WMH burden only indirectly (Fig. 1 and 2). This pattern suggests that lifestyle-related risk may exert its influence at an earlier stage of small vessel disease, affecting perivascular dynamics before structural white matter changes appear.1 Yet across all pathways, WMH emerged as the point where vascular and lifestyle-related influences ultimately converged. Together, these findings support a framework in which ePVS reflect upstream alterations in perivascular function, whereas WMH represent the structural endpoint through which diverse risk profiles translate into cognitive deficits.1 

In this way, the present study establishes a theoretical model for distinct yet interconnected biological cause-and-effect associations between cardiovascular and lifestyle factors, WMH and ePVS, and visuospatial & executive functions in older adults.
