Introduction

Total sleep deprivation combined with morning light therapy (TSD+LT) is a well-established chronotherapeutic intervention for bipolar disorder (BD), capable of rapidly reducing depressive symptomatology by modulating sleep-wake cycle and circadian rhythms1. Despite its clinical efficacy, the neurobiological mechanisms underlying TSD+LT remain poorly understood, and evidence on neural correlates of treatment-related changes is still limited. Resting-state fMRI offers a suitable approach to address this gap, as bipolar depression is characterised by functional alterations in intrinsic large-scale networks2. Within this context, graph theory provides a powerful framework to assess large-scale brain architecture, given its capability to characterise network topology by quantifying integration (i.e., efficiency of information exchange across distant regions) and segregation (i.e., degree of specialised local and global cluster among neighbouring regions)3,4. This approach has been previously applied in bipolar depression, with converging evidence suggesting that this disorder is associated with abnormal imbalance between over segregated network architecture and disrupted integration across intrinsic networks, potentially underpinning rumination and impaired emotion regulation5. However, to our knowledge, no longitudinal study has yet investigated whether TSD+LT can induce changes in graph-theoretical properties of functional brain networks, and their relationships with clinical improvements. Thus, in the present study, we investigated whether a chronotherapeutic intervention in bipolar depression induces changes in whole-brain global network topology, and whether such reorganisation is linked to reductions in depressive symptoms and to a reconfiguration of brain functional architecture toward the network organisation observed in healthy controls.

Methods

Fourty-six inpatients with BD (16 males, 30 females; mean age = 48.11 ± 11.78 years) in a current depressive episode were recruited at IRCCS San Raffaele Hospital (Milan, Italy). Patients underwent a one-week chronotherapeutic protocol combining three cycles of 36-h of total sleep deprivation (Days 1, 3, 5; 07:00-19:00 the following day), each followed by a night of recovery sleep (Days 2, 4, 6). LT was administered through 30-minute exposure to a 10,000-lux bright white light, during the TSD nights at 3.00 a.m. and again in the morning after recovery sleep, approximately 30 minutes after awakening between 8.00 a.m. and 9.00 a.m. During deprivation nights, patients were prevented from going to bed and monitored while remaining in the designated common areas of the psychiatric ward to ensure full adherence to the sleep deprivation protocol. MRI data were acquired on a 3T Philips Ingenia CX scanner with a 32-channel head coil at before (Day 0) and after TSD+LT protocol completion (Day 7). Resting-state fMRI and depressive symptoms (scored with the 13-item Beck Depression Inventory, BDI-13) were acquired on the same days at before (Day 0, T0) and after TSD+LT protocol completion (Day 7, T1), ensuring tight temporal correspondence between neuroimaging and clinical assessment to capture state-dependent neural correlates of depressive symptom severity. In addition, twenty-nine healthy controls (13 males, 16 females; mean age = 46.72 ± 11.01 years; HC) underwent a single rs-fMRI session with the same acquisition protocol. The preprocessing of resting-state fMRI data was performed with FSL, and included motion and slice-timing correction, brain extraction, 6-mm FWHM spatial smoothing, high-pass filtering (100 s), variance normalisation, and boundary-based registration to individual T1 and MNI152 space. Functional time series were extracted from 112 cortical and subcortical regions (Harvard-Oxford atlas, cerebellum excluded). For each subject, 112×112 Pearson correlation matrices were computed and converted into weighted graphs by relative thresholding across 10-30% network densities (1% steps). Three main global network graph metrics were computed in GraphVar using Brain Connectivity Toolbox functions: global efficiency (i.e., integration), global clustering coefficient (i.e., mean local segregation), and modularity (i.e., inter-modular segregation). Moreover, at nodal level, regional segregation was indexed using clustering coefficient and local efficiency. Within-subject general linear models (GLMs) tested T1-T0 changes in patients, including age, sex, pharmacological load, episode frequency, and baseline BDI-13 as covariates; ΔBDI was included as continuous covariate to assess whether patients with greater symptom improvement showed larger whole-brain network changes. Additional GLMs compared HC and BD at baseline and post-treatment, controlling for age and sex. All effects were assessed across the full density range with false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple comparisons, only results that remained significant across the full range of thresholds were considered. 

Results

TSD+LT produced a robust antidepressant effect in BD patients, with BDI-13 scores decreasing from 16.11 ± 7.83 at baseline to 10.52 ± 9.10 after one week (paired t = 5.45, p < 0.001). At the global network level, one-week of chronotherapeutic intervention induced a significant increase in global efficiency across all thresholds (m = 0.06 ± 0.013; pFDR < 0.001). The global clustering coefficient also increased significantly across the full threshold range (m = 0.10 ± 0.026; pFDR < 0.001). In contrast, modularity showed a significant decrease at all thresholds (m = -0.056 ± 0.01; pFDR < 0.05), Critically, only modularity changes were tightly coupled to clinical improvement: larger reductions in depressive symptom severity (i.e., higher ΔBDI) were associated with greater decreases in modularity across densities (mean β = -0.32 ± 0.01; pFDR < 0.05), whereas changes in global efficiency and clustering did not show significant interactions with ΔBDI after FDR correction. Between-group analyses revealed that at baseline, BD patients exhibited significantly higher modularity than HC across all densities (mean d = 0.049 ± 0.005; pFDR < 0.05), indicating a globally over-segregated functional architecture. Global clustering was also reduced in patients relative to HC, with significant differences emerging at a subset of thresholds (Figure 1a). After TSD+LT, no significant between-group differences remained for any of the global graph metrics (pFDR > 0.05) (Figure 1b). Post-treatment modularity values in BD converged toward the HC profile across densities, and the BD-HC gap observed at baseline was no longer detectable (Figure 1c). At the nodal level, TSD+LT increased local segregation in frontal regions, with higher clustering in the right superior (m = 0.41 ± 0.1; pFDR < 0.05) and inferior frontal gyri (m = 0.40 ± 0.07; pFDR < 0.05), while both mean clustering and mean local efficiency decreased in the medial frontal cortex (FMC; clustering:  FMC.L, m = -0.6 ± 0.18; FMC.R, m = -0.48 ± 0.21; efficiency: FMC.L, m = -0.74 ± 0.19; FMC.R, m = -0.65 ± 0.22; all pFDR < 0.05). Local efficiency additionally increased in the bilateral inferior temporal gyrus and in subcortical regions including the left thalamus (m = -0.47 ± 0.21; pFDR < 0.05) and left caudate (m = -0.44 ± 0.10; pFDR < 0.05), No significant associations emerged between ΔBDI and changes in nodal clustering or nodal local efficiency after FDR correction. In Baseline BD and HC comparinson, BD shows reduced segregation in bilateral occipital pole (OP; clustering: OP.L, m = -0.14 ± 0.01; efficiency: OP.L, m = -0.12 ± 0.02; OP.R, -0.13 ± 0.02; pFDR < 0.05) and whereas no significant nodal differences were observed after treatment.

Conclusion

This longitudinal resting-state fMRI study suggests that the rapid antidepressant effects of TSD+LT in bipolar depression are underpinned by a functional reorganisation of whole-brain network topology, characterised by a shift toward a more integrated and less modular large-scale brain architecture. Increases in global efficiency and clustering suggest that chronotherapy shifts the brain toward a more optimal small-world configuration, supporting efficient long-range communication while preserving locally specialised processing3,4. Decreases in modularity across all densities, together with their tight association with ΔBDI, suggest that reductions in large-scale modular segregation represent a core network-level correlation of clinical response. Patients showing the greatest symptom improvement were those who shift more from a pathological over-segregated configuration toward a more integrated network architecture are those who show the greatest symptom improvement after the TSD+LT protocol. The comparison with HC further supports a state-dependent normalisation after one week of chronotherapeutic treatment. Before treatment, BD patients displayed an over segregated modular architecture relative to HC, whereas after one week of TSD+LT their global metrics, particularly modularity, were statistically comparable to those of HC across densities. In line with global effects, nodal changes in local segregation provided converging evidence for regional functional reorganisation, involving frontal, temporo-occipital, and subcortical regions. Moreover, the absence of post-treatment BD-HC differences at the nodal level further supports a broad functional reorganisation toward a healthy functional topology. Taken together, our results indicate that chronotherapy can restore large-scale functional organisation in BD toward a more normative configuration, aligning the connectomic profile of bipolar depression with that of healthy individuals. Within this framework, TSD+LT showed a neuroplasticity effect in the broader connectomics of mood disorders, suggesting that its rapid antidepressant effects are mediated by a shift toward a more integrated large-scale brain network topology. Graph-theoretical measures, particularly modularity, emerge as promising state-dependent biomarkers of chronotherapeutic response, supporting precision psychiatry approaches that leverage brain network architecture to monitor and predict antidepressant outcome in mood disorders.
