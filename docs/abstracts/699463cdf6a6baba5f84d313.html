Introduction

In our previous work1, we reformulated connectome-based predictive modeling (CPM) such that the connectivity networks were predefined for six cognitive construct brain networks. Rather than deriving predictive features from the whole brain, we quantified the extent to which each network’s connectivity fluctuated with task performance across 16 widely used behavioral test scores. In this framework, the predictive accuracy associated with a given network served as a feedback measure of how strongly that behavioral test depends on that specific cognitive construct network. The aim was not to localize the circuitry underlying an external measure, but instead to begin with brain networks of interest and assess their relative contributions to each behavioral metric. In doing so, we “tested the tests,” providing an interpretable quantitative evaluation of what each measure captures in terms of underlying brain function. Here, we aim to extend this approach to identify highly predictable test composites whose predictive signatures are distinct across networks. In other words, we seek composite tests that are network-specific, exhibiting strong predictive performance for one network while showing weak predictive performance for the remaining ones. Such composites allow us to isolate behavioral measures that map selectively onto particular neural systems, enhancing interpretability and functional specificity. To do this, we sequentially evaluated growing composite scores for combinations of n = 2, 3, and 4 tests.

Methods

A transdiagnostic fMRI data set2 composed of two resting and six task runs was collected at Yale from 302 demographically diverse subjects, including both healthy individuals and patients. The Shen2683 atlas was applied to the data, and the pairwise correlations of mean node time courses were computed to generate the eight (one for each of the eight runs) 268 × 268 connectivity matrices per subject from which the construct networks were extracted. While any network of interest can be predefined, here, we defined the six cognitive construct networks: attention, perception, declarative memory, language, cognitive control, and working memory. Each construct network was defined as the set of edges connecting nodes identified from NeuroSynth4. For each construct term, we extracted coordinates from the corresponding activation map in NeuroSynth and mapped them onto Shen268 nodes. In the connectivity matrices, we included the full row for every node associated with that given construct. Across the imaging runs, the cognitive construct networks ranged from 7 × 268 to 12 × 268 nodes. Predictive modeling for each construct network on the test measures was done using separate kernel ridge regression models. Connectivity matrices from the two resting-state and the six task runs were concatenated, yielding feature sets composed of edges from matrices ranging in size from 7 × 268 × 8 to 12 × 268 × 8. Predictive modeling was performed on 16 behavioral tests from six standardized test banks. Predictive power is quantified using the coefficient of determination (R²), which reflects the proportion of variance in the observed behavioral scores accounted for by the model’s predictions. The dataset was divided into 200 participants for training and 102 for validation. In the first step, we quantified the predictive power for all two-test combinations (120 unique pairs generated from the 16 behavioral tests). A combination was considered desirable if it satisfied the following criteria: (1) the predictive power of the cognitive construct network on the composite was within the top 5% of all composites (6/120); (2) the network’s predictive power on the composite was significantly greater than its predictive power on either individual test; (3) the isolated difference (defined below) for the construct of interest was greater than zero; and (4) the isolated difference for the composite ranked within the top 5% (6/120). We define the isolated difference as the predictive power of the cognitive construct of interest on a given composite minus the maximum predictive power of the other five constructs on that same composite. Predictive power and isolated differences were computed across 20 random data splits (100 iterations each), and composites were ranked based on their aggregated performance, from which the top 5% were identified. Next, we evaluated how cognitive construct networks performed as the composite size increased. Three-test combinations were generated by adding each of the remaining 14 behavioral tests to the two-test pairs. We selected three-test composites that met two criteria: (1) the predictive power of the construct of interest was significantly greater than its predictive power on the corresponding two-test composite, and (2) the isolated difference remained greater than zero. Four-test combinations were then evaluated in the same manner by adding each of the remaining 13 behavioral tests to the three-test composites, again requiring that the predictive power significantly exceed that of the previous composite and that the isolated difference remain positive.

We used paired one-tailed t-tests (MATLAB) at the 5% significance level to compare predictive performance across composite scores averaged over 100 iterations for each of the 20 fixed random data splits. Because the composite-building procedure followed a strict sequential criterion of terminating as soon as p > 0.05, multiple-comparison correction was not applied.

Results

Only three distinct two-test combinations met the selection criteria: finger windows & matrix reasoning and vocabulary & matrix reasoning for the language network, and finger windows & verbal learning for the cognitive control network. None of the remaining four cognitive construct networks yielded any two-test combinations that met the selection criteria. Figure 1a shows the ranking of all 120 two-test combinations by predictive power (left) and by isolated difference (right) for the example language network. Figure 1b shows a zoomed-in view of the top 5% of test pairs for both metrics for the language and cognitive control networks. The two-test composites that satisfied the criteria for their respective networks are highlighted in magenta. For three-test composites, four combinations met the criteria for the language network (finger windows & matrix reasoning & Boston Naming Test; finger windows & matrix reasoning & reading; finger windows & matrix reasoning & color-word interference; and finger windows & matrix reasoning & vocabulary). No three-test composites met the criteria for the cognitive control network. Extending the analysis to four-test composites, the language network yielded one qualifying composite (finger windows & matrix reasoning & vocabulary & Boston Naming Test), which satisfied both the network-isolation requirement (isolated difference> 0) and had significantly greater predictive power than the most predictive three-test composites. Figure 1c summarizes the predictive performance of the language and cognitive control networks on individual tests and on all qualifying composites (up to n = 4).

These findings were replicated in an independent subset of 100 participants drawn from the original 302. The two-test composites outperformed their constituent tests (p< 0.05 for 5 of the 6 pairwise comparisons; p> 0.05 only for the comparison between matrix reasoning and vocabulary & matrix reasoning for the language network). Across networks, the top two-test composites consistently ranked in the top 11% of all 120 evaluated pairs (language: finger windows & matrix reasoning ranked 1/120; vocabulary & matrix reasoning ranked 13/120; cognitive control: finger windows & verbal learning ranked 5/120). The isolated differences for the highest-ranking pairs were within the top 6% or greater than zero (language: finger windows & matrix reasoning ranked 1/120, isolated difference = 0.0184; vocabulary & matrix reasoningranked 2/120, isolated difference = 0.0092; cognitive control: finger windows & verbal learning ranked 7/120, isolated difference = -0.0049). For the language network, three of the four three-test combinations significantly exceeded their corresponding two-test composites (p< 0.05), with only finger windows & matrix reasoning & vocabulary not reaching significance (p> 0.05). All four three-test composites exhibited isolated differences greater than zero. The four-test composite similarly outperformed the three-test composites (p< 0.05) and also had a positive isolated difference.

Conclusion

We identified combinations of behavioral tests for which the cognitive construct networks show distinctly high predictive power. This analysis yielded test combinations of up to n = 2 for the cognitive control network and n ≥ 4 for the language network. In contrast, the other four cognitive construct networks did not produce any combinations that achieved both high and uniquely strong predictive performance. Several factors may account for this pattern. For example, multiple networks often predict the same test combinations well, satisfying the high-prediction criterion but not the uniqueness criterion, likely reflecting the broad influence of general intelligence on performance across tasks. In addition, the analysis is constrained by the limited set of 16 behavioral tests and six cognitive construct networks, reducing the chances that any given network will uniquely and strongly predict a specific test combination. Expanding the behavioral test battery and adopting more fine-grained cognitive construct network definitions may lead to additional combinations meeting both criteria. Finally, none of the existing tests were designed with neuroimaging data to inform the brain systems supporting performance, nor was this a consideration during test development. Future work may involve creating new tests that are better aligned with these goals.
