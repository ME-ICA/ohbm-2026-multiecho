Introduction

Primary Progressive Aphasia (PPA) is a neurodegenerative syndrome characterized by a progressive decline of language abilities, with three main clinical variants, nonfluent/agrammatic (nfvPPA), semantic (svPPA), and logopenic (lvPPA), each associated with distinct linguistic profiles and patterns of gray and white-matter degeneration ^1. In clinical practice, differential diagnosis depends mainly on clinician’s evaluation of speech production and neuroimaging, making the process time-consuming, subjective, and difficult to standardize. Automatic analysis of connected speech, through quantitative linguistic features, enables objective characterization of linguistic impairment ^2, while diffusion MRI provides sensitive markers of large-scale language network disruption ^3 ^4. 

This study aimed to support the clinical classification of PPA variants by developing a pipeline that automatically generates speech transcripts and extract linguistic features from them, integrating linguistic and diffusion-MRI measures in a multimodal classifier, and implementing the workflow in a web-based application. Finally, the automatic transcription and feature extraction processes were evaluated against manual gold-standard, and both approaches against the web-based application.

Methods

Data were collected at IRCCS San Raffaele Scientific Institute and included 91 healthy controls and 94 PPA patients (38 nfvPPA, 36 svPPA and 20 lvPPA). Diffusion tensor MRI data were available for 80 HC and 85 PPA patients. Spontaneous connected speech was audio-recorded during the Picnic scene picture description task from the Western Aphasia Battery in 38 HC and 81 PPA patients.

Audio recordings underwent preprocessing for quality control and were automatically transcribed using a commercial automatic speech recognition system (Microsoft Azure). To identify a reliable preprocessing method, multiple audio preprocessing pipelines were compared in terms of Word Error Rate (WER) against manual transcriptions. Two fully automatic pipelines showed the lowest WER values and no significant differences between them: resampling to 16 kHz and conversion to mono, and loudness normalization. Linguistic features were automatically obtained in Python using a combination of existing libraries and custom-developed pipelines. 

Fractional anisotropy (FA) maps were generated from processed diffusion MRI data, and an atlas was then used to extract mean FA values for each region of interest as white-matter features.3 Linguistic and imaging features were normalized and combined into a multimodal classifier.

Multiple supervised classifiers were evaluated within the multimodal framework, and the best-performing model was selected based on cross-validated balanced accuracy. A multinomial logistic regression classifier with L1 regularization was trained to discriminate among the three PPA variants using a nested cross-validation scheme, with an outer 5-fold split for performance estimation and an inner 5-fold split for hyperparameter tuning and feature selection. Balanced accuracy was selected as the main metric because classes were imbalanced. Automatic transcriptions and linguistic features were compared with manual gold-standard annotations to compute Word Error Rate (WER) and relative feature extraction error. Finally, a prototype web-based application was developed to integrate audio preprocessing, automatic transcription with a supervised revision step, allowing clinicians to correct transcription errors, and subsequent automatic feature extraction, ensuring that the linguistic measures accurately reflect the original speech. 

Results

The automatic transcription system achieved a mean WER of 12 ± 7% in HC and 19 ± 10% in PPA, with no significant differences across PPA variants. 

Automatically extracted linguistic features captured complementary dimensions of connected speech production, including lexical (e.g., type-token ratio and noun/verb usage), syntactic measures (e.g., mean length of utterance), semantic-informative measures (e.g., information content units) and temporal–fluency aspects (e.g., speech rate and pause-related features). Distinct variant-specific profiles emerged: nfvPPA patients showed reduced utterance counts, shorter utterances, and slower speech production; lvPPA patients exhibited increased pauses and word-finding difficulties with relatively preserved utterance output; svPPA patients showed fluent and syntactically preserved speech with reduced semantic informativeness and increased meta-linguistic comments. The mean relative error in linguistic feature extraction was 12 ± 8%.

The best overall classifier was the multimodal L1-regularized logistic regression model based on automatically extracted linguistic features combined with white-matter derived imaging features. This model achieved a balanced accuracy of 87 ± 6.9% and a macro F1-score of 86.7 ± 6.7% across test folds, outperforming unimodal speech-only (81.0 ± 9.7%) and imaging-only classifiers (74.1 ± 7.4%). The aggregated confusion matrix showed high correct classification rates for nfvPPA (93%) and svPPA (95%), with most misclassifications involving lvPPA, consistent with its known clinical and anatomical heterogeneity2 (Results figure). Misclassified nfvPPA and lvPPA cases were evenly distributed between the other two classes, whereas all misclassified svPPA cases were assigned to lvPPA.

The web-based application was evaluated on an independent test set of 10 PPA patients (3 nfvPPA, 3 svPPA, 4 lvPPA). Within this web-app application, supervised revision performed by a clinician of automatic transcriptions achieved a mean WER of 9 ± 3% across the two operators and the test set. Inter-rater variability was still present, but the two operators reached a mean per-subject agreement of 91 ± 4%.

Conclusion

This study presents an end-to-end multimodal framework for automated PPA variant differentiation, combining speech-derived linguistic markers with diffusion MRI measures of white-matter integrity. The proposed pipeline showed strong agreement with clinician-derived annotations and high diagnostic performance when integrating complementary behavioral and neuroimaging information.

The interactive web platform enables efficient clinician-in-the-loop supervision while preserving standardized and automated feature extraction. This approach provides an objective and reproducible tool to support PPA diagnosis and may facilitate harmonized assessments across centers, with potential applications in longitudinal monitoring and clinical trials.
