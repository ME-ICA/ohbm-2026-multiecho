Introduction

White matter hyperintensity (WMH) is a hallmark of cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD), visible on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). It is highly prevalent during normal aging, and is a strong predictor of dementia,stroke, and mortality1. A recent histopathological work on age-related WMH on post-mortem brain revealed demyelination and astrocytic water channel disruptions in a pattern of decreasing severity with increased distance from WMH2, emphasizing the need for the comprehensive characterizations of microstructural alterations within WMH and its penumbra. In order to investigate the impact of WMH on microstructural properties beyond lesions themselves we based our approach on a normal appearing white matter analyze (NAWM)3. Moreover, to understand its evolution over time in cognitively normal elderly, we analyzed longitudinal MRI data from the ADNI3 dataset. Ultimately, we characterize the microstructural properties with free-water diffusion tensor imaging (FW-DTI) to overcome the limitations of conventional DTI metrics that fail to resolve changes related to axonal fiber integrity from disruption in water homeostasis.

Methods

White matter microstructural alterations associated with CSVD are investigated using diffusion MRI data from 216 cognitively normal older adults enrolled in ADNI-3 (Fig.1.a). All participants were scanned with 3T MRI, including T1-weighted, FLAIR, and diffusion acquisitions. WMHs are segmented using the SHIVA‐WMH detector4 (Fig.1.b), then labeled and harmonized across timepoints using scilpy scripts. For the analysis of NAWM, concentric rings are generated around the lesions, with a thickness of 2 mm. This process was repeated six times, creating a total of six NAWM masks representing layers of increasing distance from the WMH, for a total diameter of 12 mm. These rim-shaped ROIs are refined by including only voxels from the predefined white matter segmentation from recon-all-clinical which was further corrected with the lesion mask to ensure full inclusion of WMHs (Fig.1.c). Diffusion preprocessing is performed with TractoFlow5 to correct DWI data from eddy current(Fig.1.d). Corrected DWI was then used with freewater_flow to calculate both the extracellular free-water fraction (FWF) and to tissue-specific diffusion metrics (Fig.1.e). 

In order to assess 1) the spatial gradients of microstructural alterations within WMH and the broader penumbra 2) their longitudinal progression, and 3) the impact of WMH load on both the spatial gradients and progression, we applied separate linear mixed models for each target microstructure metrics (FWF, FA-t, MD-t, AD-t, and RD-t). Each model included fixed effects for spatial distance (WMH and penumbra layers), time, global WMH load log+1-transformed(total WMH volume), and their interactions, as well as age and sex. Subject-specific random intercepts were added to account for within-individual correlation across layers and timepoints. Distance from the WMH boundary (0 up to 12mm) was modeled using a 6th-degree orthogonal polynomial (poly(distance, 6)) to allow sufficient flexibility to model non-linear spatial gradients of microstructural change.

Results

Across all metrics, there were clear microstructural gradients from WMH to the perilesional NAWM, extending up to 12 mm from the lesion border (Fig. 2). Both free water fraction (FWF) and tissue-specific diffusivities were markedly elevated within WMH and progressively decreased with increasing distance from the lesions. Among the diffusivity metrics, AD-t exhibited the most spatially extended abnormality, with a more gradual decline across the penumbra (Fig. 2, AD plot). Reflecting the differential spatial gradients of AD-t and RD-t, FA-t showed a bell-shaped pattern (Fig 2, FA plot), with the lowest value within WMH, then increasing up to 4mm from lesions, and slowly decreasing again away from the lesion. None of the microstructural gradients interacted with timepoints, indicating that the overall pattern of gradients did not change significantly longitudinally. In contrast, all metrics showed robust interaction between spatial distance and global WMH burden (all p < 0.001), revealing different spatial profiles as a function of the WMH load (Fig 2, facets showing predicted profile for each tertile of total WMH volume). No metrics showed significant three-way interaction between spatial distance, timepoint, and WMH load, meaning that the lesion load-dependent spatial gradients did not change longitudinally.

Independent of the spatial gradients, FWF and MD-t exhibited significant interaction between WMH load and timepoint (p = 0.018 and 0.012 for FWF and MD-t), in which subjects with higher WMH loads show more longitudinal increases (albeit very small) in these metrics. There were significant main effects of WMH load for FWF and all tissue diffusivity metrics (p = 0.011 for RD-t and all p < 0.001 for FWF, AD-t and MD-t), showing the overall increase in these metrics in subjects with higher WMH load. Even though very subtle, there was evidence for the overall longitudinal increases in FWF, MD-t, and RD-t (all p < 0.01).

Conclusion

Microstructural alterations in perilesional NAWM follow clear and robust spatial gradients extending up to 12 mm from the WMH border. Across all metrics, WMH tissue shows the strongest abnormalities, which gradually normalize with increasing distance, reflecting a chronic state of disrupted white-matter organization. Beyond these spatial effects, subtle longitudinal increases in FWF and MD-t indicate slow, diffuse progression of extracellular and tissue-related alterations. Together, these findings reinforce the concept of a structured WMH penumbra, where microstructural pathology extends beyond the visible hyperintensity and continues to evolve subtly over time. However, the different diffusion metrics do not appear to be affected in the same way, FA-t and AD-t appear less suited to capturing these variations, whereas MD-t, FWF, and RD-t are more responsive, reflecting demyelination, axonal rarefaction, and increased extracellular water2. To confirm these interpretations, the inclusion of an age-matched healthy control group  (free of WMH) would be essential to compare diffusion values within equivalent tissue regions and disentangle lesion-driven effects from those related to spatial location. Nevertheless, the strong influence of total WMH volume already demonstrates that the presence of lesions exerts a pathological impact both within the lesion core and throughout the penumbra, and that this influence persists longitudinally. It would also be valuable to examine lesion effects over a longer follow-up period to better characterize the temporal evolution of the penumbra. Finally, to deepen our understanding of lesion-induced microstructural degradation, more advanced diffusion models (such as NODDI) would offer greater specificity and sensitivity in capturing white-matter tissue changes.
