Introduction

Long COVID can affect over 10 systems with over 200 symptoms reported, with some of the most common and persistent being fatigue and cognitive difficulties1, yet the neurophysiological mechanisms underlying these complaints remain poorly understood. Structural studies also show that individuals who had COVID can exhibit approximately 2 percent cortical grey matter loss, particularly in regions supporting cognition, suggesting a biological basis for persistent symptoms2. Some studies have already observed alterations in cortical excitability in individuals with Long COVID who have cognitive and fatigue symptoms 3, yet the neural basis of Long COVID symptoms remains unclear, and no prior work has examined sensorimotor oscillations during movement, resting-state spectral profiles, and functional network organization within the same individuals, or how these features change after cognitive demand.

Movement-related beta dynamics, including movement-related beta desynchronization (MRBD), are a sensitive marker of cortical inhibition and have been shown to decrease with aging4. Functional connectivity and graph-theoretical metrics quantify large-scale communication, offering complementary insights into brain organization. Our study directly examines these oscillatory and network features in Long COVID, assessing their alterations and their relationship to persistent symptoms.

Methods

This study used high-density EEG to characterize both movement-related and resting-state neural activity in individuals with Long COVID compared with matched controls. We quantified sensorimotor beta dynamics during handgrips, spectral parameters, and alpha periodic properties during rest, as well as functional connectivity and graph-theoretical metrics before and after a motor learning task. We also examined whether these EEG features were related to fatigue and cognitive complaints.

Forty adults participated (20 Long COVID, 20 matched controls). Individuals with neurological comorbidities or conditions that could mimic brain fog or fatigue were excluded. Participants taking medications known to alter EEG activity were also excluded.

EEG was recorded during 50 isometric handgrips and during rest (with eyes open and eyes closed). This was repeated before and after a motor learning (ML) task. The ML task involved 100 trials (five blocks of 20) in which participants adjusted grip force to remain within visually presented targets (Fig. 1).

The EEG data were processed using MATLAB and Brainstorm. Preprocessing included notch, high-pass, low-pass, and band-pass filters, ICA-based artifact removal, re-referencing, and resampling. Movement-related beta desynchronization (MRBD) and post-movement beta rebound (PMBR) were extracted from C3/C5 during handgrips. Alpha periodic and aperiodic properties (central frequency, power, bandwidth, offset, and exponent) were estimated using spectral parameterization with the FOOOF algorithm during eyes-open rest on electrodes C3 and C5 and on full electrode analysis of resting-state with eyes closed. Resting-state whole-brain connectivity was computed using wPLI and dPLI. Graph-theoretical metrics included global efficiency and participation coefficient, derived from weighted connectivity matrices at baseline and post-ML.

Group comparisons at baseline used t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, depending on distribution. Pre–post ML task effects were tested using linear mixed models to assess Group x Time interactions. Bonferroni correction was applied for sensorimotor electrodes; false discovery rate correction was applied to exploratory correlations. Symptom measures included the Cognitive Failures Questionnaire (CFQ) and Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS).

Results

Long COVID participants showed reduced PMBR at C3 (p = 0.04) (Fig. 2) and a slower alpha central frequency at C5 (p = 0.005), reflecting altered inhibitory and oscillatory dynamics within the sensorimotor cortex. Additionally, they exhibited a narrower whole-brain alpha bandwidth at baseline (p = 0.03).

Following the ML task, participants with Long COVID demonstrated altered network reconfiguration. Specifically, global efficiency showed a smaller pre–post increase in the Long COVID group compared to controls (p = 0.01), and participation coefficient showed a larger pre–post decrease (p = 0.003).

Neural markers were associated with symptom severity. Lower PMBRs at C3 were correlated with higher CFQ scores (r = −0.35, p = 0.02). Slower alpha central frequency at C5 correlated with greater fatigue (T = −0.26, p= 0.02). Reduced network flexibility was associated with worse symptoms: a greater decrease in participation coefficient was linked to higher CFQ (r = 0.34, p = 0.03) and higher FSS (r = 0.25, p = 0.03). Smaller global efficiency increases correlated with greater fatigue (T = 0.23, p = 0.04).

Conclusion

This study identifies EEG signatures of Long COVID across sensorimotor and whole-brain analyses. Individuals with Long COVID showed reduced post-movement beta rebound and slower alpha oscillations, suggesting impaired inhibitory resetting and altered cortical excitability. Whole-brain analyses revealed narrower alpha bandwidth and altered network reconfiguration following cognitive demand, indicating disrupted neural adaptation to increased demands.

Associations with fatigue and cognitive symptoms on the self-reported symptom questionnaires suggest that an impaired ability to adapt to cognitive demands at the network level may be related to symptom expression. Together, these findings highlight oscillatory slowing, reduced inhibitory brain responses, and impaired network adaptability as candidate biomarkers of Long COVID. They also point toward potential targets for neuromodulation or cognitive rehabilitation approaches aimed at restoring flexible information processing in this condition.
