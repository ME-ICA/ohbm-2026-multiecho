Introduction

Currently, only 50-70% of epilepsy patients achieve seizure freedom after surgery. In the most challenging cases, a preoperative intracranial EEG (stereo-EEG, SEEG) is necessary to estimate the area that needs to be resected to achieve seizure freedom (i.e. the epileptogenic zone, EZ). The current gold standard for EZ estimation from SEEG is based on observing the electrophysiological changes occurring at the beginning of spontaneous seizures (i.e. the seizure onset zone, SOZ). The drawback of the SOZ is that it necessitates recording of spontaneous seizures, which can sometimes take several weeks or fail altogether. This has sparked a search for interictal (i.e. between seizures) SEEG biomarkers to identify the EZ. 

The classical interictal biomarker of epilepsy is the interictal epileptic discharge (IED), whereas high frequency oscillations (HFO) have more recently been established as markers of the EZ/SOZ and as predictors of surgical outcome1. Quantifying HFOs occurring during IEDs may further enhance the accuracy of EZ localization. Despite these advances, the event-based biomarkers cannot unambiguously define the SEEG contacts that need to be surgically removed to render the patient seizure free. 

Relative entropy (REN), calculated between adjacent SEEG contacts was shown to be as accurate as HFOs in estimating the EZ in patients with good postsurgical outcomes2. However, REN can be derived under multiple modeling assumptions, and a systematic evaluation of these variants as predictors of the SOZ or EZ is lacking.

This study assesses the performance of multiple REN variants for localizing the SOZ in SEEG. We hypothesize that REN variants are not equivalent in their discriminatory power and that at least a subset will match or surpass the predictive performance of established event-based metrics. Accordingly, we aim to determine which REN variants offer the strongest discrimination and to determine their potential value as biomarkers for surgical planning.

Methods

Our cohort consisted of 42 patients with drug refractory epilepsy, each implanted with 11-17 SEEG electrodes containing 8-18 contacts (Dixi Medical, France). The study was approved by The Helsinki University Hospital Institutional Research Review Board, with a waiver of consent because of the study’s retrospective and observational nature. 

The SEEG signals were acquired using a referential montage at a sampling rate of 2048 Hz, with a common reference contact placed in the white matter. Only contacts located in gray matter were included in further analyses. Bad channels were excluded, and 10 minutes of artefact- and seizure-free sleep and wake data were extracted based on clinical annotations. After exclusions 91 ± 11 SEEG channels per patient were included in computational analysis. 

REN was computed between adjacent SEEG contacts using the referential montage. First, the SEEG signals were decomposed into nine frequency bands (1-4 Hz, 4-8 Hz, 8-12Hz, 12-20 Hz, 20-40 Hz, 60-80 Hz, 80-160 Hz, 160-250 Hz, and 260-600 Hz) and each activity was segmented via 1-second non-overlapping windows. The probability density function (PDF) of each frequency-specific 1-s segment was then estimated via three different methods: 10-bin histograms, 100-bin histograms, and kernel probability estimators (KDEs). The following four variants of REN were then computed for each frequency band, 1-s segment, and PDF estimate: direction-dependent (DD) REN, absolute difference (AD) REN, Jeffrey’s divergence (JD), and Jensen-Shannon divergence (JSD). Finally, the results were averaged across the 1-s segments, and this procedure was repeated for sleep and wake data, and for each patient.

HFOs, consisting of ripples and fast ripples (FR), were automatically found by a validated detector3. Specifically, bipolar SEEG signals were band-pass filtered within 80-500 Hz and then subdivided into narrower frequency bands spaced 15 Hz apart. The wide- and narrow-band filtering procedures were implemented via equiripple finite impulse response filters of orders 200 and 508, respectively. HFOs were identified when: (1) the root mean square amplitude within any narrow frequency exceeded three times the background amplitude, and (2) the event duration reached a minimum of four cycles of the effective duration of the impulse response associated with each narrowband filter. 

IEDs (spikes) were detected using an established automatic detector4 and combined with HFOs yielding spike-ripples and spike-FRs; defined as spikes co-occurring with an HFO within 100 ms of its margin. Spike-gamma events were detected using an automatic dectector5. In brief, spike-gamma was defined when continuous gamma power within 30-100 Hz increased over three cycles before the rapid deflection characterizing the IED.

We evaluated the performance of each feature for predicting SOZ channels independently from wake and sleep data using a leave-one-subject-out cross-validation scheme (LOOCV). For each LOOCV training set, we trained a simple Linear Discriminate Analysis (LDA) model to discriminate SOZ from non-SOZ with likelihood cutoffs optimized via 10-fold subject-level cross-validation and Bayesian optimization. Since each training set was imbalanced due to SOZ channels being a minority class; we randomly under-sampled the majority class (non-SOZ channels) in each training subject and repeated this process 10 times using newly under-sampled training data to create a bagging LDA classifier. After that, we employed the optimized likelihood cutoffs to fit a new bagging LDA classifier on the entire training data with majority class under-sampling. This trained model was then used to infer the channel labels of the testing set (the left-out subject), subject-level binary classification metrics were computed, and this entire process was repeated for each left-out subject, i.e., for each LOOCV fold.

Results

Performance metrics included balanced accuracy, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC), and F1-score. We defined the best REN feature from the four REN variants, three different PDF estimation methods, and across the nine frequency bands by using performance metrics from their training set and by choosing the highest median. After defining the best REN feature, performance metrics from the testing set were compared between the best REN features and event-based markers, as shown in Fig 1, using Wilcoxon rank sum test and Cliff’s delta (δ). P-values were corrected via the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure.

With respect to balanced accuracy, the best REN feature was the 10-bin JD REN at 80-160 Hz for the wake state and 100-bin DD REN at 80-160 Hz for the sleep state. Comparison of the best REN features and event-based markers did not detect any significant difference in either vigilance state (median (IQR) balanced accuracy: 0.63 (0.23) vs. 0.53-0.60 (0.15-0.29) in wake state and 0.69 (0.17) vs. 0.66-0.71 (0.19-0.30) in sleep state, respectively; Cliff's delta for the different comparison -0.06 < δ < 0.30 in both states). Continuing with AUROC, the best REN feature was 10-bin DD at 80-160 Hz for both states. There were no statistical differences compared to event-based markers in either state (median (IQR) AUROC scores: 0.76 (0.26) vs. 0.65-0.78 (0.23-0.36) in wake state and 0.69 (0.16) vs. 0.66-0.71 (0.19-0.30) in sleep state, respectively; Cliff's delta for the different comparison -0.07 < δ < 0.18 in both states). Furthermore, the best REN features based on the AUPRC were the KDE JSD at 80-160 Hz for wake state and the 10-bin DD at 80-160 Hz for sleep state. The difference between the best REN features and event-based markers features was not statistically significant in either state (median (IQR) AUPRC scores: 0.42 (0.44) vs. 0.34-0.42 (0.47-0.72) in wake state and 0.53 (0.36) vs. 0.45-0.54 (0.43-0.48) in sleep state, respectively; Cliff’s delta for the different comparison -0.07 < δ < 0.07). Finally, for F1-score, the best REN features were the 10-bin DD REN at 60-80 Hz for wake state and the 100-bin DD REN at 80-160 Hz for sleep. Evaluation between the best REN features and event-based markers in both vigilance states was not able to detect any significant difference (median (IQR) F1-scores: 0.36 (0.30) vs. 0.04-0.34 (0.46-0.62) in wake state and 0.41 (0.26) vs. 0.40-0.46 (0.38-0.61), respectively; Cliff’s delta for different comparison -0.07 < δ < 0.35 in both states).

Conclusion

This study provides a systematic evaluation of multiple REN variants, calculated from sleep and wake SEEG data, to assess their capacity in localizing the SOZ. We showed that the best REN features are in fact equivalent to the event-based markers in their abilities to identify the SOZ. The most effective probability density functions were typically estimated by the 10-bin and 100-bin histograms, and the best performing REN variant was generally the direction-dependent REN. We also found that the best performing REN was consistently calculated for the neural activity at 80-160 Hz, indicating that with lower sampling rates, one can still maintain the standard performance level of the event-based markers, some of which require higher sampling rate. Such robustness enables the inclusion of patient data acquired at lower sampling frequencies (~500 Hz), thereby promoting scientific collaboration and facilitating large-scale multi-center studies. Nonetheless, our results also indicate that REN or event-based markers used in isolation provide only moderate discriminative power. Consequently, combining REN at specific frequency bands with event-based markers should be further studied to enhance SOZ localization performance.
