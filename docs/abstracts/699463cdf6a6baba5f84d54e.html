Introduction

Object manipulation requires continuous adjustments of motor commands based on an object’s weight and other physical properties. Prediction play a crucial role in this process, enabling the motor system to generate appropriate grip and load forces. Although the human primary motor cortex (M1) is known to contribute to the generation of motor commands, it remains unclear how predictive signals are represented and integrated across its distinct cortical layers. Given that each M1 layer exhibits specialized input–output functions (Huber, L., et al 2017), prediction-related processing may be differentially distributed across the layers. In this study, we acquired high-resolution (0.68 mm) fMRI at 7T while participants performed a predictive object manipulation task, allowing us to identify depth-dependent activation patterns associated with hand motor control.

Methods

Twelve participants performed four inverted T-shaped object manipulation tasks. Two mass weights (300 g and 400 g) were placed in one of three locations. As shown in Figure 1A, in the Predictable Random Mass task (PRM), a visual cue indicated the upcoming mass location, enabling prediction. In the Unpredictable Random Mass task (URM), the cue did not correspond to the actual location. The Predictable Center Mass task (PCM) used only the center position, and the No Mass task (NM) involved lifting the object without additional mass. Layer-specific 7T fMRI data were acquired following procedures in Huber et al. (2017), using interleaved BOLD and VASO sequences (TR = 3570 ms). The nominal resolution was 0.68 mm in-plane with 1.2-mm slices. Preprocessing and GLM analyses were conducted using AFNI and FSL, and laminar analyses were performed with LayNii (Huber et al., 2021). The study was approved by the ethics committees of Okayama University and the National Institute for Physiological Sciences.

Results

Task-induced fMRI signal changes in M1 were observed in all participants. Figure 1B shows the averaged layer-specific VASO profiles for the four tasks under both weight conditions. All tasks involving a mass exhibited stronger activation in superficial layers compared with the NM task, for both 300 g and 400 g conditions. However, no clear weight-dependent differences were observed across layers. Furthermore, the PRM task elicited significantly stronger superficial-layer activation than the URM and PCM tasks.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that object manipulation involving a mass engages superficial layers of the human M1, regardless of weight magnitude. This consistent superficial layer activation across mass conditions indicates that the presence of a load, rather than its specific magnitude, is the primary factor driving responses in M1. Importantly, predictive object manipulation (PRM task) elicited the strongest superficial-layer activation, exceeding both unpredictable and nonpredictive conditions. These results suggest that predictive processing during object manipulation is represented by enhanced activity in superficial cortical layers, highlighting the layer-specific contribution of M1 to motor control.
