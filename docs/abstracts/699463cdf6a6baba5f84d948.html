Introduction

The hierarchical organization of pain processing remains a subject of ongoing debate. Unlike other sensory modalities, pain lacks a singular primary sensory cortex. One key cortical target of nociceptive input is the posterior insula. In the Embodied Predictive Interoceptive Coding (EPIC) model1, Barrett and Simmons build on anatomical work by Barbas and colleagues describing the hierarchical, layer-dependent cytoarchitecture of the insula2. In this framework, cytoarchitecturally complex granular regions of the posterior insula form reciprocal feedforward and feedback connections with granular regions of the anterior insula. These interactions follow a laminar profile, with superficial layers of the posterior insula preferentially connecting to deep layers of the anterior insula. While this model is grounded in extensive tracer studies in non-human primates, its functional implementation in humans has yet to be demonstrated. Here, we leverage two high-resolution functional MRI datasets to map this circuitry during resting state and in response to evoked thermal pain.

Methods

Two datasets were used in this study. The first dataset, originally acquired to optimize whole-brain VASO functional imaging3, comprised approximately 20 hours of fMRI data collected from a single participant during a naturalistic movie-watching paradigm at 0.84 mm isotropic resolution. The cortical ribbon was parcellated into 11 layers, and layer-specific functional connectivity was computed between the hypergranular posterior insula (pINS) and the ventral agranular anterior insula (aINS), as defined by the Brainnetome atlas, using AFNI. The second dataset included five participants who each completed four experimental visits involving repeated painful thermal stimulation during a pain-learning task. For this dataset, 270–285 minutes of high-resolution 2D-EPI BOLD data were acquired per participant at 0.8 mm isotropic resolution.

Results

Layer-dependent functional connectivity analyses in the first dataset revealed significant coupling between superficial layers of the posterior insula (pINS) and deep layers of the anterior insula (aINS) (r = 0.12–0.32, p < 0.0001; Fig. 1). In contrast, connectivity from superficial layers of the aINS to deep layers of the pINS was weak or non-significant (r = −0.02–0.07, p = 0.007–0.97), indicating a marked asymmetry in laminar interactions. In the second dataset, pain-evoked activation maps demonstrated significant activation of the middle and superficial layers of the pINS and the deep layers of the aINS during thermal stimulation, surviving false discovery rate (FDR) correction (p < 0.01).

Conclusion

In the first dataset, we identified functional correlates of the hypothesized layer-dependent insular architecture, characterized by stronger functional connectivity between superficial layers of the posterior insula (pINS) and deep layers of the anterior insula (aINS) relative to the converse pattern. In a second, independent dataset, we observed robust activation of these regions in response to experimentally applied thermal pain, supporting their established role in pain-related processing. Future work will extend these analyses in the second dataset to examine how feedback signalling from the aINS relates to pain learning and within individual variability in subjective pain ratings.
