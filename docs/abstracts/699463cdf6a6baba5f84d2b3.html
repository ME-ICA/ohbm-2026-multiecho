Introduction

The differential diagnosis of neurodegenerative dementias is inherently challenging because of substantial overlap in clinical manifestations and similarities in structural neuroimaging findings. To improve diagnostic efficiency and accuracy, deep learning–based methods such as Convolutional Neural Networks have been proposed for the automatic classification of brain MRIs. However, despite their strong predictive performance, these models find limited clinical utility due to their opaque decision-making processes. In this work, we aim to leverage reasoning-capable large language models (LLMs) to generate rationales that inform and support the diagnostic process, directly addressing key limitations of prior methods.

We propose a framework that integrates two core components to enhance diagnostic transparency. First, we introduce a modular pipeline for converting 3D T1-weighted brain MRIs into textual radiology reports. Employing specialized brain-parcellation deep-learning models such as AssemblyNet^1, together with statistical analyses based on normative modeling^2, the pipeline generates reports that capture clinically relevant features. Second, we explore the potential of modern LLMs to assist clinicians in the differential diagnosis between Frontotemporal dementia subtypes—behavioral variant Frontotemporal Dementia (bvFTD), semantic variant Primary Progressive Aphasia (svPPA), and nonfluent variant Primary Progressive Aphasia (nfvPPA)—as well as Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and normal aging (CN), based on the generated reports. 

Unlike post-hoc explainability methods that retrospectively justify model decisions, our framework generates diagnostic rationales as part of the inference process—producing causally grounded explanations that inform and guide the model’s decision-making process. In doing so, our framework demonstrates competitive diagnostic performance relative to classification-only methods, while offering rationales that support its diagnostic conclusions.

Methods

To support neuroimaging-based diagnostic reasoning by LLMs, we propose a modular pipeline that converts T1-weighted 3D MRI scans into structured textual radiology reports. The reports describe clinically relevant neuroanatomical features—such as regional atrophy patterns and hemispheric asymmetries—using semantically interpretable terms (e.g., hippocampal atrophy). In contrast to end-to-end deep learning models, which depend on large paired datasets and may fail to capture subtle volumetric changes critical for differential diagnosis, our approach combines established high-resolution deep learning–based parcellation methods with normative statistical modeling, preserving clinically meaningful detail while enabling interpretable and verifiable intermediate results. 

The pipeline consists of four main stages: (1) Fine-grained brain parcellation: anatomical segmentation is performed using AssemblyNet^1, a state-of-the-art framework based on a multiscale ensemble of 3D U-Net models. (2) Quantitative volumetric report: absolute regional volumes are computed from voxel-wise segmentations and normalized by total intracranial volume (ICV) to yield relative volume ratios, enabling comparison across subjects while accounting for inter-individual differences in brain size. (3) Atrophy estimation: clinical relevance is assessed by comparing individual volume ratios to age- and sex-adjusted normative models derived from a large lifespan dataset^2, comprising 2,944 cognitive normal participants aged 9 months to 94 years. For each structure, we compute a Structural Deviation Score (SDS), defined as the number of standard deviations from the expected normative value. Negative SDS indicates atrophy, whereas positive SDS indicates enlargement. (4) Quantitative to qualitative severity mapping: SDS values are translated into a textual radiology report using a seven-point severity scale ranging from normal to severe. Findings are hierarchically organized by anatomical domain (cortical, subcortical, ventricular) and ranked by severity. For bilateral structures, both global and asymmetric volume changes are evaluated, with hemisphere-specific findings explicitly reported to support the identification of lateralized disease patterns.

The resulting structured radiology reports are subsequently provided as input to an LLM for diagnostic reasoning. We adopt a prompting strategy designed to elicit open-ended and comprehensive clinical interpretation of the neuroimaging findings. Specifically, the model is instructed to assume the role of a neurologist with expertise in neurodegenerative diseases and to interpret the T1-weighted MRI radiology report accordingly. To promote careful engagement with the imaging-derived features, the prompt explicitly requires the model to reason exhaustively before producing a final diagnosis. This intermediate reasoning step encourages systematic evaluation of regional atrophy patterns, hemispheric asymmetries, and structural deviations described in the report. The final output is structured as a ranked list of differential diagnoses, reflecting the clinical diagnostic process in which multiple candidate conditions are considered and prioritized based on their concordance with the observed neuroimaging evidence.

We evaluate the zero-shot diagnostic performance of a LLaMA-3.3 model with 70B parameters, a large-scale generalist language model, on the task of differential diagnosis among CN, AD, and frontotemporal dementia subtypes (bvFTD, nfvPPA, svPPA). We construct a robust and diverse evaluation cohort by aggregating T1-weighted MRI scans from three complementary datasets: the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI)^3, the Neuroimaging Initiative for Frontotemporal Lobar Degeneration (FTLDNI)^4, and the ARTFL-LEFFTDS Longitudinal Frontotemporal Lobar Degeneration (ALLFTD)^5. Results

In Figure 2, we report class-wise specificity, sensitivity, and balanced accuracy (BACC) for the zero-shot diagnostic performance of the evaluated LLaMA-70B model based on textual radiology reports automatically generated from T1-weighted MRI using our pipeline. For comparison, we also report the performance of an SVM trained using 10-fold cross-validation on continuous Structural Deviation Scores (SDS), as described in the Methods section. Notably, the SVM is trained and operates directly on continuous features, whereas the LLM is evaluated in a zero-shot setting using textual radiology reports in which SDS values are discretized into qualitative severity categories (e.g., mild, moderate, severe).

Despite this difference, the LLaMA model achieves a BACC only five percentage points lower than the SVM. This result supports several aspects of the proposed framework: (i) the automatically generated reports effectively capture clinically relevant neuroanatomical information; (ii) their distribution and terminology are consistent with real-world radiology reports likely encountered during the LLM’s pre-training; and (iii) LLaMA, despite being a generalist and non-specialized medical model, possesses sufficient pre-trained clinical knowledge to interpret neuroimaging-derived findings and use them to guide its diagnostic reasoning in a meaningful way.

This behavior is further illustrated by the diagnostic reasoning example shown in Figure 2 (excerpted for brevity). In this case, the model correctly analyzes the atrophy patterns from the automatically generated T1-weighted MRI report and conducts an explicit differential diagnosis, ultimately producing a ranked list of candidate conditions. The model motivates each diagnosis by linking specific atrophy patterns to known disease phenotypes and explicitly expresses uncertainty by assigning relative probabilities to each class.

While the zero-shot performance of a generalist LLM based on T1-weighted MRI findings alone is already notable, it is important to emphasize that clinical diagnosis in practice relies on additional information beyond structural imaging. Patient history and standardized neuropsychological evaluation often provide critical disambiguation, particularly in cases with widespread or overlapping atrophy affecting multiple cognitive domains (e.g., behavior and language). Consistent with this, the higher sensitivity@2 metric—indicating whether the ground-truth diagnosis appears among the top two predictions—reflects the model’s uncertainty in selecting a single most likely diagnosis and mirrors the diagnostic overlap commonly encountered in real clinical settings. 

Finally, beyond raw classification performance, the explicit diagnostic reasoning generated by the LLM provides greater transparency into the rationale underlying its predictions. By explicitly linking neuroanatomical findings to candidate diagnoses, the model offers clinicians a concrete basis for assessing the plausibility and trustworthiness of its outputs. In contrast, conventional black-box classifiers such as SVMs or end-to-end vision models typically rely on post hoc explainability techniques that produce visual saliency or attention maps over MRI scans. While these methods highlight regions that influence the model’s output, they lack semantic attribution: they do not explicitly identify anatomical structures (e.g., the hippocampus) nor explain their clinical relevance (e.g., why hippocampal atrophy supports a diagnosis of Alzheimer’s disease). Moreover, such explanations are generated after the prediction and do not participate in the decision-making process itself, limiting their ability to provide causally grounded justifications.

Conclusion

We proposed a framework for the differential diagnosis of neurodegenerative dementias that combines neuroimaging-derived evidence with the reasoning capabilities of LLMs. By converting T1-weighted MRI scans into structured textual radiology reports using fine-grained parcellation and normative modeling, our approach enables semantically grounded diagnostic reasoning that links regional atrophy patterns to disease phenotypes and produces ranked differential diagnoses with explicit rationales. We evaluated the framework on a large, heterogeneous cohort using a five-class differential diagnosis setting. Despite operating in a zero-shot setting, a generalist LLM achieves diagnostic performance comparable to a supervised SVM trained on continuous features, while providing substantially greater explainability. Unlike conventional classifiers that rely on post hoc explainability, our framework generates causally grounded, semantically rich diagnostic explanations as part of the inference process, enhancing transparency and clinical trust.

Future work will extend this framework by evaluating a wider range of language models and integrating complementary clinical information, such as neuropsychological assessments and longitudinal data, which are essential for real-world diagnostic decision-making. Overall, our results highlight the potential of combining interpretable neuroimaging pipelines with reasoning-capable large language models to move beyond black-box prediction toward more transparent and diagnostically informative AI systems for neurodegenerative disease assessment.
