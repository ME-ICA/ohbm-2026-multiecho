Introduction

Cerebral cortical thickness generally varies between 2-4 mm and is a marker of neurodevelopmental and degenerative disorders. Volume-based analysis methods are widely used to evaluate cortical thickness estimates, but they suffer from partial‑volume effects and resolution limits that limit their precision. Surface‑based methods improve precision due to a significantly increased spatial resolution but are thus computationally expensive and use Euclidean distances, which can often fail to map homologous points across surfaces.1 Tracing streamlines between surfaces according to Laplace’s equation (∇2φ=0), provides orthogonality and one‑to‑one correspondence, but precision is again limited by voxel size.2 To address these limitations, we developed a surface‑based cortical thickness estimator that implements the Laplace equation directly on local pial and white surface patches using a boundary element method (BEM). Our aims were to (i) construct a closed cortical patch from geodesic discs on the pial and white surfaces and solve the Laplace boundary‑value problem with Dirichlet conditions on pial and white boundaries and a Neumann condition on the side wall, (ii) trace streamlines between surfaces and define cortical thickness as cumulative path length, and (iii) validate the approach against different morphological structures with respect to two‑way reciprocity, agreement with ground‑truth thickness and robustness to surface perturbations.

Methods

We formulated cortical thickness as the streamline length following the gradient of a potential field between the pial and white surfaces. For a given pial vertex, a geodesic disc was extracted on the pial mesh, and a corresponding patch was segmented on the white mesh. A side wall was constructed by connecting the boundary loops to obtain a closed cortical patch. The grey matter domain was modelled as Ω, bounded by pial (Dirichlet φ = 1), white (φ = 0) and side (Neumann ∂φ/∂n = 0) surfaces

A boundary element method (BEM) was used to solve the boundary integral equation on the triangulated surfaces.3 Constant flux density was assumed per triangle and boundary integrals were evaluated analytically. Near-singular interactions were handled by adaptive recursive subdivision with error tolerance 10-10. The resulting flux solution enabled evaluation of φ and ∇φ at arbitrary points within Ω. 

Thickness paths were computed by first jumping inward to Ω normal to the pial surface to reduce near-singularly gradient calculation error. The process then involved following the gradient streamlines through Ω, iterating xk+1 = xk - α∇φ(xk) with step size α = 0.05 mm, with the BEM facilitating a direct calculation of ∇φ throughout Ω. An angle-change guard limited abrupt changes ≥30º in direction to protect against to near-singularity errors by the boundary. Tracing ended on intersection with the white surface. Cortical thickness at the pial origin was defined as the cumulative length of the streamline. Thicknesses are evaluated in the central 50% of geodesic area of each patch, to protect against streamlines veering close to the side walls.

The data used included twelve real cortical patches (geodesic radius 20 mm), generated using Freesurfer surface reconstruction on T1-weighted magnetic-resonance images of 1 mm isotropic resolution from one patient.1 In addition, we tested on fifty synthetic cortical patches (radius 20 mm) were generated by normally shifting grey‑mid vertices +-h/2 mm to produce known thicknesses h ∈ {0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4}. Analytic data comprised of five disks and five hemispherical shells with radii and thicknesses equal to that of the synthetic cortical patches.

Across experiments, performance was evaluated against pre-specified acceptance tests. For our two-way reciprocity test (Test 1), a sampled streamline was considered to satisfy reciprocity if the pial to white to pial return geodesic displacement d was less than 0.05 mm and the forward-to-reverse thickness asymmetry met <5%; in addition, we report the proportion of paths with asymmetry <1% to demonstrate rare deviations. 

For ground-truth accuracy (Test 2), agreement was assessed by absolute thickness error ε with target ε < 0.05 mm and by Bland-Altman analysis; Bland–Altman plots were interpreted as acceptable when differences were tightly centred with negligible bias, had narrow limits of agreement, and had no proportional bias across the tested thickness range. 

For robustness (Test 3), perturbations used vertex-wise Gaussian normal displacements across σ ∈ {0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.8} as a proportion of mean edge length and were applied to the twelve real cortical patches. Robustness was considered acceptable when valid-path (passed Test 1) percentage remained stable for slightly perturbed meshes retaining good element shape quality, evaluated using PyVista’s shape_quality function.4 When valid-path proportion began to deteriorate, the corresponding shape quality was deemed too significant, and users are advised to avoid using data of this quality level or lower. 

Thickness heatmaps enabled precise visualisation of local thickness variation over pial patches (Figure 1). A shifted hemispherical annulus experiment was used specifically to test whether the local maximum remained clearly defined and was approached evenly from all directions. Results

Two-way reciprocity (Test 1): Across 6,118 traced paths spanning synthetic patches (n = 4,055), analytic hemispheres (n = 600), analytic disks (n = 510), and real cortical patches (n = 953), 100% met the start–return geodesic test (Geo < 0.05 mm) and 100% met the asymmetry <5% test (Table 1). For synthetic and analytic geometries, 100% of paths also achieved asymmetry <1%. For real cortical patches, 99.48% of paths achieved asymmetry <1%; per-patch p95 asym ranged 0.08–0.61%, indicating rare but small deviations well below the 5% acceptance test.

Ground-truth accuracy (Test 2): Bland–Altman analysis showed tight agreement with known ground-truth thickness. For analytic geometries, the mean bias was 0.001 mm with limits of agreement [0.000, 0.001] mm; for synthetic cortical patches, the mean bias was 0.001 mm with limits of agreement [-0.003, 0.004] mm. There was however some visible proportionality of error to the degree of thickness. We attribute this to the ground-truth thickness being defined along a straight normal displacement whereas the Laplace streamlines are curved throughout the domain. This is an effect that scales with patch thickness. Overall, these findings confirm high accuracy and precision against ground truth across tested geometries in the sense required by the pre-specified endpoints.

Robustness to perturbations (Test 3): Vertex-wise Gaussian normal perturbations (0.05-0.8 × mean edge length) applied to the twelve real cortical patches demonstrated that mesh-quality metrics provide a practical acceptance screen. Valid-path proportion (paths passing Test 1) and thickness errors were stable for mildly perturbed meshes that retained good triangle shape quality but deteriorated as perturbation increased and element quality degraded. This supports our screening rule: require triangle-wise shape_quality mean >0.9 and coefficient of variation <0.1 for reliable results. All analysis of real cortex used 20 mm geodesic radius patches. However, larger radii are feasible at increased computational cost. Our method, results and data can be used and analysed at our git repository: github.com/AlastairSnell/laplace_thickness_release.

Conclusion

We present a surface-based Laplace cortical thickness estimator that operates on local cortical patches using a boundary element formulation. By solving a mixed-boundary Laplace problem directly on triangulated patch surfaces, the method yields a framework in which field gradients can be evaluated anywhere in the grey-matter domain. Following these gradient streamlines provides orthogonal, one-to-one trajectories between boundaries, defining thickness as cumulative streamline length. Validation across analytic geometries, synthetic cortical patches with imposed ground truth, real cortical patches, and perturbed versions of these real patches, demonstrated two-way reciprocity across trajectories with ground-truth agreement and robustness to noise. Path validity was stable when mesh quality remained high, and a practical recommendation provides an actionable threshold for excluding unreliable patches in future research. Together, these results support the use of patch-level BEM-Laplace thickness mapping for sensitive morphological analysis, particularly in highly convoluted cortical folds. Future work will focus on extending the method to whole-brain coverage, incorporating additional surface-based morphological features, and developing more sophisticated visualisation tools to interpret our analysis.
