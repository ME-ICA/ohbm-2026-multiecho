Introduction

Diffusion-tensor imaging (DTI) is a viable source of data on brain structural organization on macroscopic scale. DTI tractography methods deliver weighted connectomes, which can be processed with advanced graph theory and topological methods, uncovering global principles along which networks in human brain are organized and providing insights into information-processing streamlines. The key macro-structural features to consider in this context are cliques, being densely interconnected sets of regions allegedly responsible for intensive specialized processing, and loops, defined through topological cavities – uninterrupted closed cycles of connections with no shortcuts – which can be attributed to broadband pathways delivering information between the specialized modules.^1

The key aim of current investigation was to understand the degree of interaction between structures on node and clique level, namely, are nodes of the cliques more likely to participate in persistent loops, and which properties a clique is likely to exhibit to be connected to a loop. Furthermore, we aimed to assess the location of the cliques of these properties within connectome to determine the regions and resting-state functional networks (RSNs)^2 that house more of mentioned cliques.

In the current study we apply clique detection and persistent homology to DTI-based connectomes from a large cohort of individuals. For each subject, we identify maximal cliques, map them to RSNs and anatomical regions, and quantify their structural properties. In parallel, we construct topological filtrations in order to identify one-dimensional persistent features in the connectome (so-called persistent loops). Finally, we investigate how these higher-order structures interact by relating node participation in maximal cliques and loops, and by quantifying the extent to which maximal cliques are incident to persistent loops, providing a joint description of “solid” and “hollow” motifs in the connectome.

Methods

I. Data acquisition and connectome construction

A MRI scans were obtained for a cohort of subjects (N=803) using 3T Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma scanner with a 64-channel RF coil. Diffusion-weighted images were obtained using a multi-shell acquisition scheme with b-values of 1000, 2000, 4000 s/mm2, which were applied in 180 directions each, along with 6 B0 images for each shell. The scans were acquired using a spin-echo EPI protocol with TR/TE = 3500/94 ms and  a resolution of 1.6 mm3 isotropic. 

Diffusion images were corrected for movement and eddy-current distortions with FSL, and MD maps were extracted using DIPY. Multi-tissue DTI tractography was performed by the means of MRtrix3, and connectivity matrices were constructed based on Brainnetome atlas^3 weighted by the number of streamlines.

II. Clique detection

For each subject, the structural connectome was represented as a weighted, undirected graph G=(V,E), where nodes corresponded to anatomical regions and edges to tractography-derived connections. All maximal cliques C ⊆ V were enumerated with a Bron-Kerbosch-type algorithm.

For a clique C of size s = |C|, the average degree in the clique is then vol(C)/s. Boundary edges were defined as

δ(C) = {{vi,vj} ∈ E : vi ∈ C, vj ∉ C},

with number of boundary edges |δ(C)|.

Embeddedness of a node v in clique C was computed as degC(v)/deg(v), where degC(v) is the number of neighbors of v inside C, and deg(v) is degree of node v in G. Average clique embeddedness was defined as

Emb(C) = 1/s ∑v∈C degC(v)/deg(v)

II. Persistence analysis

To characterize topological structures in the connectomes, we applied a weight rank clique filtration based on a Vietoris-Rips complex. For each subject, edge weights were converted to distances and normalized to [0,1]. Edges were added in order of increasing distance: at filtration value f, all edges with distance ≤f were present, and a simplex entered the complex once all of its edges had appeared. This yielded a nested sequence of simplical complexes on which we computed persistent homology in dimensions 1 to obtain the H1 classes (loops).

For each homology class we recorded its birth ρb and death ρd, defined as the filtration values at which the class appeared and was filled in. From these we derived the lifetime value l = ρd-ρb. To identify nodes comprising a minimal loop representative for each H1 class, we extracted the 1-skeleton at ρb, consisting of all edges with distance ≤ρb+ε.

III. Loop-clique interaction

At this point, we have comprehensively identified and localized every maximal clique and persistent loop in the connectome of every subject (see Fig. 1 for illustration), rendering them ready for the statistical analysis. At the node level we computed a loop participation count nloops, defined as the number of minimal H1 classes containing a given node, as well as clique participation ncliques, as the number of maximal cliques containing given node.

To quantify the association between clique and loop participation, we used a subject-wise correlation approach. For each subject, we computed Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient ρ between node-wise loop and clique participation across all regions. The resulting correlations were Fisher z-transformed, and subjected to a one-sample t-test against zero, treating subjects as the unit of inference. We report the group-level effect as the mean Fisher z, its back-transformed correlation ρ with 95% confidence interval, and Cohen's d relative to zero.

At clique level, we computed the loop incidence metric Cloops for every clique, which is a number of H1 loops, adjacent to given clique by ≥1 node. To assess the significance of the differences in clique measures across regions and RSNs we used one-way ANOVA and Tukey test for post-hoc pairwise comparisons when ANOVA was significant (p < 0.05). The interaction between Cloops value and graph theory based clique measures was estimated through pairwise Spearman rank correlations.

Results

I. Cliques, which are connected by loops, exhibit distinctive features and are unevenly distributed across connectome.

Across subjects, the number of loops incident to a clique was only weakly related to clique size (ρ=0.07, p<10-16) but showed a stronger positive association with the number of boundary edges (ρ=0.22, p<10-16) and a negative association with average node embeddedness (ρ=-0.14, p<10-16). Thus, loops preferentially involved cliques that were highly connected to the rest of the graph rather than particularly large or internally dense. Spatially, cliques with highest boundary connectivity were mostly found in subcortical (μ|δ(C)|=465.94, occurring in n=771 subjects) and visual (μ|δ(C)|=421.38, n=421) Yeo networks and in Frontal (μ|δ(C)|=450.07$, n=667) and Occipital (μ|δ(C)|=416.86, n=560) lobes and subcortical nuclei (μ|δ(C)|=464.42, n=764), with comparatively lower boundary connectivity in temporal and parietal regions. 

Post-hoc comparisons on clique boundary edges confirmed that subcortical cliques had significantly higher boundary connectivity than default, frontoparietal, limbic, dorsal attention, somatomotor and visual networks, and that subcortical nuclei exceeded all cortical lobes (all pairwise p<0.001), while the reverse relation was proven significant for average embeddedness. Frontal and occipital lobes also showed higher boundary connectivity than parietal cortex, while subcortical nuclei were the most prominent in this measure. Together, these results indicate that topological cycles tend to arise at the interfaces between densely interconnected regions, such as subcortex where cliques have many external connections but only moderate internal embeddedness. Furthermore, we shall point out the visual processing-related regions in the occipital lobe, as well as across the rest of the visual resting-state network, which exhibit a higher concentration of boundary-rich cliques connected by persistent loops to subcortical areas.

II. Nodes participating in cliques are likely to participate in loops.

Across all subjects, node-wise clique and loop participation were positively associated. Subject-wise Spearman correlations ρ (between ncliques and nloops were obtained. The distribution of ρ values was unimodal and clearly shifted above zero (mean ρ=0.18, SD=0.08, median=0.18, range [-0.108,0.426]), with only a small number of near-zero or negative correlations (Figure 2, bottom right panel). Most subjects had correlations in the range from 0.05 to 0.30, indicating a consistent though moderate association. Fisher z-transformed correlations showed a similar pattern, with mean z=0.18 (SD=0.08) and a narrow spread around this value (Figure 2, bottom left panel), while a Q-Q plot of z against the theoretical normal distribution indicated approximate normality.

For group-level inference we tested the null hypothesis of zero mean Fisher z using a one-sample t-test. The result was highly significant: t(802)=62.27, p<10-16. This indicates that the average correlation across subjects is reliably positive. Back-transforming to the correlation scale yielded a group-level mean ρ¯=0.180 with 95% confidence interval [0.174, 0.185]. The associated effect size was large (Cohen's d=2.20 relative to zero). Together, these results show that, across individuals, nodes that participate in many maximal cliques also tend to participate in many persistent loops, and that this relationship is consistent in magnitude across the sample.

Conclusion

Sizemore et al. showed that structural brain networks contain large cliques supporting rapid, localized processing and loop-like cavities that form long-range pathways linking evolutionarily diverse regions.^1 The present work advances this picture from small group-averaged samples to a large DTI cohort, quantifying how clique architecture and persistent loops co-occur in individual connectomes and at the level of functional systems. By combining node-wise clique participation, clique-level graph theory statistics, and explicit minimal-cycle reconstruction, we were able to characterize how “solid” (maximal cliques) and “hollow” (persistent loops) motifs are spatially arranged in the human structural connectome.

The analyses demonstrate that loops preferentially engage cliques with high boundary connectivity and moderate embeddedness, rather than particularly large or internally dense cliques. Correlations between loop incidence and clique measures were strongest for the number of boundary edges and negative for embeddedness, whereas clique size showed only a weak association. One-way ANOVAs across Yeo networks and lobes further indicated that boundary-rich cliques are disproportionately concentrated in subcortical nuclei and in frontal and occipital cortices, with comparatively lower boundary connectivity in temporal and parietal regions. Together, these results indicate that topological cycles tend to arise at the interfaces between densely interconnected regions, such as subcortex, where cliques have many external connections but only moderate internal embeddedness.

It's interesting no note the higher concentration of loop-adjacent cliques in visual processing systems. Visual resting-state network regions in the occipital lobe, as well as visual association areas, exhibit a higher concentration of boundary-rich cliques that are frequently connected by persistent loops to subcortical structures. Thus, subcortico-visual pathways emerge as prominent carriers of topological cycles, consistent with the idea that higher-order structure is organized along hierarchical sensory-subcortical axes.^4 This pattern complements recent findings that overlapping high-order clique clusters can act as structural boundaries between interacting functional subnetworks in human connectomes.^5 Together, these observations support a view in which cliques and loops jointly organize the interface between local and global connectivity in structural brain networks, balancing segregation and integration.

The robust positive association between node-wise clique and loop participation across more than 800 individuals suggests that a subset of regions systematically multiplexes local and long-range roles, functioning as higher-order hubs in the sense of recent higher-order connectomics work. Such regions simultaneously participate in many maximal cliques and in many persistent cycles, positioning them to influence both within-module processing and inter-module communication. 

By explicitly linking graph-theoretic clique measures, persistent homology, and large-cohort variability, the study provides a scalable template for incorporating topological signatures into structural connectome analyses and for relating these signatures to functional and clinical phenotypes in future work.
