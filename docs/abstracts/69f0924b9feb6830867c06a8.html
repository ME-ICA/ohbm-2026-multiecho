Introduction

Myelin may represent a modifiable treatment target in neurodevelopmental disorders, yet accessible in vivo tools for assessing myelin alterations in clinical settings are limited. Prior work suggests standard MRI scans, T1-weighted (T1w) and T2-weighted (T2w), may be used to compute a ratio (T1w/T2w) that provides a reasonable estimate of cortical myelin content.1 While the T1w/T2w ratio may be less specific than alternative in vivo myelin mapping techniques, the T1w/T2w ratio can be derived from clinical MRI sequences and requires fairly minimal postprocessing, making it an ideal option for clinical translation. 

Neurodevelopmental disorders including autism and attention-deficit/-hyperactivity disorder are among those with suspected abnormalities in brain microstructure and myelination. However, progress in understanding these conditions has been limited by substantial heterogeneity in etiology. In this study, we focused on disorders caused by single germline variants in the RAS-extracellular signal-regulated mitogen-activated protein kinase (RAS-MAPK) signaling pathway. These conditions, collectively known as RASopathies, offer a unique opportunity to study neurobiological mechanisms within a genetically defined population. Preclinical work shows that genetic variants causing RASopathies upregulate the RAS-MAPK pathway and lead to downstream cellular effects including disrupted oligodendrocyte maturation and fewer myelinated axons.2 

Thus in this study our primary aim was to investigate potential alterations in the T1w/T2w ratio, a putative marker of cortical myelin patterns, in children with RASopathies compared to typically developing (TD) peers. As an exploratory aim, we examined whether cortical T1w/T2w ratios were associated with physical health. This exploratory aim was motivated by links between myelin and motor difficulties in other conditions (e.g., multiple sclerosis3), and because physical health is a major concern in RASopathies.4

Methods

Participants

Participants with RASopathies were recruited from across the United States and Canada, while TD participants were recruited from the San Francisco Bay Area. Proof of genetic testing was required for individuals with RASopathies to demonstrate the presence of appropriate mutations. The inclusion criteria for all participants with RASopathies are as follows: a) age from 5 years, 0 months to 17 years, 11 months; and b) gestational age > 34 weeks. TD participants were group-matched to participants with RASopathies based on age, sex, handedness, socioeconomic status, pubertal status, and ethnicity. 

Physical health of participants was assessed using the Mobility and Strength Impact portions from the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS). Both portions yield T-scores (M=50, SD=10), with higher scores indicating better health. 

Imaging Protocol

Prior to the MRI, participants completed behavioral training in a mock scanner to reduce motion and anxiety during the scan. MRI data were acquired using a standard 48-channel head coil on a GE Premier 3.0 Tesla whole-body system (GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI). A whole-brain high-resolution T1-weighted magnetization-prepared rapid gradient-echo (MPRAGE) sequence was used to acquire structural T1 data with the following parameters: repetition time (TR)= 1992 ms; echo time (TE)= 2.8 ms; inversion time (TI)= 900 ms; flip angle=8o; matrix size= 240 х 240; field-of-view= 240 x 240 mm; slice thickness= 1.2 mm; 146 slices; acquisition time (TA)= 4 minutes 24 seconds. A 3D isotropic fast-spin echo sequence (CUBE) was used to acquire structural T2 data: TR= 3000 ms; TE= 100 ms; matrix size= 240 x 240; 1 mm isotropic voxel size; TA=4 minutes 58 seconds. 

Image Analysis

Given histological evidence that the T1w/T2w signal is more robust in cortex than in white matter,5 we focused our analysis on the cortical grey matter. Basic neuroscience studies show a gradient of decreasing myelination from the grey-white boundary to the cortical surface, thus we explored the T1w/T2w ratio at three depths: the grey/white matter boundary, midline, and cortical surface (Figure 1). FreeSurfer (version 7.2, http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu) was used to generate a bias-corrected T1w image and this was used as the reference. The T2w image was registered to the bias-corrected T1w image using mri_coreg and mri_convert with resampling, and both images underwent bias field correction using the ANTS software package. The T1w/T2w ratio maps were computed voxel-wise by dividing the corrected T1w image by the corrected T2w image, providing an intensity-normalized map for each subject. The T1w/T2w ratio maps were calculated at three depths of the cortical surface. Average values were extracted from 68 regions-of-interest (ROIs) using the Desikan-Killiany atlas at each cortical depth.

Statistical Analysis

ANCOVA was used to assess between-groups differences (RASopathies, TD) in the average T1w/T2w ratio at each depth (cortical surface, midline, grey/white boundary) for each of the 68 cortical regions, including age, sex, cortical thickness, curvature, and Euler number (as an index of motion) as covariates. The false discovery rate (FDR) was used to correct for multiple comparisons.

For our exploratory aim, we examined relationships with physical health outcomes using average T1w/T2w ratios from the midline of cortical regions aggregated from the Desikan-Killany atlas as follows: cingulate, frontal, insula, motor, parietal, occipital, and temporal. To assess if there was a relationship between T1w/T2w and the physical health measures, we conducted Pearson and Spearman correlations. Pearson correlations were used except in cases of non-normal residuals, in which case Spearman correlations were used. Correlations were conducted separately in each group (RASopathies, TD) and subsequently across the entire sample.

Results

Results

A total of 112 subjects (Mage=10.7, SDage=3.38, age range 6.05 – 17.8 years; 54 female, 58 male) were included in the analysis: 86 were in the RASopathies group (age range 6.05 – 17.8 years) and 26 were in the TD group (age range 6.13 – 16.8 years).

Analysis of 68 cortical regions revealed widespread reductions in the T1w/T2w ratio in RASopathies compared to TD across all three cortical depths. Although the broad spatial patterns are similar, the subjects with RASopathies show noticeably lower T1w/T2w signal across the brain compared to TD. Of the 68 regions examined, reductions in T1w/T2w were observed in 50 regions at the cortical surface, 63 at the midline (Figure 2), and 60 at the grey/white matter boundary (all pFDR<.050). At all sampling depths, T1w/T2w in the left rostral anterior cingulate (surface: d=1.01, pFDR<.001; midline: d=0.777, pFDR=.002; grey/white boundary: d=0.791, pFDR=.003) and right temporal pole (surface: d=0.850, pFDR=.001; midline: d=0.677, pFDR=.003; grey/white boundary: d=0.767, pFDR=.003) showed pronounced effects. 

Given the large number of regions with reduced T1w/T2w ratios in RASopathies compared to TD, we additionally investigated whether the difference can be explained by a uniform scaling factor. To estimate the scaling factor, we fit three linear regression models – one each for the cortical surface, midline, and grey/white matter boundary regions. In all three models, we found strong linear relationships. The models based on regional T1w/T2w from the cortical surface and from the midline both estimated slopes of 1.069 (R2=0.99, p<.001), while the model based on T1w/T2w from the grey/white matter boundary fit a slope of 1.064 (R2=0.99, p<.001). These results suggest the T1w/T2w values in the TD group are, on average, 6.4 to 6.9% higher than those in the RASopathies group. 

We tested associations between aggregated cortical T1w/T2w ratios and physical health and cognitive outcomes. The aggregated cortical T1w/T2w ratios were calculated as averages across selections of 68 cortical ROIs into the frontal, temporal, parietal, occipital, cingulate, insula, and motor regions. Analyses were first conducted separately within each group (RASopathies, TD); however, Fisher’s r-to-z transformation indicated no significant differences between the slopes, so correlations were subsequently examined in the combined sample. Significant Spearman correlations were found between all six regions and both PROMIS mobility and strength impact ratings. The most significant associations were between strength impact ratings and T1w/T2w in the motor (rs=0.330, pFDR<.001), occipital (rs=0.344, pFDR<.001), and parietal regions (rs=0.329, pFDR<.001).

Conclusion

Conclusions

Our findings suggest decreased cortical myelin in children with RASopathies. This result may be linked to the affected oligodendrocyte lineage observed in preclinical studies. Results from our exploratory aim revealed a significant relationship between the cortical T1w/T2w ratio and gross motor and strength scores across participants. Given the observational nature of this study, the direction of this relationship remains unclear. It is possible that reduced cortical myelination may contribute to less efficient neural transmission and thus poorer motor performance. Conversely, motor impairments in RASopathies could limit opportunities for activity-dependent myelination, which is known to support myelin plasticity.

While T1w/T2w is used widely as a proxy for intracortical myelin, its interpretation requires caution. The signal is influenced by other biological properties including water and iron content and does not provide a direct measure of myelin. While the present study did not include an alternative myelin-specific measure (e.g., myelin water fraction, quantitative magnetization transfer), future work could incorporate these techniques to probe the specificity of observed T1w/T2w differences. Additional limitations include our broad age range (6-17 years) and the use of parent-rated health outcomes that may not capture objective nor stable measures of functional impairments.

Ultimately our findings reveal widespread reductions in cortical myelin in RASopathies, potentially reflecting underlying disruptions in neuronal migration and oligodendrocyte development observed in preclinical models. Furthermore, the association between cortical myelin and motor functioning and stamina may point to the relevance of the T1w/T2w ratio as a biomarker for these difficulties. These results provide new insights into cortical brain development in RASopathies and highlight the need for future work to determine their clinical significance and potential for informing targeted interventions.
