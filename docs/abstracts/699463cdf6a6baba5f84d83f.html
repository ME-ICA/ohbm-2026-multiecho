Introduction

Brain activations associated with mathematical and linguistic processing arise in largely distinct cortical networks. While the language network is predominantly left-lateralized and involves regions of the temporal and frontal lobes, the mathematics network, which has been much less studied, primarily engages parietal regions and tends to be more bilaterally organized.

These regions can be put in evidence by simply contrasting brain activity evoked by listening to sentences having mathematical content (“a square has four sides”) versus sentences having non mathematical content (e.g. “the leaves fall from the trees”) (see Amalric & Dehaene, ,2016,  2019)

In the present work, we analyze an fMRI dataset where participants listened to such sentences (as well as to meaningless sentences and to lists of words). We perform an item-based analysis, that is,  compute individual maps for each sentence  (Bedny et al. 2007; Norman-Haignere et al. 2015). We find that the second level analysis performed  with sentences as the random effect are more sensitive than the standard subject-based analysis. Then, using principal component analysis, we produce maps that reveal the dimensions along which the sentences differ the most. These maps not only reveal clear clusters corresponding to the categories of sentences in the original design, but also enable an exploratory, data-driven investigation, and to look at patterns that are hidden when only looking at the subject-based analysis.

Methods

Stimuli

320 stimuli consisting of short sequences of 6 to 13 French words (average = 9.2 words) were used. They were distributed into 8 experimental conditions, the first three of which corresponding to mathematical statements, the next three to non-mathematical statements, and the last two to meaningless sentences or lists of words.

  • Calculation, e.g., “Thirty-five minus six is seventy-nine.”

  • Arithmetic, e.g., “All integers can be divided by one.”

  • Geometry, e.g., “A circle has an infinite number of axes of symmetry.”

  • General knowledge, e.g., “A canary is a small yellow songbird”

  • Contextual knowledge, e.g., “In India, cows are meat animals”

  • Social knowledge, e.g., “To Napoleon, Austerlitz is the name of a train station.”

  • Meaningless sentence, e.g.,“To lions, a sail is a pensive bottle”

  • Lists of words, e.g., “Thirst escape cucumber fast glass underground small.”

The statements expressed by the sentences (first 6 conditions) are either true or false as the task of the participants is to judge their truth status. They are instructed to respond ‘False’ to meaningless sentences and lists of words.

The stimuli are presented either visually (using word by word, rapid serial visual presentation [RSVP]) or auditorily during the fMRI experiment. The audio versions of the stimuli were recorded by a native French speaker; their average duration was 3.1s; all stimuli, including word lists, were recorded with a similar sentence-like prosody.

Participants and MRI acquisition

A total of 31 participants, all of them right-handed, male, and native French, participated in this study. The protocol was approved by the Comité de Protection des Personnes (IRB) and all participants gave their informed consent. MRI data were acquired on a 3 Tesla Siemens Prisma system equipped with a 64 channels coil (Resolution=1.75mm isotropic, TR=2.1s; TE=30ms).

Procedure

All stimuli were presented to each participant, either in auditory or visual form (RSVP: 350ms/word). The experiment consisted of 5 consecutive runs lasting about 10 minutes each. Each run featured 64 stimuli, that is, 8 from each experimental condition (4 in the visual modality and 4 in the auditory modality), randomly intermixed. The order of stimuli was randomized for each subject. The number of True (T) or False (F) statements was equalized in each category and sub-category. Stimuli were presented every 8secs. They were preceded by a warning cue, namely a red cross for written stimuli and a beep for spoken stimuli, occurring 350 ms before the stimulus, then  and followed by the display of a fixation cross at the center of the screen. 

Data analysis

Data were converted from dicom to nifti format and organized following the BIDS specification, then preprocessed using fmriprep with the default parameters. The resulting images, realigned in the MNI152 space, were analyzed using custom-made python code using the Nilearn package (http://nilearn.github.io). First, we performed a  by-subject analysis, using the 8 conditions as predictors and entering the individual maps (smoothed at 8mm) in a within-subject ANOVA. Second, we computed a by-item analysis in the following way: for each participant, design matrices were created for each sentence, with one regressor for the given sentence, and another one regrouping all other sentences (Mumford et al. 2012). This permitted us to obtain a z-map for each sentence and each subject. For each of 319 stimuli (1 item had to be discarded because of a chopped recording), we computed an average z-map by averaging the participants’ z-maps smoothed at 8mm. These item-based maps were entered in a second-level ANOVA model, with the 8 experimental conditions as regressors.  Finally, to perform the principal component analysis, we linearized the maps, obtaining a vector of size “n_voxels” for each stimulus. The input of the PCA is an array of size n_sentences X n_voxels.

Results

For both the item-based and the subject-based model, we computed the contrasts “Math vs. Language”, “Meaningless sentences vs. Lists of words” and “General knowledge vs Meaningless sentences”. Visual inspection of the corresponding z-maps show that both models yield similar maps but with higher peak z-scores and larger extents for the item-based than the subject-based model. Thus, the item-based maps show more sensitivity, which can be due to less variation between sentences than between subjects and/or a larger number of degrees of freedom.

Regarding the PCA analysis, the first principal component (PC) explains more than 1/4th of the total variance and represents the axis of strongest variance across the entire brain. Math sentences have the strongest weights on this component, while middle values correspond to lists of words and meaningless sentences and the lowest weights are attributed to non-mathematical sentences. The brain map associated to this component contrasts sentences with highest weight vs those with the lowest weights. It highlights the parietal cortex, the inferior temporal gyrus and the middle frontal gyrus in red/orange vs the angular gyrus, the inferior frontal gyrus and anterior temporal pole as well as temporal and frontal medial gyrus in blue. This contrast is extremely similar to the univariate contrast “Math vs Language” based on the apriori categories of the experimental design. (Figure 1A top).

The second principal component explains more than 13% of the variance. The variance of this second component reflects the difference between meaningful and meaningless sentences. Indeed, all the meaningless categories (lists of words and meaningless sentences) have the highest weights. The rest of the weights are dedicated to all meaningful categories, shuffled all together. The corresponding brain maps highlight the posterior part of the inferior temporal gyrus and the superior frontal gyrus in red/orange vs the angular gyrus, the middle frontal gyrus and the left superior temporal gyrus (Figure 1A bottom).

The scatter plot on Panel B of Figure 1 displays each sentence in PC1-PC2 space. PC1, the x-axis, separates math sentences (on the right) from non-math statements (left and center). PC-2, the y-axis, separates meaningless and syntaxless (list of words) stimuli from the other, meaningful, sentences. Interestingly, some sentences in the calculation category are positioned higher on the y-axis than other sentences. This may be due to the fact that these sentences refer to equations written with letters and have quite simple syntax. 

The first two components of the PCA analysis recover the categories from the experimental design. When looking at the sorting of sentences according to PC5, we discovered that it classified sentences based on the mention of spatial information, regardless of categories. The brain map associated to PC5 highlights the parahippocampal gyrus and entorhinal cortex as well as the retrosplenial cortex, the left posterior inferior temporal gyrus and the posterior part of the angular gyrus, areas that have been cited in the literature to be implicated in spatial location, geometry, and/or navigation.

Dots are colored according to the category the sentences belong to. Each ellipsis colored by category draws a 2𝜎 covariance ellipse around the mean point of each category. The dimensions of these ellipses are given by the (scaled) eigenvalues of the covariance matrix, and it is rotated such that its semi-major axis lies along the largest eigenvector. 

Conclusion

In this study, we analyzed brain responses to math and language sentences obtained from 31 participants. We observed that an item-based analysis is more sensitive than the classical subject-based method. Moreover, a data driven PCA analysis not only recovers the categories from original experimental design, but allows us to uncover dimensions that a traditional method would have missed. The first principal component separated Math from Language (explaining over 25% of the variance), mapping accurately onto known networks: parietal and medial frontal regions for math, versus inferior frontal and temporal regions for language. The second component distinguished meaningless from meaningful sentences, while the fifth component highlighted spatial information, a dimension that was not included in the design, and that recruited navigation-related areas like the parahippocampal gyrus.

In the PC1-PC2 plane, the clusters corresponding to the original categories show good separation, modest overlap. Specific misclassifications suggest that comprehension difficulties (math not being understood) or intense mental imagery (for sentences in the non-math categories) can shift neural signatures across categories. 

The present data could also be explored using  representational similarity analysis (RSA). However, the present approach offers a key advantage in that it allows each component to be projected back onto the brain, which is not possible with standard RSA. Furthermore, RSA combined with multidimensional scaling (MDS) is limited to the visualization of the two principal axes of variance and does not provide a quantitative assessment of variance explained or component ranking. In contrast, the presented method enables quantification of variance across components.

The by-item analysis presented here relied on sentence maps obtained by averaging over 31 participants. The same type of analysis performed on single-participant data does not reveal stable patterns, at least at 3T. We are currently analyzing a replication of the experiment at 7T where the individual results recover the original conditions, probably due to the better temporal signal to noise ratio of the scanner. Obtaining robust maps of activation to unique sentences in a single subject would pave the way to longitudinally explore effects of expertise or of learning in individuals.
