Introduction

Previous studies have identified alterations in resting-state functional connectivity (rs-FC) in obesity, including increased connectivity in the salience network, decreased connectivity within the default mode network, and differences in hypothalamic and striatal functional connectivity.1 These brain regions and networks are known to directly or indirectly contribute to food intake by integrating internal physiological signals with motivational, cognitive, and behavioral processes. Alterations in rs-FC within these circuits may therefore be associated with dysregulated eating behavior observed in obesity. Dopaminergic signaling plays a central role in motivated behavior, including the initiation, reinforcement, and regulation of food intake, and differences in striatal dopamine systems have been demonstrated in individuals with obesity. However, how inter-individual variability in striatal dopamine signaling relates to large-scale brain functional connectivity patterns remains incompletely understood. Therefore, the current study investigated the association between whole-brain functional connectivity and striatal dopamine D2/3 receptor binding potential (D2/3R BP), in people with a healthy weight and in people with obesity. Using a connectome-based predictive modeling approach, we aimed to identify a resting-state functional connectivity network predictive of baseline striatal D2/3R BP.

Methods

Participants
Data were previously collected as part of a clinical trial (Registry ID: NL-OMON28668). In this single-blind, randomized, controlled, crossover study, both lean subjects (N = 27) and subjects with obesity (N = 29) underwent [123I]IBZM SPECT to assess striatal D2/3R BP and BOLD fMRI scanning before and after intragastric infusions with water, lipid and glucose.2 For this post-hoc analysis, only pre-intragastric infusion baseline resting-state BOLD fMRI and baseline [123I]IBZM SPECT (i.e. post-intragastric infusion with water, serving as control condition) data were used. 

MRI acquisition and analysis
Resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) data were collected on a 3.0T Philips MR Scanner (Philips Medical Systems) with a 32-channel receive-only head coil, using a T2* gradient-echo EPI sequence (parameters: TR/TE = 1,700/35 ms; FOV = 216×216×124 mm; voxel size = 2.7×2.7×2.7 mm; 1410 dynamics; MB factor = 2; SENSE = 1.7). Data acquisition included 8 minutes of rs-fMRI before the start of each intragastric nutrient infusion. Rs-fMRI data were corrected for motion and susceptibility distortions, co-registered to T1-weighted (T1w) scans and normalized to MNI using fMRIPrep (v1.2.3.). Next, additional preprocessing steps were applied using custom MATLAB scripts (Mathworks, R2022b). Six rigid-body motion parameters were low-pass filtered,3 and motion parameter derivatives and squares were subsequently calculated. Then, nuisance regression was performed with linear and quadratic drift, 24 motion parameters, and mean signal from gray matter, white matter and cerebrospinal fluid. This was followed by low pass filtering with a Gaussian kernel of 0.11 Hz and removal of the first three volumes. Motion scrubbing was performed with a filtered FD > 0.3 mm threshold, and subjects with less than 15 minutes of data after scrubbing were excluded from analysis. Finally, rs-fMRI data of all three runs was concatenated and used as input for functional connectivity matrix construction using the Shen-268 atlas (figure 1).

SPECT acquisition and analysis
SPECT imaging data were acquired using a brain-dedicated system (InSPira HD; Neurologica, parameters: matrix = 128×128; energy window = 135–190 keV; slice thickness = 4 mm; acquisition time per slice = 4 min). SPECT scanning started two hours after [123I]IBZM infusion, and 45 minutes post-intragastric infusion with water, using an intravenous bolus of 64 MBq followed by continuous infusion of 16 MBq/h. Images were reconstructed using an iterative expectation maximization algorithm and corrected for attenuation. Region-of-interest masks of the striatum (i.e. caudate nucleus and putamen) were obtained using Freesurfer (v5.3.0.) on individual T1w MRI scans. Individual masks of the occipital cortex, used as reference region for non-specific binding, were created by registering the occipital cortex from the Harvard-Oxford cortical atlas to individual T1w scans with FSL. Subsequently, SPECT images were co-registered to an individual’s T1w MRI scan and the dopamine D2/3 receptor availability in the striatal and occipital cortex masks was quantified. From this, the striatal D2/3R BP was calculated as the specific-to-non-specific binding ratio (i.e. (mean striatal binding – mean occipital binding)/mean occipital binding).

Statistical analysis
Connectome-based predictive modeling (CPM) with leave-one-out cross validation and an edge selection threshold of p < .05, was performed for the separate subject groups (i.e. lean and with obesity), as well as for the whole sample combined.4 Prior to performing CPM, quality control was performed on functional connectivity matrices, which included calculating L2 distance followed by visual inspection, as well as exclusion of nodes with zero-connections. Normality of striatal D2/3R BP values and the correlation between mean filtered framewise displacement and striatal D2/3R BP were also assessed. Preprocessing and baseline analyses were preregistered (osf.io/acxhv). 

Results

One participant was excluded from the analysis due to an insufficient number of usable timeframes remaining after motion scrubbing. Quality control analysis on functional matrices based on L2 distance revealed one outlier, which was excluded from analysis upon visual inspection. Hence, CPM analyses were performed on data from 26 lean subjects and 28 subjects with obesity. Across subjects and runs, 14 out of 268 nodes had zero connections and CPM analyses were therefore run without these nodes. Motion control analyses showed no significant correlations between mean filtered framewise displacement and striatal D2/3R BP in either the whole sample (r = 0.02, p = 0.86), or in the separate groups (lean: rs = 0.24, p = 0.24; people with obesity: rs = -0.02, p = 0.92).

CPM analyses on the combined sample, using Pearson’s correlations, resulted in a mean count of 729 positive and 1388 negative edges that were associated with striatal D2/3R BP (figure 2). However, the model did not show predictive ability (correlation between observed and predicted D2/3R BP values = -0.19). 

CPM analyses on data from the separate subject groups were performed using Spearman’s correlation because of non-normality of the striatal D2/3R BP data. Neither the CPM model constructed on the lean (r = -0.43) or obesity subject group (r = -0.11) was found to predict striatal D2/3R BP (lean: mean count positive/negative edges = 928/1050; obesity: mean count positive/negative edges = 881/1819).  

Conclusion

In the current post-hoc analysis, we did not observe an association between whole-brain resting-state functional connectivity strength and striatal D2/3R BP. Rather than indicating an absence of dopaminergic influence on functional connectivity per se, this finding suggests that baseline striatal D2/3R BP may not be robustly reflected in global resting-state connectivity patterns.
Striatal dopamine signaling is known to exert regionally and circuit-specific modulatory effects, for instance within cortico-striatal pathways, rather than uniform influences across the whole brain. While we hypothesized that individual differences in fasting striatal D2/3R BP would relate to resting-state functional connectivity strength, such effects may be spatially constrained and therefore diluted when modeled at the level of whole-brain connectivity.
CPM analysis has most frequently been applied to behavioral and cognitive outcomes, and typically demonstrate moderate correlation strength (r ≈ 0.2 - 0.5).4 As these outcome measures emerge from widespread network interactions, these characteristics may favor CPM performance. In contrast, striatal D2/3R BP represents a localized neurochemical trait, and the incorporation of a large number of edges that are not directly modulated by striatal dopamine receptor availability may increase variance and obscure more specific associations in whole-brain CPM models. Restricting CPM analyses to cortico-striatal or dopamine-sensitive networks may therefore shed further light on the predictive contribution of network-specific edges.
Although CPM has previously demonstrated predictive performance in a sample of comparable size,5 larger samples (i.e. N > 100) are generally preferable to improve model stability and generalizability. Sample size may have contributed to variability in model performance, particularly in subgroup analyses, but is unlikely to fully explain the absence of predictive associations observed here.
Additionally, as D2/3R BP is reflective of both receptor availability and tonic dopamine, it may not capture dynamic dopaminergic processes that may interact more directly with functional connectivity. We therefore aim to also apply CPM on nutrient-induced striatal dopamine release, which might show stronger coupling with (change in) functional connectivity strength.
Finally, a non-linear relationship between striatal D2/3R BP and whole-brain functional connectivity strength might be hypothesized, but cannot be captured by the linear regression analysis used in the current CPM analyses. We therefore aim to incorporate non-linear estimation methods with CPM in future analyses and explore whether more complex relationships exist between dopamine signaling and brain functional connectivity.
