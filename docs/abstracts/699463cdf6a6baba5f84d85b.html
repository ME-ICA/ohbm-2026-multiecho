Introduction

Autoimmune limbic encephalitis (LE) is a frequent subtype of autoimmune encephalitis and is typically classified according to neuronal autoantibodies such as leucine-rich glioma-inactivated protein 1 (LGI1), contactin-associated protein 2 (CASPR2), glutamic acid decarboxylase 65 (GAD65), or N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR) antibodies.1 While antibody status is central in current diagnostic frameworks and MRI studies show that antibody-defined subtypes are associated  with group-level neuroimaging patterns2, patients sharing the same serology nonetheless exhibit striking heterogeneity in both imaging findings and clinical trajectories. This discrepancy highlights the limitations of antibody-based phenotyping and underscores the need for imaging strategies that capture disease expression at the individual level.

Recent advances in normative modeling and network-based mapping allow quantification of subject-specific structural deviations and identifying regions most susceptible to disease-related atrophy, which are referred to as epicenters. These approaches shift the focus from predefined serological categories toward data-driven, connectivity-informed phenotypes that may better reflect underlying biological processes. In LE, where inflammation and network dysfunction extend beyond the mesiotemporal system, such methods offer the opportunity to test whether antibody subgroups truly form coherent imaging phenotypes or whether structural pathology instead converges onto shared or divergent network-level patterns independent of serological classification.

In this study, we derive individual cortical and subcortical atrophy maps in antibody-positive LE and apply network-based atrophy modeling, dimensionality reduction, and unsupervised clustering to determine whether imaging-based phenotypes align with established antibody groups or reveal distinct structural patterns that transcend antibody status.

Methods

Participants

We retrospectively included 49 patients with antibody-positive, non-paraneoplastic limbic encephalitis treated at the University Hospital between 2005 and 2022. All patients fulfilled Graus’ diagnostic criteria1. For inclusion, patients were required to have at least one available T1-weighted MRI scan acquired later than 24 months after disease onset (defined as first symptom presentation). We used the latest available scan from each eligible patient for the analysis. This criterion ensured that network-based atrophy modeling was performed exclusively on late-stage scans. Antibody subtypes included GAD65, LGI1, CASPR2 and NMDAR.

MRI Acquisition and Preprocessing

Isotropic (0.8 or 1 mm) T1-weighted MRI scans were acquired on a 3T Siemens scanner (MAGNETOM TrioTim) at the Human MRI Core Facility of the University of  Bonn using two different scan parameters due to a change in head coil during the study period. Images were processed using the FastSurfer longitudinal pipeline (v2.4.2) to ensure robust within-subject cortical reconstruction. FreeSurfer-compatible outputs were generated using FreeSurfer v7.4.1, including Desikan–Killiany atlas–based cortical parcellations and regional cortical thickness estimates. A total of 68 cortical regions (34 per hemisphere) were extracted per scan. All segmentations were visually inspected for accuracy

Normative Modeling and Atrophy Map Generation

Regional cortical thickness measures were submitted to the ENIGMA Lifespan Working Group’s CentileBrain normative modeling platform.3 For each subject and region, z-scores were calculated relative to a large, harmonized reference dataset of healthy individuals, adjusting for age, sex, acquisition site, scanner characteristics, and FreeSurfer version. Cortical atrophy maps were generated by thresholding z-scores at zero, retaining only negative values indicative of cortical atrophy while setting positive deviations to zero.

Network-based atrophy modeling

Cortical atrophy maps of each patient were used as input for network-based atrophy modeling. Individual whole-cortex atrophy patterns were spatially correlated with the ENIGMA normative structural connectivity matrix using Pearson correlation coefficient.4 Epicenter susceptibility was estimated for both cortical and subcortical regions by correlating individual cortical atrophy with region-specific connectivity profiles, encompassing cortico–cortical as well as subcortico–cortical connections. The Pearson correlation coefficient was Fisher’s r-to-z transformed. An epicenter is a region whose structural connectivity profile spatially resembles a given cortical atrophy map. Epicenter susceptibility maps were retained at the individual level for data-driven phenotype identification and were additionally averaged at the group level for antibody-specific epicenter analyses. For all downstream analyses, maps from patients with right-hemispheric disease focus were left–right flipped yielding an affected/contralateral hemispheric orientation.

Imaging-Based Phenotype Identification

To identify imaging-based phenotypes independent of antibody status, principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to individual epicenter susceptibility maps. Principal components were ranked by explained variance. Individual components explaining at least 10% of variance were retained for k-means clustering performed for 2 ≤ k ≤ 4. The optimal number of clusters was determined based on the maximum silhouette score.

Clinical Characterization and Statistical Analysis

To assess clinical relevance, demographic and clinical variables, including age at disease onset, disease duration at MRI, antibody subtype distribution, and treatment response (defined as the occurrence of seizure freedom at any time during treatment), were compared across imaging-based clusters. Statistical analyses were performed using SciPy and statsmodels. Categorical variables were calculated using Fisher’s exact test.

Results

Disease Epicenter Susceptibility across Antibody Subgroups 

Group-averaged epicenter susceptibility maps demonstrated partially overlapping yet distinct spatial patterns across antibody-defined subgroups (Figure 1A). For CASPR2 patients, the strongest cortical epicenter susceptibility was observed in bilateral isthmus cingulate cortex and contralateral cuneus, whereas subcortical epicenters were centered on the affected hippocampus, contralateral amygdala and affected accumbens. In GAD65-associated limbic encephalitis, cortical epicenter susceptibility was dominated by bilateral cuneus and contralateral pericalcarine cortex, with subcortical involvement primarily affecting both hippocampi and the contralateral amygdala. LGI1-positive patients showed cortical epicenters in the affected cuneus, entorhinal cortex and contralateral pericalcarine cortex, while subcortical epicenters were located in bilateral nucleus accumbens and contralateral hippocampus. 

In NMDAR-positive patients, cortical epicenter susceptibility was most pronounced in bilateral anterior cingulate cortices, including rostral and caudal anterior cingulate regions. Subcortical epicenters primarily involved the nucleus accumbens, caudate and thalamus of the affected hemisphere. All four  group-averaged maps consistently revealed a pronounced degree of symmetry in epicenter susceptibility.

Individual Disease Epicenter Susceptibility and Phenotype Identification

Principal component analysis of individual epicenter susceptibility maps reduced dimensionality of the interindividual variability. The first three components explained 42.6%, 12.5% and 10.4%, accounting for 65% of total variance across subjects (Figure 1B). These components were retained for subsequent clustering. K-means clustering identified two phenotypes as the optimal solution (k= 2; silhouette score = 0.42) (Figure 1C). Cluster-averaged epicenter susceptibility maps revealed distinct spatial profiles (Figure 1D). For cluster 1 (n=34), strongest cortical epicenters were observed in bilateral cuneus and pericalcarine cortex, while subcortical epicenters predominantly involved bilateral hippocampi and the contralateral amygdala. In contrast, cluster 2 (n = 15) exhibited more spatially extensive epicenter susceptibility values. Cortical epicenters in cluster 2 were centered on the affected rostral anterior cingulate and caudal anterior cingulate bilaterally, whereas subcortical epicenters primarily involved the bilateral nucleus accumbens and affected caudate. Overall, cluster 2 demonstrated greater engagement of anterior cingulo-striatal and associative networks compared to the more posterior-mesial pattern observed in cluster 1. The cluster-averaged maps demonstrated a high degree of bilateral symmetry in epicenter susceptibility.

Antibody Proportion by Cluster 

The distribution of antibody subtypes differed quantitatively across the two imaging-based clusters (Figure 1E). GAD65 patients were predominantly assigned to cluster 1 (24 of 28 patients). LGI1 patients were distributed more evenly across clusters (5 in cluster 1, 4 in cluster 2), as were CASPR2-positive patients (4 in each cluster). NMDAR patients were less frequent overall and showed a greater proportion in cluster 2 (3 of 4 patients) compared to cluster 1. Binomial testing and correction for multiple comparisons indicated that GAD65 status was significantly associated with Cluster 1 membership (FDR-corrected p = 0.00072). No significant association was observed for the other subgroups.

Therapy Response

Based on this classification, therapy response differed across imaging-based clusters (Figure 1F). In cluster 1, 10 patients met criteria for therapy response and 24 were classified as non-responders. In cluster 2, 10 patients met criteria for therapy response and 5 were classified as non-responders. The association between imaging-based cluster membership and therapy response was statistically significant (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.026). 

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that subject-specific, network-based imaging can delineate meaningful structural phenotypes in autoimmune limbic encephalitis (AE) that extend beyond traditional antibody-based classifications. Group-averaged epicenter susceptibility maps revealed partially distinct but broadly overlapping patterns across antibody subtypes. 

Dimensionality reduction and clustering identified two reproducible imaging-based phenotypes. Cluster 1 reflected a posterior–mesial pattern anchored in cuneus, pericalcarine cortex, and hippocampi. Cluster 2 exhibited a more anterior cingulo-striatal profile, involving the rostral and caudal anterior cingulate and nucleus accumbens, aligning with emerging evidence for extralimbic network engagement in surface-antibody mediated AE. These findings highlight heterogeneous disease expression that does not map cleanly onto serological classification.

Cluster membership was associated with therapy response with a statistically significant distribution across the two imaging-derived phenotypes. The enrichment of GAD65 patients in the non-responder cluster is clinically coherent, as GAD65-associated AE is well known to follow a more chronic disease course with comparatively modest response to immunotherapy and antiseizure treatments.5 Accordingly, the clustering framework successfully recapitulated a clinically relevant dimension of disease behavior. Several limitations must be acknowledged. Antibody-specific sample sizes were small, limiting generalizability. Furthermore, hemispheric flipping to standardize laterality was necessary due to frequent bilateral or ambiguous clinical seizure onset and likely introduced noise. Looking ahead, imaging-based phenotyping may be particularly valuable in seronegative autoimmune encephalitis, where diagnostic uncertainty remains substantial. Extending this framework by integrating functional connectivity models and conducting cross-disease comparisons with other network-mediated disorders could refine the specificity of identified phenotypes and improve predictive modeling. As autoimmune encephalitis is increasingly recognized as a network-level disorder, multimodal normative approaches hold promise for enhancing individualized disease characterization and guiding future treatment stratification.
