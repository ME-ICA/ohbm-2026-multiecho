Introduction

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) has become routine therapy for movement disorders (e.g., Parkinson’s disease, dystonia, dyskinesia) and a promising intervention for psychiatric diseases (e.g., obsessive compulsive disorder and Tourette Syndrome)(1). In nearly all DBS-responsive conditions, therapeutic benefit is achieved by modulating pathological neural circuits within the basal ganglia–thalamocortical system. As most of the relevant DBS effects are achieved through stimulation of axonal fibers rather than neurons, precise identification and mapping of the relevant white matter pathways are essential for accurate DBS targeting. Diffusion MRI (dMRI) tractography has emerged as a crucial tool to support and substantially improve targeting accuracy and stimulation programming. However, the spatial resolution of conventional dMRI (1-2 mm) cannot resolve the fine-scale complex organization of the relevant basal-ganglia fiber pathways and related subcortical nuclei, limiting both surgical precision and mechanistic insights into DBS. Here we leverage the highest-resolution ex vivo dMRI tractography data acquired in whole human hemispheres to date, as part of the BRAIN CONNECTS center for Large-scale Imaging of Neural Circuits (LINC) on the first-of-its-kind Connectome 2.0 scanner. We aim to comprehensively map the organization and internal topography of the basal ganglia-thalamocortical circuits, but also of several small, surrounding pathways implicated in motor, cognitive, and limbic functions. Mapping these pathways at a finer scale is crucial for understanding the neural substrates underlying therapeutic stimulation and avoiding stimulation-induced side effects.  To demonstrate this, we align these anatomical reconstructions to “sweet spots” from previous clinical studies, providing a refined framework that links stimulation sites to underlying circuits.

Methods

Data acquisition and processing: Two ex vivo human brain hemispheres (one left and one right hemisphere) were included in this study (males, 70 and 63 years at time of death). Specimens were fixed in 10% formalin for at least two months and transferred into sealed plastic bags filled with PLP prior scanning on the 3T Connectome 2.0 (MAGNETOM Connectom.X, Siemens Healthineers, Gmax = 500 mT/m) equipped with a custom, 64-channel coil array for imaging ex vivo human brains. 320 diffusion weighted volumes with b-values of 4,000/8,000/12,000/25,000 s/mm2 in 64/64/64/128 directions were collected at 400 µm isotropic resolution using a multi-shot 3D EPI sequence optimized for ex vivo dMRI (TE/TR=43/500 ms, ∂/D=10.4/19.5 ms, 16 shots). Data preprocessing includes MPPCA denoising, correction of B0 inhomogeneities and eddy current induced distortion, and correction of gradient non-linearity induced image distortions. Fiber orientation distributions are reconstructed using a multi-shell, multi-tissue constrained spherical deconvolution approach in MRtrix3. Probabilistic tractography is propagated from 5 random seed locations in every white-matter voxel. Cortical and subcortical segmentations are obtained in FreeSurfer (v. 7.5.0) through SAMSEG, combined with the NextBrain atlas, and through the recon-all stream, combined with a parcellation scheme that translated the anatomical definitions of regions from Petrides et al. 2012 to the surface of the fsaverage template. 

 

Pathway delineation and atlas creation: We delineated 41 white matter pathways in both samples. Pathways are iteratively generated in a two-step approach (Figure 1). First, they are manually extracted from the whole-brain tractograms using inclusion and exclusion regions of interest (ROIs) in Trackvis. The ROIs and resulting pathways are meticulously validated by comparison with cadaveric dissection studies in humans (2), axonal tracer studies in non-human primates (3), and supervision by an expert neuroanatomist (SNH). The streamlines from each pathway are converted into voxel visitation maps that are thresholded to obtain the core of the pathway and binarized. Tractography is then seeded again from each voxel within this mask; the threshold, number of seeds, maximum tract length, cutoff, and seed_cutoff, are optimized empirically for each pathway. The following nuclei are manually delineated in each sample to maximize anatomical accuracy: the internal and external segments of the globus pallidum (GPi and GPe); the subthalamic nucleus (STN); the zona incerta (ZI); the pedunculopontine nucleus (PPN); the substantia nigra (SN); the fields of Forel (H1 and H2); the mammillary bodies (MB); the red nucleus (RN); the habenula (HB). Streamlines within the striato-cortical, thalamo-cortical, and subthalamo-cortical projections are clustered based on the location of their cortical terminations and streamline terminations are mapped within the STN, thalamus, and putamen. White matter pathways and subcortical nuclei are then mapped to the MNI ICBM 2009b nonlinear common space (0.5x0.5x0.5 mm) using a label-based registration approach. We applied SAMSEG, combined with the NextBrain atlas to the in vivo MNI T1-weighted template images and then used the symmetric normalization (SyN) registration method in ANTs to register the segmentation map of the in vivo template (masked to include only a single hemisphere) to that of the ex vivo hemisphere. The inverse of the resulting non-linear deformation field was used to map the models of the cortico-subcortical pathways and subcortical nuclei onto template space.

Connectomic analysis of DBS stimulation maps: We extracted probabilistic stimulation maps from the published literature using the Lead-DBS normalization pipeline. Maps were resampled to the MNI ICBM 2009b nonlinear common space (0.5x0.5x0.5 mm), thresholded at 20%, binarized, and used as inclusion ROIs to extract streamlines from the atlas associated with the stimulation volume. 

Results

The very high resolution of the present dataset allows us to accurately identify all the major components of the basal ganglia thalamocortical circuits along with adjacent white matter pathways and subcortical nuclei that are in close spatial and functional relation to the basal ganglia but are not traditionally associated with it. By mapping both small and large-scale connections, we were able to reconstruct the direct, indirect and hyperdirect circuits in their entirety (Figure 2a). The tegmental region is very complex, with several pathways converging and overlapping. We can identify all of these different components, including the classical ansa subdivision (AL, LF, SF), the more recently described ansa subthalamica (AS), and connections that heretofore have only been described in classical anatomical and tracer studies (3) (GPi-Pf, GPi-Hb) (Figure 1b). Along with specific pallidofugal and STN pathways, we delineate the fornix (FX), the stria medullaris (SM), the stria terminalis (ST), the anterior commissure (AC), the mammillo-thalamic tract (MT), the fasciculus retroflexus (FR), the medial lemniscus (ML), the medial forebrain bundle (MFB), the optic tract (OT), the dentatothalamic tract (DTT). To further validate the accuracy of our reconstructions, we map the topographical organization of the cortico-striatal, cortico-thalamic, and cortico-subthalamic pathways, as they travel from the frontal cortex to their targets, and replicate organizational principles established by tracer studies in non-human primates (4). In the putamen, we observe a ventromedial to dorsolateral gradient: motor and premotor regions project to dorsolateral and central sections caudal to the AC, whereas prefrontal projections were concentrated anterior to the AC (5). Streamlines from vmPFC are sparse and confined to the rostral ventromedial putamen (Figure 2c). The thalamus exhibits a rostrocaudal gradient with PFC terminations located rostral to premotor and motor terminations. The STN exhibits a rostrocaudal and ventromedial to dorsolateral organization, with dPFC projections concentrated in the medial half of the nucleus, whereas motor projections concentrated dorsal in the lateral half (4). In agreement with tracer studies we identify only sparse STN-vmPFC streamlines, correctly located within the medial tip of the STN (4). Finally, we investigate connectivity fingerprints associated with both clinical improvement and stimulation-induced side effects, linking specific symptoms to different pathways. Fear-related side effects associated with the FX, the AC, and the MT, all pathways implicated in fear conditioning and emotional regulation. Pleasantness was distinctively associated with the SM, ST, and vmPFC projections. Phosphenes are uniquely linked to the optic tract. For clinical improvement, dystonia is associated with the premotor and motor components of the hyperdirect pathway, whereas OCD shows a more frontal connectivity profile involving the AC and the vmPFC and dPFC projections (Figure 2d). 

Conclusion

This new open-access atlas will facilitate DBS targeting and advance our understanding of circuit-level disease mechanisms and therapeutic outcomes. The atlas can be integrated into normative connectomic workflows or used to guide individualized tractography in patient-specific targeting. By enabling more precise and complete anatomical mapping between stimulation sites and clinical outcome, it provides a critical framework for optimizing therapy, improving electrode placement, and advancing personalized neurostimulation strategies. The need for more detailed circuit models is becoming increasingly important with the adoption of modern directional electrodes, which can shape distinct stimulation volumes - each defined by specific amplitudes and frequency – to selectively engage targeted fiber pathways.
