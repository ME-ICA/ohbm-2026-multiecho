Introduction

The interaction between the brain and the heart via the autonomic nervous system represents a fundamental yet underexplored dimension of human physiology. According to the neurovisceral integration model, this interplay is supported by the central autonomic network (CAN), a distributed system of brain regions that contributes to control autonomic outflow1. 

Heart rate variability (HRV) provides a non-invasive window into cardiac autonomic states, yet peripheral autonomic measures alone cannot reveal the central mechanisms underlying cardiac modulation. Integrating brain and cardiac signals represents a powerful framework to characterize the neurophysiological bases of autonomic control. HRV-driven functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) enables data-driven mapping of brain regions whose hemodynamic activity co-varies with autonomic dynamics2. Beyond autonomic modulation, effective brain-heart integration also relies on brain sensitivity to ongoing cardiac activity and its central elaboration. Direct evidence linking cardiac and brain activity remains limited, particularly in psychiatric disorders.

Major depressive disorder (MDD) represents a compelling model to investigate brain-heart interactions, given consistent evidence of autonomic imbalance, altered interoceptive processing, and increased cardiovascular risk3. Here, we combined simultaneous resting-state electrocardiography (ECG) and fMRI in individuals with late-onset MDD and healthy controls (HC). We applied HRV-driven fMRI to investigate brain correlates of autonomic regulation, and a complementary heart rate (HR)-driven fMRI analysis to map brain regions associated with cardiac rate fluctuations, as an index of cardiac afferent signaling. By jointly examining autonomic regulation and cardiac-related brain representation, this innovative multimodal approach aims to provide a more comprehensive characterization of brain-heart interactions and to identify alterations associated with late-onset MDD.

Methods

Forty participants were included in the present study, comprising 20 HC (69.9±10.8 years, 7M/13M) and 20 patients with MDD (66.2±7.7 years, 11M/9F). Clinical diagnoses were assessed using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5, Clinician Version (SCID-5-CV). Only individuals with late-onset MDD (≥ 45 years) were included. All participants provided a written informed consent to the study protocol, which was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethical Committee of Fondazione IRCCS Ca’ Granda Ospedale Maggiore Policlinico (Milan, Italy).   

Participants underwent a multimodal MRI session using a 3T Philips Achieva DStream scanner equipped with a 32-channel head coil. Resting-state fMRI data were collected over 500 s using a multi-transmit T2*-weighted echo planar imaging (EPI) sequence (voxel size=2 mm isotropic, TR=2500 s). During fMRI acquisition, simultaneous electroencephalogram (EEG)-ECG recordings were collected using an MR-compatible Geodesic EEG System including a single-lead ECG channel (fs=1000 Hz). A high-resolution T1-weighted structural image was acquired using a 3D turbo field echo (TFE) SENSE sequence (1 mm isotropic) and served as anatomical reference.

fMRI preprocessing was performed using HALFpipe and included volumes realignment, registration to structural image, tissue segmentation, and normalization to MNI space. Spatial smoothing, grand mean scaling, and temporal filtering were applied. To enhance data quality, multiple denoising strategies were compared using quantitative metrics, and nuisance regression was performed including mean white matter, cerebrospinal fluid, and global signals4.

ECG signals were corrected for gradient artifact. R peaks were semi-automatically detected and the tachogram was computed as their difference in time. An ECG-derived respiration signal was estimated and resampled at each R peak to obtain the respirogram. The tachogram and respirogram were entered into a time-varying bivariate autoregressive model, a cross-spectral analysis that enables to identify linear frequency relationships between the two signals in input. Sympathetic activity was estimated as the time-varying low-frequency (LF) power integral of the tachogram, removing respiration-driven spectral components. Vagal activity was indexed by the time-varying power integral of the power spectrum reflecting respiration influence on the tachogram. This interaction is mainly linked to respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA), reflecting vagal activity at the respiration rate, typically in the HRV high-frequency range5. LF and RSA time series were convolved with the canonical hemodynamic response function to account for the intrinsic delay of the neurovascular coupling.  

HRV-driven fMRI. An HRV-driven fMRI analysis was performed to investigate the central hemodynamic and metabolic correlates of cardiac autonomic variability. Time-varying LF and RSA time series were used as regressors of interest in a voxelwise general linear model (GLM) to identify cortical and subcortical regions with a significant blood oxygenation level dependent (BOLD) response to sympathetic and vagal modulation. Six motion parameters were included as regressors of non-interest. Subject-level contrast maps were computed to assess sympathetic- and vagal-related BOLD responses. Group-level analyses were conducted on contrast maps using GLMs including age, sex, and diagnosis as covariates. Statistical inference on group effect and between-group differences was performed using T-contrasts with a threshold of p<0.001 (random effects).

HR-driven fMRI. In a complementary analysis, subject-level HR fluctuations were used as regressors of interest in a voxelwise GLM to identify brain regions whose BOLD activity co-varies with cardiac rate dynamics, as a proxy of cardiac-related afferent and cerebrovascular signaling. Group-level analyses and statistical inference were performed analogously to the HRV-driven fMRI analysis.

Results

The HRV-driven and HR-driven fMRI analysis enabled the identification of brain regions whose BOLD activity was significantly associated with autonomic modulation and cardiac rate-related fluctuations, respectively. We report i) HRV-related and HR-related activations across the entire sample, controlling for sex, age, and diagnosis, and ii) between-group differences between individuals with MDD and HC. 

HRV-driven fMRI. 

Sympathetic modulation (i.e., LF regressor). Across all participants, LF power dynamics were associated with significant positive BOLD responses in the right anterior cingulate gyrus, right precuneus, bilateral angular gyrus, right supramarginal gyrus, and left lingual gyrus, indicating that enhanced sympathetic activity corresponded to local hemodynamic increases. Significant negative LF-related BOLD responses were observed in the left superior medial frontal gyrus, left anterior cingulate gyrus, left precuneus, left middle cingulate gyrus, and bilateral thalamus. Between-group comparisons revealed significantly reduced LF-related BOLD responses in MDD patients compared to HC in the right anterior insula, right inferior temporal gyrus, cerebellar vermis, right supramarginal and postcentral gyrus, left paraterminal gyrus, and brainstem regions including the left inferior colliculus and periaqueductal gray (PAG).

Vagal modulation (i.e., RSA regressor). Across the entire sample, vagal activity was associated with significant positive BOLD responses in the cerebellar vermis, whereas significant negative responses were observed in the right anterior, middle, and posterior cingulate gyri, right postcentral gyrus, and left parahippocampal gyrus. Group comparisons indicated that individuals with MDD exhibited significantly higher RSA-related BOLD responses in the right hippocampus and left precuneus compared to HC. Conversely, HC showed significantly lower vagal-related activations in the bilateral anterior cingulate gyrus, right anterior insula, and cerebellum.

HR-driven fMRI. 

HR-driven fMRI analysis revealed significant positive cardiac rate-related BOLD responses in the right thalamus and in right middle and posterior cingulate gyri across the sample. Between-group comparisons showed that individuals with MDD exhibited significantly greater HR-related BOLD responses than HC in the bilateral thalamus, middle cingulate gyrus, right hippocampus, left frontal regions, right middle temporal gyrus, and left hypothalamic area. In contrast, individuals with MDD showed significantly reduced HR-related BOLD responses in the right posterior insula compared to HC.

Conclusion

In the present study, we applied a novel multimodal ECG-fMRI framework to investigate brain-heart interactions in late-onset MDD, jointly probing the neural correlates of autonomic modulation and cardiac activity. By integrating time-resolved estimates of sympathetic and parasympathetic activity with BOLD signals, we characterized CAN dynamics in a data-driven way and identified alterations in brain-autonomic coupling associated with MDD. Across all participants, HRV dynamics consistently mapped onto regions classically involved in autonomic control, including cingulate, insular, sensorimotor, and limbic areas, supporting the feasibility of simultaneous ECG-fMRI to capture spontaneous neurovisceral interactions1,2.

Individuals with MDD showed distinct alterations in both sympathetic- and vagal-related BOLD responses. Reduced sympathetic-related activations emerged in key interoceptive and salience regions, most prominently in the right anterior insula, with alterations in supramarginal, postcentral, and temporo-limbic areas. These findings suggest a weakened coupling with sympathetic activity within circuits supporting bodily awareness and sensorimotor integration. Altered vagal-related responses, particularly in the hippocampus and precuneus, further point to a dysregulated interaction between parasympathetic modulation and networks implicated in stress regulation and self-referential processing. Together, these results indicate that MDD may involve a broad disruption of central autonomic regulation, extending beyond canonical affective circuits.

Our HR-driven fMRI analysis provided complementary information on cardiac-related brain responses, indexing central representation of cardiac afferent signaling. Individuals with MDD exhibited enhanced HR-related BOLD responses in thalamic, cingulate, hippocampal, and hypothalamic regions, alongside reduced responses in the posterior insula, which has a crucial role in processing internal bodily sensations. This pattern suggests an altered central elaboration of cardiac-related signals in MDD, potentially reflecting changes in how peripheral cardiovascular information is integrated within the central autonomic network. Over time, chronic alterations in cardiac pulsatility may contribute to long-term changes in neural responsivity to cardiac signals. Notably, the convergence of HRV- and HR-driven alterations in insular and cingulate regions highlights these areas as key hubs where dysregulation of both autonomic control and cardiac-related signaling may interact.

From a systems-level perspective, our findings support a view of MDD as a disorder of the brain-heart axis, characterized not only by impaired autonomic regulation but also by altered neural responses to ongoing cardiac activity. Such dual alterations may contribute to altered interoceptive processing, emotional dysregulation, and increased physiological vulnerability observed in depression. The integration of HRV-driven and HR-driven fMRI thus provides a more comprehensive framework for capturing distinct yet complementary components of brain-heart interactions, with potential implications for stratification of clinical subtypes and for the development of targeted neuromodulation strategies.

In conclusion, this study indicates that combining neural and cardiac signals enables a richer characterization of neurovisceral function in both health and disease. While HRV-informed fMRI captures central autonomic modulation, HR-informed fMRI probes the cortical representation of ongoing cardiac signals. By jointly assessing both perspectives, our findings suggest that late-onset MDD is associated with alterations across multiple levels of the brain-heart axis. This multimodal approach offers a promising framework for future longitudinal and interventional studies aimed at disentangling causal mechanisms and informing therapeutic strategies addressing autonomic and interoceptive dysfunctions in affective disorders.
