Introduction

Effective decision-making in complex environments depends on the accurate interpretation of ambiguous sensory information. Training has been shown to improve visual judgments— a skill known as perceptual learning— by disinhibiting the visual cortex, as indicated by decreased GABA, the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter in the human brain1. Further, alpha oscillations (8-13 Hz) are thought to periodically inhibit the transmission of visual information to the primary visual cortex2. Entrainment at an individual’s alpha frequency (IAF) has been shown to enhance perceptual learning3. Yet, the inhibitory brain mechanisms that underlie entrainment and lead to enhanced perceptual learning remain largely unknown. Here, we capitalize on an integrated MRI protocol that combines functional magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) with functional MR measurements to investigate functional neurochemical and brain activity mechanisms that underlie individualized alpha entrainment during training on a visual discrimination task.  

Methods

Fifty four participants (27 Male, 27 Female; mean age = 23.33 ± 4.36) were trained on a signal-in-noise pattern discrimination that involved discriminating radial versus concentric Glass patterns embedded in noise. Entrainment was induced by visual flicker (grey square presented repeatedly at alpha frequency) that preceded the presentation of the Glass pattern stimulus per trial. We employed EEG to measure each participant’s individual alpha frequency (IAF; parietal, occipital channels3) at resting state before training on the task. We trained two groups of participants during 7T scanning: for the Match group (n=27) the flicker frequency matched each participant’s IAF; for the Non-match group (n=27) the flicker frequency deviated ≈1 Hz away from each participant’s IAF. For each trial, the Glass pattern stimulus was presented in the left hemifield after flicker-based entrainment at alpha trough phase. We employed a combined fMRI-MRS sequence4 (fMRI and MRS acquisition within the same TR = 4.5 s) that covered the occipital-temporal cortex (fMRI: 1.2 mm isotropic; MRS: 2x2x2 cm3, right hemisphere) and allowed us to track both functional MRS-GABA and fMRI activity during training. To investigate fMRI learning-dependent changes during training, we analysed the data using deconvolution analysis for radial and concentric Glass patterns vs. fixation baseline in the lateral occipital cortex (LOC). We employed Multivoxel pattern analysis (MVPA) to decode activity related to radial vs. concentric patterns using leave-one run out cross-validation. Functional MRS data were pre-processed (water, frequency and phase correction) and GABA levels were referenced to total Creatine or water.

Results

Our results showed enhanced learning and brain plasticity for the Match group (IAF-driven entrainment) compared to the Non-Match group (alpha entrainment frequency deviates from IAF). In particular, participants in the Match group showed significantly faster learning rate (t-test: t(52) = 3.18, p < 0.05, Cohen's d = 0.867) and higher fMRI activation (Mixed-design ANOVA, main effect of Group: F(1, 44) = 5, p < 0.05, partial η² = .102), while decreased stimulus activation with training (main effect of Training: F(5.29, 232.69) = 2.63,  p < 0.05, partial η² = .056). MVPA showed that decoding accuracy was significantly higher for the Match than Non-Match group (permutation t-test: t = -2.42, p < 0.05), suggesting enhanced visual representations for improved perceptual discriminability. Finally, our functional MRS results showed changes in GABAergic inhibition during training (LME; Training x group (Non-Match) = 0.028 (CI: 0.001-0.055); p < 0.05) with GABA increase for the Match group earlier in the training while GABA decrease later in the training.

Conclusion

Our results provide evidence that individualized alpha entrainment accelerates learning and enhances brain plasticity, as evidenced by increased activation, enhanced stimulus representations and GABAergic inhibition changes in the visual cortex for improved perceptual decisions.
