Introduction

Hispanic Americans experience disproportionate rates of health disparities across multiple chronic conditions, including mental health and cancer, emphasizing an urgent need for a more comprehensive understanding of Hispanic health. Because many key health conditions and early disparities begin to form during adolescence, disentangling the interrelations between adolescent biopsychosocial development and risk/resilience factors would provide a nuanced understanding of the social, cultural, and environmental determinants of health among Hispanic youth. Although substantial prior work has studied individual sociocultural factors, such as cultural identity, values, and practices, a more integrated and holistic approach is needed. To this end, we drew upon Coll et. al’s integrative model1 and have proposed a conceptual framework for Hispanic adolescent development that highlights the multifaceted and multilevel nature of the sociocultural environment by linking knowledge across three domains of influence (i.e., sociocultural environment, [neuro]biological, and behavioral) and across three levels of influence (i.e., individual, interpersonal [family], and community). In the current study, we applied our conceptual framework to investigate the complex interplay between sociocultural environment, neurodevelopment and risk/resilience factors for health disparities among Hispanic youth.

Methods

For Hispanic-identifying youth within the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study2, we accessed the complete sociocultural and resting-state fMRI data at the three and four-year follow-up (ages 12-14; n=236, release 5.1). This included 20 sociocultural environment variables and 66 rsFC Gordon network3 variables (i.e., 10 correlations of within-network connectivity and 56 unique pairwise correlations of between-network connectivity) that were residualized, mean-centered, and normalized (Fig. 1A).To examine how sociocultural environment at the individual, family, and community levels relates to rsFC among ABCD youth, we applied partial least squares correlation (PLSC), which is a multivariate analytic approach that analyzes the relations between two sets of variables (here, sociocultural environment and rsFC) and can accommodate multicollinearity between variables within a dataset. Following prior work4,5, PLSC decomposition generated three matrices that describe (i) variable loadings for rsFC measures, (ii) variable loadings for sociocultural measures, and (iii) all the singular values. These matrices were used to generate latent dimensions (LDs) that consisted of a latent variable pair (one column per data block), which were derived from linear combinations of the original variables (i.e., the variables measuring sociocultural environment and resting state functional connectivity) (Fig. 1B). To evaluate statistical significance, permutation testing was performed for each LD and bootstrapped standard error estimates were computed for each specific variable loading. Loadings with bootstrap ratios (BR) greater than 2.5 (corresponding to p < 0.01) were considered reliable and significant. Upon identifying significant LDs via PLSC, we conducted a regression analysis to examine how the sociocultural-rsFC patterns captured by the LDs relate to physical and mental health measures (Fig. 1C). Linear mixed effect models determined whether identified within- and between-network rsFC correlations were associated with physical and mental health measures, while adjusting for all identified confounding and precision covariates, including random effects to account for the structure of the sample including study site and family relationship.

Results

In examining how sociocultural environment at the individual, family, and community levels relates to rsFC among Hispanic youth, we found two significant latent dimensions (LD) of association. The LD1 accounted for about 29% of shared variance between sociocultural environment and rsFC, whereas LD2 accounted for about 20% of shared variance. Within LD1, Child Opportunity Index (COI) was positively associated with within-Default Mode Network (DMN) and within-Visual Network (VN) connectivity (Fig. 2A, B). Additionally, the NaNDA Disadvantage Index (NDI) was negatively associated within connectivity between the DMN and the Cingulo-Opercular Network (CON), Dorsal Attention Network (DAN), and VN (Fig. 2, A, B). Within LD2, the Caregiver Ethnic Identity Exploration (EIE-C), Ethnic Identity Commitment (EIC-C), Heritage Acculturation (HA-C) scores were inversely associated with connectivity between the DMN and Somatomotor Mouth Network (SMN), as well as the VN and Ventral Attention Network (VAN). Specifically, positive BR for the EIE-C, EIC-C, and HA-C scores were significantly associated with negative BR for the DMN-SMN and VN-VAN connectivity. Using the latent scores from the significant LDs, we first found a significant association between LD1 and the wheeze/whistle health measure (p = 0.037), but no significant associations for LD2. Next, using within- and between-network connectivity, we observed significant physical and mental health associations involving networks from both LDs. Specifically, within LD1, the Sleep (Munich Chronotype Questionnaire) chronotype was significantly associated with DMN-CON (p = 0.04) and DMN-DAN (p = 0.04) connectivity. Within LD2, mean blood pressure was significantly associated with DMN-SMN connectivity (p = 0.04). 

Conclusion

Our results highlight the influence of sociocultural environment on neurobiological pathways that underlie health outcomes among Hispanic youth. Specifically, latent brain-environment dimensions identified the most prominent sociocultural levels of influence were at the individual and community levels, with higher opportunity environments supporting widespread functioning of DMN, VN, CON, and DAN networks and caregiver cultural identity influencing sensory processing and youth perception of family context. Further, these patterns of associations between sociocultural and rsFC variables were linked to evening sleep chronotype and wheezing/whistling, which may represent sleep behaviors and asthma conditions disproportionately impacting Hispanic youth. Inverse associations between NDI and networks of cognitive control, goal-directed allocation, and visuospatial processing with sleep chronotype outcomes may help explain previous reports of maladaptive sleep patterns, such as late-night schedules linked to lower school performance. Sensory network associations with wheezing/whistling may indicate how neighborhood pollution, poor housing conditions, and limited access to care contribute to respiratory and asthma risk among Hispanic communities. Altogether, these results support our proposed holistic conceptual framework for Hispanic adolescent development, which emphasizes the multifaceted and multilevel nature of the sociocultural environment, and offers a foundation for culturally informed, tailored interventions that enhance well-being within Hispanic communities. Future work will examine whether distinct sociocultural dimensions differentially predict other health outcomes across adolescence, helping clarify their unique neurodevelopmental impact.
