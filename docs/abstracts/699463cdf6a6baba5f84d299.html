Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a highly prevalent and disabling psychiatric disorder. Recurrent depressive disorder (MDD-R), is characterized by repeated episodes of depression and is associated with more severe cognitive and functional impairments compared to single episode depressive disorder (MDD‑SE)1. Early rs-fMRI studies, together with later meta-analyses and reviews established the view of MDD as a disorder of large-scale functional systems, but they also noted substantial variability in the spatial patterns and directions of effects across studies. Furthermore, recent large multi-site initiatives have not converged on a unified connectivity signature of depression2,3. A substantial part of this heterogeneity stems from differences in analytic choices. Topological Data Analysis (TDA) through persistent homology provides a complementary framework capable of interrogating functional organization at a global, multiscale level, with reduced reliance on thresholding and analytic hyperparameters, as well as reported robustness to acquisition and preprocessing-related variability4,5. In this study, we apply TDA to the large dataset REST-meta-MDD Project of the DIRECT consortium2 to explore alterations in whole-brain functional connectivity (FC) in individuals with MDD with emphasis on the effect of recurrence, and the possible leverage of medication. We aim to provide new insights of complex interactions in MDD brain functional organization through a novel yet robust framework.

Methods

We utilized resting-state fMRI and phenotypic data from 1,490 individuals (776 MDD and 714 Controls, aged 18-65, 15 sites) obtained from the REST-meta-MDD Project. All scans were locally preprocessed at each site using an identical standardized protocol that included slice-timing correction, realignment, nuisance regression, spatial normalization, and global signal regression following the original REST-meta-MDD protocol2. Whole brain functional connectivity (FC) matrices were computed using Pearson cross-correlation between regions defined by the Power’s 264 atlas. Site-related confounding effects were controlled using Correcting Covariance Batch Effects (CovBat) harmonization, applied to subject-level connectivity features while preserving biological variables of interest.

Topological Data Analysis was applied using persistent homology. For each subject, correlation matrices were transformed into distance matrices (d = 1 − r) and used to construct Vietoris–Rips (VR) complexes across filtration values (Fig. 1A). In a VR complex, edges and triangles are formed when all pairwise distances between two or three nodes, respectively, fall below the filtration parameter ε. Thus, increasing ε corresponds to progressively relaxing the correlation threshold commonly used in network neuroscience (Fig. 1B). Persistence is defined then as the difference between the ε value at which a topological feature appears and the ε at which it disappears. The zeroth Betti number (B₀) quantifies connected components, whereas the first Betti number (B₁) quantifies one-dimensional cycles whose interior has not yet been filled by triangles. Betti curves summarize how the number of components or cycles evolves across filtration values (Fig. 1C). 

To obtain a single global descriptor of each subject’s topology across all scales, we computed the area under each Betti curve (AUC; see shaded regions in Fig. 1C).  B₀ AUC summarizes the global integration profile, reflecting how rapidly the network transitions from isolated nodes to a single connected component, whereas B₁ AUC captures the overall load and persistence of cycles across scales. In addition, for each subject we quantified the number of cycles contributing to B₁ AUC and their mean persistence. For anatomical interpretation, cycles were localized using atlas coordinates and classified as intra- or inter-network based on the network membership of their constituent nodes.

Associations between topological descriptors and clinical factors (diagnosis, illness chronicity, and medication status) were assessed using regression models matched to the statistical nature of each metric. B₀ AUC was analyzed using multiple linear regression, whereas B₁ AUC and mean persistence were analyzed using generalized linear models with a Gamma distribution and log link. Cycle counts were analyzed using negative binomial GLMs. All models included the clinical factor of interest, sex, age, years of education, and head motion (mean framewise displacement) as covariates. Group contrasts were evaluated using two-sided bootstrap tests (10,000 resamples) with false discovery rate (FDR) correction.

Results

Whole-brain topological organization differed significantly between individuals with major depressive disorder (MDD) and controls. In the full sample (n = 1,490), MDD was associated with higher B₀ AUC (Cohen’s d = 0.19, FDR-p < 0.001)  and B₁ AUC (exp(β) =1.05,  FDR-p <0.001), indicating altered large-scale integration and increased topological complexity of the functional connectome (Fig. 2A–B).

When stratifying by illness chronicity (n = 1,296), both single-episode (MDD-SE) and recurrent MDD (MDD-R) showed elevated B₀ AUC relative to controls  (MDD-SE: Cohen’s d = 0.16, FDR-p < 0.05; MDD-R: Cohen’s d = 0.22, FDR-p < 0.05), with no difference between subtypes. In contrast, B₁ AUC exhibited a clear gradient across chronicity, increasing progressively from controls to MDD-SE (exp(β) = 1.04 FDR-p < 0.01) and reaching the highest values in MDD-R (exp(β) = 1.11, FDR-p < 0.001), with MDD-R showing significantly higher values than MDD-SE (exp(β) = 1.08, FDR-p < 0.001) (Fig. 2C–D). These effects were not explained by illness duration or current symptom severity, as neither variable was significantly associated with B₀ AUC or B₁ AUC within the MDD group.

Medication status strongly modulated B₁ AUC effects. In participants with complete chronicity and medication data (n = 1,166), B₁ AUC was significantly elevated in unmedicated single-episode MDD (uMDD-SE; exp(β) = 1.07, FDR-p < 0.01) and medicated recurrent MDD (mMDD-R; exp(β) = 1.09, FDR-p < 0.01) relative to controls, whereas medicated single-episode (mMDD-SE) and unmedicated recurrent MDD (uMDD-R) did not differ from controls after correction (Fig. 2E). Direct contrasts further revealed lower B₁ AUC in mMDD-SE compared to uMDD-SE (exp(β) = 0.92, FDR-p < 0.01), and higher B₁ AUC in mMDD-R compared to mMDD-SE  (exp(β) = 1.11, FDR-p < 0.01), indicating a joint influence of chronicity and medication on whole-brain topology.

To clarify the source of the B₁ AUC increase, we decomposed it into the number of one-dimensional cycles and their mean persistence. Across diagnostic, chronicity, and medication-defined groups, B₁ AUC differences were consistently driven by a higher number of cycles, whereas mean persistence did not differ between groups. Anatomically, these excess cycles were predominantly inter-network rather than intra-network, suggesting increased redundancy across large-scale brain systems in MDD.

Conclusion

This study provides the first large-scale application of TDA persistent homology to resting-state functional connectomes in MDD. We show that MDD is characterized by systematic alterations in whole-brain topology, including increased B₀ AUC, but more prominently, elevated B₁ AUC, reflecting a weakened integration profile and an enhanced cycle load, respectively. Crucially, B₁ AUC showed graded sensitivity to illness chronicity and was strongly modulated by medication status. Decomposition analyses revealed that these effects were driven by an increased number of inter-network cycles rather than by greater mean cycle persistence. 

Together, our findings suggest that MDD is associated with a reconfiguration of whole-brain network topology, particularly affecting redundancy in cross-network interactions in a way sensitive to both disease progression and pharmacological treatment. Furthermore, by capturing global multiscale properties of brain organization hidden to conventional connectivity analyses, persistent homology offers a complementary framework for characterizing large-scale network dysfunction in MDD and represents a valuable addition to the methodological toolbox for studying complex brain disorders.
