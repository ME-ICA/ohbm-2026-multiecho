Introduction

Single unprovoked seizures are common with 1 in 10 people experiencing one in their lifetime. However, there is no effective way to predict who will develop epilepsy. It has been reported that high frequency oscillations (HFOs) associated with somatosensory evoked field (SEF) are prolonged in some patients with epilepsy.1 Therefore, it is a potential biomarker to identify those patients with a single unprovoked seizure who will develop epilepsy. It has been proposed that somatosensory HFOs represent localized activity of inhibitory interneurons receiving thalamocortical input that regulates the activity of excitatory pyramidal neurons. The aims of this prospective study were (1) to quantify somatosensory SEF HFOs in children with a single unprovoked seizure compared to typically developing peers, (2) to determine whether HFO characteristics differ between patients who do and do not later develop epilepsy, and (3) to examine candidate cellular and circuit mechanisms underlying SEFs using biophysical modeling of neocortical microcircuits. We hypothesized that prolonged late somatosensory HFOs would be observed in patients who later develop epilepsy and would reflect altered cortical inhibitory dynamics.

Methods

Participants with a diagnosis of a single unprovoked seizure, no other neurological disorder, and at least one year of clinical follow-up were prospectively recruited from the New Onset Seizure Clinic at Cincinnati Children’s Hospital Medical Center. Patients who were diagnosed with childhood epilepsy syndrome including childhood absence epilepsy and self-limited epilepsy with centrotemporal spikes (SeLECTS) were excluded. Age and sex matched typically developing peers were also recruited. All participants underwent volumetric T1-wighted MRI for MEG source localization purposes. SEFs were obtained with a whole-head 275-channel CTF-MEG system with 4kHz sampling rate. The electrical stimulation consisted of 200 microsecond monophasic rectangular pulses constant current applied to median nerves at the wrist bilaterally. Conventional N20m latencies were measured after 3-300Hz filter applied to the averaged data. HFO durations were measured, after the data was filtered from 400-800Hz, from the first to the last potential amplitude above 3 standard deviations from the averaged background noise and latency interval and from the ascending slope of the conventional N20m component to the ascending slope of the P30m. These were categorized in two segments; early and late HFOs divided by the N20m peak. Biophysical simulations of SEFs were performed using the Human Neocortical Neurosolver (HNN)2 to model candidate cellular and circuit mechanisms contributing to N20m generation and associated HFOs.

Results

Nineteen patients (age range: 6.7-17.8 years, mean: 11.2 years, F/M=7/12) with a single unprovoked seizure were included in this study. Seven patients were diagnosed with epilepsy, (1=idiopathic localized-related epilepsy: bifrontal and left central, 6=epilepsy undetermined as to focal or generalized). Twelve had not been diagnosed with epilepsy after one year since the single seizure diagnosis. Two patients were excluded; one was diagnosed with SeLECTS, the other was on anti-seizure medication at the time of MEG acquisition. Twenty-one age and sex matched typically developing controls were included. Somatosensory HFOs were not reliably detected when N20m amplitudes were below 300 fT in either group, indicating a minimum evoked response amplitude required for HFO detection. There were no significant differences in N20m latency and early HFO latency between control, epilepsy and non-epilepsy patients. The latency of late HFOs of the left median nerve were significantly prolonged in patients with epilepsy (5.1 ± 0.7ms, p=0.01) and without epilepsy (5.5 ± 2.2ms, p=0.005) compared to control group (2.3 ± 1.7ms). For the right median nerve, the late HFO latency was significantly prolonged in patients with epilepsy compared to the control (6.6 ± 4.5 vs. 4.6 ± 2.1ms, p=0.045). There were no differences in the early HFOs between groups. 

Biophysical simulations reproduced N20m generation but did not demonstrate group-specific differences under the current modeling assumptions.

Conclusion

This prospective study provides preliminary evidence that prolonged late somatosensory HFOs are associated with epilepsy diagnosis following a single unprovoked seizure. The findings support late HFOs as a candidate biomarker reflecting altered cortical inhibitory or thalamocortical dynamics rather than changes in early sensory processing. The absence of group-specific effects in biophysical simulations highlights the need for expanded modeling of inhibitory interneurons and thalamocortical drive. Larger cohorts and refined computational models may clarify the cellular mechanisms underlying pathological somatosensory HFOs and advance mechanistically informed biomarkers for epilepsy risk stratification.
