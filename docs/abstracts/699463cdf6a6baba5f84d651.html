Introduction

Colorectal cancer is the 3rd most commonly diagnosed cancer globally. Among survivors, approximately 40% exhibit significant cancer-related cognitive decline in executive functions. Despite their decreased performance during objective testing, many of the same survivors will subjectively report normal cognitive symptoms similar to healthy controls.1,2 The discordant nature of these symptoms greatly hinders the clinical detection of colorectal CRCI along with survivorship care aimed at mitigating its adverse effects. Using resting-state fMRI (rsFMRI) and graph theory analysis, we investigate the neural mechanisms underlying the discrepancies between objective and subjective colorectal CRCI symptoms.

Methods

Thirty-nine participants including 21 colorectal cancer survivors (stage I or II, age 60.7 ± 7.7 years) who had surgery without adjuvant therapy within 12 months and 18 age-matched healthy controls (HCs, age 57.3 ± 9.1 years). All participants had no prior malignancy or neurological disorders. Each completed a battery of objective neuropsychological tests and subjective questionnaires as detailed in Table 1. MRI was performed on a Siemens MAGNETOM Terra 7T scanner from which T1-weighted MP2RAGE (0.7 mm3 isotropic) and T2*-weighted BOLD rsfMRI (TR=1000 ms, 1.6 mm3 isotropic, 485 volumes) images were acquired with standard pre-processing applied.3

Graph theory analysis was based on the functional connectivity matrix of 90 AAL2 regions of interests. Using the GRETNA toolbox, sparse networks were generated across 7 sparsity threshold values (0.1 to 0.4, step 0.05) and analyzed to compute global and nodal metrics including small-world properties, network efficiency, and shortest path lengths. Prior to group comparisons, the area under the curve (AUC) of each metric across the full sparsity range was corrected for the effects of age, sex, education, depression, and anxiety using ordinary least squares regression. Nodal analysis included false discovery rate correction.

Results

Compared to HCs, the survivor group exhibited significantly lower information processing speed (p = 0.009) but similar perceived cognitive impairment (PCI, p = 0.214) as assessed by the TMT-A and FACT-Cog. No significant differences in HVLT, COWAT, BDI, or STAI scores were observed. In terms of MRI graph theory analysis, the survivor group showed significantly lower local (p = 0.008) and global network efficiencies (p = 0.023) along with significantly longer path lengths (p = 0.01) as seen in Figure 1. For HCs, the AUC of local (r = -0.589, p = 0.010) and global network efficiencies (r = -0.636, p = 0.005) were significantly and inversely correlated with PCI scores. This indicates a self-awareness mechanism where higher network efficiency is linked to more critical perceived cognition. In contrast, the survivors showed weakly positive correlations (local efficiency: r = 0.253; global efficiency: r = 0.277), suggesting that their subjective cognitive report was decoupled from their measured network efficiency. Additionally in HCs, the AUC of the nodal local efficiency of the posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) was inversely correlated with PCI scores (left PCC: r = -0.595, p = 0.009; right PCC: r = -0.669, p = 0.002). This strong correlation was absent in the survivor group and further supports the theory that the PCC's integrity drives cognitive self-awareness in healthy individuals.

Conclusion

Our results suggest that network alterations in the PCC may be responsible for the discrepancies observed between objective and subjective CRCI in colorectal cancer survivors. This brain region is often associated with self-referential introspection and the non-significant correlation between the survivor's network efficiencies and their PCI within this region may explain their lack of insight into their own objective cognitive deficits.
