Introduction

Uncovering the mechanistic link between the anatomical structure and functional activity of the brain, dubbed the structure-function relationship, is of keen interest in the field of neuroscience.1 Prior work has suggested that macroscopic, region-level neuronal activity can be largely explained by the flow of electrophysiological signals along the white matter tract connective network in the brain, and does not depend on complex and nonlinear microscopic neuronal wiring.2 Model based approaches to predicting function from structure thus often utilize the MRI-derived brain structural connectome (SC) graph, which encodes gray matter brain regions as nodes and white matter tract connections as edges weighted by empirically determined connectivity strength. These graph based models can then predict the brain’s functional activity and how the brain will respond to external stimulation, such as that delivered by deep brain stimulation (DBS) or transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS).

A wide variety of graph based models have been employed to capture the brain’s structure-function relationship, from dynamic system and neural mass models to deep learning based approaches. This study focuses on the previously proposed Spectral Graph Model (SGM).2,3,4 Key attractive features of the SGM include its linearity, parsimonious biophysical parameter set, and demonstrated ability to capture the brain’s resting state power spectral distribution (PSD) and functional connectivity (FC) as derived from functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and magnetoencaphalography (MEG). However, prior work has suggested that the model may still be over-parameterized. In this work, we demonstrate two advances: 1) reduce the parameterization of the SGM while maintaining the model’s ability to capture rich dynamics in FC and PSD as well as accurately fit empirically observed resting state functional data; and 2) utilize the SGM to predict the brain’s response to external stimulation.

Methods

Model Theory. The SGM is a model of brain oscillations, represented via eigen-decomposition of the Laplacian of the structural connectivity matrix.2 The SGM has a linear, closed form solution in the frequency domain of the following form:

X(ω) = (jωI + τg-1Fg(ω)ℒ(ω))-1Hlocal(ω)P(ω)

where X(ω) is the frequency domain solution of macroscopic brain signals, I is the identity matrix, Fg(ω) is the fourier transform of a neural response function, ℒ(ω) is the complex Laplacian derived from the brain’s complex structural connectome graph, Hlocal(ω) is a transfer function capturing activity of populations of neurons, P(ω) is the Fourier transform of the external or region-specific driving signal, and w is angular frequency, and τg is a model parameter.2,3,4,5

Identifiability Study. We perform a study in order to determine the identifiability of parameters in the SGM. To do this, we randomly sample 100 sets of SGM parameters, θsample,n from a uniform distribution within the previously specified biophysically plausible range for each parameter and simulate the brains FC and PSD using the SGM.2,3,4 We then go backwards, and use SGM model inference to predict SGM parameters θinfered,n from the simulated PSD and FC. Inferring SGM parameters from FC and PSD is accomplished using simulation based inference (SBI) as previously specified.4 By comparing θsample,n to θinfered,n we can analyze SBI's ability to recover SGM model parameters as well as the examine the identifiability of each model parameter.

Dimensionality Reduction. We propose a parameter reduction to the SGM. This parameter reduction is accomplished by setting a subset of the original SGM model parameters to constant values, and by constraining multiple parameters to adopt the same value. Constant values for parameters are assigned in a manner that keeps the SGM model solutions stable and biophysically reasonable. This reduction reduces the parameter set of the SGM from (τe, τi, gei, gii, τg, v, α) to (τe, τi, α). Only 3 parameters are required to fit the reduced SGM to regional power spectra, and only two parameters are required to fit the reduced SGM to FC following this proposed model dimensionality reduction. To test the Reduced-SGM model's efficacy in predicting empirical PSD and FC, we use SBI to fit both the full SGM and parameter-reduced SGM, denoted here as Reduced-SGM, to a dataset of subject specific resting state FC and PSDs derived from MEG from a 36 subject cohort used previously in SGM studies, and observe whether the parameter reduction affects the model’s ability to capture empirically observed functional recordings of the brain.

External Stimulation. In previous studies the SGM has assumed that the driving signal, P(ω) at each region is uncorrelated across regions and time when predicting FC and PSD. However, we can modify this assumption to predict the brain’s functional behavior when driven by an external source. When computing both the PSD and FC using the SGM assuming that P(ω) is uncorrelated across regions and time, the term falls out of the closed form equation for FC and PSD, as both compute the expectation E(P(ω)PH(ω)) = σ2I, where σ2 is the variance (which disappears after normalization). Instead you can assume that a driving signal causes the expectation of driving noise P to be given as follows:

E(P(ω)PH(ω)) = σ2I + s(ω)sT(ω)

where s(ω) is the vector specifying the stimulation magnitude for each region at signal frequency ω = 2πf for frequency f in Hz. We then compare the FC and PSD that the SGM predicts for stimulated and unstimulated brains under varying sets of simulation regions, frequencies, and magnitude, as well as SGM parameters to those predicted by the SGM under unstimulated conditions.

Results

Uncovering Parameter Identifiability. The results of the model identifiability study indicate that SBI based parameter inference is able to recover most of the SGM model parameters with a high degree of fidelity, but that some of the model parameters display a poor correlation between true and recovered parameter values when using SBI to infer parameters from simulated FC and PSD. Namely, the v, τe, τg, and τi parameters each display high degree of identifiability, while the α, gee, and gii parameters do not demonstrate recoverability, indicating that these parameters may lack identifiability in the SGM model.

Reduced-SGM Captures Empirical MEG Functional Activity. Despite lowering the dimensionality of the SGM by reducing the number of biophysical parameters that can vary from seven to three when predicting PSD and from three to two when predicting FC, the Reduced-SGM is able to capture a rich set of outputs in PSD and FC, such as varying number of local peak frequencies and regional signal power in PSD, and variety in region-specific correlations in FC. The Reduced-SGM also demonstrates a preserved ability to capture empirical subject-specific resting-state FC and PSD as derived from MEG recordings as compared to the higher dimensional SGM. The model dimensionality reduction lowers the pearson’s r correlation coefficient metric between empirical and model-fit FC by 0.004 on average, and lowered the pearson’s r metric between empirical and model-fit PSD by 0.011 on average (both over a cohort of 36 subjects), neither of which are statistically significant. 

External Stimulation’s Effect on Functional Activity. SGM simulations display previously unseen patterns of outputs in PSD and FC when driven by an external stimulatory signal. The external stimulation has three aspects that can be varied: The frequency of signal, the regions of the brain that are stimulated, and the magnitude (or intensity) of the driving signal. Modulating each aspect of the driving signal has a different impact on the simulated brain activity as predicted by the SGM. Modulating the frequencies of the driving signal tunes the frequency band that is altered in the stimulated power spectra. In other words, the effect of stimulation in PSD shows no ‘leakage’ outside of frequencies that are specified by the stimulation, as the SGM models signals as exhibiting frequency specific responses in the brain. Increasing the intensity of the stimulation signal predictably has the most direct impact on the regions that are stimulated, which are where the largest increases in regional power are observed. However, there is also a notable increase in signal power in regions that are connected to the stimulated regions (corresponding to adjacent edges on the brain’s structural connectome) but not directly stimulated by the driving signal. A general pattern that emerges is that the stronger the white matter tract connection between a stimulated and an unstimulated region, the greater the increase in regional power in the unstimulated region. This principle applies also when modulating the regions that are stimulated, and when observing the effect of stimulation on predicted FC. The greatest observed effect on FC due to stimulation is observed in correlations between the stimulated regions and regions they are strongly connected to. However, more subtle secondary effects are also observed, such as an increase in regional signal correlation between regions that are not themselves stimulated, but which are strongly connected to stimulated regions.

Conclusion

The results of the parameter identifiability study on the SGM suggest that the model may be overparameterized, and that exploring dimensionality reduction methods to the model is warranted. Specific parameters of the SGM display poor correlation between ground truth (randomly sampled) values and SBI-inferred values from simulated FC and PSD (originating from the randomly sampled parameters), meaning that they may have minimal impact on modulating global patterns of electrophysiological signals in functional recordings of brain activity, and can be reduced out of the model. The parameter reduction method proposed here successfully reduces the dimensionality of the SGM while maintaining the model’s ability to capture a diverse space of output power spectra and functional connectivities. The parameter reduced SGM model also preserves the previously demonstrated ability of the SGM to capture empirically observed subject-specific resting-state FC and PSD obtained from MEG recordings of a 36 subject cohort of healthy adults. 

The effect of externally stimulating the brain on FC and PSD as predicted by the SGM reveals nontrivial effects on brain function. As expected, the largest changes in brain function between stimulated and unstimulated brains occur in regions that are directly stimulated, with the effect size growing as the intensity of the stimulating signal increases. However, there are notable secondary effects, such as increase in regional signal power and interregional signal correlations that are observed in unstimulated brain regions as well, and which are not trivially understood. These effects appear more prominently in regions with strong connections to stimulated regions on the brain’s structural connectome, which intuitively makes sense as the SGM models signal transmission along edges of this network representation of the brain. These secondary effects are important in gaining a holistic understanding of the predicted impact of brain stimulation on brain function. Brain stimulation techniques have steadily garnered acceptance in research and clinical settings, such as TMS for treatment-resistant depression and DBS to treat neurological conditions such as Epilepsy, Parkinson’s disease and even obsessive-compulsive disorder. The ability to model the effect of these types of external stimulations on brain function can thus provide support for such practices through various means, such as identifying possible candidate brain regions to stimulate in treatment, and understanding the impact of modulating the driving signal prior to in-vivo testing.
