Introduction

Schizophrenia spectrum disorders (SSD) involve positive, negative, and cognitive symptoms, with cognitive impairments strongly tied to functional outcomes yet poorly addressed by current treatments. Reduced P300 amplitude is one of the most robust neural markers of these deficits 1 and is particularly pronounced in patients with higher symptom severity.2,3 The P3b subcomponent of P300 reflects top-down attentional allocation and memory updating,1 and its generators include the ACC and DLPFC regions that show consistent structural and functional abnormalities in SSD4,5 and may contribute to diminished P3b amplitudes. Because excitation–inhibition imbalance involving glutamatergic and GABAergic systems is thought to underlie dysfunction in these regions and pharmacological disruption of these systems reduces P3b amplitude in healthy sample,1 examining ACC and DLPFC GABA and Glx levels in relation to P3b abnormalities may help clarify the neurochemical basis of cognitive deficits in SSD.

Methods

We studied 214 participants (107 SSD, 107 HC), assessing diagnosis with the MINI, symptoms with the PANSS, and cognition with the BACS, and clustered SSD patients into lower- (SSD–, n=65) and higher-symptom (SSD+, n=41) groups using k-means on PANSS scores. Auditory P3b event-related potentials were recorded during an oddball task, and amplitudes and latencies were extracted from frontal, central, and parietal electrode clusters. Single-voxel ¹H-MRS was acquired with MEGA-sLASER in the ACC or left DLPFC (lDLPFC), and GABA and Glx were quantified using Osprey and LCModel. All analyses controlled for age and sex, including ANCOVAs for group differences, linear regressions with bootstrap CIs for metabolite–P3b associations, and partial correlations for links to cognition and behavior.

Results

Patients had slower oddball task reaction times (F(1,210)=16.75, p<.001) and lower accuracy (F(1,210)=7.47, p=.007) than controls. P3b amplitudes were reduced in SSD at central (F(1,206)=9.52, p=.002, pFDR=.007) and parietal sites (F(1,209)=29.22, p<.001, pFDR<.001). SSD+ showed significant reductions at parietal sites (EMM=−2.91, pFDR=.004) and trend-level reductions at central and frontal sites, whereas SSD– showed only a trend-level reduction at parietal electrodes (pFDR=.069). Latencies did not differ. ACC metabolites did not differ between groups. In the lDLPFC, GABA showed a trend toward elevation in SSD (F(1,94)=3.65, p=.059) and was significantly higher in SSD– compared to controls (F(1,84)=5.15, p=.026). lDLPFC GABA levels were positively associated with P3b amplitudes at central (B=4.42, p=.001) and parietal (B=3.08, p=.020) sites within SSD, supported by significant GABA × Group interactions (central: B=5.94, p=.020; parietal: B=4.69, p=.048). Cluster analyses confirmed these associations only in SSD– (central: B=3.65, p=.024; parietal: B=3.05, p=.031). Across SSD groups, higher P3b amplitudes correlated with better behavioral performance (accuracy r≈.29–.37, ps≤.003) and faster responses (parietal r=−.27, p=.006), whereas lDLPFC GABA showed no direct cognitive or behavioral associations. Glx levels (ACC or lDLPFC) showed no group differences or P3b relationships.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that P3b amplitude reductions reflect cognitive dysfunction in SSD and are more pronounced in patients with higher symptom severity. Elevated lDLPFC GABA in patients, particularly in lower-symptom patients, was positively associated with P3b amplitudes, indicating that enhanced inhibitory tone in this region may help offset neurophysiological reductions, although the functional significance of this relationship remains tentative. Overall, these results provide initial in vivo evidence linking regional GABAergic function to cortical electrophysiological patterns associated with cognitive impairment in SSD, highlighting the need for further work to determine whether such GABAergic changes reflect compensatory or illness-related processes.
