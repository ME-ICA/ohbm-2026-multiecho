Introduction

Machiavellianism, one of the Dark Triad, is characterized by amorality, desire for control, desire for status, and distrust of others 1. Higher Machiavellian traits have been linked to flexible goal-directed control and strategic prosociality, which further contribute to the job performance 2. Prior neuroimaging studies have identified associated brain areas of these traits 2. However, it remains unclear how human brains configure their functional organization to support Machiavellian traits. Here, we adopted an eigenmode-based approach 3 to quantify segregation and integration of functional brain network and explore how Machiavellian tendencies emerge from interactions among large-scale brain systems.

Methods

We recruited 108 healthy adults (age = 24 ± 4.58 years, 34 males) and collected their resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) data. To assess their Machiavellian traits, all participants were required to complete a Machiavellian Personality Scale (MPS) 4 which has four dimensions: (a) amorality (“Amorality”), (b) desire for control (“Control”), (c) distrust of others (“Distrust”), and (d) desire for status (“Status”). The rs-fMRI data were first preprocessed with a typical pipeline, including steps of slice timing, realignment, spatial normalization, smoothing, regressing nuisance variables (i.e., head motion parameters, white matter and cerebrospinal fluid signals), and filtering. We constructed a functional connectivity (FC) matrix (i.e., functional network) using Schaefer100 atlas for each participant.

The FC matrix C was first decomposed as C = UΛUT with eigenvectors U and eigenvalues Λ. We then applied the nested-spectral partition (NSP) method 5 based on eigenmodes to detect the hierarchical modules in functional networks. Integration index HIn was defined as the functional interaction at the first level, which represent a single module comprising whole brain network: HIn = H1/N = Λ12M1(1-p1)/N2, where Λ12M1 is the weighted module number normalized by region number N, and p1 is a corrected factor. Accordingly, segregation index was defined as the sum of functional interactions across the other levels: HSe = ∑Hi/N = ∑Λi2Mi(1-pi)/N2 (i ≥ 2). The balance between integration and segregation was defined as the difference between the two indices Hb = HIn – HSe. 

For subnetwork analysis, the 100 brain regions were assigned to 7 subnetworks according to Yeo’s partition. Then, we conducted Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA) to explore association between network integration/segregation indices (HIn, HSe, and Hb) of the seven subnetworks and the four dimensions of MPS. For regional network, a principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the regional integration/segregation indices to reduce the dimensions before running CCA. Effects of age and sex were controlled in the above CCA analyses. Finally, to explore neurobiological mechanism underlying the Machiavellian regional pattern, we performed a meta-analysis with 28 neurotransmitter-specific maps obtained from positron emission tomography (PET) or single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) data.

Results

At the subnetwork level, CCA analysis showed a significant association between the subnetwork segregation (HSe) of default mode network (DMN) and control network (CN) and Amorality and Control dimensions (first canonical r = 0.46, p = 0.004) (Fig. 1A). For subnetwork integration (HIn), the first canonical correlation between HIn of DMN and dorsal attention network (DAN) and Amorality and Control dimensions is marginally significant (r = 0.43, p = 0.059) (Fig. 1B). For subnetwork integration-segregation balance (Hb), the first canonical correlation between Hb of CN and somatomotor network (SMN) and Distrust is marginally significant (r = 0.45, p = 0.088) (Fig. 1C). 

At the regional level, only regional Hb exhibited marginally significant correlation between Hb of posterior part of DAN and visual cortex with Amorality and Control component (first canonical r = 0.45, p = 0.053) (Fig. 1D). Moreover, the Machiavellianism related Hb significantly associated with the transmitter maps of serotonin, dopamine, acetylcholine, and glutamate (p < 0.05, FDR corrected) (Fig. 1E), suggesting that these transmitters underlay the functional reconfiguration related to certain Machiavellian traits.

Conclusion

Our findings delineated a comprehensive network mechanism for Machiavellian traits, integrating evidence of integration and segregation at different scales. Furthermore, different dimensions of Machiavellianism exhibited unique representations in terms of integration-segregation in human brain network. This architecture appears to support flexible, context-dependent social strategizing in individuals with Machiavellian traits. Overall, our work refines the neurobiological basis of Machiavellianism and underscores the potential utility of network integration-segregation balance as biomarker of social cognition.
