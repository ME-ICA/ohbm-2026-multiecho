Introduction

1. Introduction

Frontotemporal lobar degeneration (FTLD) ^1 devastates patients and families yet remains substantially under-recognized compared with Alzheimer’s disease (AD). As the leading cause of early-onset dementia and the second most common cause overall, FTLD affects individuals in midlife and leads to profound decline across behavioral, language, and motor domains. While neuroimaging has revealed robust and syndrome-specific patterns of cortical atrophy, white matter hyperintensities (WMH),^2 a routinely observed MRI marker, remain poorly characterized in FTLD despite their established clinical value in other neurodegenerative disorders.

In AD, WMH are now understood to reflect not only vascular injury but also amyloid-related small vessel pathology, neuroinflammation, and glymphatic dysfunction. In FTLD, however, the evidence base is remarkably sparse. Our systematic review identified only 14 relevant studies: most focused on genetic cohorts and often limited by small samples. Non-genetic studies have primarily examined bvFTD or pooled all FTLD syndromes to contrast with other diseases, obscuring meaningful within-spectrum variability. Only two studies have compared WMH across multiple FTLD syndromes, both relying on the same open dataset, limiting demographic diversity, replication, and generalizability. Critically, WMH have not been systematically studied across the full FTLD spectrum, and no investigation has been conducted in German cohorts.

The present study aimed to systematically evaluate WMH across the full FTLD spectrum, as well as AD, MCI, and logopenic variant PPA (lvPPA) as control cohorts, using Fazekas visual rating scale.^3,4 Specifically, we aimed to (1) determine the prevalence and distribution of clinically relevant WMH across FTLD syndromes; (2) assess the influence of demographic and disease-related factors, like age, sex, education, and disease duration; and (3) investigate the association between WMH severity and cognitive impairment.

Methods

2. Methods

2.1. Participants

Data for analyses were obtained from the multi-centric FTLD consortium study in Germany (http://www.ftld.de/). All participants underwent a comprehensive standardized battery of clinical, neuropsychological, biomarker, and neuroimaging assessment in accordance with strict standard operating procedures (SOPs). Patients were classified according to established international diagnostic consensus criteria. Following the application of predefined exclusion criteria, a total of 971 participants classified into ten diagnostic groups were included for further analyses. Detailed information on participant’s selection is presented in Figure 1. 

2.2. Assessment of white matter hyperintensity

White matter hyperintensities were visually rated on axial FLAIR by an experienced rater using the Fazekas scale, which grades periventricular hyperintensities (PVH) and deep white matter hyperintensities (DWMH) separately on a 0 – 3 scale, with higher scores indicating greater severity. To provide a more detailed regional assessment, both PVH and DWMH were rated separately in the left and right hemispheres, yielding four Fazekas scores per participant. In addition, we derived a modified overall Fazekas score, defined as the maximum rating across all regions, as this measure was considered to best capture clinically relevant WMH burden

2.3. Statistical analysis

From a clinical perspective, a Fazekas score one is considered normal in elderly while greater than one is considered indicative of clinical pathology. Therefore, we first examined the distribution and prevalence of WMH (overall Fazekas score >1) across diagnostic groups and subsequently tested whether prevalence rates differed significantly among groups. ^5 Group comparisons were performed using the Chi-square test, followed by pairwise comparisons with FDR correction for multiple comparisons, when the overall test was significant.

To investigate how WMH burden, measured by Fazekas scores, is associated with diagnostic group and demographic variables, a series of regression models were conducted. We first conducted ordinal logistic regression (OLR) to evaluate the association between WMH burden and diagnostic group, while adjusting for age, sex, education, disease duration, and interactions between diagnosis and each covariate. A stepwise model-building strategy was employed. The initial model included age and diagnosis, and sequentially we added the other covariates, i.e., sex, education, disease duration and their interaction with diagnosis. Pos-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted, when the main effect of diagnosis was significant. 

To further explore whether the effect of age on WMH burden was linear or nonlinear, we fitted three types of regression models (i.e., linear regression, polynomial regression, and restricted cubic spline regression) to each subscale. Models were compared using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and coefficient of determination (R2). Exploratory two-stage piecewise linear regression was conducted if nonlinearity of age-WMH burden association was observed to detect 1) onset breakpoint: the earliest age at which the association between age and WMH burden became significant. This was defined by a shift from a non-significant slope before the breakpoint to a significant slope afterwards; 2) acceleration breakpoint: from the onset point onward, we searched for a second age breakpoint where the slope of WMH accumulation increased significantly, indicating an acceleration in severity. 

To investigate the association between WMH burden and cognitive performance and severity of dementia, we performed a partial Spearman correlation adjusting the effects of age and sex. 

False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to control for multiple comparisons, with a significant threshold set at FDR-adjusted p < 0.05.

Results

3. Results

3.1. Prevalence of white matter hyperintensities significantly varies in dementia syndromes

The distribution of Fazekas scores across groups is summarized in Figure 2. The prevalence of clinically relevant WMH (Fazekas > 1) varied substantially across 10 groups (Figure 2A). The lowest prevalence rates were observed in CBS (12.1%), MCI (15.9%), HC (18.8%), and bvFTD (18.8%), all below 20%. In contrast, higher rates (>30%) were found in AD, nfvPPA, lvPPA, PSP, and ALS+bvFTD; with ALS+bvFTD showing the highest prevalence (41.7%). 

Across all ten groups, the prevalence of clinically relevant WMH (overall Fazekas > 1) differed significantly (). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons (Figure 2B) indicated that prevalence of WMH was significantly higher in ALS+bvFTD and PSP than in groups with prevalence rates below 20%. Conversely, CBS and bvFTD showed significantly lower prevalence compared with all groups exceeding 30%. Distribution analyses of Fazekas scores further revealed diagnostic group-specific patterns (Figure 2C). Specifically, the result showed a clear gradient from predominantly absent or mild WMH in HC, MCI, CBS, and bvFTD to increasing moderate-severe WMH in svPPA, nfvPPA, lvPPA, AD, PSP, ALS+bvFTD. Finally, hemisphere breakdown of Fazekas scores indicated largely symmetrical distributions across hemispheres, without systematic lateralization effects (Figure 2D-E).

3.2. Severity of white matter hyperintensities in dementia is related to aging

OLR models revealed a robust and consistent effect of age on WMH burden across all subscales, independent of sex, education, disease duration, diagnostic groups, and their interactions with diagnostic group. Higher age was associated with higher Fazekas scores, indicating that increasing age is associated with greater WMH severity. 

Using two-stage piecewise regression, we identified consistent onset breakpoints at approximately age 43 across all subscales, where the slope of age-WMH association shifted from non-significant to significant positive. Prior to this point, WMH burden showed no significant association with age, indicating minimal accumulation in early to mid-adulthood. A subsequent acceleration breakpoint was observed between ages 63 and 73, marking a further steepening of WMH accumulation with aging. 

3.3. Severity of white matter hyperintensities varies in dementia syndromes also if controlled for aging

Diagnosis showed a significant main effect across all stepwise models, from the basis (i.e., age + diagnosis) to the fully adjusted model (i.e., age + diagnosis + sex + disease duration + education). In initial models (i.e., including age and diagnosis), patients with ALS+bvFTD showed significantly greater PVH burden compared to multiple diagnostic groups, including MCI, CBS, AD, bvFTD, and nfvPPA. PSP patients also exhibited higher PVH burden than MCI in the left hemisphere. For DWMH, patients with lvPPA, nfvPA, AD, and MCI demonstrated significantly higher severity than CBS, while DWHM load in lvPPA and AD groups also exceeded that in bvFTD. These patterns remained largely consistent, when education and disease duration were included in the models. Notably, the inclusion of sex, either as a single covariate or alongside other factors, eliminated diagnosis-related effects on PVH, whereas DWMH differences remained robust. 

3.4. Severity of white matter hyperintensities is related to cognitive impairment

Partial spearman correlation analyses revealed a significant negative association between MMSE and WMH after adjusting for age and sex. This finding was consistently observed across all WMH subscales, although the effect sizes were small. In contrast, no significant associations were found between WMH and either global CDR or FTLD-CDR scores.

Conclusion

4. Conclusions

In this large multicenter study, we systematically quantified WMH across the full spectrum of FTLD syndromes, as well as AD, lvPPA, and MCI, using the Fazekas scale. WMH burden increased strongly with age, showing minimal accumulation before midlife (≈43 years) and accelerated progression after 63 years for PVH and after 70–73 years for deep WMH (DWMH). WMH severity differed significantly across syndromes, with the highest burden in ALS+bvFTD and PSP and the lowest in CBS, MCI, HC, and bvFTD. WMH severity was modestly but significantly associated with global cognition (MMSE), but not with dementia-specific severity measures (CDR/FTLD-CDR).

WMH burden was unevenly distributed across syndromes in both prevalence and regression analyses. Clinically relevant WMH (Fazekas >1) was uncommon in HC, MCI, CBS, and bvFTD (<20%), but frequent in AD, nfvPPA, lvPPA, PSP, and ALS+bvFTD (>30%), with the highest prevalence in ALS+bvFTD (>40%). Ordinal regression revealed distinct patterns for PVH and DWMH. PVH differences were driven mainly by ALS+bvFTD and, to a lesser extent, PSP. In contrast, DWMH showed broader group effects, with higher burden in AD, lvPPA, nfvPPA, and MCI compared with CBS, and greater DWMH in AD and lvPPA than in bvFTD.

ALS+bvFTD exhibited the highest burden of clinically relevant WMH, predominantly due to PVH. The periventricular region is particularly vulnerable because of its watershed vascular supply, dependence on cerebrospinal fluid clearance, and susceptibility to venous collagenosis and ischemia. These vulnerabilities may be amplified in ALS+bvFTD by degeneration of periventricular projection fibers, suggesting a convergence of vascular susceptibility and disease-specific neurodegeneration. Confirmation in independent cohorts is warranted.

In contrast, bvFTD showed low WMH prevalence and significantly lower DWMH burden than AD and lvPPA, differing from several prior volumetric studies. These discrepancies likely reflect methodological differences, as volumetric approaches capture total WMH load, including small, non-confluent lesions, whereas Fazekas ratings emphasize confluent, clinically relevant lesions. Cohort characteristics may also contribute, underscoring the need for studies directly comparing visual and volumetric WMH measures in diverse populations.

Although effect sizes were small, WMH burden was negatively associated with global cognitive performance, consistent with prior evidence linking WMH to cognitive impairment. The lack of association with dementia severity scales in our study may reflect the lower sensitivity of visual rating compared with volumetric quantification for detecting subtle lesion effects.

In summary, WMH burden varies substantially across the FTLD–AD spectrum. PVH is most pronounced in ALS+bvFTD, whereas DWMH provides greater discriminatory power between AD, lvPPA, and bvFTD. Age is the strongest determinant of WMH, with onset in midlife and accelerated accumulation after the mid-sixties. These findings support the clinical utility of Fazekas ratings for capturing syndrome-specific WMH patterns and their cognitive relevance.
