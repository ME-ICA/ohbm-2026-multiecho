Introduction

Schizophrenia is a psychiatric disorder with positive, negative, and cognitive symptoms that have been linked to abnormalities in neural oscillations and large-scale brain networks. Among high-frequency population events, sharp-wave ripples (SPW-Rs) are brief, highly synchronous oscillations that support memory consolidation and transient coordination between hippocampus and neocortex.

However, the basic properties of human ripples in noninvasive recordings—such as their frequency-specific characteristics, spatial distribution, and organization across large-scale networks—remain incompletely understood. In particular, it is unclear how hippocampal and cortical ripple generators differ in their topography and coupling, and how these features are altered in schizophrenia.

Recent work suggests that ripple dynamics are abnormal in schizophrenia. A PET–MEG study by Nour and colleagues reported elevated hippocampal ripple power that covaried with NMDA receptor availability, and our previous MEG study in awake patients demonstrated increased ripple occurrences and less organized spatiotemporal patterns associated with positive symptom severity. Yet these studies did not distinguish hippocampal from cortical ripple sources or characterize ripple-based interactions across cortical networks.

In the present study, we combined high-frequency magnetoencephalography (MEG) with ripple-specific source reconstruction to separately characterize hippocampal and cortical SPW-R–like events in schizophrenia and healthy controls across two independent sites. We quantified their frequency-specific spatial distribution, temporal coordination, and transition patterns between hippocampal and cortical systems, and examined how these measures relate to clinical symptom dimensions. We hypothesized that schizophrenia would be characterized by increased ripple occurrence, altered distribution of cortical ripples across canonical networks, and reduced hippocampo–cortical transitions.

Methods

We analyzed resting-state MEG data from 74 healthy controls (HC) and 45 patients with schizophrenia (SZ) recruited at Gunma University Hospital and Kumagaya General Hospital (2014–2024). Diagnoses were confirmed by SCID, and PANSS, GAF, and premorbid IQ (JART) were assessed. All participants provided written informed consent. The study was approved by the Gunma University Ethical Review Board for Medical Research Involving Human Subjects and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

MEG Acquisition and Preprocessing

At Gunma, MEG was recorded using a 306-channel Elekta Neuromag system (1001.6 Hz; online low-pass 260 Hz). At Kumagaya, a 160-channel RICOH system was used (2000 Hz; low-pass 500 Hz). Eyes-open resting data (5 min) were analyzed. Environmental noise was removed using MaxFilter (Gunma) or dual signal subspace projection (DSSP; Kumagaya). Both sites applied oversampled temporal projection (OTP). ECG/EOG artifacts were removed using SSP and ICA. No low-pass filtering below 250 Hz was applied to preserve high-frequency content.

Ripple Detection

Ripple detection followed a GPU-accelerated implementation (Ohki et al., 2024). Signals were band-pass filtered (80–240 Hz at Gunma; 80–480 Hz at Kumagaya), rectified, and iteratively thresholded using gamma-distribution fitting (α = 0.01). Ripple candidates were defined as ≥5 suprathreshold peaks occurring within ≥6 cycles on the same sensor. Adjacent peaks were merged within sensors.

Frequency Classification

For each detected ripple, power spectral density (PSD) was computed using Welch’s method (200 ms windows, 50% overlap). PSDs were normalized within the ripple band, and events were assigned to the frequency bin with the maximum normalized peak (80–240 Hz; additional 280–480 Hz bins at Kumagaya).

Ripple-gedMEG and Source Reconstruction

Ripple-related spatial filters were obtained using generalized eigendecomposition of ripple-band vs broadband covariance matrices. Source estimation was performed with LCMV beamforming using MRI-derived forward models. Ripple-locked source-power maps were morphed to fsaverage for group analyses.

Event Consolidation and Classification

Two-stage consolidation removed redundant detections. Events with ≤10 ms temporal overlap, ≤10 mm spatial distance, or PSD correlation ≥0.85 were merged. Remaining near-duplicates within the same anatomical/functional label were also combined. Events with hippocampal ROI power ≥2× surrounding cortex were classified as hippocampal; all others were labeled cortical.

Cortical Network Assignment

Cortical ripples were mapped to the Yeo-7 networks (Visual, Somatomotor, Dorsal Attention, Ventral Attention, Limbic, Frontoparietal, Default Mode) based on peak source locations in fsaverage space. Event counts were normalized by network voxel size (events per 1000 voxels).

Ripple Characterization

Ripple counts were computed separately for hippocampal and cortical events. Left–right hippocampal counts were compared using within-subject permutation tests (10,000 swaps). Temporal coordination was assessed using co-occurrence fractions across ±50–1000 ms windows and inter-event intervals (0–500 ms) for HIP (hippocanpus)→HIP, HIP→CTX (cortex), CTX→HIP, and CTX→CTX transitions.

Statistical Analyses

Between-group differences in ripple counts, durations, and power were tested using non-parametric permutation tests with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction. Associations between ripple activity and psychiatric symptoms were evaluated using negative-binomial generalized linear models, with ripple count as the dependent variable and PANSS or GAF scores as predictors, controlling for age, sex, IQ, sleepiness, antipsychotic dose, and site. Network-wise ripple densities were examined using multiple linear regression. Ripple transition shares were modeled using binomial GLMs with total ripple load as a covariate. HC3 robust standard errors were applied in all models.

Results

Spatial Distribution of Hippocampal and Cortical Ripples

Hippocampal ripples were localized to the bilateral hippocampal formation across all frequencies (80–240 Hz) in both HC and SZ, with no group differences in spatial extent. Cortical ripples were broadly distributed across the Yeo-7 networks (Fig2a). After voxel normalization, overall topography was similar across groups; however, SZ showed frequency-specific deviations. Ripple density was reduced in limbic regions at 80 Hz, reduced in frontoparietal and increased in somatomotor and ventral attention networks at 160 Hz, and reduced in default mode with increased dorsal attention engagement at 240 Hz. These findings indicate preserved global organization but altered frequency-specific redistribution in SZ.

Basic Characteristics of Ripple Events

Ripple events occurred abundantly across all frequencies. Cortical ripples outnumbered hippocampal ripples (~4.7× in HC; ~5.2× in SZ). Event counts peaked near 120 Hz. No hemispheric lateralization was observed for hippocampal ripples. Temporal analyses showed that hippocampal and cortical ripples frequently clustered within short windows. Cortex–cortex co-occurrence was highest, followed by hippocampo–cortical pairs. Inter-event intervals were shortest for CTX→CTX and longest for HIP→HIP. SZ exhibited reduced hippocampo–cortical transitions (HIP→CTX, CTX→HIP), whereas CTX→CTX and HIP→HIP did not differ from HC, indicating selective impairment of long-range coupling.

Group Differences in Ripple Counts and Event Properties

Hippocampal ripple counts were significantly higher in SZ across most frequencies, showing ~2.5–3.6-fold increases. Cortical ripple counts were also elevated, showing ~3–4-fold increases across 80–240 Hz (Fig2b). Ripple durations showed only scattered differences without consistent patterns. Raw power differences seen at higher frequencies disappeared after adjusting for event count, indicating that the primary abnormality was increased occurrence rather than altered event morphology. 

Ripple Counts and Psychiatric Symptoms

Negative-binomial GLMs showed that higher total ripple rate was strongly associated with greater PANSS positive symptoms and general psychopathology. No significant association was found with PANSS negative symptoms. GAF scores were negatively associated with ripple rate, indicating poorer functioning in patients with more frequent ripple activity.

Network-Specific Ripple Density and Symptoms

Multiple regression analyses revealed no significant associations between PANSS scores and ripple density in any individual Yeo-7 network after FDR correction. Thus, symptom associations reflected global ripple load rather than altered proportional distribution across networks.

Ripple Transition Dynamics and Symptoms

Hippocampo–cortical transitions (HIP→CTX, CTX→HIP) were positively associated with PANSS positive symptoms, even after controlling for total ripple load. Cortex–cortex transitions showed a negative association with positive symptoms. Negative symptoms showed no significant effects after correction. Lower GAF scores were associated with increased hippocampo–cortical transitions and reduced CTX→CTX transitions.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive noninvasive characterization of hippocampal and cortical ripple dynamics in schizophrenia by combining high-frequency MEG with ripple-specific source reconstruction across two independent cohorts. Several key conclusions emerge.

First, the spatial organization of ripple generation was largely preserved. Hippocampal ripples remained confined to the bilateral hippocampal formation, and cortical ripples were broadly distributed across canonical large-scale networks. This indicates that the fundamental anatomical substrates supporting ripple generation are intact in schizophrenia.

Second, ripple occurrence was markedly elevated in schizophrenia, with approximately three- to fourfold increases in the cortex and 2.5- to 3.5-fold increases in the hippocampus. In contrast, ripple duration and power differences disappeared after adjusting for event count, demonstrating that the primary abnormality lies in the frequency with which ripple-generating circuits are engaged rather than in the morphology of individual events. These findings point to a globally heightened propensity for ripple generation across hippocampal and cortical systems.

Third, while the general cortical topography of ripples was preserved, schizophrenia showed frequency-specific redistribution across networks. Higher-order networks including the frontoparietal, default mode, and limbic systems showed reduced contributions at certain frequencies, whereas somatomotor and attention networks exhibited increased ripple density. This suggests subtle alterations in how cortical systems participate in ripple-related activity, without evidence of a global breakdown of network architecture.

Fourth, temporal coordination revealed selective impairments. Although ripple co-occurrence patterns and overall timing structure were retained, hippocampo–cortical transitions were significantly reduced in schizophrenia, whereas cortico–cortical and intra-hippocampal transitions were preserved. This indicates a specific degradation in long-range coupling between hippocampus and cortex, a pathway that normally supports memory-related updating and information integration.

Fifth, ripple metrics showed robust clinical relevance. Total ripple rate was positively associated with PANSS positive symptoms and general psychopathology and negatively associated with global functioning. Importantly, hippocampo–cortical transition rates were also positively linked to positive symptom severity, even after controlling for total ripple load, whereas cortico–cortical transitions showed inverse associations. These results suggest that both excessive ripple generation and altered patterns of ripple-based coupling contribute to symptom expression.

Taken together, the findings indicate that schizophrenia is characterized not by a loss of ripple physiology, but by a shift toward globally increased ripple activity combined with impaired long-range coordination. The combination of heightened ripple occurrence and selective disruption of hippocampo–cortical coupling may bias large-scale neural systems toward unstable or inefficient modes of updating, thereby linking ripple dysregulation to the emergence and severity of psychotic symptoms. Future work should clarify whether these abnormalities vary across illness stages, respond to treatment, or predict clinical trajectories, and whether interventions targeting ripple activity could help stabilize large-scale network function in schizophrenia.
