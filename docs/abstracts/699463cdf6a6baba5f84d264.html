Introduction

        Aging process is not only associated with the decline of physical fitness and mental state, but also accompanied with the change of brain structures, such as atrophy of gray matter or decrease in cortical thickness.1 With the structural features presented by brain MRI, recent studies have shown that age can be predicted accurately by using deep-learning models. Moreover, the difference between predicted age and chronological age, termed as the predicted age difference (PAD) or the brain age gap, can be explained as a deviation from normal aging. For example, positive PAD, which implies accelerated aging, has been reported in several neurodegenerative and psychiatric diseases.2 To overcome the heterogeneity in normal development of central nervous system, training of such deep-learning models relies on large-scale neuroimaging data, which are usually collected from datasets publicly shared by governments or collaborative initiatives. However, transfer learning of the pre-trained model could be crucial before its application on a local cohort due to possible divergence in imaging equipment, scanning parameters, injection of contrast agent, and even ethnicity of participants. Purpose of this study is to explore the clinical relevance of PAD using the health check dataset of our local hospital, in which only contrast-enhanced T1-weighted MRI (CE-T1WI), instead of the conventional non-contrast T1WI, was obtained. Therefore, we aim to develop a brain age model by using non-contrast T1WI of healthy volunteers collected from seven open-access datasets (n = 4171) and implement two fine tuning approaches (tuning all layers or fully-connected layers only) with CE-T1WI of healthy subjects (n = 747) in our local dataset for comparison. Furthermore, the more accurate fine-tuned model was applied to investigate the PAD of patients with insomnia (n = 105), diabetes mellitus (n = 86), arrhythmia (n = 41), and anxiety disorder (n = 41).

Methods

        To develop the pre-trained model for brain age prediction, 3D T1WI of 4,171 healthy subjects (age: 5-100 years) has been collected from Information eXtraction from Image (IXI, n = 561), Open Access Series of Imaging Studies-1 (OASIS-1, n = 316), Functional Connectomes Project (FCP, n = 1080), Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange (n = 547 for ABIDE-I and n = 571 for ABIDE-II), the enhanced Nathan Kline Institute-Rockland Sample (NKI-RS, n = 411), and Lifespan Human Connectome Project Aging (HCP-A, n = 685). In addition, 3D CE-T1WI has been obtained from the health check center at our local hospital by using minimal TE, TR = 1540-1640 ms, TI = 900 ms, flip angle = 9°, 160 axial slices in a field of view of 237 mm and a voxel size of 0.9×1.0×1.1 mm3. According to the personal diagnostic record, patients with neurodegenerative or neurological diseases were excluded. After image quality check, 983 subjects (age: 19-88 years), including 747 healthy subjects and 236 patients with diabetes mellitus, arrhythmia, myocardial infarction, valvular heart disease, coronary artery disease, insomnia, anxiety disorder, and depression disorder, were recruited. In consideration of adequate sample size, evaluation of PAD was only performed on those with insomnia (n = 105), diabetes mellitus (n = 86), arrhythmia (n = 41), or anxiety disorder (n = 41). 

        Minimal pre-processing has been performed on all images to overcome the variation in imaging parameters and data preparation among different databases and meanwhile to avoid loss of information. The procedure included reorientation, brain extraction to remove non-brain tissues, rigid registration to the MNI152 T1 template, normalization of intensity, and data resampling by applying Computational Anatomy Toolbox (CAT12.9)3 to generate an unbiased and intensity-normalized T1WI in 1-mm isotropic resolution. In this study, a 3D convolutional neural network with residual learning framework4 was modified for brain age prediction. As shown in Fig.1, our model was composed of 6 residual blocks and one fully-connected (FC) block, in which sex and main magnetic strength (B0) were encoded as binary inputs and concatenated to the second FC layer. Data gathered from open-access datasets were randomly split into training (n = 3471), validation (n = 350), and testing sets (n = 350), with age of the latter two sets being uniformly distributed. Also, training data were selected from all age groups equally in each epoch to minimize possible bias. For those age groups with more subjects, data can still be fully exploited during iterative epochs of model training. We trained our age prediction model from scratch (Adam optimizer, learning rate = 0.001, dropout = 0.2, and batch size = 4), and model weights with minimal validation loss of mean absolute error (MAE) in 1000 epochs was selected as the pre-trained model. On the other hand, data of healthy subjects from the local health check dataset were divided into training (60%, n = 449), validation (20%, n = 149), and testing sets (20%, n = 149) with identical age distribution. To achieve adaptation to the local CE-T1WI dataset, two strategies of fine tuning, either model weights of all layers or only those of FC layers being tuned, were implemented independently with the same hyperparameters except training epochs limited at 100. MAE and coefficient of determination (R2) of validation and testing sets were calculated. In addition, a bias-adjustment scheme using linear regression on validation data was performed to remove bias in age prediction.5 After that, the fine-tuned model was used to estimate the PAD of all patient groups. With the testing set of healthy subjects in our local dataset served as control, significant difference was examined (p < 0.05) using two-sample t test.

Results

        With the non-contrast T1WI collected from worldwide open-access datasets, the validation and testing MAE of our model reaches 3.413 and 3.524 years, respectively, which was improved to 3.216 and 3.463 after bias correction. Figure 2a demonstrated the MAE and R2 of the local validation and testing sets (n = 149 for both) by using the pre-trained model and two fine-tuned models (tuning on FC layers and all layers, respectively). Compared with the pre-trained model without any fine tuning, MAE and R2 of both fine-tuned models was obviously improved, indicating that fine tuning successfully mitigates the discrepancy between large-scale open data and the local dataset, which includes, but not limited to, the contrast enhancement of blood flow (non-contrast vs contrast-enhanced) and ethnicity (Caucasian as majority vs all eastern Asian). Our results also showed that the tuning-all-layers model is more accurate than the tuning-FC-layers model (bias-corrected test MAE = 3.091 < 3.549; R2 = 0.852 > 0.809), both exhibiting an MAE comparable with the original performance of the pre-trained model (test MAE = 3.463). 

        By using the tuning-all-layers model, bias-corrected PAD of diabetes mellitus, arrhythmia, insomnia, and anxiety disorder was summarized in Fig.2b. Compared with the control (PAD = 0.673, n = 149) composed of the whole testing set of our local data, the PAD of diabetes mellitus (3.738) and arrhythmia (2.415) was significantly higher (p < 0.05). However, the chronological age of the control group was lower than that of diabetes mellitus and arrhythmia (p < 0.05). Therefore, a subset with least difference in age distribution was selected independently from the control for each comparison to serve as the age-matched control group. After that, significantly higher PAD only exists in patients with diabetes mellitus (p < 0.001, see Fig.2b for details) with an effect size of 0.883 (Cohen’s d). To avoid the mixture effect due to comorbidity, we also excluded the diabetes patients with other disease(s), and the mean PAD of diabetes-only patients (n = 49) was 4.230, keeping the significant difference persistent (p < 0.001).

Conclusion

        Compared with the pre-trained model without any fine tuning, our fine-tuned models reduced the test MAE substantially from 7.362 to 3.549 (tuning-FC-layers) and 3.091 (tuning-all-layers), exhibiting successful adaptation to the unseen dataset, in which only contrast-enhanced T1WI was collected from local inhabitants. By using the tuning-all-layers model, clinical relevance of PAD was evaluated on participants with diabetes mellitus, arrhythmia, insomnia, and anxiety disorder. In comparison with the age-matched control group, higher PAD was only associated with diabetes mellitus, which is in agreement with previous findings.2 It is therefore anticipated that PAD could reflect the aging-related biological change of an unseen dataset with careful fine tuning of the brain age prediction model.
