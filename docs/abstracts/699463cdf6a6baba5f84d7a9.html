Introduction

Quantitative Susceptibility Mapping (QSM) is a powerful MRI technique for non-invasively measuring tissue magnetic susceptibility, providing insights into iron deposition and myelin content. However, conventional QSM cannot characterise sub-voxel susceptibility distribution. Within a single voxel, paramagnetic (e.g. iron) and diamagnetic sources (e.g. myelin) may coexist, and their opposing contributions could cancel each other out, potentially masking pathological changes. This is particularly relevant in Parkinson’s disease (PD), where changes in age-related iron accumulation and myelin loss occur.

Chen et al developed DECOMPOSE-QSM1 to address this limitation by decomposing bulk susceptibility into sub-voxel paramagnetic and diamagnetic components using multi-echo gradient-echo MRI data. This produces Paramagnetic Component Susceptibility (PCS), Diamagnetic Component Susceptibility (DCS), and Composite Susceptibility (CompS) maps. Recent studies applied source-separated brain susceptibility mapping in PD compared to healthy controls (HC), demonstrating links between paramagnetic and diamagnetic susceptibility, and PD diagnosis as well as PD-associated motor and cognitive symptoms, in both subcortical and cortical regions2,3. However, these prior studies have been limited to relatively small cohorts and have not examined the full spectrum of PD disease severity across both cortical and subcortical structures.

Thus, we present source-separated susceptibility maps in a larger cohort of 59 PD patients and 17 healthy controls. We examine both deep subcortical and cortical regions across a broad range of disease severity.

Methods

In our study, 59 people with PD aged 50-80 (age = 66.9±7.0, 20 female) within 10 years of diagnosis, and 17 aged-matched HC (age = 67.7±6.0, 8 female) were scanned on a 3T MRI system. T1-weighted magnetisation-prepared rapid gradient-echo images were acquired with the following parameters: 1×1×1 mm voxels, matrix = 256×256×176, echo time (TE)=3.34 ms, repetition time (TR)=2530 ms, inversion time (TI)=1100 ms, flip angle=7 degrees. Two multi-echo gradient-echo multiparameter maps (MPMs) with proton density- and T1-weighting were acquired with the following parameters: flip angle = 6 / 21 degrees (PDW / T1W), TR=25 ms, TE1=2.3 ms and TE8=18.4 ms (equidistant echoes), bandwidth = 488 Hz/pixel, matrix = 224x256x180 with 0.8x0.8x0.8 mm voxels.

To generate source-separated susceptibility maps, the following pipeline was applied to data from both proton density weighted and T1 weighted acquisitions: phase unwrapping was completed using the rapid opensource minimum spanning tree algorithm (ROMEO), and the phase quality map over echoes was used to create two masks for two-pass brain masking. Otsu thresholding was applied to create the pass 1 mask, and a hole filling algorithm was applied to create the pass 2 mask. Per-echo background field removal was completed using projection to dipole fields (PDF), and per-echo dipole inversion was completed using STAR-QSM for both masks. The first and second pass susceptibility maps were combined at each echo to give 8 susceptibility maps per proton density weighted and T1 weighted contrast. These susceptibility maps along with corresponding per-echo magnitude images were then processed using the DECOMPOSE-QSM algorithm1 to generate PCS, DCS, and CompS maps. The maps derived from the T1W acquisition were co-registered to PDW space and were averaged across acquisitions to give the final PCS, DCS, and CompS maps for each participant.

Regions of interest (ROIs) were defined in each participant’s native proton density weighted space. Cortical ROIs were derived using the Schaefer functional atlas with 200 parcels. Subcortical ROIs were defined using the MRICloud atlas and included the caudate, globus pallidus interna, globus pallidus externa, putamen, subthalamic nucleus, substantia nigra (SN), and red nucleus due to previous reports of subcortical involvement in PD2,3.

For each participant, bilateral ROI summary statistics were computed by averaging across left and right homologous regions. From these ROI statistics, group differences between PD brains and HC brains were assessed using linear models with age and sex as covariates. False-discovery–rate (FDR) correction was applied for analyses conducted across the Schaefer atlas. In contrast, basal ganglia structures defined by MRICloud were tested without multiple-comparison correction, reflecting strong prior expectation of basal ganglia involvement from previous work2,3,4.

Results

Source-separated susceptibility values in subcortical regions 

Analysis of subcortical regions revealed increased susceptibility in the SN in PD patients compared to controls. This difference was significant in both the composite and paramagnetic susceptibility-derived maps (PCS: β = –0.01538, SE = 0.00534, t = –2.88, p = 0.0041; CompS: β = –0.01558, SE = 0.00507, t = –3.07, p = 0.0022) (see Figure 1A). No other subcortical regions showed significant differences between PD and control participant brains.

Source-separated susceptibility values in cortical regions

Two ROIs showed significant group differences between individuals with PD and HC. In the CompS map, secondary visual cortex (V2) demonstrated significantly lower susceptibility values in the PD group (β = 0.00482, SE = 0.00134, t = 3.58, p = 0.00034, FDR-corrected p = 0.039) (see Figure 1B). However, this was not significant for either the paramagnetic or diamagnetic maps. In contrast, in the PCS map, the ventral posterior cingulate cortex (vPCC) showed significantly higher paramagnetic susceptibility values in PD (β = –0.00542, SE = 0.00152, t = –3.58, p = 0.00035, FDR-corrected p = 0.040) (see Figure 1C). This difference in vPCC was not seen in the CompS values, and was only revealed using source separation. No other cortical regions exhibited significant differences after multiple-comparison correction.

Conclusion

We demonstrate that source separation of susceptibility values can reveal differences between patients with PD and controls, in both cortical and subcortical regions.

Our findings corroborate previous results showing increased paramagnetic and composite, but not diamagnetic susceptibilities, in the SN in PD2,3 . Our observation of increased paramagnetic susceptibility in the substantia nigra of PD patients aligns with previous studies despite methodological differences stemming from different source separation algorithms being used in previous work (QSM-ARCS and APART-QSM)2,3. The similarity of the composite and paramagnetic susceptibility changes (and lack of significant diamagnetic susceptibility change) in the substantia nigra shows that the change in this region is primarily driven by an increase in paramagnetic susceptibility, likely iron. We have extended these findings to a larger cohort of PD patients spanning a range of disease severities.

Additionally, we found increased paramagnetic susceptibility in the ventral posterior cingulate cortex in PD compared to controls. This difference was only revealed through source separation and was masked in the composite map by diamagnetic components. Conversely, decreased composite susceptibility was observed in the secondary visual cortex in PD, with no corresponding changes in the paramagnetic or diamagnetic maps.

By jointly examining composite, paramagnetic, and diamagnetic susceptibility maps, we disentangled the opposing susceptibility values, likely to be driven primarily by iron and myelin, within single voxels. This reveals that cortical susceptibility differences in PD may not be driven solely by iron accumulation but instead reflect more complex changes in composition that may be obscured when using a single QSM map.

Our study is the first to extract source-separated susceptibility maps from a multi-parametric mapping (MPM) acquisition, enabling future integration with additional MPM measures such as proton density, R1, R2*, and magnetisation transfer saturation. This approach demonstrates the value of MPMs for detailed characterisation of PD-related tissue changes and provides a framework for future studies.

In conclusion, separating paramagnetic and diamagnetic susceptibilities in a large PD cohort across disease severities enabled detection of cortical alterations that are not apparent using conventional QSM approaches. This approach offers a more detailed understanding of PD-related tissue changes and establishes a foundation for future studies linking sub-voxel susceptibility alterations alongside MPM metrics to clinical and cognitive symptoms in PD.
