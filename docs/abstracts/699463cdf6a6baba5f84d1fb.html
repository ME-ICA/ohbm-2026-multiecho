Introduction

Psychosis is a heterogeneous condition but understanding how brain structure varies across several disorders that fall under its umbrella may offer insight into the pathways associated with its development. Morphometric brain connectomes derived from neuroimaging data can facilitate this by providing multifeature representations of disease expression (Seidlitz et al., 2018; Wang & He, 2024). Of current approaches, Morphometric INverse Divergence (MIND) is a particularly novel method that samples multivariate feature distributions to estimate structural similarity across brain regions (Sebenius et al., 2023). While MIND has been well-validated, its methodological advances have not yet been used to explore differences in disorders of psychosis.

Methods

We applied MIND to T1-weighted MRI data from the Bipolar and Schizophrenia Network on Immediate Phenotypes (B-SNIP) study (Tamminga et al., 2014), which included 281 cases (schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, bipolar I disorder) and 182 healthy controls after quality control (N = 463, ages 15-65). Images were acquired on 3T scanners and processed using FreeSurfer 5.3. Whole-brain and region-specific MIND scores for each participant were calculated using morphometric features including cortical thickness, mean curvature, sulcal depth, surface area and grey matter volume. Subsequent analyses evaluated case-control MIND differences among subgroups via multivariate regression and spin permutation tests (n = 10,000 tests).

Results

Whole-brain and regional MIND scores from B-SNIP correlated strongly with those from the ABCD study, the primary dataset originally used for MIND validation (r = 0.97). Global MIND was found to be significantly predictive of case status, relative to controls (p < 0.001). Shown in Figure 1, a distinct bilateral pattern of increased MIND was observed among cases in postcentral and medial occipital areas, along with decreased MIND in superior temporal regions. Similar differences were also reported in a recent study by García-San-Martín et al. (2025), which correlated with observed case-control differences in this data set at r = 0.53 despite substantially different sample characteristics. Compared to controls, subjects with schizophrenia in this study displayed highest mean regional MIND scores (+0.890), followed by those with schizoaffective disorder (+0.338); case-control differences for both these subgroups were most pronounced in somatosensory and visual cortices, with stronger regional differences observed in schizophrenia. Subjects with bipolar I disorder exhibited lower mean regional MIND compared to controls (-1.356), most evidently in auditory and language processing areas. Subsequent spin permutation tests revealed significant and distinct case-control differences across all three case subgroups (FDR-adjusted p < 0.001).

Conclusion

Reported results indicate MIND is a reliable and sensitive measure of cross-disorder case-control differences in structural similarity. This is the first evidence that these differences, which across all cases are most evident in postcentral, visual and superior temporal areas, are robust across data sets and implies presence of a consistent underlying disease signature. The observed pattern of differences between schizophrenia, schizoaffective and bipolar I subjects in this study also appears to suggest an axis of morphometric brain change involving sensory and language areas, which may tie together otherwise distinct disorders of psychosis. Future investigations of these differences at a more granular level will help better characterise these structural profiles and probe their aetiological underpinnings.
