Introduction

Living in our inherently social world requires constant adaptation to changing environmental demands. Two relevant key processes are empathy (embodied sharing of another person’s emotions) and Theory of Mind (ToM, understanding another person’s thoughts and feelings). Large heterogeneity in how these processes are conceptualized has led to inconsistent findings regarding which neural networks underlie empathy and ToM. However, a minimal consensus has shown empathy to be associated with neural activation primarily located in the ventral attention and frontoparietal network (such as the anterior insula, inferior frontal gyrus, supramarginal gyrus, and somatosensory cortex), and classical ToM tasks engaging neural regions primarily located in the default mode network (such as temporoparietal junction, medial prefrontal cortex, precuneus, and posterior cingulate cortex). The relationship between empathy and ToM as mental capacities enabling dynamic information processing in social interactions (both at the neural and behavioral level) is complex and situation-specific, and describing this relationship is further compounded by a lack of conceptual clarity. While there is a long-standing research tradition studying the two capacities as independent, recent evidence from naturalistic social tasks shows that, under certain task demands, the neural networks underlying empathy and ToM processing interact. Here, we tackle these problems by assessing the neural interaction patterns of networks related to empathy and ToM in a naturalistic complex social task that simultaneously assesses both processes. We investigate task-modulated changes in functional connectivity from key regions previously associated with empathy (bilateral anterior insula) and ToM (bilateral temporoparietal junction). Specifically, we aimed to identify situation-specific patterns of network organization and reorganization that guide the interplay of socio-affective and -cognitive processes.

Methods

Overall, we included data from 143 participants (mean age = 25.405 ± 6.545 years, age range: 18-57 years, 72 female)  as part of a larger study1. Participants performed the EmpaToM task during an fMRI scanning session, a naturalistic video-based paradigm designed to measure empathy and ToM2. In short, participants are presented with a video clip (~15 seconds), in which a character describes an alleged autobiographic event which differs in both, emotionality and ToM demand. This experimental design resulted in a 2 (emotionality: negative vs. neutral) x 2 (ToM demand: ToM vs. non-ToM) factorial design, each condition being presented 12 times, with 48 trials in total. 

Functional data were preprocessed using SPM12, including slice timing correction and realignment to the mean image. Anatomical images were co-registered to the mean functional images, then normalized, and the resulting transformation matrices were used to normalize the functional images to MNI space. Normalized images were spatially smoothed with a Gaussian filter of full-width half-maximum at 8mm and temporally high-pass filtered with a cut-off of 128s. 

To identify network organization changes specific to context changes in the video conditions of the EmpaToM task, we employed generalized psychophysiological interaction (gPPI3) implemented in the Conn toolbox. We calculated effective connectivity from four regions of interest (ROI) related to social cognition (bilateral anterior insula, bilateral temporoparietal junction) with all other voxels in the brain. The exact location of the ROIs was derived from meta-analytic connectivity modeling and clustering analysis of regions related to social skills4, and masks for the ROIs were defined using the meta-analytically derived atlas by Dosenbach et al.5. We selected those parcels for which the peak coordinates showed the smallest Euclidean distance to our target coordinates.

At the first level, we calculated separate bivariate multiple regression models for each target voxels, including the gPPI predictors: psychological factor (boxcar timeseries of task effects for the task video phases, convolved with a canonical hemodynamic response function), physiological factor (BOLD timeseries for each seed ROI), interaction term (psychological factor * physiological factor). At the group level, we fitted a 2x2 within-subjects ANOCA to calculate condition-effects of interest (empathy: emotional – neutral videos; ToM: ToM – non-ToM videos; interaction effect). Results were corrected for multiple comparisons using family-wise error rate of p<0.05 at the cluster level.

Results

Our results suggest that socio-affective and socio-cognitive processing rely on differentiable patterns of neural network organization: while empathic processing relied on the interaction of neural networks from different networks, abstract mental state reasoning relied on the interaction of neural regions primarily within the default mode network. Seeding from the right anterior insula in the main effect of empathy (emotionally negative > neutral videos), we observed negative functional coupling with the right middle frontal gyrus, precuneus, and superior frontal gyrus (located in the frontoparietal network, FPN), the right angular gyrus, and left superior temporal sulcus ( both located in the default mode network, DMN), along with positive coupling with the right occipital gyrus (located in the visual network), and the right supramarginal gyrus (located in the ventral attention network, VAN), and the anterior cingulate cortex (located in the DMN). Seeding from the left anterior insula, we observed positive functional coupling with the calcarine gyrus, as well as negative functional coupling with the right middle frontal gyrus (located in the FPN), the right precuneus, and bilateral angular gyrus (all located in the DMN). Finally, seeding from the right temporoparietal junction, negative functional coupling emerged with the left cerebellum and superior temporal sulcus (located in the DMN). Seeding from the left temporoparietal junction yielded no significant patterns of functional coupling (across all investigated contrasts).

Looking at the contrast of ToM (ToM > non-ToM videos), we observed positive functional coupling between the right anterior insula and bilateral inferior frontal gyrus (located in the FPN), and negative functional coupling with the right cuneus ( in the visual network), and precuneus. Seeding from the left anterior insula yielded negative functional coupling with the left cerebellum, bilateral middle occipital gyrus (located in the dorsal attention network and DMN), as well as the left supramarginal gyrus. Finally, seeding from the right temporoparietal junction yielded positive functional coupling with the left superior temporal sulcus.

The interaction effect of empathy and ToM only yielded significant functional coupling when seeding from the right anterior insula. Specifically, we observed negative functional coupling with the left occipital gyrus, right calcarine and fusiform gyrus ( all located in the visual network), the right precuneus and posterior cingulate cortex (in the FPN), as well as negative functional coupling with the left superior temporal sulcus (in the DMN).

Conclusion

In line with previous findings, the simultaneous processing of socio-affective and socio-cognitive information was associated with increased functional coupling between regions classically associated with either empathy or ToM (such as an increase in functional coupling between the right anterior insula and precuneus and posterior cingulate cortex that was observed for the interaction effect of empathy and ToM). More specifically, our results suggest that processing only affective information (main effect of empathy) and the simultaneous processing of affective and cognitive information (interaction effect of empathy and ToM) relied on an increased between-network interaction, specifically regions in the VAN and visual network. By contrast, only processing socio-cognitive information (main effect of ToM) yielded primarily decreased functional coupling between networks involved in intermediate- and higher-order cognitive processing (e.g., coupling between the right anterior insula and precuneus, and between the left anterior insula and the bilateral middle occipital and left supramarginal gyrus). Interestingly, we observed an increase in functional coupling between neural regions within the DMN (between the right temporoparietal junction and the left superior temporal gyrus). These findings align with previous research showing that particularly more complex and naturalistic social cognition tasks (arguably more similar to how these processes are experienced in real-life social interactions) require increased interaction between different neural networks, similar to other higher-order cognitive abilities such as intelligence or creativity.

Task-modulated patterns of functional coupling, particularly from the bilateral anterior insula, exhibited flexible reorganization of functional connectivity depending on the experimental condition. These results, combined with the anterior insula’s extensive brain-wide patterns of connectivity, could suggest that the anterior insula is a social cognition-relevant network hub that flexibly reconfigures the patterns of neural network interaction and organization to adapt to changing contextual demands. 

Taken together, our results suggest that dynamic changes in the overall neural network configuration, along with differentiable network organizational principles, enable successful navigation of dynamic social environments.
