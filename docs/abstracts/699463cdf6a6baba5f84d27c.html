Introduction

Dense fMRI functional connectomes (grayordinate-wise connectivity matrices) offer a fine-grained representation of an individual’s functional connectivity. However, these matrices can contain billions of entries and require tens of gigabytes of memory per subject. Even with optimized implementations1, analyzing more than a few individuals’ dense connectomes directly remains computationally challenging. As a result, analyses on large-scale datasets (e.g. UK Biobank) are conducted on parcellated or template-based representations. This dimensionality reduction imposes strong constraints and low spatial resolution on the connectivity matrix, potentially masking finer-grained differences.

Although the dense connectome is high-dimensional, its rank is much smaller since the number of volumes is far less than the number of grayordinates, and preprocessing steps such as spatial smoothing and denoising further reduce the effective degrees of freedom. Moreover, the number of plausible functional networks is biologically constrained and unlikely to approach the number of grayordinates in a scan. Rather than representing an individual’s dense connectome explicitly, we can use an exact low-rank factorization.

Therefore, to exploit this intrinsic low-rank structure, we propose a kernel-based framework for analyzing connectomes. Using a low-rank factorization and a linear kernel, we compute similarities between dense connectomes under a log-Euclidean metric. This enables machine learning methods such as regression, PCA, and classification in the dual space. To support interpretability, we reconstruct low-rank approximations of the corresponding weight vectors in dense connectome space, providing insight into which networks contribute to a given effect. We apply this framework to HCP rest and task fMRI to predict individual traits, classify sex and task conditions, and show that the reconstructed dense connectomes reveal interpretable spatial patterns at voxel-level resolution.

Methods

Under the framework of kernel machine learning methods, the subject-level log-transformed dense connectomes, Li ∈ Rv x v, can be viewed as elements of the implicit feature space, while the low-rank factorization derived from the demeaned time series, Xi ∈ Rt x v, serves as the input representation. Here, i indexes subjects, t is the number of time points for subject i, and v is the number of grayordinates. Rather than assuming a Euclidean metric over the dense connectome space, we construct the input space such that all inner products correspond to the log-Euclidean metric in the implicit space. This metric better captures the geometry of symmetric positive-definite (SPD) matrices and has been shown to yield more reliable connectivity analyses2. Additionally, K can be demeaned across subjects or conditions allowing for an efficient way to recenter the tangent space and improve sensitivity to the effect of interest.

While the kernel allows us to implicitly use dense connectomes in machine learning models, it does not directly show what elements in connectome space were driving the observation of interest. Rather than compute this sum directly, we developed a streaming, low-rank optimization technique based on the Hyperbolic SVD3. This result allows any weighted sum or contrast of dense connectomes to be efficiently computed and decomposed into its low-rank factorization. This decomposition allows the spatial modes within a reconstructed dense connectome to be analyzed with techniques such as Independent Component Analysis (ICA). We demonstrate that MIGP4 can be interpreted as a special case of our indefinite, tangent-space reconstruction framework when uniform subject weighting is used and distances are measured in the log-Euclidean, rather than Euclidean metric. 

Linear models with regularization in the dual space work well with large datasets, but the ability to use priors in the primal space will increase reliability and statistical power on smaller datasets. We further introduce a framework for efficient regularization, either during optimization in the primal space or directly within inner product computations. Further, the inner product can be regularized in space or time (Equation 4). These weights can be learned or defined from biological or condition-specific constraints and help mitigate sensitivity to spatial misalignment. This approach is not just an alternative to classical parcel-based connectome analysis but a generalization that subsumes it due to the fact that Wi can be simplified to match classic templates or parcels.

We applied the framework to HCP resting-state and task fMRI data (object vs face recognition). For resting-state data, we evaluate our models in their ability to predict sex, working memory accuracy, and crystallized and fluid cognition. Cognitive targets were deconfounded by regressing out participants' age, sex, and their interaction before model fitting. For the Tools vs Faces contrast, we trained a linear classifier (kernel LDA) in the dual space and reconstructed the corresponding discriminant log-connectome via the weighted sum in Equation 3. Models used elastic-net regression or regularized linear classifiers with nested, family-wise–stratified cross-validation and hyperparameter tuning.

Results

We applied this kernel framework to HCP resting-state (N=1003 participants) and Tools vs Faces task (one session per condition) fMRI data. Features from the resting-state Gram matrix predicted individual differences in working memory task accuracy, endurance measures, and cognitive performance (Table 1). Cognitive composite measures showed the strongest effects (CogCrystalComp and CogTotalComp: r = 0.32), with working memory performing similarly (r = 0.29) and endurance showing more modest prediction after deconfounding (r = 0.23). These patterns are in line with prior work relating resting-state connectivity to crystallized intelligence, fluid intelligence, and working memory capacity5. The same features also reliably encoded sex differences, achieving a balanced classification accuracy of 86% and an AUC of 0.93.

The Gram matrix formed from the Tools vs Faces conditions also strongly encoded task-relevant information, achieving balanced classification accuracy of 99%. Because each subject contributed one scan per condition, demeaning the kernel within subject across conditions resulted in a reliable subject-specific contrast. To visualize which regions were driving this separation, we used the reconstruction method in Eq. 3. After reconstructing the discriminant, low-rank factorized log-connectome from the kernel LDA weights, effectively representing the weight matrix for the primal solution, we applied spatial ICA to identify the spatial maps driving differences in connectome space. These maps highlight regions such as the fusiform face area and lateral occipital/posterior inferior temporal cortex, consistent with face and tool selective activations.

Conclusion

By combining a log-Euclidean Riemannian metric with a kernel formulation, we showed that scalable machine learning methods can be applied directly to dense functional connectomes while operating only on low-rank subject factors and an n x n Gram matrix. These dual-space models achieved accurate prediction of individual differences and task conditions. Moreover, using an exact linear correspondence between dual and primal solutions, these weights can be mapped back to dense log-connectomes. The streaming, indefinite reconstruction method recovers low-rank, high-dimensional weight matrices in the primal space, making it possible to visualize task-discriminative and phenotype-related connectivity patterns at full spatial resolution. This combination of efficiency, flexibility, and interpretability makes the long-standing problem of direct dense connectome analysis practically tractable and establishes a general framework for future methodological and applied work on dense functional connectivity.
