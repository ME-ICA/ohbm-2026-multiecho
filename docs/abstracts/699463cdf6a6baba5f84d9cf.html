Introduction

Musical skill learning engages distributed auditory–motor and memory networks and is accompanied by experience-dependent functional reorganization and structural brain plasticity. 1-3 However, it remains unclear how familial similarity shapes multivariate neural responses during skill acquisition, and whether such effects in music depend on auditory ability. We studied monozygotic (MZ) twins scanned pre- and post-training during an in-scanner melody-learning task. We tested whether MZ pairs show higher multivoxel pattern similarity than unrelated pairs (“twin advantage”), whether this differs across sequence-length conditions and training, and whether it is moderated by auditory discrimination ability measured with the Swedish Musical Discrimination Test (SMDT). 4

Methods

Twenty-nine MZ twin pairs (N = 58; age 40–65 years, M = 48.8, SD = 6.89; 32 female) completed two fMRI sessions: pre-training (T1) and post-training (T2), separated by 6 weeks of piano practice. Imaging data were acquired using a 3T Siemens Magnetom Prisma (20-channel head coil) at the Stockholm University Brain Imaging Center. Functional images were acquired with a gradient echo pulse, echo planar (EPI) T2*-weighted sequence with TR = 2.11 s, TE = 30 ms, 68 interleaved slices, 2 x 2 x 2 mm3 voxel size, multiband factor = 2. In each session, participants learned a novel 4‑bar melody via audiovisual instruction, progressing through conditions that varied sequence length: 1 bar (initial encoding of novel material), 2 bars (integration), and 4 bars (full sequence), with four repetitions per condition. Participants performed with the right hand using a 4‑button response box. Imaging data were preprocessed (slice-timing correction, realignment and unwarping, co-registration to the T1-weighted structural image, structural tissue segmentation, normalization to MNI space, 4 mm smoothing) using SPM12; nuisance regressors (motion/physiological components) were estimated in CONN; and longitudinal processing (CAT12) generated normalized images unbiased by time point. First-level GLMs yielded condition-specific contrast images (2 sessions × 3 lengths × 4 repetitions). A task-responsive mask was defined from the grand-average playing > implicit rest contrast (FWE-corrected, p < 0.05). For each condition, we computed pairwise multivoxel pattern similarity between all subject pairs using Pearson correlation across voxels and Fisher z-transformation (z_ij), labeling dyads as MZ or unrelated. Effects of pair type, session, length, repetition, and pair-mean centered SMDT were assessed using dyadic cross-classified mixed-effects models in R (lme4/lmerTest), with random intercepts for both individuals ((1 | subject_i) + (1 | subject_j)) to account for each participant contributing to multiple dyads.

Results

Across conditions, MZ pairs showed higher pattern similarity than unrelated pairs (pair_type: F = 89.2, p < 2×10⁻¹⁶). Similarity also varied with session (F = 49.4, p = 2.1×10⁻¹²), sequence length (F = 10.9, p = 1.85×10⁻⁵), and repetition (F = 13.2, p = 1.40×10⁻⁸), with robust global interactions consistent with learning dynamics (session×length: F = 41.3, p = 1.26×10⁻¹⁸; session×repetition: F = 16.8, p = 7.01×10⁻¹¹; length×repetition: F = 12.2, p = 1.06×10⁻¹³). Critically, the twin advantage was auditory-ability dependent in a session- and length-specific manner (pair_type×session×length×SMDT: F = 2.91, p = 0.020). Follow-up estimated marginal means indicated that this effect was selective to initial exposure (1-bar) at T1: the MZ–unrelated difference increased from ~0.01 at low SMDT to ~0.11 at high SMDT, whereas corresponding effects post-training were small (Figure 1). Quantifying moderation directly, the SMDT-related increase in the twin advantage was significant only for the 1-bar condition at T1 (difference in SMDT slopes: 0.077, 95% CI [0.043, 0.110], p < 0.001).

Conclusion

A dyadic pairwise pattern-similarity approach revealed a robust MZ “twin advantage” in task-evoked multivariate fMRI responses during melody learning. Beyond familial effects, multivoxel pattern similarity showed pronounced learning-related dynamics across the entire cohort, varying systematically with session, sequence length, and repetition, consistent with experience-dependent reconfiguration of task representations. Importantly, auditory ability selectively amplified the twin advantage during initial encoding of novel material, indicating that familial similarity is most evident at first exposure and less pronounced outside this early-encoding phase. Together, these findings demonstrate that genetically informed inter-subject pattern similarity can dissociate (i) global learning-related representational changes shared across individuals from (ii) twin-specific similarity patterns linked to perceptual ability, providing a scalable framework for studying how nature and experience jointly shape neural representations during skill acquisition.
