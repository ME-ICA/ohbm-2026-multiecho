Introduction

Perivascular spaces (PVS) are fluid-filled compartments surrounding cerebral blood vessels that play a critical role in metabolic waste products clearance and interstitial fluid transport. Enlargement of PVS (EPVS) has been increasingly recognized as an MRI biomarker of cerebrovascular dysfunction, neuroinflammation, neurodegeneration, and glymphatic function impairment. Advances in high-resolution MRI have enabled more accurate quantification of EPVS burden, revealing associations with hypertension, diabetes, obesity, and cognitive decline,1–3. Despite evidence connecting EPVS to systemic health, sex difference in EPVS burden and its cardiometabolic determinants remain poorly understood. 

Sex-related biological factors–including differences in brain anatomy, hormone profiles, fat distribution, immune responses, and cerebrovascular function–may influence PVS structure and function. Given the strong sex disparities observed in multiple brain aging and neurodegenerative pathways, it is essential to determine whether EPVS exhibits sex-specific vulnerability to cardiometabolic risk factors. 

In this study, we examined age-related associations with EPVS volumes in the white matter (WM) and basal ganglia (BG) in a healthy adult and evaluated whether associations between EPVS burden and cardiometabolic risk factors differ by sex. By integrating high-field 7T MRI with a broader set of cardiometabolic risk factors, we aimed to provide a better understanding of sex-dependent susceptibility in PVS dysfunction and its relevance to vascular-metabolic brain aging.

Methods

Participants: A total of 328 adults completed physiological assessments and MRI. Participants were excluded for overlapping WM hyperintensities with EPVS (n=76), absence of T2-weighted scan (n=75), insufficient image quality (n=21), or failed imaging registration and PVS segmentation (n=4). The final sample included 157 participants (mean age 58.37 ± 7.52 years), with 74 males (mean age 57.32 ± 8.02 years) and 83 females (mean age 59.30 ± 6.97 years). All participants provided written informed consent in accordance with institutional review board approval.

MRI acquisition: All scans were performed on Siemens Magnetom 7 T scanner (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) using the 1st and 2nd generation Tic-Tac-Toe RF head coil. 3D T1-weighted MPRAGE was acquired with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 3000 ms, echo time (TE) = 1.96-2.17 ms, inversion times = 1200 ms, and spatial resolution = 0.75 x 0.75 x 0.75 mm3 with matrix size = 230 x 320 x 256. 3D T2-weighted SPACE was acquired with TR = 4100 ms, TE = 365-369 ms, and spatial resolution = 0.6 x 0.6 x 0.6 mm3 with matrix size = 276 x 320 x 224. 

Systemic variables: Demographic (age, sex), anthropometric (body mass index (BMI), body fat percentage, waist and hip circumferences), metabolic (glucose, lipids, cholesterol, and insulin resistance), and clinical disease status (diabetes, hypertension, and lipid disorder) were obtained from questionnaires, physiological measurements, and laboratory assays.

EPVS segmentation: Structural MRI data were preprocessed using Human Connectome Project pipelines. EPVS segmentation was performed on T2-weighted images using a Haar transform-based EPVS enhancement algorithm,4 followed by minor manual refinements. FreeSurfer 6.0 was used to delineate WM and BG regions and estimate intracranial volume (ICV). EPVS volumes were quantified in mm3 for WM and BG separately.

Statistical analyses: Statistical analyses were performed using MATLAB (R2025a, MathWorks, Natick, MA). WM and BG PVS volumes were analyzed separately. Sex differences in regional EPVS burden were tested using independent t-tests. Association between EPVS volumes and systemic variables were evaluated using partial correlation controlling for relevant covariates (age, sex, ICV for overall analyses; age and ICV for sex-stratified analyses). Age-related associations were assessed similarly.  Statistical significance was defined as p-value < 0.05 (uncorrected).

Results

No significant sex differences were observed in WM EPVS (p = 0.880) or BG EPVS volumes (p = 0.633). WM and BG EPVS volumes were also not significantly correlated in the overall sample (p = 0.082), nor in sex-stratified analyses (males: p = 0.116; females: p = 0.392).

Table 1 and Table 2 show significant partial correlations between cardiometabolic risk factors and WM or BG EPVS volumes, respectively. In the overall cohort, WM EPVS volume showed significant positive associations with age (r = 0.19, p = 0.018), diabetes (r = 0.22, p = 0.006) and hypertension (r = 0.26, p = 0.001). When analyses were stratified by sex, the association with age remained significant only in females (r = 0.24, p = 0.034). Several metabolic markers–including hip circumference (r = 0.23, p = 0.042), insulin (r = 0.24, p = 0.037), and homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) (r = 0.24, p = 0.032)–were also positively associated with WM EPVS volume exclusively in females. Diabetes demonstrated a strong relationship in females (r = 0.42, p < 0.001), whereas hypertension was positively associated with WM EPVS in both males (r = 0.27, p = 0.023) and females (r = 0.29, p = 0.010).

BG EPVS volume demonstrated a robust association with age in the overall sample (r = 0.37, p < 0.001). This age effect remained significant in both males (r = 0.37, p = 0.001) and females (r = 0.39, p < 0.001). A positive association with high-density lipoprotein (HDL) emerged only in males (r = 0.24, p = 0.046).

Conclusion

This study demonstrates distinct sex-specific and region-specific associations between EPVS burden and cardiometabolic risk factors in healthy adults. WM EPVS volume showed relationship with metabolic markers–including fasting insulin, HOMA-IR, hip circumference, and diabetes–exclusively in females, whereas BG EPVS exhibited a male-specific relationship with HDL. Hypertension was consistently associated with WM EPVS across sexes.

Our findings support prior work linking EPVS to diabetes and hypertension1, while revealing sex-dependent metabolic effects that have not been fully characterized. Although prior studies have reported higher EPVS burden in males,5 our sample showed no sex differences in EPVS volumes, possibly due to differences in population characteristics or methodological variations in EPVS quantification. The regional dissociation between WM and BG EPVS suggests that different vascular or glymphatic mechanisms may govern EPVS burden across brain regions.

Overall, our findings reveal sex-dependent patterns linking cardiometabolic health to PVS enlargement, suggesting that males and females may experience different pathways of vascular or glymphatic vulnerability. Understanding sex-dependent vulnerability may improve interpretation of EPVS as a biomarker of glymphatic dysfunction, vascular aging, and metabolic brain health. These findings underscore the need to elucidate the sex-specific mechanisms that connect systemic metabolic states with brain aging processes.
