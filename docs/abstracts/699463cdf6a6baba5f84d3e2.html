Introduction

Patients with left-hemisphere glioma are likely to experience language disorders induced by tumor resection surgery.¹ These deficits can either be overcome during recovery or lead to long-term damage. For surgical planning, it is of interest to predict recovery capacity prior to surgery. Multiple studies on large cohorts of healthy subjects have shown that functional connectivity (FC) predicts cognitive measures and behavior, making it a promising feature for clinical predictions.² FC matrices lie on the Riemannian manifold of the positive semi-definite (PSD) matrices. Riemannian manifolds locally exhibit near Euclidean properties. This implies that if a set of PSD matrices are close enough to one another, operations such as distances, products, averages or linear interpolations can be computed much like in conventional matrix or vector spaces. In practice, defining a boundary where this approximation holds is not obvious. Thus, some applications may benefit of approaching these operations considering the geometry of the Riemannian manifold. The log-Cholesky metric provides a simple and efficient mapping for PSD matrices to a Euclidean space,³ making it a suitable embedding method for FC. In this work, we leverage the log-Cholesky FC embedding to analyze connectivity in each hemisphere of the brain. We define a measure for interpretability on the log-Cholesky embedding using the gradient of the inverse mapping. We validate our method on a large database of healthy subjects that defines a reference in latent space. We then study the differences between the embeddings of patients recovered from postoperative language deficits and those with long-term impairment on a small preliminary pre-surgical dataset of glioma patients.

Methods

We hypothesize that FC in the hemisphere contralateral to the glioma can predict a patient’s preoperative capacity for recovery. Thus, assessing similarity between FC within the healthy hemisphere of patients and FC within each hemisphere of healthy controls might reveal differences between recovered and impaired subjects (Figure 1d). For such, we need a representation for FC where difference is mathematically well-defined and computationally efficient. This representation should also minimize overlap between left and right-hemisphere within-connectivities, reflecting hemispheric differences. Therefore, we use log-Cholesky mapping as an embedding for FC, where one can measure differences using Euclidean norms and where we find distinct left and right embeddings (Figures 1c and d). To compare connectivity within each hemisphere, we use a homotopic atlas where parcellations are equivalent across hemispheres. For interpretability in the log-Cholesky embedding, we define a metric to quantify how FC varies between two points in the latent space: let f be the log-Cholesky mapping, given two FC matrices A and B, and their respective embeddings f(A) and f(B), we compute the integral of the gradient of the inverse mapping (f -1) along the line connecting f(A) and f(B) (Figures 1b, e and f). The result is a matrix representing the total change in connectivity for each parcel as one moves along the line.

We consider two resting-state fMRI (rsfMRI) datasets: the Young Adult dataset of the Human Connectome Project (HCP) and a preliminary pre-surgical dataset with 8 patients that had a glioma at the insula of the left hemisphere of the brain. All patients underwent surgery with the same neurosurgeon and exhibited postoperative perseveration. Language disorders were evaluated with picture-naming tests in pre and postoperative and four months after surgery. Three patients were deemed recovered. rsfMRI data was projected into the 400-parcels and 7-networks homotopic atlas proposed by Yan et al.⁴ From each subject's 400x400 FC matrix, we derive two within-hemisphere connectivity matrices, each of size 200x200 (Figure 1a). For patients, only the right-hemisphere FC matrices are used. The log-Cholesky embedding of each 200x200 matrix is computed via Cholesky decomposition followed by application of the logarithm on the diagonal of the resulting matrix (Figure 1a). To ensure bijectivity of the mapping, eigenvalues of FC matrices were clipped to a small positive value. 

To validate our interpretability metric on HCP, we compute the path integral along the line connecting the centroid of the right-hemisphere embeddings to the centroid of the left-hemisphere embeddings (Figure 1e). From the 200x200 resulting matrix, we compute a percentage absolute change (PAC) for each parcel by summing the absolute values along the columns and dividing by the sum of the absolute values of all entries. We validate our findings by selecting the parcels with highest PACs (top 5%) and performing a meta-analysis to identify terms associated with these areas on each hemisphere.

To analyze the embeddings of patients, we compute the referred integral along the line from the right-hemisphere centroid of HCP to the embedding of patient's right-hemisphere FC (Figure 1f). The resulting 200x200 matrix is aggregated into a 7x7 matrix with a measure of weighted absolute changes from network-to-network (WACNN). The entry (i,j) on the WACNN is computed by summing the absolute values of entries corresponding to parcels in network i (rows) and network j (columns), followed by normalization by the number of parcels in networks i and j. To find relevant differences, we performed a t-test on WACNN matrices between recovered and impaired groups, correcting the p-values for multiple comparisons using the False Discovery Rate (FDR) method.

Results

The result of the path integral along the line connecting the right-hemisphere centroid to the left-hemisphere centroid for healthy subjects shows that connectivity changes are not uniformily distributed across parcels (Figure 2a). Instead, they are concentrated primarily within parcels of the default mode and frontoparietal control networks, with additional relevant changes occuring in parcels of the ventral and dorsal attention networks (Figure 2a). The parcels with the top 5% highest PACs values are located mainly the inferior parietal lobe and prefrontal cortex (Figure 2b). The meta-analysis retrieved terms indicating that the selected areas in the left hemisphere are more strongly linked to language. Some examples of associated terms are "language", "sentence", "semantic" and "speech". However, in the right hemisphere, terms such as "theory of mind", "mental states" and "default" are more strongly associated with the areas. These differences suggest that our method captures known functional asymmetries between the two hemispheres.

The result of the path integral along the line connecting the right-hemisphere centroid of HCP and each patient's FC embedding showed substantial variability across patients, consequently, the WACNN matrices for each patient presented distinct patterns (Figure 2c and d). Consistently across patients, the connections with the limbic network were the ones with the smallest changes. The same was observed for the connections between the somatomotor network with default mode and frontoparietal control networks (Figure 2c and d). The connections between parcels within the same network, in average, showed higher weighted absolute changes, notably for impaired patients. The connection of these networks with the ventral attention network were the ones that showed the highest variability in their weighted absolute changes across patients (Figure 2c and d). The intensity of the change of such connections is greater in impaired subjects compared to recovered patients (Figure 2d). Out of the 49 connections, five distinct connections are identified as statistically relevant to distinguish the recovered from the impaired group when controlling the false discovery rate at 5%. When we drop this tolerance to 1%, two connections involving the dorsal attention network remain significant (Figure 2d).

Conclusion

The use of the log-Cholesky mapping to embed FC provides a suitable way to perform Euclidean operations on connectivity matrices, while avoiding approximation errors that might exist when one apply such operations directly on the PSD matrices. We derive a formal interpretation of matrix differences from this representation computing the integral of the gradient of the inverse mapping along the line connecting two embedded vectors.

Using this methodology we found significant differences in the representations of recovered and impaired patients on a preliminary clinical dataset. The small sample size of the patient dataset precludes drawing firm conclusions about the attributes of the contralateral connectivity that associate with a higher recovery capacity. Nevertheless, our results points to a promising and interpretable methodology for deriving predictions of clinical outcomes from connectivity patterns, which justify further investigation.
