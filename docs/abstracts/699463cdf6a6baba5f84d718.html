Introduction

Pitch is a fundamental carrier of communicative intent in human speech, conveying prosodic and lexical information [1]. A core auditory challenge is speaker normalization—extracting consistent linguistic meaning from acoustic variability across speakers [5]. Listeners achieve this by rapidly estimating vocal range using signal-intrinsic cues like harmonic structure.

While the neural basis of pitch extraction—involving temporal versus place coding—is debated, higher-order processing is largely attributed to cortical networks, consistent with the dual-stream model [15]. This corticocentric view, however, may overlook the thalamus, the obligatory gateway to the cortex. The ventral medial geniculate body (vMGB) is not a passive relay; its complex receptive fields suggest an active role in filtering ascending information, shaped by corticothalamic feedback. Animal studies causally link impaired vMGB feedback to a loss of spectral precision needed for resolving harmonic cues, but its role in human pitch normalization remains unverified due to the challenge of non-invasively modulating deep targets.

We employed Temporal Interference (TI) stimulation [21] to selectively modulate the vMGB. TI generates a low-frequency envelope at depth via interfering high-frequency fields, sparing superficial tissue [23]. We hypothesized that if the vMGB acts as a spectral sharpener, TI should enhance pitch normalization, specifically for high relative pitches. At low frequencies, robust temporal phase-locking may render thalamic enhancement redundant. As pitch ascends, reliance shifts to fragile place-based cues, creating a bottleneck where thalamic sharpening should be most beneficial. Our results confirm this frequency-specific dissociation, providing the first causal evidence for the auditory thalamus as an active computational hub in speaker normalization.

Methods

Participants

A total of 24 right-handed, healthy adult participants (12 females, 12 males; mean age = 24.2 ± 2.8 years) were recruited for this study. This sample size was determined a priori using GPower software (version 3.1.9.7)*. Based on a mixed-design ANOVA (between-factor: Group, with 2 levels; within-factor: Time, with 2 levels), an estimated medium effect size (f = 0.25), α = 0.05, and a desired power (1-β) of 0.80, the calculated total sample size was N=24. Participants were randomly assigned to either the Active TI stimulation group (n=12) or the Sham stimulation group (n=12). All participants were native Mandarin Chinese speakers with no history of neurological, psychiatric, or hearing disorders. Normal hearing sensitivity was confirmed by pure-tone audiometry (thresholds≤25 dB HL at octave frequencies from 250 to 8000 Hz). The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of University of Science and Technology of China, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the experiment.

Experimental Design

This study employed a mixed-factorial design. The between-subjects factor was Stimulation Group (Active TI vs. Sham). The within-subjects factors were Time (Pre-stimulation vs. Post-stimulation assessment) and Relative Pitch (High vs. Low). The primary dependent variable was pitch normalization accuracy, quantified using the Fisher z-transformed Spearman rank-order correlation coefficient (ρ) between participants' subjective pitch ratings and the ground-truth relative pitch values.

Stimuli and Apparatus

The auditory stimuli consisted of the vowel /a/ produced by 20 native English speakers (10 females, 10 males). Each speaker produced the vowel at eight distinct pitch levels within their natural speaking range. All stimuli were normalized for loudness and duration (500 ms). Electric field simulations for TI stimulation targeting the left ventral medial geniculate body (vMGB) were performed using SimNIBS 4.0 based on individual T1-weighted structural MRI scans to ensure targeting accuracy  (Figure 1a and b).

Procedure

The experiment consisted of two separate sessions (Active TI and Sham) conducted on different days, with the order counterbalanced across participants (Figure 1c). Each session followed an identical procedure: first, participants completed a pre-stimulation assessment of the pitch normalization task; then, they received 30 minutes of either Active TI or Sham stimulation; finally, a post-stimulation assessment identical to the pre-test was administered immediately after the stimulation period.

Temporal Interference (TI) Stimulation

TI stimulation was delivered using a battery-driven stimulator. The stimulation parameters were set to generate an effective envelope frequency of 40 Hz at the target (vMGB). For the Active TI group, the current was ramped up to 2 mA (peak-to-peak) and maintained for 30 minutes. For the Sham group, the current was ramped up and then immediately down after 30 seconds to mimic the initial sensation without delivering effective stimulation.

Data Analysis

Behavioral data were analyzed using a mixed-design ANOVA with Stimulation Group (Active, Sham) as the between-subjects factor, and Time (Pre, Post) and Relative Pitch (High, Low) as within-subjects factors. Post-hoc comparisons were conducted using independent and paired samples t-tests with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. The significance threshold was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Validation of Non-invasive Thalamic Targeting and Baseline Pitch Perception‌

Electric field simulations confirmed focal delivery of temporal interference (TI) stimulation to the left ventral medial geniculate body (vMGB) with high on-target specificity (On-Target Ratio > 1.8). Pre-stimulation behavioral tests established robust baseline pitch normalization, with subjective ratings strongly correlating with ground-truth relative pitch values (Figure 1d).

vMGB Modulation Selectively Enhances High-Pitch Normalization‌

Low-Pitch Range (≤0.5)‌: No significant difference between active TI and sham groups (t₂₂ = -0.77, p = 0.45;  Figure 2a), consistent with redundant thalamic modulation under robust temporal coding.

High-Pitch Range (>0.5)‌: Active TI stimulation significantly improved normalization accuracy versus sham (t₂₂ = 2.45, p = 0.023;  Figure 2a). Sham groups showed negligible changes, while active stimulation enhanced high-frequency pitch resolution where spectral cues dominate.

Thalamic Modulation Preferentially Sharpens Ordinal Pitch Coding‌

In high-pitch trials, TI enhanced both linear (Pearson’s r, p = 0.032) and ordinal (Spearman’s ρ, p = 0.023) correlation metrics (Figure 2b). Stronger effects on ordinal fidelity suggest vMGB sharpens rank-order discrimination rather than uniformly scaling pitch perception.

Robustness of Relative Pitch Processing Across Speaker Genders‌

Analysis restricted to male speakers confirmed frequency-specific effects:

Low-Pitch‌: No TI effect (p = 0.737).

High-Pitch‌: Large effect size persisted (Cohen’s d = 0.73), though statistical significance was marginal due to reduced trials (t₂₂ = 1.78, p = 0.088;  Figure 2c), confirming thalamic processing of relative spectral position independent of absolute frequency or speaker gender.

Conclusion

Our results provide the first direct causal evidence in humans that the auditory thalamus functions as an active computational hub for speaker normalization, rather than a passive sensory relay. By non-invasively potentiating vMGB activity via TI stimulation, we observed a selective enhancement of pitch normalization performance that was specifically restricted to the high-relative-pitch regime, with no observable impact on low-pitch perception. This frequency-specific dissociation aligns with the theoretical dichotomy of pitch coding, where robust temporal phase-locking at low frequencies renders thalamic modulation redundant, while fragile place-code dependence at high frequencies benefits from active spectral sharpening.

The observed dissociation can be explained by distinct neural codes across frequency ranges:

Low-Frequency Processing‌: Supported by robust temporal phase-locking in the brainstem, often reinforced by acoustic markers like glottal fry, creating a performance ceiling that makes additional thalamic gain control unnecessary.High-Frequency Processing‌: Phase-locking fidelity deteriorates, forcing reliance on fragile place-based spectral cues that benefit from thalamic sharpening.

Crucially, the effect persisted even when restricting analyses to male speakers, confirming that thalamic processing operates on relative spectral position rather than absolute physical frequency.

Our findings mirror recent animal studies showing that ablation of corticothalamic feedback to vMGB impairs mistuned harmonic detection. The anatomical basis for this modulation resides in Layer 6 corticothalamic pathways, which enable cortex to shape ascending sensory inputs. We propose that high-relative-pitch stimuli act as high-demand signals recruiting these feedback loops.

The study suggests the thalamus acts as a "hardware accelerator" for spectral preprocessing, offering insights for developing more efficient bio-inspired speech recognition systems that achieve robust speaker normalization with reduced training overhead.

Our successful application of TI stimulation demonstrates its potential as a precise tool for dissecting deep-brain cognitive mechanisms in healthy populations, bridging the gap between deep-brain physiology and complex human behavior.
