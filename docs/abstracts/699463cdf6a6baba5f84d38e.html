Introduction

As Parkinson’s disease (PD) progresses individuals experience debilitating non-motor symptoms, beyond the primary motor ones, which greatly increase disability1. Cognitive impairment afflicts up to 40% of non-demented individuals, affecting mental flexibility, executive function (planning, set-shifting, inhibitory-control), language, and working-memory. Hallucinations are another considerable affliction with up to 80% experiencing them throughout the disease course. These include minor hallucinations, i.e. hallucinations of presence or passage without sensory components and misperceptions of visual stimuli, and more advanced structured hallucinations, i.e. typically fully formed visual hallucinations. In addition, individuals with PD also experience other non-motor symptoms like anxiety, depression, apathy, impulsivity and delusions.

The root cause of these symptoms is poorly understood but the past decade has made various associations with characteristics of brain organisation. Specifically, with time-invariant functional connectivity (FC), thought to reflect preferential connections across brain regions that identify a base network architecture, and with time-varying FC, which may be a more fine-grained property reflective of network communication across the architecture2. Despite the latter producing promising inferences, results within both characteristics are not always concordant, and associations have been limited to niche domains. Here we set out to directly test whether these characteristics capture an underlying pathological process in PD that may underpin these non-motor symptoms. To do so, we started by modelling network architecture and communication, and afterwards predicted batteries of symptoms and test scores through these models. Our results indicate that network communication explains a significant percentage of symptom variance and may capture a fundamental dysfunction in PD brain organisation that highly contributes or underpins these symptoms.

Methods

Population and assessments

Non-demented individuals with PD were recruited from partner hospitals across Switzerland. Patients performed neurological examinations with their referral neurologist, and neuropsychiatric assessments (with a neuropsychologist) focused on four domains: cognition, hallucinations, other neuropsychiatric phenomena, and behavioural (depression, loneliness, apathy and impulsivity).

Imaging and preprocessing

Patients underwent a single session of resting-state fMRI at Campus Biotech (Geneva, CH) or the University Hospital of Bern (CH). The scanners, Siemens MAGNETON Prisma, used echo-planar sequences (EPI, TR = 0.7 s, TE = 30 ms, flip angle = 52°, acceleration factor of 8) with an in-plane resolution of 2x2 mm and a slice thickness of 2 mm (no gap, FOV = 256 mm, 72 slices). Structural images were acquired using a T1-weighted MPRAGE sequence (208 slices, FOV = 256 mm, TR = 2.2s, TE = 2.96ms).

Preprocessing of functional images used predefined CONN pipelines with slice-timing correction, spatial re-alignment with concomitant field distortion correction, interpolation of frames with high framewise displacement (> 0.9 mm), normalisation to MNI space, and spatial smoothing (6mm FWHM). Data was then denoised, including linear detrending and regressing out six movement parameters from the realignment phase, 18 RETROICOR-based parameters related to cardiac and respiratory phases (and interactions), one for respiratory-volume per unit and another for heart-rate variability. Finally, a band-pass filter between 0.008Hz and 0.09Hz was used.

Network architecture

To model time-invariant FC, i.e. functional network architecture, we resorted to group spatial independent component analysis through an implementation known as FastICA deployed in the CONN toolbox. This method obtains a single set of components for the group. Network architecture can then be represented for each participant as a N x N covariance matrix between the components. For this application we selected 20 independent components, with a neuroimaging expert classifying components into cortical sources (17), cerebellum (1) and noise (2).

Network Communication

To model time-varying FC, i.e. functional network communication, we resorted to the HMM-MAR toolbox 3, which contains a Hidden Markov Model (HMM) implementation designed to capture dynamic aspects of FC. The key assumptions of this HMM application are that latent states rule the correlational properties between the independent components (network architecture) and that those latent states vary over time, promoting different correlational properties between the components at those times (Figure 1). At the same time, the latent states exhibit different likelihoods of transitioning between them. We fixed the number of latent states at three, without optimising this, and repeated the HMM 100 times, given that HMM solutions are not deterministic.  

Prediction of symptoms and test scores

The last step of the analyses was to predict test scores from models of network architecture and network communication. To do so we used kernel ridge regression based on distance matrixes within-modality, which allowed us to compare predictions between models of different modalities. We validated this using a k-fold approach that left out 3 patients for the testing group. For all predictions, we de-confounded the effects of age, gender, acquisition site and years of schooling. Additionally, for tests related to hallucinations, other neuropsychiatric phenomena, and behavioural, we de-confounded the effect of cognitive scores by including the outcome of the best predicted cognitive score. This was due to known correlations between cognitive impairment and these domains. Finally, a permutation structure repeated the predictions 5000 times per HMM repetition while shuffling the labels to evaluate through a null-distribution that accounted for the HMM shared components, whether the predictions were significant or not.

Results

Population and assessments

In total 51 individuals with PD completed the protocol (37 males). The average age was 68.24 years (SD: ±7.43) and average disease duration 8.9 years (SD: ±4.21). Patients had an average motor score (UPDRS-III) of 19.14 ±10, a Hoeh-Yahr of 2 ± 0.5, and a Levodopa equivalent daily dosage of 852.53 ± 489.50 (agonists only: 125.06 ±143.45). Every patient scored at least 22 in the Montreal Cognitive Assessment but otherwise there was significant variability in other cognitive scores, for example the fronto-cortical sub-score of the PD-CRS showed a range of 43 – 93 with an average of 69.78 ± 13.08 and the time to complete the Stroop C4 was 50 to 197 seconds with an average of 82.63 ± 28.86 seconds. With respect to hallucinations, 19 had structured and minor hallucinations, 14 had only minor hallucinations, and 18 did not present any hallucinations. The other domains showed different levels of anxiety, depression, and impulsivity, but less variability was observed in the neuropsychiatric domain outside hallucinations.

Network communication and non-motor symptoms

We used 20 independent components to model network architecture and followed by an HMM with three latent states to model network communication across this architecture. Then, through a machine learning approach based on kernels over dissimilarity matrixes, we assessed how network communication could predict patient scores and symptoms across a series of domains.

Our analyses revealed that time-varying FC is capable of predicting various symptoms, with particular emphasis on the cognitive and hallucination domains. The patient HMMs were able to explain 30.22% (median) of the variance of the fronto-cortical sub-score of the PD-CRS (P < 0.001; Figure 2A), 18.39% for the raw score of the Rey-figure (P = 0.005), 25.64% for the time to complete the Trail-Making Test A (P <  0.001) and 24.18% for the time to complete the Stroop C4 (P < 0.001). For hallucinations (Figure 2B), the median explained variance was 17.72% for the hallucination sub-score of the SAPS (P = 0.010) and 18.25% for the frequency of structured hallucinations (P = 0.005), with the frequency of minor hallucinations at 14.40% but not achieving significance (P = 0.073). Other predicted symptoms included the self-initiation score of the DAS with a median explained variance of 15.81% (P = 0.029), the HADS anxiety score with 14.97% (P = 0.049) and the UPDRS-I with 29.71% (P < 0.001).

In addition, to understand if model parameters considerably affected predictions, we ran 5000 repetitions of all possible combinations of 15 to 50 independent components and two to six latent states. Analysing a predictive range defined by the median explained variance plus/minus twice the standard deviation (i.e. rewarding performance but penalising instability), showed predictions were stable for every latent state when using 15 to 28 independent components and not statistically different than those using the original choice of parameters (e.g. FC-PDCRS: median explained variance = 28.05%, median high-bound = 37.22%, median low-bound = 18.98%). However, increasing the number of independent components led to significantly more erratic predictions (e.g. FC-PDCRS: median explained variance = 21.11%, median high-bound: 41.21%, median low-bound: -2.34%; P < 0.001). Although we could not distinguish between inability to resolve model complexity or less independent components better representing the ground truth, this validated the original parameter choice.

Network architecture and symptom scores

We also predicted test scores from network architecture (time-invariant FC) using the same machine-learning approach. Across all domains network architecture only predicted the time to complete the Stroop C4 (median explained variance: 20.04%, P = 0.009) and the executive functioning sub-score of the DAS (median explained variance: 16.04%, P = 0.045).

Conclusion

We inquired whether changes to a characteristic of brain organisation, network communication, could underpin the development of non-motor symptoms in PD, by explicitly modelling time-varying FC and using those models to predict symptoms. Our results demonstrate that network communication predicts several symptoms across the cognitive, hallucination, and behavioural domains, potentially identifying it as an ongoing pathological process in PD leading to the symptoms.

Network communication (i.e. time-varying FC) outperformed network architecture (i.e. time-invariant FC) in predicting non-motor symptoms. PD initially afflicts the brainstem via α-synuclein-mediated neurodegeneration, degrades sub-cortical to cortex connectivity, and eventually leads to neurodegeneration in cortical areas as well. As this process progresses we hypothesize that impairments in neural modulation affect stability and transitions across large-scale networks before a fundamental network architecture reorganisation is seen2. This is compatible with observations of impaired coupling between attentional and default-mode networks4, and follows the pathological time course where areas such as the frontal eye field (dorsal attentional network) and anterior cingulate regions (salience network) lose incoming sub-cortical connectivity before being impacted by neurodegeneration. As such, the ability of time-varying FC to recover fast state-dependent fluctuations makes it more adequate to capture early pathological changes.

The strongest predictions emerged in cognition, aligned with the idea that cognitive flexibility hinges on the ability of the central executive network (CEN) and dorsal attentional network (DAN) to dynamically allocate resources to other task-relevant networks. We hypothesise that with network communication degradation, transitions may slow, become less precise or responsive, producing longer response times, more errors and reduced executive throughput. Indeed, the most predicted scores were on the fronto-cortical PD-CRS, visuoconstructive memory (Rey figure), processing speed (TMT-A) and interference control (Stroop C4), the exact type of tests that require rapid switching and allocation of resources. The explained variance across these measures suggests that time-varying FC is capturing deficiencies in network communication present at rest, in frontoparietal control, visuo-attentional and salience systems, that may be a critical substrate for the cognitive phenotype of PD.

A complementary and clinically relevant pattern was also observed for hallucinations. Network communication predicted frequency of structured and almost of minor hallucinations, more than it did severity (note that the SAPS is a mixed assessment). We hypothesise frequency is related to an ongoing instability in large-scale brain network organisation, specifically how often the system falls into hallucinatory states, whereas severity may rather depend on up/downstream representational, sensory, or contextual content only relevant once those states are entered. The emphasis on frequency resonates with work in psychedelic induction of hallucinations, which has demonstrated that higher-entropy and hence lower energy demanding systems promote more frequent and random transitions across brain states5. By analogy, PD-related disruption of brain organisation may lead to looser control over network communication, leading to more random transitions, thereby raising hallucination frequency. An important clinical implication may be to prioritise frequency as a circuit‑level marker of organisational fragility and treatment response, while treating early-stage severity as a complementary outcome shaped by environment, medication state and comorbid cognitive impairment.

Our findings revealed that brain organisation in PD suffers pathological alterations that fundamentally underpin non-motor symptoms, which may also provide important clinical insights and help study disease progression.
