Introduction

Post-stroke aphasia arises from widespread brain network disruption rather than isolated cortical lesions. Predicting which structural pathways aphasic patients could recruit for language recovery requires interpreting residual connectivity in the context of broader anatomical and behavioural constraints. Two population-level distributions are especially informative as priors: healthy anatomical pathway probabilities, which reflect typical routes that commonly support language, and lesion–symptom associations, which identify structural (dis)connections reliably linked to language impairment. Together, these priors capture normative network organization and the behavioural relevance of specific connections.

However, current post-stroke connectomic approaches do not integrate these two forms of information. Analyses based solely on the lesioned connectome struggle to differentiate missing edges from functional reorganization, while fully data-driven clustering lacks anatomical or behavioural guidance. Consequently, we lack methods to identify patient-specific network communities, the interconnected brain regions that work together to support behaviour.

Here, we introduce a Bayesian hierarchical stochastic block model (hSBM) that embeds each patient’s connectome into a dual-prior, multilayer framework. Healthy pathway probabilities provide a normative reference graph, and lesion–symptom mappings introduce behaviourally meaningful constraints. For each individual patient, the model infers multiple communities arranged hierarchically, reflecting viable anatomical pathways and lesion-limited brain structure. Our central question is whether jointly leveraging these two priors yields communities that provide deeper mechanistic insight and more accurate prediction of post-stroke language outcomes.

Methods

To characterize individual post-stroke brain–language network organization, we developed a dual-prior Bayesian hierarchical stochastic block model (hSBM). We applied this approach to high-resolution MRI data and detailed language assessments (Comprehensive Aphasia Test, CAT) from 35 chronic aphasic stroke patients. Each patient’s structural graph was modeled jointly with two population-derived priors: (i) a healthy probabilistic language-pathway network and (ii) a lesion-deficit graph derived from a large chronic stroke cohort. We then evaluated the model by comparing its predictive accuracy and the distinctiveness of its inferred community structure in four different conditions.

2.1 Prior construction:

Healthy language-network prior.

A normative language graph was constructed using functional language localizer MRI data from 806 participants (mean age 30.23 years, 19–75; 329 males, 477 females).1 For each participant, we quantified structural connections2 and extracted shortest-path routes3 supporting language tasks. Aggregating across individuals yielded a path-probability map, where each edge’s probability was defined by its frequency of occurrence across all shortest paths (e.g., appearing in 800/806 participants gives P = 0.993). This map captures common structural pathways supporting normal language function.

Lesion-behaviour prior.

To incorporate behaviourally relevant connectivity, we used the Aphasia Recovery Cohort dataset4 (N=228 chronic post-stroke patients; mean age 58 years, 27-80; 140 males, 88 females; mean time post-stroke 3.86 years, 0.11-21.9). We quantified each patient’s lesioned connectome2 and computed the association between structural disconnection and impaired language function assessed by Western Aphasia Battery Aphasia Quotient. Edges with significant correlations (FDR-corrected p < 0.05) were retained, forming a behaviourally relevant lesion-deficit graph.

2.2. Individualized Inference

For each patient in our own sample (N=35; mean age 58.6 years, 29–82; 25 males, 10 females; mean time post-stroke 10.34 years, 1–34), we constructed individual structural graph and inferred hierarchical communities using hSBM implemented in graph-tool.5 The healthy prior initialized the block structure, while the lesion-deficit graph formed a second edge layer. The model minimized description length through 1000 agglomerative Markov Chain Monte Carlo sweeps, allowing partitions to transition away from the healthy prior and adapt to each patient’s residual brain topology.

2.3. Feature Extraction

From each inferred hierarchy, we computed topological features summarizing community efficiency and organization, including intra- and inter-block efficiencies, the number, size, and density of non-empty communities, and partition entropy.

2.4. Model Comparison

We compared four conditions: (1) no prior, (2) healthy prior only, (3) patient prior only, and (4) dual priors in understanding predictive performance and community distinctiveness in our patient sample. For each condition, we trained Random Forest regressors (100 estimators, max depth = 5) using 5-fold cross-validation to predict comprehensive CAT language scores. Mean R² values were compared across conditions using ANOVA and post-hoc t-tests. To assess whether priors induced unique community organization, we computed pairwise Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) between partitions generated under each model. Significant results are reported at p < 0.01.

Results

A repeated-measures ANOVA showed a significant effect of model type on prediction accuracy (F = 6.389, p < 0.001).  The dual-prior model achieved the highest predictive accuracy for overall CAT language scores (R² = 0.903), significantly outperforming both the healthy-prior (R² = 0.839) and no-prior (R² = 0.826) models (p < 0.001), while the patient-prior model alone showed comparable strong performance (R² = 0.891).

By contrast, the community structures produced by the dual-prior model were topologically distinct from all other models. The average dissimilarity (1 – ARI) between dual-prior partitions and single-prior or no-prior partitions was 0.773, demonstrating that the dual-prior solution did not simply interpolate other configurations. Instead, combining normative pathway probabilities with lesion-behaviour constraints yielded a unique hierarchical architecture.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed the first dual-prior Bayesian hierarchical stochastic block model (hSBM) for post-stroke aphasia connectomics. This generative framework advances network inference by integrating normative structural pathway probabilities with patient-derived lesion–behaviour topology, moving beyond conventional clustering methods by embedding anatomical constraints and behavioural relevance into the model.

Applied to 35 chronic post-stroke aphasic patients, the dual-prior model produced community structures that most strongly aligned with language performance and were topologically distinct from other models. A repeated-measures ANOVA confirmed a significant effect of model type on prediction accuracy, with the dual-prior model achieving the highest accuracy for CAT language scores. While patient-prior information alone was a strong predictor, the dual-prior solution showed high dissimilarity from single-prior and no-prior models, indicating a unique hierarchical architecture shaped jointly by normative pathways and lesion–behaviour constraints.

These findings reveal how stroke reshapes the language connectome and how the resulting communities support preserved linguistic function. They also offer a mechanistic account of variability in aphasia outcomes: recovery may depend on specific hierarchical communities shaped by the interaction between preserved normative pathways and lesion-imposed constraints. This modelling framework therefore provides a route toward individualized, mechanistically interpretable biomarkers, advancing future approaches to aphasia rehabilitation.

Methodological extensions will further enhance this framework. Incorporating longitudinal connectomes will enable tests of whether evolving community structure predicts treatment responsiveness, while addressing current constraints in sample size and parameter space will help refine priors and improve generalizability. These developments will allow stronger inference about the mechanisms linking structural reorganization to behavioural recovery.

This framework has broad implications for neuroscience research and clinical practice. For research, it provides an anatomically grounded, behaviourally informed generative framework applicable to other neurological conditions. Clinically, this approach may support connectome-guided rehabilitation by identifying patient-specific network targets that are mechanistically linked to recovery trajectories, moving the field from descriptive correlates to actionable network-based interventions. Together, these contributions provide a foundation for future neurorehabilitation tools.
