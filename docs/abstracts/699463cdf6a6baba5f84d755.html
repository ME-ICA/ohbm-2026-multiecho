Introduction

Post-stroke sensorimotor outcomes show substantial variability, partly associated with differences in lesion locations. Three key white matter pathways—the corticospinal tract (CST), dorsal-column medial-lemniscus (DCML), and corpus callosum (CC) —form an interconnected network that supports sensorimotor control and interhemispheric communication. Because these pathways intersect in close spatial proximity within the brain, stroke lesions occurring at different anatomical levels may disrupt them to varying degrees, potentially leading to distinct clinical manifestations.1,2 Although prior work has shown that lesions at higher CST levels—where CST and callosal motor fibers converge—are more likely to compromise CC integrity, whereas lower CST lesions do not necessarily induce retrograde callosal degeneration.2 However, evidence remains limited to the CST–CC system and largely excludes the DCML pathway. The specific effects on the three interconnected tracts related to the lesion locations remain unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the impact of different lesion locations on the white matter integrity of the CST, DCML, and CC pathways, and to further examine the contribution of pathway integrity to sensorimotor function in chronic stroke patients.

Methods

Twenty-one chronic stroke patients with lesions involving CST were divided into two groups based on lesion location: lesion directly involved CC (CCi group, n = 11, age = 57.66 ± 11.77 years, affected hemisphere = 8 right : 3 left, time after stroke = 15.90 ± 7.23 months) and non-CC lesion (CCn group, n = 10, age = 60.72 ± 9.22 years, affected hemisphere = 3 right : 7 left, time after stroke = 15.37 ± 14.21 months). 

    Diffusion-weighted images were acquired on a 3T MRI system with 102 diffusion-encoding gradient directions. The maximal diffusion sensitivity bmax was 4000 s/mm2. A 32-channel phased-array head coil was used for all MRI acquisitions. DSI Studio3 (Chen version, Feb 5, 2024) was used to conduct tract-specific analysis. Deterministic tractography of the CST, DCML, and CC was reconstructed using the AutoTrack algorithm4 to minimize subjective bias (Tracking Threshold: 0.1%; Step Size: 0.5mm; n = 6000 streamlines). Following automated reconstruction, the CC tract was manually cleaned to exclude lateral streamlines that intersected or paralleled CST and DCML pathways. This procedure ensured that the final CC-body retained only the central corpus callosum segment. All tracts included in subsequent analyses were non-overlapping and anatomically distinct (Figure 1). Mean quantitative anisotropy (QA) values were calculated to quantify the microstructural integrity of CST (affected and unaffected sides), DCML (affected and unaffected sides), and CC-body. 

    Sensorimotor function was assessed by Fugl-Meyer Assessment-Upper Extremity (FMA-UE) Motor and Sensory Subscales, respectively.

    Normality was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and baseline between-group differences were examined using independent t tests and chi-square tests. The differential impact of lesion locations on white matter integrity across the three tracts was analyzed using multivariate analysis of covariance (MANCOVA). Lesion volume was included as a covariate to control its potential confounding effect, and group (CCi vs. CCn) was specified as a fixed factor. A multiple linear regression model was used to examine the contribution of pathway integrity to sensorimotor function. Five neural tracts and their interactions with lesion location (tract x group) were initially entered, yielding eleven independent variables in the regression model. Interaction terms were included to test whether the relationship between tract integrity and sensorimotor function differed by lesion location.5 A stepwise model-selection procedure was applied to identify the most parsimonious set of variables that contribute to the main sensorimotor outcome. All alpha levels were set at .05.

Results

Compared with the CCn group, CCi group revealed significantly larger lesion volume (p = .008), lower QA values of the CST-affected (p = < .001) and DCML-affected tracts (p = .008), and lower scores on the FMA-UE Motor (p = .001) and Sensory Subscales (p = .030) (Figure 2). 

After controlling for lesion volume and specifying lesion location as a fixed factor, significant differences remained in the QA values of the CST-affected (F(1, 18) = 9.656, p = .001, = .518, power = .959) and DCML-affected tracts (F(1, 18) = 4.965, p = .019, = .356, power = .738). The stepwise multiple regression identified a single significant variable (F(1, 19) = 59.220, p < .001), explaining 75.7% of the variance in FMA-UE Motor scores (R2 = .757, adjusted R2 = .744). Of the eleven variables entered, only CST-affected QA remained in the model, demonstrating a strong association with motor impairment (β = .870, t = 7.695, p < .001). Multiple regression analysis for sensory scores was not conducted due to the lack of variability in the CCn group.

Conclusion

This study showed that higher CST lesions involving callosal fibers led to more pronounced microstructural damage in key sensorimotor pathways, reflected by lower QA values. Patients with higher-level lesions also demonstrated significantly poorer motor and sensory performance compared with the lower-lesion group. Regarding the multiple linear regression analysis, CST-affected QA accounted for the greatest proportion of variance in motor outcomes and remained the only significant variable after considering other tracts and lesion-location factors, underscoring the dominant role of CST integrity in explaining chronic motor impairment. These findings support CST-affected integrity as a strong candidate imaging biomarker of motor impairment in stroke. A limitation of this study is that motor and sensory outcomes were assessed using FMA-UE. Although the FMA-UE motor subscale is a robust assessment of motor ability, its sensory subscale is less sensitive to sensory deficits in chronic stroke patients. As a result, sensory scores in the CCn group showed no variability, restricting the evaluation of relationships among neural networks, lesion location, and sensory function. Future studies could incorporate more sensitive sensory assessments to better detect patients’ sensory impairment to clarify the roles of CST, DCML, CC, and lesion location in sensorimotor recovery. Establishing a more rigorous understanding of these biomarkers and the interdependence among sensorimotor tracts and lesion locations will be essential for clarifying structure–function coupling and for guiding individualized rehabilitation strategies for clinical practitioners.
