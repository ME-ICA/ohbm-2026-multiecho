Introduction

Essential tremor (ET) is the most prevalent movement disorder, affecting 0.3-0.9% of the population1, and rated through the Clinical Rating Scale for Tremor (CRST). While initial treatment is pharmacological, about 30% of patients are treatment-resistant and seek surgical alternatives2.

MRI guided Focused Ultrasound (MRgFUS) has emerged as an effective incisionless surgery for ET that thermally ablates targeted brain regions, like the established Ventral intermediate Nucleus of the Thalamus (VIM) and its surrounding white matter tracts3. Diffusion tensor imaging permits visualization of these tracts and their structural changes through metrics like fractional anisotropy (FA), which reflects white matter structural integrity by measuring the direction of water molecule diffusion within tissue. Previous work has found that changes in FA from one day after MRgFUS surgery correlated to CRST improvement4, however, long term effects remain unclear.

In this study, we aim to investigate longitudinal brain remodeling following MRgFUS treatment and its relationship with outcome over three points: pre-operative, three months, and twelve months post-operative. We will determine if structural tract changes are related to CRST outcomes over time.

Methods

We retrospectively analyzed 133 ET patients who underwent unilateral VIM-MRgFUS at Toronto Western Hospital (n = 38) and Sunnybrook Health Sciences Centre (n = 95). Subjects’ CRST scores, T1-weighted MRI, and diffusion weighted imaging (DWI) scans were acquired pre-operatively and at three and twelve months post-operatively.

DWI scans were preprocessed using the FSL toolbox and fit with a diffusion tensor model through DTIFit (FMRIB Diffusion Toolbox v. 2.0, https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/) to generate FA maps for all subjects. An average FA map was created with FSL’s TBSS (Tract-Based Spatial Statistics, [Smith 2006]), part of FSL [Smith 2004]. It was then nonlinearly registered into standard Montreal Neurological Institute space using FNIRT [Andersson 2007a, 2007b] and split to make FA images in template space for each subject and timepoint. Significant clusters were identified using FSL’s cluster tool (p<0.001) and FA values were extracted from the ten largest clusters. To quantify change, post-operative FA values were subtracted from pre-operative and correlated with absolute change in CRST score.

Results

We were able to identify widespread brain changes following MRgFUS that evolved over time and produced meaningful relationships with clinical outcomes. When examining the relationship between CRST score and time, we discovered four main clusters whose correlations differed over the first post-operative year, located in the parahippocampal gyrus, cuneus, supplementary motor cortex, and insula (Figure 1). The cuneus cluster showed continuous negative associations with tremor improvement (three months: Spearman R = -0.33, p = 0.03; twelve months: Spearman R = -0.34, p = 0.06). Others demonstrated stronger relationships at twelve months than three, like cluster in the parahippocampal gyrus, suggesting that there are delays or malleable white-matter remodeling after treatment (three months: Spearman R = -0.07, p = 0.64; twelve months: Spearman R = -0.29, p = 0.10). These findings exhibit longitudinal brain remodeling after MRgFUS, and that these structural changes are partially related to outcome over the first year after treatment.

Conclusion

In this study, we examined how VIM-MRgFUS for ET causes broad morphological changes in the brain, well beyond motor areas. DWI analyses elucidated key areas of post-operative reorganization: clustering in the cuneus, supplementary motor cortex, parahippocampal gyrus, and insula. Some regions demonstrated early and continuous associations with tremor improvement, while others only became related to outcome later, implying that networks undergo remodeling at different times.

We present evidence of structural and connectivity changes after MRgFUS surgery. These findings propose that there are both sustained and time-dependent morphological changes, and that individual outcomes may be shaped by distinct patterns of neuroplasticity.
