Introduction

Fear responses consist of subjective fear experience and physiological-autonomic activation. These two facets of the fear response closely interact, but are thought to be supported by partially dissociable pathways.^1 It has been shown that cognitive reappraisal (CR), an emotion-regulation strategy in which individuals modify the perceived meaning of threatening events, can reduce initial subjective fear response, but its effectiveness in modulating autonomic activity is variable.^2 Meta-analyses targeting the neural correlates of CR found that it engages cognitive control regions in the prefrontal cortex and posterior parietal lobe,^3 areas that anatomically overlap with key nodes of the multiple-demand network (MDN).^4 However, most prior CR studies have relied on explicit reappraisal instructions during presentation of fear-inducing images,^3 whereas real-world encounters with threat are more immersive and often depend on implicit reinterpretation guided by contextual information and prior experience. Here, we combined immersive virtual reality (VR) with 7T fMRI to examine the effects of implicit cognitive reappraisal on subjective and physiological fear responses. We further tested whether individual differences in subjective fear modulation would be associated with neural modulation, and whether such associations converge in domain-general control systems involved in cognitive regulation.

Methods

Participants and experimental design: The study consisted of a VR session and a 7T fMRI session. In the VR session, 75 participants experienced a series of scenarios designed to evoke primitive fears (heights, spiders, drowning, darkness, and enclosed spaces; Figure 1A), interleaved with relaxing baseline environments. Physiological responses (heart rate, electrodermal activity (EDA), respiration) and subjective fear ratings were recorded during the experience. To implicitly elicit cognitive reappraisal, each scene was presented twice, with the second experience preceded by information that reframed the scene as less threatening. Following VR, a subset of 42 participants completed a 7T fMRI session comprising three tasks: (i) fear localizer task: participants viewed short video excerpts derived from the VR scenarios, featuring fearful or relaxing environments from a first-person perspective. (ii) cognitive reappraisal task: participants viewed short (~8 s) videos depicting events with potentially catastrophic outcomes, but leaving the ending ambiguous. Each video was presented twice, with the second viewing preceded by either a calming or a neutral information slide (Figure 1B). (iii) naturalistic movie task: participants watched a 9-minute segment from a ‘free-solo’ documentary showing a climber ascending a dangerous cliff. The movie was viewed twice, and during the second presentation participants already knew that the outcome was positive.

fMRI Analyses: The CR and the fear localizer tasks were analyzed using a general linear model (GLM) framework. GLM-derived ROIs were further used for correlating neural activity to behavioral measures. The naturalistic movie data were analyzed using a neural typicality approach ^5: for each voxel, an individual participant’s time series was correlated with the group mean time series computed in a leave-one-out manner, providing a voxel-wise index of how typical each participant’s response was relative to the group.  

Results

VR session 

Both subjective ratings and physiological responses showed substantial intersubject variability and were significantly reduced during the second, compared with the first, presentation of each scene (p < 0.001 for rating and EDA; Figure 2A). No correlation was found between subjective ratings and physiological measures (maximal r = 0.2), suggesting that these measures capture distinct components of the fear response.

fMRI session 

(i) Fear localizer task: Contrasting fearful versus relaxing VR videos revealed widespread activation across frontoparietal and attentional control networks, with the strongest effects observed in bilateral inferior frontal junction (IFJ; Figure 2B, left). Across participants, greater differential fear activation in the right IFJ correlated with higher subjective fear ratings during VR exposure (r = 0.4; p = 0.01), indicating that this region tracks individual differences in subjective fear experience. No such relationship was observed in the left IFJ (r=0.07).

(ii) Cognitive reappraisal task: Implicit reappraisal of videos (preceded by calming versus neutral information) reduced activation in core nodes of the multiple-demand network,^4 including bilateral IFJ, superior temporal sulcus (STS), and posterior dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (dmPFC; Figure 2B, middle). A similar but more left-lateralized activation pattern emerged when contrasting periods of viewing neutral versus calming text slides (Figure 2B, right). 

To link neural and behavioral CR measures, we correlated modulation in IFJ activation with the change in subjective ratings across VR exposures (first-second viewing). We found that right IFJ modulation when contrasting neutral and calming text significantly correlated with the magnitude of subjective fear rating reduction in VR (r = 0.37; p = 0.02), indicating that individuals showing stronger CR-related IFJ modulation in the fMRI CR task also exhibited larger decrease in subjective fear rating in VR. 

(iii) Naturalistic movie task: Neural typicality analysis revealed overall higher voxelwise typicality during the first compared with the second viewing of the naturalistic movie. Individual differences in typicality modulation within the right IFJ negatively correlated with the degree of subjective fear reduction in VR (r=-0.31; p=0.05), suggesting that individuals showing stronger behavioral reappraisal exhibited less typical (more idiosyncratic) neural responses during repeated naturalistic viewing.

Conclusion

Subjective and physiological measures of fear response were both modulated by implicit cognitive reappraisal, yet remained largely independent, consistent with dual-pathway accounts of fear.^1 Across three fMRI paradigms, activity within MDN nodes, most prominently the right IFJ, was modulated when subjective fear level was altered. Modulation of right IFJ activation tracked individual differences in subjective fear reduction in VR, suggesting a trait-like reappraisal capacity index, not specific to the set of stimuli presented in VR. Together, these results demonstrate that implicit cognitive reappraisal, induced without explicit strategy instructions, engages domain-general control systems and that activity modulation within these systems predicts changes in subjective manifestations of fear response. Combining immersive VR with naturalistic fMRI provides a powerful framework for studying individual variability in fear regulation in real-world contexts.
