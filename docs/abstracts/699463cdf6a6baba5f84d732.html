Introduction

Working memory (WM) relies on the rapid coordination of distributed neural networks to maintain and manipulate information. The N-back task is a well-established paradigm for probing WM, as it parametrically increases cognitive load to assess information maintenance and updating1. EEG studies of WM have traditionally focused on static spectral power changes and event-related potentials. However, such time-averaged measures may obscure transient, sub-second network reconfigurations that support cognitive processing.

Increasing evidence suggests that cognition emerges from dynamic brain states defined by short-lived patterns of phase synchronization across distributed regions. Phase-based approaches provide a principled framework for capturing these dynamics with high temporal resolution. Leading Eigenvector Dynamics Analysis (LEiDA) identifies dominant phase-locking configurations from instantaneous functional connectivity, enabling characterization of recurring brain states and their temporal properties2. Although LEiDA has been primarily applied to fMRI, its use with EEG allows frequency-resolved investigation of brain state dynamics at the millisecond scale. Phase-based EEG state analyses have been shown to capture transient network configurations during complex behaviors, underscoring their relevance for cognitively demanding tasks3.

Building on this framework, the present preliminary study adopts a dynamic, phase-based brain state perspective to examine how large-scale network configurations reorganize with increasing cognitive load. Using a visual N-back task (0-back, 1-back, and 2-back), we test whether task difficulty modulates the probability of occurrence and lifetime of distinct theta- and alpha-band brain states, providing insight into the temporal organization of functional connectivity supporting executive demands.

Methods

EEG data were acquired from three participants performing a visual N-back working memory task with parametrically increasing cognitive load (0-back, 1-back, and 2-back; two blocks per condition). Recordings were obtained at 500 Hz using a 32-channel Brain Products EEG system and processed in MATLAB using EEGLAB. Data were re-referenced to the common average reference and band-pass filtered (0.5–30 Hz). Independent component analysis (extended infomax) was applied in a conservative manner to identify and remove artifact-related components.

Preprocessed EEG signals were downsampled and band-pass filtered into theta (4–7 Hz) and alpha (8–12 Hz) frequency bands. Phase-based brain state dynamics were characterized using LEiDA. Within the LEiDA framework, instantaneous phase time series were obtained via the Hilbert transform, and time-resolved phase-coherence matrices were computed across channels. The leading eigenvector of each matrix was then extracted to represent the dominant phase-coupling pattern at each time point. Leading eigenvectors were clustered using k-means clustering to identify recurrent phase-based brain states, with the number of clusters determined via silhouette analysis.

For each frequency band and brain state, dynamic metrics, including probability of occurrence and mean lifetime, were computed separately for each cognitive load condition. Load-dependent effects were assessed using non-parametric statistical tests, including Friedman tests followed by Bonferroni-corrected Wilcoxon signed-rank post hoc comparisons.

Results

Identification of Dynamic Functional States

The k-means clustering analysis of the leading eigenvectors (k=3) revealed three distinct, recurrent patterns of phase synchronization (centroids) for both frequency bands. State 1 displayed a Posterior-Dominant (Visual/Attentional) phase-locking pattern. State 2 exhibited a Frontal-Dominant (Executive) configuration characterized by strong frontal-midline coherence. State 3 showed a diffused topology with contributions from posterior/parietal regions.

Alpha Band Dynamics

In the Alpha band (8–12 Hz), the temporal properties of the visual network were sensitive to cognitive load. The Probability of Occurrence for the Posterior-Dominant state (State 1) showed a significant main effect of load (Friedman test, p = 0.030). Similarly, the Lifetime of State 1 was significantly modulated by task difficulty (Friedman test, p = 0.030).

However, despite the significant main effect, conservative post-hoc comparisons indicated that specific pairwise differences did not survive Bonferroni correction. For both probability and lifetime, the difference between the 0-back and 2-back conditions approached significance (uncorrected p = 0.031) but failed to reach the corrected threshold (p_{bonf} = 0.094). No significant load-dependent modulation was observed for the Frontal (State 2, p=1.0) or Diffused (State 3, p=0.22) states in the alpha band.

Theta Band Dynamics

In the Theta band (4–7 Hz), cognitive load significantly modulated the dynamics of multiple states.

Frontal-Dominant State (State 2): The executive state showed a significant main effect of load for both Probability (Friedman test, p = 0.011) and Lifetime (Friedman test, p = 0.0302). Post-hoc analysis revealed that this state was more prevalent and stable during higher load conditions. Specifically, comparisons between 1-back vs. 2-back and 0-back vs. 2-back showed uncorrected significance (p = 0.031 for both), though these effects trended toward but did not reach significance after Bonferroni correction (pbonf = 0.094).

Posterior/Parietal State (State 3): This state also exhibited significant modulation in Probability (Friedman test, p = 0.011) and Lifetime (Friedman test, p = 0.011). Post-hoc tests suggested a reduction in state presence between 0-back and 1-back (uncorrected p = 0.031, pbonf = 0.094) and 1-back and 2-back (uncorrected p = 0.031, pbonf = 0.094), indicating a shift in network configuration as task demands increased.

Overall, while Friedman tests confirmed that cognitive load significantly alters the temporal landscape of theta and alpha band networks, strict Bonferroni corrections suggest that larger sample sizes may be required to robustly confirm specific pairwise differences.

Conclusion

Working memory load reliably modulates oscillatory brain dynamics, most prominently reflected by increases in frontal midline theta activity and task-related suppression of posterior alpha rhythms during N-back performance4,5. In this preliminary study, we extended our analysis beyond static assessments to characterize the sub-second temporal dynamics of functional brain networks supporting working memory. By applying LEiDA to EEG data acquired during an N-back task, we demonstrate that increasing cognitive load reorganizes both the temporal stability (lifetime) and recurrence (probability) of distinct phase-locked brain states.

Our primary finding reveals a frequency-specific trade-off between sensory processing and executive control states. In the theta band (4–7 Hz), increasing cognitive load shifted the brain’s dynamic repertoire toward a frontal-dominant state, with a significant increase in state lifetime, suggesting that higher executive demands are met by sustaining phase-locking within control networks for longer contiguous periods. Conversely, alpha-band (8–12 Hz) dynamics were characterized by a load-dependent reduction in the stability of a posterior-dominant state, mirroring classical observations of alpha desynchronization in visual–parietal networks that facilitate information processing under task demands.

Although global modulation of these states was statistically significant (Friedman tests, p < 0.05), conservative post hoc comparisons did not consistently survive Bonferroni correction, underscoring the preliminary nature of these findings and the need for larger cohorts to robustly delineate load-specific effects.

Future work should extend this framework using EEG source localization approaches (e.g., eLORETA or beamforming) to link sensor-level phase-locking patterns to their anatomical generators. Moreover, integrating simultaneously acquired physiological measures, such as heart rate variability or respiratory volume per time, would enable a multimodal assessment of cognitive effort. Relating transient brain states to autonomic arousal markers may provide a more comprehensive account of the neurophysiological costs associated with sustaining high-load working memory states.
