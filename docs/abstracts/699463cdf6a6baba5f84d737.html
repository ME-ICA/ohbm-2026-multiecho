Introduction

The medial temporal lobe (MTL) is a core region of the brain that supports important cognitive functions, and is selectively vulnerable to various age-related neurodegenerative diseases. The MTL is composed of cytoarchitectonically and functionally distinct subregions—including the hippocampus (HPC), entorhinal cortex (ERC), parahippocampal cortex (PHC), and perirhinal cortex (PRC), including Brodmann Area 35 and 36 (BA35/36)—each contributing uniquely to cognitive processing1. Therefore, fine-grained characterization of MTL subregional architecture has become essential for various studies. Cytoarchitectonic characterization of MTL subregions is the gold standard reference for imaging studies.

However, manually delineating MTL subregions from histological slides remains highly challenging. The lack of universally accepted definitions of subfield boundaries and the limited availability of experts trained to perform such annotations pose substantial obstacles2. Moreover, the annotation process is extremely labor-intensive, often requiring several hours for a neuroanatomist to annotate the MTL in even one hemisphere. The development of accurate automated methods for segmentation is therefore critical for advancing our ability to characterize the MTL cytoarchitectonically. Reliable delineation of MTL subregions can reveal region-specific vulnerability, improve mapping to broader brain networks, and substantially advance mechanistic and computational studies of MTL organization in neurodegenerative disease.

Methods

111 donors underwent postmortem MRI followed by 3D printing of individually-tailored MTL molds designed for sequential 0.5-mm sectioning, enhancing remapping of sections to MRI-space (Fig. 1A). Sections were processed with a Nissl staining series into histological slices and underwent 20x microscopy magnification for manual annotation by a single neuroanatomist. Neuroanatomists manually annotated boundaries between MTL subregions on histological sections. For 17 donors (1,734 sections), full regional masks were generated in postmortem MRI-space after coregistering histological boundaries to MRI space. These masks were then transformed back to the Nissl-stained histological sections (Fig. 1A). This dataset was divided into training (n=14 donors, 1,403 slices) and testing (n=3 donors, 331 slices) sets. Segmentation was performed using a nnU-Net framework, which adaptively configures preprocessing, network architecture, and training parameters to accommodate the heterogeneous size and morphology of histology data. Within this U-Net structure, a multi-task semantic segmentation framework was employed, by jointly predicting both regional groupings (e.g., HPC) and individual subregions (e.g. HPC subfield CA1) in parallel (Fig. 1A). Each class is treated as an independent binary classification task, enabling the model to accurately determine pixel-wise membership across all classes simultaneously.

We performed two experiments to gain insight into what information our UNet model used for regional segmentation: 1) Anatomists typically use both gross anatomical landmarks and fine cytoarchitectural details for region delineation. The high-resolution images allow for visualization of cytoarchitectonic details within MTL subregions (Fig. 1B), but using full-resolution data also imposes significant computational demands. To determine an optimal balance between anatomical fidelity and computational efficiency, we conducted a resolution evaluation (x4, x16 or x16 downsampling) on a subset of slices using Dice similarity coefficients (DSC) as the primary metric. 2) Second, we tested whether providing the model with additional structural priors would be useful. Specifically, we tested the model using just the region masks as ground truth, and then additionally training on both the masks and the neuroanatomist boundary annotations as inputs.  

Results

For experiment 1, a ×16 downsampling level offered the best compromise between segmentation performance and compute time (Fig. 1C), and was therefore selected for subsequent experiments. For experiment 2, we found that incorporating both the masks and the boundary annotations as input led to measurable improvements in segmentation performance (Fig. 2B), particularly for small subregions. Quantitative evaluation demonstrated generally high segmentation accuracy across most MTL regions (Fig. 2A). However, inconsistencies and biases in the ground-truth masks—arising for example from coregistration errors when moving the manual masks between the MRIs and histology slices—limited the interpretability of quantitative metrics alone. Consequently, visualization-based assessment was essential for a subjective evaluation of model behavior. Visualization inspection revealed that the model sometimes produced well-done subregional borders and sometimes created more coherent boundaries than those present in the manual labels, suggesting that it was able to correct for errors in the ground truth (Fig. 2D). The model tended to perform better (on all labels) in sections including hippocampus, and performance tended to be better in the anterior vs. the posterior MTL (Fig. 2C).

Conclusion

Our results demonstrate that automated segmentation of MTL subregions on histological data is both feasible and promising. Several notable findings emerged from this work.

  1. Overall segmentation performance is reasonably strong, indicating that the model is suitable for use in future experiments, especially when paired with appropriate post-processing strategies.

  2. Cortical extraction is highly accurate, with consistently clean and anatomically coherent delineations across samples.

  3. Remarkably, the model is able to refine boundary errors from the ground-truth, suggesting that the learned representation is robust to imperfect ground truth labels.

  4. The model appears to use both detailed cytoarchitectonic features and large anatomical landmarks for delineation, but the large landmarks appear to be more important.

Together, these findings highlight both the potential and the remaining challenges of automated MTL segmentation, and they set the stage for future methodological innovations that may further advance quantitative neuropathological research.
