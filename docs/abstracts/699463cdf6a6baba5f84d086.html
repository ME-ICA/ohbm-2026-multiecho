Introduction

Language is generally left lateralised in the brain, yet suprisingly some distinct groups, such as, the dyslexic population and the bilingual population, show a reduction in this left lateralisation, resulting into a more balanced pattern of activation. Despite this shared atypical brain organisation for language processing, the reading skills of these two groups strikingly diverge.  

On the one hand, children with dyslexia struggle acquiring reading, despite explicit instruction. They seem to recruit the right hemisphere (RH) in an attempt to compensate for a left hemisphere (LH) dysfunction, leading to a bilateral yet inefficiently connected system. On the other hand, children raised speaking two language, i.e. early bilinguals, have better reading outcomes.1 In fact, early bilingual experience is thought to modify connectivity patterns in the brain, promoting a better integrated system across hemispheres. Both cases would lead to an apparently similar bilateral activation that would translate into reduced lateralization.

However, in the case of bilingualism, we hypothesize that the underlying mechanisms resulting in bilateral activation would be different, stemming from stronger interhemispheric connectivity (IHC) through the corpus callosum (CC), positively affecting reading outcomes. CC is the largest white matter tract responsible for IHC, performing the task of recruitment and integration across the two hemispheres. We posit that the bilingual context is a complex task that increases IHC. This would result in the RH supporting the LH to boost processing in typical or to restore activation in reading difficulties such as in developmental dyslexia, where genetic risks of reading difficulties are present.2

Our main objective is to test this framework by evaluating whether (A) the positive association between early bilingualism and reading is mediated by the CC, and (B) the positive relationship with cognitive genetic scores and reading is mediated by CC metrics.

Methods

The participants are part of the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development study (ABCD®; https://abcdstudy.org/), a 10-year follow-up longitudinal study. It includes 11,886 children at baseline from ages 9 to 10 from the United States recruited between September 2016 and August 2018. To test our hypotheses, we selected those children from the original sample whose data was acquired for all the independent variables defined in our theoretical construct. The final sample size for hypothesis (A) was 9945 children and 5458 children for hypothesis (B), as genetic analyses were restricted to those of the largest homogeneous ancestry group in the dataset, i.e. the European.

Mediation models were estimated using continuous measures of our theoretical constructs: bilingualism indexes and polygenic scores (PGS) as predictors, CC properties as mediator, and reading aloud as outcome (Figure 1).

Genetic risk was operationalized through polygenic scores (PGS), which are individual-level variables, computed from a Genome-Wide Association Study, that represent a weighted-average of the effect of the variants or single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with a specific trait.3 We calculated for each participant of European ancestry the PGS for cognitive performance (CP),4 which explains 5-10% of the variance in reading performance in this dataset.1

For the bilingual index, we computed a weighted score from questionnaire items related to language use in demographic, acculturation, school attendance, and grades, and family environment information questionnaires. From the score sum we obtained a continuous score, the “bilingual continuum”, for the whole sample.

The mediators fitted in the model were measurements of the CC segmented volume of the structural MRI (sMRI: anterior and posterior CC volume, intracranial volume (ICV)) and the full CC volume from diffusion MRI (dMRI). These measures were obtained with 3T MRI scanners (General Electric 750, Philips, Siemens) across the 21 research centers affiliated to the ABCD®. The sMRI measures were defined by the “aseg” segmentation atlas in Free Surfer, and dMRI volume was calculated from the ROI CC defined with AtlasTrack and obtained running a linear estimation with log-transformed diffusion weighted signals. Reading was the outcome of the mediation models and was defined by the raw score of the participant in the Oral Reading Recognition Task®. In addition, we used the Vocabulary and WISC-V (non-verbal intelligence) scores as control outcome variables. We analyzed the sample at the baseline timepoint (mean age=9.9 years old), using R Studio®.

We first performed mixed effects models between the predictors (bilingualism measure or PGS) and the reading outcome and between the predictor and the mediators (CC measures) using the “lme4” package. All models included age, sex as covariates and acquisition site was included as a random effect. Next, we performed mediation mixed models were tested using the “mediation” package (1000 simulations to estimate SE), separately for each predictor and moderator.

For reading and for each of the two control outcomes (vocabulary and intelligence), a total of 20 mediation models were tested. Each mediation model included one of two predictors (PGS CP and bilingual continuum) and four mediators (ICV, dMRI full CC volume, sMRI anterior CC volume and sMRI posterior CC volume). Each CC mediator was tested three times: without ICV adjustment, and with two types of adjustments: ICV normalization (dividing the CC measure by ICV), and the including ICV as covariate. Bonferroni correction for multiple testing comparisons was applied per outcome (i.e. 0.05/20=0.0025).

Results

Across all the mediation models, both bilingualism and the polygenic score for cognitive performance (PGS CP) showed statistically significant direct (significant ADE) and total effects (TE) on reading performance (Figure 2), but only the PGS did for vocabulary and intelligence.

The bilingual continuum showed a consistent indirect effect on reading through the anterior CC volume, across every mediation model, regardless of ICV adjustment methods. Without ICV adjustment, the average causal mediation effect (ACME) was -0.00165 (95% CI =-0.00292; -0.00058; p < 0.0025), indirect pathway explaining about 3.4% of the total effect (Prop= -0.03421; CI = (-0.0829, -0.01119); p =< 0.0025). With ICV normalization or when ICV was entered as a covariate, the sign of the indirect effect reversed, yielding a positive ACME that remained statistically significant.

The mediation pathways lining the PGS to reading performance through the CC were strongly dependent on how ICV was handled. Without ICV adjustment, total CC volume acted as a significant positive mediator, and ICV itself also mediated the relationship, reflecting a global brain-size effect. Neither the anterior nor the posterior CC segment volumes contributed to mediation in this analysis. After controlling for ICV (covariate), the anterior CC became significant negative mediator (p=0.028), whereas the mediation via total CC lost significance. With ICV normalization, the indirect effect through the anterior CC grew larger (p=0.006). This shift indicates that that, once overall brain size is accounted for, the anterior segment of the CC uniquely captures positive genetic influences on reading. 

To test whether the mediation patterns observed for reading were unique, we repeated the same set of analyses using two alternative control outcomes: vocabulary and intelligence. These sensitivity analyses show that the bilingualism-reading association pattern through the CC is specific, whereas the PGS-reading relationship shares a global CC/ICV mediation with other cognitive domains, although it differs in that only reading shows a robust anterior-CC mediation after ICV control. This specificity supports the notion that bilingual experience uniquely engages anterior inter-hemispheric pathways to benefit reading, beyond the broader genetic influences on cognition.

Conclusion

The present study sought to disentangle how environmental factors, such as bilingualism, and genetics influence the brain to impact reading outcomes, with a particular focus on the CC as a proxy of interhemispheric communication.

Our findings reveal striking divergence in how environmental (bilingualism) versus genetic (PGS CP) factors influence reading through white matter structure. The bilingualism effect showed specificity: indirect effects through anterior CC were observed for reading but not for vocabulary or intelligence, suggesting that bilingual experience engages specific inter-hemispheric pathways that support orthographic and phonological processing rather than global cognitive enhancement. The anterior CC specificity for reading aligns with its known role in connecting language-relevant regions across hemispheres. Overall, these results suggest that bilingual experience may fine-tune inter-hemispheric integration specifically for processes demanding dual-language orthographic mapping and phonological access.5

In contrast, PGS CP effects were domain-general: mediation through total CC volume (pre-ICV adjustment) extended across all three cognitive outcomes, reflecting shared genetic architecture for brain size and general cognitive ability. The strong positive mediation of PGS CP effects through total dMRI CC volume on all three outcomes in unadjusted models disappeared after ICV adjustment and was equally strong for ICV as a mediator, indicating it was primarily attributable to global brain size rather than CC-specific white matter organization.

The choice of ICV adjustment fundamentally alters inferred mechanisms. For bilingualism, unadjusted models suggest potential suppression effects or neural efficiency, while adjusted models suggest proportionally enhanced connectivity. For PGS CP, adjustment attenuates effects, possibly due to over-controlling for genetically influenced brain size (collider bias). We do not prioritize one specification over another; rather, these patterns highlight that mediation through brain structure depends critically on assumptions about the causal role of overall brain size. Future work with genetically informed designs (e.g., sibling comparisons, Mendelian randomization) may better disambiguate these pathways and determine whether bilingualism truly reorganizes brain structure or whether observed associations reflect unmeasured genetic or environmental confounding.

Another factor to take into account was the difference in sample size across the analysis. The models containing genetic scores were limited to European ancestry participants in order to avoid population stratification confounds. It is thus arguable that the effects found only nominally significant in this population could potentially become more reliable with larger, more diverse samples.

In conclusion, our results support that the recurrent exposure to complex bilingual environments configures the brain toward a more balanced and bilaterally integrated organization, particularly via the anterior corpus callosum. These structural adaptations are graded along a bilingual continuum, specific to reading rather than domain-general cognition, and potentially compensatory, offering alternative pathways that may support more efficient reading development and buffer against developmental reading vulnerabilities. While causal questions remain, these findings challenge deficit-based views of bilingualism and position bilingual experience as a modifiable environmental factor with potential to enhance both brain structure and cognitive outcomes. Future research integrating longitudinal designs, genetically informed samples, and multimodal neuroimaging will be critical to establishing causal mechanisms and translating these insights into evidence-based educational practice.
