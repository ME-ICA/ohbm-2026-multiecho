Introduction

Premature epigenetic aging has been associated with altered cognition, but the underlying changes in brain structure and function are not very well understood. Our previous research showed that altered brain dynamic was associated with accelerated epigenetic aging and worse cognitive outcomes in young adult women. Here, we aim to extend these findings to brain structure and test whether the relationship between premature epigenetic aging and worse cognitive outcomes in young adulthood might be mediated by deviations from normative brain development.

Methods

We performed a neuroimaging - epigenetic follow-up of the European Longitudinal Study of Pregnancy and Childhood (ELSPAC) prenatal birth cohort in young adulthood and tested the effect of premature epigenetic aging on deviations from normative brain development and cognitive outcomes. A total of 257 young adults (50% men, 28-30 years old, European ancestry) provided a buccal swab to assess DNA methylation, took part in structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain using 3T Siemens Prisma, and cognitive assessment using WAIS-IV to calculate their full-scale, performance, and verbal IQ. DNA methylation was assessed using Illumina EPIC chip, and epigenetic aging was calculated using the Horvath’s epigenetic clock1, and another two epigenetic clocks developed to reflect not only aging as such but also disease and mortality – the AltumAge2 and CheekAge3. DNA methylation age was residualized for chronological age, proportion of epithelial cells, and batch, and saved as the epigenetic age gap estimates (EpiAGE). T1-weighted data were pre-processed in FreeSurfer v7.1.1, which provided subcortical volumes for the 14 Aseg atlas regions and cortical thickness and surface area for the 68 Desikan-Killiany regions. CentileBrain normative models4, trained on 37407 MRI scans from individuals aged 3-90 years, were used to quantify deviations from age- and sex-specific normative brain development. Associations between EpiAGE, deviations from normative brain development, and cognitive function were tested using linear regression, and the FDR method was used to correct for multiple comparisons in each family of analyses. Finally, moderated mediation tested the mediatory role of altered brain development in the relationship between epigenetic aging and cognitive performance, and whether this indirect effect might be moderated by sex.

Results

Premature epigenetic aging calculated using the Horvath’s epigenetic clock was associated with greater negative deviations from age- and sex-specific normative development of putamen (left: R2=0.06, FDRp=0.01, right: R2=0.03, FDRp=0.04) and caudate (left: R2=0.06, FDRp=0.01, right: R2=0.06, FDRp=0.01), but no other deviations from normative development of subcortical volumes (p>0.16, FDRp=0.41), cortical thickness in the 68 regions (p>0.04; FDRp>0.91), or surface area in the 68 regions (p>0.03, FDRp>0.92). In women (but not men), premature epigenetic aging calculated using Horvath’s epigenetic clock was also associated with lower full-scale IQ (interaction with sex: p=0.02, women: R2=0.04) and particularly performance IQ (interaction with sex: p=0.02, women: R2=0.07). Further analyses revealed women-specific relationships between greater negative deviations from normative development of putamen and lower full-scale IQ (left: interaction p=0.01, women: R2=0.07; right: interaction p=0.03, women: R2=0.05), and particularly lower performance IQ (left: interaction p=0.02, women: R2=0.07; right: interaction p=0.04, women: R2=0.05). Moreover, in women, the deviations from the normative development of putamen volume mediated the relationship between Horvath’s EpiAGE and full-scale IQ (Figure 1; left: ab=-0.22, SE=0.12, 95% CI [-0.49; -0.02]), right: ab=-0.13, SE=0.09, 95% CI [-0.33; -0.003]). The deviations from age- and sex- specific normative development of the caudate did not mediate the relationship. No similar results were found using the two epigenetic clocks developed to reflect not only aging as such but also disease and mortality (CheekAGE, AltumAGE).

Conclusion

We conclude that premature epigenetic aging in young adulthood calculated using Hrovath’s epigenetic clock is associated with altered normative development of the dorsal striatum and that in women, the deviations from normative development of the putamen mediate the relationship between premature epigenetic aging and worse cognition. These findings substantially extend our previous research in the same cohort about the role of brain dynamics in epigenetic aging and cognition to brain structure, and specifically the dorsal striatum. Moreover, given the fact that dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra release dopamine to the dorsal striatum, our findings fit with previous PET research on age-related dopaminergic decline in the dorsal striatum, as well as further research on age-related dopamine loss and cognitive deficits5. Overall, these findings suggest that the dorsal striatum and its function, including cognitive flexibility, goal-directed behavior, planning, working memory, attention, and learning, might be potential targets to prevent premature epigenetic aging and cognitive decline in women.
