Introduction

Classification of Electroencephalographic (EEG) data is a key component of modern Brain Computer Interfacing (BCI), allowing for the rapid mapping of brain activity to an external device. One growing area of EEG classification is through Riemannian Geometry, which can more accurately represent the non-linearity of EEG data.^1 These methods first convert the EEG signal into covariance matrices on which Riemannian distances can be calculated. Whilst these methods have shown strong potential, the conversion to pure covariance matrices means that any information stored within the temporal domain is lost whilst inter-channel data is emphasised. We believe that this information is key for accurate BCI classification, and thus allowing the classifier to capture and utilise this domain is vital.

We propose a time-series focused extension to standard Riemannian classification by first splitting the data into smaller intervals, applying a Riemannian MDM classifier to each, then weighting these intervals based on a separability measure using convexity. We evaluate our method against both current Riemannian methods and some traditional BCI classification methods on a large motor imagery based dataset, broken up into four different classification problems, and find our approach leads to up to a 15% increase in accuracy.

Methods

Riemannian geometry based methods work by first mapping the group of EEG signals to a set of symmetric positive definite (SPD) covariance matrices. This set of SPD matrices creates a manifold, or curved surface on which Riemannian geometry can be applied. Most commonly, this involves calculating distances between the points on the manifold, allowing for more accurate distances than traditional methods such as Euclidean. Often this involves calculating distances relative to some other point on the manifold, such as the geometric mean. Riemannian based methods also allow for easy transfer learning, as the mean can be quickly recalculated to include the new data.

We adapt the traditional Riemannian approach of Minimum Distance to Mean (MDM).^2 Here, a prototype mean is calculated for each class, then the Riemannian Distance for a given instance is calculated to those means. Prediction is then made based on which class the instance is closest to. We adapt this by first splitting the data into smaller intervals, running an MDM classifier for each, and then ensemble the final predictions.

As not all recordings may have precise and even timings, there is often some level of offset between different sessions. Therefore we allow for a level of warping in the temporal domain by calculating an intervals distance not just to the mean prototype for that interval, but to one in either direction as well, and selecting the smallest total distance.

As not all intervals contain useful information for classification, treating each equally would lead to degraded results. Therefore we introduce a weighting mechanic for each interval based on the concept of convexity in sets. Convexity is the idea that a set is convex if all points in the shortest path between any two points in the set are also within the set, and has been used previously as part of a neural network.^3 We instead implement it as a method of calculating how effective each interval can split the classes by treating the instances belonging to each class as a separate set. To calculate convexity, we calculate distances between all instances, then test whether the shortest paths between each point in each class crosses into other class sets. A final convexity score for each interval is calculated based on the average of all classes. Intervals which have higher convexity scores indicate more effective splitting of classes, and thus will lead to higher classification accuracy. We give these intervals more weight in the ensemble by allowing them to have more votes for prediction.

Results

For our experiments we select a large and popular motor imagery based dataset that contains 4 different classification tasks.^4^5 The first two tasks involve the participants opening and then closing either their left of right hand, first with real movements and then with imagined movements. The last two involved opening and closing either their hands or feet, again with both real and imagined movements. 109 participants took part in the data collection, each completing 3 two minute runs of each task. The data was recorded at 160Hz with 64 EEG channels. We remove 5 participants due to detected irregularities in their data, leaving 104 participants. Of particular interest to us is the classification of raw, unprocessed data and so the only other processing that occurred was to extract 12 second windows for each instance of each class. We decided on a window size larger than what is usually found for BCI studies as we were interested in our classifiers ability to extract the useful parts of a longer signal rather than pre-segmented data.

We compare our created method with 5 other Riemannian based approaches. Two based on MDM, one using a K-Nearest-Neighbour approach, a support vector machine approach and a method which maps the data back onto a flat plane at a certain tangent. We also compare to three approaches commonly used for BCI based problems, Support Vector Machines (SVM), Logistic Regression and Random Forest.

For each experiment we split the data into two sections, the first to train the model and the second to evaluate performance. We repeat this process 5 times for each classifier tested and average results to ensure that there is no bias within any given split. We also ensure that data from no participant is found in both the training and testing partitions to avoid contamination from different recording sessions. Our experiments were performed in Python using the Aeon and PyRiemann toolkits.

Our results show that our approach leads to a significant increase in accuracy over other Riemannian based approaches, tested using a pariwise Wilcoxon signed ranked test with p<0.05. This indicates that our design can extract the relevant sections of the signal which other Riemannian methods cannot due to removing the temporal domain. This is supported by the increase in accuracy seen when we introduce convexity as a weighting mechanic, further boosting the classifiers performance. Support Vector machines were the only other classifier to perform similarly, but with an approximate three times increase in computation time compared to our Riemannian approach.

Conclusion

We have demonstrated that by a simple adaption of weighting intervalling, we can improve Riemannian based BCI classification accuracy significantly, clearly outperforming all other existing Riemannian approaches by up to 15%. This shows that the temporal domain contains valuable information which is lost in existing Riemannian methods. We also demonstrate the effectiveness of convexity as a weighting scheme for classification, which we believe could have uses as a weighting mechanic outside of just this classifier. One area of further work would be on improving the computation speed of the classifier either through further algorithmic optimisation, or compiling into a more efficient language such as C. This would be needed as we believe our classifiers intervalling design would make it ideal for real time BCI classification.
