Introduction

Frontotemporal dementia (FTD) is a group of clinically, pathologically, and genetically heterogeneous neurodegenerative disorders associated with frontal and temporal atrophy. It is the second most common cause of early-onset dementia after Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and typically appears between ages 50 and 65. This study focuses on the three main FTD phenotypes: behavioral variant FTD (bvFTD), semantic variant of primary progressive aphasia (svPPA), and nonfluent variant of primary progressive aphasia (nfvPPA). Despite improved clinical criteria, FTD remains challenging to diagnose, especially in early stages, when symptoms may mimic psychiatric disorders or atypical AD, highlighting the need for objective biomarkers.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has emerged as a powerful tool for detecting structural and functional alterations associated with neurological disorders, including FTD. Diffusion MRI (dMRI), particularly diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), has shown strong potential for detecting variant-specific white-matter degeneration. Studies report that FTD typically affects anterior pathways, such as frontal tracts and the uncinate fasciculus, while each variant shows distinct white-matter patterns aligned with its cortical involvement. These findings support DTI as a promising tool for differential diagnosis between FTD phenotypes1,2.

Fractional anisotropy (FA) and mean diffusivity (MD) are the principal diffusion tensor metrics used to characterize white-matter microstructure. FA describes the directional selectivity of the random diffusion of water molecules, while MD reflects the overall magnitude of diffusion regardless of direction. Alterations in these measures can reveal atrophy in white-matter pathways, making them useful indicators in neurodegenerative conditions such as FTD.

This study aims to 1) characterize the FA and MD patterns in FTD, 2) to study explainable machine learning algorithms to differentiate FTD phenotypes.

Methods

Cohort

The dMRI data used in this study were obtained from the Frontotemporal Lobar Degeneration Neuroimaging Initiative (FTLDNI, htpps://memory.ucsf.edu/research-trials/research/allftd) via the Image and Data Archive (IDA, https://ida.loni.usc.edu/login.jsp). FTLDNI is a multicentric longitudinal project including diffusion, structural, functional MRI and PET images acquired across 1–7 visits at three sites: University of California San Francisco (UCSF), Mayo Clinic, and Massachusetts General Hospital.

From the original 4269 DTI scans from 337 participants, we built a homogeneous subset. Only UCSF scans, representing most of the dataset, were retained to reduce inter-site variability. To ensure protocol consistency, only dMRI scans with 64 gradient directions were included, a criterion met by 64.2% of the images. Moreover, from all the FTD participants, only the ones with variants bvFTD, svPPA or nfvPPA have been kept. The study was cross-sectional, so we only kept the first visit of each participant. Finally, as inclusion criteria, we established that healthy controls (CTR) must have CDR=0, and FTD individuals must have CDR>0.

Image Acquisition and Data Processing

The diffusion-weighted images used in this study were all acquired at UCSF with 64 diffusion-weighted directions (b = 2000 s/mm²) to ensure data homogeneity; however, other acquisition parameters varied slightly across scans. Selected images had voxel sizes of 2.2 × 2.2 × 2.2 mm³, repetition times (TR) ranging from 6.6 to 8.7 s, echo times (TE) from 86 to 115 ms.

After downloading the data from the IDA platform, all images were reorganized to follow the BIDS3 specification. Then, these dMRI data were preprocessed using FSL, including motion and eddy-current correction, b0 extraction, and brain extraction (HD-BET4). FA and MD were estimated with dtifit. Diffusion maps were registered to MNI152 space via nonlinear transforms and combined with the JHU ICBM-DTI-81 White-Matter Atlas (1 mm) to extract mean FA and MD values per region. A group skeleton mask was created using the Tract Based Spatial Statistics (TBSS)5 tool in FSL.

Statistical Analyses

All the analyses were conducted using the regional mean FA and MD values extracted from each subject and each region. Group differences between clinical groups were assessed using ANCOVA, with age and sex included as covariates. We focused first on comparisons between CTR and FTD. Then, we examined differences between CTR and each FTD variant separately: bvFTD, svPPA, and nfvPPA. These analyses were conducted in R version 4.4.2, with the Bonferroni correction applied across all regions.

To replicate the results, voxel-wise statistics were computed through randomise command, testing clinical group differences along the white-matter skeleton, with 5000 iterations. Resulting images were thresholded at corrected p level of 0.05.

Machine Learning Model

To further investigate the potential of FA and MD to discriminate across groups, we implemented a Random Forest classifier, combined with SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values to provide model explainability and identify the regions most contributing to classification. We trained several models: one distinguishing CTR from individuals with FTD; another classifying the three main FTD variants (bvFTD, svPPA, and nfvPPA); and additionally pairwise models comparing each FTD variant. We tested 3 different feature sets: all FA values+age+sex, all MD values+age+sex, and all FA+MD values+age+sex.

Model performance was evaluated using 5-fold cross-validation stratified to ensure robust and reliable estimates. For each model, we obtained the confusion matrix and the resulting accuracy, sensitivity and specificity metrics. SHAP values were then analyzed to determine the most informative regions and to evaluate whether the classifier-driven markers aligned with those found in the statistical analyses.

Results

The resulting dataset includes 175 participants: 64 healthy controls (CTR) (mean age 65.75±5.7, 51.56% females) and 111 FTD (mean age 63.76±7.57, 42.34% females): 46 bvFTD (mean age 60.89±6.84, 34.78% females), 34 svPPA (mean age 63.12±6.34, 41.18% females), and 31 nfvPPA (mean age 68.61±7.65, 54.84% females).

Group differences in mean FA values across brain regions reveal statistically significant differences between the CTR and all FTD groups in 32 of 39 regions (Bonferroni-adjusted threshold: p < 1.28×10⁻³). In all these regions, CTR showed higher FA than FTD. Mean MD values showed significant differences in all 39 regions, with MD values of FTD patients being always higher than CTR. When examining CTR versus each FTD variant separately, MD consistently showed larger and more widespread group differences than FA. Across comparisons, the CTR-bvFTD contrast exhibited the strongest and most extensive effects, whereas the CTR-nfvPPA comparison showed the smallest number of significant differences. Figure 1 presents the results of this analysis, highlighting brain regions with significant differences.

The voxel-wise comparisons revealed a widespread pattern of reduced FA and increased MD (corrected p>0.05) covering a large part of the skeleton for CTR vs FTD and showing slightly different patterns for the three variants separately, in accordance with the region-wise statistical results.

For the Random Forest model classifying CTR vs FTD, the following performance values were obtained:

- FA: accuracy = 80.00% ± 4.78%, sensitivity = 89.19%, specificity = 64.06%

- MD: accuracy = 89.71% ± 2.91%, sensitivity = 92.79%, specificity = 84.38%

- FA + MD: accuracy = 90.29% ± 2.91%, sensitivity = 93.69%, specificity = 84.38%.

In the multiclass model distinguishing the three FTD variants (bvFTD vs svPPA vs nfvPPA), the obtained accuracies were:

- FA: 71.26% ± 7.41%

- MD: 75.77% ± 12.77%

- FA + MD: 75.73% ± 7.69%

Inspection of the confusion matrices revealed that most misclassifications involved svPPA and nfvPPA being incorrectly classified as bvFTD. To further examine these patterns, pairwise classifiers were trained:

- bvFTD vs nfvPPA (bvFTD as the positive class)

              - FA: accuracy = 74.08% ± 6.66%, sensitivity = 84.78%, specificity = 58.06%

- MD: accuracy = 76.58% ± 10.87%, sensitivity = 84.78%, specificity = 64.52%

- FA + MD: accuracy = 74.00% ± 9.35%, sensitivity = 82.61%, specificity = 61.29%

- bvFTD vs svPPA (bvFTD as the positive class)

- FA: accuracy = 83.75% ± 5.00%, sensitivity = 89.13%, specificity = 76.47%

- MD: accuracy = 91.25% ± 5.00%, sensitivity = 97.83%, specificity = 82.35%

              - FA + MD: accuracy = 86.25% ± 6.12%, sensitivity = 93.48%, specificity = 76.47%

- nfvPPA vs svPPA (nfvPPA as the positive class)

- FA: accuracy = 87.69% ± 6.15%, sensitivity = 83.87%, specificity = 91.18%

- MD: accuracy = 86.15% ± 10.20%, sensitivity = 80.65%, specificity = 91.18%

- FA + MD: accuracy = 87.69% ± 9.23%, sensitivity = 87.10%, specificity = 88.24%

In general, it was observed that in FA + MD (+ age + sex) models, MD features contributed more strongly to the model’s decisions than FA features.

Conclusion

This study provides robust group differences in diffusion MRI metrics, with FTD participants showing widespread reductions in FA and increases in MD relative to CTR. These microstructural abnormalities were observed across nearly all assessed regions, aligning with established evidence of anterior-predominant white-matter vulnerability in FTD.

Beyond the global comparison between CTR and FTD participants, we further examined differences between CTR and each of the three main clinical variants: bvFTD, svPPA, and nfvPPA. Individual contrasts between each subtype and the control group revealed distinct patterns of white-matter disruption, capturing the heterogeneity that typifies the FTD spectrum. These results reinforce the notion that modeling white-matter heterogeneity may help disentangle the anatomical differences underlying clinical FTD variants.

Classification analyses complemented these findings by assessing the discriminative value of diffusion metrics. MD consistently outperformed FA across all models, indicating that global diffusivity alterations provide a more reliable signal of pathology. Combining both metrics yielded only marginal improvements over MD alone, suggesting that MD carries most of the relevant discriminative information. While distinguishing among FTD variants proved more challenging, an expected outcome given their anatomical and clinical overlap, pairwise classifications between svPPA and nfvPPA achieved relatively high accuracy. Misclassifications were largely driven by svPPA and nfvPPA cases being labeled as bvFTD, reflecting the broader and more variable anatomical involvement characteristic of the behavioral variant.

We incorporated SHAP-based explainability, identifying the regions that contributed most strongly to classification. Notably, the features with the highest SHAP values closely matched those showing significant group differences in the statistical analyses, supporting the classifier’s decisions. The predominance of MD features in the combined models further underscores its sensitivity to microstructural degeneration in FTD.

Overall, this study provides convergent evidence that diffusion MRI, and particularly MD, captures meaningful white-matter alterations associated with FTD and its clinical variants. Although classification of subtypes remains challenging, integrating traditional statistical analyses with explainable machine-learning methods constitutes a promising framework for developing more refined, subtype-specific neuroimaging biomarkers. Future work should expand these analyses to larger samples, incorporate multimodal imaging, and examine longitudinal trajectories to characterize disease progression better and improve early diagnostic precision.
