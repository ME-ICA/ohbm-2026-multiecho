Introduction

Parental mental health is a key factor influencing the mental health development of children and adolescents.1 However, existing research has long been heavily focused on maternal influences while neglecting paternal impacts. Paternal psychological distress exerts an independent and persistent predictive effect on offspring’s problem behaviors.2 Therefore, it is imperative to adopt a biparental perspective that simultaneously incorporates and differentiates the respective influences of both parents.

Most existing longitudinal studies only focus on relatively short developmental periods or adopt long intervals between measurements, leading to insufficient capacity to capture the detailed dynamics of association changes. Models such as Latent Growth Curve Models and Autoregressive Models tend to estimate an averaged, stable correlation coefficient or presuppose a linear pattern of association changes, thereby masking potential non-linear fluctuations and critical peaks of the relationships at specific ages.

To address these limitations, the present study introduces the Time-Varying Effect Model (TVEM).3 This model enables the regression coefficients between parental anxiety/depression and offspring problems to be estimated as continuous functions of time, thus dynamically depicting the trajectory of association strength across ages and precisely identifying critical developmental time points where the influence reaches peaks or undergoes turning points.

To this end, the present study will dynamically characterize the continuous age-varying trajectories of the association strength between paternal and maternal anxiety/depression diagnosis history and emotional and behavioral problems in children and adolescents, so as to identify critical developmental time points. It will also dynamically examine the moderating role of child gender in the aforementioned associations, to determine whether gender differences exist in specific age groups or specific problem domains.

Methods

2.1 Participants

Participants were recruited from the Millennium Cohort Study. The present study utilized data collected at the ages of 3, 5, 7, 11, 14, and 17 years. The baseline sample size was 12,555.

2.2 Measures

2.2.1 Emotional and Behavioral Problems in Children and Adolescents

The Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) covers five domains: prosocial behavior, peer problems, emotional problems, conduct problems, and ADHD symptoms. The peer problems and emotional problems subscales can be combined to form a broader internalizing problems domain, while the conduct problems and ADHD symptoms subscales constitute a broader externalizing problems domain.

2.2.2 Parental Anxiety and Depression

Parental history of diagnosed major depression and/or severe anxiety was assessed using a single-item screening question.

2.2.3 Other Variables

The present study collected the following key demographic variables and covariates:

Parental age

Treatment history: Assessed via the single-item question: "Have you ever received treatment for anxiety or depression?"

Partner relationship satisfaction: Assessed via the single-item question: "How happy are you with your relationship with your partner?"

Highest parental educational attainment: Categorized according to the levels of the UK National Vocational Qualification (NVQ).

Parental ethnicity: Classified based on the 6-category standard of the UK Census.

Biological parent-child relationship: Used to distinguish whether the respondent is the child’s biological parent.

2.3 Statistical Analysis

Data analyses were conducted using the Time-Varying Effect Model (TVEM) with the tvem package (Version 1.4.1) in R software. Time-varying coefficients and their confidence intervals were visualized to intuitively reveal the dynamic trajectory of variable associations over time.

Results

3.1 Age-Varying Associations Between Parental Anxiety/Depression and Children’s Emotional and Behavioral Problems

The positive association between paternal anxiety/depression and children’s externalizing problems was only significant from 2 to 13 years of age, and the strength of this association consistently decreased with age. The positive association between paternal anxiety/depression and children’s ADHD remained significant across the entire age range of 2 to 18 years, with the association strength staying stable over time. The association between paternal anxiety/depression and children’s conduct problems was only significant from 3 to 13 years of age, and the strength of this association consistently decreased with age.

The associations between paternal anxiety/depression and children’s internalizing problems, emotional problems, as well as peer problems were all significant from 2 to 18 years of age, with these association strengths remaining stable over time.

The associations between maternal anxiety/depression and children’s externalizing problems, ADHD, as well as conduct problems were all significant across the entire age range of 2 to 18 years. The associations with externalizing problems and ADHD stayed stable over time, while the association with conduct problems consistently decreased with age.

The associations between maternal anxiety/depression and children’s internalizing problems as well as emotional problems were both significant from 2 to 18 years of age, whereas the association between maternal anxiety/depression and children’s peer problems was only significant from 2 to 17 years of age. Specifically, the association between maternal anxiety/depression and children’s internalizing problems increased with age from 2 to 14 years, peaked at 14 years of age, and then declined thereafter. The association between maternal anxiety/depression and children’s emotional problems increased with age from 2 to 15 years, peaked at 15 years of age, and then declined thereafter. The association between maternal anxiety/depression and children’s peer problems increased with age from 2 to 13 years, peaked at 13 years of age, and then declined thereafter.

3.2 Moderating Role of Child Gender in the Age-Varying Associations Between Parental Anxiety/Depression and Children’s Emotional and Behavioral Problems

The moderating role of child gender in the associations between paternal anxiety/depression and children’s externalizing problems as well as internalizing problems was non-significant across all ages.

The moderating role of child gender in the associations between maternal anxiety/depression and children’s externalizing problems as well as conduct problems was significant from 2 to 18 years of age. By contrast, the moderating role of child gender in the associations between maternal anxiety/depression and children’s ADHD, internalizing problems, emotional problems, as well as peer problems was non-significant across all ages.

Further analyses revealed that the strength of the association between maternal anxiety/depression and boys’ externalizing problems was significantly greater than that with girls, and this gender difference remained stable across all ages. The strength of the association between maternal anxiety/depression and boys’ conduct problems was significantly greater than that with girls; this gender difference was substantial at most ages but showed a narrowing trend during 13 to 15 years of age.

Conclusion

By applying the Time-Varying Effect Model, the present study systematically revealed the dynamic trajectories of parental anxiety/depression impacts on offspring emotional and behavioral problems and the moderating role of child gender.

Analyses of dynamic trajectories demonstrated that parental anxiety/depression exhibited persistent and stable associations with offspring internalizing problems (emotional and peer problems), indicating that parental psychological distress constitutes a chronic background risk factor. In contrast, parental influences on externalizing problems showed divergent patterns: paternal influences on externalizing problems (especially conduct problems) weakened to non-significance during adolescence (after approximately age 14 years); while maternal influences remained significant throughout the period, the association strength with conduct problems also showed a decreasing trend. Notably, the association between ADHD symptoms and parental anxiety/depression (particularly paternal anxiety/depression) remained stable across all ages, suggesting that the neurodevelopmental basis of ADHD is more sensitive to early-life risks. In-depth analyses further found that the associations between maternal anxiety/depression and children’s emotional and peer problems peaked in early adolescence (ages 13–15 years), which coincides with key developmental tasks of this period; no such peak was observed for paternal influences.

Differences between paternal and maternal influences were evident: paternal influences followed an "age-related attenuation" pattern, with a marked decline in the impact on externalizing behaviors during adolescence; whereas maternal influences exhibited greater "breadth" and "critical period sensitivity"—they covered all problem domains consistently over time and formed risk peaks for internalizing problems during adolescence. These differences may be related to traditional family role divisions and parent-child interaction patterns: as primary caregivers, mothers’ mental health has a more extensive and dynamic impact on children’s daily management and emotional development; paternal influences, in contrast, may operate more through early-established interaction patterns or genetic pathways, and weaken as children gain greater independence.

The moderating role of child gender was specific: it only moderated maternal influences. The association strengths between maternal anxiety/depression and boys’ externalizing and conduct problems were significantly greater than those for girls. This finding aligns with the "gender intensification" hypothesis, which posits that mothers may be more likely to become trapped in coercive interaction cycles with sons, or unconsciously reinforce the expression of externalizing behaviors in boys, thereby amplifying associated risks.

The findings of this study have clear practical implications, suggesting that interventions should be based on a three-dimensional precision framework:

Developmentally sensitive: Prioritize prevention of paternal-related externalizing risks during childhood, and focus on maternal-related emotional risks during early adolescence.

Parentally differentiated: Distinguish intervention priorities for fathers and mothers.

Gender-sensitive: Implement targeted interventions for mother-son interaction patterns.

The study has several limitations. First, parental mental health was assessed via retrospective self-report; future research should incorporate clinical evaluations. Second, the findings are inherently correlational, and causal interpretations must be made with caution. Third, the study did not explore specific mediating mechanisms (e.g., parenting styles). Future research can integrate multi-time-point diagnostic data, causal inference designs, and mechanism exploration to deepen understanding of transgenerational risk transmission.
