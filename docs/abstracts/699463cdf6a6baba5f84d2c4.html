Introduction

When a 70-year-old person moves their fingers to press a button, their brain works harder as compared to that of a 25-year-old doing exactly the same task. Instead of a compact motor network, older adults recruit broader brain regions1. This recruitment of additional brain areas in older adults (OA) as compared to young adults (YA) is referred to as neural hyperactivation. Some researchers see this as a compensation strategy: additional regions step in to compensate for age-related damage and help preserve the same level of motor performance2. Those studies report positive correlation between the extent of activation and motor performance. While studies finding no such correlations have concluded it reflects “neural noise”, as once-specialized networks lose their boundaries, leading to an inefficient spill-over of neural activity3. So far, these conclusions are almost entirely based on correlations between BOLD signal and behavior, leading to contradictory results and interpretations. A promising way to resolve this debate is to directly test what happens in the hyperactive region on a molecular level. Changes in glutamate (the main excitatory neurotransmitter) and gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) (the main inhibitory neurotransmitter) levels might contribute to the extent of hyperactivation in OA. Understanding how GABA and glutamate levels relate to hyperactivation in OA, and how these neurometabolic changes are associated with motor performance will provide an answer to the source and function of hyperactivation. In this pilot study, we explored how stimulation-induced neurometabolic changes relate to hyperactivation and motor performance.

Methods

1. Participants 

We recruited young (YA, 18–35 years) and older adults (OA, 60–80 years). The exclusion criteria were MRI/tDCS contraindications, systematic, neurological or psychiatric disease, use of psychoactive medication, MoCA ≤ 25, LQ < 40. 

2. Study design

The study consisted of 3 sessions (see Figure 1A). The goal of the 1st session was to identify age-related hyperactivation (OA > YA) and to define individualized HD-ctDCS targets. After that, only the OA returned for 2 stimulation sessions. HD-ctDCS was applied to reduce the activity in hyperactive regions. Pre- and post-stimulation, the neurometabolite levels at rest and BOLD signal during the task performance were recorded. Thus, session 1 contrasted YA vs OA to locate hyperactivation. Sessions 2 and 3 contrasted OA within-subject across condition (active vs. sham) and time (pre vs. post) for neurometabolite levels, brain activity, and motor outcomes.

3. Motor task

Participants performed an auditory-cued alternating finger-tapping task (alternating index–middle, tempo: 3.5 Hz) in the scanner: 10 cycles of 30 s TAP / 30 s REST (see Figure 1C). Taps were recorded via an MRI-compatible response box.

4. Stimulation protocol

Individualized HD-ctDCS (Soterix MXN33 system) was optimized per participant using the SIMNIBS toolbox based on T1/T2 anatomy and including information from task-fMRI activation to target hyperactive regions. Active stimulation consisted of ramping up, followed by sustained stimulation, and a ramp down; sham consisted of brief ramps only (see Figure 1B).

5. MR Data acquisition

T1- and T2-weighted anatomical scans and whole-brain task fMRI (gradient-echo EPI, 3 mm isotropic voxels) were acquired on a 3T MR scanner. MEGA-PRESS edited MRS data (TR/TE: 2000/68 ms) were acquired to obtain GABA+ (GABA + co-edited macromolecules) and combined glutamate-glutamine (Glx) levels for bilateral secondary somatosensory cortex (S2) at rest (voxel: 18.75 ml, 25*30*25 mm3; 160 ON/160 OFF spectra). The voxel was centered at the peak of the group-level S2 hyperactivation (see Figure 1D) and adjusted to avoid inclusion of skull and large CSF spaces. 

6. Data processing

fMRI

The data were preprocessed using fMRIPrep, followed by 5 mm FWHM smoothing in SPM12. First-level GLMs modeled TAP vs REST blocks; individual contrast maps (TAP > REST) were entered into group-level analyses. Hyperactive regions were defined as OA > YA (p < 0.001 uncorrected). Stimulation induced hypoactive regions were defined as Pre-stimulation > Post-stimulation and contrasted with Pre-sham > Post-sham. 

MRS

MRS data were processed with the Gannet (v3.3.2; default settings). Data were excluded from analysis if residual fit error > 12%; linewidths for water ≤ 9.4 Hz; linewidths for creatine ≤ 9.1 Hz and if artifacts (lipid contamination, residual unsuppressed water signal) that influences metabolite quantification were detected upon visual inspection  (number of exclusions=0). For MRS outcomes (GABA+ and Glx in bilateral S2), we compared active versus sham HD-ctDCS using paired t-tests on pre–post change scores.

Behavior data

Tapping performance was evaluated using reaction times (RT), sound-based and sequence-based accuracy. Paired t-tests were used to compare behavior between conditions (stimulationpre-post versus shampre-post). Speed–accuracy balance ratio per participant was computed by z‑scoring and averaging metrics, and taking their ratio (speed ÷ accuracy).

General statistics

We used general linear models (GLMs) to examine how individual differences in neurometabolite levels relate to motor performance and fMRI activation. Specifically, we fitted multiple regression models with behavioral outcomes and ROI-based fMRI metrics (peak activation and extent of activation) as dependent variables, and Glx/GABA+ (and derived excitation–inhibition balance indices) as predictors, allowing us to quantify how much variance in behavior and activation could be explained by the MRS measures across participants.

Results

We recruited 23 right-handed participants: 12 YA (22.7±1.8 years; 6 females) and 11 OA (73.6±3.5 years; 4 females).

1. fMRI

fMRI identifies S2 as an area of hyperactivation in OA (see Figure2A).

As compared to YA, OA showed robust task-related hyperactivation in bilateral S2. These bilateral S2 clusters were used as the primary targets for individualized HD-ctDCS. Active HD-ctDCS, compared with sham, induced hypoactivation in the left parietal region of OA.

2. MRS 

MRS results point towards a lower excitatory-inhibitory balance following tDCS (see Figure2C).

n the left S2, GABA+ increased following active stimulation (mean change = +0.12 ± 0.56 institutional units [iu]) and decreased with sham (−0.35 ± 0.78 iu), yielding a significant differential effect (+0.48 iu, p = 0.017). In right S2, Glx decreased with active stimulation (−0.58 ± 1.24 iu) relative to sham (−0.02 ± 1.13 iu), resulting in a nonsignificant differential effect (−0.55 iu, p = 0.15). Across both S2 regions, active stimulation showed a consistent tendency toward lower excitation–inhibition balance (Glx/GABA+), driven by these distinct neurometabolite changes. 

Baseline metabolite levels moderated stimulation effects: participants with low baseline Glx showed limited capacity for further reduction (floor effect), while those with high baseline GABA+ showed limited capacity for increase (ceiling effect).

Power analyses based on these effect sizes suggested that 61 older adults would be needed to replicate these findings in a larger study with 80% power.

3. Behavioral data

OA performance is more accuracy-oriented as compared to YA and wasn’t changed by tDCS (see Figure2B).

Compared to YA, OA showed slower reaction times (119ms vs 103ms) and less anticipatory responding (8% vs 47%) but higher accuracy (52% vs 40% correct), reflecting a speed-accuracy tradeoff biased toward accuracy. HD-ctDCS had no effect on behavior. Power analyses indicated that 50–69 participants would be needed to replicate this finding with 80–95% power.    

4. GLMs

Neurometabolite levels explain 10–15% of variance in fMRI activation patterns and behavioral outcomes.

For behavioral outcomes, higher baseline GABA+ in right S2 was associated with faster RTs (β = -35.49, p = 0.072) and showed a trend toward better sound accuracy (β = −0.061, p = 0.056). 

For fMRI–MRS relationships, stimulation-induced changes in the left hemisphere S2 activation were correlated with changes in E/I balance (r = 0.862, p = 0.006) and Glx (r = 0.725, p = 0.042), suggesting that greater increase in glutamate was associated with larger activation extent. In the right hemisphere, peak T-values in S2 were negatively correlated with changes in GABA+ (r = -0.659, p = 0.076) and in E/I balance (p = 0.056, R² = 0.150), indicating that higher activation was associated with higher E/I balance. Cross-hemisphere relationships were also observed: left S2 peak T-values predicted right-hemisphere Glx levels (R² = 0.128, p = 0.044), with higher left activation associated with lower right Glx. These GLM results suggest that neurometabolite levels explain 10–15% of variance in fMRI activation patterns and behavioral outcomes, providing preliminary evidence for links between neurochemistry, brain activation, and motor performance.

Conclusion

Bilateral S2 showed stronger activation in older than in young adults during identical finger tapping, which alone can be interpreted both as compensatory recruitment or dedifferentiation of sensorimotor networks. Aligning with the compensation theory, S2 could be hyperactive due to age-related reliance on additional somatosensory resources. On the other hand, it could be a by-product of dedifferentiation of sensorimotor networks. 

By targeting these hyperactive S2 regions with tDCS, we modulated local neurochemistry, increasing GABA+ and tending to reduce Glx, which shifted excitation–inhibition balance towards inhibition. We hoped that change would lead to decrease in fMRI activation extent of S2. Although we did not observe significant change in S2 after stimulation, statistical modeling shows that these neurochemical shifts explained ~10–15% of the variance in activation patterns.  Baseline neurometabolic levels were associated with some behavioral outcomes, with higher right-hemisphere GABA+ at baseline being related to faster responses and better sound accuracy, suggesting that a more inhibitory or balanced S2 state may support more efficient behavior. Despite neurometabolic and activation effects, motor performance did not change, pointing towards the dedifferentiation theory. 

Together, these findings demonstrate the feasibility and sensitivity of a multimodal HD-ctDCS–MRS–fMRI approach to probe the neurochemical basis of age-related hyperactivation. Power analyses indicate that a larger sample (≈50–70 OA) is required to repeat these findings.
