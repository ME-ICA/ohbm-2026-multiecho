Introduction

FMRI signal from eye orbits (fMRIeye) has been used as a proxy for eye movements and eye movement-related brain activity.  Specifically, fMRIeye contains information regarding the eye position, open/close state, and correlates with resting-state (RS) cortical activity in visual and somatosensory areas (reviewed in Frey et al., 20211). Linear methods (such as mean, variance, principal component analysis, multi-voxel pattern analysis, or rigid-body registration) applied to the fMRIeye result in time series (fMRIeye regressors) that approximate the real eye movement. However, each of these methods creates different kinds of time series, and explanatory power of those methods for eye movements have not been compared. Even if they have enough explanatory power, they may be related to different cortical systems due to difference in the quantification methods. In addition, fMRIeye contains oscillations from other phsyiological systems that may confound the fMRIeye regressors2. In this work, we systematically compare the different fMRIeye regressors with each other, to simultaneously recorded eye tracker data, to head motion, and to the cortical activity during resting-state in different frequency bands. 

Methods

Dataset: 

 An open-access resting-state (RS) fMRI dataset with simultaneous eye tracking was used³. Eighty-three participants were scanned; analyses focused on 29 participants with high-quality eye tracker data. Participants belonged to two age groups: 20–30 years (n = 45, median = 23) and 65–75 years (n = 38, median = 68). During the 480-s RS scan, participants kept their eyes open and fixated on a central cross. 

MRI data and preprocessing: 

 Structural T1 images were acquired with 1-mm isotropic resolution. RS EPI images were collected with 3-mm slices (no gap), axial orientation, 49 slices, interleaved acquisition, TE = 30 ms, TR = 2.5 s, flip angle = 75°. Cortical RS preprocessing included slice-timing correction, motion correction, band-pass filtering (0.01–0.1 Hz), regression of nuisance signals (head motion, framewise displacement, white matter, CSF), and 6-mm FWHM smoothing. Mean time series were extracted from 500 regions using the Schaefer-Yeo parcellation. 

fMRI eye-orbit preprocessing: 

 Eye-orbit signals were processed using DeepMReye¹. Eye images were registered to a template generated from 29 participants fixating centrally. Voxelwise Z-scored signals were computed, and non-normalized signals were retained to preserve anatomical information for later analyses. 

Eye tracker data and preprocessing: 

 Eye tracking data were available for 77 participants and recorded at 60 Hz using a ViewPoint EyeTracker (Arrington Research, USA). Right-eye gaze position and pupil size were measured. Participants were monitored to avoid prolonged eye closures (>5 s). In our previous work, we employed an artifact detection algorithm based on pupil dimensions to exclude noisy data⁴. Twenty-nine participants with low artifact rates (18 ± 2%) were selected for further analyses. We use the same set of subjects in this study. Gaze positions on screen (ETx, ETy) were converted to visual angles and downsampled to the fMRI TR using the median value every 2.5 s. 

Calculation of fMRIeye regressors: 

 fMRIeye regressors were computed using multiple linear approaches: mean signal, band-passed mean, variance, standard deviation, rigid-body motion parameters (6 DOF) from optic nerve ROIs and whole-eye images, first 10 principal components, MVPA similarity to the first time point, and weighted center-of-mass (x, y, z). All regressors were derived separately for each eye, combined and separately, and from normalized and non-normalized images. 

Comparison with eye tracking, head motion, and RS brain activity: 

 Right–left eye correlations were computed for each regressor. Frequency characteristics were assessed using FFT-based power spectra. Correlations between normalized and non-normalized regressors and with head motion parameters were calculated using Pearson’s r. Linear regression models assessed how well each fMRIeye regressor explained variance in ETx and ETy. The same framework was applied to RS cortical data, using mean time series from each of the 500 regions as dependent variable 

Results

Across 29 subjects, MVPA (mean Pearson’s r=0.95±0.02) and PCA1 (mean Pearson’s r=0.89±0.17) showed the highest absolute correlation between right and left eyes. Motion parameters derived from the optic nerves resulted in correlations close to zero. Normalization of the fMRIeye via Z-scoring decreased the consistency between the eyes for MVPA and PCA1 but increased drastically for the methods that rely on the anatomical information such as center of mass (mean r before=0.03±0.01, after= 0.61±0.3) and motion correction (mean r before= 0.04±0.06, after=0.45±0.28) using whole eye images. FFT analysis showed that the most common frequency band for the fMRIeye regressors are between 0-0.01 Hz, followed by 0.01-0.02 band, and 0.02-0.1 Hz. MVPA (mean r=0.65±0.06) and PCA1(mean r=0.6±0.06) were the regressors that showed the highest correlation with the motion parameters, and the band-passed mean has the lowest correlation (mean r=0.06±0.01). Median R2 across all subjects showed that the same regressors explained the most variance in ETx (mean R2 for MVPA=0.21±0.2, PCA1= 0.19±0.23 )and ETy (mean R2 for MVPA= 0.21±0.21, for PCA1=0.17±0.21), although this overall explained variance (around 20%) is low.  

Lastly, variance in the cortical RS data across whole brain for all subjects were the best explained by the Mean_bandpassed regressor (mean R2: 0.02±0.03).  The specific bands (0.01-0.025, 0.025-0.05, 0.05-0.075, 0.075-0.01, 0.01-0.02) showed different explanatory power in cortical RS data, bands within 0.01-0.25 being the highest (mean R2 = 0.02±0.001). Following this lead, all the fMRIeye regressors were band-passed between 0.01-0.025 Hz. This setting drastically increased the overall R2 (mean R2 across all features= 0.02±0.01).   The ROIs whose time series were the most relevant to MVPA and PCA1 regressors after band-passing were somotomotor and visual, and medial frontal regions.  

Conclusion

Principal component analysis and MVPA regressors explain the most variance in the gaze position recorded by an eye tracker. When it comes to the brain activity, explanatory power across different fMRIeye regressors are very similar, but MVPA and PCA methods are still favoured thanks to the explained variance with EXt and EXy. The specific cortical systems that are captured by fMRIeye regressors are to be investigated. The band-pass frequency that explains the most variance in the cortical activity suggests the interval that contains the least amount of physiological noise, as the cardiac and breathing-related oscillations are recorded within the low frequency band (0-0.015 Hz)5 and high (0.1 Hz and above)4. It could be said that fMRIeye regressors that are calculated via linear methods are valid tools to approximate the eye movements to brain activity, but their explanatory power for both eye tracker data and brain activity remains low. Future work could investigate further denoising approaches for these fairly simple metrics to improve their statistical power.
