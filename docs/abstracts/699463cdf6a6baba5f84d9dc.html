Introduction

Emotions emerge from distributed brain networks whose activity is shaped by underlying neurochemical systems. Emotions modulate behaviour through coordinated actions of the central and peripheral nervous systems, and their dysregulation is central to many psychiatric disorders. Therefore, understanding emotions from the perspective of neurotransmitter and neuromodulator systems is critical, particularly given that many of these systems constitute key pharmacological targets. Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) has been used for mapping the large-scale neural circuits engaged during emotional processing, revealing patterns of activation across cortical and subcortical regions. However, fMRI signals are inherently indirect, reflecting hemodynamic changes rather than the molecular mechanisms that modulate neural activity. As a result, linking emotion-related brain activation to specific neurotransmitter systems remains a major challenge in affective neuroscience. Molecular imaging with positron emission tomography (PET) offers complementary insights by providing spatial maps of neurotransmitter receptor and transporter distributions, including those of the dopamine, serotonin, and opioid systems, which are known to modulate affective and motivational processes1,2. Although PET studies are limited by radioligand availability and practical constraints, population-level receptor maps derived from PET data provide a valuable reference for interpreting fMRI findings3. In the present study, we adopt an fMRI-only approach to investigate neural responses associated with emotional processing, while contextualising these responses using existing PET-derived receptor maps. By comparing patterns of blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) activity with the known spatial distribution of receptors, we aim to infer the potential neurochemical substrates underlying observed functional activations. 

Methods

Ten healthy participants (20–28 years; mean age = 23; 5 females) took part in the study. Participants underwent a first (f)MRI scanning session, followed by an onboarding lecture, and a second scanning session with the main experiment. Before the main experiment, participants used the Matter App (https://matter.xyz/app) to collect images representing positive personal memories, following instructions given during onboarding. For each memory, participants rated the extent to which nine positive emotions were present: enthusiasm, sexual desire, recognition, family love, contentment, friendship love, amusement, pleasure, and gratitude. These emotions were selected based on the family tree of positive emotions proposed by Shiota et al.4 . During the main fMRI experiment, participants viewed each image for 5 s while recalling the associated memory and its emotional content. The image was then replaced by a grey fixation dot with a yellow border, during which participants continued recalling the memory for 15 s. Trials were separated by 20 s rest periods, indicated by the removal of the yellow border. Emotion ratings were repeated after scanning. MRI data were acquired on a 7T Siemens Magnetom Plus scanner at Scannexus (Maastricht, NL) using a 1Tx/32Rx head coil (Nova Medical). Functional images were collected with a multiband EPI sequence (1.8 mm isotropic; TR = 1000 ms; TE = 22 ms). High-resolution anatomical images were obtained using an MP2RAGE sequence (0.7 mm isotropic). Functional data were preprocessed using BrainVoyager and FSL, including slice-timing correction, motion correction, and distortion correction. Functional images were coregistered to the anatomy using boundary-based registration within BrainVoyager. Beta maps were estimated using the GLMsingle Python toolbox, incorporating polynomial temporal confounds, denoising-derived regressors, and ridge regularisation. Task predictors were modelled over the full 20 s trial period, with an additional regressor for the 5 s image-viewing phase to account for perceptual effects. Single-subject beta maps were normalised to MNI space using non-linear registration “Syn” available in the ANTs software and smoothed with a 3-voxel FWHM Gaussian kernel. Emotion-specific beta maps were estimated at the subject level using fixed-effects models with ordinal predictors derived from the emotion post-scan ratings. Group-level emotion maps were obtained using random-effects analyses of the individual emotion maps. To examine neurochemical correlates, PET-derived receptor density maps for ten neurotransmitters were included from previous studies3: serotonin (5HT1a, 5HT1b, 5HT2a, 5HT6), dopamine (D1, D2), cannabinoid (CB1), GABAa, opioid (MOR), and glutamate (mGluR5). Correlation partial least squares (PLS-C) analysis was applied to relate receptor distributions to group-level emotion activation patterns. Both fMRI and PET data were parcellated into 246 regions using the Brainnetome atlas. PLS latent variables yielded emotion and neuro scores, with PLS weights and loadings reflecting single map contributions and correlations to each latent component. Statistical reliability of the emotion/receptor loadings was assessed using 10,000 bootstrap iterations, with bootstrap ratios greater than 2 indicating significance.

Results

The PLS-C analysis revealed a robust multivariate correspondence between receptor distribution profiles and emotion-related brain activation patterns. The first latent component exhibited a high degree of spatial similarity between the emotion and receptor datasets (Figure 1a) and accounted for 70% of the total variance (Figure 1b). Examination of the emotion and receptor loadings associated with this component revealed the presence of two distinct subgroups (Figure 1b–c). Specifically, emotions such as pleasure, family love, excitement, and sexual desire showed negative loadings, mirroring the pattern observed for D1, D2, and MOR receptors. In contrast, gratitude and friendship showed positive loadings, comparable to those of 5HT2a, GABAa, 5HT1a, mGluR5, CB1, and 5HT1b receptors. This dissociation suggests the involvement of partially distinct neurochemical mechanisms underlying the two emotion clusters. Notably, several emotions and receptor maps did not exhibit significant loadings based on the bootstrap assessment, at least with respect to the first and most dominant latent component.

Conclusion

The main patterns revealed by the PLS analysis are largely consistent with previous findings on emotional processing. In particular, hedonic emotions such as pleasure, excitement, and sexual desire showed strong associations with opioid and dopaminergic receptor distributions, in line with extensive evidence linking these neurotransmitter systems to reward processing, motivation, and subjective feelings of pleasure. Interestingly, family love clustered within this same neurochemical profile. Although family love is often associated with oxytocin, its grouping with opioid and dopamine systems may reflect previously hypothesised interactions between oxytocin, opioids, and dopamine pathways5. The inability to directly assess oxytocin receptor involvement represents a methodological limitation, as whole-brain oxytocin receptor maps are currently unavailable and therefore could not be included in the multimodal analysis. The association of friendship with a receptor subgroup including cannabinoid receptors is also in line with prior evidence highlighting the role of the endocannabinoid system in regulating social behaviour. Not all emotions considered in the study were equally well characterised by the PLS analysis. This may reflect a lack of strong or consistent group-level activation patterns for certain emotions, or the involvement of neurochemical mechanisms that were not captured by the variance explained by the first latent component of the model. For some emotions, such as gratitude, which in this analysis was associated with a spatial pattern involving serotonin and cannabinoid receptors, direct molecular evidence remains sparse. As a result, these associations should be interpreted cautiously and regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive. Additional data and targeted experimental designs will be necessary to clarify the neurochemical substrates of these emotions and to disentangle the overlapping contributions of various neurotransmitters. Overall, this work demonstrates the potential of multimodal fMRI–PET analysis to non-invasively investigate the neurochemical correlates of a broad set of positive emotions. By integrating functional activation patterns with neurotransmitter receptor distributions, this approach provides a framework for linking emotional brain functions to underlying neurochemical systems. Altogether, these findings offer new insights into how multiple neurotransmitter systems jointly support affective processes and present testable hypotheses that can be addressed in future causal studies.
