Introduction

Statistical power, or ability to detect true effects of many neuroimaging studies, falls far below the recommended standard of 80%¹'², achieving only 8-31% average power¹. Consequently, researchers frequently miss key effects, resulting in inconclusive findings and inefficient usage of time and resources¹'².

A known solution to low power lies in performing proper a-priori power calculations, where researchers estimate sample sizes to reliably detect true effects³. However, few neuroimaging studies provide power calculations, as existing approaches for power estimation are limited.

Parametric approaches require distributional assumptions that, when violated, produce incorrect results and cannot correctly estimate power for widely-used methods like TFCE or cluster-based inference. A non-parametric power estimation algorithm has been proposed by  Noble et al.², but it was developed as limited proof-of-concept research code rather than a distributable toolbox, unable to accommodate diverse data types, datasets, and test types.

Here, we present PRISME (Power Resampling Infrastructure for Statistical Method Evaluation), a MATLAB toolbox for power benchmarking neuroimaging. PRISME addresses the computational and flexibility limitations of previous approaches through several innovations. First, we leveraged permutation recycling to reduce algorithmic complexity, allowing larger scale and faster power analysis. Second, we developed algorithmic optimizations for widely-used statistical methods and added support for one-sample, two-sample, and correlation analyses, to allow for empirical power analysis at a new scale. We demonstrate this with power estimates across 40 ABCD studies and 5 HCP voxel activation studies.

Methods

The power estimation procedure implemented in PRISME is structured around repeated subsampling to evaluate the ability of statistical inference methods to detect effects known to be present in the full dataset. Researchers can use large reference datasets to estimate power for planned studies with smaller sample sizes under similar experimental conditions. To do so, it utilizes two distinct computational paths: a repeated-sampling loop that evaluates the methods in multiple subsets of the data, and a single estimation step that defines the ground truth effect map. The results of both paths are then combined to provide power calculations.

In the repeated-sampling path, n subjects are randomly sampled from the larger pool of N available subject-level connectivity matrices. The t-statistics are obtained with mass univariate regression, which are then used by the statistical inference methods described below. The statistical inference methods, in turn, produce p-values for each repetition. For each repetition, p-values are compared to a significance threshold to identify statistically significant effects (i.e., where the null hypothesis is rejected and effects are detected).

PRISME evaluates statistical power at four inference levels. Variable level: Power to detect effects in individual analysis units: FC links (edges) between brain regions for FC data, or individual voxels for activation data. Cluster level: Power to detect effects in spatially contiguous groups of variables through pooled inference (e.g., using Cluster Size or TFCE methods). Network level: Power to detect aggregate effects within variables in predefined large-scale functional systems (e.g., default mode, salience networks). Whole-brain level: Power to detect any significant effect across all variables, i.e., if an effect can be detected in any of the studied variables. Each repetition produces p-values whose quantity depends on the inference level: one per variable (variable-level and cluster-level methods), one per network (network-level methods), or a single value (whole-brain inference).

PRISME currently implements the following statistical inference methods: Parametric-FDR, Parametric-FWER, cNBS-FDR, cNBS-FWER, Cluster Size, TFCE, and mv-cNBS. For methods involving multiple tests, both False Discovery Rate (FDR) and Family-Wise Error Rate (FWER) corrections are implemented. FDR controls the expected proportion of false positives among significant results, while FWER controls the probability of making at least one false positive. In total, counting correction methods separately, seven inferential procedures are currently implemented.

Ground truth effects: Separately, ground truth effects are computed using the full sample of all N subjects by fitting the group-level GLM and computing t-statistics for all variables, and averaged across networks for network-level inference. With the explicit assumption that ground truth effects define true effects², edges and networks are labeled as true effects in the direction of their t-statistic sign. As noted by Turner et al.⁴, ground truth estimated from finite samples may not precisely capture all effect sizes. However, this provides conservative power estimates: including uncertain small effects in ground truth increases detection difficulty, thereby evaluating the capability of inference methods to detect weak effects. For whole-brain level inference, there are no directional effects. If the ground truth effects indicate that any variable has a non-zero t-statistic, then the positives resulting from inference methods at this level are considered true positives.

At the end of the workflow, detected effects from each repetition are compared to ground truth effects. Statistical power for a given brain area or whole-brain test is defined as the proportion of correctly identified effects across all repetitions. This yields power probability estimates for all inference methods according to the method's inference level. 

Results

Computational Benchmarking Results:

PRISME has several computational optimizations that promote computational efficiency. It recycles permutations and GLM fits across all statistical inference methods rather than recalculating for each. Second, we proposed several optimized versions of known state-of-the-art statistical inference methods (TFCE and cNBS) combined with C++ implementations. Third, processing repetitions in batches while saving for checkpointing enables computation resilient to crashes and interruptions.

For the computational speed benchmark tests, we first conducted a power calculation using the HCP dataset for 5 studies across 5 sample sizes (n = 20, 40, 80, 120, 200). Afterwards, we used the reported average time per study reported by Noble et al. for an estimation of the total time to conduct the same power calculation under similar computing conditions. The estimated time reported by Noble et al. from their research code was 20 days, compared to 20 hours with PRISME.

The computational advantages would be greater for other test types. HCP studies primarily use one-sample t-tests requiring the matrix inversion of a single column design matrix, while ABCD studies include two-sample t-tests and correlation analyses with 2-column design matrices that require more computational time to compute the matrix inversion. As PRISME recycles GLM fits across all inference methods rather than recomputing for each method, the efficiency gains for ABCD datasets would exceed the 25× speedup benchmarked on HCP. However, it is impossible for us to benchmark this as Noble et al. research code was not capable of performing these tests.

Accuracy and validation:

We validated PRISME through two methods. First, we verified that the implementation of statistical inference methods accurately detects known effects using synthetic datasets for each test type (one-sample, two-sample, and association tests). This testing framework confirmed 100% detection accuracy for known synthetic effects and provides method developers with a sanity check for new implementations. Furthermore, this testing framework is implemented as both a standalone script and as a GitHub action.

Second, we replicated and extended the HCP power analysis from Noble et al.² (Results Figure). We analyzed 5 FC task studies using 7 inference methods (Parametric-FDR, Parametric-FWER, cNBS-FDR, cNBS-FWER, Cluster Size, TFCE, and mv-cNBS) across 4 sample sizes (n = 250, n = 500, n = 1000, n = 2000) and compared to their original 3 sample sizes (n = 40, n = 80, n = 120). This design enables direct benchmarking of PRISME power calculation.

The power results from Noble et al.² closely match the results produced by PRISME, within reasonable expected error (Results Figure).

For voxel-level analyses, we generated power curves across sample sizes for five HCP tasks (n = 20, 40, 80, 120, 200; 100 repetitions). While previous literature measured detection rates at n = 20⁵ for cluster-based voxel inference, this is a full-scale direct power calculation for multiple statistical inference methods provided by a toolbox.

Having validated the power calculation accuracy of PRISME, we demonstrate its computational capability by performing power analyses at a scale previously infeasible: 40 ABCD studies (compared to 5 HCP studies in the validation) encompassing both two-sample t-tests (sex differences) and correlation tests (age, cognitive, and behavioral measures). The Results Figure presents the study with the highest observed power: sex differences in resting-state FC, where all methods except cluster size and edge-level parametric FWER exceed 80% power at n = 200.

Conclusion

Overall, PRISME is the first resolutely empirical power benchmarking framework for neuroimaging, providing support for more statistical inference methods, test types, and data types than other neuroimaging power calculators. It uses a non-parametric algorithm, so researchers do not need to worry about violating distributional assumptions that cause errors in parametric approaches. PRISME is designed as a simple, plug-and-play tool for routine power calculations. This claim is validated by the broad scope of power calculations provided by PRISME: the five FC studies and the 5 voxel studies for the HCP dataset, and the 40 studies from the ABCD dataset. Overall, this covers two data types (spatial maps and networks), three test types (t sample, t2 sample, and association), and any statistical inference method that maps t-statistics to p-values, with seven methods currently implemented.

Computational efficiency is achieved from the permutation recycling strategy, an integrated architecture for better C++ and MATLAB implementation, and an improvement of existing statistical inference methods. Combined, all these optimizations greatly increase computational efficiency, making large-scale empirical power calculations feasible. For example, the in-depth power analysis of the ABCD dataset with 40 studies, which could only be achieved with 25× overall speedup compared to previous existing empirical power calculations.

Although PRISME’s computational optimizations make large-scale empirical power calculation viable and ground truth effect estimation using large datasets is broadly applicable, it has limitations. This effect estimation comes from finite samples and may not accurately capture true effect sizes. Ongoing work aims to evaluate the nature and degree of the resulting error.

PRISME is a computationally efficient framework that enables data-driven empirical power benchmarks to facilitate the development of power calculators and related tools that facilitate more informed, data-driven study planning. As neuroimaging still struggles with the reproducibility challenge partly caused by underpowered studies, accessible tools to improve study planning for adequate power become critical. We anticipate that this power benchmarking tool will contribute to efficient resource allocation, reliable findings and improved reproducibility in neuroscience.
