Introduction

Adolescence is widely recognised as one of the most formative and critical periods of life. The brain undergoes significant structural and functional reorganisation, which, in turn, is thought to underpin the substantial cognitive and behavioural development that occurs during this period. Importantly, these brain and behavioural changes are shaped by a plethora of environmental factors, ranging from immediate familial circumstances to broader neighbourhood contexts. Understanding how these factors intersect during this formative period of life is critical for understanding individual differences in cognitive and mental health trajectories. In the present study, we investigated how macro- and micro-environmental factors predict changes in brain structure and, in turn, changes in mental health and cognition across adolescence.

Methods

We used the ABCD dataset (1) to examine the environment-brain-behaviour mediation pathway at both baseline (N = 6,911; 3,605 M, 3,606 F; mean age = 9.93) and longitudinally across three timepoints (N = 1,628; 879 M, 749 F; ages 8-15). The Child Opportunity Index (COI 2.0) (2) was used to measure various aspects of the macro-environment (e.g., social/economic, education, and health/environment opportunities at the neighbourhood-level), while a summary latent factor score (3) was used to measure aspects of the micro-environment (e.g., parental characteristics, socioeconomic status). The Childhood Behaviour Checklist (CBCL) was used to measure mental health traits (4), while the NIH Toolbox Cognition Battery was used to measure neurocognition across a range of key domains (5). Centile scores, indicating where an adolescent's brain volume falls relative to same-age and same-sex peers, were used to measure population-referenced brain volumes. Age-normalised change scores were calculated for all metrics for longitudinal analysis. Across both the baseline and longitudinal analysis, mixed effects models were used to, first, independently assess the associations between the environment, brain, and behaviour. The environment-brain-behaviour mediation pathway was subsequently examined for all combinations of environment, brain, mental health, and cognitive measures that showed significant associations in step 1. For each analysis, testing site and family ID (to account for sibling pairs) were modelled as random intercepts, while age and sex were modelled as covariates. Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) corrections were applied within each analysis category. 

Results

At baseline, we found that adolescents with an improved macro- and micro-environment showed larger brain volumes relative to their peers and, in turn, improved mental health and cognitive outcomes. As a result, in several cases, brain centile scores mediated the associations between environmental factors and cognitive and mental health outcomes (Figure 1A).

Longitudinally, we found that (a) adolescents with an improved macro- and micro-environment showed upward shifts in centile scores, (b) adolescents that show upward shifts in centile scores in targeted regions show improvements in mental health and cognition, and (c) adolescents with an improved macro- and micro- environment show improvements in some domains of mental health and cognition (e.g., externalising and Picture Vocabulary Task). However, although we observed independent longitudinal associations between the environment, the brain, and behaviour, the longitudinal environment-brain-behaviour mediation pathway was not significant in most cases (Figure 1B).

Conclusion

The present study identified both baseline and longitudinal associations between the environment, brain, and behaviour during adolescence. The findings provide confirmatory evidence for the critical role the environment plays in shaping the adolescent brain, cognition, and mental health. Additionally, both mental health and cognition are also significantly impacted by changes in brain structure, such that larger brain volumes relative to peers are associated with improved outcomes. While independent associations between these factors persist both at baseline and longitudinally, the findings suggest that the environment-brain-behaviour mediation pathway is more informative in predicting baseline rather than longitudinal effects. The finding represent an important step towards the identification of biomarkers and enviro-types that shape cognition and mental health during this critical period of development. In turn, this may support the development of both neurobehavioral interventions and policy initiatives aimed at improving the environment to promote positive outcomes for adolescents.
