Introduction

Concept learning entails a fundamental trade-off between stability and flexibility of rule representations. While rule representations need to be stable and generalizable, they also need to remain sufficiently flexible to allow for adaptivity to changes in the environment. Computational theories propose that the brain arbitrates this stability-flexibility trade-off by adjusting the exploratory scope of its neurocognitive operations within multidimensional feature spaces. However, the neural correlates underlying these mechanisms remain unclear.1 Specifically, it remains unresolved whether the high computational cost of rule adaptation stems solely from rule reconstruction or from a suboptimal expansion of the search space (over-exploration) imposing distinct neural demands, or both.

Current neurobiological models suggest two systems supporting concept learning: an abstract-model network (MTL-vmPFC) supports structural generalization and conceptual compression, whereas a control network (lPFC, AI, dACC) mediates conflict monitoring and belief updating.2-4 Building on this framework, we hypothesize that control network recruitment reflects a distinct computational demand: while generalization relies on low resource rule maintenance in medial regions, rule adaptation requires additional cost, for potentially suboptimal expansion of the search space. Specifically, we consider that exploration of feature spaces during rule adaptation involves the incorporation of non-diagnostic features, which in turn necessitates greater engagement of the control network to support the computational load when enlarging the search space .

Methods

We acquired functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) data from 34 healthy young adults (18 females; mean age = 22.6 ± 2.7 years) performing a two-block, binary reward-based categorization task, in which participants made Yes/No category decisions based on visual object stimuli. Stimuli were polyomino shapes defined by three binary feature dimensions (color, orientation, texture), yielding 23=8 unique exemplars per set. To assess generalization and adaptation, we employed a partial-to-full set block-wise transition design: block 1 presented a restricted subset of 4 exemplars, while block 2 introduced the complete set of  8. Based on the partial-to-full set design, we manipulated the rule transition between blocks to establish three conditions, which varied in the requisite number and configuration of diagnostic features for optimal generalization or adaptation to block-2 novel stimuli. In the Preservation condition, the rule acquired in block 1 remained optimal for categorizing  block-2 stimuli; in the Extension condition, the block-1 rule had to be extended to an additional diagnostic feature dimension to accommodate novel block-2 exemplars; and in the Reconstruction condition, the block-1 rule became invalid and a new rule based a different set of diagnostic features had to be learned for block-2 stimuli. Crucially, in these three conditions, the optimal rule for both blocks was always defined by a minimal subset of diagnostic features, 1 or 2 dimensions, establishing a ground-truth solution relative to the full feature space. Thus, if participants successfully identified the ground-truth rule, categorization could theoretically be optimal by filtering out irrelevant dimensions. To fully capture the trial-wise dynamics of strategy under these varying conditions, and building on evidence that the brain organizes conceptual knowledge into map-like geometric structures,5 we developed a geometric representation of subjective rules by mapping participants’ trial-by-trial responses onto a 3D Euclidean space, defined by the three binary feature dimensions. We calculated the angular deviation (θ) between the resulting subjective rule plane and the ground-truth rule plane to quantify strategy alignment. As an index of strategy-shift magnitude, we calculated the Hamming distance (HD) between block 1 and block 2 feature selection vectors, to quantify the number of modified dimensions in each participant’s internal rule model. This index operationalizes rule re-application (HD = 0, no block 1-to-2 feature changes) versus progressively broader strategy shifts (HD = 1-3) . We analyzed trial-wise accuracy, response times, and angular deviation using hierarchical Bayesian generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs), stratified by rule conditions and HD. Neuroimaging data were acquired during the above task using a 3T Skyra system (National Cheng-Chi University, Taiwan), 64-channel head coil, multiband factor 2, with 6 runs of echo-planar imaging scans, each with 625 volumes, 60 axial slices, 96 x 96 2.5 mm isotropic voxels, TR=752ms. T1- and T2-weighted contrast images were also acquired for spatial registration and normalization to standard space. Functional data were preprocessed using fMRIprep and in-house scripts, with statistical analysis based on univariate General Linear Models (GLMs). We computed interaction contrasts targeting block-wise changes (e.g., [Reconstruction > Preservation] x [Block 2 > Block 1]) to isolate neural signatures of rule Preservation, Extension, and Reconstruction.  Significant clusters were identified using a cluster-forming threshold of Z > 3.1 and a cluster-level FWE-corrected threshold of p < 0.05.

Results

Analyzing accuracy changes from block 1 to block 2, paired t-tests revealed significant gains in stimuli categorization success in the Preservation  (p < .001) and Extension (p = .006) conditions, while Reconstruction showed no reliable change. Accordingly, direct comparisons at block 2 confirmed that accuracy in the Reconstruction condition was significantly lower than in both preservation and extension conditions (p < .001). Regarding response time (RT), only the Preservation condition exhibited a significant reduction across blocks (p < .001); in contrast, no reliable changes were observed for Extension or Reconstruction conditions. Crucially, the lack of improvement resulted in block 2 RTs being significantly slower for Reconstruction relative to Preservation  (p < .001) and Extension (p = .002) conditions. Taken together, these accuracy and RT patterns indicate that learning improvements were greatest when the original rule could be preserved, moderate when it had to be extended, and relatively absent when it had to be reconstructed, consistent with increasing rule-shift cost from Preservation to Extension to Reconstruction conditions. To understand the source of the strategy-shift costs, hierarchical GLMM analyses stratified by HDs revealed that the magnitude of feature modification predicted distinct accuracy changes from block 1 to 2: trials with rule re-application (HD=0) achieved rapid ceiling accuracy, whereas increments in HD (1 → 2 → 3) were associated with slower increases in accuracy correspondingly. Consistent with the stratification by HD, convergence toward decision planes with angular deviation θ=0° (relative to ground truth) characterized trials with low-cost block transitions (i.e. the ground-truth rule was successfully identified). In contrast, sustained non-zero deviations characterized the persistent inclusion of non-diagnostic features during expansive strategy shifts, reflecting a suboptimal strategy despite available simpler solutions, and difficulty in identifying the ground-truth rule.

Neurally, we observed a functional dissociation tracking these behavioral and computational costs. In the Preservation condition, blood oxygen level dependent (BOLD) signal from 1 to 2 decreased in bilateral visual regions, including occipital pole, intracalcarine cortex, and lateral occipital cortex, together with increased BOLD in the hippocampus and ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC), consistent with the lower-cost neurocomputational maintenance of a compressed rule representation. Conversely, the Reconstruction condition elicited the highest block-wise recruitment of the lPFC, insula, and ventral striatum. Importantly, activity in the control-related regions rose with increasing rule-shift cost (Reconstruction > Extension). To urther probe the neural signatures associated with suboptimal adaptation (HD > 1 and angular deviation ≠ 0), we compared whole-brain BOLD responses across block transitions with high versus low feature modification, as defined by the HD. Block transitions with high HD, involving 2–3 modified features, showed higher activation in occipital and occipitotemporal visual cortices, including occipital pole and lateral occipital cortex, than rule re-application block transitions with HD=0, suggesting that expansive re-exploration of feature space is accompanied by intensified processing of visual feature.

Conclusion

Our findings reveal that concept learning relies on a functional dissociation of networks during rule generalization and rule adaptation. While rule generalization was supported by sensory cortex attenuation, rule adaptation involved a shift to the control network. Critically, we found that the presumably higher neural cost of rule adaptation was associated with a tendency toward optimal exploration behavior. Specifically, the HD and geometric angular deviation indices revealed that adaptive strategy shifts were characterized by expansive modification of  participant internal rule models and persistent inclusions of non-diagnostic features, rather than optimal alignment with ground-truth rules. These broad strategy shifts, higher HDs, were associated with intensified sensory cortex activation, indicating a costly reliance on the extra processing of visual features. Consequently, the control network is recruited to support the higher computational demands during additional rule search. By linking geometric measures of behavioral strategy alignment with brain functional network dissociations, our framework integrates theories of structural abstraction with executive control, offering a mechanistic explanation for why unlearning and adapting is neurocomputationally more expensive than generalizing existing rules.
