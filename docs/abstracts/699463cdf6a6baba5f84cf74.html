Introduction

Primary brain tumors, particularly gliomas, represent a significant clinical challenge due to the complex interplay between tumor biology, treatment effects, and brain health. Patients with brain tumors often experience cognitive decline1 and changes in brain structure2, which significantly impact quality of life and functional outcomes. Brain age estimation, derived from structural neuroimaging using machine learning, has emerged as a powerful tool for quantifying overall brain health3. Brain age gap (predicted brain age minus chronological age) serves as an indicator of accelerated aging, with positive values indicating older-appearing brains4. Brain age gap is particularly well-suited as an index of overall brain health in primary brain tumors because tumors and their treatment produce widespread, diffuse effects throughout the brain rather than isolated focal changes. Gliomas are infiltrative tumors that extend beyond visible boundaries, causing structural and functional alterations in distant regions through edema, inflammation, and disrupted connectivity5. Radiation therapy and chemotherapy produce diffuse white matter damage, cortical thinning, and vascular changes extending throughout the brain. We examined longitudinal trajectories of brain age gap over 24 months in brain tumor patients, compared trajectories between glioma and non-glioma patients, and investigated associations with cognitive function.

Methods

Eighty-eight patients with primary brain tumors were recruited for a prospective observational study examining effects of fractionated partial brain radiotherapy on brain structure and cognition (2014-2024). Eligibility criteria included age >18 years, Karnofsky performance status >70, estimated life expectancy >1 year, and ability to undergo neurocognitive testing in English. Patients with prior brain radiotherapy were excluded. The analysis included 406 observations from 88 unique participants across 6 timepoints (baseline, 3, 6, 12, 18, 24 months). Mean age was 45.7 years (SD=12.8, range: 20.8-76.7 years); 54.4% were male. Observations per timepoint: baseline (n=82), 3 months (n=77), 6 months (n=75), 12 months (n=71), 18 months (n=54), 24 months (n=47). MRI scans were acquired on a 3.0T GE 750 scanner using an 8-channel head coil. Anatomical images were acquired using 3D T1-weighted inversion recovery spoiled gradient-echo sequences (TE/TR=2.8/6.5 ms; inversion time=450 ms; flip angle=8°; FOV=24 cm). Brain age was calculated using brainageR (version 2.1), which predicts age from T1-weighted structural MRI scans using a Gaussian Process Regression model trained on 3,377 healthy individuals (mean age=40.6 years, SD=21.4, range 18-92 years). Preprocessing included tissue segmentation using SPM12, spatial normalization to brainageR-specific template space, and quality control. Predicted brain age correlated strongly with chronological age (r=0.888, p<0.001). Brain age gap was calculated as predicted brain age minus chronological age, where positive values indicate older-appearing brains. Longitudinal trajectories were examined using linear mixed-effects models fitted with restricted maximum likelihood estimation using the nlme package in R (version 4.4.2). The primary model included brain age gap as the dependent variable, with timepoint (continuous: 0, 3, 6, 12, 18, 24 months), chronological age, sex, and tumor grade as fixed effects, and a random intercept for subject. To compare trajectories between glioma and non-glioma patients, we extended the model to include diagnosis and diagnosis by timepoint interaction, controlling for age, sex, tumor grade, planning target volume (PTV), and radiation dose. A similar model examined IDH status differences within glioma patients (n=50 with available IDH data). Baseline associations with demographic and clinical characteristics were examined using all available baseline data (n=82). For continuous variables, partial correlations with brain age gap adjusted for age and sex were calculated. For categorical variables, t-tests (binary) and one-way ANOVA (multi-category) were used, all adjusted for age and sex using ANCOVA. Neuropsychological testing included DKEFS Color Naming (CN) and Word Reading (WR) scores at baseline, 3, 6, and 12 months. Partial correlations between brain age gap and cognitive scores, controlling for age and sex, were calculated separately for each timepoint using the ppcor package. 

Results

Baseline analyses revealed significant associations between brain age gap and key clinical characteristics. IDH status was significantly associated with baseline brain age gap (ANCOVA, p=0.031): IDH-wildtype patients had mean brain age gap of 1.80 years (SE=0.72) versus -2.03 years (SE=1.02) for IDH-mutant patients (difference=3.83 years). Tumor grade was also significantly associated (p=0.041): high-grade tumors showed mean brain age gap of 0.22 years (SE=0.86) versus -2.25 years (SE=0.81) for low-grade tumors (difference=2.47 years). The primary mixed-effects model revealed a significant increase in brain age gap over time (β=0.173 years/month, SE=0.019, t(316)=9.20, p<0.001), corresponding to increases of 2.1 years over 12 months and 4.2 years over 24 months (Figure 1). The model showed good fit (AIC=2251.16, BIC=2275.14). Random intercept variance was 5.49 (SD=2.34 years), indicating substantial between-subject variability. Mean brain age gap increased progressively: 0.57 years at 3 months, 1.58 years at 6 months, 1.78 years at 12 months, 3.31 years at 18 months, and 3.97 years at 24 months. The diagnosis by timepoint interaction was significant (β=-0.112, SE=0.041, t(315)=-2.75, p=0.006). After controlling for age, sex, PTV, and radiation dose, glioma patients showed faster increase (0.202 years/month, SE=0.022, t(315)=9.23, p<0.001) compared to non-glioma patients (0.090 years/month), corresponding to 2.4 years versus 1.1 years over 12 months. No significant baseline difference between groups was observed (p=0.170). Within glioma patients, IDH status by timepoint interaction was not significant (β=0.016, SE=0.056, t(210)=0.28, p=0.776), with both groups showing similar rates (~0.20 years/month). PTV showed marginal positive association with brain age gap (β=0.014, SE=0.007, t(83)=1.99, p=0.050), as did radiation dose (β=0.004, SE=0.002, t(83)=1.95, p=0.054). Partial correlations revealed a significant negative association between brain age gap and DKEFS Color Naming at 3 months (partial r=-0.273, p=0.038), indicating higher brain age gap was associated with poorer processing speed performance, independent of age and sex. Associations at other timepoints were in the same direction but non-significant: baseline (partial r=-0.133, p=0.266), 6 months (partial r=-0.149, p=0.263), 12 months (partial r=-0.207, p=0.120). No significant associations were found for Word Reading at any timepoint.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates progressive brain aging in brain tumor patients, with brain age gap increasing by 0.173 years per month (2.1 years over 12 months) over a 24-month follow-up period. Glioma patients showed more than two-fold faster rates of increase (2.4 years over 12 months) compared to non-glioma patients (1.1 years), independent of demographic factors and treatment variables. This finding suggests that glioma patients experience more rapid progressive brain aging, which may reflect infiltrative tumor characteristics causing widespread structural and functional brain changes beyond the visible tumor mass, as well as factors beyond treatment intensity. The lack of significant baseline differences between groups suggests that trajectory differences emerge over time, potentially reflecting progressive disease-related changes or cumulative treatment effects. The substantial between-subject variability in baseline brain age gap (SD=2.34 years) and significant associations with IDH status (3.83-year difference) and tumor grade (2.47-year difference) highlight the heterogeneity in brain health at presentation. The progressive increase in brain age gap likely reflects the cumulative impact of diffuse disease- and treatment-related processes on global brain integrity. Brain age gap is particularly well-suited as a global index of brain health in this context, as primary brain tumors and their treatment produce widespread, diffuse effects throughout the brain rather than isolated focal changes. Radiation therapy and chemotherapy produce diffuse white matter changes, cortical thinning, and vascular alterations that extend throughout the brain, making brain age gap, which integrates information across the entire brain, a more comprehensive measure than focal metrics. The significant association between brain age gap and processing speed performance at 3 months (partial r=-0.273, p=0.038) provides evidence for a link between brain aging and cognitive function, suggesting that brain age gap captures unique variance in cognitive performance beyond demographic factors. The timepoint-specific nature of this association may reflect a critical period during early recovery when brain age gap is most predictive of cognitive performance. The progressive increase in brain age gap suggests that brain tumor patients may benefit from ongoing long-term monitoring of brain health and early intervention strategies. The faster rate of increase in glioma patients highlights the need for targeted interventions in this population. The association between brain age gap and cognitive function at 3 months suggests that early assessment may help identify patients at risk for cognitive decline, allowing for timely intervention. Future studies with longer follow-up periods, larger sample sizes, and examination of additional cognitive domains would further enhance our understanding of brain aging trajectories in brain tumor patients.
