Introduction

Infantile hydrocephalus (IH) is an early-life neurological condition marked by ventricular enlargement and altered brain development. School-aged children with IH frequently exhibit difficulties in visuospatial processing and mathematics, reflecting disruptions in the neural systems that support numerical cognition^1. Prior neuroimaging work shows that IH is associated with atypical large-scale network organization, including frontal hyperconnectivity and reduced occipito-temporal coupling linked to executive and visual–spatial deficits^2,3. Mathematical learning relies on a distributed frontoparietal–occipital network, and emerging evidence from children with developmental dyscalculia demonstrates that targeted numerical training can normalize aberrant connectivity patterns^4. Whether similar experience-driven plasticity can be harnessed in children with early brain injury remains unknown. Here, we test whether a 3-month adaptive computerized math intervention can improve math skills and reorganize functional connectivity in school-aged children with IH.

Methods

Eleven children with IH (7–12 years) and nineteen typically developing (TD) peers completed a 3-month adaptive math training program (Calcularis; Dybuster AG). Children practiced approximately 15–20 minutes per session, 3–5 days per week. Standardized math assessments (WIAT-III Numerical Operations, Math Fluency; WISC-IV Arithmetic) were administered before and after training. Resting-state fMRI (TR = 1000 ms; eyes open; 6 minutes) was acquired at both sessions using a Siemens 3T Prisma scanner. Preprocessing followed a pediatric-optimized pipeline using FSL, ANTs, and the CONN toolbox, including motion correction, pediatric-template normalization, anatomical CompCor denoising, and band-pass filtering (0.008–0.09 Hz). Twenty-six subject-specific cortical ROIs (frontal, parietal, temporal, occipital) were defined from FreeSurfer-based parcellations refined for each participant. For each session, ROI-to-ROI functional connectivity (Fisher-z correlations) was computed, producing a 26×26 matrix. Connectivity change was quantified as ΔFC = Post – Pre at both global (mean FC across all edges) and ROI-pair levels. Group differences in FC change were evaluated by comparing ΔFC distributions between IH and TD children. Behavioural pre–post changes were assessed using paired t-tests within each group, and between-group differences in change were examined using independent-samples t-tests.

Results

Both groups showed significant improvements in all math subtests (p < .001), with the IH group demonstrating greater gains in Addition (p = .0054) and Numerical Operations (p = .0010). At the neural level, resting-state functional connectivity analyses revealed that IH participants exhibited a reduction in mean whole-brain connectivity from pre- to post-intervention (Δ = –0.0461), whereas TD children showed only a minimal overall change (Δ = +0.0038). A between-group comparison of connectivity change scores confirmed that IH children demonstrated significantly greater reorganization than TD peers (p < .001, Cohen’s d = –0.71). At the regional level, IH participants showed post-training increases primarily in visual pathways including bilateral pericalcarine and fusiform regions and decreases involving frontoparietal and prefrontal connections, including the intraparietal sulcus and bilateral frontal poles. TD children also exhibited detectable but smaller changes, with increases most prominent in occipital–temporal connections (e.g., fusiform–pericalcarine, superior temporal–pericalcarine) and decreases primarily in frontal and fronto-temporal pairs (e.g., frontal pole–pars triangularis, frontal pole–middle temporal).

Conclusion

Three months of adaptive computer-based math training produced measurable changes in both behaviour and brain organization in children with IH. IH participants showed clear evidence of functional network reconfiguration, including increased connectivity within visual processing pathways and reductions in diffuse frontoparietal coupling, a pattern consistent with more efficient and domain-relevant network engagement. TD children also demonstrated localized changes, largely within posterior perceptual circuits, though the magnitude of reorganization was substantially smaller. Together, these results suggest that experience-driven neuroplasticity remains robust in children with early hydrocephalus and that targeted cognitive training can influence the architecture of large-scale functional networks. Beyond improving math performance, such interventions may help guide neural systems toward more specialized and efficient configurations, highlighting the potential of individualized digital programs to support learning and brain health in vulnerable pediatric populations.
