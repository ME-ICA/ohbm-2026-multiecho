Introduction

Emotion regulation (ER) – the capacity to identify, understand, and modify emotional responses – is a core psychological process shaping mental health and the development and maintenance of mental disorders. Difficulties in ER are closely linked to symptom severity in major depressive disorder (MDD) and social anxiety disorder (SAD), and improvements in ER capacities predict therapeutic success across treatments. Although ER has been associated with limbic (e.g., amygdala, insula) and prefrontal (e.g., dorsolateral and ventrolateral prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex) regions, the underlying neurobiological mechanisms remain insufficiently understood^1. Many prior studies rely on categorical diagnostic groups, single neuroimaging modalities, and univariate analytic approaches, limiting their ability to capture the complexity of ER as a transdiagnostic and dimensional construct.

Machine learning (ML) offers a promising framework for modeling individual differences in ER capacity and processing large amounts of data by integrating multimodal neuroimaging and self-report data (demographic, environmental, clinical data). Such predictive approaches are relevant for precision psychotherapy, where treatment personalization is guided by biological, psychological, and environmental markers. However, the extent to which neural data – alone or in combination with self-report data – can meaningfully predict ER capacity is yet unclear. Moreover, few studies evaluate the relative added value of neuroimaging over readily available self-report and clinical measures.

To address this gap, we tested theory-driven hypotheses in a transdiagnostic sample. We expected (1) neural data to reliably predict individual ER capacity, (2) multimodal MRI data integration to outperform single MRI modalities, (3) models combining neural and self-report data to yield the strongest predictive performance, and (4) prefrontal and limbic areas to emerge as the most important neural features. 

Methods

We conducted a secondary analysis of the Transdiagnostic Investigation of Emotional Processing in Depression and Anxiety (TIP) project (preregistration TIP: https://osf.io/s6jqc/; this project: https://osf.io/9kngd/). The sample included n=128 participants (HC=38, MDD=34, SAD=14, SADMDD=42). Clinical, environmental, and sociodemographic variables were assessed using clinical interviews and extensive questionnaires; ER capacity was assessed with the sum scores of the subscales “dysfunctional ER strategies” and “functional ER strategies” as defined in the Cognitive Emotion Regulation Questionnaire. Since there is higher evidence for dysfunctional strategies to be associated with psychiatric disease^2 , the dysfunctional score will serve as the primary outcome and the functional score as the secondary score.

Neuroimaging data included structural MRI, diffusion tensor imaging, resting-state fMRI, and two task-based fMRI paradigms (one ER task and face-matching/”Hariri”). Preprocessing followed state-of-the-art pipelines for each modality.

We implemented machine learning regression models (Random Forest and Support Vector Regression) using nested 10×10 cross-validation, extensive hyperparameter optimization, and 1,000-permutation significance testing within PHOTON-AI^3. Separate models were constructed for (a) each single MRI modality, (b) combined multimodal neuroimaging features, (c) self-report variables only, and (d) integrated neural and self-report models. Feature importance analyses were planned but reserved for models showing statistical significance.

Results

Preliminary results contradict all preregistered hypotheses. Neural data did not reliably predict individual ER capacity – (1) neither single MRI modalities (2) nor the modal combining all neuroimaging data achieved significant performance. Self-report variables yielded the highest predictive accuracy and (3) even outperformed the combined neuroimaging and self-report model. (4) Because no neural model reached reliable predictive performance, feature-importance analyses were not conducted. However, for a further examination of the fourth hypothesis, follow-up analyses using a region-of-interest (ROI)–based approach, rather than the current whole-brain modelling strategy, are planned to examine whether specific brain regions may contribute to ER prediction.

Conclusion

These findings directly contradict our preregistered hypotheses that neural and multimodal MRI models would yield strong or superior predictions of ER capacity. Instead, self-report variables consistently emerged as the most informative predictors. This challenges the widely held assumption that neural signatures provide substantial incremental value for individualized prediction of ER and highlights the need to re-evaluate the role of MRI-based ML approaches in precision psychotherapy. Ongoing analyses will determine whether ROI-based neural models can improve predictive accuracy or meaningfully complement non-neural data. Future work should also systematically examine whether alternative preprocessing pipelines or feature extraction strategies could enhance the predictive utility of neuroimaging data.
