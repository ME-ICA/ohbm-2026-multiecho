Introduction

Early disruptions in white matter development increase the risk of later neurodevelopmental impairments1-3, yet assessing neonatal brain connectivity remains challenging due to rapidly changing morphology. Because traditional graph theory depends on atlas-based parcellation to maintain consistent node definitions and graph sizes, its applicability is limited in developing and pathological populations. Graph neural networks (GNNs) offer a means to learn network topology directly from connectomic data.4 We present a novel atlas-independent framework for neonatal connectome analysis using voxel-level parcellation and a graph convolutional network architecture that accommodates diverse brain morphologies without predefined anatomical templates. To support interpretability, we apply integrated gradients as a post-hoc attribution method to identify the nodes that contribute most strongly to classification outcomes.

Methods

We analyzed diffusion MRI data from 302 neonates across four etiologies: healthy controls, infants born very preterm, infants with congenital heart disease, and infants with spina bifida aperta. The dataset comprised healthy controls and preterm-born infants from the Developing Human Connectome Project as well as all four etiological groups from three cohort studies at the University Children’s Hospital Zurich. All included subjects had T2-weighted structural MRI and diffusion-weighted MRI available. 

Structural connectomes were generated using anatomically constrained probabilistic tractography and SIFT2-based streamline weighting. Two parcellation strategies were implemented. First, atlas-based parcellation using the ENA33 neonatal atlas produced fixed-size graphs consisting of 94 cortical and subcortical regions. Second, atlas-independent voxel-level parcellation defined nodes as individual gray matter voxels, allowing subject-specific network construction without reliance on predefined anatomical templates and resulting in variable-sized graphs. For both parcellation strategies, weighted streamlines connecting node pairs were summed to define edge strength.

A spatial graph convolutional network was used to classify connectomes into four etiologies. Node features included spatial coordinates, total connection strength, and three directed connection strength measures. The network consisted of a projection to a 48-dimensional space, a graph convolution layer, max-pooling readout, and two fully connected layers. A multilayer perceptron (MLP) served as a non-convolutional baseline. Models were trained using 5-fold cross-validation with three seeds per fold. Class imbalance was addressed through stratified sampling, and performance was evaluated using macro-averaged F1, precision, and recall. 

To enable post-hoc interpretability, integrated gradients were applied to compute node-level attribution values for each output class.5 Attribution values were mapped to atlas regions for visualization and explored to characterize etiology-specific decision patterns. 

Results

Voxel-level parcellation substantially increased the number of nodes compared to atlas-based connectomes, enabling high-resolution graph representations of neonatal structural connectivity. Degree distributions exhibited a right-skewed pattern typical of structural connectomes, with a small proportion of highly connected nodes. Edge pruning reduced extreme node degrees while retaining the majority of total connection strength.

Classification performance differed across model types and parcellation strategies. Atlas-based multilayer perceptron models showed the lowest performance (macro F1 = 0.41), whereas introducing graph convolution improved atlas-based classification (F1 = 0.62). When applied to voxel-level parcellation, both models improved; however, the graph convolutional network achieved the highest performance overall (F1 = 0.78), particularly when combined with pruning, suggesting that GNNs are better suited to learning from fine-grained connectomic representations. Precision and recall mirrored these gains.

Embedding visualizations demonstrated that separability between etiologies increased progressively across model layers (Figure 1B). Early embeddings showed clustering influenced by scanner source and postmenstrual age, whereas later-stage embeddings were primarily organized by etiology, indicating that the model learned representations that reduced confounding factors. Confusion matrices (see Figure 1A) reflected clearer separation for preterm birth and spina bifida aperta, whereas congenital heart disease and controls showed greater overlap. 

Integrated gradients generated node-level attribution maps, which showed etiology-dependent patterns across cortical and subcortical nodes. Aggregation into atlas regions enabled interpretation at a regional level (Figure 1C), highlighting the Rolandic operculum, inferior parietal lobule, and inferior frontal gyrus as regions of potential relevance in congenital heart disease and prematurity. This demonstrated that voxel-level graph learning can be complemented by post-hoc attribution to explore decision-relevant components of the connectome. 

Conclusion

This work presents an atlas-independent framework for neonatal connectome analysis using voxel-level parcellation and graph convolution. The approach accommodates diverse brain morphologies and improves etiology classification compared to atlas-based methods. By pairing voxel-level graph learning with post-hoc attribution through integrated gradients, this method provides a means to examine decision-relevant components of the connectome while avoiding predefined anatomical templates. This offers a way to relate model decisions to connectome structure, indicating the potential of explainability within graph learning frameworks. Together, these contributions extend graph-based learning and attribution methods to neonatal structural connectomes and establish a foundation for future classification, prediction, and exploratory analyses in developing and pathological neonatal populations.
