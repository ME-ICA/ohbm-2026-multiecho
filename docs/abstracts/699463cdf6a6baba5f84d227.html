Introduction

It is well known that during unilateral movements, there is a predominant activation of the contralateral primary motor cortex (M1). However, several studies have shown that, under certain conditions, the ipsilateral M1 can also contribute to motor control. For example, using fMRI, Ehrsson and colleagues1 showed that a power grip mainly involves the contralateral M1, while a precision grip activates both M1s as well as fronto-parietal areas. Similarly, Verstynen and colleagues2 suggested that the recruitment of the ipsilateral hemisphere increases with task complexity: the greater the motor demand, the greater the involvement of the ipsilateral M1. To investigate the role of the ipsilateral hemisphere during unilateral movements of varying complexity, this study uses functional Near Infrared Spectroscopy (fNIRS). fNIRS is a non-invasive neuroimaging technique that measures changes in cortical hemoglobin oxygenation using near-infrared light, allowing neural activity to be indirectly estimated with good temporal resolution and greater tolerance to movement than other methods. Thanks to these characteristics, fNIRS is a particularly suitable tool for studying the motor cortex during the performance of natural motor tasks. The aim of this study was therefore to examine the extent to which the ipsilateral M1 is recruited during unilateral finger movements of varying complexity, using fNIRS to record cortical activations.

Methods

We recruited 19 healthy participants (mean age ± SD: 24.8 ± 4.7 years). The experimental protocol consisted of six conditions, each comprising five trials (20 seconds of task and 20 seconds of rest). The conditions differed in terms of sequence type, Finger Tapping (FT), Simple Sequence (SS), or Complex Sequence (CS), and execution pace, which could be Self-Paced (SP) or Maximal (Max). Cortical hemodynamics were recorded using fNIRS. The setup included 46 standard channels (optode distance 3 cm) and 8 short-separation channels (8 mm). Coverage included premotor, sensorimotor, associative, parietal, and frontal areas. fNIRS data were analysed in MATLAB using Homer3 functions. Noisy channels were removed, and the remaining signals converted into optical density changes. Motion artifacts were identified and corrected using a combination of Spline and Wavelet algorithms3. Subsequently, the signals were band-pass filtered (0.1–3 Hz), and the optical density changes were converted into changes in oxy- and deoxy-hemoglobin concentration (HbO and HbR, respectively). The mean hemodynamic functional response (HRF) among trials for each participant, condition, and channel was obtained using a General Linear Model (GLM) approach, which also allowed the regression of short-separation channels. For the analysis, channels belonging to the same Brodmann Area (BA) were averaged, resulting in 22 regions of interest (11 for each hemisphere). For each BA and condition, the average amplitude of the HbO component was extracted in the 5–15 s window from the beginning of the task. These values were used for statistical analysis. A repeated measures ANOVA was performed with four within-subject factors: Sequence (3 levels: FT, SS, CS), Pace (2 levels: SP, Max), Hemisphere (Left, Right), and BA (11 levels). Bonferroni-corrected post-hoc comparisons were used to analyse main effects and significant interactions. 

Results

ANOVA showed a main effect of Sequence (p = 0.029), Hemisphere (p < 0.001), and BA (p < 0.001). Post-hoc analyses showed that the left hemisphere was systematically more active than the right. During SP movements, activity in the different BAs was greater in the SS (FT-SS: -0.030 µM, p = 0.021) and CS (FT-CS: -0.031 µM, p = 0.001) sequences than in the FT condition. BA6, BA4, BA40, and BA7 showed higher activity in CS than in FT, and BA4 was also more active in SS than in FT. Moreover, in the FT condition, activity was higher in the Max condition than in the SP condition (SP-Max: -0.033 µM, p < 0.001). Regardless of the Sequence, BA8, BA3, and BA7 were more active in Max than in the SP condition. 

Conclusion

The results of this study confirm and expand on evidence that the ipsilateral hemisphere contributes to the control of unilateral movements, particularly when the motor demand increases in terms of complexity or speed. The systematically greater activation of the left hemisphere compared to the right suggests functional dominance related to fine motor control of the right hand. The increase in cortical activity for the SS and CS sequences compared to FT during SP movements highlights that the complexity of the motor pattern significantly modulates the recruitment of cortical areas. Similarly, the greater activation observed in Max conditions indicates that the speed of execution requires increased cortical involvement, presumably to maintain accuracy and coordination. The specific pattern of the involved BAs (BA3, BA4, BA6, BA7, BA8, BA40) highlights that multiple areas contribute to the execution of more complex movements. Overall, these results support the hypothesis that the contribution of the ipsilateral hemisphere is not residual, but represents a functional support mechanism in tasks that require greater coordination, planning, or speed of execution, in line with the literature describing an increase in bilateral recruitment proportional to the complexity of the task. This aspect may have important clinical implications. In some neurological conditions, such as multiple sclerosis, it has been observed that, compared to healthy subjects, patients tend to recruit a greater number of cortical networks during movement execution, presumably to compensate for a functional deficit. Targeted rehabilitation can promote the recovery of more efficient motor strategies, bringing activation patterns back to those typical of healthy subjects3.
