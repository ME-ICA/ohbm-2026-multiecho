Introduction

A family history of substance use disorder (FH of SUD) is a well-established risk factor for altered brain development during adolescence.^1 However, the mechanisms through which familial risk influences large-scale brain network dynamics remain poorly understood. Environmental exposures, prenatal factors, and inherited genetic liability may differentially mediate these effects, potentially via sex-specific pathways.^2 Our prior work identified robust, sex-specific alterations in brain network dynamics associated with FH of SUD before substance exposure,^3 but the relative contributions of environmental, prenatal, and genetic mechanisms underlying these effects remain unclear.

Methods

Data was drawn from the baseline visit of the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study (N = 1,886; age = 10.02 ± 0.62 years; 53% female). Brain network dynamics were quantified using transition energy (TE), a network control theory metric indexing the energetic cost of transitioning between brain states given the underlying structural connectome. To dissociate contributions of structural connectivity and functional state configuration, TE was computed using two complementary approaches: (i) a group-average structural connectome paired with subject-specific cortical and subcortical state centroids derived from resting-state fMRI, and (ii) subject-specific structural connectomes derived from diffusion MRI combined with canonical binary Yeo-7 cortical network states. Sex-stratified mediation analyses were implemented using structural equation modeling to test whether associations between family history of substance use disorder (FH+ vs FH−) and global or network-level TE were mediated by environmental context, prenatal substance exposure, or polygenic risk for substance use. Separate single-mediator path models were fit for each TE outcome and mediator, decomposing FH effects into direct and indirect components. Environmental mediation was tested using a composite environmental factor defined as five principal components (in one model) from a multivariate PCA capturing neighborhood, family, school, and peer-related exposures. Prenatal mediation models used a binary indicator of maternal substance use during pregnancy, and genetic mediation models used standardized polygenic risk scores derived from large-scale genome-wide association meta-analyses. All models were run separately in males and females and adjusted for age, in-scanner head motion, and scanner-related effects; polygenic risk models additionally accounted for top 10 genetic principal components ancestry. Indirect effects were evaluated using bootstrapped confidence intervals, and standardized effect estimates were used to compare direct and indirect contributions across sexes, networks, and imaging modalities.

Results

Principal component analysis of environmental variables yielded five principal components explaining approximately 40% of total variance, which reflected socioeconomic neighborhood factors, family support, school environment, and exposure to threat and trauma.

Across sexes and brain networks, FH+ was associated with widespread alterations in TE, as previously observed.^3 For functional TE, environmental context showed significant indirect effects linking family history of substance use disorder (FH) to TE, with clear sex- and network-specific patterns. In males, significant environmental indirect effects were observed for global TE (std = −0.026, 95% CI [−0.109, −0.023]) and across multiple networks, including visual (std = −0.020, CI [−0.086, −0.007]), somatomotor (std = 0.035, CI [0.026, 0.150]), dorsal attention (std = −0.029, CI [−0.120, −0.025]), limbic (std = −0.024, CI [−0.114, −0.018]), frontoparietal (std = −0.032, CI [−0.148, −0.037]), and cerebellar networks (std = −0.019, CI [−0.095, −0.006]). In females, significant environmental indirect effects were fewer and limited to the somatomotor (std = 0.038, CI [0.039, 0.144]), default mode (std = 0.021, CI [0.011, 0.089]), and subcortical networks (std = 0.019, CI [0.007, 0.087]).

For prenatal exposure, indirect effects were largely absent, with isolated significant effects in the male subcortical network (std = −0.012, CI [−0.054, −0.003]) and the female frontoparietal network (std = −0.016, CI [−0.071, −0.006]). Polygenic risk scores showed no significant indirect effects for functional TE in either sex.

For structural TE, environmental mediation was observed selectively in limbic networks in both females (std = −0.055, CI [−0.250, −0.008]) and males (std = −0.057, CI [−0.252, −0.031]), and in dorsal attention and ventral attention networks in females only (DAT: std = −0.048, CI [−0.216, −0.016]; VAT: std = −0.056, CI [−0.223, −0.043]). Prenatal and polygenic mediators showed no consistent indirect effects for structural TE (Figure 2).

Conclusion

Across TE formulations defined by functional and structural dynamics, environmental context was the only mediator class that consistently explained (indirectly) FH-related differences in adolescent brain network dynamics, with broader mediation in males for functional TE and more focal mediation in females, and selective mediation of structural TE in attention/limbic systems. Prenatal exposure and polygenic risk showed little to no mediation, suggesting that, at baseline in early adolescence, familial risk for SUD is transmitted to brain network dynamics primarily through environmental pathways rather than through prenatal exposure or inherited genetic liability. These findings highlight environmental context as a key, potentially modifiable target for reducing neurodevelopmental risk associated with familial SUD history.
