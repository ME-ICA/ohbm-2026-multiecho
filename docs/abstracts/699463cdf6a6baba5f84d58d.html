Introduction

Motor planning is a complex neural process that underpins the ability to anticipate, represent, and specify parameters of upcoming actions prior to execution. It requires selecting the appropriate effector (i.e., the body part used to perform an action) and transforming abstract motor goals (i.e., representations of intended action outcomes before effector selection) into executable movement plans1. Planning may unfold sequentially: first, an abstract goal is represented; next, the effector is selected; and finally, the movement is specified2. Prior fMRI studies have shown that motor goals can be represented in effector-specific regions before movement details are specified1. However, it remains unclear where motor goals are represented in the brain prior to effector selection and how these representations are transformed into effector- and movement-specific codes.

Time-resolved multivoxel pattern analysis (MVPA) in delayed grip-force paradigms has been essential for testing where and when action-specific information is represented and how it is transformed during motor planning3 4. In a recent fMRI study, intended grip-force intensities were decoded from multivariate activation patterns in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) during early action selection, and from the dorsal premotor cortex (PMd), reflecting a transformation to movement-specific codes3. A second study showed that contralateral sections of the intraparietal sulcus (IPS) represent force intensities in an effector-specific format during early motor planning4. However, because effector-specific information was always available, these studies could not determine where force intensities are represented in an abstract, effector-independent format during early motor planning, nor how they are transformed into effector- and movement-specific codes.

To address these questions, the present fMRI study employed a modified delayed grip-force task with time-resolved MVPA and cross-decoding analyses.

Methods

N = 22 right-handed participants were included in the analysis (age 28.9 ± 5.53; 11 female). 

Before MRI, human participants completed a training session where grip-force transducers were calibrated to their maximum force, and they were familiarized with four force intensities (1: 4.15–20.85%; 2: 29.15–45.85%; 3: 54.15–70.85%; 4: 79.15–95.85% of maximum force). During fMRI, participants performed a delayed grip-force task. Each trial began with a Grip-force cue specifying two intensities to maintain during a 5-second Delay 1, followed by an Effector cue indicating which hand would prepare which force during a 5-s Delay 2. Participants then applied the prepared forces simultaneously for 1.5 s, and performance accuracy was calculated as the mean force during the final 0.75 s. Each participant completed four runs of 48 trials, with each experimental condition (grip-force intensity × hand) presented 12 times per run.

fMRI data were acquired on a 3T Siemens Prisma at the Center for Cognitive Neuroscience Berlin (CCNB), using an EPI sequence (64-channel head coil; 48 slices, interleaved; TR = 1 s; 2 × 2 × 2 mm voxel size; multiband factor 3), yielding 1000 functional images per run. Trial onsets were time-locked to image acquisition for time-resolved analysis, and preprocessing was limited to spatial realignment to preserve the spatiotemporal structure of the fMRI data. A finite impulse response (FIR) model estimated run-wise beta values for each 1-s time bin (6 bins for Delay 1, Delay 2, and execution, and 2 for the ITI). High-pass–filtered data (cut-off 192 s) entered the first-level GLM, modelling each condition.

To identify when and where force intensities were parametrically coded, we applied time-resolved support vector regression (SVR)3 4. Beta estimates were z-scored and a four-fold leave-one-run-out cross-validation schema used. A searchlight (r = 4 voxels) was applied at each time bin to generate whole-brain prediction accuracy maps. Two SVRs (one per hand) predicted the four grip-force intensities from multivoxel activation patterns, with label distances adjusted via Fechner’s law to account for differences in perceived difficulty3. Accuracy maps thus reflected local activation patterns encoding abstract or effector-specific force intensities. Accuracy maps were normalized, smoothed, and entered a second-level flexible factorial design, in terms of SPM implementation of an ANOVA, with t-contrasts testing above-chance decoding across time bins (at p < 0.05 FWE-corrected at the voxel level).

We then conducted cross-decoding analyses to identify regions representing grip-force intensity in an effector-independent format. Using the same FIR model as the main analysis, SVRs were trained on beta estimates reflecting one hand and tested on the other4. Accuracy maps entered a second-level design modelling time bins (t1–t20); t-contrasts tested above-chance decoding in the second half of each period. An additional conjunction analysis identified regions coding force intensities in an abstract format across delays, including maps from main decoding (Delay 1) and cross-decoding (both delays). The second-level design included three factors (main decoding, Delay 1, cross-decoding Delays 1–2). T-contrasts tested above-chance decoding, and conjunction analysis tested against a conjunction null hypothesis revealed regions encoding force intensities in an abstract format (p < 0.05, FWE corrected).

Finally, cross-regression decoding assessed whether parametric codes during Delays 1 and 2 resembled movement-specific codes during execution. SVRs were trained and tested on all combinations of time bins from the second half of each period, yielding 81 cross-regression maps per hand. Maps were averaged into 9 training × testing period combinations and entered two second-level ANOVAs (one per hand) with training and testing as 3-level factors. T-contrasts assessed above-chance decoding for each training–testing pair (p < 0.05, FWE corrected).

Results

Twenty-two participants applied grip force accurately in 62 ± 18.5% of trials, with performance consistent across runs, indicating no training or fatigue effects. A 2 × 4 repeated-measures ANOVA showed no main effect of hand, but a significant effect of force intensity, and a hand × intensity interaction; post hoc tests revealed that the lowest force was easier than higher intensities for both hands. Fechner’s correction was applied to account for subjectively perceived differences in force intensities in the neuroimaging analyses3. All trials were included in MVPA analyses.

To identify regions parametrically coding force intensities, we conducted time-resolved SVR. T-contrasts were computed for Delay 1 (t5–t7) and Delay 2 (t11–t13) using the second half of each period. During Delay 1, i.e., before effector specification, a single t-contrast across hands revealed above-chance decoding in the PCu. In Delay 2, after specification, t-contrasts computed independently for each hand revealed lateralized clusters in contralateral l-pIPS/r-pIPS, l-aIPS/r-aIPS, l-S1/r-S1, and l-PMd/r-PMd. During execution (t17–t19), higher prediction accuracy was observed in contralateral motor cortices. These findings indicate a transformation from effector-independent representations of grip-force intensity in the PCu to effector-specific parametric coding in contralateral IPS and PMd.

To identify brain regions representing grip-force intensity in an effector-independent format, we conducted a whole-brain cross-decoding analysis. SVRs were trained on beta estimates reflecting preparation with one hand and tested on the other, then vice versa. T-contrasts were computed for each delay on the same time bins as the main decoding. Consistent with the main analysis, a single bilateral cluster in the PCu showed robust above-chance decoding in both delays (p < 0.05, FWE-corrected). Above-chance decoding during Delay 1 likely reflects abstract, effector-independent coding of force intensity, while decoding during Delay 2 either abstract coding or simultaneous representation of intensities for both hands.

To test the stability of abstract codes of force intensities, we performed a conjunction analysis combining maps from cross-decoding (Delays 1 and 2) and main decoding (Delay 1). T-contrasts and conjunction analysis revealed the PCu (at p < 0.05, FWE-corrected). These results indicate that parametric codes in the PCu represent force intensities in a similar, abstract format throughout delays. Together, the main and cross-decoding analyses provide converging evidence that the PCu represents force intensities in an abstract, effector-independent format during motor planning.

To assess whether multivariate patterns of grip-force intensity during Delays 1 and 2 resembled those during execution, we conducted whole-brain cross-regression decoding. T-contrasts were computed separately for each hand (at p < 0.05, FWE-corrected). Cross-regression between Delay 1 and execution revealed no regions with above-chance decoding, suggesting that abstract representations in the early delay are transformed into a different code during movement execution. In contrast, cross-regression between Delays 1 and 2 showed above-chance decoding in the bilateral PCu, indicating that an abstract, effector-independent code is maintained across delays. In contrast, cross-regression between Delay 2 and execution revealed lateralized clusters in contralateral aIPS and PMd for each hand, indicating that movement-specific codes of force intensities can be found in a late motor planning phase. This result indicates that across delays, the neural representation of intended force intensity transitions from an abstract code in the PCu to a movement-specific format representation in contralateral aIPS and PMd. Together, these findings indicate a transformation from abstract to effector- and movement-specific format representations during motor planning.

Conclusion

In this fMRI study, we investigated how intended grip-force intensities are encoded and transformed across successive stages of motor planning. Using a delayed grip-force task combined with time-resolved SVR, we tested whether parametric codes of grip-force intensities during a first delay period (preceding effector specification) are represented in an abstract, effector-independent format, and how these representations are subsequently transformed into effector- and movement-specific codes during a second delay. Our paradigm allowed us to separately examine where abstract codes of force intensity are represented and where they are later represented into effector- and movement-specific codes.

During Delay 1, graded force intensities could be decoded from the PCu. Previous time-resolved MVPA studies using delayed-response paradigms have shown that the PCu contributes to planning hand movements during delay periods preceding goal-directed actions1. Crucially, above-chance decoding in the PCu has been found prior to effector specification, indicating that medial PPC regions encode effector-independent information during early motor planning. Consistent with these results, we found that graded force intensities were parametrically coded in the PCu in an abstract, effector-independent format, preceding effector- and movement-specific representations in contralateral aIPS and PMd. This suggests that above-chance decoding in the PCu reflects an initial stage of motor planning in which abstract action features (e.g., intended force intensity) are encoded before being transformed into effector- and movement-specific codes.

During Delay 2, above-chance decoding was systematically lateralized to contralateral aIPS and PMd, reflecting effector-specific coding. The IPS has been consistently found to parametrically represent anticipated force intensities for each hand, reflecting its role in transforming abstract motor goals into effector-specific plans3. In contrast, the PMd is typically associated with movement-specific coding1. For example, fMRI–EMG studies show strong correlations between PMd activity and MEp amplitudes during imagery of grip-force intensities, reflecting encoding of muscle contraction levels. Complementing this, rTMS applied to the l-PMd prior to a delayed grip-force task disrupts the influence of predictive cues on force execution, highlighting its anticipatory role5. Time-resolved SVR and cross-regression decoding showed that parametric codes in PMd progressively resemble those found during execution3. These results indicate that PMd refines effector-specific information (initially encoded in aIPS) into detailed movement plans and anticipates concrete motor parameters required for execution.

Cross-decoding analyses revealed stable, effector-independent representations in the PCu across delays, whereas cross-regression decoding showed that these abstract representations did not generalize to execution. Temporal generalization between Delay 2 and execution yielded similar lateralized patterns in aIPS and PMd, indicating that movement-specific codes emerge during late planning. Together, these findings provide strong evidence for a two-stage transformation process: abstract, hand-independent representations in the PCu are progressively reformatted into effector- and movement-specific codes in aIPS and PMd.

Together, our results offer novel evidence on how intended force intensities are represented and transformed across successive stages of motor planning. Initially encoded in the PCu in an abstract, effector-independent format, force intensities are subsequently transformed into effector- and movement-specific codes in aIPS and PMd. These findings support a two-stage model of motor planning2, highlight the temporal and functional specialization of PCu and PMd, and provide a detailed framework for understanding how abstract intentions and motor goals are progressively transformed into executable movement plans.
