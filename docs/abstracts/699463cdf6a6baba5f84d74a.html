Introduction

For decades, visuospatial attention has been mainly described through a cortico-centric lens, emphasizing cortical Dorsal Attention Network and Ventral Attention Network as the core substrates of attentional orienting and reorienting processes1. Yet, abundant evidence points to a role of subcortical structures, such as the pulvinar, the largest thalamic nucleus, which has extensive bidirectional connection to virtually the entire cortex2. These reciprocal (feedforward and feedback) pulvino-cortical loops may regulate attentional filtering and maintenance of task-relevant information, thereby influencing both attention and working memory2. 

A well-known behavioral aspect of spatial attention is the ‘pseudoneglect’ phenomenon: healthy people tend to exhibit a subtle yet systematic spatial bias toward one hemispace, typically leftward3. Neural mechanisms underlying this bias remain unresolved. We therefore investigated whether resting-state pulvino-cortical functional connectivity is associated with the magnitude of this spatial bias in healthy participants, providing novel insights into the subcortical contributions to visuospatial attention.

Methods

Fifteen healthy participants, all right-handed, completed a behavioral Landmark task and a MRI scanning session. The Landmark task was adapted from Mitchell, 20203. Stimuli consisted of horizontal lines with three possible full lengths (95, 150 or 195 mm), bisected by a vertical marker, yielding either symmetric or asymmetric left/right segments. Segment-length asymmetry was manipulated at 0% (equal in 162 trials), 10%, 15% or 20%, with the longer segment appearing either on the left or right side (respectively 27 trials per line length for each side, totaling 81 trials). Participants indicated which segment was longer (left or right). For equal trials, participants were instructed to withhold responses.  

Behavioral quantification of spatial bias:

Individual spatial bias was quantified using Signal Detection Theory (SDT). For each participant, we computed their response criterion separately for trials in which the left segment was longer (CL) and trials in which the right segment was longer (CR) :  

CL or R = -0.5 [ Z(HitL or R) + Z(FAL or R) ]

where the HitL is the proportion of correct ‘left-longer’ responses (similarly HitR for right-longer trials), and FAL stands for the false alarm rate which is the proportion of incorrect "left-longer" responses across all trials (respectively “right-longer” responses for FAR). Hit and false-alarm rates were z-transformed prior to criterion computation. Spatial bias magnitude was defined as the absolute difference in response criteria :

|CLR| = |CL - CR|;

where larger values indicate greater asymmetry in perceptual decision criteria between hemifields. 

MRI acquisition and analysis:

Structural and resting-state fMRI data were acquired on a 3T Siemens Prisma Fit scanner (fMRI resting-state, duration = 8 min, TR: 800 ms, voxel size= 3×3×2.5 mm³ ;T1, TR: 2 s, voxel size= 0.8 mm isometric). Preprocessing and connectivity analyses were conducted using the CONN functional connectivity toolbox (v22) in Matlab-R2023b. The preprocessing pipeline included realignment, unwarping, outlier detection, normalization, and smoothing (Gaussian kernel of 4 mm FWHM). Denoising step involved regression of confounds such as white matter/CSF signal and estimated motion parameters (6 standard realignment parameters derived from SPM). We used an atlas-based approach combining bilateral pulvinar region of interest (ROI) from Krauth thalamic delineation4 with seven cortical networks from Yeo’s atlas 20115. For each participant, average BOLD timeseries were extracted from each ROI, and pairwise functional connectivity was computed using Pearson correlation scores. Correlation coefficients were then Fisher r-to-z-transformed. At the group-level, high- vs low-bias differences in ROI-to-ROI connectivity were assessed using two-sample t-tests. Left and right pulvinar4 were examined separately in relation to cortical networks5.

Results

Participants were further classified into high-bias (n=7) and low-bias groups (n=8) using median split on |CLR|. The resulting between-group difference in |CLR| showed a large effect size (Cohen's d = 2.7), confirming distinct bias magnitudes.

No significant connectivity involving the left pulvinar was observed in either group. In contrast, the right pulvinar exhibited distinct connectivity profiles depending on the magnitude of the bias, quantified by |CLR|. 

Specifically compared to the low-bias group, high-bias participants showed significantly stronger functional connectivity between the right pulvinar and DAN (t = 2.34, p<0.05), as well as between the right pulvinar and limbic network (t = 3.82, p<0.05). A significant group difference was observed between the DAN and VAN (t = −2.6, p < 0.05), indicating reduced DAN–VAN connectivity in the high-bias group compared with the low-bias group.

Conclusion

These preliminary results suggest that individual spatial attention asymmetries are reflected in differential right pulvinar connectivity patterns, supporting a subcortical contribution to visuospatial attention with right-hemisphere dominance. 

High-bias individuals might rely more heavily on pulvinar–DAN coupling, therefore building more asymmetrical internal attentional priority maps of space (e.g., favoring the left or right side), whose distribution is partly shaped by the pulvinar2. The orbitofrontal cortex (within Limbic network), involved in motivation and arousal, is also more highly coupled with the right pulvinar in the high-bias group, possibly favoring more liberal responding toward either the left or right hemifield. Finally, connectivity between the DAN and VAN was significantly reduced in the group with greater spatial attention asymmetry, which mirrors the network imbalance observed in pathological hemineglect after stroke and might reflect lower efficacy of the VAN in its role of ‘circuit-breaker’/ reorienting when the DAN is activated, e.g. after changes in attention focus across successive trials1.

Pulvino-cortical connectivity could help predict the magnitude of the spatial bias amongst healthy subjects. The pulvinar complex appears to be key to attentional control by modulating cortical-cortical communication2. These findings require confirmation from a larger cohort.
