Introduction

The human cerebral cortex follows an orderly spatial logic in which anatomical positions and functions are closely linked. This cortical topography extends from sensory maps (e.g., retinotopic or homunculus) to high-level transmodal systems such as the default-mode network (DMN), whose distant position from primary cortices supports internally generated cognition. The inferior parietal lobule (IPL) – a subregion of the DMN – lies at the junction of visual, auditory, and tactile systems, making it a primary cortical site for scientists to examine how cortical geography shapes mental functions.

Mental imagery – the voluntary simulation of sensory experience without external input – provides a powerful test case for probing this question. Although imagery engages regions overlapping with perception, most studies have focused on vision and overlooked multisensory imagery and the role of transmodal cortex in the DMN. Contemporary accounts propose that the IPL serves as a multimodal buffer, integrating and maintaining information from multiple sensory domains, yet the spatial topography of this multimodal integration in the IPL remain unclear.

To test whether the topographical arrangement of IPL subregions reflects their sensory affinities and distances from  distinct primary sensory systems, the authors used a novel fMRI paradigm including single-modality (visual, auditory, tactile) and dual-modality (audio-visual, audio-tactile, tactile-visual) imagery. Using ecologically valid and semantically rich prompts rather than minimalist stimuli to maximally engage transmodal association cortex, in the present study we examined how unimodal and multimodal imagery are processed across the parietal cortex. Complementary resting-state analyses assessed whether this task-based topography corresponds to intrinsic connectivity between sensory and heteromodal systems. Together, these approaches reveal how the parietal cortex orchestrates internally generated multisensory experience.

Methods

Participants: Twenty-four healthy, right-handed adults (mean = 28 ± 5.5 years; 9 male) took part with ethical approval from the University of Oxford. All were fluent English speakers, had no neurological or psychiatric history. All participants reported an average score of the Vividness of Visual Imagery Questionnaire (VVIQ) above 3 (96% reported a score > 3.5), indicating no aphantasia.

Design and Task: Participants completed six imagery conditions – three single-modality (visual, auditory, tactile) and three dual-modality (audio-visual, audio-tactile, tactile-visual) – plus two simple baseline perception tasks (auditory noise, visual arrow). Each trial comprised a 5 s instruction, 10 s silent imagery, and a 2.5 s vividness rating using a 4-point scale. In dual-modality trials, participants added a second sensory element on top of the single-modality imagery just created. Each condition appeared 12 times across 6 scanning runs (72 imagery periods). The two baseline tasks (noise & arrow) provided externally directed control conditions for isolating introspective imagery-related activation. Before fMRI scanning, participants practised all tasks and rated imagery vividness along visual, auditory, and tactile scales (Likert metric: 1–9); the same ratings were repeated post-scan to assess consistency.

Stimuli: Seventy-two short sentences (up to 14 words) served as imagery prompts, evenly distributed across the six conditions. A pilot study confirmed that each instruction selectively evoked significant more content in its intended modality than non-intended ones. Stimuli were presented using E-Prime 3.0; responses were made via an MR-compatible four-button box.

MRI Acquisition: Scanning was performed on a Siemens 3 T PRISMA system with a 32-channel head coil. Structural: T1-weighted MPRAGE (1 mm isotropic). Functional: multiband EPI (TR = 1355 ms, TE = 32.4 ms, voxel = 2 mm, 72 slices, multiband = 4). Resting-state: 10 min 10-s eyes-open fixation. 

Preprocessing: Data were converted to NIfTI and preprocessed in SPM12. Functional images underwent field-map correction, realignment, co-registration, MNI normalisation (2 mm voxels), and 4 mm FWHM smoothing. Low-frequency drift was removed (128 s cut-off). Resting-state data were processed in CONN, with WM/CSF and motion signals regressed out and band-pass filtered (0.008–0.09 Hz).

Statistical Analysis: A general linear model (GLM) included eight regressors (six imagery + two baseline), convolved with the canonical HRF. Motion and rating periods were modelled as nuisance regressors. Additional GLMs used parametric modulation with participants’ continuous imagery ratings (“how visual”, “how auditory”, “how tactile”) and a composite multisensory score to identify modality-specific and integrative effects. Group-level analyses were based on random-effects models of individual maps.

Region-of-Interest and Surface Analyses: The lateral parietal cortex was segmented into cytoarchitectonic ROIs using the Jülich Brain Atlas to assess modality-specific responses across the lateral parietal cortex. Complementary surface-based analyses (CAT toolbox, FreeSurfer fsaverage) verified topographic precision.

Geodesic Distance Analysis: Using the methods developed by Margulies et al. (2016), we selected the maximally responsive top 200 vertices of each type of imagery and calculated their geodesic distances to different primary sensory cortices. 

Resting-State Connectivity: Four functionally defined IPL clusters (visual, auditory, tactile, & multisensory clusters from task-based analysis) served as targets for connectivity with primary sensory seeds (V1, A1, S1) and midline DMN nodes. Fisher Z-transformed correlations quantified the correspondence between intrinsic connectivity between targets and seeds.

Meta-analytic Decoding: Unthresholded group-level maps were submitted to the NeuroSynth decoder to identify cognitive topics showing maximal spatial correspondence with imagery-evoked activation patterns.

Results

Behavioural validation: Participants generated vivid and modality-specific imagery (mean vividness ≈ 3.8 out of 4) with no reliable difference between the six conditions of imagery. Ratings before and after scanning were highly correlated (p < 10⁻⁶), confirming stable imagery performance.

Modality-specific organisation: Functional analyses revealed distinct zones of the lateral parietal lobe for different sensory modalities. The posterior parietal cortex was most responsive to visual imagery, the ventral/middle parietal cortex to auditory imagery, and the anterior parietal cortex to tactile imagery. Data-driven clustering confirmed this tripartite layout. Whole-brain results showed these modality-selective regions were confined to the left inferior parietal lobule (IPL), forming a 'posterior–ventral–anterior' tripartite structure aligned with visual, auditory, and somatosensory cortices. Results of geodesic distance showed that visual imagery is closest to V1, auditory imagery to A1, tactile imagery to S1, and multisensory imagery is further away from the primary sensory cortices. 

Parametric modulatory analysis based on continuous ratings: When behavioural ratings were used as regressors, graded activation patterns emerged along the IPL: posterior visual, mid-ventral auditory, and anterior tactile clusters partially overlapped, showing smooth transitions between modalities. A combined multisensory score is significantly associated with activity in the central section of the IPL, indicating that richer multisensory imagery engaged a higher-order hub in the IPL.

Adding new sensory elements: Dual-modality imagery, which required adding a new sensory feature to existing imagery, produced modality-dependent parietal shifts. Adding visual elements enhanced posterior IPL and occipital activity; adding auditory content activated ventral IPL and temporal areas; and adding tactile content recruited anterior IPL and supramarginal areas. These loci mirrored the single-modality pattern, indicating that imagery construction follows a spatially organised map.

Supra-additive multisensory effects: Comparing dual-modality imagery with the sum of its component conditions revealed supra-additive activity in the IPL, with loci varying by modality pair: audio-visual imagery peaked ventrally near the temporoparietal junction, audio-tactile imagery in central IPL, and tactile-visual imagery dorsally near the supramarginal gyrus. Overlapping clusters converged in the IPL’s central section, marking a transmodal zone for sensory integration.

Replication across analyses: Volume- and surface-based analyses produced consistent results such that the posterior IPL for visual imagery, ventral IPL for auditory, anterior IPL for tactile, and central IPL for multisensory imagery. This revealed a robust motif – a multimodal centre & unimodal peripheries.

Resting-state connectivity: Intrinsic connectivity during rest mirrored these task-based activations. Each parietal subregion showed strongest coupling with its corresponding sensory cortex (V1–posterior IPL; A1–ventral IPL; S1–anterior IPL), whereas the central IPL was most connected with the midline structures that belong to the DMN. This indicates that imagery-related organisation reflects underlying connectivity structure.

Meta-analytic validation: NeuroSynth decoding confirmed functional specificity such that visual imagery aligned with vision and navigation, auditory with language and speech, tactile with touch and action, and multisensory imagery with social and mentalising processes.

Summary: Across convergent analyses, mental imagery of different sensory modalities occupied distinct but adjacent parietal territories, while multisensory imagery centred on a hub surrounded by unimodal clusters. These results reveal a topographically organised scaffold in the IPL, where unimodal representations encircle a central hub in the IPL specialised for integrating imagined sensory experiences from multiple sources.

Conclusion

This study reveals that mental imagery is topographically organised within the inferior parietal lobule. Visual, auditory, and tactile imagery occupy distinct yet adjacent regions, while multisensory imagery converges in a central transmodal hub strongly connected with the default-mode network. Furthermore, the cortical loci of each type of mental imagery is spatially configured by their connectivity with and distance to different primary sensory systems – they are preferentially connected with their respective sensory cortex in the intrinsic task-free state, and they happen at a location that is physically closer to the relevant primary cortex than unrelated ones. When there is a need to create imagery involving multiple modalities, parietal activities coalesce from three sides, converging at the middle IPL locus that is further more distant from all three primary systems (V1, A1, S1). This spatial motif – a multimodal centre surrounded by unimodal peripheries – suggests that the parietal cortex integrates internally generated experiences according to its intrinsic connectivity. By linking sensory modalities, multimodal integration, the physical geometry of brain activities, and the fascinating phenomenon of mental imagery, the findings highlight topography as a key organising principle underlying how the brain constructs rich, multisensory experiences in the absence of external input.
