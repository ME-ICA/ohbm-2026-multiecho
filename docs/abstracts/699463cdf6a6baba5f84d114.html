Introduction

Plasticity in the human brain plays an important role in learning, homeostasis and has a relevant role in pathologies such as epilepsy1 or depression. It is also the putative mechanism exploited by brain stimulation therapies. While many studies exist to characterize plasticity, direct brain-wide measurements on a large cohort of patients are rare. The f-tract database2, consisting of SEEG responses to single pulse electrical stimulations (SPES) of patients with intractable epilepsy, offers the possibility of mapping brain short-term plasticity during pulse trains, and identifying regions with stronger plasticity. We show that plasticity under these conditions is a complex phenomenon to measure, appearing more frequently (but not exclusively) in the medial temporal areas of the brain, with a dominant contribution from pre-synaptic modulation. We also show that an increased short-term plasticity depends on the patient's seizure type. 

Methods

The goal is to compute the plasticity metrics for parcel pairs (stimulated-recorded), where parcels are defined according to Lausanne 2008 parcellation2, and define plasticity maps for the whole brain. We also compute aggregated scores conditioning to the stimulated or recorded parcel. 

To measure plasticity, we study the existence of trends in excitability across multiple stimulations. We select, from the f-tract database2, 734 patients aged 15-55 with refractory epilepsy, implanted with SEEG. Patients were administered a pulse train of biphasic stimulations at 1Hz, of which we select the first 15 pulses, discarding the first and 16th and further if successive if present, so 14 in total. Due to the presence of highly heterogeneous electrical artifacts which are easily mistaken for neural modulation even after data cleaning with standard techniques, channels were selected with a number of statistical criteria and we only kept channels showing: 

- strong activity after stimulation compared to the pre-stimulation baseline 

- no pre-existing strong trends in the baseline 

- absence of large artifacts. 

We remove the linear trends from the signals and use three measures of excitability: amplitude after stimulation, area difference between individual SPES responses in a train and the average CCEP, regression coefficient between individual SPES responses in a train and the CCEP . To measure the trend strength, we compute three slopes in excitability with the Theil-Sen method and we combine the three z-scores of the logarithm of the values with a correction for correlation (computed on the whole database). To compute a p-value, we use the Mann-Kendall test for trends and we combine the three values with the Stouffer method with correlation correction. These p-values have an interpretable correspondence with trend directions with 0 indicating descending trends and 1 ascending trends. We use the Cauchy combination test to aggregate p-values from a single parcel in an individual recording, in order to handle both sparsity and statistical dependence. Then we use a Stouffer's method to aggregate recordings from the same parcel pair, and a binomial test (threshold 0.2) to identify the fraction of patients with strong plasticity in a certain pair and compute the p-value. Finally, we apply Benjamini-Hochberg to all parcel pairs as FDR procedure. For trend magnitude estimation, we use an analogous pipeline, but with successive weighted averages instead. After these procedures, we have, for each parcel pair, two sparse matrices Mij and Fij for magnitudes and frequencies respectively, plus a FDR matrix FDRij. To assign scores to individual parcels, we define OUT, IN and SELF scores as averages of rows, columns and diagonal values. 

We study the association between plasticity and epilepsy subtype, defined by the seizure onset macro-regions: frontal, central, parietal, insula, temporal. We select only patients who have a single hemisphere affected by epilepsy and use a hemisphere-symmetrized analysis. Because of the frequent co-occurrence of subtypes, we both do a broad comparison between patients with a certain subtype and all those who do not, and a restricted version in which we only use patients with single epilepsy type. We use the median difference between an epilepsy subtype and baseline to quantify the effect and use Mann-Whitney test for p-values. Then, we aggregate by stimulated parcel by averaging and by the Stouffer method, for effect size and p-values respectively, and finally we apply FDR selection.

Results

We show that, as shown in previous works3, 1Hz stimulation can elicit plasticity in the 15 second window we consider in our analysis, and we note a strong consistency between plasticity in the 10-60ms and the 60-350ms intervals of the response. We have attempted to identify the direction of the trend, but the very strong correlation suggests that superposition of different sources might make these results inconclusive. 

We create plasticity maps showing both the frequency and magnitude of modulation. Hippocampus and amygdala appear to be the most plastic regions. By visually analyzing the map and, quantitatively, via rank-1 factorization, we show that plasticity is mostly associated with the stimulated parcel, rather than the recorded parcel, implying that the degree of plasticity can be, as a first approximation, predicted by the location of the stimulation alone. While we cannot identify the specific mechanism, this strongly suggests that the pre-synaptic mechanisms dominate the modulation. While previous works often focus on the general association between distance4, excitability magnitude, in our case we identify that short-term plasticity at 1Hz is localized in specific areas of the brain, with hippocampus and amygdala clearly dominating, followed by the other medial temporal area, while cortical regions show relatively weak plasticity. 

Our analysis of the correlation between the plasticity subtype and higher short-term plasticity reveals an association with the cortical macro-regions of the seizure-onset zones in central, frontal and temporal epilepsy. It should be noted that these regions already show weak plasticity, so it is not surprising that results are only significant after aggregation by stimulated parcel, with almost no p-value falling under the FDR significance threshold. This could also be due to the small size of subsamples, or to the fact that actual seizure-onset zones of different patients are smaller than the macro-regions.

Conclusion

We have shown that 1 Hz stimulation, which is often considered as a weaker protocol4 in terms of plasticity induction compared to high frequency ones (>= 10 Hz), can elicit plasticity in a short time window of 15 seconds. While other studies suggest that plasticity is mostly positively associated with proximity to the stimulation point4, we show that, with a sufficiently large sample size, we can identify areas where plasticity is more likely to be observed. However, by studying the fraction of patients showing strong plasticity, it is clear that there is a large variability among individuals, with only a few parcels showing modulation in a large fraction of patients. We have summarized these findings in maps that could be useful in the choice of targets for neurostimulation techniques4,5. Due to the known, albeit not fully understood, connection between altered plasticity and epilepsy1,3, we have studied the association of differences in plasticity with the macro zone of epileptic seizure-onset and we have found a positive one for most epilepsy subtypes.
