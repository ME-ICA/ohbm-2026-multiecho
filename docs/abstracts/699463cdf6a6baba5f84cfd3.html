Introduction

Sleep plays a vital role in memory consolidation and the maintenance of cognitive and physiological functioning. In older adulthood, sleep becomes shorter, fragmented and less efficient, and a growing body of evidence links disrupted sleep with an elevated risk of neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD). In particular, poor sleep has been associated with alterations in cortical thickness and hippocampal structure, two markers closely tied to cognitive decline and neurodegenerative progression. However, findings remain inconsistent, with a notable lack of literature on the hippocampus. These discrepancies may arise from methodological differences, as much of the existing work relies heavily on subjective measures such as perceived sleep quality or daytime sleepiness, which may be biased and correlate weakly with objective assessments. A further gap concerns how different aspects of sleep interact to influence neural outcomes. Most studies examine sleep duration or sleep continuity in isolation, despite evidence that time spent in bed does not necessarily reflect restorative sleep. Two individuals may spend a similar amount of time in bed yet differ substantially in fragmentation or wakefulness. Very few studies have examined whether sleep continuity modifies the relationship between time in bed and cortical structure, a factor that may account for inconsistencies in prior findings. To address these gaps, we investigated how objective and subjective sleep measures relate to cortical and hippocampal thickness, and whether sleep continuity, indexed by sleep fragmentation (SF) and wake after sleep onset (WASO), alters the association between time in bed and cortical thickness.

Methods

Participants were drawn from the PRe-symptomatic EValuation of Experimental or Novel Treatments for AD (PREVENT-AD) Registered repository available at https://registeredpreventad.loris.ca, which comprises of cognitively normal older adults with a family history of sporadic AD, aged 55 years and older. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data was obtained from T1-weighted structural images using a 3T scanner and preprocessed using FreeSurfer 7.4.1 with default recon-all options. Objective sleep was assessed via actigraphy in 72 participants for cortical thickness and 73 participants for hippocampal thickness. For subjective sleep measures, 274 participants completed the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) and Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS). Associations between sleep measures and vertex-wise cortical or hippocampal thickness were examined using vertex-wise mixed-effects models, with Random Field Theory (RFT) cluster correction for multiple comparisons and age and sex as covariates. Significant clusters were extracted as regions of interest, and mean thickness within each cluster was modelled using linear mixed-effects models to account for some repeated measures. Moderation analyses tested whether sleep continuity (indexed by SF or WASO) altered the relationship between time in bed and cortical thickness. Significance was set at p < 0.05 after correction.

Results

For subjective measures, poorer self-reported sleep quality (PSQI) was associated with decreased thickness in the left superior frontal cortex (cluster PRFT = .007) whereas higher daytime sleepiness (ESS) was linked to increased hippocampal thickness in the left CA2 subfield (cluster PRFT = .008). For objective measures, longer sleep time (ST) was associated with decreased thickness in the right supramarginal (cluster PRFT = .03) and right precentral gyrus (cluster PRFT = .047), longer sleep onset latency (SOL) corresponded to decreased thickness in the left posterior cingulate (cluster PRFT = .02), and higher WASO to decreased thickness in the right precuneus (cluster PRFT = .005). Longer time in bed (TIB) and sleep time was associated with reduced hippocampal thickness in the left CA1 subfield (TIB: cluster PRFT = .03; sleep time: cluster PRFT = .01). Moderation analyses revealed that the relationship between TIB and cortical thickness depended on sleep continuity. Significant positive interactions between TIB and SF were found for cortical thickness measurements in the right postcentral (cluster PRFT < .001), left superior temporal (cluster PRFT < .001), right superior parietal (cluster PRFT < .001), left superior frontal (cluster PRFT = .002), left postcentral (cluster PRFT = .002), and right middle temporal cortices (cluster PRFT = .04). For participants with high SF, longer TIB was associated with increased thickness in these regions. Similarly, interactions between TIB and WASO revealed significant positive clusters in left superior parietal (cluster PRFT < .001), left middle temporal (cluster PRFT = .002), left postcentral (cluster PRFT = .02) and right postcentral cortices (cluster PRFT = .04). For participants with high WASO, longer TIB was associated with increased thickness in these regions.

Conclusion

These findings provide new insights into how both objective and subjective sleep relate to brain structure in older adults. By incorporating actigraphy-based sleep metrics, the study moves beyond reliance on subjective reports and offers a more ecologically valid assessment of sleep. The results identify vulnerable brain regions sensitive to various sleep characteristics. Furthermore, these results showed that sleep continuity modifies the impact of time in bed on cortical structure, highlighting the protective role of time in bed in preserving cortical thickness when sleep continuity is low. Overall, the study underscores the importance of sleep continuity as a modifiable risk factor and informs interventions targeting sleep quality to maintain brain structure and, by extension, cognitive function. Future longitudinal studies with larger sample sizes are needed to determine causality.
