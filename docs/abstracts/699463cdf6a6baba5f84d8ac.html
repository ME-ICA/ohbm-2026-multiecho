Introduction

Spinal functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) has emerged as a powerful tool for non-invasive study of spinal cord function. Despite its rapid development in recent years, the technique remains mostly limited to the cervical spine and thus offers a window into the function of a relatively small portion of the cord. In contrast, fMRI of the lumbosacral spine remains largely unexplored despite the possibility to image segments of the cord receiving projections from lower limbs, pelvis, and lower back. Given the prevalence of pain conditions originating in these regions, lumbosacral fMRI has great translational potential. Here we examine neural responses in the spinal cord in health to varying intensities of noxious and non-noxious thermal stimulation of two regions of the leg, thigh (innervated via dermatome L2-L3) and shin (dermatome L4-L5).

Methods

Thirty-two healthy adult volunteers (15 females, 17 males; mean age ± standard deviation (SD) = 23 ± 6 years) completed lumbosacral spinal fMRI while undergoing two runs of a heat stimulation paradigm applied to the right leg, once on the medial thigh (innervated by dermatome L2-L3) and once on the lateral shin (dermatome L4-L5). Heat stimuli were delivered via an MRI-compatible thermal stimulator (QST Lab, www.qst-lab.eu). The task consisted of three classes of individually-thresholded stimulus intensities, including low (20/100 visual analogue scale, VAS), medium (50/100 VAS), and high (70/100 VAS). Stimuli were delivered in trains consisting of 16 trials (1s duration) of one stimulus intensity, with 'heat spikes' of 1 or 2 degrees Celsius added to half of the trials allowing maintenance of painful perception while avoiding sensitisation and skin damage, followed by a VAS rating period (8s duration). Each stimulus class was repeated 10 times, resulting in a total of 30 stimulation trains delivered in a pseudorandomised order and a total run time of approximately 15 minutes per stimulation site. A conventional spinal cord T2-weighted structural scan1 and gradient echo EPI functional data with custom shimming optimisation2 were acquired on a 3T GE Signa Premier XT System. Functional scans covered the whole lumbosacral spinal cord extending into the neighbouring spinal segmental level T12 (576 volumes, 23 slices, slice thickness = 4mm, slice gap = 1mm, TR = 1.6s, TE = 30ms, flip angle = 90°, ASSET factor = 2, FOV = 150mm, acquisition matrix = 96×96, reconstructed in-plane voxel size = 1.17x1.17mm).

Pain ratings were analysed using a 2x3 repeated measures ANOVA, with stimulation site (thigh, shin) and stimulus intensity (low, medium, high) as factors. Spinal cord functional data were motion-corrected for x- and y-translations, and smoothed with a 2D Gaussian kernel of 2mm full width at half maximum. Warping parameters for spatial normalisation were determined by segmenting and registering functional data to the PAM50 template via a subject-specific T2-weighted image, using the inferior-most part of the conus medullaris and the lumbosacral enlargement as landmarks. First-level models (FSL FEAT) included explanatory regressors for each stimulus intensity class (low, medium, high) and VAS rating periods, along with slice-wise nuisance regressors accounting for physiological noise and in-scan motion, and volume censoring regressors (DVARS > 75 percentile + 1.5 of the interquartile range). First-level contrasts explored the overall effects of stimulation (any stimulation > rest) and differences between varying stimulus intensities (medium > low, high > medium, high > low). To further investigate the relationship between pain ratings and spinal cord activation, a parametric modulation analysis was conducted. First-level models for this analysis included one regressor coding all stimulation trials, a parametric regressor representing subjective pain ratings, along with regressors for VAS rating periods and nuisance variables, as described above.

One-sample t-tests explored task-related activation related stimulus intensity at each stimulation site separately and for the average of both sites. Paired-samples t-tests compared localisation of activation related to distinct stimulation sites. Group-level inference was performed using randomise (FSL) with threshold-free cluster enhancement (5000 permutations, pFWE < 0.05).

Results

There was a significant interaction effect between stimulation site and stimulus intensity on pain ratings (F(2, 62) = 6.70, p = 0.004, ηp2 = 0.02). Ratings were significantly higher for the thigh than the shin for medium (thigh mean ± SD = 36.02 ± 11.38; shin mean ± SD = 29.34 ± 13.27; t = 3.73, p = 0.001) and high stimulus intensities (thigh mean ± SD = 58.19 ± SD = 8.93; shin mean ± SD = 52.10 ± 10.78; t = 2.96, p = 0.006), but not for low (thigh mean ± SD = 17.11 ± 8.71; shin mean ± SD = 16.47 ± 9.67; t = 0.54, p = 0.593).

A direct comparison of thigh and shin stimulation revealed a significant cluster of medial activation at spinal level T12 in response to all stimuli classes combined compared to rest. Additionally, parametric modulation analyses showed greater activation in a right ventral region of spinal level L2 to stimuli rated as less painful for thigh compared to shin stimulation (pFWE < 0.05). No other clusters survived multiple comparisons correction.

Conclusion

We demonstrated discernible patterns of spinal cord activation in response to thermal stimulation of distinct leg regions. Behavioural data confirmed the validity of our evoked pain paradigm, with reported pain increasing with stimulus intensity. However, we did not observe corresponding intensity-dependent changes at the expected levels of the cord. The observed activations localising more rostrally than anticipated based on the known dermatomal innervation patterns, may reflect some of the additional challenges related to spatial normalisation of lumbosacral data. Given the substantial individual differences in this region’s anatomy, ongoing efforts to use spinal nerve rootlets as anatomical landmarks will likely improve the precision of spinal cord functional mapping3,4. These preliminary findings contribute to the modest body of literature exploring the feasibility of lumbosacral fMRI3,5 and represent the first experimental study of evoked pain in this cord region. Although promising, our results highlight the need for continued methodological refinement to improve the precision of spinal cord fMRI and support future clinical applications.
