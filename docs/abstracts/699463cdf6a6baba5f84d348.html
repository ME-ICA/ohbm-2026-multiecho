Introduction

Habits are automatic behaviors facilitating daily activities by reducing cognitive demand and enabling efficient task performance. Yet, they can also result in behavioral inflexibility, hindering the ability to adapt to changes in the environment1. Neuroimaging studies suggest that the posterior putamen is particularly involved in habit expression in humans. However, habits are difficult to induce in humans and findings have not been consistently replicated2, highlighting the need for further investigation and paradigms that can reliably induce habits in the laboratory. Since many habitual behaviors rely on motor activity, motor learning may offer a useful framework for investigating human habit formation3. Based on this, we developed an fMRI-adapted version of our previously published motor sequence learning task (MSLT)4 to investigate the neural mechanisms underlying motor automatization.

Methods

Twenty-nine participants (55% female, mean age (SD) = 24.4 (6.4) years) completed the MSLT across two consecutive days during fMRI. In each trial, a red dot appeared at one out of four positions on the screen and participants were told to respond as fast and accurately as possible, using the corresponding button on a four-button box. The task involved two experimental conditions: In learning blocks, participants implicitly learned a fixed sequence of 12 visual stimuli, while test blocks intermittently introduced deviations from the learned sequence by omitting one item at a random position. During these skip-events, we measured whether participants correctly omitted the respective item (correct skips) or incorrectly executed the omitted response despite its absence, which we called action slips and interpreted as an indicator of behavioral inflexibility.

Results

With prolonged training, reaction times within learning blocks significantly decreased (t(28) = 16.15, p <.001), while action slips within test blocks increased (t(28) = 11.40, p <.001), indicating a shift towards automatic and inflexible responding. Whole-brain fMRI analyses revealed reduced activity in the (pre)motor cortex, anterior insula, occipital gyri, and cerebellum with prolonged training (pFWE <.05), suggesting reduced sensory and motor processing. In contrast, small-volume correction within our AAL putamen mask revealed increased activation in the bilateral posterior putamen with prolonged training (pFWE <.05). When contrasting action slips in test blocks with correct skip-events, we observed greater activation in the putamen (small-volume correction, pFWE < .05), suggesting that inflexible, habitual responding was related to activity in this region.

Conclusion

The increased activity in the posterior putamen within learning blocks underscores its central role in the transition from flexible, goal-directed control to more inflexible, automatic responding. These findings support the MSLT as a promising paradigm for investigating the mechanisms underlying the development of motor automaticity related to human habit formation.
