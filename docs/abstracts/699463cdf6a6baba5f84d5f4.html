Introduction

Population aging has shifted healthcare priorities toward preserving functional trajectories. Physio-cognitive decline syndrome (PCDS) defines a transitional state where mobility and cognitive impairments coexist in functionally independent older adults.1 As PCDS increases risks for falls, dementia, and mortality, elucidating its neurobiological basis is crucial. Neuroimaging studies indicated PCDS involves shared structural substrates within cerebellar-limbic circuits and vascular regions, reflecting converging neurodegenerative and vascular pathways.2,3 However, current assessments rely on individual biomarkers that fail to capture the aggregate brain burden. These observations suggest that PCDS is associated with accelerated brain aging, highlighting the need for a quantifiable index of cumulative structural impact.

Brain age, estimated from machine learning models trained on brain features, provided a robust signature for accelerated brain aging. The brain age gap (BAG), the difference between predicted brain age and chronological age, was associated with increased mortality, cognitive decline, and frailty.4,5 However, prior research has focused on global BAG estimates, providing limited insight into regional patterns of accelerated brain aging, particularly in populations with PCDS. Furthermore, the generalizability and reproducibility of brain age models in clinical applications remain unexplored. Cross-national validation is essential to establish brain age as a reliable clinical biomarker.

In this study, we developed global and regional brain age models using a machine learning framework to estimate individual brain age in two independent aging cohorts. A regional BAG classifier was subsequently constructed to distinguish PCDS from robust individuals, validated for generalizability across cohorts, and interpreted using explainable AI techniques.

Methods

This cross-national study utilized data from two independent Asian cohorts: the I-Lan Longitudinal Aging Study (ILAS, Taiwan) and the National Institute for Longevity Sciences-Longitudinal Study of Aging (NILS-LSA, Japan). The ILAS cohort comprised 125 participants (75 robust, 50 PCDS; mean age 72.69 years), while the NILS-LSA cohort included 211 participants (137 robust, 74 PCDS; mean age 73.27 years). Participants were categorized as robust or PCDS based on consensus criteria involving sex-stratified grip strength, walking speed (bottom 20%), and cognitive status assessed via MMSE. Additionally, a training dataset of 1,482 neurologically healthy individuals (age 18–92 years; 681 men, 801 women) was recruited from five sites in Taiwan to construct the brain age prediction models.

All T1-weighted MRI scans were processed using voxel-based morphometry in SPM12, including segmentation, spatial normalization to Montreal Neurological Institute space, and smoothing. Regional gray matter volume was extracted from 442 regions of interest (400 cortical from fine-grained parcellation, 14 subcortical from Harvard-Oxford atlas, 28 cerebellar from SUIT atlas), providing regional mean GMV and voxel-wise GMV features for model development.

Global and regional brain age prediction models were trained on the training dataset using Support Vector Regression (SVR) with a radial basis function (RBF) kernel via the Scikit-learn library. Hyperparameters (C and gamma) were optimized using a grid search within a 5-fold cross-validation framework. To address the inherent ‘regression-to-the-mean’ bias associated with age prediction, a linear regression correction procedure was applied to the predicted brain age estimates. These optimized and bias-corrected models were then applied to the two cohorts to calculate the BAG value.

Group differences in global and regional BAG between PCDS and robust groups were assessed using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), controlling for age, age², sex, education years, depression scores (CES-D), and total intracranial volume (TIV). Regions showing significant BAG differences in the ILAS cohort were selected as input features to train a regional BAG-based Support Vector Machine (SVM) classifier for PCDS identification. The classifier's performance was evaluated using 5-fold cross-validation in the ILAS dataset (training set) and externally validated in the NILS-LSA dataset (independent testing set) using Area Under the Curve (AUC) and accuracy metrics. Finally, model interpretability was assessed using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to explore the feature importance to identify key brain regions and rank the brain regions most influential in distinguishing PCDS from robust aging. The detailed analysis pipeline was illustrated in Figure 1.

Results

Global brain ages in two groups across ILAS and NILS cohorts

In the ILAS dataset, participants with PCDS exhibited significantly larger BAG (BAG = 4.38 ± 6.10) compared to the Robust group (BAG = -0.23 ± 8.21, p = 0.027; η²ₚ = 0.041). Similarly, in the NILS dataset, PCDS participants had significantly larger BAG (BAG = -1.39 ± 7.57) than the Robust group (BAG = -4.59 ± 8.66, p = 0.032; η²ₚ = 0.023) (Figure 2-A).

Regional BAG and Regional BAGs classification performance in ILAS cohort

Using regional-level brain-predicted age models, our analysis revealed a region-specific pattern of advanced aging in the PCDS group in ILAS dataset. A total of 53 brain regions exhibited significant differences in BAG between the PCDS and Robust groups after adjusting for potential confounders. These regions encompassed 35 cerebral cortical areas, primarily associated with the somatomotor network, dorsal attention network, limbic network, and default mode network. Additionally, eight subcortical regions demonstrated significant BAG differences, including the bilateral thalamus, hippocampus, pallidum, right putamen, and right accumbens. Furthermore, 10 cerebellar regions were also identified as exhibiting accelerated aging in the PCDS group (Figure 2-B). Additionally, the PCDS classifier using the BAG values of the 53 brain regions demonstrated robust performance, achieving a mean balanced accuracy of 0.750 ± 0.088, a mean accuracy of 0.768 ± 0.082, and a mean area under the curve (AUC) of 0.829 ± 0.075, compared to the classification model based on all 442 regions, which achieved a mean balanced accuracy of 0.677 ± 0.081, a mean accuracy of 0.704 ± 0.065, and a mean AUC of 0.771 ± 0.062.

Regional BAGs classification application and model interpretability with SHAP

We applied the model to the independent NILS dataset to evaluate the generalizability of the regional BAG-based classifier. The classifier maintained its predictive performance, achieving an accuracy of 0.659 and an AUC of 0.728, comparable to its performance in the ILAS dataset. Additionally, the model demonstrated a sensitivity of 0.703 and a specificity of 0.635, further supporting its ability to distinguish PCDS from Robust individuals across independent cohorts (Figure 2-C). Furthermore, we utilized SHAP values to show the top 10 most influential features in classification were located within the limbic system, default mode network, somatomotor network, dorsal attention network, and subcortical regions. Among these, the left temporal pole (limbic system) exhibited the highest feature importance, followed by the somatomotor-related regions, the right prefrontal cortex (default mode network), and dorsal attention-related regions. Furthermore, the right pallidum (subcortex network) also played a significant role in classification performance.

Conclusion

This study provided a comprehensive assessment of the association between physio-cognitive decline syndrome (PCDS) and accelerated structural brain aging. Our findings indicated that individuals with PCDS have a significantly larger brain age gap (BAG) than robust controls, suggesting an advanced deviation from typical aging trajectories. Notably, the brain regions most affected were localized within the somatomotor network, limbic system, and default mode network, as well as key subcortical structures, including the thalamus, hippocampus, and pallidum, as well as select cerebellar regions. These brain region play a central role in motor control, cognitive processing, and emotional regulation, further supporting the classification of PCDS as an integrative syndrome characterized by both physical and cognitive impairment. 

Importantly, the observed regional brain aging changes in PCDS appear distinct from those traditionally associated with Alzheimer’s disease, suggesting a unique pathophysiological mechanism underlying this condition. Additionally, our findings demonstrated the robustness and generalizability of brain age model across independent Asian cohorts and underscores the potential utility of neuroimaging-derived biomarkers in identifying individuals at risk for physio-cognitive decline.
