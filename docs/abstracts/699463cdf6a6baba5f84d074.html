Introduction

Reward-related deficits such as anhedonia and avolition are core, treatment‑resistant features across psychiatric disorders and are closely linked to neurocognitive impairments in anticipated pleasure and episodic future thinking (EFT). Convergent neuroimaging evidence implicates the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) as a key hub for valuation, positive prospection and motivational drive, however, pathway‑specific interventions targeting this key hub are scarce.

Methods

In a pre‑registered randomized double‑blind sham‑controlled rt‑fMRI neurofeedback (NF) trial (NCT06787248; n=61, final n=53), healthy participants were allocated to an active NF intervention receiving real‑time feedback from individually localized left vmPFC, or a sham control intervention receiving feedback from individually localized left primary motor cortex. Participants used positive EFT (vivid imagination of personally relevant joyful future events) to up‑regulate the target region across four NF runs, followed by a transfer run without feedback. Preregistered primary outcome was training‑induced change in vmPFC activation. Secondary outcomes were anticipated pleasure ratings and motivational effort on the Effort Expenditure for Rewards Task (EEfRT). Resting‑state fMRI pre‑ and post‑training assessed changes in vmPFC connectivity. 

Results

Compared with the sham group, the NF group rapidly acquired volitional control over vmPFC activity, showing significantly greater vmPFC up‑regulation across training runs and sustained increases during the transfer run without feedback. Neurofeedback‑induced vmPFC up‑regulation was accompanied by higher anticipated pleasure and greater regulatory efficacy. Resting‑state analyses revealed increased functional connectivity between vmPFC and putamen after training in the NF group, indicating plasticity within motivational learning circuits.

Conclusion

Results demonstrate rapid acquisition of vmPFC self-regulation and enhanced motivation through an ecologically-valid and pathway-specific strategy and underscore the therapeutic potential of personalized neurofeedback for motivational dysfunctions. Findings support further clinical translation of personalized vmPFC neurofeedback interventions in disorders characterized by anhedonia and avolition (see (1))
