Introduction

Multi-Echo Stimulated Echo (MESTIM) Radial Diffusion Spectrum Imaging (RDSI)¹˒² enables mapping of complex fiber orientations by providing a model-free estimate of the voxel-wise Orientation Distribution Function (ODF),³ which reflects the structural connectivity of the human brain. MESTIM RDSI is particularly efficient because it acquires multiple diffusion-weighted images per diffusion-encoding direction within each TR (Figure 1). Tractography relies on sharp orientation information, which requires high-quality, high-b-value images with clearly visible fiber pathways to resolve multiple fiber crossings.4

High b-value images are challenging to acquire since the diffusion gradients strongly attenuate the signal. To compensate for the signal loss, the echo time (TE) must be as short as possible to reduce further signal loss due to T2-relaxation. This, however, shortens the diffusion time (which reduces the distance the spins diffuse) leading to less pronounced fiber pathways.

In the MESTIM sequence (Figure 1), the mixing time, during which magnetization is stored along the longitudinal axis, is the primary contributor to the effective diffusion time. As a result, the molecular sampling length depends on the mixing time. Longer mixing times lead to an increased fiber anisotropy,5 but also cause signal decay. This signal decay is mostly governed by T1-relaxation. However, with the improving gradient systems and the increased SNR available at ultra-high field MRI (7 T), this signal loss can be partially compensated, enabling the use of longer mixing times for high-quality diffusion MRI at ultra-high field.

This study investigates whether increasing the mixing time of the MESTIM sequence improves fiber contrast in diffusion-weighted images, which could ultimately enhance tractography. The MESTIM sequence was tested on a 7T MRI scanner for the first time, taking advantage of its longer T1 relaxation times.

Methods

MESTIM DSI: MESTIM DSI computes the probability that within a given effective diffusion time, Δeff, 1H protons diffuse a certain distance, R, within a voxel. This average propagator, p(RΔeff,q), is computed from the diffusion images, S(q,Δeff), according to2

p(RΔeff,q)≈ ∫ S(q,Δeff)e-i2πRΔeffΔeff dΔeff

For MESTIM DSI, the effective diffusion time is mostly dominated by the mixing time, TM, since Δeff=TM+TE/2. Hence, increasing the mixing time in the MESTIM sequence increases the effective diffusion sampling length.

Image acquisition: After obtaining written informed consent of two volunteers, in vivo imaging was performed on an investigational 7T MRI (Siemens Healthineers Terra.X Impulse Edition, Forchheim, Germany) system equipped with a 32-channel 1H head coil (Quality Electrodynamics (QED), Mayfield Heights, OH, USA). Two data sets were collected with mixing times (TM) of 40 and 48 ms, respectively, and bmax= 4100 s/mm2 radius 4 half sphere, 63 diffusion gradient directions with four b=0 (b0) images, 252 q-space points in total). The TE  (time between the center of the last RF excitation pulse and the center of the stimulated echo readout ) was 42 ms for each readout with receiver bandwidth 1600 Hz/px, 2.5×2.5×2.0 mm3 resolution, 34 slices, multi-band factor 2 and 5/8 partial Fourier, leading to a total acquisition time (TA) of 5:09 min.

Image reconstruction: The images were reconstructed offline using in house software (Matlab R2024b, Mathworks).2,5

Results

Overall, images acquired with longer mixing times appear sharper, with fiber tracks that are more clearly delineated (Figure 2). This effect was consistent across all diffusion-weighted images but was most pronounced at the highest diffusion weighting (b4). In particular, for TM = 48 ms, fiber pathways appear finer and more distinct, resulting in improved overall visibility.

Conclusion

We demonstrated that increasing the mixing time in MESTIM DSI improves the visibility of fiber pathways compared with shorter mixing times. This occurs because water molecules diffuse over longer distances and encounter more structural barriers, increasing the apparent anisotropy. In other words, the extended diffusion time allows protons to probe a larger portion of the fiber architecture, resulting in more pronounced fiber tracts (Figure 2).

Although enhanced signal along fibers could theoretically arise from motion, we examined all diffusion directions and consistently found that TM = 48 ms produced finer, more distinct fiber pathways that better follow the underlying tract structure.

Because stimulated echoes experience T1 relaxation, the MESTIM diffusion approach is particularly well suited for ultra-high-field (7 T) imaging, where longer T1 values help compensate for signal loss.

We performed MESTIM DSI on a 7T MRI scanner for the first time. Longer mixing times improve the visibility of fiber pathways in diffusion-weighted images by enhancing anisotropic contrast along the fibers and reducing blurring. Future work will compare the resulting ODFs obtained at different mixing times.
