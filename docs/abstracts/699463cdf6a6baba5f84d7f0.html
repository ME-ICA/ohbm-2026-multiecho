Introduction

Psychosis is a disabling syndrome that typically emerges in youth, and affects over 20 million people worldwide. Antipsychotic medications remain the standard treatment but are ineffective or poorly tolerated in approximately 40% of patients. Given the need for better treatment, studies have investigated transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), a noninvasive therapy that targets different cortical regions for different disorders.

TMS is a noninvasive neuromodulation therapy with FDA-cleared targets for several neuropsychiatric disorders, including depression, nicotine addiction, and obsessive–compulsive disorder. However, efforts to treat core psychotic symptoms—delusions, hallucinations, and disorganized thinking—have targeted heterogeneous cortical regions, yielded mixed results, and been limited by variability in targeting and study design. Although TMS has demonstrated safety and tolerability in psychosis, no stimulation target has been reliably associated with improvement specific to psychosis, highlighting the need for mechanistically grounded targeting strategies.

Lesions provide causal evidence linking brain regions to symptoms, making them uniquely valuable for identifying stimulation targets. If lesions that cause the same symptom occur in different brain regions, these regions may be part of a network involved in modulating that symptom. Lesion network mapping (LNM) maps lesions onto a normative functional connectome to identify common functional connections (here termed circuits) associated with a given symptom. This approach has successfully informed stimulation targets across multiple neuropsychiatric conditions. Here, we integrate lesion-induced psychosis with convergent interventional data from DBS, TMS, and ECT to test whether diverse causal perturbations converge on a shared functional circuit for psychosis and whether engagement of this circuit predicts clinical outcomes.

Methods

Methods:

Lesions: We performed a systematic review and analyzed 153 published cases of new-onset lesion-induced psychosis. Each lesion’s normative functional connectivity (rsFC) map was computed using a healthy connectome (n=1000). Sensitivity was assessed by overlap of thresholded rsFC maps; specificity was tested using voxelwise two-sample t-test (Bonferroni method for multiple comparisons correction) between case lesions and 1,156 control lesions not associated with psychosis. We repeated analyses excluding lesions intersecting the hippocampus to test circuit- vs direct lesional effects. Symptom subgroup analyses (delusions, hallucinations, thought disorder, negative symptoms) assessed convergence via spatial similarity metrics and leave-one-group-out tests. External validation used an independent penetrating head trauma cohort (n=181) with neurobehavioral ratings, testing whether lesion–circuit similarity related to psychosis-relevant items versus control items.

TMS: In a clinical-high-risk (CHR) cohort receiving TPJ TMS (active n=31; sham n=27), individual electric fields (E-fields) were modeled at each participant’s stimulation site and correlated with the lesion-derived psychosis circuit. A regression model tested whether conversion to psychosis at 1-year follow-up depended on circuit engagement. In a separate accelerated iTBS pilot comparing rmPFC vs DLPFC targeting in schizophrenia (interim n=20), targets were defined using standardized scalp coordinates based on a target derived from our lesion data (rmPFC approximately midway between PFz and AFz, 10–20 EEG).

DBS: We performed a systematic review of all cases of DBS either causing psychosis or used to treat psychosis. In 36 cases with psychosis-related outcomes, electrodes  and stimulation volumes were reconstructed using Lead-DBS. Within-subject comparisons tested whether settings associated with psychosis were more connected to the lesion-derived circuit than settings not associated with psychosis. In cases of DBS used to treat psychosis, a LNM analysis similar to above was performed and psychosis outcomes were correlated with strength of rsFC between the DBS stimulation volume and the lesion-derived functional circuit.

ECT: In 52 patients with schizophrenia receiving ECT from two different cohorts (n=24 from NTNU, Norway; n= 28 from NIMHAS, India) pre/post structural MRI was processed using convergent pipelines (CAT12 for volume-based analyses; FreeSurfer longitudinal for surface-based analyses; Freesurfer NextBrain for ROI based analyses; hippocampal subfields via ASHS with anatomist review). Linear mixed-effects models tested whether symptom change was associated with structural change within the lesion-derived circuit.

Results

Across lesion cases, psychosis localized to a common hippocampal-centered circuit with peak convergence in the posterior subiculum: 84% (129/153) of lesions showed connectivity to this locus, and voxelwise specificity versus control lesions peaked in the same region (pFWE<0.0005). Excluding lesions intersecting the hippocampus yielded a highly similar circuit (spatial r=0.98), supporting a circuit-level effect rather than direct hippocampal damage. Symptom subgroup maps were strongly similar (median spatial r= 0.84); by contrast, lesions producing isolated simple auditory hallucinations mapped preferentially to primary/subcortical auditory pathways rather than the psychosis circuit.

In the independent penetrating head trauma cohort, lesion–circuit similarity was selectively associated with psychosis-relevant symptom ratings (suspiciousness p=0.025; unusual thought content p=0.046) relative to nonpsychotic control symptoms.

In TMS, the effect on 1-year conversion in the CHR cohort depended on stimulation-site connectivity to the psychosis circuit (interaction beta = -1.61, p=0.0017). Furthermore, in the prospective iTBS pilot, rmPFC stimulation resulted in greater reduction of positive symptoms compared to DLPFC sites (interim n=20, p=0.05, Cohen’s d=0.87).

In DBS, settings associated with psychosis were more connected to the lesion-derived circuit than non-psychosis settings within the same individuals (p=0.0039). In ECT, gray-matter change within the circuit predicted symptom improvement (p=0.0014), with convergent signals across volumetric, surface-based, and hippocampal subfield analyses.

Conclusion

Lesions, DBS, TMS, and ECT converge on a shared functional circuit centered on posterior hippocampal/subicular connectivity that is causally linked to psychosis. These multimodal results provide converging causal validation for a hippocampal-centered psychosis circuit. The findings are currently being tested in circuit-targeted TMS stimulation trials in schizophrenia.
