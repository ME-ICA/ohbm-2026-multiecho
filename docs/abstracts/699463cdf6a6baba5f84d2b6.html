Introduction

Craving, defined as an intense desire or urge to engage in specific motivated behaviors such as substance use, is a hallmark feature of substance use disorders and is strongly linked to poorer treatment outcomes and elevated relapse risk.1 Consequently, identifying reliable biomarkers of craving has become a major goal in addiction neuroscience.

While functional neuroimaging studies have consistently linked craving to large-scale brain network alterations, most prior work has focused on group-level effects, limiting their translational potential for individual-level prediction. Connectome-based Predictive Modeling (CPM)2 is a machine learning approach that leverages whole-brain functional connectivity to predict individual differences in behavior, such as craving. 

Prior CPM studies predicted craving during cue-reactivity tasks, yet craving can also arise spontaneously without external triggers.3 Resting-state fMRI captures such intrinsic neural processes and may reflect baseline vulnerability to craving. Importantly, extending prediction to transdiagnostic samples is a critical next step, as different SUDs share overlapping neural mechanisms.4 Transdiagnostic modeling therefore enables the identification of common circuit-level predictors of craving beyond substance-specific biomarkers.

Consequently, in the present study, we applied CPM in a transdiagnostic SUD sample of individuals with cannabis, opioid, and tobacco use disorder to identify a functional connectivity network predictive of craving. We validated this network in an independent cohort of patients with alcohol dependence and tested its sensitivity to craving fluctuations in a second independent sample of tobacco users.

Methods

Participants. A total of 78 non-detoxified individuals with SUD (mean age = 35.51 years, 31 females) meeting at least three ICD-10 criteria for cannabis (CUD), tobacco (TUD), or opioid use disorder (OUD) were recruited. The final transdiagnostic SUD sample comprised 44 participants with CUD, 18 with TUD, and 16 with OUD.

Craving assessment. Craving was assessed using the Mannheim Craving Scale (MaCS)5, a reliable and valid self-report instrument assessing substance-specific craving over the past seven days. 

MRI acquisition. MRI data were acquired on 3T Siemens Magnetom Prisma scanners using 64-channel head coils. Preprocessing included motion correction, slice timing and distortion correction, ICA-based denoising (ICA-AROMA), spatial smoothing, grand mean scaling, temporal filtering, spatial normalization and registration to the MNI152 template. Manual quality control assessed temporal signal-to-noise ratio, head motion, and registration to the T1 image.

Network construction. Functional brain networks were constructed by calculating the Pearson correlation between the time series of all pairs of regions based on the Brainnetome Atlas. Correlation coefficients were normalized using Fisher’s r-to-z transform.

Connectome-based predictive modelling. We applied CPM2 to identify functional connections predictive of craving. In the training set, each edge was correlated with craving scores, and edges surpassing a threshold (p < .005) were used to construct positive and negative predictive networks. The positive network comprised edges where stronger connectivity was associated with higher craving, whereas the negative network comprised edges where weaker connectivity was associated with higher craving. Network strength scores were computed for each subject by summing connectivity values across selected edges. Prediction performance was evaluated using leave-one-out cross-validation. Model accuracy was quantified by computing Pearson’s and Spearman’s correlation between true and predicted scores. Statistical significance was determined via 10,000 permutation tests. Control analyses were conducted using alternative feature-selection thresholds (p < .01 and p < .001) and by adjusting for potential confounders (substance class, age, sex, site, education, smoking severity, head motion).

Computational lesion analysis. To assess the contribution of individual brain regions and networks to craving prediction, we conducted node-wise and network-wise lesion analyses. In the node-wise approach, each of the 246 regions was systematically removed from the connectivity matrix and CPM was rerun. For network-wise lesioning, regions were grouped into eight large-scale networks, and each network was removed separately. Changes in prediction performance between the full and lesioned models, tested with Steiger’s Z test, quantified regional and network importance.

External validation. We tested the craving network in two independent samples. To assess generalizability across substances, we applied the network to patients with alcohol dependence (n = 41) who underwent resting-state fMRI and reported current alcohol craving. To test sensitivity to craving state fluctuations, we examined subjects with tobacco use disorder (n = 28) who were scanned twice—once in a sated state and once after ≥10 hours of abstinence—with craving assessed at both time points. All fMRI data were preprocessed identically to the discovery sample. Identified craving networks were used as masks to extract connectivity strength, which was entered into linear models to predict craving. Prediction accuracy was assessed using Spearman’s and Pearson’s correlations, with significance tested via 10,000 permutations.

Results

CPM results. Both the positive and negative networks significantly predicted self-reported craving across SUDs (positive network: rho = 0.27, r = 0.27, p = .017; negative network: rho = 0.22, r = 0.24, p = .046). The combined network demonstrated the strongest predictive performance (rho = 0.42, r = 0.42, p = .003; Fig. 1A). Results were robust across different feature-selection thresholds and after adjustment for substance class, age, sex, site, education, smoking severity, and head motion.

Network characterization. The combined craving network comprised 79 edges connecting 77 nodes, with 12 positive and 67 negative connections (Fig. 1B). The right medial OFC emerged as a hub, accounting for 67.09% of all network connections.

Computational lesion analysis. Node-wise lesioning revealed the largest drop in prediction performance after removal of the right medial OFC. Additional significant reductions were observed after lesioning the left lateral middle frontal gyrus, right dorsal posterior cingulate cortex, left lateral superior parietal lobule, bilateral ventromedial parieto-occipital sulcus, and left rostral lingual gyrus (Fig. 1C). Network-wise lesioning showed that removing the limbic network or the DMN significantly reduced prediction performance.

External validation I: Alcohol dependence. In an independent sample of patients with alcohol dependence, the identified craving network significantly predicted cognitive aspects of craving (rho = 0.33, r = 0.28, p = .018). This effect was driven by the positive network (rho = 0.33, r = 0.28, p = .018), while the negative network showed no association (rho = -0.03, r = 0.01, p = .586). In contrast, motivational aspects of craving were significantly predicted by the overall craving network (rho = 0.29, r = 0.32, p = .033). This effect, however, was driven by the negative network (rho = 0.30, r = 0.29, p = .027), while the positive network showed no association (rho = -0.01, r = 0.13, p = .503).

External validation II: Craving state sensitivity. In an independent sample of tobacco users during experimentally induced craving, the craving network significantly predicted overall nicotine craving (QSU-G total score: rho = 0.39, r = 0.24, p = .019), as well as the desire/intention to smoke (QSU-G Factor 1: rho = 0.34, r = 0.24, p = .036). Importantly, state-related changes in network connectivity from satiety to craving predicted corresponding changes in overall craving (QSU-G total score: rho = 0.53, r = 0.64, p = .018) and the desire/intention to smoke (QSU-G Factor 1: rho = 0.41, r = 0.50, p = .048). No significant effects were observed for anticipated relief from negative affect.

Conclusion

Using connectome-based predictive modeling (CPM), this study identified a transdiagnostic resting-state functional connectivity network that predicts subjective craving across individuals with cannabis, opioid, and tobacco use disorder. The network generalized to independent samples of alcohol-dependent patients and smokers, supporting a shared neural signature of craving across substances.

The craving network comprised a positive and a negative subnetwork. The positive network was dominated by connections between the default mode and frontoparietal networks, suggesting that heightened craving is associated with increased coupling between self-referential and control circuits. Stronger DMN–FPN connectivity may reflect the recruitment of cognitive control resources toward craving-related internal states, thereby biasing attention toward urges and substance-related thoughts.

The negative network, was dominated by the right medial OFC, a core region of the reward circuit. Higher craving was associated with reduced OFC connectivity to visual, somato-motor, and dorsal attention networks, indicating diminished integration of valuation processes with systems that guide attention and external sensory processing. Consequently, valuation may become less constrained by top-down control and competing sensory input, allowing internally generated reward expectations to dominate and thereby sustain craving.

Validation in patients with alcohol dependence yielded two key findings. First, it demonstrated that the network generalizes beyond the substances included in the discovery sample, supporting transdiagnostic models of addiction based on shared circuit-level dysfunctions. Second, the positive network was specifically associated with cognitive preoccupation, whereas the negative network primarily indexed the motivational urge to drink, suggesting that distinct dimensions of craving may be supported by partially separable neural mechanisms. Moreover, validation in an independent sample of tobacco users showed that the combined network predicted both absolute craving after abstinence as well as changes in craving relative to a sated baseline. This state sensitivity highlights the translational potential of the network, as biomarkers tracking intra-individual fluctuations may be especially useful for just-in-time clinical interventions.

Our study has several limitations that should be considered. First, the sample size was modest, highlighting the need for larger transdiagnostic samples. Second, craving was assessed via subjective self-reports. Future studies could incorporate more objective measures, such as choice paradigms, to increase ecological validity. Lastly, the identified network does not allow conclusions about the neural mechanisms of craving in other addictive behaviors, such as behavioral addictions, eating disorders, and compulsive sexual behaviors. 

In sum, by integrating transdiagnostic modeling, cross-cohort validation, and within-subject dynamics in craving, this study provides evidence for a robust, transdiagnostic craving signature in SUDs. Future work should assess the network’s predictive utility for longitudinal outcomes such as relapse risk and treatment response.
