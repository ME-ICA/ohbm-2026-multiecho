Introduction

What happens in the brain during sleep has become a topic of growing interest, particularly in relation to brain clearance processes, which are difficult to measure directly in humans. Fluctuations in blood oxygen level dependent (BOLD) signal and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) have been proposed as a non-invasive proxy for sleep-related brain clearance. Using simultaneous EEG and fMRI, 1 introduced BOLD-CSF coupling as a marker of coordinated neurovascular and CSF dynamics during sleep. Their work suggested stronger CSF signals during non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep, particularly stage 2, although deep sleep was limited in their dataset.

Studying sleep inside the MRI scanner is technically and practically challenging, and many studies are limited to wakefulness or light sleep. People with central disorders of hypersomnolence, including narcolepsy type 1 (NT1), narcolepsy type 2 (NT2), and idiopathic hypersomnia (IH), experience excessive daytime sleepiness and fall asleep easily, making them well suited for sleep studies inside the scanner. In this study, these groups were compared with healthy controls who underwent partial sleep deprivation. Importantly, during a 30-minute nap opportunity inside the scanner, participants with central disorders of hypersomnolence were able to reach not only light sleep but also deep NREM sleep and rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, which are usually difficult to obtain during MRI scanning.

Using BOLD-CSF coupling as a proxy for brain clearance-related processes, the first aim of this study was to examine how BOLD-CSF coupling varies across sleep stages. The second aim was to assess whether BOLD-CSF coupling during sleep differs between people with central disorders of hypersomnolence and healthy controls, building on previous work showing no group differences during wake 2.

Methods

Participants and study design 

This preregistered study (osf.io/492vs/) included 97 participants: 24 with NT1, 18 with NT2, 35 with IH, and 20 sex- and age-matched healthy controls. Data were collected at two sites, Montréal (n = 49) and Amsterdam (n = 48). All participants underwent a concurrent EEG-fMRI session with a 30-minute nap opportunity inside the scanner.

EEG was recorded using MR-compatible high-density EEG (256 channels, EGI) sampled at 1000 Hz, with electrode impedance below 100 kΩ. The MRI helium pump was temporarily switched off during EEG acquisition, and signals were recorded using an online Cz reference. Gradient and ballistocardiogram artefacts were removed using BrainVision Analyser. Data were band-pass filtered (0.1-50 Hz) and downsampled to 200 Hz. Sleep was manually scored according to AASM criteria (Wake, NREM1, NREM2, NREM3, REM) in 30-second epochs. Given the challenging recording environment, staging was performed in pairs with consensus required. Four scorers (two per site) contributed and were not blinded to diagnosis to aid interpretation of atypical sleep patterns.

Definition of sleep segments for BOLD-CSF analysis
Individual epochs (30 seconds) were considered too short for stable estimation of BOLD-CSF coupling. Following Fultz and colleagues (2019)1, consecutive runs of identical sleep stages were identified, and only runs of at least four epochs (≥120 s) were retained. Shorter runs were discarded, while longer runs were split into segments of four to seven epochs (120-210 s) to maximise data retention while keeping segment lengths comparable. Each segment was treated as an independent analysis window.

MRI acquisition and BOLD-CSF coupling

Functional MRI data were acquired using harmonised protocols on a Philips Ingenia 3.0T CX scanner in Amsterdam and a Siemens 3T Magnetom Prisma Fit scanner in Montréal. The acquisition was not specifically designed for CSF inflow analyses and was analysed retrospectively; accordingly, the most inferior slice consistently intersected around the central canal rather than the fourth ventricle. At both sites, 32-channel head coils were used with comparable parameters (TR = 1650 ms, TE = 30 ms, flip angle = 72°, voxel size = 2.7 mm isotropic). High-resolution T1-weighted images (1.0 mm isotropic) were acquired for anatomical reference.

BOLD data were minimally preprocessed using fMRIPrep, including motion correction, and a cortical grey matter mask was derived from FreeSurfer segmentation. CSF signals were extracted using a manually delineated CSF mask defined on the bottom slice of the functional MRI, with conservative voxel selection to minimise partial-volume effects. Both BOLD and CSF signals were normalised, detrended, and low-pass filtered (<0.1 Hz). For the BOLD signal, the negative derivative of the global signal was computed, with negative values set to zero. BOLD-CSF coupling was quantified using cross-correlation, testing time lags from -15 to +15 s in steps of one TR (1.65 s). For each segment, the amplitude of the peak closest to zero lag was extracted as the primary outcome measure (representative example shown in Figure 1).

Statistical analysis
Statistical analyses were performed in R using linear mixed-effects regression models. The main model was specified as:

BOLD-CSF coupling amplitude ~ sleep stage + group + site + (1 | participant),

With sleep stage as a within-participant fixed effect, group as a between-participant fixed effect, site as a covariate, and participant as a random intercept. Model-based estimated marginal means and pairwise contrasts were used to assess differences between sleep stages and diagnostic groups.

Results

Sleep during the nap opportunity

Although the protocol targeted a 30-minute nap opportunity, actual acquisition times varied across participants, resulting in differences in sleep duration and the opportunity to reach deeper sleep stages. Sleep opportunity was relatively consistent, with a median of 30 minutes (IQR 5) across the whole sample. Across groups, most participants slept during the nap (defined as ≥5 minutes of sleep; 71/97 participants), although success rates varied, ranging from 19/35 participants with IH to the large majority of participants in the other groups (20/24 in NT1, 16/18 in NT2, and 16/20 in HC). Among participants who slept, median sleep duration was 20.25 minutes (IQR 7.25) in NT1, 18.25 minutes (IQR 11.38) in NT2, 17.5 minutes (IQR 7.5) in IH, and 20.25 minutes (IQR 5.75) in HC, respectively. Sleep stage distribution across the groups is shown in Figure 2A.

BOLD-CSF coupling across sleep stages

BOLD-CSF coupling was computed for each sleep segment. Random subsets of participants were visually inspected to confirm expected coupling patterns, with a representative example shown in Figure 1. In total, 97 participants contributed 632 sleep segments across all stages. Segment counts were highest for Wake (82 participants, 301 segments) and NREM2 (68 participants, 232 segments), with fewer segments for NREM1 (25 participants, 30 segments), NREM3 (18 participants, 36 segments), and REM sleep (16 participants, 33 segments). Mean BOLD-CSF coupling amplitudes increased from Wake (mean ± SD: 0.58 ± 0.21) to NREM1 (0.66 ± 0.15), NREM2 (0.66 ± 0.15), NREM3 (0.67 ± 0.13), and REM sleep (0.69 ± 0.14). Tukey-corrected pairwise contrasts of model-based estimated marginal means showed significantly higher BOLD-CSF coupling during NREM2 compared with Wake (t = -4.15, p < .001), whereas no significant differences were observed for the other sleep stages. BOLD-CSF coupling values across the sleep stages are shown in Figure 2B.

BOLD-CSF coupling in central disorders of hypersomnolence

No significant differences in BOLD-CSF coupling amplitude were observed between diagnostic groups. Mean BOLD-CSF coupling amplitudes (averaged per participant across all included segments, independent of sleep or wake) were comparable across groups (NT1: 0.64 ± 0.14; NT2: 0.65 ± 0.09; IH: 0.58 ± 0.17; HC: 0.66 ± 0.11). BOLD-CSF coupling values across the diagnostic groups and controls are shown in Figure 2C.

Conclusion

In this study, we investigated BOLD-CSF coupling during sleep using a unique simultaneous EEG-fMRI dataset acquired during a 30-minute nap opportunity inside the MRI scanner. Despite the challenging scanning environment, most participants were able to sleep, and a subset reached deep NREM and REM sleep. This allowed us to examine BOLD-CSF coupling across multiple sleep stages, and to compare coupling between people with central disorders of hypersomnolence and healthy controls.

Addressing our first research aim, we observed clear sleep-stage differences in BOLD-CSF coupling. Coupling was strongest during NREM2 sleep compared with Wake, in line with previous work by 1. Contrary to the expectation of progressively stronger coupling with deeper sleep, BOLD-CSF coupling did not further increase during NREM3 sleep. This may reflect known reductions in cerebral blood flow and lower-amplitude vascular and CSF fluctuations during deep NREM sleep, which are expected to alter BOLD-CSF correlation patterns 3. In addition, coupling estimates during NREM3 and REM sleep were based on fewer segments, limiting statistical power for detecting differences involving these stages.

With respect to our second research aim, we did not observe differences in BOLD-CSF coupling between people with NT1, NT2, IH, and controls, while correcting for the effect of sleep. This suggests that BOLD-CSF coupling, and the processes it reflects, which are proposed to relate to brain clearance mechanisms, may not be markedly altered in central disorders of hypersomnolence.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. Simultaneous EEG-fMRI recordings are inherently noisy, and sleep scoring these data remains challenging. In addition, although our dataset included deeper sleep stages that are rarely observed during MRI scanning, data availability for NREM3 and REM sleep remained limited. Additionally, CSF signals measured with standard fMRI primarily capture oscillatory CSF dynamics coupled to vascular fluctuations rather than net CSF influx or clearance 4, and BOLD-CSF coupling should therefore be interpreted as a relative measure of coordinated BOLD-CSF dynamics across sleep stages.

Together, these findings demonstrate the feasibility of studying sleep-stage-dependent BOLD-CSF coupling inside the MRI scanner and provide further evidence for a prominent role of NREM2 sleep. Ongoing and future work will extend these analyses by examining additional fMRI-derived outcomes within this dataset to further characterise sleep-related brain physiology in central disorders of hypersomnolence.
