Introduction

The primary objective of this study is to investigate neurophysiological alterations in functional brain connectivity associated with cannabis dependence using EEG data and advanced machine learning techniques. Cannabis addiction, characterized by persistent use despite negative consequences, has been linked to cognitive deficits, impaired executive functions, and increased risk of psychiatric comorbidities. Despite its prevalence, objective diagnostic tools for cannabis dependence are limited, often relying on subjective self-reports1. This study addresses this gap by examining resting-state EEG signals to identify biomarkers distinguishing cannabis-dependent individuals from healthy controls. Analysis focuses on functional connectivity using Phase Lag Index (PLI) and Weighted Phase Lag Index (WPLI) across five EEG frequency bands (delta, theta, alpha, beta, gamma), reflecting disruptions in neural synchronization and network topology due to chronic cannabis use2. Four machine learning classifiers are evaluated for accurate identification based on EEG features3: Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest, k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN), and Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM)4. Dimensionality reduction via Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and feature selection are applied to optimize performance. Additionally, topological properties of functional brain networks are analyzed using graph-theoretical measures such as connection density, clustering coefficient, and node centrality, providing insight into altered network architecture in cannabis users5. This research aims to develop a non-invasive, objective, and data-driven framework for detecting cannabis dependence, advancing understanding of its neural underpinnings, and enabling improved diagnostic tools and targeted interventions. Integrating advanced EEG analysis with multiple machine learning approaches offers both clinical relevance and mechanistic insights into cannabis-related brain alterations.

Methods

This study investigated alterations in functional brain connectivity associated with cannabis dependence by integrating EEG analysis with advanced machine learning techniques. A total of 102 participants were included, comprising 46 individuals with cannabis use disorder according to DSM-5 criteria and 56 healthy controls, matched for age and gender. Cannabis users were recruited from certified treatment centers, and informed consent was obtained from all participants following ethical guidelines. EEG acquisition used a 32-channel system following the 10–20 international system. Participants were seated comfortably, instructed to remain relaxed with eyes closed. Resting-state EEG was recorded for 5 minutes per participant at 1000 Hz, with electrode impedances below 5 kΩ. Recordings were segmented into sixty 5-second epochs to enable detailed temporal analysis and ensure statistical reliability. Preprocessing included 1–60 Hz band-pass filtering and Independent Component Analysis (ICA) to remove artifacts from eye movements, muscle activity, and environmental noise. Cleaned signals were segmented for feature extraction. Functional connectivity was assessed using Phase Lag Index (PLI) and Weighted Phase Lag Index (WPLI), which are robust to volume conduction and provide reliable estimates of inter-regional neural synchronization. Connectivity features were calculated across all channel pairs in five EEG frequency bands: delta (1–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–13 Hz), beta (13–30 Hz), and gamma (30–60 Hz), capturing both slow and fast oscillatory interactions reflecting cognitive and executive function alterations associated with chronic cannabis use. Feature selection was conducted using independent t-tests with Bonferroni correction (p < 0.06), followed by Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to reduce dimensionality and optimize classifier performance. Four machine learning algorithms were applied: Support Vector Machine (SVM) with RBF kernel, Random Forest (120 trees), k-Nearest Neighbors (k=5), and Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM). Models were trained with 5-fold cross-validation and evaluated for accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and area under the ROC curve (AUC) to ensure robustness and generalizability. Graph-theoretical analysis was performed on functional connectivity networks to examine topological differences between cannabis users and controls. Metrics including connection density, clustering coefficient, and node centrality quantified structural and organizational alterations, complementing findings from phase-based connectivity and machine learning classification. Overall, this methodology integrates rigorous EEG preprocessing, multi-band functional connectivity analysis, feature selection, robust machine learning classification, and graph-theoretical assessment. This approach allows detailed investigation of neurophysiological alterations underlying cannabis dependence and supports the development of objective, non-invasive, data-driven frameworks for diagnosing substance use disorders.

Results

The results of this study revealed significant differences in EEG-based functional connectivity between cannabis-dependent individuals and healthy controls. Functional connectivity measures derived from Weighted Phase Lag Index (WPLI) and Phase Lag Index (PLI) indicated clear distinctions, with WPLI consistently providing more discriminative information, highlighting its sensitivity to disruptions in neural phase synchronization caused by chronic cannabis use. Among the four machine learning classifiers—Support Vector Machine (SVM) with RBF kernel, Random Forest (120 trees), k-Nearest Neighbors (k=5), and Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM)—the SVM classifier using WPLI features achieved the highest performance, with 85% accuracy, 83% sensitivity, 87% specificity, and AUC of 0.89, demonstrating robust discrimination between cannabis users and controls. Random Forest reached 80% accuracy, 78% sensitivity, 82% specificity, and AUC of 0.84, while k-NN achieved 76% accuracy, 74% sensitivity, 78% specificity, and AUC of 0.80. GBM, an ensemble learning approach, achieved 82% accuracy, 80% sensitivity, 84% specificity, and AUC of 0.86, outperforming k-NN and approaching Random Forest performance. These results indicate that SVM and GBM are particularly effective in capturing non-linear patterns in EEG connectivity associated with cannabis dependence. Comparison of WPLI and PLI features showed that WPLI consistently outperformed PLI across all four classifiers, confirming the advantage of weighted phase connectivity in detecting neural synchrony disruptions. Analysis across EEG frequency bands revealed that the most discriminative features were in the delta (1–4 Hz) and gamma (30–60 Hz) bands. Cannabis users exhibited increased phase synchrony in these bands, linked to behavioral control, cognitive regulation, and impulsivity, while changes in theta, alpha, and beta bands were less pronounced. Graph-theoretical analysis further demonstrated network alterations in cannabis-dependent individuals. They displayed higher network density in delta and gamma bands, indicating hyperconnectivity, and elevated clustering coefficients in the gamma band, suggesting enhanced local connectivity as a potential compensatory mechanism. Node centrality was significantly higher in Fp1, Fp2 (prefrontal), T3 (temporal), and P4 (parietal) regions, reflecting over-recruitment and reduced network flexibility. These findings highlight both global and local network disruptions affecting executive functions, attention, and self-regulation. Overall, chronic cannabis use is associated with significant neurophysiological alterations in brain connectivity. Combining advanced EEG functional connectivity analysis with four machine learning classifiers enabled accurate discrimination between cannabis-dependent individuals and controls. SVM and GBM excelled at detecting complex, non-linear patterns, Random Forest leveraged ensemble trees, and k-NN provided a reliable baseline. These results support the use of phase-based EEG connectivity and machine learning as objective, data-driven approaches to identify neural biomarkers of cannabis dependence and facilitate non-invasive diagnostic frameworks.

Conclusion

This study provides comprehensive evidence that chronic cannabis use is associated with significant alterations in brain functional connectivity, as measured by electroencephalography (EEG). By leveraging advanced phase-based connectivity metrics, particularly the Weighted Phase Lag Index (WPLI), in combination with four machine learning classifiers—Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest, k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN), and Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM)—we successfully differentiated cannabis-dependent individuals from healthy controls with high accuracy. These results highlight EEG as a reliable, non-invasive, and cost-effective tool for the objective diagnosis of cannabis dependence, offering a data-driven complement to traditional subjective assessments such as self-reports and clinical interviews. Among the classifiers, SVM applied to WPLI features achieved the highest performance, with 85% accuracy, 83% sensitivity, 87% specificity, and an AUC of 0.89, demonstrating its effectiveness in handling high-dimensional, non-linear EEG data. Random Forest and GBM achieved robust performance (~80–82% accuracy), reflecting their ability to integrate multiple decision rules and sequential learning to capture complex connectivity patterns. k-NN showed slightly lower performance (76%), but remained valuable as a simple, distance-based approach. These results emphasize that employing multiple machine learning methods enhances predictive reliability and cross-validates neurophysiological biomarkers, improving generalizability and robustness. Neurophysiologically, cannabis-dependent participants exhibited increased phase synchrony, particularly in the delta (1–4 Hz) and gamma (30–60 Hz) frequency bands. Delta band alterations are linked to attentional deficits and self-regulation impairments, whereas gamma band hyperconnectivity likely reflects compensatory mechanisms in executive function and impulse control. Graph-theoretical analysis revealed higher connection density and clustering coefficients in cannabis users, especially in the gamma band, indicating hyperconnectivity and reduced network flexibility. Elevated node centrality was also observed in prefrontal (Fp1, Fp2), temporal (T3), and parietal (P4) regions, suggesting over-recruitment of critical areas involved in executive function, decision-making, and impulse regulation. Integrating EEG-based connectivity metrics with multiple machine learning models provides dual advantages: identification of objective, sensitive neurophysiological biomarkers and development of robust predictive classifiers. By combining SVM, Random Forest, k-NN, and GBM, both linear and non-linear patterns across different frequency bands and connectivity measures are captured, enhancing diagnostic accuracy and reproducibility. This approach moves beyond single-model diagnostics, supporting personalized assessment and potentially informing individualized therapeutic strategies based on connectivity profiles. Overall, these findings provide strong evidence that EEG-derived functional connectivity measures, particularly phase-based metrics such as WPLI, combined with robust machine learning algorithms, can serve as effective diagnostic tools for cannabis dependence. This methodology offers a non-invasive, objective, and data-driven framework for early detection, improved clinical assessment, and targeted intervention. By elucidating network-level alterations, it also contributes to a deeper understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying cannabis use and may guide the development of connectivity-based rehabilitation or neurofeedback strategies, ultimately enhancing treatment outcomes and mitigating the cognitive and behavioral consequences of chronic cannabis consumption.
