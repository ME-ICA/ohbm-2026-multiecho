Introduction

Curiosity motivates humans to seek information beyond immediate needs, shaping exploration, learning, and creativity. While dopaminergic reward systems have traditionally been implicated in curiosity, recent evidence suggests that curiosity also reflects arousal and uncertainty regulation, which are functions strongly associated with the locus coeruleus–norepinephrine (LC–NE) system. The LC provides widespread cortical neuromodulation that adjusts attentional gain, promotes exploration, and facilitates adaptive responses to salient or unpredictable events. However, how stable LC characteristics relate to individual differences in curiosity dynamics remains poorly understood. To examine this relationship, we developed the Photographic Art and Storytelling Task, designed to distinguish initial, novelty-driven curiosity from outcome-dependent changes in curiosity (Fig. 1). To examine how curiosity-related behavior relates to LC organization, participants underwent 7T resting-state fMRI scanning. LC functional connectivity gradients were then derived using connectopic mapping and trend surface modeling. 

Methods

Sixty-eight young adults completed the Photographic Art and Storytelling Task (Fig. 1). Participants first viewed a series of photographs and rated their initial curiosity for each image, reflecting novelty- and uncertainty-driven interest. They then read the opening segment of the story associated with each selected photograph. Story content was intentionally manipulated to be either interesting or boring: interesting stories incorporated personal narratives or historical context, whereas boring stories contained only descriptive information. After reading each story segment, participants re-rated their curiosity, indicating their desire to know the rest of the story (subsequent information seeking). This reappraisal phase allowed us to assess how initial curiosity was modulated by the outcome of the story (i.e., the extent of outcome-contingent change in curiosity). To quantify this effect, we computed a story bias ratio, defined as the change in curiosity ratings between the initial appraisal and the reappraisal after story reading, normalized by their sum. Larger values indicate greater outcome dependence of curiosity, regardless of direction (increase or decrease). A subset of participants (N = 39) also underwent 7 T resting-state functional MRI scans to assess LC functional connectivity. LC functional topography was estimated using a standard connectopic-mapping pipeline[1]. We applied a published LC mask[2] and cortical–subcortical parcellations[3,4], and derived voxelwise LC-to-whole-brain connectivity maps for each participant. These connectivity profiles were decomposed using Laplacian Eigenmaps to identify low-dimensional gradients representing the LC’s intrinsic organization (e.g., rostro–caudal and left–right axes). Finally, the gradient maps were summarized with third-order trend surface modeling, yielding spatial parameters that capture individual differences in LC topographic organization. These LC parameters were then related to behavioral indices of outcome-contingent modulation of curiosity, as reflected in the story bias ratio.

Results

Supporting the effectiveness of our experimental manipulation, interesting story outcomes elicited higher curiosity ratings upon reappraisal than boring outcomes (B = 2.90, 95% CI [2.62, 3.19], t = 24.84, p < .001). Initial curiosity also persisted, such that photographs initially rated as highly curiosity-evoking maintained elevated reappraisal ratings (B = 1.51, 95% CI [1.23, 1.79], t = 12.92, p < .001). A significant interaction emerged between story outcome and initial curiosity (B = –0.48, 95% CI [–0.76, –0.19], t = –2.89, p = .004), indicating that curiosity for less engaging photographs was more modifiable following story outcome exposure. To examine whether LC organization contributed to these individual differences, we related participants’ LC functional connectivity gradients to the story bias ratio, which indexes outcome-contingent modulation of curiosity. Participants with less asymmetric and more rostral-like LC connectivity patterns exhibited lower story bias ratios (Fig. 2), suggesting reduced dependence of curiosity on story outcome. This pattern indicates that LC architecture contributes to greater stability of curiosity across affective contexts, consistent with the LC’s proposed role in regulating arousal and attentional flexibility rather than encoding valence per se.

Conclusion

Our findings highlight a critical role for the LC in supporting stable, outcome-invariant curiosity. Individuals with more rostral-like and symmetrical LC functional connectivity showed weaker modulation of curiosity by outcome valence, suggesting that a more coherent LC organization buffers against affective carryover from prior experiences. In other words, when the LC’s connectivity profile favors efficient integration across its rostro–caudal axis, curiosity appears to remain driven by informational value rather than transient emotional tone. These results extend theoretical accounts of the LC-NE system as a valence-general regulator of arousal and cognitive flexibility, providing the first evidence that intrinsic LC functional architecture relates to how curiosity adapts or resists modulation following affectively salient outcomes. Importantly, these associations arise from resting-state connectivity patterns, indicating that trait-level LC organization may influence how individuals regulate curiosity in everyday contexts. Together, the findings advance an LC-centered framework of curiosity, in which LC architecture stabilizes exploratory motivation by minimizing affective interference. Future multimodal work combining high-field MRI, pupillometry, and task-based neuroimaging could directly characterize how LC–NE dynamics interact with dopaminergic circuits to shape curiosity-driven learning and exploration.
