Introduction

Reactive astrogliosis is a key feature of Alzheimer’s disease (AD), yet the physiological factors governing individual susceptibility to this neuroinflammatory process remain incompletely understood.1 Emerging evidence suggests that aquaporin-4 (AQP4)-mediated water exchange across the blood-brain barrier (BBB) is essential for maintaining cerebral fluid homeostasis and facilitating solute clearance.2 Disruption of this vascular-astrocytic interface may therefore exacerbate the brain's vulnerability to proteopathic stress. However, the in vivo relationship between BBB water exchange and astrocytic reactivity has not been directly characterized in humans. Moreover, the extent to which β-amyloid (Aβ) pathology modifies this association remains unknown. In this study, we utilized advanced multimodal neuroimaging in cognitively normal older adults to investigate these mechanisms. We primarily aimed to characterize the association between BBB water exchange and reactive astrogliosis, further testing the hypothesis that this relationship could be moderated by cerebral Aβ burden.

Methods

We evaluated a cohort of 85 cognitively normal older adults (Mean Age: 67.6 ± 7.4 years; 40 Males, 45 Females) with complete multimodal imaging and Aβ status data. The imaging protocol included: (1) 11C-labeled Pittsburgh Compound-B (11C-PIB) Positron Emission Tomography (PET) to quantify cerebral Aβ burden; (2) 18F-(S)-(2-methylpyrid-5-yl)-6-[(3-fluoro-2-hydroxy)propoxy]quinoline (18F-SMBT-1) PET, a highly selective tracer for Monoamine Oxidase-B (MAO-B), to quantify reactive astrogliosis;3 and (3) 3-Tesla Diffusion-Prepared Pseudo-Continuous Arterial Spin Labeling (DP-pCASL) MRI. By analyzing ASL signal fractions in intra- and extra-vascular compartments, we quantified the water exchange rate (kw) across the BBB as an in vivo proxy for AQP4 perivascular polarization. Pearson correlation analysis was used to evaluate direct associations between regional kw and ¹⁸F-SMBT-1 binding. General linear models were then employed to assess these regional associations within a moderation framework, testing the hypothesis that BBB water exchange efficiency interacts with Aβ burden to modify the severity of downstream neuroinflammation.

Results

Among the 85 cognitively normal older adults included in the analytic cohort, 69 were classified as Aβ-negative (Aβ-) and 16 as Aβ-positive (Aβ+). The groups were comparable in age (67.1 ± 7.5 vs. 69.4 ± 7.0 years; p = 0.26), sex (female 52.2% vs. 56.2%; p = 0.99), and education (15.8 ± 2.2 vs. 16.8 ± 3.0 years; p = 0.16). Global cerebral Aβ burden was higher in Aβ+ participants (¹¹C-PIB SUVR: 1.77 ± 0.40 vs. 1.14 ± 0.08; p < 0.001) than in Aβ- participants. Regional ¹⁸F-SMBT-1 SUVR was higher in Aβ+ participants within Default Mode Network hubs: precuneus (1.52 ± 0.15 vs. 1.72 ± 0.28; p < 0.001), inferior parietal lobe (1.36 ± 0.14 vs. 1.53 ± 0.23; p < 0.001), and isthmus cingulate (1.58 ± 0.16 vs. 1.72 ± 0.23; p = 0.007). No group differences were observed in medial temporal regions (hippocampus p = 0.98; entorhinal cortex p = 0.76). Region-wise correlations showed positive associations between ¹¹C-PIB and ¹⁸F-SMBT-1 in the precuneus (r = 0.55, p < 0.001), inferior parietal lobe (r = 0.51, p < 0.001), and isthmus cingulate (r = 0.42, p < 0.001).

To investigate the direct relationship between BBB water exchange and astrocyte reactivity, we evaluated the association between regional Kw and ¹⁸F-SMBT-1 SUVR values. Pearson correlation analysis revealed a significant negative association between BBB water exchange efficiency and reactive astrogliosis. Lower regional Kw values were consistently associated with higher ¹⁸F-SMBT-1 binding in key vulnerability hubs, including the inferior parietal lobe (r = -0.26, p = 0.01) and isthmus cingulate (r = -0.27, p = 0.01). A similar trend was observed in the precuneus (r = -0.20, p = 0.07), but it did not reach statistical significance.

To determine whether Aβ pathology modifies these associations, we next evaluated the impact of Aβ status on BBB water exchange and its relationship with astrocyte reactivity. We first found that regional Kw did not differ between Aβ- and Aβ+ groups in the inferior parietal lobe (104.6 ± 30.5 vs. 107.0 ± 38.9; p = 0.82), isthmus cingulate (105.8 ± 32.2 vs. 104.3 ± 40.0; p = 0.89), or precuneus (108.5 ± 31.3 vs. 114.3 ± 33.9; p = 0.54). Next, we applied moderation analyses to assess whether the association between BBB water exchange and astrocytic reactivity differed by Aβ status. Significant Kw × Aβ status interactions were observed for ¹⁸F-SMBT-1 binding in the inferior parietal lobe (Pinteraction = 0.009) and precuneus (Pinteraction < 0.001). Stratified analyses demonstrated strong inverse associations in the Aβ+ group (inferior parietal r = -0.65, p = 0.007; precuneus r = -0.65, p = 0.007), but no significant associations in the Aβ− group (p > 0.05). The isthmus cingulate showed no significant interaction (Pinteraction = 0.27), with a trend in Aβ+ (r = -0.44, p = 0.09) that was absent in Aβ- (Figure 1). 

Conclusion

This study establishes an in vivo link between compromised BBB water exchange (Kw) and increased reactive astrogliosis in cognitively normal older adults. We also demonstrate that this association differs in those with high versus low cerebral Aβ burden. In Aβ+ individuals, preserved BBB water exchange was linked to significantly lower astrocytic reactivity, whereas lower exchange rates correlated with increased inflammation. These observations may support a potential "resilience" model where the vascular-astrocytic interface reflects the brain's response to proteopathic stress.4 Since high Kw may reflect preserved AQP4 polarization, it may potentially facilitate solute transport and mitigate the astroglial response to Aβ burden. Notably, this interaction was absent in the Aβ- group, suggesting that variations in water exchange capacity may be particularly consequential in the presence of Aβ pathology. While longitudinal data are needed to confirm causality, these findings imply that strategies enhancing AQP4-mediated water exchange might be explored for attenuating the impact of Aβ burden on downstream neurotoxicity, potentially delaying disease progression in at-risk older adults. 5
