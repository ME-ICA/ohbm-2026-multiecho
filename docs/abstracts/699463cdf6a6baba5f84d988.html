Introduction

Interpersonal regulation is central to how close others shape each other’s affective and behavioral responses, including the escalation or de-escalate of negative social exchange^1. Sibling relationships provide a naturalistic context to study these dynamics due to their long-shared history, high familiarity, and mutual influence^2, and characteristic duality of both high intimacy and conflict. In this hyperscanning study, we combined a competitive real-time aggression paradigm with dyadic modeling to examine how adult siblings dynamically influence one another’s trial-by-trial behavioral and neural responses. 

The amygdala–medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) network constitutes the primary neural system of interest, given its established role in social information processing and emotional valence evaluation, and particular involvement in the regulation of reactive aggression.^3 It has been shown that the mPFC, with a functional gradient from dorsal (dmPFC) to ventral (vmPFC) regions, plays a central role in self–other representation and valence evaluation^4, and top-down modulation of affect^5. In parallel, the amygdala is crucial for detecting and responding to socially salient or threatening cues. 

We examine cross-brain influences within this circuit by investigating how one sibling’s dmPFC/vmPFC activity during provocation predicts the other’s amygdala response when deciding how aggressively to act on the next trial. This approach allows us to test whether siblings' neural responses dynamically shape one another, allowing us to investigate interpersonal regulation during real-time social interaction. Further, this approach enables us to test if inter-brain mechanisms are systematically influenced by dyadic, individual, and contextual factors.

Methods

A healthy sample of 50 adult sibling pairs (N = 100) completed an interactive version of the Taylor Aggression Paradigm (iTAP), a sequential aggression task in which each player repeatedly viewed the partner’s provocation level and selected their own response in the next trial. Throughout the task, we recorded simultaneous neural and behavioral data using hyperscanning to capture trial-by-trial interpersonal influence. 

Behavioral data were analyzed using actor–partner interdependence models (APIMs) with trial-wise provocation as predictors of subsequent aggression. We estimated actor effects (stability of one’s own prior aggression) and partner effects (reactivity to the partner’s provocation), and tested moderators including dyad-level mutuality (measurement of sibling intimacy, similarity, and involvement) and game-related covariates (game outcome, fake vs. interactive blocks). Neural APIMs focused on a priori regions of interest: amygdala responses during decision-making as outcome, predicted by dmPFC/ACC and vmPFC activity during provocation. We further examined moderation by dyadic mutuality to test interpersonal regulation hypotheses at the neural level.

Results

Behavioral analyses revealed significant actor and partner effects on next trial aggression. Higher actor aggression in the previous trial predicted increased aggression in the subsequent trial (actor effect: b = 0.36, t(2540) = 21.01, p < .001), indicating strong within-person stability across trials. In addition, higher provocation from the partner in the previous trial was associated with increased actor aggression on the following trial (partner effect: b = 0.25, t(2540) = 14.64, p <.001), reflecting interpersonal reactivity. Further, partner effects were significantly moderated by dyadic mutuality, were higher mean mutuality amplified sensitivity to partner provocation across trials (Partner x dyad-level mutuality: b = 0.065, t(2538) = 2.327, p = .020). Game outcome significantly moderated interpersonal aggression dynamics. Losses were associated with higher subsequent aggression and amplified reactivity to partner provocation (Partner × Loss: b = 0.14, t(2534) = 3.25, p = .001), whereas ties selectively strengthened actor-driven carryover effects, increasing the influence of one’s own prior aggression on subsequent behavior (Actor × Tie: b = 0.11, t(2534) = 2.44, p = .015). 

Neural APIMs indicate region-specific interpersonal effects within the mPFC-amygdala circuit: Greater partner dmPFC/ACC activity during provocation was associated with reduced amygdala activation in the actor at the subsequent decision (b = −0.041, t(2912) = −2.57, p = .010), whereas partner vmPFC activity showed a weaker, non-significant positive association with actor amygdala responses (b = 0.030, t(2912) = 1.88, p = .061). Actor effects differed by region, with vmPFC activity positively predicting own amygdala activation (b = 0.074, t(2912) = 4.66, p < .001), while dmPFC/ACC actor effects were not significant (p = .12). 

Conclusion

Across behavioral and neural levels, the results suggest that aggression in sibling interactions is dynamically shaped by both self-related stability and interpersonal regulation. Behaviorally, actors showed strong carryover of their own prior aggression as well as pronounced reactivity to partner provocation, indicating that aggressive responding is jointly driven by intrapersonal inertia and feedback. Importantly, partner effects varied as a function of dyadic mutuality, suggesting that relationship quality modulates sensitivity to others’ behavior during conflict. 

Neurally, interpersonal influences emerged within the amygdala–mPFC circuit in a region-specific manner. Partner dmPFC/ACC activity during provocation predicted reduced amygdala responses in the actor during subsequent decisions, consistent with an interpersonal regulatory pathway. In contrast, vmPFC effects were primarily actor-specific and showed no reliable partner modulation. We will further examine how dyadic and individual factors moderate these interpersonal neural dynamics. 

Together, these findings provide evidence for trial-by-trial interpersonal regulatory mechanisms in close relationships and highlight dissociable mPFC–amygdala pathways through which siblings influence one another’s responses during social conflict.
