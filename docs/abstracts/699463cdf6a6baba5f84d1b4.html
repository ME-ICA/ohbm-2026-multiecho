Introduction

The cerebral cortex is organized along multiple functional axes that reflect systematic variations in connectivity, cytoarchitecture, and function across the cortical surface. This organization can be captured by macroscale functional gradients: continuous dimensions describing transitions in connectivity patterns, such as those distinguishing sensory from association regions or visual from somatomotor cortices [1]. In adults, these gradients are well characterized, but their developmental origins remain poorly understood.

The earliest postnatal months represent a critical window for the emergence of large-scale brain organization, yet functional gradient analysis in early infancy has been limited by the scarcity of large, high-quality datasets and the unique challenges of infant fMRI, including motion sensitivity, low signal-to-noise, and rapidly changing anatomy.

Here, we introduce a vertex-level functional gradient framework for the infant brain, combining macroscale connectivity analysis with the Hurst exponent, a neurophysiological marker of excitation-inhibition balance, to characterize cortical organization and its underlying dynamics. Applying our infant-centric pipeline to data from the Healthy Brain and Child Development (HBCD) Study, we examine functional gradients and Hurst exponent in infants aged 0–6 months. We test whether hierarchical organization is present at birth, whether primary functional systems show accelerated early maturation, and whether functional gradient organization predicts Hurst exponent patterns and their developmental trajectories.

Methods

Data. We analyzed resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) from the HBCD 1.0 release [2], comprising over 900 scans from 466 infants aged 0–6 months. Data were processed using our infant-centric pipeline, including motion correction, EPI distortion correction, boundary-based registration to T2-weighted images, and one-step resampling to native T2w space. Denoising was then performed using high-pass filtering and ICA-AROMA [3].

Functional Gradients. Volumetric fMRI data were mapped onto the cortical surface mesh using Connectome Workbench. Functional connectivity (FC) matrices were computed based on vertex-wise time series(Figure 1a). Following [4], we retained the top 10% strongest connections for each vertex and applied diffusion map embedding to estimate the sensorimotor-association (S-A) and visual-somatosensory (V-S) functional gradients. Individual gradients were aligned to a reference template [4] using Procrustes alignment, ensuring consistent ordering and polarity of gradients across subjects. 

Hurst Exponents. To estimate cortical excitation–inhibition (E/I) balance, we computed the Hurst exponent as a neurophysiological proxy [5]. Each vertex's BOLD signal was modeled as a multivariate fractionally integrated process, with Hurst exponents estimated via univariate maximum likelihood estimation combined with discrete wavelet transforms(Figure 1a). Prior work suggests that a heightened E:I ratio reduces the Hurst exponent of a BOLD time series by flattening the 1/f slope observed in local field potentials, thereby linking increased cortical excitability to lower temporal autocorrelation [5].

Lifetime trajectories. Vertex-wise developmental trajectories were modeled using generalized additive mixed models (GAMMs). Gradient values (S-A or V-S) or Hurst exponent served as the dependent variable, modeled as a smooth function of log-transformed age using cubic splines (k = 5). A random intercept for each subject accounted for repeated measurements.

Results

Functional Gradient Differentiation. We focused on the sensorimotor–association (S-A) gradient, which captures the primary axis of functional hierarchy from primary sensorimotor to high-order association cortices, and the visual–somatomotor (V-S) gradient, which captures a key secondary axis separating visual processing from somatomotor functions.  Both functional gradients showed increasing differentiation over the first 6 months of life (Figure 1b). Vertex-wise GAMMs showed divergent age-related trends at the gradient poles for both gradients, indicating that contrast between the poles increases over time. The S-A gradient showed the strongest developmental change at the primary sensorimotor pole, while the V-S gradient showed peak changes at the visual cortex pole. Association regions consistently exhibited weaker or negative effects, which confirmed the overall increasing gradient differentiation. These developmental patterns align with the known hierarchical organization observed in the adult brain.

Hurst Exponent Trajectories. The Hurst exponent decreased significantly across the cortex from 1 week to 6 months, with the largest decreases in sensorimotor and visual regions, spatially overlapping with areas of strongest gradient maturation. Network-level analyses confirmed that somatomotor and visual networks exhibited the highest within-network variance, reflecting heterogeneous E/I development (Figure 2a). GAMM-fitted trajectories revealed a sharp initial decline in Hurst exponent across all networks, with the steepest decrease occurring in the first 6 weeks. The first derivative showed peak acceleration around 6 weeks, followed by stabilization at approximately 3 months (Figure 2c).

Gradient–E/I Coupling. Functional gradient maturation was spatially coupled with shifts in local E/I balance. Vertex-wise analyses revealed negative correlations between Hurst exponent and both S-A (r = −0.335) and V-S (r = −0.289) gradients: regions at the primary ends of both gradients exhibited lower Hurst values, indicating higher E/I ratio. This coupling strengthened over development, with correlation slopes becoming steeper by 6 months (Figure 2b). Regions showing the strongest gradient differentiation also exhibited the largest Hurst decreases.

Conclusion

We demonstrate that functional gradient differentiation and excitation-inhibition dynamics are spatially coupled during early infancy, with primary sensory regions showing both the strongest gradient maturation and the largest shifts toward excitation dominance. This coupling suggests that local physiological changes in E/I balance may drive the emergence of macroscale functional organization. The developmental trajectory follows a sensorimotor-first pattern: primary regions differentiate rapidly in the first 6 weeks before stabilizing, while association cortices lag behind, consistent with hierarchical models of cortical maturation. Our vertex-level framework, combining functional gradient analysis with Hurst exponent mapping, enables fine-scale characterization of neurophysiology in the infant brain. Applied to the HBCD cohort, this approach establishes normative trajectories of gradient-E/I coupling that may facilitate early detection of atypical patterns implicated in neurodevelopmental disorders. As longitudinal HBCD data become available, this framework will enable tracking of individual developmental trajectories across infancy.
