Introduction

Machine learning is used to develop brain age models to predict biological age from neuroimaging data. The difference between an individuals' model predicted age and true chronological age, commonly referred to as the brain age gap (BAG), has been shown to be a robust biomarker for various age-related health outcomes and neurodegenerative diseases.1 However, the vast majority of extant brain-age models are trained on large, imaging studies from Europe, North America, and China, with limited representation from African or South Asian populations. This imbalance restricts the clinical utility of brain-age biomarkers in regions where neurological conditions may have different epidemiological patterns, environmental exposures, and socioeconomic determinants of health.2 Transfer learning allows existing models to be fine-tuned using a smaller sample from a target population, offering a less resource-intensive alternative to training from scratch. This has potential to help improve transportability to under-represented groups and to overcome scanner-related variability. In this study, we aimed to investigate whether transfer learning can be used to produce valid brain age predictions when evaluated in populations outside of the training distribution. Using 3D T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data from the Aga Khan University's Brain Resilience in Kenya (BRK) programme and Longitudinal Aging Study in India - Diagnostic Assessment of Dementia (LASI-DAD) study, we assessed the: (i) the zero-shot performance of existing models (no fine-tuning), (ii) the performance of fine-tuned models and (iii) the effect of varying the amount of fine-tuning data in a controlled ablation study. 

Methods

Datasets and preprocessing: Demographic, cognitive and T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data were obtained from two independent cohorts: BRK and LASI-DAD. The BRK study was designed to generate robust multi-modal data across two timepoints, spaced 12 months apart, to identify biopsychosocial risk factors for accelerated and resilient aging in Kenyan adults aged over 35 years. The LASI study is a nationally representative survey of the health, economic status, and social well-being of the Indian population aged 45 and older.3 The LASI-DAD cohort, is an ongoing sub-study of the LASI sample focusing on dementia diagnosis, with two waves of data collection completed thus far. The cohort used in this study spanned 11 sites and multiple scanners, with all scans acquired following a protocol based on the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI). 

A summary of the dataset characteristics is provided in Figure 1. Only participants labelled as cognitively unimpaired or healthy were used. The LASI-DAD cohort was split into train, validation and test sets using stratified age–sex groups to maintain demographic balance. The BRK dataset was pre-partitioned into training and testing to provide a fixed evaluation benchmark, but was similarly stratified into training and validation sets. All scans underwent an identical preprocessing pipeline implemented in ANTsPy. The pipeline included N4 bias-field correction, brain extraction, affine non-linear registration to the MNI152 template, intensity normalisation, and resampling to an isotropic 1mm grid (1283 voxels).

Transfer learning: In this study we leveraged 3D DenseNet networks pre-trained in prior work using several public MRI datasets.4 Gravina and colleagues defined Training Paradigms (TPs) depending on the populations that the models were pre-trained on. Here, we investigated three of the proposed TPs, following the same naming conventions:

  • TP1: Models were pre-trained using groups defined by Gravina and colleagues (Asian, Black, or White).
  • TP2: The TP1-White model was fine-tuned using participants from the Asian and Black groups, obtaining TP2-Black and TP2-Asian.
  • TP3: A model was pre-trained using participants from all available groups.

The pre-trained models were used for zero-shot performance on the test set, or fine-tuned using participants set aside for training. Validation participants were used to monitor performance during training. Model outputs were converted into predicted ages using the same scaling as Gravina and colleagues (where ages are normalised by dividing by 100 during training, so predictions are multiplied by 100 during inference). A data augmentation pipeline comprising of translations, rotations up to 10°, ±10% zooms, contrast, noise, and intensity shifts (applied with low probability) was used during fine-tuning to encourage robustness. Models were fine-tuned using a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4070 Ti SUPER GPU.

To determine which fine-tuning approach was most effective, we evaluated different transfer learning strategies across all the pre-trained networks, using the BRK fine-tuning data. All fine-tuning experiments used identical hyperparameters and a training protocol with a batch size of 10, the Adam optimiser, a learning rate of 1x10-4, mean-absolute-error (MAE) loss and a maximum of 100 training epochs with early stopping based on minimum validation loss. Performance was assessed by reporting the mean absolute error (MAE), the BAG, and regression agreement with the identity line (R2) on the held out test set. Both metrics reflect the model's ability to map the predicted brain age to the real biological age. The MAE describes the overall closeness, and the BAG is used to reflect whether on average the predicted ages are older (positive BAG) or younger (negative BAG). However, in a healthy population, where there is an assumed absence of neuropathology, we expect both the MAE and BAG to be close to zero. 

Results

Selecting the optimal transfer learning strategy: Figure 2a presents the MAE values obtained across different fine-tuning strategies, where different amounts of the network were “unfrozen” and updated during fine-tuning. Among the configurations tested, unfreezing the final three DenseNet blocks (dense2_4) provided the most reliable improvement and was adopted for all remaining analyses. Across models, the dense2_4 configuration consistently achieved the lowest MAE and near-zero BAG, indicating improved accuracy and reduced bias. More conservative strategies (e.g. head) did not allow sufficient adaptation, while more aggressive modes (e.g. all, which unfreezes the entire network) introduced instability due to the small dataset size. Intermediate strategies, such as unfreezing one to four DenseNet blocks (dense1_4, dense3_4 and dense4, freezing only batch-normalization layers (freeze_bn), or gradual unfreezing of all layers (progressive), performed reasonably well overall, but exhibited reduced accuracy for the TP1-Black network. The strong performance of dense2_4 is consistent with expectations for domain adaptation, as unfreezing the final three DenseNet blocks enables effective feature adjustment while preserving stable low-level representations.5 This balance yielded the most reliable cross-population transfer. Therefore, dense2_4 was selected as the fine-tuning strategy for all subsequent analyses.

Cross-population performance of pre-trained models: Table 2 (Figure 2a) summarises the performance of pre-trained models with and without fine-tuning. Zero-shot inference showed moderate performance for both cohorts across most of the TPs. Fine-tuning generally improved error and calibration, although this effect was not universal: for the BRK dataset, both TP1-Asian and TP2-Asian models performed worse after adaptation. In the BRK cohort, models pre-trained on the larger (TP1-White) or more demographically varied datasets (TP3) transferred most robustly, achieving comparatively low MAE and near-zero BAG even before fine-tuning. For the LASI-DAD dataset, fine-tuning reduced the MAE for all models except TP2-Asian and the lowest MAE were obtained by the fine-tuned TP2-Black model. The scatter plots in Figures 2b and 2c show the test predictions for the best model based on the fine-tuned MAE. In the BRK dataset, the fine-tuned R2 was 0.66, whilst in LASI-DAD, fine-tuning worsened the R2 to a value of 0.10.

Ablation study: To assess how fine-tuning performance relates to the amount of available training data, we performed a data-ablation analysis. For the optimal configuration (dense2_4), fine-tuning was repeated using only a proportion of the training set, which was varied between 10% and 100% in 5% intervals. These results are shown in Figures 2d and 2e. In the BRK cohort, the resulting MAE values exhibited a relationship with data availability. Performance improved steadily as larger fractions of the training set were used, with a trend indicating diminishing gains beyond approximately 50–55 samples. Importantly, the model remained relatively stable down to around 35 samples, highlighting a lower bound to the amount of data required for population adaptation. This trend was also observed in the LASI-DAD dataset, where results show that increasing the amount of fine-tuning data leads to lower MAEs, with minimal gains beyond 100 samples.

Conclusion

Across both cohorts, fine-tuning generally yielded lower MAEs and better accuracy, with the dense2_4 strategy yielding the lowest MAE and BAG in the BRK dataset. In the BRK cohort, the TP2-Black model performed second best overall, likely due to its two-stage pre-training on a large TP1-White dataset followed by adaptation to the TP2-Black population, which is demographically similar to the BRK cohort. In contrast, models trained on smaller or more homogeneous subsets – such as the TP1-Asian model – showed weaker cross-population transportability. These networks entered fine-tuning with less stable feature representations, limiting the extent to which additional data could improve performance. The lower performance and low R2 for the fine-tuned LASI-DAD models reflects the challenge of adapting pre-trained brain models to new populations, in this case the Indian population, who were largely under-represented in the original study (where the Asian group consisted predominantly of participants from East Asian countries).4 Moreover, beyond demographic differences, the various scanner and acquisition protocols used across the pre-training and fine-tuning datasets is also likely to influence predictive performance. Whilst this work focuses on models pre-trained on brain age prediction, further research could also explore whether more general, foundation models lead to better downstream predictive accuracies, particularly in more extreme out-of-distribution scenarios. 

The ablation results showed that performance remained stable down to approximately 35 samples in the BRK data, indicating that only modest amounts of cohort-specific data are required for reliable adaptation. However, the LASI-DAD ablation results suggest that more samples are required to achieve similar MAEs of ∼5 years, demonstrating the increased challenge when the target cohort differs substantially from the pre-training population. Additionally, the age distribution in the LASI-DAD sample is significantly narrower than the range observed from the original study (which included participants aged between 18–97). Therefore, whilst MAEs of ∼4–6 years in the BRK cohort are promising, in the LASI-DAD sample this corresponds to 12–18% of the total age range (34 years), which is a more significant error.

Nonetheless, our findings demonstrate that large pre-trained brain-age models can be adapted to other populations with limited additional data. Whilst there is additional scope to investigate model uncertainty particularly within new, unseen cohorts, overall the moderate gains of transfer learning provide a practical route toward generalised brain-age estimation and the broader goal of translating predictive neuroimaging biomarkers to under-represented global regions.
