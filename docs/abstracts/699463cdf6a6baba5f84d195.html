Introduction

Alcohol use is a severe public health problem and is responsible for a substantial part of the global burden of disease and mortality. Rates of alcohol use, high-risk drinking, and alcohol use disorder (AUD) have increased at a higher rate in women compared to men over the last decade. Women experience worse alcohol-related health consequences compared to men, including greater risk and susceptibility to the neurotoxic effects of alcohol, such as neurodegeneration. Thus, there is a critical need to identify underlying neurobiological mechanisms of sex-specific alcohol-related consequences to inform individualized treatment options. White matter damage, such as atrophy and reorganization, is a hallmark injury of chronic alcohol consumption and AUD. In vivo macrostructural diffusion MRI and postmortem studies in people with AUD have confirmed shrinkage in white matter volume and loss of myelination in the corpus callosum, cerebellum, and cortex that may be reversed with abstinence.1,2 Deficits in white matter impact cognitive function and these alcohol-related neuroadaptations may contribute to impairments in executive function that drive continued drinking. Alcohol intake, even at low to moderate levels, negatively affects white matter tissue microstructure.3,4 However, investigations of alcohol-related microstructural deficits and more granular measurements of white matter tissue integrity such as with neurite orientation dispersion density imaging (NODDI), by sex are scarce and mixed.5 This study used dMRI (DTI and NODDI) to examine sex-specific relationships between alcohol consumption and white matter integrity in a large sample of people from the UK Biobank.

Methods

Our sample included 55,486 UK Biobank participants (52% women) with a mean age of 64±8 years for women and 65±8 years for men. Drinking data were self-reported quantities of several types of alcohol-containing beverages (red wine, white wine, fortified wine, champagne, beer, cider, hard liquor, other alcohol beverages), which were converted to daily UK units (1 UK unit=10 mL) based on prior literature3. White matter measures were obtained through imaging-derived phenotypes previously analyzed by the UK Biobank which accounted for intracranial size and head motion. Imaging-derived phenotypes were used from global and select regions of interest based on prior literature implicating effects of alcohol and sex.5 These included: the anterior limb of the internal capsule (ALIC), posterior limb of the internal capsule (PLIC), cerebral peduncle, body of the corpus callosum, genu of the corpus callosum, external capsule, fornix cres/stria terminalis, and inferior cerebellar peduncle. Associations between alcohol consumption and white matter tract integrity were examined using multiple linear regressions. Sex-stratified main effects of drinking and sex-by-drinking interactions were examined and uncorrected for multiple comparisons based on a priori hypotheses. To control for several potentially confounding factors, the following covariates were included: age, body mass index, education level, cigarette smoking status, socioeconomic status (Townsend Deprivation Index), menopause status, hormone replacement therapy status, and scanner site. 

Results

Across the whole sample, individuals drank between 0 to 32.9 standard UK units (10 mL/unit) daily with most participants drinking at low to moderate levels. Men drank a mean of 2.6±2.4 UK units per day, and women drank a mean of 1.4±1.5 UK units per day, (p<0.001). For both men and women, we found negative associations between drinking and whole-brain fractional anisotropy (FA – axonal integrity) and isotropic volume fraction (ICVF – neurite density), as well as positive relationships between alcohol drinking and whole-brain mean diffusivity (MD – tissue degradation), axial diffusion (AD – axonal loss), radial diffusion (RD – demyelination), isotropic volume fraction (ISOVF – tissue loss), and orientation dispersion (OD – axonal disorganization) (p’s<0.001). We also identified significant interactions of sex by drinking on white matter. Specifically, some of the negative relationships between drinking and white matter were significantly stronger in men than women, i.e., more drinking was associated with lower FA in the left (p=0.037) and right (p=0.016) external capsule; lower AD (p=0.033) and ICVF (p=0.048) in left cerebral peduncle; and lower ICVF (p=0.029) in the right inferior cerebellar peduncle. Similarly, some of the positive relationships between drinking and white matter were significantly stronger in men than women, i.e., more drinking was associated with higher MD (p=0.017) and AD (p=0.019) in the left ALIC; higher MD (p=0.005) and RD (p=0.004) in the right inferior cerebellar peduncle; and higher OD (p=0.015) in the left fornix cres/stria terminalis. Some positive relationships were significantly stronger in women compared to men, i.e., more drinking was associated with higher AD (p=0.011) and OD (p=0.015) in the left fornix cres/stria terminalis; and higher OD in women in the left ALIC (p=0.037) with no significant relationship observed in men. 

Conclusion

These preliminary results indicate that alcohol’s effects on white matter tract degradation are sex-dependent, even in sample with a mean low to moderate drinking level. For both men and women, more drinking is related to poorer whole-brain outcomes including less axonal integrity, neurite density, and more tissue degradation, axonal loss, demyelination, tissue loss, and axonal disorganization. In brain regions previously shown to be impacted by alcohol use, relationships between drinking and tissue degradation, axonal loss, tissue loss, and neurite density, were stronger in men than women. Although region-specific deficits in women were not as prominent as expected, women showed stronger relationships between drinking and axonal loss and axonal disorganization than men. Additionally, these early results highlight hemispheric specificity in these sex-specific deficits that are more prominent in the left hemisphere. Taken together, these data indicate that alcohol use leads to poorer white matter tract integrity, and these effects are more widespread in men, which could be due to a greater range of drinking in the men. Findings from these early analyses may suggest specificity to samples with low to moderate drinking levels.
