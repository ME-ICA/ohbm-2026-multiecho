Introduction

Cortical function is tightly coupled to its metabolic environment. However, how neurometabolites are distributed across the human cortex at the macroscale remains poorly understood. Although structural and functional connectomes have revealed prominent gradients of cortical organization, they do not capture the biochemical distributions and activities that underpin neuronal signaling and computation. Recent advances in whole-brain, high-resolution MRSI now enable in vivo mapping of neurometabolite distributions across the cortex, providing a unique opportunity to examine whether cortical metabolism is "structured" along large-scale gradients and how these gradients are related to cytoarchitectural and functional hierarchies.

In this study, we obtained whole-brain simultaneous mapping of key neurometabolites, including N-acetylaspartate (NAA), commonly associated with neuronal integrity; choline (Cho), linked to membrane turnover; myo-inositol (mI), associated with glial signaling; creatine (Cr), related to energy metabolism; glutamine (Gln), reflecting glia–neuron coupling and glutamate (Glu) and γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), reflecting excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmission. Using multi-center whole-brain MRSI data from healthy adults spanning early to late adulthood, we quantified these seven neurometabolites and derived cortical metabolic gradients that capture the principal axes of biochemical variation across the cortex.

To situate these metabolic gradients within a multiscale organizational framework, we examined their correspondence with established cortical hierarchies, including laminar differentiation, intracortical myelination, mitochondrial organization, and large-scale cognitive functional architecture. By integrating molecular, structural, and cognitive information, this work establishes neurometabolic gradients as a fundamental dimension of macroscale cortical organization, complementary to known microstructural and functional hierarchies.

Methods

Eighty-eight healthy adults participated in the study (mean age = 31.3 ± 8.8 years, range = 20–51 years; 24 males). MRI data were acquired across three sites in Shanghai, China (Shanghai Mental Health Center, Huashan Hospital, and Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital) using Siemens 3T scanners. Ten traveling volunteers were scanned at all sites to support cross-site harmonization.

Whole-brain proton magnetic resonance spectroscopic imaging (¹H-MRSI) data were acquired using the SPICE (Spectroscopic Imaging by Exploiting Spatiospectral Correlation) sequence (resolution = 2.0 × 3.0 × 3.3 mm³; field of view = 240 × 240 × 120 mm³),1  alongside structural T1-weighted MRI (resolution = 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm³). Cortical regions were defined using the Schaefer-100 atlas; regions affected by severe magnetic field inhomogeneity (primarily orbitofrontal and temporal pole areas) were excluded. Site effects in neurometabolite concentrations were corrected using TS-ComBat.

Neurometabolite concentrations were z-scored across cortical regions, and principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to identify dominant axes of neurometabolic variation. Participant-specific components were aligned using iterative Procrustes rotation to derive group-representative gradients.2 Associations with cortical microstructure were assessed using intracortical myelination (T1w/T2w). Molecular associations were examined using mitochondrial markers from the MitoBrainMap resource, including mitochondrial density, respiratory chain complex activities, and oxidative phosphorylation capacity.3 Statistical significance was assessed using Pearson correlations with spin-based permutation testing.

Cognitive associations were derived from probabilistic meta-analytic Neurosynth maps.4 Activation maps were parcellated to the Schaefer-100 atlas and thresholded (z > 0.5). Each topic was embedded in the two-dimensional metabolic-gradient space by computing the centroid of its thresholded activation pattern, defined as the mean PC1 and PC2 values across activated parcels.

Results

The cortical neurometabolic landscape was organized along a small number of robust macroscale gradients. The principal gradient (PC1) captured a progressive increase in neurometabolite concentrations across the cortex and explained the majority of variance (70%) in metabolic organization (Figure 1). PC1 varied systematically across cortical laminar differentiation classes, with paralimbic regions occupying higher gradient values, idiotypic and unimodal cortices clustering at lower values, and heteromodal association cortex spanning intermediate positions (Figure 2A). Dunn post-hoc tests (BH-FDR) showed paralimbic cortex differed from all other classes (all q ≤ 0.001), while non-paralimbic comparisons were not significant (all q ≥ 0.700). Similar hierarchical transitions were observed across Economo–Koskinas cytoarchitectural classes, revealing a motor-to-transmodal axis of neurometabolic organization (Figure 2B).

The principal gradient showed correspondence with cortical microstructure, exhibiting a negative association with intracortical myelination (T1w/T2w; r = −0.37, Pspin = 0.089). At the molecular level, PC1 was strongly associated with mitochondrial organization, including respiratory chain complex II activity (r = −0.62, Pspin = 0.010), mitochondrial density (r = −0.56, Pspin = 0.015), mitochondrial respiratory chain capacity (r = −0.49, Pspin = 0.020), and total oxidative phosphorylation capacity (r = −0.45, Pspin = 0.035). 

Neurometabolic gradients were also systematically related to cognitive organization (Figure 2E). Negative gradient loadings were associated with perceptual and action-related processes, whereas positive loadings corresponded to affective and motivational functions, placing the principal gradient along an externally to internally oriented cognitive axis.

Conclusion

This study provides the first population-level, in vivo characterization of cortical neurometabolic gradients in the human brain. Our experimental results showed that regions located higher along the principal gradient had higher neurometabolite concentrations, lower intracortical myelination, reduced mitochondrial density and oxidative energy capacity, and preferential involvement in internally oriented cognitive functions. These findings may establish neurometabolic gradients as a fundamental macroscale organizational dimension of the human cortex, linking cellular energetic architecture to large-scale brain organization and providing a framework for investigating metabolic alterations in health and disease.
