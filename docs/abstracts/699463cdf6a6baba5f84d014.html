Introduction

Neurofeedback (NF) enables individuals to regulate their neural activity through real-time feedback, offering a promising non-pharmacological approach for modulating brain function. However, nearly 40% of users fail to acquire self-regulation1, possibly due to fluctuations in motivation, arousal, and attentional engagement. Because sustained attention is difficult to maintain, NF performance is frequently affected by mind-wandering. Monitoring engagement fluctuations is therefore essential for optimizing NF protocols2, yet NF alone provides limited information about real time engagement.

Physiological signals such as eye tracking (ET) and electrodermal activity (EDA) offer a powerful complementary window into cognitive states. Pupil diameter for instance is a sensitive marker of cognitive load, attentional allocation and task engagement3. Skin conductance response, the phasic component of EDA reflecting sympathetic arousal, provides additional information about cognitive load and engagement4. Together, these modalities can track variations in engagement that directly influence learning success.

In this work, we use ET and EDA features to model cognitive performance for 2 different cognitive tasks from real-time physiological fluctuations. Our goal is to establish a physiologically grounded model for estimating cognitive engagement, with direct applications to adaptive NF. Cognitive performance is indeed a marker of engagement as the latter is necessary to get the former. Such model could dynamically adjust task difficulty to counteract disengagement and maintain optimal arousal, ultimately improving NF efficacy.

Methods

We recorded the gaze behavior and EDA of 25 participants performing 2 different cognitive tasks: Stroop and Addition. For Stroop task participants have to tell if the color written on the bottom of the screen matches the color of the font of the word at the top. For Addition task, they have to select the sum of 2 numbers among 2 choices. The 2 tasks begin with 30 seconds of baseline (only a gray dot is displayed) and then have 3 blocks equally difficult for Stroop task and with increasing difficulty for Addition (reduced time to answer each trial for each new block). Our goal is to use ET and EDA features to predict the performance of participants which is defined as the accuracy of the response multiplied by the proportion of the allocated time that has been used. First we extracted the integrated skin conductance driver (ISCR) for EDA, and the pupil diameter (PD), fixation duration, blink number and duration and gaze divergence for ET. For each task and each participant independently we normalized the signals using z-score.

To determine whether physiological markers of engagement can predict cognitive performance, we designed a series of linear models integrating progressively richer sets of features. Our approach assumes that optimal task performance emerges from a balanced combination of attentional focus and arousal, both of which are reflected in eye-tracking and electrodermal signals. We therefore sought to identify which physiological features best account for trial-by-trial variability in performance during two cognitive tasks (Stroop and Addition).

We constructed six successive models (M0–M5), increasing gradually in complexity. The baseline model (M0) included only demographic covariates (age group and sex). M1 incorporated additional contextual variables: time-on-task, task difficulty, and participants’ baseline physiological levels (mean and standard deviation prior to z-scoring). These baseline measures were included to reintroduce individual differences that are removed during z-normalization.

In M2, we added the z-scored physiological features (pupil diameter, fixation duration, gaze divergence, blink duration, blink number, ISCR) averaged over the 1sc window preceding each response. M3 complemented these short-term fluctuations with 10sc windows to capture slower modulations of arousal and attention. Because ISCR and fixation duration showed non-Gaussian distributions, M4 included their log-transformed versions (using log1(x) = log(x + |min(x)| + 1)) to improve model stability. Finally, M5 added a behavioral predictor: the average performance achieved in the preceding 9 seconds (excluding the last second), enabling the model to integrate recent behavioral dynamics. The details of all the features included in the different models is summed up in the figure.

All models were trained and tested separately for each task (referred to as Stroop/Addition model) and jointly across tasks (referred to as Global model). We used an 80/20 split, yielding 936/233 samples for Addition, 2736/683 for Stroop, and 3671/917 for the combined dataset. To evaluate performance, we computed the Normalized Mean Square Error (NMSE) and the coefficient of determination (R²) on test data.

Our evaluation followed two main goals: obtain a robust estimate of each model’s predictive ability, and determine whether increasing model complexity significantly improves performance. To ensure robustness, we used bootstrap resampling with 100 repetitions of training and testing, averaging the resulting metrics. To compare successive models, we performed paired t-tests and computed Cohen’s d. This allowed us to determine not only whether performance improvements were statistically significant, but also whether they were practically meaningful.

Results

The performance of all the models is visible in the figure.

Stroop model: The best performance was achieved by M5 (that includes all predictors) with an average NMSE=0.188 and R²=0.300. The addition of new predictors helped the model to fit better our data, as it can be observed in the comparison among the different versions. Specifically, there were significant differences, with high and medium effect sizes respectively, between M0 and M1, and M1 and M2. Furthermore, the difference was also significant between M4 and M5 for R² and almost significant for NMSE but with a weaker effect size. This means that considering the previous performance improved predictions slightly. Nevertheless, M2 model is probably the best compromise between accuracy of prediction and complexity of the model as M5 includes 11 additional predictive variables for a limited improvement. When examining M5 specifically, predictions are generally accurate, but deviations appear for extreme performance values (perf=0 or 1). In this task, actual performances were mostly concentrated between 0.05 and 0.6, limiting the model’s ability to learn patterns associated with very high scores. Additionally, because only six trials exceeded a performance of 0.75, we cannot rule out that some of these correct responses resulted from chance, likely due to slightly delayed answers to the previous trial, given that response time contributes to the performance metric.

Addition model: As for the Stroop task, the best model for Addition was M5 (NMSE=0.239, R²=0.550). However, for this task, the improvement in metrics from M2 to M5 was very small compared to the Stroop task models. We observed a significant difference combined with a high effect size between models M0 and M1 and models M1 and M2, but for the next ones the p-values were never significant and the effect sizes were always weak. This means that, unlike the Stroop task, in the Addition task taking into consideration the previous performance did not help to predict the future one. We understand that this difference between tasks may be explained because in the Stroop task it was easier to acknowledge an incorrect response after giving it compared to Addition task, and that could influence future performance. Once again M2 is the best compromise between accuracy and complexity of the model. As in the Stroop task, examining M5 predictions for the Addition task shows good alignment with ground truth for mid-range performances, but reduced accuracy for extreme values (perf=0 or 1). Several high scores close to 1 may reflect correct responses given by chance—likely due to a late response to the previous trial, since performance incorporates reaction time. This interpretation is supported by the noticeable gap between mispredicted high-performance values and the rest of the high-performance range, observed in both tasks.

Global model: As for the 2 independent models, the best version is M5 (NMSE=0.208, R²=0.396). However, for this model the only version that does not significantly improved the results is M3 (i.e. including  10s temporal windows). All the other versions improved significantly both the NMSE and the R² compared to the previous one. Specifically, this improvement had a high effect size between M0 and M1, M1 and M2 and M4 and M5, and a medium effect size between M3 and M4. It is likely that combining the two tasks increases the complexity of the data and that predictors that might not contribute the model for a single task became of relevance in this context. For the global M5 model, predictions remain close to the ground truth, with a slight tendency to underestimate performance. As in the task-specific models, extreme values remain the most difficult to predict. Importantly, combining both tasks does not degrade prediction accuracy, indicating that the selected physiological predictors generalize well across different cognitive contexts and remain relevant for estimating performance independently of the task.

Conclusion

In this work, we built and evaluated a predictive linear model of cognitive performance using multi-modal physiological signals derived from eye-tracking and electrodermal activity. Our approach demonstrates that these markers (pupil diameter, blink-based indicators, gaze divergence, and ISCR) contain rich information about cognitive engagement that can be leveraged for real-time performance estimation. Using these features, we trained task-specific models as well as a global model integrating data across the two used cognitive tasks: Stroop and Addition. All models successfully predicted performance, confirming the relevance and robustness of physiological markers as continuous estimators of cognitive state. Predictions were most accurate near average performance, while extreme performance values were harder to capture, likely due to the small number of very high performance values and the multiple reasons leading to a wrong answer.

Importantly, the global model generalized beyond individual task characteristics, demonstrating that the physiological features we used in this work capture processes that are not dependent of the considered cognitive task. This ability to transfer across contexts suggests that such model could be deployed in a wide range of applications without requiring task-specific recalibration. Indeed our findings demonstrate the feasibility of continuously tracking engagement through physiological signals. Such capability is very useful for adaptive systems that adjust task difficulty, feedback, or cognitive load based on the user’s current state. This approach is especially relevant for neurofeedback, where fluctuation in engagement and arousal levels contribute to non-responder effects. Integrating our predictive framework into training protocols could help maintaining users within an optimal engagement zone and improve results.

Although our sample size represents a limitation, this study provides a proof of concept demonstrating that eye-tracking and electrodermal markers can effectively support predictive modeling of cognitive performance. Future work should expand on these findings by testing adaptive neurofeedback paradigms while incorporating additional physiological features to monitor participants engagement and improve training outcomes.
