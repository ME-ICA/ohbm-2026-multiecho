Introduction

Imitating an observed action requires extracting its movement kinematics, a process supported by the mirror neuron system (MNS). This mechanism becomes particularly critical when actions are displayed as point-light displays (PLDs), which convey exclusively kinematic information devoid of pictorial or contextual cues. Previous studies have demonstrated similar recruitment of the MNS and of the broader Action Observation Network (AON) during observation of fully visible (FV) and PLD hand actions^1. However, more recent evidence shows that specific AON subregions respond differentially to actions varying in velocity or direction, indicating sensitivity to kinematic parameters^2. Here, we investigated whether short-term motor learning is differentially facilitated by observing fully visible actions, which provide rich visual and contextual information, versus PLDs conveying kinematic information alone.

Methods

A preliminary behavioral study involved 30 healthy right-handed young adults who performed a short-term motor learning task requiring observation and imitation of 20 complex right-hand digit sequences (8 steps executed in 5 seconds) presented in FV or PLD format. Motor performance was quantified using an accuracy-based scoring system. Each 8-tap sequence was segmented into six progressive triplets. One point was assigned for each correctly executed triplet, resulting in a maximum score of 6 points per sequence. For each condition (FV or PLD), participants performed 20 sequences, yielding a maximum performance score of 120 points (6 × 20). The maximum total performance score across both conditions was 240 points (120 + 120). To assess learning dynamics over time, performance scores were averaged across four consecutive clusters, each comprising five trials. Short-term motor learning was analyzed using a repeated-measures ANOVA with Condition (FV; PLD) and Time (Cluster 1–4) as within-subject factors. When appropriate, post hoc comparisons were performed to characterize learning trajectories across clusters.

A subsequent fMRI experiment was conducted in a new cohort of 42 healthy right-handed young adults, divided into two sex- and age-matched groups of N=21 participants each. Participants were right-handed; demographic characteristics were as follows: FV group — age: 24.6 ± 4.4 years; sex: 10 M, 11 F; PLD group — age: 23.9 ± 2.6 years; sex: 9 M, 12 F. fMRI data were acquired on a 3T GE Signa Premier scanner using a 48-channel head coil. Visual stimuli and task instructions were presented through MR-compatible Nordic NeuroLab visual goggles. The fMRI protocol included three phases. In the pre-training phase, participants performed finger-tapping sequences using an MRI-compatible keyboard-piano (S2 MIDI keyboard; Smit Lab^3) while viewing numerical cues through MRI-compatible goggles. In the training phase, each group observed its assigned hand model (FV or PLD) performing complex digit sequences and imitated them. The post-training phase replicated the pre-training task to assess training-related changes. Data were analyzed using univariate repeated-measures flexible ANOVA (2 groups x 2 hands x 2 times) and multivariate searchlight MVPA to identify voxels discriminating pre- and post-training activation patterns across groups.

Results

Overall motor performance accuracy in the preliminary behavioral task did not differ significantly between FV and PLD conditions, indicating comparable levels of performance across observation modalities. A significant main effect of Timerevealed the presence of short-term motor learning, with accuracy improving across successive trial clusters. Crucially, a significant Condition × Time interaction was observed (p<0.001), indicating distinct learning dynamics across conditions. Specifically, the FV condition exhibited a relatively linear increase in accuracy across clusters, whereas the PLD condition showed a more stepwise learning pattern, characterized by a marked performance improvement between clusters 1 and 2 (p<0.008) and between clusters 2 and 3 (p<0.001).

Concerning the fMRI task, performance accuracy during the training phase revealed a significant main effect of Time(p<0.0001), indicating short-term motor learning across clusters in both groups. No significant Group × Time interaction emerged (p=0.09), suggesting a comparable learning trajectory over time. Descriptively, the PLD group showed lower accuracy than the FV group in the first cluster; however, performance progressively increased across subsequent clusters, reaching levels comparable to the FV group in later clusters.

Observation of finger sequences activated canonical MNS/AON regions, including occipito-temporal cortex, posterior parietal cortex, premotor and prefrontal areas bilaterally. Additional clusters included the supplementary motor area (SMA), middle cingulate cortex (MCC), and bilateral cerebellum (Lobules VI and VIII) (p<0.001 uncorrected at voxel level; p<0.05 FWE cluster).

Regarding pre- to post-training performance changes, both groups improved similarly for both hands (Time: p<0.001). No main effect of groups nor interactions with times or hands emerged. At the neural level, both groups showed reduced activation after training in SMA, MCC and hippocampus, consistent with neural efficiency mechanisms (p<0.001 uncorrected at voxel level; p<0.05 FWE cluster)^4.

The searchlight MVPA identified discriminative voxel patterns that differentiated pre- vs post-training activations in each group. For FV > PLDs, clusters emerged in bilateral cerebellum (Lobules VI and VIII), left inferior parietal lobule (IPL), left ventral premotor cortex (PMv), anterior MCC, and thalamus (VPL nucleus) (threshold for independent sample t test p<.001 uncorrected at voxel level; p<0.05 FWE cluster). Conversely, PLDs > FV revealed clusters in superior SPL, dorsal premotor cortex, SMA, anterior supramarginal gyrus (aSMG) bilaterally, and right cerebellum (Lobules V and VI). 

Conclusion

These findings provide novel evidence on the neural and behavioral mechanisms supporting observation-based motor learning from kinematic information. Despite their minimal visual features, PLDs effectively supported short-term motor improvement, demonstrating that kinematic cues alone are sufficient to drive learning. However, the underlying mechanisms may differ: FV-based learning may rely more on motor resonance and sensorimotor facilitation based on the MNS, whereas PLD-based learning may be driven by a network encoding movement feature independently of action goals. Future studies should test these hypotheses by comparing FV and PLD training with actions differing in goal structure and kinematic profiles.
