Introduction

Post-stroke aphasia affects approximately one-third of left hemisphere stroke survivors, profoundly disrupting communication abilities, social participation, and quality of life. Accurate early prediction of both aphasia risk and severity from acute neuroimaging could transform clinical decision-making, enabling personalised interventions and prognostic counselling.

Nonetheless, predicting language outcomes from neuroimaging remains challenging owing to the complex heterogeneity of lesion patterns, individual differences in language network organisation, and the intricate relationship between ischaemic damage and functional impairment. Traditional lesion-symptom mapping approaches have identified putative critical language regions, but translating these findings into individualised predictions has proven challenging thus far.

Recent advances in foundation modelling, such as TabPFN (Tabular Prior-data Fitted Network1), offer state-of-the-art performance on tabular prediction tasks even in the context of small-scale data. Where lesion anatomy can be expressed in compact vectorial form, these transformer-based architectures, pre-trained on millions of synthetic datasets, could capture complex nonlinear lesion-deficit relationships. This promising approach, however, has yet to be applied to lesion data.

Here, we hypothesised that different lesion representations may differentially predict the risk of post-stroke aphasia and its severity, reflecting distinct, data regime-constrained perspectives on the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms. Specifically, we compared representations based on lesion masks (capturing lesion topology), structural disconnectomes (capturing anatomical network disruption probability), and white matter streamline tractography (capturing tract-level integrity) compressed via multiple dimensionality reduction techniques (VAE, PCA, UMAP) to identify optimal distributed predictors for each category of clinical outcome.
