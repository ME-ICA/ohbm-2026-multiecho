Introduction

Mapping functional brain circuits at the levels of cortical columns and layers requires mesoscale (submillimeter) resolution, with sufficient temporal resolution to measure relevant signals, and a functional MRI (fMRI) contrast specific to the area and depth of cortical activity. While recent advances have allowed for laminar imaging in limited field of view slabs, meeting these criteria with whole-brain coverage is technically challenging, with previous approaches being limited in their achievable temporal resolution1. 

In this study, we leverage the Next Generation (NexGen) 7T scanner, specifically designed for high-resolution cortical neuroimaging2, to fully optimize our approach. The dedicated hardware of the scanner allows us to achieve cerebral blood volume (CBV) weighted functional imaging using VAscular Space Occupancy (VASO)3, with whole-brain coverage, submillimeter resolution and sufficient temporal resolution to reveal cortical networks in a movie watching paradigm4. VASO has been shown to have greater functional specificity than the Blood Oxygen Level Dependent (BOLD) contrast that has generally been used for fMRI3, making it attractive for resolving the true location of brain activity across cortical depth

Methods

Data were collected from three subjects on the NexGen 7T MRI scanner2 using an 8-ch transmit, 64-ch receive array coil. Images were acquired using a Skipped-CAIPI 3D EPI VASO3 sequence. Images were collected with a dual-polarity readout, with the polarity of the readout alternating per volume acquisition, to correct for the  “fuzzy ripple” artifacts that arise from gradient-imperfections when pushing to high resolutions, image acceleration factors and imaging bandwidths5. The advanced head-only Impulse gradients on the NexGen 7T allowed for shorter-echo spacings to reduce readout time, and the high-channel array coil allowed for a high-level of acceleration to further speed-up image readout, allowing for whole-brain, sub-millimeter VASO imaging without an extended repetition time.

VASO scan parameters were: 0.85mm isotropic resolution, whole volume TR 5s, TE 16.2ms, TI 1128.5/3071.6ms, Partial Fourier 7/8, Skipped-CAIPI 4x4z2 (total acceleration 16), Bandwidth 1766 Hz, Echo Spacing 0.63ms. Participants undertook 3-4 runs (45-60 min) of a movie watching task (using a 15-min collection of movie clips from the 7T HCP study (a.k.a. MOVIE1)). Data were denoised using NORDIC, motion corrected using ANTS, and the fuzzy-ripple artifact was removed using the dual-polarity read-out calibration approach5. Blood-nulled and not-nulled (BOLD) timeseries data were used to create a BOLD-corrected VASO timeseries after temporal upsampling to an effective volume TR of 2.5s. Images collected using the optimized sequence showed high levels of detail without artifacts following dual-polarity correction (Figure 1). The temporal mean of the T1-weighted VASO images were used for tissue segmentation using Freesurfer, the gray-matter ribbon was exported to functional space using SUMA, and cortical depth (layers) and distance (column) ROIs were generated using LayNII. After averaging across runs, MELODIC ICA was run on the BOLD and VASO time-series at the subject level to check for any observable functional network components. 

Subsequently, whole-brain connectomes were defined to assess known patterns and novel layer-specific features of functional brain organization. Data were denoised using regression (aCompCor, 6 motion regressors), scrubbing and band-pass filtering (0.008–0.1 Hz), and concatenated across runs. Time-series were averaged for three layers (deep, middle, superficial) of 25k cortical columns (max distance between points 2.5 mm), and connectivity was measured by pairwise correlations. VASO and BOLD networks were then assessed based on the presence of sensory-associative connectivity gradient via diffusion map embedding, connectivity density mapping, connectivity with large-scale networks, and the similarity of network fingerprints across the three layers. Large-scale networks were estimated based on 7 network seeds of a probabilistic atlas (62% threshold, right hemisphere only).

Results

After ICA analysis, the expected components arising from a movie watching experiment could be resolved both in the BOLD time-series, and also in the VASO time-series after a moderate amount (1mm FWHM) of within-layer smoothing, demonstrating that whole-brain, submillimeter CBV imaging can be used to study whole-brain networks and circuits. 

Both VASO and BOLD data showed firmly established organization of the sensorimotor-association (SA) axis (Figure 2) and high centrality in the association cortex (2B). In these associative areas, connectivity density was also predominantly stronger in the superficial than deep layers using both VASO and BOLD, consistent with previous layer-fMRI reports suggesting a superficial layer emphasis in network communication of frontal regions. Putative layer-specific features emerged from network correlation analysis, where cortical depths sometimes mapped preferentially to different large-scale functional networks (2C) and in the similarity of network connectivity across depths (2D). With BOLD alone, it would be unclear how much of these patterns are driven by neural connectivity or large vein biases. Having vein-free VASO available here can augment the interpretation of these results.

Conclusion

Previous implementations of whole-brain, high-resolution functional imaging using CBV contrasts to maintain functional specificity have been limited by the complexity of balancing the requirements of coverage, resolution and functional contrast. Long imaging times and reduced temporal resolution were typically required, which limited the applicability of these techniques in many cases. Pushing the performance of EPI at high-resolutions can also lead to artifacts arising from gradient imperfections that can limit the achievable resolution in many cases. 

Combining the hardware of the NexGen 7T with novel sequence readout approaches, we were able to use both shorter echo spacings and high parallel imaging factors to speed up image acquisition, allowing us to collect whole brain, high-resolution VASO data in a single inversion cycle, as opposed to the multiple inversion cycles per image required in previous implementations1. This allowed imaging at temporal resolutions sufficient to resolve common functional networks, and to investigate layer-connectivity within these networks. Future work will focus on how to best investigate layer-connectivity profiles across the brain, and fully evaluate the relative advantages of VASO for these methods. Further optimization of the imaging sequence may also allow for further improvements in imaging resolution, offering the capability of better resolve activity across depth even in thinner regions of the cortical ribbon.

The use of rich stimuli such as movies also allows for the possibility of using voxelwise encoding model approaches in combination with VASO, which could potentially yield important insights into the kinds of information processed in different cortical networks.
