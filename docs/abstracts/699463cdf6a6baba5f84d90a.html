Introduction

Brain age estimation provides a data-driven way to quantify deviations from normative brain aging. The so-called brain age gap (BAG, brain age – chronological age) has been repeatedly demonstrated to carry clinical utility as a non-linear and age-specific summary marker of structural brain changes due to neurodegenerative diseases like Alzheimer’s disease (AD).1 However, most brain age models are currently trained on mixed-sex datasets, implicitly assuming that aging trajectories are sufficiently similar across sexes to justify a shared normative reference. This assumption is at odds with established evidence demonstrating that brain aging patterns differ by sex,2 and that these differences interact with AD pathology3 and lifestyle.2 Notably, women show higher lifetime risk for AD dementia2 and accumulate tau pathology more rapidly compared to men.4 Despite these well-established differences, it remains insufficiently understood to which degree sex-specific brain age models capture distinct neurobiological aging signals across sexes, and to which BAGs are associated with AD risk and progression markers within sexes.

In this study, we systematically evaluated: (1) The feasibility and prediction accuracy of mixed-sex vs. sex-specific brain age models; (2) regional patterns of feature importance within-sex; and (3) sex-specific associations between BAG and cognitive performance, tau pathology, and lifestyle. Our goal was to refine our understanding of the biological specificity and clinical interpretability of brain age markers with respect to sex differences.

Methods

T1-weighted MRI scans of 619 cognitively normal subjects (CN; N=359 women; N=260 men; mean age=75.6y, range=60-90y) and 796 patients with mild cognitive impairment or dementia (N=398 women; N=398 men; mean age=74.9y, range=60-90y) were obtained from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative. MRI scans were preprocessed using standardized pipelines including skull stripping, bias-field correction, linear registration to MNI space, and within-scan min-max intensity scaling. A 3D convolutional neural network architecture was used to train three models for the estimation of chronological age from structural MRI scans, i.e., brain age: (1) a mixed-sex model; (2) a women-only model; and (3) a men-only model. Training was performed using five-fold cross-validation, with data split into five different training, validation and testing samples. To stabilize model training, we added longitudinal MRI scans of training subjects to each training fold. Within each fold of the mixed-sex model, training, validation and test sets were balanced for sex. This resulted in approximately 480 scans for training, 40 for validation, and 50 for testing per sex (depending on the fold). For the sex-specific models, each fold used the same subjects as the mixed-sex model, but restricted to the relevant sex. After training, the regression-to-the-mean effect was corrected using a linear model estimated based on the validation data. Finally, the trained model was applied to the CN test and patient data and BAGs were computed for each subject.

Subsequent analyses were conducted within each fold as follows:
(1) One-sample t-tests against zero were performed on BAGs in CN women and men to verify whether each training strategy, mixed-sex and sex-specific, produced BAG distributions appropriately centered around zero as an indicator of accuracy.

(2) Occlusion sensitivity analysis on CN subjects revealed which brain regions most critically informed the age estimation paradigm.

Next, we used the sex-specific models to examine sex differences in brain aging and biological and symptomatic AD progression, as well as lifestyle risk factors across subjects through the following analyses:
(3.1) A linear model tested the association between sex-specific BAGs and tau pathology (PET signal intensity in inferior temporal lobe), while correcting for age, years of education and amyloid status.

(3.2) An additional linear models tested the association between sex-specific BAGs and memory impairment (ADNI-MEM), while correcting for the same covariates as in 3.1 and tau pathology.

(3.3) Finally, nine linear models tested the associations between sex-specific BAGs and lifestyle risk factors for dementia5 (depression, social isolation, hypertension, lifetime duration of alcoholism and smoking, low education, obesity, cholesterol levels, and sleep quality), while correcting for age, amyloid status and tau pathology.

Across linear models, an effect was deemed robust when it was significant (α=0.05) in at least 3/5 (i.e., more than half of) cross-validation folds.

Results

Both the mixed-sex and sex-specific training strategies yielded highly accurate estimations of chronological age based on brain scans (women: mixed-sex mean absolute error [MAE]=2.0-3.4y, sex-specific MAE=2.5-3.4y; men: mixed-sex MAE=2.3-3.2y, sex-specific MAE=2.3-3.8y). In CN women, BAGs were lower than zero in three out of five test sets (i.e., folds) of the mixed-sex model, and in one out of five in men; whereas no such deviations were observed in the sex-specific models. This potentially suggests that sex-specific training may better center BAGs and more accurately capture sex-specific age-related changes. MCI and especially dementia patients presented with an increased BAG across training strategies (Fig. 1A).

Regional saliency analyses showed that distinct features were informative for the estimation of age from MRI scans in women versus men. In men, the right-hemispheric temporal lobe was an important, i.e., salient region for the estimation of age, whereas in women, feature importance was more widely spread throughout the brain (Fig. 1B). The mixed-sex model’s salient features yielded a regionally blended aging signature that appeared to combine regions used by the women- and men-only models.

Higher sex-specific BAGs were associated with elevated inferior temporal tau levels in men (P<0.05 in all folds), and to a lesser extent in women (P<0.05 in 3/5 folds). Similarly, BAGs were more reliably associated with memory impairment after consideration of tau in men (P<0.05 in 4/5 folds in men, 2/5 folds in women). Interestingly, women also showed both higher tau burden and better memory performance (t-test women vs. men: P<0.05 in all folds). Lifestyle did not systematically affect BAGs in either sex beyond the effects of age, amyloid status and tau pathology, while cholesterol levels could not be investigated due to sparse overlap of tau PET and information on cholesterol. Exploratively, we also assessed associations of BAG and lifestyle without correcting for tau pathology and found a robust association between higher BMI and lower BAGs in women in 4/5 folds. Moreover, longer duration of alcoholism was associated with higher BAGs in men, and social isolation was associated with higher BAGs in women in one fold, respectively.

Conclusion

Brain age gaps (BAGs) have been shown to capture age-specific aspects of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) progression by quantifying the degree to which brain structure appears older than expected. However, established models for the assessment of brain age mostly ignore well-established sex differences in aging trajectories and AD vulnerability. Therefore, we sought to determine whether sex-specific modeling and analysis improves the biological specificity and clinical interpretability of BAGs.

Our findings confirm that sex is indeed a meaningful axis of variation in brain aging. Sex-specific training yielded comparable accuracies compared to mixed-sex models despite comprising only half the training sample size, and they uncovered regional aging patterns that were different across women and men. These results suggest that combining women and men into a single training sample merges distinct trajectories of structural aging, potentially reducing the interpretability of derived BAG values as individualized markers of neurodegeneration.

Clinically, BAGs derived from sex-specific models showed slightly different associations with tau pathology and cognitive impairment in women versus men. In men more so than in women, BAGs were robustly associated with both tau pathology and downstream memory impairment. Together with the strong influence of right-hemispheric temporal regions in the men-only model, regions also heavily affected in advanced AD, this pattern suggests that in men, AD-related brain changes may resemble accelerated aging of the brain. In contrast, women required a more globally distributed aging signal for accurate prediction. This may reflect greater heterogeneity in age-related structural decline in women, potentially making their brain aging trajectories more complex for machine learning models to capture reliably. Since women in our study showed better memory performance despite higher tau burden, it will be an important next step to identify factors that might modulate how tau affects BAGs and downstream cognition within-sex, such as additional lifestyle, genetic or hormonal factors. Moreover, it should be investigated to what extent brain age estimation frameworks can be modified to more robustly capture clinically relevant deviations from healthy aging in women.

The results of this study should be interpreted in the context of some limitations. First, the number of training subjects used in this study, although common in medical imaging tasks, is relatively small for the complex demands of a convolutional neural network. In the next step, we will investigate whether the reported effects are also present when using machine learning algorithms with lower complexity. Moreover, the sex-specific models did not yield lower estimation errors compared to the mixed-sex models, which may appear counter-intuitive to our conclusion. However, this observation is likely attributable to the smaller training samples for sex-specific models, as the mixed-sex model had access to twice as much data (women + men). We intentionally retained the full mixed-sex sample, rather than halving it for a direct comparison, to evaluate model differences under realistic conditions where sex-specific models naturally have fewer training examples.

Taken together, our findings highlight sex as an essential stratifier for improving the specificity and interpretability of brain age as a marker of neurodegeneration. Incorporating sex-sensitive brain age models may enhance the precision and clinical utility of brain age markers to the extent that they may be considered useful disease progression markers not only for disease, but also therapy monitoring in AD.
