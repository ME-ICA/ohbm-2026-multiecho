Introduction

Misophonia is a sound sensitivity disorder in which specific everyday sounds trigger unusually strong negative emotional reactions, with clinically significant symptoms affecting up to 18% of the population.1 Although neuroimaging studies implicate functional alterations in auditory-insular networks in misophonia,1 the underlying structural substrates remain poorly understood.

Tissue-based volumetric analysis of regions-of-interest (ROIs) in a magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) study showed reduced cerebrospinal fluid volume in misophonics within right auditory and anterior insular regions, consistent with local tissue differences in areas previously implicated functionally.2 However, the anatomical origin of these differences is unknown. Cortical thickness offers a direct method to localise potential microstructural alterations within these heterogeneous cortices and has not previously been investigated in misophonia.

Here, we examined cortical thickness across subregions of the right early auditory cortex and right anterior insula in individuals with and without misophonia. This allowed us to test whether specific subregions show increased thickness associated with misophonia, providing a precise structural characterisation of auditory–insular networks in this disorder.

Methods

We analysed T1-weighted structural MRI data from 30 individuals with misophonia and 30 matched controls acquired on a Siemens Prisma 3T scanner (1 mm3 MPRAGE). Misophonia status was defined using the Sussex Misophonia Scale. These data were originally collected as part of an MRS study in which ROIs were the right early auditory cortex (centred on Heschl’s gyrus) and the right anterior insula.3 Our structural analyses focused on these right-hemisphere regions.

T1-weighted images were processed in FreeSurfer (version 7.4.1). Cortical thickness (mm) was estimated at each vertex and averaged within subregions defined using the Glasser parcellation. For the anterior insula, we included those parcels overlapping the original spectroscopy ROIs: anterior agranular insula, anterior ventral insula, middle insula, Area Pir, posterior insula 2, and frontal opercular areas FOP1–FOP5. For the early auditory cortex, we included primary auditory cortex (A1), lateral belt, medial belt, parabelt and retroinsular cortex. Median thickness values were computed for each parcel to limit the influence of outliers and parcel edges. All surface reconstructions and ROI placements were visually inspected.

Two logistic regression models were used to test whether cortical thickness predicted misophonia status (misophonia versus control group). One model included the ten right anterior insula parcels as predictors; the other included the five right early auditory parcels. Variance inflation factors and tolerance values were examined to assess multicollinearity. Analyses were conducted in jamovi (version 2.3.28.0).

In ongoing work, we are extending the sample to N = 179 by adding 109 structural datasets (Figure 1). This will enable whole-brain voxel-based morphometry and analyses of resting-state and sound-evoked fMRI responses to misophonic, aversive and neutral audio-visual stimuli, with post-stimulus affect ratings and experience-sampling following the resting-state scan.

Results

In the anterior insula model (n = 60), cortical thickness in the frontal operculum 2 (FOP2) significantly predicted misophonia status (odds ratio [OR], 33.86 [95% CI, 1.27 to 899.37], P = .035), with greater thickness in the misophonia group. No other anterior insula parcel significantly predicted misophonia status (all P > .12).

For the early auditory cortex model (five right-hemisphere parcels), cortical thickness in the retroinsular cortex (RI) significantly predicted misophonia status (OR, 23.88 [95% CI, 1.04 to 546.78], P = .047), again with greater thickness in misophonics. No other parcel of the early auditory cortex reached significance (all P >.09).

A combined plot of parcel-wise thickness (Figure 2) shows that both FOP2 and RI are thicker in the misophonia group compared to controls, with overlapping distributions but clear shifts in group means.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence of cortical thickness differences in right-hemisphere auditory–insular subregions between misophonics and controls. Increased thickness of FOP2 within the anterior insula and of RI within the early auditory cortex localises the structural variability suggested by a tissue-based volumetric analysis in an MRS study,3 and is consistent with functional accounts emphasising altered integration of sound and affective processing in misophonia.2

FOP2 is part of the frontal operculum and situated in the superior circular sulcus of the insula.3 It has functional connectivity to the auditory cortex,3 and the amygdala, with the latter linked to feelings of anger and rejection.4 RI lies at the superomedial end of Heschl's gyrus, with all the subregions of the early auditory cortex located at its borders.3 During emotional music listening, fMRI scanner noise interacts with affective neural processes in the right retroinsular cortex, possibly reflecting involuntary changes in attention to auditory stimuli.5 Thicker cortex in the FOP2 and RI may reflect altered development or plasticity in networks that link acoustic features to salience and negative emotion in misophonia.

Overall, our findings suggest that misophonia is associated with spatially specific, rather than diffuse, structural alterations within the right auditory–insular circuitry. Analyses in an enlarged structural and functional dataset (N = 179) will test the robustness of these effects at the whole-brain level and examine how structural differences in FOP2 and RI relate to resting-state connectivity, sound-evoked responses and individual differences in misophonia severity.
