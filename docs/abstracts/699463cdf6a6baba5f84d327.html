Introduction

In precision medicine, there is significant interest in leveraging resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) to predict cognition and behavior measures (e.g. fluid intelligence or neuroticism) across healthy and clinical populations. This has traditionally been accomplished by training machine learning algorithms on handcrafted functional connectivity (FC) features (e.g., Pearson’s correlation, partial correlations) to predict a given phenotype.1 

Alternatively, brain foundation models (BFMs) utilize abundant unlabeled brain imaging data, such as rs-fMRI, to learn robust representations that can be transferred to small-scale dataset for downstream applications, such as phenotypic prediction.2,3 BFMs learn representations directly from rs-fMRI time series, as opposed to using traditional functional connectivity (FC) features, such as Pearson’s correlation.4

Existing BFMs have so far been evaluated on a limited number of non-brain-imaging phenotypes, and their potential to predict a wide range of non-brain-imaging phenotypes remains underexplored. Here, we compare two BFMs, BrainLM and Brain-JEPA,2,3 with two “classical” machine learning models, Kerner Ridge Regression and meta-matching,4,5 which utilize handcrafted FC features in phenotypic prediction in two large-scale datasets.

Methods

Our experiments were conducted using two datasets: Human Connectome Project Young Adult (HCP-YA; N = 1,019) and Human Connectome Project Aging (HCP-Aging; N = 656). We selected 58 phenotypes from HCP-YA and 45 from HCP-Aging, covering measures of cognition, emotion, and personality. For each participant, we used ICA-fix preprocessed resting-state fMRI time series in fsLR space. 

To compare the prediction performance between brain foundation models (BFMs) and machine learning models using typical functional connectivity (FC) features, we included 4 pipelines in our experiments (Figure 1): BrainLM, Brain-JEPA, Kernel Ridge Regression (KRR) and meta-matching.2,3,4,5 

We chose BrainLM and Brain-JEPA because the two models achieved state-of-the-art performance among models trained with time-series features at the time we designed the experiments. Both models use a Vision Transformer backbone but differ in learning objectives. BrainLM uses a masked-prediction task, whereas Brain-JEPA uses a joint embedding approach. Each model was pretrained on UK BioBank before fine-tuned on HCP-YA/Aging. Specifically speaking, the models are pretrained and fine-tuned using time series parcellated with 450 regions of interest (ROIs), including 400 cortical (Schaefer-400) and 50 subcortical (Tian-Scale III) parcels. Consistent with previous studies,3 the time series were down-sampled by sampling the first frame of every 3 frames, resulting in 400 and 160 timepoints (frames) per rs-fMRI run for HCP-YA and HCP-Aging participants, respectively. 

KRR and meta-matching served as non-foundation-model baselines using traditional FC features. KRR is a simple classical machine learning algorithm widely used for phenotypic prediction.4 KRR predicts phenotypes based on similarity between individuals’ FC. The idea is that if two participants have more similar FC, then their phenotypes are also more similar.  

Meta-matching is a recent transfer-learning method that predicts phenotypes in a target dataset using phenotype predictions generated in a source dataset.5 In this pipeline, a deep neural network is pretrained on UK BioBank dataset to predict 67 UK Biobank non-brain-imaging phenotypes using 419 × 419 resting-state FC matrices (419 ROIs: Schaefer-400 cortical and 19 FreeSurfer subcortical). Then a KRR model is further trained using the UK BioBank phenotypic predictions as inputs to predict HCP-YA/Aging phenotypes. Accordingly, in KRR and meta-matching settings, we parcellated the preprocessed time series data to 419 ROIs and then compute the 419 × 419 FC matrices. 

For each phenotypic prediction task, we designed two different training-validation-testing strategies: 100-shot and 60-20-20 split. In 100-shot settings, we randomly sampled 100 participants and split them into 80 training and 20 validation participants. The training participants were used for training and validation participants were used for tuning the hyperparameters. The remaining participants (919 for HCP-YA and 556 for HCP-Aging) were used for testing. Prediction performance was evaluated using Pearson’s Correlation in the testing participants. The whole process was repeated 100 times with different random sampling of 100 participants. 

The same procedure was used in the 60-20-20 split settings, except that the participants were randomly split into a 60%-20%-20% ratio for training, validation and testing. The 60-20-20 random split was repeated 10 times. We used the bootstrapping and corrected resample t-test to compute statistical significance of prediction performance differences for the 100-shot and 60-20-20 split experiments, respectively.5

Results

Figure 2 shows the prediction performance of the various models. Both BrainLM and Brain-JEPA-B used a “base” Vision Transformer (ViT) backbone with ~86M parameters. Brain-JEPA-S used a “small” ViT with ~22M parameters.

In 100-shot settings, each boxplot represents the distribution of average prediction performance (Pearson’s Correlation) across 57 HCP-YA or 45 HCP-Aging non-brain-imaging phenotypes across 100 random splits (Figures 2a & 2c). P values comparing different algorithms are shown in Figure 2e. Bold values indicate statistical significance after false discovery rate (FDR) correction with q < 0.05.

On HCP-YA, both KRR and meta-matching were statistically better than all brain foundation models (BFMs). Meta-matching also outperformed KRR. On HCP-Aging, KRR was statistically worse than both Brain-JEPA models, but not BrainLM. Meta-matching achieved the best performance, outperforming KRR, Brain-JEPA and BrainLM. 

In the 60-20-20 split settings, each boxplot represents the distribution of average prediction performance (Pearson’s correlation) across 58 HCP-YA or 45 HCP-Aging non-brain-imaging phenotypes across 10 random splits (Figures 2b & 2d). P values comparing different algorithms are shown in Figure 2f. Bold values indicate statistical significance after FDR correction with q < 0.05. 

On HCP-YA, both KRR and meta-matching were statistically better than all BFMs. KRR was numerically better than meta-matching but the difference was not statistically significant. On HCP-Aging, KRR was numerically better than all BFMs, but the differences were not significant. Meta-matching was statistically better than all BFMs. Meta-matching was numerically better than KRR but the difference was not statistically significant.

There were several interesting observations from our results. In the 100-shot setting, brain foundation models (BFMs) underperformed relative to KRR on HCP-YA but outperformed KRR on HCP-Aging. In the 60-20-20 split setting, KRR outperformed all BFMs on HCP-YA, while its performance on HCP-Aging was not significantly different from that of the BFMs. We hypothesize that the learned representation from UK BioBank population in BFMs benefited phenotypic prediction in HCP-Aging participants, since the age distribution of the two datasets were more similar compared with HCP-YA (age range: 22-35 for HCP-YA, 36-100+ for HCP-Aging, 40-69 for UK BioBank). Additionally, BFMs experienced smaller decrease in prediction performance than KRR when shifted from 60-20-20 split to more data-limited 100-shot settings, suggesting that BFMs were relatively data-efficient.

Conclusion

We evaluated BFMs and classic machine learning models for phenotypic prediction across different experimental settings. In both the 100-shot and 60-20-20 split experiments, KRR and meta-matching demonstrated better prediction performance than BFMs. While current BFMs did not outperform KRR and meta-matching in the majority of our experiments, we believe that they may have the potential to leverage large-scale unlabeled data to learn robust representation and might enhance phenotypic prediction, especially in the few-shot scenarios. Overall, our findings provide useful insights for the community and help guide future improvements in BFMs.
