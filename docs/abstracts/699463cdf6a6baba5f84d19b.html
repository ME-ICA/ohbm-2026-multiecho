Introduction

Wide-field optical imaging(WOI) is a functional neuroimaging technique that enables low-cost, high-throughput imaging of the dorsal mouse cerebral cortex with high temporal and spatial resolution.1,2WOI captures both hemodynamic signals(akin to BOLD-fMRI) and fluorescence signals(e.g.,genetically encoded calcium indicators), providing a bridge between cellular-level neural activity and macroscopic functional neuroimaging.

Despite advantages, WOI has two major limitations:(1)quantitative spectroscopy and fluoroscopy are constrained by oversimplified light modeling;(2)WOI produces 2D images that are difficult to integrate with cross-sectional imaging modalities. Photon propagation is typically modeled using a semi-infinite geometry, relating changes in reflected light to absorption via the modified Beer–Lambert Law. While this enables estimation of oxy- and deoxygenated hemoglobin and fluorescence signals, it fails to capture true imaging geometry, including tissue-specific optical properties and refractive effects at curved or angled interfaces. In human optical neuroimaging, such as near-infrared spectroscopy or diffuse optical tomography, anatomically accurate models improve quantitative accuracy. Such modeling is critical in the mouse brain, where small length scales cause photon transport to deviate from diffusion assumptions, amplifying geometric inaccuracies.3

To address these limitations, we generate subject-specific mouse head models and use Monte Carlo photon simulations. Modelling for each camera pixel enables more accurate characterization of photon propagation, accounting for the cranial window, skull curvature, and cortical origins of hemodynamic and fluorescent signals. This framework improves the accuracy and interpretability of WOI and supports optical neuromonitoring methods such as diffuse optical spectroscopy(DOS) and diffuse correlation spectroscopy(DCS), facilitating development of optical biomarkers translatable to human bedside neuroimaging.

Methods

We aimed to model two neuromonitoring systems: (1) widefield optical imaging (WOI) performed with visible light illumination and detection via a camera and (2) a combined DOS/DCS fiber-optic based system. Both modalities are performed with an acrylic cranial window secured to the intact skull with transparent dental cement. To perform WOI, the mouse head is held below the imaging system, and different light emitting diodes are sequentially illuminated to capture temporal and spatial patterns in absorption. DOS/DCS measurements are performed with a fiber-holder mounted to the window. Frequency-modulated light is injected via a source fiber, and detector fibers are similarly coupled. Both systems can thus by modelled in the same overall geometry with modifications of the photon source and detection.

To generate a realistic mouse head mesh, mice underwent computed tomography (CT) with 220 projections; the scan was reconstructed with 34 μm isotropic resolution (Fig. 1A). The CT volume was segmented semi-automatically using MATLAB (R2025b) and ITK-SNAP with manual corrections applied following quality control (Fig. 1B). Segmented volumes included the cranial window (acrylic), dental cement, skull, and brain. Of note, with the current protocol, there is little contrast between the dental cement and scalp/skin. As such, in this preliminary pipeline, scalp (which is mostly lateral to the imaged field-of-view) was included as part of the dental cement segmentation. We then used iso2mesh to generate a tetrahedral mesh,4 constraining the maximum circumradius of tetrahedra to < 2 mm (Fig. 1C).

Following mesh generation, Monte Carlo photon transport was modeled using MMCLAB (Fig. 1D).5 Optical properties were assigned based on previous literature. To demonstrate feasibility, we first simulated illumination from a pencil beam incident on the superior surface of the mesh, centered in the x–y plane. A total of 1×10⁹ photons packets were simulated. To improve computational efficiency, Snell/Fresnel boundary reflections were disabled. For this preliminary pipeline, fluence was recorded as a single time-integrated measurement over 0–5 ns.

Results

Herein, we show the results of this pipeline using a single 8-month-old female C57bl/6 mouse. CT resulted in a high-resolution image of the entire mouse head including the attached cranial window without distortion. Different tissue and window volumes were segmented with high fidelity (except for the previously noted difficulties separating dental cement from scalp, Fig. 1B). These segmentations resulted in a full subject-specific 3D mesh (Fig. 1C) measuring 484 × 496 × 187 mm³ in which Monte Carlo was performed (Fig. 1D).

As a proof of concept for modelling of photon propagation, we then demonstrated fluence from a continuous-wave, pencil beam source at five wavelengths relevant to our WOI and DOS/DCS systems (470 nm, 530 nm, 590 nm, 625 nm, and 785 nm). Fluence profiles in the brain were as expected with red and near infrared light propagated further through the brain (Fig. 2).

Conclusion

Previous modelling of light propagation in the mouse for diffuse optics has been performed with semi-infinite geometries or small inclusions, which limits accuracy and 3D integration. Here, we demonstrate the first subject-specific mesh creation and Monte Carlo modelling for individualized light modelling in the brains of mice. While preliminary, these results demonstrate the potential of our proposed Monte Carlo pipeline to combine optical neuromonitoring, neuroimaging, and CT to improve the accuracy of optical measurements. Future work will assess how anatomic modelling, partial volume effects, and optical properties affect spectroscopic and fluoroscopic accuracy. Additionally, by modelling the WOI image in the 3D geometry, WOI can be accurately combined with MRI, CT, histology, and the Allen Brain Atlas. These advances will allow a more complete understanding of how genetic variation, cytoarchitecture, and injury affect functional biomarkers.
