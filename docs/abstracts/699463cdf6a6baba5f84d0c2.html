Introduction

The hippocampus is an allocortical structure that is critical for memory, learning, and adaptability. One of the brain’s most structurally malleable regions, the hippocampus exhibits prolonged environment-dependent development that in turn influences the maturation of structurally connected areas of the prefrontal cortex (PFC).1 Animal studies have shown that environmental deprivation reduces myelination of hippocampal pyramidal neurons whereas environmental enrichment promotes it,2 suggesting that myelin growth in the hippocampus may mechanistically mediate its environmental sensitivity. In addition, myelination of hippocampal neurons that send long-range outputs to the PFC may facilitate hippocampal-driven refinements in PFC circuitry. In this project, we use 7T quantitative MRI collected longitudinally to test the central hypothesis that myelination of the hippocampus in youth is impacted by the environment, linked to the pruning of glutamatergic neurons in the PFC, and integral to hippocampal-dependent forms of memory. Furthermore, in light of cross-species evidence that the structure, function, neurobiology, and connectivity of the hippocampus exhibit graded change along its anterior-posterior (i.e., longitudinal) axis,3 we investigate whether myelin development and its biobehavioral consequences differ between anterior, middle, and posterior hippocampal regions. We expected that developmental increases in hippocampal myelin would vary in magnitude and timing along its anterior-posterior axis and be diminished in youth exposed to higher levels of socioeconomic environmental deprivation. We additionally predicted that higher levels of hippocampal myelin would be associated with pruning-related reductions in glutamate concentration in the PFC and better visuospatial working memory.

Methods

MRI: We leveraged ultra-high field (7T), multi-modal MRI collected from an accelerated longitudinal youth sample (10-32 years; 150 individuals with 239 scans; 75 female). MP2RAGE-derived uniform (UNI) images and quantitative T1 maps were processed to measure myelin in the anterior, middle, and posterior hippocampus. The UNI image was used as a T1w image for hippocampal segmentation. The T1 map was used to compute R1, a quantitative myelin-sensitive imaging measure that has been validated against myelin histology.4 To mitigate the potential effects of residual B1+ transmit field inhomogeneity on the acquired MP2RAGE data, the UNICORT algorithm was applied to both denoised UNI images and raw quantitative T1 maps. Following UNICORT correction, UNI images were processed with FreeSurfer’s longitudinal processing stream to segment the hippocampus. Volumetric R1 maps were computed from quantitative T1 images (R1 = 1/T1 in sec-1) and R1 was averaged within the bilateral anterior, middle, and posterior hippocampus (primary analysis) as well as in cortical and subcortical regions (specificity analyses). We additionally incorporated magnetic resonance spectroscopic imaging (MRSI) data collected from the same participants to examine relationships between hippocampal R1 and glutamate concentration in the medial and dorsolateral PFC. MRSI scans were acquired in an oblique axial slice and spectra were processed with LCModel as detailed in prior work.5 Only R1 maps and MRSI neurometabolite data that passed stringent visual and quantitative quality control were analyzed.

Environment: We studied the impact of the developmental environment on hippocampal R1 using geocoding-derived measures of neighborhood deprivation. Participant’s childhood addresses (i.e., where they lived prior to age 18) were linked via census tracts to both the Area Deprivation Index (primary analysis) and the Child Opportunity Index (specificity analysis). The Area Deprivation Index assesses environmental disadvantage using indicators such as income, education, employment, and housing quality. The Childhood Opportunity Index provides indices of neighborhood quality in three domains: socioeconomic opportunity (indexed by public assistance and homeownership rates, income, and high-skill employment), educational opportunity (based on early childhood, elementary, and secondary education indicators), and environmental health (related to pollution, health resources, and safety).

Memory: A subset of participants completed the Cambridge Neuropsychological Test Automated Battery (CANTAB). The CANTAB spatial span, which assesses visuospatial working memory, was used to test the relevance of hippocampal myelination to a form of working memory that requires communication between the hippocampus and PFC. 

Statistical Modeling: All statistical tests utilized generalized additive mixed models (GAMMs) with hippocampal R1 as the dependent variable. Independent variables included a smooth function for age, sex as a linear covariate, and random intercepts per participant. Linear covariates were added to evaluate fixed effects of interest (neighborhood deprivation, MRSI glutamate, and visuospatial working memory) and varying coefficient interactions were added to test whether relationships between R1 and these factors significantly differed by age. GAMMs were fit separately for the anterior, middle, and posterior hippocampus; the false discovery rate was used to correct p-values for multiple comparisons across these three regions in all analyses. To provide insight into the magnitude and timing of age-related increases in R1, we calculated the first derivative of the age spline. The average first derivative indexes the average rate of R1 increase over the full age range studied; the largest first derivative identifies the age at which the rate of R1 increase is maximal. Standardized slopes were computed to quantify the strength of relationships between R1 and linear covariates. 

Results

R1 significantly increased with age in the anterior, middle, and posterior hippocampus (all pFDR < 0.001), indicative of protracted hippocampal myelination in humans that continues throughout adolescence (Fig. 1a). The average rate of R1 change increased along the anterior-posterior axis; as a result, developmental trajectories were significantly shallower in the anterior hippocampus than in middle and posterior regions (interaction p < 0.05; Fig. 1b). Charting the timing of myelination milestones revealed that the posterior and middle hippocampus experience the largest increases in R1 at ages 10-11 years. The anterior hippocampus, in contrast, exhibited a mid-to-late adolescent enhancement in R1-indexed myelin growth, with the rate of R1 increase being maximal at age 17.2 years (Fig. 1b). An analysis of sex effects showed that sex differences in hippocampal R1 also diverged along its anterior-posterior axis (Fig. 1c). Males had higher R1 in the posterior hippocampus (pFDR = 0.018) whereas females had higher R1 in the anterior hippocampus (pFDR = 0.018); no significant sex differences were observed in the middle region.

Geocoding-based analyses uncovered that environmental deprivation becomes embedded in the microstructure of the developing hippocampus. In particular, a higher area deprivation index was associated with significantly lower R1 in the posterior (pFDR = 0.040), middle (pFDR = 0.007), and anterior (pFDR = 0.007) hippocampus (Fig. 1d), paralleling animal model evidence that environmental deprivation leads to lower hippocampal myelin density. The strength (i.e., slope) of the R1-environment relationship was strongest in the anterior hippocampus, followed by middle and posterior regions (Fig. 1e). Strikingly, the area deprivation index was more strongly associated with R1 in the anterior and middle hippocampus than all other cortical and subcortical regions (Fig. 1f), highlighting hippocampal myelin’s unparalleled environmental sensitivity. Both the anterior and middle hippocampus additionally exhibited significant age-by-environment interactions (pFDR < 0.05), with the impact of environmental deprivation on R1 development strengthening during mid-to-late adolescence (Fig. 1g). We used the Child Opportunity Index to better parse specific features of the neighborhood environment that accounted for observed relationships between deprivation exposure and hippocampal myelin levels. Socioeconomic opportunity was significantly related to hippocampal R1 (posterior: pFDR = 0.044, middle: pFDR = 0.099; anterior: pFDR = 0.044), but educational opportunities and environmental health were not.

We hypothesized that stronger structural input from the hippocampus to the PFC would facilitate the normative developmental pruning of glutamatergic pyramidal neurons in cortex, linking greater intrahippocampal myelination to lower prefrontal glutamate. Integrating quantitative myelin mapping with spectroscopic imaging, we found that higher R1 in the posterior (pFDR = 0.012), middle (pFDR = 0.009), and anterior (pFDR = 0.041) hippocampus was indeed associated with lower glutamate concentration in the medial PFC—a prefrontal region that receives robust monosynaptic inputs from the hippocampus. In contrast, hippocampal R1 was not significantly related to glutamate concentration in the dorsolateral PFC, which receives only sparse axonal input from hippocampal regions. Finally, we tested whether myelin levels throughout the hippocampus were important for participant’s visuospatial working memory capacity, which requires the integration of hippocampal and PFC circuitry. Higher R1 in most of the hippocampus (posterior: pFDR = 0.069, middle: pFDR = 0.015; anterior: pFDR = 0.015) was related to better CANTAB spatial span working memory; the relationship between hippocampal R1 and cognition became stronger with age in the anterior hippocampus only (age-by-cognition interaction pFDR = 0.005).

Conclusion

Developmental and environmental influences on myelin-sensitive R1 spatially and temporally vary along the hippocampus’s anterior-posterior axis, revealing that a major axis of hippocampal organization is also a primary axis of environmentally-sensitive hippocampal development. The posterior hippocampus—which supports navigation, fine-grained spatial representations, and perceptual memory—undergoes robust and sustained myelination that is moderately sensitive to the environment and maximal earlier in youth. In contrast, the anterior hippocampus—involved in emotional and motivational processing, course spatial representations, and abstract memory—experiences a potentiation of myelin growth in the late teenage years. This late adolescent enhancement in anterior hippocampal myelination coincides with a window of heightened environmental receptivity, suggestive of a unique sensitive period of environment-driven myelination and circuit consolidation.

Animal models have shown that there is an upregulation of hippocampal-driven synaptic plasticity (LTP/LTD) in the medial PFC during the adolescent period.1 This upregulation facilitates hippocampal-dependent refinements in cortical glutamatergic connectivity. Our findings relating hippocampal myelin content to glutamate concentration in the medial PFC provide evidence that a similar phenomenon occurs in adolescence in humans, with implications for developmental improvements in visuospatial working memory. Together, this study identifies hippocampal myelination as a central mechanism underlying the developing brain’s environmental sensitivity and furthermore implicates this mechanism in the maturation of PFC circuit properties and cognitive ability.
