Introduction

The dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) supports the formation of new memories by exerting top-down control over memory encoding-related processes.¹ How increased dlPFC activity alters the dynamics of the episodic memory network is not fully understood. High-definition transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) can enhance prefrontal activity, including during memory encoding.2 We therefore combined ultra-high-field 7-Tesla fMRI with Dynamic Causal Modeling (DCM) and group-level Parametric Empirical Bayes (PEB) modelling to examine how high-definition tDCS of the left dlPFC alters effective connectivity within a broader memory network. The network includes the left dlPFC, the left hippocampus, the left inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), and the visual word form area (VWFA). These areas support word processing, semantic elaboration, and episodic memory formation.

Methods

We randomly assigned 58 healthy participants (mean age 25.1 ± 3.4 years, 63.8% female) to real or sham anodal high-definition tDCS of the left dlPFC (1 mA for 20 min). Stimulation was applied during an fMRI task in which participants had to encode 50 emotionally valenced words. After stimulation, a free recall followed (Fig. 1). Functional MRI data were acquired during encoding and free recall on a 7-Tesla Siemens MAGNETOM Terra scanner using a prototype gradient-echo echo-planar imaging sequence with dual-polarity GRAPPA Nyquist ghost correction.3 We preprocessed fMRI data using fMRIPrep and used SPM12 (Wellcome Centre for Human Neuroimaging, London, UK) running on Matlab R2024b (The MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA) for first-level, whole-brain, and DCM analyses. We used the subsequent memory effect as contrast of interest, consisting of later remembered versus later forgotten words. For each participant, a DCM was estimated, with a driving input of all encoding trials (i.e., all words) and a modulatory input representing later remembered words (i.e., successfully encoded words). We used the four above-mentioned regions of interest (left dlPFC, left IFG, VWFA and left hippocampus) for DCM. We assessed group-level effects using PEB modelling, estimated the mean model of all participants, and compared real to sham stimulation.

Results

We had to exclude eight participants due to movement (mean framewise displacement > 0.2 mm and/or max framewise displacement > 2.8 mm) or inconsistent stimulation. For the sham condition (Fig. 2A), we found that there was a stronger influence from the VWFA to the left hippocampus, and from the left IFG to the VWFA when participants successfully encoded words. However, the influence in the opposite directions - from the VWFA to the left IFG and from the left hippocampus to the VWFA - was weaker (with a high probability, Pp > 0.95). With stimulation, the pattern of effective connectivity changed significantly (Fig. 2B). The flow of information from the VWFA to the left IFG increased, particularly when words were later remembered. At the same time, the effect of the VWFA on the left hippocampus was reduced (again, with high probability, Pp > 0.95).

Conclusion

Our results indicate that, in the sham condition, successful encoding was associated with enhanced connectivity from visual word-processing areas to the hippocampus. In addition, successful encoding was associated with increased feedback from prefrontal semantic regions to visual processing areas, alongside suppression of other routes within the network. This pattern provides a reference baseline against which stimulation-induced neuronal reorganization of the memory network can be interpreted. Increasing left dlPFC activity with tDCS strengthened the influence from the VWFA to the left IFG, indicating that visual word information was more strongly routed towards frontal regions involved in semantic evaluation. At the same time, the reduced influence from the VWFA to the hippocampus suggests reduced reliance on a direct perceptual–memory pathway and greater engagement of a coordinated network. Taken together, our results suggest that high-definition tDCS of the left dlPFC altered the encoding strategy. Rather than sending visual word information directly to the hippocampus, the network shifted toward greater visual–prefrontal interaction. This shift may reflect increased engagement of semantic processing pathways, potentially supporting deeper encoding. A key next step is to determine whether individual differences in stimulation-related changes in effective connectivity predict memory performance.
