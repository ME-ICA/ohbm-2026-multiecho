Introduction

The locus coeruleus (LC) is a brainstem nucleus that is the main source of norepinephrine (NE) in the brain. Given its role in brain- and body-wide regulation of arousal and stress, altered LC-NE processing may play a role in the development of arousal-related psychopathology1. Despite decades of research suggesting disrupted NE functioning in anxiety and trauma-related disorders, LC disruption in psychopathology has been difficult to investigate in humans due to its small size and poor contrast with standard neuroimaging methods. Neuromelanin-sensitive (NM) imaging provides high contrast in catecholamine-rich areas including the locus coeruleus2. NM signal strength is associated with the concentration of neuromelanin, which is synthesized from catecholamines and accumulates within cells. Greater NM signal intensity in LC is hypothesized to result from increased catecholamine turnover, reflecting greater NE signaling, while reduced intensity correlates with dementia-related neurodegeneration. Changes in LC NM signal intensity have been previously linked to hyperarousal-linked psychiatric disorders and symptoms, including post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)3. However, it is unclear whether LC changes are specific to PTSD or related to transdiagnostic domains of pathological fear or distress.   

Methods

51 participants adult participants (38 female, 10 male, 3 transgender/nonbinary; 40 White, 4 Asian, 3 Black; mean age 31.4 +/- SD 9.1, range 22-54), oversampled for high anxiety symptoms, underwent neuromelanin-sensitive (NM), structural T1-weighted, and functional MRI scanning.

Participants completed a structured clinical interview and self-report measures of psychopathology. Participants were scanned at 3T at two sites. Structural T1-weighted (site 1: TR 2300 ms, TE 2.98 ms, flip angle 9, 1x1x1mm; site 2: TR  2300 ms, TE 2.96 ms, flip angle 9, 1x1x1mm), neuromelanin-sensitive (site 1: TR 500 ms, TE 2.64 ms, flip angle 8,  0.5x0.5x1.0 mm; site 2: TR 385 ms, TE 3.8 ms, flip angle 25, 0.43x0.43x3mm) and functional MRI scans ( both sites: TR 900 ms, TE 30.2 ms, flip angle 53, 2.4x2.4x2.4mm, 12 minutes) were collected. Structural T1w and fMRI images were preprocessed using fMRIprep. NM scans were skull-stripped and registered to T1 and MNI space with FSL BET and ANTs. Masks for locus coeruleus4 and reference pons ROIs were registered to native NM space for each subject. NM signal intensity was extracted from pons and LC masks.

Normalized LC signal intensity was calculated using an average of five highest intensity voxels in LC mask5 and average signal within pons reference region. Average, median, and maximum framewise displacement was calculated across fMRI runs for each subject. Correlations were calculated between clinical measures and normalized LC intensity, accounting for motion and scanning site.

Results

In this sample, 62% of participants met diagnostic criteria for any DSM-5 anxiety-related disorder (anxiety disorders, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and PTSD). 14% of participants met diagnostic criteria for PTSD. We found significant (p<0.05, uncorrected) correlations between motion- and site-corrected LC NM signal intensity and scores on the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ), with higher levels of childhood trauma associated with higher NM signal intensity. We also found significant (p<0.05, uncorrected) positive correlations between LC NM signal intensity and PTSD diagnosis. Positive correlations were also found between LC NM signal intensity and additional measures of trauma-related symptoms, although they did not reach significance. No significant relationships were found between LC NM signal intensity and other anxiety disorder diagnoses or transdiagnostic measures of fear, distress, and avoidance. In addition, no significant correlations were found between subject motion during fMRI scanning and any clinical measures.

Conclusion

These results provide support for a growing body of work connecting traumatic stress with heightened NM signal intensity in the locus coeruleus. NM signal intensity may indicate underlying alterations in catecholamine processing within the LC that could relate to the development and maintenance of trauma-related symptoms.
