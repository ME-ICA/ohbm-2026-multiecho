Introduction

Alzheimer's Disease is associated with morphological changes and atrophy in key regions of interest in the brain. Given its chronic neurodegenerative nature, early diagnosis would help patients get the care they require, before significant irreversible brain atrophy has occurred.

This study examines the statistical power of popular diffeomorphic tensor-based morphometry (TBM) techniques to detect neurodegeneration in the hippocampus and enlargement of the inferior lateral ventricle, in cognitively intact subjects with normal cognition, before cognitive or functional impairment occurs. We use this information in a classification scheme to investigate if we can predict which subjects with normal cognition at baseline will remain stable versus which subjects will progress to mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or Alzheimer’s dementia (AD).

Methods

A total of N=120 cognitively normal (CN) amyloid-beta positive subjects (average age 74 years) were selected for their T1-weighted 3D MRI scans from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative database (ADNI) database2. For each CN subject, two time points were chosen for the study, such that the time between scans was greater than 0.75 years and less than 1.25 years. This time frame was chosen to estimate an accurate annualized atrophy rate in each region of interest (ROI), and thus determine if the same atrophy patterns observed when comparing CN patients to MCI or AD patients can be seen in the CN progressors vs. CN non-progressors case. For the purpose of this study, a progressor is defined as a patient who will receive an MCI or AD diagnosis at any point in the future after the follow-up scan. The main biomarker for this study was defined to be the ratio of the hippocampus volume and the inferior lateral ventricle volume (defined by the hippocampal volume ratio, HVR=Hippocampus/(Hippocampus + Inf. Lat. Ventricle)). This ratio was chosen because we conjectured that naturally occurring hippocampus volume variation might mask atrophy in the data. We tested this conjecture (HVR vs. hippocampal volume) for the classification task. The N subjects were divided into 2 cohorts, consisting of subjects that were cognitively normal at baseline and stayed stable (CNs, n=80), subjects who eventually progressed (CNp) to mild cognitive impairment (n=25) or progressed to AD (n=15). Note that no subject was actually classified as MCI or AD at the second follow-up scan. This is a relatively new idea and is arguably a much more impactful problem to solve. A breakdown of the population is provided in Fig. 1 to summarize data variability vis-a-vis hippocampal, ventricular volumes and cognitive markers used in similar experiments as ours. 

Each intra-subject pair of MRI scans from the group was registered using one of three pure diffeomorphic registration toolkits: Large Deformation Diffeomorphic Mappings (LDDMM)4, Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs)5 and the Toolkit for Adaptive Riemannian Optimization for Multi-Scale Diffeomorphic Registration (FireANTs)3. The latter two rely on the symmetric normalization algorithm, but FireANTs is a new, GPU-compatible version. The mean annualized atrophy rate over all voxels in their respective ROI was calculated and used to perform a statistical power analysis based on the estimated sample size (n) required to detect 25% reduction in the annualized atrophy rate in the CN cohort, with 80% or 90% power, using a two-sided test and significance level α = 0.05 (referred to as n80 and n90, respectively). It is calculated using the formula n = 2σ̂D2 (z1-α/2 + zpower)² / (0.25β̂)², where β̂ refers to the annual average difference between stable and progressors biomarkers, σ̂D is the standard deviation over both groups and z* is the standard z-score1. Finally, because of the low-dimensionality of the data, we also decided to survey their classification capabilities using classic logistic regression, while balancing weights to take data skewing into account.

Results

We observe that all three registration methods yield hippocampal volume and HVR metrics that provide a stable and comparable classification result, which can be surprising given the short time span studied to infer statistical knowledge. Future progression of AD in a CN cohort has rarely been carried out because diagnosis relies on cognitive decline and the loss of independence in activities of daily living, often occurring 10-15 years after the first symptoms are seen. We set the benchmark with ANTs registration, which provided a slight power advantage over the other two algorithms. Indeed, ANTs required 741 subjects in total to detect a 25% decline in atrophy rate over 1 year with 80% power (991 subjects in total for 90% power). LDDMM and FireANTs were able to produce similar but less successful results at (n80, n90) = (940, 1258) and (802, 1074), respectively (see Fig. 2). The classification scores are very similar. There is a slight reversal of trends that occurs. Indeed, balanced logistic regression of HVR changes between subjects yields accuracies of 0.675 for ANTs and LDDMM, and 0.65 for FireANTs, while sensitivity is 0.6250 for the former pair, and 0.65 for FireANTs. Specificity was 0.7 for ANTs and LDDMM, and 0.65 for FireANTs. Interestingly, classification based on hippocampal volume only made accuracy (LDDMM: 0.5083, ANTs: 0.4917, FireANTs: 0.5083), sensitivity (LDDMM: 0.5750, ANTs: 0.5000, FireANTs: 0.5500) and specificity (LDDMM: 0.4750, ANTs: 0.4875, FireANTs: 0.4875) all hover around 0.50, backing our claim that the anatomical variability of the hippocampus would negatively affect predictive ability over such a small snapshot in time.

Conclusion

In this study, we have provided a baseline to address a problem that has rarely been attempted - predicting progressors versus non-progressors from MRI scans of cognitively normal subjects. Our study was based on a comparison between three popular nonlinear registration methods. In fact, with the arrival of better, more accessible, GPU-compatible algorithms such as LDDMM and FireANTs, the baseline we set can likely be further improved. We hope that this prediction problem enters the same sphere of rich literature and discussion in which AD diagnosis from MRI scans presently lives.
