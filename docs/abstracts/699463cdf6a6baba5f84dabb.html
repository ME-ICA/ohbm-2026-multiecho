Introduction

Studies of hand control usually focus on reaching and grasping an object. However, our interactions with objects extend beyond grasp, often to moving and placing the object to another location1. Grasping and placement share several features, including eye-hand coordination patterns2 and the use of visual features such as location and orientation3, but they differ in terms of intentionality and sensory feedback. Grasp starts without haptic feedback and ends with physical contact with the object, whereas place begins with haptic feedback and ends without it once the object is released. While grasp relies heavily on actual visual perception of a physical object, placement relies more on an internal representation or mental image of the movement outcome. Overall, these behavioural findings suggest that although grasping and placing might share similar motor mechanisms, they might employ different cognitive and sensory mechanisms. However, the neural mechanisms for these differences are poorly understood. For instance, the visuomotor channel hypothesis has allowed us to dissociate between the reach and the grasp components in hand movement4,5. For reaching, the visual information travels through the posterior intraparietal cortex (pIPS), the dorsal premotor cortex (PMd) to reach the primary motor cortex (M1), while for grasping, the information travels in parallel to the anterior intraparietal cortex (aIPS), to the ventral premotor cortex (PMv) to reach M1. It is unclear how this differentiation is reflected in placement. Therefore, the current research approach aims to explore the neural correlates of object placement and how they differ from those of grasping. Given that both actions require the integration of similar object features, we expect some overlap in the brain regions activated. However, we hypothesize that differences in the brain activity and functional connectivity will emerge due to differences in movement goals and the sensory information available.

Methods

We employed an event-related fMRI task in which twenty participants alternated between grasping and placing a rectangular object supported on a template on an inclined platform. Each trial began with a target presentation (illumination of the object or an empty template), followed by a planning delay period before receiving a cue to perform the required movement (either grasp or place) and return to the home position on their pelvis (Fig. 1). The object / template was presented at two locations (left / right of central fixation) and two orientations (clockwise / counterclockwise), randomized and controlled from behind by sliding / rotating the template across the platform. Data analyses were performed using the general linear model (GLM). We modelled our predictors using two time periods (planning and action phases) based on movement type, resulting in a total of four predictors of interest: Planning-Grasp, Planning-Place, Action-Grasp, and Action-Place. We then employed a whole-brain univariate analysis, region-of-interest analysis, and graph theory analysis (GTA) of 232 cerebro-cortical nodes to understand how the cortex integrates these action cues over time.

Results

We employed an event-related fMRI task in which twenty participants alternated between grasping and placing a rectangular object supported on a template on an inclined platform. Each trial began with a target presentation (illumination of the object or an empty template), followed by a planning delay period before receiving a cue to perform the required movement (either grasp or place) and return to the home position on their pelvis (Fig. 1). The object / template was presented at two locations (left / right of central fixation) and two orientations (clockwise / counterclockwise), randomized and controlled from behind by sliding / rotating the template across the platform. Data analyses were performed using the general linear model (GLM). We modelled our predictors using two time periods (planning and action phases) based on movement type, resulting in a total of four predictors of interest: Planning-Grasp, Planning-Place, Action-Grasp, and Action-Place. We then employed a whole-brain univariate analysis, region-of-interest analysis, and graph theory analysis (GTA) of 232 cerebro-cortical nodes to understand how the cortex integrates these action cues over time.

Our univariate analysis looked at the overall increase in BOLD signal and applied a cluster correction with a z-threshold of 2.3 with a corrected cluster significance threshold of P = 0.05. During the planning phase, grasp and place result in widespread increases in activity in the frontal cortex, insula, cingulate cortex, superior temporal gyrus (STG), V1, and the cerebellum. Furthermore, we found task-specific increases in activation in the right extrastriate region and the left STS in preparation for a grasp, and the right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, left M, and left S1 in preparation for placement. During the execution phase, grasping and placement both result in a vast increase in activity in the insula, the middle and posterior cingulate cortex, STG, V1, and the cerebellum. We found task-specific increases in activation in the left supplementary motor area, the left anterior prefrontal cortex, and the left extrastriate cortex during grasp preparation, and in the right extrastriate region during placement preparation. 

To understand how placement relates to the reach-and-grasp regions, we then used a region-of-interest analysis to compare changes in β weights between grasp and place in left V1, pIPS, aIPS, PMv, PMd, S1, and M1. The aIPS, S1, M1, and PMd showed a significant increase in activity during the placement planning phase compared to the grasp planning phase; however, there was no significant difference during the execution phase in these regions.  A Weighted k-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) classifier revealed that information from S1, PMd, pIPS, and M1 during the planning phase could distinguish between grasp and placement with an accuracy of 78.9%.

Turning to functional connectivity, our GTA’s modularity analysis revealed a similar three modules for grasp and place (Cerebello-Occipital-Parietal Module, Frontal Module, Central Sulcus Module). When we inspected their respective modularity scores, grasp vs place, we found a significant difference for the Cerebello-Occipital-Parietal Module and Frontal Module, but no difference for the Central Sulcus Module (Fig. 2). This implies that even though grasp and place share the same community grouping, they differ in the strength of these connections.

Conclusion

This fMRI study identifies three essential ways in which placement compares to reach-to-grasp movements: 1) voxelwise comparisons show differ between our conditions in the cortex and the cerebellum, 2) classification from our regions of interest analysis shows action related region can dissociate between grasp and place above chance, and 3) functional connectivity analysis show how action intention cared by Frontal module and the sensory information carried by the Cerebello-Occipital-Parietal Module differ in their modularity index but converge in regions involved with movement execution in central sulcus module. Overall, these data suggest that grasp and placement share several overlapping cerebello-cortical mechanisms, but also exhibit regional specialization, likely related to different intentions and sensory feedback.
