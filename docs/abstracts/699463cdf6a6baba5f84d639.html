Introduction

Many physical phantoms are used to validate MRI, most are used to assess structural imaging (i.e., resolution and distortions) but few can assess anisotropic diffusion1,2. A new synthetic phantom has been engineered to mimic anisotropic diffusion in white matter, composed of fiber bundles that vary in diameter, orientation, length, and exhibit variable and parameterized degrees of crossing and overlap. This phantom is designed to serve as a benchmark for validating and standardizing diffusion MRI methodologies, with the ultimate goal of optimizing acquisition protocols and facilitating harmonization of diffusion data across multiple imaging sites and longitudinal studies. 

Initial evaluations have focused on assessing the reproducibility of diffusion-derived metrics under identical scanning protocols across different scanners3, as well as monitoring temporal stability over several months for advanced diffusion measures such as kurtosis and outputs from constrained spherical deconvolution4.

However, for the phantom to function as a true ground truth reference—enabling quantitative comparisons across institutions—replicated phantoms must exhibit structural equivalence and closely matched diffusion characteristics within predefined tolerances. Achieving this level of consistency is critical for ensuring that observed differences in diffusion metrics reflect biological or methodological factors rather than variability in phantom construction. The work presented here, demonstrates a way to assess structural equivalence across phantom replicas while using a repeat scan of the same phantom as a baseline for comparison.

Methods

Three replica phantoms (phantom-v1, -v2 and -v3) were scanned in a 32-channel headcoil on a 3T MR750 (GE Healthcare) scanner on different days within a month and phantom-v1 was scanned twice, one week apart. A standard 2-mm isotropic 30-direction DTI protocol was used (three b=0 volumes, TE=83.7 ms, TR=8.8 s) in addition to a 1-mm isotropic T1-weighted (T1w) clinical protocol. A short additional diffusion scan was acquired with reversed phase-encode polarity to enable distortion correction. The T1w images were used to identify the fiber bundles of interest through a phantom-specific semi-automated mask-making algorithm (work not shown here). The work here focusses on a section of the phantom with six in-plane (axial) bundles of interest (see Fig.1). All bundles have a diameter of approximately 4 mm except for one (mainBifurcation) which has a diameter of 6.7 mm. They are of varying lengths and placed in various orientations along the axial plane.

First, images are corrected for B0-induced distortions and eddy currents using FSL (FMRIB Software Library) tools (topup, eddy). Then, in-house python software, using primarily Dipy, CV2 and Nibabel libraries, is used to fit the data to the tensor model to produce diffusion metrics, determine the bundle masks, clip them by FA (>0.1) and then bin them into bins of length 2.35 mm along the bundle axis. An average value of a diffusion metric can then be computed per bin to create a bundle profile. Here, FA is used and we compare FA profiles across phantom replicas (Test2: phantom-v1 vs -v2, Test3: phantom-v1 vs -v3) for each of the six bundles of interest. As a baseline, we also compare the FA profiles of the scans performed on the same phantom 1-week apart (Test1).

The following statistical tests are used to assess consistency of bundles across replicas: two one-sided tests (TOSTs) are performed to determine a tolerance FA value for equivalence for each bundle (α=0.05). Pearson's r is used to determine if the profiles are correlated and the root-mean-squared error is also computed. 

Results

Fig.2 shows sample results of the binning and clipping of the obliqueA bundle for phantom-v1 on one of the scan days. The corresponding color-coded FA map is also shown (green=AP, red=RL). Paired profiles are plotted superimposed for the three tests. The table contains results of the bundle-wise statistical tests. To determine an overall tolerance level, the individual bundle profiles are concatenated to produce one single long profile which is then used in the statistical tests. This results in overall tolerance levels of 0.04 , 0.03 and 0.04 for Test1, Test2 and Test3, respectively. Using these overall profiles yields highly significant (p<<0.0001) correlations: r values 0.56, 0.43, 0.62,  for the three tests, respectively. RMSE values are 0.11, 0.10 and 0.08 overall, for the three tests, respectively.

Conclusion

This analysis reveals that tolerance levels depend on the specific scans (scanner performance and phantom placement) and not on the phantom version as Test1 yields similar tolerance values to Test2 and Test3. Most bundle-wise tolerance levels were ≤ 0.07 with the exception of one bundle (mainBifurcation) which is the shortest and thickest one. This result was consistent across tests. Significant correlations can be seen for some bundles but not all, likely due to the lack of datapoints associated with shorter bundles but also due to inconsistencies arising at bundle crossing regions which introduce some variability to the voxel content depending on voxel-to-structure placement. The concatenated bundle profiles increased our statistical power, yielding significant correlations and low tolerance values of 0.03-0.04 across tests, indicating that the phantom replicas are suitable for multi-site studies as they produce similar results to the same phantom being scanned twice.
