Introduction

The noradrenergic locus coeruleus (LC) is critical for regulating sleep, arousal, attention, cognition, and  memory1. Non-invasive characterization of the LC integrity can be done through magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), particularly using high-resolution ultra-high field (UHF) MRI, as it provides high-resolution capabilities facilitating imaging of the deeply located tiny (approximately 1.5x1.5x15mm) LC2. At UHF MRI neuromelanin (NM) sensitive MRI, utilizing the magnetization transfer (MT) based imaging techniques allows to calculate the so-called LC-contrast, i.e., the difference between the image intensity in LC intensity and the surrounding tissues which is considered to reflect the integrity of the LC. Nevertheless, the implicit arbitrary scaling of MRI images renders the LC-contrast a non- or semi-quantitative metric, and precludes establishing direct association between the LC-contrast and the tissue microstructure. LC-contrast has been partially linked2 to other truly quantitative metrics, such as R1, R2, and R2* relaxation rates, quantitative measurements of MT, and proton density (PD). The contrast mechanisms behind LC-contrast and exact and relationships between quantitative tissue parameters and the LC-contrast remain, however, a matter of investigation.

In this work we employed a multiparameter mapping (MPM) approach to obtain the quantitative measurements of the LC which we related to the LC-contrast. Our goal was to assess which tissue properties are more likely to drive the LC-contrast in NM-sensitive MRI, and which ones are the prominent candidates for building the biophysical models linking the MRI-based measurements to the LC microstructural integrity.

Methods

The current sample consists of 28 healthy volunteers (12 males) with age ranging from 21 to 73y (median of 29). Participants were scanned using the UHF 7T Terra MRI (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) with a 8-Tx/32-Rx RF head coil (Nova Medical, Wilmington, MA, USA).

The MPM protocol3 comprised three (T1-weighted, PD-weighted, MT-weighted) whole-brain 3D FLASH-based multi-echo sequences covering the sagittal FoV of 250×218×173 mm3 with isotropic resolution of 0.6 mm (TR 22.4 ms, FA PDw/MTw/T1w 7/7/22°, six equispaced echoes with TE ranging from 3 ms to 15.6 ms for PDw and T1w and 4 equispaced echoes with TE ranging from 3 ms to 10.56 ms for MTw (the latter achieved by applying a 4 ms, 130° Gaussian-shaped pulse, 3 kHz off-resonance), CAIPIRINHA acceleration in two phase encoding directions with acceleration factor R=2 in each, kT-points pulses for RF homogeneity improvement and AFI based B1 mapping for transmit field correction. The B1 transmit field mapping protocol was acquired at an isotropic 4 mm resolution (two TR values of 25 ms and 125 ms, TE 2.12 ms, nominal pulse flip angle of 55°, GRAPPA acceleration in one phase encoding direction with an acceleration factor R=2). Two field mappings were performed per protocol to account for different B1+ distributions when using the kT-points excitation pulses and the Gaussian-shaped MT-pulses.

Images used for the LC-contrast calculation in NM-sensitive MRI and for the LC segmentation were acquired with a MT-weighted turbo flash (MT-TFL) sequence2, where a multi-shot 3D readout was performed in a 30 mm slab with the across-slab resolution of 0.5 mm and 0.4×0.4 mm2 resolution in the slab plane. The sequence parameters were TR = 544 ms, TE = 4.08, flip angle = 8°. Sampling the centre of k-space was preceded by 20 long off-resonant Gaussian sinc pulses (pulse length = 10 ms, pulse offset of 2000 Hz and nominal flip angle of 350°). The field of view was placed perpendicular to the dorsal surface of the pons and covered an area between the inferior colliculi and the caudal border of the pons.

The whole-brain images were processed through a pipeline comprising LCPCA-based denoising of the anatomical magnitude images, hMRI-toolbox4 (0.6.1) based quantitative parameter maps calculation, employing incomplete spoiling correction, and two-iteration weighted least squares R2* fitting. The LC-specific MT-TFL images were processed through a segmentation pipeline, which included ANTs-based co-alignment of the images to a subject-specific anatomical space and manual delineation of the LC based on voxel intensities and prior knowledge of the LC anatomy by two raters for each participant (Figure 1). The same raters also have delineated a reference region in the pontine tegmentum. 

Next, an intersection of LC masks for all the raters was used to create individual consensus LC masks, similarly consensus masks of reference region in the pons were created for each participant. The  values of R1, R2*, PD and MTsat, as well as the intensity values of the MT-TFL images were extracted from the combined left and right LC regions and the reference region, and outliers in each region and each participant were removed using generalized extreme Studentized deviate test for outliers. LC-contrast in the MT-TFL images was calculated as the difference between the median intensity in the LC region and the median intensity in the reference region, relative to the latter. Quantitative contrast was calculated as the difference between median values of the corresponding quantitative parameter in the LC and reference region. The resulting quantitative contrasts were used as predictor variables (ΔR1, ΔR2*, ΔPD and ΔMTsat) and response variables (LC-contrast) in general linear models built in MATLAB 2022b.

Results

High-resolution quantitative maps of human brain were obtained and co-aligned with MT-weighted TFL images of the LC region. The consensus LC masks varied between participants from 96 voxels to 537 voxels (with median consensus mask size of 276 voxels). The consensus pons reference masks varied between participants from 1176 voxels to 6300 voxels (with mean consensus mask size of 4050 voxels). Median values of R1, R2*, PD and MTsat, as well as the values for LC-contrast were extracted for 28 participants from the resulting dataset.

The linear models yielded borderline significant dependence of the LC-contrast on ΔR1 and ΔPD, and a trend for ΔR2* dependence (Figure 2). Notably, the observed dependence in ΔR1 (53±25 p.u/s-1, p < 0.042) and ΔPD (0.89±0.39 p.u.(LC-contrast)/p.u.(PD), p < 0.033) was for LC-contrast being positively correlated to quantitative values, and in line with the previous results – negative for the ΔR2* trend (-0.37±0.20 p.u./s-1, p < 0.075). No relation with the ΔMTSat metric was observed (p > 0.80) despite the LC-contrast being driven primarily by MT-weighting.

Conclusion

These preliminary results, i.e., the correlations detected between the LC-contrast and the quantitative MR parameters suggest multiple microstructural mechanisms affecting the LC-contrast. While neuromelanin and iron content, considered to be the primary driver for the LC-contrast, can result5 in the change of R1 and R2*, the change in PD is not directly rooted in the neuromelanin content. The lack of dependence on MTSat is potentially due to different MT approaches in the MPM and MT-TFL sequences, and thus MTSat and LC-contrast being related to distinctly different  MT processes, while both being MT-related. This underlines the need for a comprehensive evaluation of the LC-contrast, being conventionally considered MT-driven, but as suggested by the presented results, comprising a mixture of contrast mechanisms.

The complexity of quantitative brain mapping acquisition and processing schemes, and on the contrary the apparent simplicity of MT-TFL acquisitions, leverages the LC-contrast derived from latter to be considered the primary option of LC integrity assessment. In that context it is important to gain a comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms causing the change in LC-contrast. This is the area where studies combining quantitative measurements in the LC with LC-contrast, theoretical evaluation of the LC-contrast origins and biophysical models will be of utter importance.
