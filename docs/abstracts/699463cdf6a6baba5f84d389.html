Introduction

Introduction: 

Parkinson's disease (PD) is one of the most common neurodegenerative disorders. Whilst typically thought of as a movement disorder, it can also cause a wide range of non-motor symptoms (NMS).

Anxiety is a common and often debilitating NMS in PD that can occur at any disease stage, including many years before motor diagnosis. This suggests that it is likely due to PD related damage to the structures that underpin anxiety, however the neuropathological basis remains poorly understood. 

Quantitative MRI (qMRI) is a microstructural imaging method that quantifies multiple physical properties of brain tissue in vivo representing surrogate biomarkers of underlying biological features, previously only observed histologically post-mortem. 

qMRI parameters and their biological interpretation include: 

  • Magnetisation Transfer saturation (MTsat): emerges from protons bound to macromolecules, largely located within myelinated white matter and thought to reflect myelin quantity.
  • Proton Density: approximates MRI-visible water concentration in tissue. 
  • R1: the rate of longitudinal relaxation, dependent on the mobility of water molecules, the local macromolecular environment, and on iron content.
  • R2*: the rate of effective transverse relaxation, dependent on perturbers of the local field, including paramagnetic materials like iron which lead to increases. Thus R2* may be used as a marker of tissue iron.

The overall aim of this work is to use qMRI to define the microstructural basis for PD anxiety. Our pre-registered hypotheses are:

  1. Anxiety in Parkinson’s is due to microstructural damage to brain tissue within anxiety network structures 
  2. This damage is unique to PD, which will be in contrast to anxiety seen in control populations. Specifically, there will be evidence of damage in PD with anxiety compared to PD without, that is absent in controls with anxiety compared to controls without

Here we present the interim cortical results of the PD cohort analysis.

Methods

Methods: 

Prior to analysis, the full experimental design was pre-registered with the Open Science Foundation (currently under embargo).

Participants: 

A subset of PD participants diagnosed within the previous two years were taken from the ongoing, longitudinal observational study Quantitative MRI for Anatomical Phenotyping in Parkinson’s Disease (qMAP-PD). 99 met inclusion criteria for allocation into anxiety groups: 

1. PD diagnosis confirmed by a movement disorders consultant using formal diagnostic criteria.

2. MRI data successfully acquired. 

3. Complete Parkinson’s anxiety scale (PAS). 

Data collection: 

All participants completed a standard battery of assessments over two baseline study visits conducted within the same month. Variables used to contrast the cohorts were:

  • Age, sex, education, ethnicity.
  • PD disease and treatment duration, levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD), MDS-UPDRS Motor Examination (part III) and Hoehn & Yahr scale (H&Y) - the latter two were the only assessments completed in the off-medication state after participants withheld taking any PD medication the morning of the study visit.
  • Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). 
  • Anxiety measured with the self-rated PAS. Beck’s Depression Inventory and Starkstein Apathy Scale were also included.

Participants were allocated to the anxiety group (PDA) if they reached above cut-off in any of the three PAS subscales or overall. The 18 least anxious scores (all between 0 and 2 and below any cut-off) were selected for the contrasting no anxiety group (PDN).

The MRI scanning session was conducted on a 3T whole-body MRI system (MAGNETOM Prisma-fit, Siemens Healthcare) using a 64-channel coil. The multi-parameter mapping (MPM) protocol encompassed 3D, multi-echo FLASH scans with T1, proton density or MTsat weighting at 0.8mm isotropic resolution.1

Anxiety networks: 

Two a priori anxiety networks were created based on a systematic review of the literature (Fig.1, 1.1 and 1.2) and uploaded to Zenodo prior to analysis2,3:

  1. Parkinson’s anxiety network: Regions reported in structural or microstructural PD anxiety literature. 
  2. High-Density Anxiety Network: Parkinson’s anxiety network plus additional regions identified as relevant for anxiety from non-PD literature

 Image pre-processing: 

MPM images were pre-processed using the hMRI toolbox.4 Quantitative susceptibility maps (QSM) were estimated using the MPM QSM pipeline (https://github.com/fil-physics/MPM_QSM). To minimise QSM streak artefacts, vessels were segmented from QSM and R2* data using a Frangi filter, removed from the QSM phase data, and re-calculated to allow more tissue-specific measures.  

A two-pass warping-segmentation pipeline was used: Following standard SPM shoot group template calculation, images were re-segmented using eight bespoke tissue priors based on independent qMRI data (using MTsat and proton-density), four cortical tissue classes (three GM, one WM) and four brainstem tissue classes (three GM, one WM). These were combined using SPM-Shoot creating the final group average template. For this analysis, warped weighted average tissue maps were calculated for the combined GM classes and WM. Cortical and brainstem data are analysed separately. For the presented cortical analysis, a 6mm FWHM Gaussian smoothing kernel was applied. Total intracranial volume (TIV) came from the SPM toolbox using the standard segmentations.

Analysis: 

MATLAB R2022a was used. Bonferroni correction was applied for each grouped analysis. Voxel-based morphometry (VBM) and voxel-based quantification (VBQ) were performed in SPM12 with age, sex and TIV as covariates to control for confounding effects. Whole-brain analyses were conducted. Results within the pre-registered ROI networks were considered significant at un-corrected p < 0.001 otherwise peak family-wise error (FWE) p < 0.05 was used. To understand the changes across parameter maps, we grouped them based on expected direction of change in neurodegeneration (Fig.1, 1.3).

Results

Results 

A. Cohort characteristics:

A total of 36 PD participants were allocated into anxiety groups (PDA = 18, PDN = 18).  Groups were matched for all demographic and PD characteristics and MoCA score. Anxiety, depression and apathy scores were significantly different between groups which is expected given the way cohorts were allocated.

B. Cortical microstructural changes:

Overall, there were 12 images analysed per participant (MTsat, proton density, R1, R2*, QSM and Jacobian images with 2 tissue maps each). No results survived FWE, therefore all results are uncorrected p<0.001 and only reported if they overlap with the previously defined anxiety circuits.  

Decreased microstructural integrity:

Regions suggestive of disease mediated tissue damage in PDA compared to PDN are summarized in Fig 2.1. Most prominent were measures suggesting increased iron, characterized by marked R2* increases in the following anxiety-network structures:

1. Bilateral hippocampi. 

2. Bilateral fusiform gyrus. 

3. Right anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and associated white matter. 

4. Smaller regions including: superior and middle frontal gyrus, medial orbital gyrus, right gyrus rectus, inferior temporal gyri and the white matter adjacent to the right nucleus accumbens. 

There were also extensive R2* increases in both parahippocampal gyri, however this region was not in the pre-specified network.

Related to this, there was also evidence of smaller regions of increased QSM in:

1. Bilateral insula. 

2. Left superior and inferior frontal gyrus. 

3. Left precuneus. 

Finally, the only non-iron related decrease in microstructural integrity was reduced MTsat adjacent to the left thalamus in PD anxiety. 

Increased microstructural integrity:

Regions showing changes that potentially reflect compensatory mechanisms or over-active networks in PDA compared to PDN are summarized in Fig 2.2.- These include a region in the left supramarginal gyrus (SMG) with focal increase in volume adjacent to increased MTsat. In addition to this, regions of increased MTsat were also observed in the left superior and inferior frontal gyrus, gyrus rectus and bilateral temporal lobes. There were also small regions of reduced QSM in both inferior frontal gyri (right > left), left insula and left superior and middle frontal gyrus, and reduced R2* in the right temporal lobe white matter. Proton density was also decreased in the right insula. 

C. Confounding influence of other mood disorders:

To address the potential confounding influence of apathy or depression scores, an additional analysis was performed post-hoc where these were added as covariates to the general linear model. Even accounting for these, the majority of decreases in microstructural integrity survived including: R2* increases in the GM of right pre-frontal cortex subregions, right ACC and left hippocampus, parahippocampal gyrus and fusiform gyrus, as well as QSM increases in the GM of the left superior frontal gyrus, insula and precuneus and right hippocampus and bilateral fusiform gyrus. The following increases in microstructural integrity survived: QSM decreases in the GM of the inferior frontal gyrus bilaterally and left middle frontal gyrus, as well as the WM of the left gyrus rectus, MTsat increases in the GM of the left SMG and right fusiform gyrus and R2* decrease in the WM of the right hippocampus. 

Conclusion

Conclusion

Differences in microstructural integrity in PD anxiety were demonstrated in the PFC, ACC, hippocampus, fusiform gyrus, insula, SMG, inferior temporal gyrus, nucleus accumbens, precuneus and thalamus, overlapping with a priori anxiety networks. This suggests Parkinson’s anxiety arises from regional microstructural damage, with possible compensatory changes. The most prominent and widespread changes were consistent with increased iron deposition in key anxiety related structures such as both hippocampi and the ACC, in addition to parahippocampal and fusiform gyri. 

The current analysis demonstrates advantages of using multimodal quantitative MRI in early PD. Our findings overlap with regional atrophy also reported in a similar analysis of PD anxiety.5 Whilst this study was in “de novo” cases, the PD anxiety cohort presented here had a lower average disease duration in comparison (1.2 years versus 2 years). There is evidence from Alzheimer’s literature that increases in iron-mediated measures happen a year or two prior to detectable atrophy. Whilst we did not detect much volume loss in the current analysis, this may not occur until later in the disease course, and we instead show that qMRI metrics provide sensitive measures of change in the absence of volumetric changes. 

Furthermore, changes in tissue iron can influence the T1w MRI signal, and result in the artificial appearance of increased volume measurements using standard pipelines. Being able to fully characterize regional microstructural profiles provides a way to better understand any regional changes. 

The approach of using uncorrected p-values at the whole-brain level with previously defined regions is commonly published in literature. Nevertheless, the increased risk of false positives at the uncorrected level should be emphasised. 

This current work will expand the presented analysis to also include brain-stem structures, and will repeat the same contrasts in healthy control anxiety vs. no anxiety groups, allowing us to define if PD-related anxiety is unique and different to anxiety experienced in the general population. Additionally, as qMAP-PD is a longitudinal study, in the future we will be able to explicitly test if the increased iron deposition at baseline progresses to atrophy over the longer term.

In summary, this work suggests anxiety in early-stage Parkinson’s is largely related to disease-related tissue damage to the hippocampi, cingulate and fusiform regions, and these changes are best captured using iron-sensitive MRI sequences.
