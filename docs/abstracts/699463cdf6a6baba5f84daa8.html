Introduction

Auditory perception depends on dynamic interactions between temporal auditory cortex and frontal regions implicated in higher-order sound processing, yet the representational structure and directionality of this communication remain unresolved. Along the auditory ventral stream, neural responses progress from primary and non-primary auditory cortex toward superior temporal and inferior frontal regions, which are frequently described as forming a functional hierarchy1. However, it is unclear whether frontal auditory fields encode distinct representational motifs or instead participate in recurrent communication that preserves canonical auditory tuning inherited from temporal cortex. Previous neuroimaging and intracranial studies have demonstrated sound-responsive activity in human ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (vlPFC), orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), and frontal operculum, but the computational role of these regions remains debated2–4. In particular, whether temporal–frontal interactions are predominantly feedforward, feedback, or bidirectional, and how these interactions depend on cortical source, hemisphere, and development, has not been systematically tested in humans.

Here, we combined spectrotemporal receptive field (STRF) modeling with population communication models (PCMs) applied to human intracranial EEG (iEEG) recordings to test whether (1) ventral frontal auditory regions share representational geometry with superior temporal cortex and (2) temporal–frontal communication is bidirectional but asymmetric, with distinct source-specific, hemispheric, and age-dependent effects.

Methods

We analyzed iEEG recordings from 31 patient-participants (aged 8–24 years) undergoing invasive monitoring for epilepsy at UPMC Children’s Hospital of Pittsburgh or UPMC Presbyterian Hospital. Electrode implantation was determined solely by clinical need. All participants or guardians provided informed consent under IRB approval.

Participants listened to a 165-sound naturalistic stimulus set including speech, music, animal vocalizations, environmental, and mechanical sounds (1.5 s duration; RMS-normalized), presented during an auditory one-back task to maintain attention. Neural responses were analyzed using broadband high-gamma activity (70–150 Hz).

Each electrode was anatomically localized and assigned to temporal auditory ROIs (A1, Belt, Parabelt, STG) and frontal auditory ROIs (vlPFC, OFC, FOP, premotor and dorsolateral frontal cortex). Spectrotemporal tuning was estimated using the Maximally Informative Dimensions (MID) framework to generate STRF-like filters for each channel5. Representational similarity across ROIs was quantified using symmetric and asymmetric rank-biserial correlation (RBC) statistics.

Directed between region communication was assessed using Population Communication Models (PCMs). Ridge-regularized linear–nonlinear models were trained to predict single-channel responses in a target ROI from lagged population activity in a source ROI using whole-trial train/validation/test splits (70/15/15%). Predictive performance was evaluated on held-out trials using Pearson correlation (ρ). Feedforward (temporal→frontal) and reverse (frontal→temporal) models were compared using Wilcoxon tests and linear mixed-effects models of the form ρ ~ age + hemisphere + (1|channel).

Results

STRF analyses revealed canonical spectrotemporal tuning motifs across the auditory ventral stream. Representational similarity analyses showed that early auditory regions (A1, Belt, Parabelt) formed a distinct low-level subspace, while STG exhibited substantial overlap with ventral frontal regions (vlPFC and OFC). Non-ventral frontal regions showed weak tuning cohesion, indicating diffuse acoustic representations.

PCMs revealed bidirectional but asymmetric temporal–frontal communication. In feedforward models (temporal→frontal), predictive performance depended strongly on temporal source. Parabelt and STG significantly outperformed A1 and Belt (A1 vs Parabelt: p = 0.030; A1 vs STG: p = 2.5×10⁻⁵; Belt vs STG: p = 8×10⁻⁶), while Parabelt and STG did not differ from each other. Overall feedforward accuracy was modest (median ρ ≈ 0.08–0.12). Mixed-effects modeling revealed no effect of age (β = −0.001, p = 0.71) but a robust hemispheric effect (β = −0.058, p = 7.4×10⁻⁶), reflecting weaker prediction when the left hemisphere served as the source.

Reverse models (frontal→temporal) yielded substantially higher predictive accuracy (median ρ ≈ 0.20–0.30). Pairwise tests showed that FOP and vlPFC significantly outperformed OFC as sources (all p < 10⁻¹²). Unlike feedforward models, reverse prediction strength increased robustly with age (β = 0.011, p = 5.8×10⁻⁸), while no overall hemispheric effect was observed (β = −0.006, p = 0.78). Source-specific analyses indicated that age-related gains were strongest for FOP and vlPFC-driven models.

Conclusion

These results demonstrate that frontal auditory regions do not form an independent representational code but instead share spectrotemporal tuning geometry with superior temporal cortex while engaging in strong recurrent communication. Temporal→frontal prediction is modest, source-specific, and hemispherically biased, whereas frontal→temporal prediction is substantially stronger and increases with development. Together, these findings support a model in which ventral frontal auditory regions participate in delayed, top-down predictive signaling that modulates canonical auditory representations.
