Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is the largest contributor to death and disability among all trauma-related injuries and has the highest incidence among all neurological disorders. Only 10% of TBI cases admitted to hospitals are reported to be severe or moderate, while 90% of those cases are a milder form of TBI, called mild TBI (mTBI). The neuronal damage following concussion can mainly be attributed to the biomechanical acceleration and deceleration forces. While both linear and rotational acceleration can lead to mTBI, rotational forces were identified to be more deleterious, particularly for the anisotropic white matter (WM) tracts due to the shear and strain they induce. The resultant disruption to the brittle microtubules within the axons leads to a multifocal or diffuse axonal injury (DAI), a hallmark of mTBI. 

Modelling brain injuries have shown that it is not the magnitude, but rather the tract-oriented strain that causes the axonal damage. In these studies, most of the brain deformation comes from strain components orthogonal to the axis of rotation. While histological studies had lent support to that model, the distribution of this orientation-dependence of damage yet to be explored in vivo. 

Diffusion MRI (dMRI) is a widely used structural MRI technique that uses water diffusion inside tissues as a proxy for microstructural integrity. While dMRI can investigate the microstructural changes induced by mTBI, resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) has the protentional to reveal the functional ramifications of WM injury. Studies that used dMRI and/or rs-fMRI have yielded contradictory results in terms of affected structures and direction of change.

In the current study1, we predicted that WM orientated orthogonally to the axis of rotation produced by mTBI will be preferentially damaged and therefore, direct and indirect functional connectivity will be diminished. 

Methods

Thirty-one 6 months old transgenic animals carrying the APOE3 allele and humanized versions of MAPT and APP were divided into two groups, sham (n = 15, 7 males 8 females) and mTBI group (n = 16, 8 males and females). The injury procedure has been described in2. Briefly, the animals were anesthetized using Ketamine and xylazine then placed on a custom-built acrylic box, covered with a pre-pierced sheet of clear plastic. A TBI device (TBI 0310, Precision Systems and Instrumentation, LLC) equipped with a custom-built, pliant, silicon tip was used to deliver the impact with 8.0 mm depth at a 3.5 m/s velocity on the bregma. Following the impact, the animal broke through the plastic top, rotated 180° around the left-right axis, and landed on the padded bottom of the box. The animals were then allowed to recover and were examined for any skull fracture or motor abnormalities. The procedure was repeated 3 times with a 24-hr interval. The sham animals received the same anesthesia, but without the injury.

Animals were anesthesia throughout the scan using isoflurane (4% for induction, 1.5-1.8 for maintenance) in 100% oxygen with 1L/min flow rate. The scans were performed using 9.4T Bruker animal scanner. All animals were scanned at baseline, then 1-week after the last impact. Anatomical, diffusion, and functional scans were acquired for both sessions. The dMRI protocol included a pulsed gradient spin echo (PGSE) sequence (i.e., 0 Hz) with gradient duration of 9.4 ms (diffusion time = 12.3 ms) and oscillating gradient (OGSE) sequences with frequencies of 60 and 120 Hz (corresponding effective diffusion times of 2.3 and 1.2 ms). 

For all frequencies, data consisted of 2 volumes of b=0 and 2 shells of 1,000 and 2,500 s/mm2 with 10 directions. The dMRI data was acquired using single shot echo planar imaging (EPI) and parameters: TE/TR = 35.5/15000 ms, and resolution of 200 x 200 x 500 μm3.

T2*-weighted images for fMRI were acquired in 30 coronal slices using a gradient-echo EPI (GE-EPI) sequence covering the whole brain with parameters: TE/TR of 12/1500 ms, 60° flip angle, and resolution of 300 × 300 × 500 μm3. Four runs with 600 volumes each were acquired consecutively for a total of 60 min. An additional T2-weighted anatomical image was acquired in the same space with the following parameters: TE/TR of 30/5500 ms, 8 averages, and voxel size of 150 × 150 × 500 μm3.

The complex dMRI repetitions underwent partial Fourier reconstruction, frequency and signal drift correction, phase alignment, and denoising. 

The 1-week scans for each subject were registered to their corresponding baseline scans. Deterministic whole brain tractography was run on the baseline scans using ExploreDTI (Fig. 1d). For each tract, the main eigenvector was used to arrange tracts into different orientation groups relative to the injury’s axis of rotation (left-right axis). A tract density map was computed for each orientation group (Fig. 1e) and the mean of various dMRI metrics was calculated for both sessions for each subject (Fig. 1C). 

FMRI data went through standard preprocessing steps including motion correction, brain extraction, coregisteration to anatomical image, corrected for field distortions, nuisance regression, high-pass filtering, and spatial smoothing.

The common coordinate framework v3 (CCFv3)3  parcellations were used to create two annotation atlases, a coarse regions atlas and a more refined subregions atlas. Functional connectivity (FC) was calculated between each pair of regions in both atlases.

The mouse connectivity atlas4 was used to define regions anatomically connected by various WM tracts that run parallel (e.g. corpus callosum) or orthogonal (e.g. corticospinal tract) to the axis of rotation. FC was calculated between regions connected via those tracts.

Following the last scan, the animals were perfused, the brain were extracts, and some tissues were silver-stained. For immunohistochemistry, the tissues were stained for GFAP and Iba-1.

Results

In the current study, we used dMRI, fMRI and histological techniques to investigate how tracts oriented orthogonally to the axis of rotation change following an mTBI.

Diffusional kurtosis unveils tract orientation dependent damage following mTBI

Mean kurtosis tensor (MKT) and radial kurotsis (RK) showed a larger decrease in the tracts oriented orthogonal to the axis of rotation following mTBI. The changes were significant in the 80-90º tract bin. Further analysis revealed sex differences in both groups. In the mTBI group, only females showed significant decreases in the MKT and RK metrics, especially at the 60 Hz frequency. 

Parcellating the brain into regions and subregions annotation atlases

To better estimate FC between brain regions, we downsampled the CCFv3 parcellation atlas to create regions and subregions atlases. The regions atlas (Fig. 2e) consisted of 22 anatomical regions (11 per hemisphere), while the subregions one consisted of 82 parcels (41 per hemisphere). 

mTBI induces wide decrease in FC across the brain

One week following the injury, the mTBI animals showed widespread decrease in FC relative to the baseline values (Fig. 2b). The largest decreases in FC was in the long-range connections that run from the frontal and temporal regions to the more caudal regions (Fig. 1e). The correlation strengths between rostral regions such as the olfactory regions and the hindbrain and the cerebellum showed the biggest decrease. 

The subregions analysis was used to evaluate if the region-specific changes can be attributed to the underlying subregions and to uncover finer changes that might have been masked by combining multiple structures together. 

This analysis has revealed that the disconnection between isocortex and the posterior regions might be driven by diminished connectivity between the frontal pole, the somatosensory/somatomotor cortex, the anterior cingulate cortex, the agranular insular cortex, and the temporal association areas with regions of the midbrain, hindbrain and cerebellum.

Other changes that were masked in the regions analysis, such as those between right isocortex and left midbrain and left olfactory areas and left cerebellum were detected in the subregions analysis. Comparing males and females did not yield any difference in both groups. 

mTBI causes disconnections between regions directly connected by WM tracts orthogonal to axis of rotation. 

To further investigate the functional consequences of orientation-dependent damage to the WM tracts, we calculated the FC between regions connected by those tracts and compared them to others connected by fibers running parallel to the axis of rotation. In contrast to atlas-based FC, this approach guarantees that there is a direct anatomical connection between the regions tested. The FC served by the corticospinal tract showed the most changes among all the tracts tested including fibers that run parallel to the axis of rotation such as the corpus callosum.  

Histological findings:

To validate our MRI results, we performed silver staining to assess axonal pathology and immunohistochemistry to evaluate inflammation in tracts that are oriented at 0º-50º (body of the corpus callosum) and those oriented at 80º-90 º (the cingulum bundle and the optic) relative to the axis of rotation produced by the mTBI. WM tracts run orthogonal to the axis of rotation showed pronounced and widespread silver-stained fibers in mTBI animals. While silver-stained fibers were absent from the body of the corpus callosum and from both groups of fibers in the sham group.

In mTBI mice, the GFAP and Iba-1 staining were more prevalent. More activated microglia were identified in contrast to the resting microglia observed in the sham group

Conclusion

In conclusion, we show that after rotational mTBI, WM tracts that are oriented orthogonally to the axis of injury experience the most damage in terms of changes in microstructural integrity detected with dMRI. Those changes lead to loss of correlation strength between regions connected (directly or indirectly) by those fiber tracts. Histological investigation further lends support to these results as fibers orientated orthogonally to the axis of rotation show more silver staining and inflammation. Furthermore, our study showed that females suffer from greater reduction in kurtosis metrics than their male counterparts. While these changes were observed in dMRI analysis, they were absent from FC analysis, which can be attributed to the fact that fMRI can be more noisy that structural MRI.
