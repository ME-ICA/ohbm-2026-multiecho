Introduction

Understanding how individual differences in brain activity relate to behavior is a central goal in cognitive neuroscience. Naturalistic paradigms such as movie watching offer a powerful way to examine complex, real-world brain dynamics beyond traditional controlled tasks. However, it remains unclear whether interindividual variability in neural responses to naturalistic stimuli carries behavioral relevance. Previous work using measures of neural typicality—the degree to which an individual’s neural time course resembles the group average—has revealed stable individual differences in social and perceptual processing (Ramot et al., 2020; Nastase et al., 2019). Yet, the behavioral meaning of such neural alignment remains largely unexplored.
 Here, we investigated whether neural typicality during naturalistic movie viewing predicts behavioral performance across multiple cognitive domains. Our approach enabled us to test whether neural alignment with the group response is associated with improved behavioral performance and whether these relationships depend on the movie’s content and cognitive demands.

Methods

Forty-seven healthy adults underwent fMRI scanning at 7 Tesla while watching three five-minute movie clips: two depicting rich social interactions and one depicting dynamic object scenes. The movies were chosen to engage distinct perceptual and social-cognitive processes. Each participant also completed a comprehensive behavioral battery across three separate days, including measures of sustained attention, working memory, motion prediction, empathic accuracy, and gaze-based social sensitivity.

Voxelwise measures of neural typicality were computed by correlating each participant’s time course with the group-average response for the same movie, generating a typicality map for each individual and film. These maps quantified how closely each voxel’s activation tracked the shared temporal dynamics of the stimulus. Behavioral scores were then correlated with voxel-wise typicality across participants for each movie. Statistical significance was evaluated using 10,000-permutation nonparametric testing with cluster-level FDR correction (p < 0.05). Spatial convergence across movies and behavioral domains was examined to identify domain-specific and integrative neural signatures.

Results

Across stimuli, greater neural typicality in functionally relevant brain networks predicted superior behavioral performance, revealing domain-specific brain–behavior associations. In general, we found effects that were both broad and content specific. During the object-based movie, higher typicality in the dorsal attention stream was associated with better performance on sustained attention and motion prediction tasks, as well as with object-gaze typicality and object-based working memory, consistent with efficient perceptual–attentional processing which does not necessarily include a social component (Figure 1, right)..In contrast, during the social movie, typicality in language-related regions predicted more accurate emotion ratings, while typicality in the temporoparietal junction (TPJ) and frontal social-cognitive areas correlated with biological motion prediction and social-gaze typicality (Figure 1, right)..Overlap analyses revealed convergence across behavioral domains, suggesting the existence of multimodal hubs that integrate perceptual, cognitive, and social information, supporting a link between neural typicality and cognitive efficiency.

Conclusion

These results align with models of neural efficiency and predictive coding, suggesting that individuals whose neural dynamics closely follow shared stimulus-driven structure use more optimized processing strategies that minimize prediction errors. Beyond their theoretical relevance, these findings highlight the translational potential of naturalistic paradigms: deviations from typical neural alignment could serve as sensitive markers of atypical processing in clinical populations. Together, these results demonstrate that greater alignment with shared neural responses during movie viewing reflects functional efficiency and integration across cognitive systems. Neural typicality thus provides a robust, generalizable marker of cognitive organization and social competence. Though some brain-behavior correlations generalized across movies, different movie content uncovered specific relationships, broadening the potential usefulness and customizability of this metric.
