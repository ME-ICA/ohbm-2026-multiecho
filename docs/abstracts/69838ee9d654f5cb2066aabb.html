Introduction

Non-invasive deep brain stimulation is a central goal in neuromodulation, holding great promise for both understanding brain function and treating neurological and psychiatric disorders. Temporal interference (TI) stimulation is a promising non-invasive technique for deep brain neuromodulation (Grossman et al., 2017). However, a key question remains unresolved: whether TI can effectively modulate deep brain neuronal activity in living humans, and how this modulation can be achieved, and if so, through which specific components of the excitatory-inhibitory balance this modulation is achieved. This has hindered the further promotion and application of this technology. Currently, findings in the field of TI research are largely confined to animal models or indirect human evidence from behavioral and fMRI studies, with inconsistent findings (Vassiliadis et al., 2024; Violante et al., 2023). These inconsistencies highlight the inadequate mechanistic understanding of how TI regulates the neurochemical environment at the neuronal and molecular levels. Neurotransmitters, serving as direct indicators of neuronal activity, are essential for neural signal transmission, such as the excitatory (Glutamate) and the inhibitory (γ-aminobutyric acid, GABA) neurotransmitter. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate whether and how TI stimulation can effectively modulate the neurochemical activity of the glutamatergic and GABAergic systems in PCC of living humans, and thereby influence the Excitatory-Inhibitory balance within these areas.

Methods

We combined individualized TI simulation with magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS). In Experiment 1, MRS data from the PCC were acquired from 25 healthy adults before and after receiving TI stimulation under three conditions: high-frequency (130 Hz, a commonly used high frequency in deep brain stimulation for treating diseases), low-frequency (5 Hz, a typical frequency range of the PCC during resting state, falls within the theta band: 4-7 Hz), and Sham. Spectra were obtained using both MEGA-PRESS and PRESS sequences to quantify stimulation-induced changes in GABA and Glx (Glutamate + Glutamine) levels. In Experiment 2, an independent cohort of 30 participants received 130 Hz TI stimulation while MRS data were acquired from both the target PCC and the off-target Cortical ROI to validate the targeted neurochemical modulation of TI.

Results

To evaluate the neurochemical effects of high- and low-frequency TI stimulation in the PCC, we measured changes in the inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA and the excitatory neurotransmitter Glx before and after TI stimulation. Our results revealed that both 130 Hz and 5 Hz TI stimulation significantly reduced GABAergic concentration in the PCC, with minimal effects on Glx. This selective reduction in inhibition led to a significant shift in the local E-I balance toward excitation. Moreover, Results from Experiment 2 showed that 130 Hz TI stimulation selectively induced significant shifts in the E-I balance within the target PCC, while no such changes were observed in the off-target Cortical ROI, which was minimally affected by the electric field.

Conclusion

This study provides the first direct evidence that TI stimulation can effectively and focally modulate the neurochemical environment of deep brain regions in living humans. The results demonstrate that TI stimulation, whether at high frequency or low frequency, consistently reduced the concentration of the inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA in the PCC. Supported by direct evidence from neurotransmitter measurements in this study, we propose a TI action mechanism framework that aims to integrate the existing disparate results, in hopes of providing a unified explanatory pathway for understanding the long-standing issue of parameter-dependent effects in this field. TI stimulation may operate through two concurrent mechanisms: entrainment and disruptive interference. Stimulation frequency and duration jointly influence which mechanism predominates. By leveraging the low-pass filtering properties of neurons, low-frequency TI generates a low-frequency envelope in deep brain regions that approximates endogenous rhythmic activity. This envelope may exert an “entrainment effect” on neuronal populations. On the other hand, high-frequency TI stimulation may exert regulatory effects by interfering with endogenous neural oscillations. During prolonged high-frequency TI stimulation, the nervous system may activate homeostatic regulatory mechanisms by reducing the level of the inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA to counteract interference and attempt to restore normal rhythms. By contrast, with short-duration stimulation, low frequency is insufficient to induce entrainment, while high frequency may cause temporary disruption of neural activity. Future research should employ real-time TI-MRS to explore the dynamic processes of TI stimulation, thereby refining the physiological mechanism framework for its regulation of neural activity, and ultimately promoting the application of this technology in cognitive science research and clinical medicine.
