Introduction

Dysfunctional fear and extinction learning are key features of posttraumatic stress disorder and anxiety-related disorders.1 Fear extinction does not represent the erasure of an original memory, but rather the formation of a new inhibitory "safety memory" that competes with the fear trace. Neuroimaging studies have delineated a network involving the amygdala, hippocampus, and ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) in the encoding of these responses.2,3 To our knowledge, no previous study has investigated the contributions of single neurons or local-field potentials (LFPs) toward fear acquisition and extinction in humans. This represents a critical gap, as LFPs reflect the summed synaptic activity of local neuronal populations—vital for understanding the synchronization of local assemblies—while single-unit recordings provide the most direct measure of neural computation. We recently analyzed macrowire iEEG data from the current cohort to map the representational dynamics of fear extinction, revealing how safety and threat contexts are differentiated across the network.4 Here, we aimed to build on these findings by using simultaneous microwire recordings to investigate the contribution of local neuronal populations and micro-LFPs. We collected intracranial microelectrode data from 19 patients with drug-resistant epilepsy while they performed a differential fear conditioning and extinction paradigm. We tested three primary hypotheses regarding cellular activity in the amygdala: 1) Neurons exhibit higher firing rates for the unconditioned stimulus (US) compared to no-US; 2) Firing rates will discriminate between the conditioned stimulus (CS+) and the safe stimulus (CS-) during acquisition; and 3) Firing rate differences will diminish during extinction. Additionally, we conducted preregistered exploratory analyses of microwire LFP data across the amygdala and hippocampus to investigate how oscillatory dynamics coordinate with these changes in single-neuron activity.

Methods

Participants: 
Nineteen patients with medically intrac­table epilepsy were included. Data collection took place at Pitié Salpêtrière Hospital, Paris. The study was approved by the local Institutional Review Board. All patients provided written informed consent.
 
Experimental Design: 
Stimuli comprised three electrical device images (a hair dryer, a ventilator, and a toaster) presented across acquisition, extinction, and test phases. We used the “unlucky backpacker” paradigm, wherein the participant is asked to imagine that they are a backpacker who visits various landscapes on holiday. Each of these landscapes represent a specific context (e.g., beach, mountain, etc.), and in each trial, they are encountered with an electrical device. Some devices are faulty, and can give an electric shock (CS+), whereas others are neutral, with no electric shock experienced (CS-). The Unconditioned Stimulus (US) combined a negative facial expression with an unpleasant scream (1 s). This intensity was calibrated to be highly aversive but non-painful. Extinction modified contingencies to create three histories: CS++ (consistent threat), CS+- (extinguished threat), and CS-- (consistent safety). The experiment was administered using Presentation software (Neurobehavioral Systems) on a Windows laptop. 

Behavior:
Behavioral differences were assessed via two-way repeated measures ANOVA (factors: trial type, phase) on average ratings, utilizing Bonferroni-corrected post hoc tests. To track progressive learning, we compared ordinal trial positions across conditions (CS++, CS+−, CS−−) using non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, applying cluster-based permutation statistics to correct for multiple comparisons.

iEEG recordings:
All microwire data were recorded via a Neuralynx amplifier at a sampling rate of 32,764 Hz. Each patient was implanted with 1-4 AdTech microwire bundles, with each bundle containing eight channels. Macroelectrode implantation sites were dictated solely by clinical needs. Bipolar LFP referencing was not possible due to a lack of microwire MNI coordinates, so we used the most reliable reference: either the hardware reference (dedicated 9th reference microwire) or a manually selected quiet channel. We selected three regions-of-interest (ROIs), namely, the amygdala, hippocampus, and entorhinal cortex.

Univariate analysis:
All raw data were preprocessed and spike sorted using wave_clus, with the default parameters. Firing rate analyses were conducted using cluster-based permutation analyses, across different phases (e.g., acquisition, extinction) and stimuli contrasts (CS+ vs CS-; US vs no-US; CS++ vs CS+- vs CS --).

LFP pipeline:
After re-referencing the data using the acquisition reference or local reference, we applied a standardized artefact rejection pipeline, based on a previous publication from our group.4 High-amplitude, non-physiological artifacts were automatically detected using a literature-verified standard deviation threshold. Artifact periods were temporarily replaced with NaNs. To create a continuous signal, the NaN gaps were filled in using linear interpolation from the surrounding clean data. The data were then band-pass filtered to keep frequencies between 1-120 Hz. A notch filter was used to remove 50 Hz electrical line noise. Finally, the data were downsampled to 1,000 Hz. The result was one data file per bundle containing the cleaned, preprocessed LFP data in a standard FieldTrip format, ready for direct input for time-frequency power analysis. We used variable wavelet cycles across the 0-100 Hz range (3-25 cycles, with a minimum of 3, and otherwise n/4 where n represents the frequency of interest) for improved time-frequency resolution at lower and higher frequencies. We also conducted a robustness check by changing the wavelet cycles range from 3-25 cycles to 5-15 cycles, to confirm that the observed results remain robust across different ranges of wavelet cycles.

Results

1. Amygdala (Acquisition); univariate: Firing rate analysis of amygdala neuronal spiking during fear acquisition revealed a significant cluster of increased firing rates for US relative to no-US trials, localized to the 475–525 ms post-stimulus window. A total of 24 neurons were included in the amygdala ROI. 

2. Amygdala (Extinction); micro-LFP: During the extinction phase, the amygdala displayed a significant reduction in power following US delivery compared to omission. This cluster of suppression spanned a broadband frequency range (~3–50 Hz) and occurred late in the epoch (1.1–1.3 s).

3. Hippocampus (Acquisition); micro-LFP: In the hippocampus, the acquisition phase was characterized by a specific cluster of enhanced spectral power in response to the US, compared to the omission of US (following CS- trials). This effect was observed in the high-gamma band (80–100 Hz) during the 1.1–1.3 s interval.

4. Hippocampus (Extinction); micro-LFP: Conversely, during extinction, the hippocampus exhibited a widespread decrease in power for the US versus no-US contrast. This significant suppression covered a broadband range (~1–50 Hz) and extended from 0.6 to 1.2 s post-onset.

5. Hippocampus (Combined); micro-LFP: Combining both acquisition and extinction phases for the US vs. no-US contrast revealed a biphasic spectral signature in the hippocampus. An initial cluster of increased high-gamma activity (~78–100 Hz) was observed at 0.2–0.45 s, followed by a significant suppression of low-frequency power (~3–18 Hz) within the 1.0–1.5 s time window.

Conclusion

We identified neural signatures of aversive outcome detection at both single-unit and LFP levels in human amygdala and hippocampus during fear conditioning. During fear acquisition, amygdala neurons rapidly differentiated aversive outcomes from their absence, showing a significant increase in firing around 475-525 ms post onset. In the amygdala during extinction, US delivery was followed by a late suppression of broadband activity (~3–50 Hz, 1.1–1.3 s) relative to US omission, indicating a safety signal. The increased theta power for safe versus aversive outcomes during extinction corroborates our recently published macrowire findings using the same dataset.4 On the other hand, our current finding indicates an increase in amygdala theta power for US vs no-US that goes beyond the theta range and extends into the alpha and gamma bands. We believe that this represents a broadband safety signal, and constitutes a neural signature of fear extinction in the amygdala. During acquisition, the hippocampus exhibited a late enhancement of high-frequency gamma activity (80–100 Hz, 1.1–1.3 s) following the US, compared to no-US (following CS-). We speculate that this result indicates the declarative nature of fear learning, based on the assumption that increased hippocampal activity represents declarative memory encoding. During extinction, the hippocampus showed a significant suppression of broadband activity (~1–50 Hz, 0.6–1.2 s) following US delivery compared to omission. This result is very similar to that observed in the amygdala during extinction, suggesting that the safety signal observed in the amygdala may be consolidated further in the hippocampus. It would be interesting to investigate the directional information flow between these structures, potentially using Granger causality, to determine whether the amygdala suppression temporally precedes and drives the hippocampal response. Across both acquisition and extinction, the hippocampus exhibited a biphasic response to the US: an early enhancement of high-gamma activity (~78–100 Hz, 0.2–0.45 s) followed by a late suppression of low-frequency power (~3–18 Hz, 1.0–1.5 s), reflecting a dynamic temporal shift from initial aversive encoding to a subsequent prolonged desynchronization.
 

Current and next steps: We are currently investigating spectral power differences between CS++, CS+−, and CS−− trials during extinction across all ROIs, employing an ANOVA-based cluster permutation framework to disentangle the specific neural signatures of threat persistence and safety learning. Next, we will conduct spike-field coherence analyses, as well as investigate the temporal overlap between single/multi-unit activity and micro-LFP activity at higher frequencies (100-150 Hz). Finally, we plan to conduct relevant phase-amplitude and phase-phase coupling analyses, with specific focus on interactions between amygdala theta and hippocampal gamma activity.
