Introduction

The balance between excitation and inhibition (E/I) neural signaling is a fundamental organizing principle of brain function and development, supporting neural computation, synaptic plasticity, and network stability. This dynamic equilibrium is particularly critical during early childhood – a sensitive period marked by rapid changes in brain structure, function, and metabolism. Disruptions in E/I balance have been reported in both adults and children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD).1,2 The Hurst exponent (H), derived from resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI), has emerged as a system-level proxy for E/I ratio by quantifying long-range temporal dependencies in BOLD signal.3 Our recent work focusing on the medial prefrontal cortex revealed differential age-related trajectories of H in preschoolers with ASD compared to typically developing (TD) peers.2 However, it remains unclear whether age-related differences in H are region-specific or reflect more widespread alterations across the cortex in early childhood in ASD. The objective of the present study is to comprehensively characterize developmental trajectories of H across the whole cortex in preschool children with and without ASD.

Methods

This study included young children with ASD and TD children, ages 1½-5 years, enrolled in a longitudinal study with up to three study timepoints. All children completed standardized behavioral assessments and underwent natural sleep MRI. ASD diagnoses were confirmed by a licensed clinical psychologist, supported by the Autism Diagnostic Schedule-2 and the Autism Diagnostic Interview-Revised. MRI data were acquired during natural sleep on two 3T scanners using 32-channel head coil: a GE Discovery MR750 and a Siemens Prisma. Resting-state fMRI consisted of 6-minute runs acquired with scanner-specific protocols. GE acquisition parameters were: TR = 800ms, TE = 35ms, flip angle = 52°, 72 slices, multiband factor = 8, 2 mm isotropic voxels. Siemens parameters were: TR = 1000ms, TE = 28ms, flip angle = 64°, 48 slices, multiband factor = 4, 2.5 mm isotropic voxels.

Rs-fMRI data were preprocessed with SPM12 and the CONN toolbox (v21), including motion correction, rigid-body realignment, outlier detection with the Artifact Detection Toolbox (ART), normalization to the Montreal Neurological Institute template, band-pass filtering (0.0008-0.08 Hz), and nuisance regression to remove physiological and motion-related confounds. Images were spatially smoothed with a 6mm full-width at half-maximum Gaussian kernel, and voxel time series were converted to percent signal change.

After preprocessing and quality assurance, 172 rs-fMRI scans were available from 81 children with ASD (61 males) and 55 TD children (28 males). Twenty-nine children (10 ASD) contributed more than one timepoint. The diagnostic groups did not differ in age at scan or image quality, indexed by root mean squared displacement (RMSD). Mean voxel-wise time series were extracted from cortical regions of interest (ROIs) defined by the Automated Anatomical Labeling atlas. The Hurst exponent (H) was estimated for each ROI using a discrete wavelet transform and fractionally integrated processes model with maximum likelihood estimation, implemented via the bfn_mfin_ml.m function in the nonfractal MATLAB toolbox. H values were averaged across left and right hemisphere homotopic ROIs, resulting in 37 ROIs. Scanner-related effects were mitigated using ComBat harmonization.4 

Linear mixed-effects models were used to examine the effects of age, diagnosis, and their interaction on H, with biological sex and RMSD included as covariates. Multiple comparisons were controlled using false discovery rate (FDR) correction.

Results

An age by diagnosis interaction was observed for the H in the precentral gyrus (t = 3.18; uncorrected p = 0.002; FDR q = 0.06). In TD children, H in the precentral gyrus increased with age, consistent with a developmental shift toward a more balanced E/I ratio after controlling for head motion (partial r = 0.35, p = 0.002). In contrast, no age-related association was observed in the ASD group (partial r = -0.10, p = 0.31; Fig. 1a). Across both diagnostic groups, significant age-related increases in H were identified in the inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), opercularis and triangularis (t = 4.84 and 4.00; FDR q = 0.0001 and 0.002, respectively; Fig. 1b). No significant effects of biological sex were identified. Ongoing work is underway on replicating these findings in an independent sample of 98 preschoolers with ASD (71 males), 43 of whom have scans at two yearly timepoints, to date. Results from this replication analysis will also be presented at the conference.

Conclusion

These findings indicate that the developmental trajectory of the Hurst exponent in the precentral gyrus differs between preschoolers with ASD and TD peers, potentially reflecting early alterations of E/I balance in the primary motor cortex in ASD. Given IFG’s role in language processing and cognitive control, the observed age-related increases in H across both groups likely reflect normative maturational processes supporting the language and executive function development in early childhood. Ongoing replication in an independent cohort will further support the relevance of these results for understanding early ASD neurobiology.
