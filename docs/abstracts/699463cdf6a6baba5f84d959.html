Introduction

Gradient analysis1 uses dimensionality reduction to identify a macroscale organizational hierarchy from functional connectivity (FC). In contrast, intersubject correlations2 (ISCs) indicate how similarly a given brain region responds to the same stimulus across participants. Despite describing brain organization from different perspectives, both measures capture core features of functional architecture by delineating default mode regions from task-positive brain regions. Here, we leverage movie-fMRI to generate movie gradients that have previously been shown to isolate sensory modalities. We then use ISC to map intersubject synchronization onto the functional hierarchy identified via gradient analyses. After identifying a robust and significant correlation between the visually-mediated functional gradient and intersubject correlations, we explore whether microstructural measures of myelin content mediate the spatial relationship between stimulus-driven brain activity and the naturalistic functional hierarchy. We expected to see an interaction between the gradient-ISC relationship with the T1w/T2w ratio, used here as a proxy for myelin3. In particular, we hypothesized that more highly myelinated regions would display a stronger correspondence between scores along the visually-mediated gradient and stimulus-evoked BOLD-signal responses, while less myelinated regions would exhibit a decreased correlation. 

Methods

Data. These analyses use minimally preprocessed 7T movie-watching data from the Human Connectome Project4. Ninety-five healthy adults (58 females, mean age 29.5 ± 3.3) from 64 families were selected based on head motion and data availability. One hour of movie data was collected over the course of four 15-minute runs across two sessions. All analyses were first conducted in a discovery dataset of 46 subjects and replicated in the remaining 49. For those participants who were also scanned at 3T (n=75), the T1w/T2w ratio was extracted and used as a proxy for myelin content3. 

Gradients-ISC correlations. Gradient analyses were performed parcel-wise using Schaefer-1000. Diffusion embedding was performed on FC matrices using the BrainSpace toolbox5, and individual gradients were aligned to a group-averaged template before being averaged. BOLD-signal timeseries data at the Schaefer-1000 level were used to compute ISCs using a group mean approach. Pearson’s correlations were computed between ISC and gradient maps, and spin tests were used to assess the significance of these correlations.

Associations with microstructure. A group mean map of myelin content was generated using the T1w/T2w ratio3, and Schaefer-1000 parcels were sorted into low, medium, and high myelin bins based on this ratio. To assess the effect of myelin content on the identified spatial relationships, Spearman’s correlations were then computed between gradient scores and intersubject correlations for parcels within each of these bins individually.

Results

We found a significant spatial correlation between ISC scores and Gradient 2 scores (r=0.74, p<0.001), and this relationship was reproduced in the replication dataset. Intersubject correlations were high in superior temporal and occipital regions, while the second functional movie gradient was anchored in visual regions and spanned to transmodal and default regions. Myelin content, as estimated from the mean T1w/T2w ratio, did influence the gradient-ISC relationship such that more highly myelinated regions demonstrated a more robust spatial correlation (r=0.81, p<0.001), while regions with moderate (r=0.73, p<0.001) and low (r=0.32, p<0.001) myelin content appeared to be less spatially correlated.

Conclusion

These results provide further support for a macroscale processing hierarchy within the brain that is exemplified under naturalistic conditions. In particular, we find that myelin content may impact the relationship between stimulus-driven BOLD signal responses at the parcel-level and the whole-brain naturalistic functional hierarchy. These findings provide compelling evidence that microstructural features might be relevant in our understanding of the relationship between intersubject and inter-region variability in the brain.
