Introduction

Results from neuroimaging analyses are often dependent on the processing pipeline and the specific parameters chosen by the investigator, as large multi-lab efforts in fMRI have shown that applying different analysis pipelines to the same dataset can yield markedly different results.1 Recently, this has extended to diffusion MRI (dMRI) tractography, as studies demonstrate that multiple different, yet equally reasonable, tractography methods can produce strikingly different representations of the same white-matter pathways.2 As a result, it is unclear whether group-level inferences made using one tractography pipeline can be expected to hold when a different, equally defensible pipeline is applied to the same data.

In this work, we ask whether two different automated bundle segmentation methods, applied to the same dataset, lead to consistent conclusions about white-matter pathways. We directly test the reproducibility and generalizability of tractography-derived sex differences in white-matter micro- and macrostructural features reported from a recent study3 with two widely used automated tractography techniques. We partnered with the group that originally reported these findings, and both laboratories independently processed the dataset using their own bundle segmentation methods, extracted a common set of microstructural features, and tested for sex-related differences. We then quantified agreement between pipelines in terms of statistical significance and effect directionality, asking whether two automated tractography methods lead to convergent versus contradictory conclusions.

We found that despite substantial differences in the definitions, shapes, and spatial extents of the reconstructed bundles, both the resulting patterns of sex effects and, critically, the biological interpretation of those effects were largely consistent across methods. This work provides bolsters confidence in the robustness and interpretability of tractography-derived findings.

Methods

Both labs used the exact same dataset: We analyzed 1,065 healthy young adults (575 women, 490 men; age range 22–35 years) from the Human Connectome Project Young Adult (HCP-YA) cohort.4 All participants underwent high resolution T1 weighted and multi shell dMRI on a Siemens Connectome Skyra 3T scanner at one of four HCP imaging sites. Diffusion MR contrast were acquired with a Siemens 3T Prisma whole-body scanner (32-channel head coil). Multishell Diffusion MR sequences were collected with multi-band = 3, b = 1000, 2000, and 3000, 90 directions per shell, TR = 5520 ms, TE = 89.5 ms, voxel size = 1.25 x 1.25 x 1.25 mm3, flip angle = 78°, matrix = 168 x 144, FOV = 210 x 180 mm.

Two widely used, fully automated tractography techniques were applied to the same preprocessed dMRI dataset. Each technique was implemented and run in a different laboratory, using that lab’s standard software environment and parameter choices. 

  • Tractography Method #1: The first technique reconstructed a whole-brain tractogram and selected the specific bundles via streamline filtering based on streamline similarity (pairwise distance) to a predefined white matter atlas.

  • Tractography Method #2: The second technique took a targeted bundle-segmentation approach, using TractSeg5 - a convolutional neural network approach that segments bundles based on a field of estimated fiber orientations.

Importantly, both techniques segmented and extracted 49 of the same intended white matter bundles (i.e., corticospinal tract, arcuate fasciculus, etc), but each lab using their own bundle definitions, methodologies, or frameworks. 

For each subject, tract, and technique, we extracted multiple diffusion and microstructural features, including axial diffusivity (AD), fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity (MD), radial diffusivity (RD), neurite density index (NDI), orientation dispersion index (ODI), and free water fraction (ISOWF). Within each laboratory, sex differences were tested independently for every tract-feature combination using Student’s t-tests comparing males and females, with Bonferroni correction applied to control for multiple comparisons across all tract-feature tests. We then computed effect size magnitudes for sex differences within each tract-feature pair.

Agreement between techniques was quantified at two levels. First, a strict agreement measure counted the percentage of tract-feature combinations in which the two pipelines showed the same significance status (both significant or both non-significant after correction) and, when significant, the same direction of effect. Second, a more permissive directional agreement metric counted the percentage of tract-feature combinations in which both pipelines showed the same direction of effect, regardless of whether each result survived significance correction. Similarly, disagreement was defined as tract-feature pairs in which both pipelines yielded significant sex effects in opposite directions, as well as a more permissive metric of disagreement quantifying any opposite directionality regardless of significance. Finally, to compare the sensitivity of the two methods, we employed a non parametric bootstrap with 10,000 iterations to estimate 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the difference in effect size magnitude between techniques for each feature, and considered sensitivity to differ significantly when the CI excluded zero.

Results

Across all 396 tract-feature comparisons, we observed strong correspondence between the two automated tractography techniques. All agreements and disagreements between the two techniques are outlined in Figure 1. When looking at strict agreement, defined as both pipelines yielding either a significant sex effect in the same direction or a non-significant result, 66.7% of tract-feature pairs agreed. When we relaxed the criterion to consider only the direction of the effect, irrespective of whether each pipeline’s result passed multiple comparison correction, agreement increased to 90.3% of comparisons. Thus, in the vast majority of cases, both techniques pointed toward the same qualitative pattern of sex differences, even when only one pipeline reached formal significance.

Direct contradictions between techniques were exceedingly rare. Disagreement defined as both pipelines yielding significant sex effects in opposite directions occurred in only 0.25% of comparisons: out of 396 tract-feature pairs, there was only one instance in which both methods produced statistically significant but directionally opposing effects. When we expanded the definition of disagreement to include any opposite directionality, regardless of significance, disagreement was 9.3%. These disagreements were typically characterized by very small effect sizes near zero in at least one pipeline, consistent with statistical noise rather than systematic reversals of effect.

We also examined whether one technique was systematically more sensitive to sex differences in particular microstructural features. The bootstrap analysis of effect size magnitude revealed that Technique 1 was significantly more sensitive than Technique 2 for detecting sex related differences in FA, ISOWF, and ODI, with 95% confidence intervals for the between-technique difference in effect size excluding zero for these measures. Conversely, Technique 2 showed significantly greater sensitivity for the remaining features (AD, MD, RD, NDI). These differences in sensitivity modulated the likelihood that a given tract-feature combination reached statistical significance, but did not generally alter the direction of observed sex effects.

Taken together, these results indicate that while tractography pipelines can differ in how often they detect statistically significant sex effects for particular features, the qualitative conclusions drawn about the presence and direction of sex differences in white matter microstructure are highly consistent across methods when applied to the same high quality dataset.

Conclusion

Correspondence between tractography methods is critical for interpreting diffusion MRI studies that rely on automated bundle reconstructions. It is crucial that investigators understand whether observed group differences, such as sex effects, are robust properties of white matter organization or artifacts of a particular processing pipeline. This issue is especially salient for tractography, which remains vulnerable to spurious streamlines and lacks an absolute anatomical ground truth.

In this large sample of HCP-YA participants, we found encouraging evidence that tractography-derived sex differences in white matter microstructure are highly generalizable across two independent, state of the art automated pipelines. Strict agreement in both significance and directionality was observed for roughly two thirds of tract-feature comparisons, and agreement in the direction of effect exceeded 90% when we did not impose a stringent significance threshold. Importantly, there was only one instance of a direct contradiction in which both pipelines produced significant sex effects in opposite directions, yielding an empirical probability of such disagreement on the order of 0.25% across nearly 400 comparisons.

These findings suggest that while some tractography techniques are more sensitive than others for particular diffusion and microstructural features, genuine disagreement between methods is very unlikely when both are applied to the same high quality data. In practice, different pipelines may influence effect size estimates and the number of tracts passing significance, but are far less likely to reverse the inferred direction of group differences.

Researchers should nonetheless consider which technique they use when probing specific pathways or features, particularly if their primary goal is maximizing sensitivity for a given metric. However, our results are overall very optimistic for tractography: they indicate that reasonable methodological choices are unlikely to lead two investigators to fundamentally opposing conclusions about sex differences in white matter. More broadly, this work provides an empirical benchmark for cross-pipeline reproducibility and gives tractography researchers greater confidence in the generalizability and robustness of their findings.
