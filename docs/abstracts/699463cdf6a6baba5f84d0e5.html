Introduction

In resting-state fMRI, spontaneous synchrony and desynchrony across brain regions form temporally varying large-scale networks (dynamic functional connectivity, dFC). These dynamic networks are associated with cognition, aging, and neurological or psychiatric conditions1.  Existing approaches for modelling dFC typically include: (1) decomposition-based methods that extract temporal modes2, (2) clustering-based methods that segment connectivity states, and (3) graph-based dynamic modeling using neural architectures such as RNNs, temporal CNNs, or transformers. These methods often rely on fixed context windows, assume linearity, or struggle to capture smooth transitions and long-range dependencies. Recent state-space models offer efficient long-sequence modeling but remain underexplored for dFC analysis.

We develop an adaptive temporal graph representation framework for modeling dFC that predicts future dFC graphs from their variable length history. The temporal lookback window at each time point is determined by the rate of newly formed edges, allowing the model to automatically adjust to meaningful connectivity transitions. To further improve spatial modeling, we incorporate brain structure and function-informed edge attributes, i.e., the left/right hemispheric identity and subnetwork membership of brain regions, enabling the model to capture biologically meaningful topological patterns. The resulting graph sequences are encoded using an edge-aware spatial message passing combined with efficient long-range temporal modeling to learn expressive representations of dynamic connectivity.

We evaluate the framework on functional link prediction and age estimation, using rs-fMRI data from Human Connectome Project. Experimental results show improved accuracy and strong generalization across sessions, outperforming both static and dynamic graph learning baselines. These findings highlight the promise of adaptive temporal representation learning for advancing dFC analysis.

Methods

We used resting-state fMRI recordings from 1,001 healthy young adults (aged 22–35) from the HCP S1200 release. Data were acquired on a 3T MRI scanner across four runs of approximately 14.5 minutes each, using multiband EPI with TR = 0.72 s and 2 mm isotropic resolution. Preprocessing followed the minimal spatial preprocessing pipeline, followed by ICA-based denoising and high-pass filtering for temporal preprocessing. Since voxel-wise timecourses are high dimensional, we reduced dimensionality by parcellating the cortex using the 100-region Schaefer atlas3. This lower-resolution choice was made because finer parcellations can lead to overfitting4. For each region, the representative timecourse was defined as the first principal component of its BOLD signals. Eight parcels identified as noise were removed, resulting in 92 ROIs. Each subject contributed a time series of 1200 TRs for each of four sessions.

dFC graphs were constructed using a sliding window approach (window size = 100; stride = 10). Pearson correlation matrices were computed within each window, with diagonal entries removed and off-diagonal connections thresholded at 0.5 to remove spurious links. Larger thresholds produce more rapidly changing graphs with higher edge turnover, whereas smaller thresholds yield smoother but potentially noisier temporal patterns. 

A key element of our approach is the adaptive selection of temporal context for each static graph. Instead of applying a fixed temporal context, we compute a novelty index reflecting the proportion of newly formed edges at each time step. High novelty indicates a rapidly changing connectivity pattern, prompting the model to use a shorter lookback; low novelty indicates stability, and therefore a longer context is adopted. This adaptivity introduces an inductive bias that aligns temporal modeling with meaningful variations in functional connectivity.

To encode spatial information, each static graph is processed by a graph neural network that incorporates node features and biologically informed edge attributes. Edge representations integrate hemispheric identity and subnetwork membership of the connected nodes. These attributes are embedded into learnable vectors and appended to the node–edge features, allowing the model to leverage topological structure during spatial message passing.

After extracting structural representations for each graph, we model their temporal evolution using a lightweight Mamba-based state-space architecture. This component captures long-range dependencies in the graph sequence with linear-time complexity by leveraging a state-space formulation that scales efficiently to long dFC sequences. The Mamba module receives the sequence of graph embeddings associated with each time step’s adaptive lookback and produces temporally enriched representations that reflect gradual and abrupt transitions in connectivity. This structure is particularly suited for rs-fMRI, where temporal dependencies may extend across long timescales and vary in strength throughout the scan.

Each node’s final embedding is expressed as the mean and variance of a Gaussian representation, allowing the model to encode uncertainty and variability in the graph structure. We train the encoder using a contrastive triplet objective: for each reference node, its neighbors serve as positive samples, while nodes two hops away serve as negatives. The objective encourages embeddings to capture topological distinctions between strongly and weakly connected regions.

For downstream prediction, node embeddings at each time step are used to predict the presence of functional links at the next step, which are evaluated using a multilayer perceptron trained with binary cross-entropy. For age estimation, temporal embeddings are aggregated using pooling and convolutional operations and passed through a regression model to predict chronological age.

Results

We evaluated our approach on the HCP Young Adult dataset for two tasks: temporal link prediction and age estimation. For link prediction, the first 300 subjects were used to train the model, and the learned encoder was then applied to a separate set of 300 subjects to generate dFC graph embeddings for the age prediction task, ensuring that the two tasks do not share training subjects.

We consider two training setups. Within-session: for each subject, each of the four sessions is split by timestamps, using the first 70% for training, the next 10% for validation, and the remaining 20% for testing. Segments from all sessions are concatenated to form the final training, validation, and test sets. Cross-session: the first two sessions are used for training, the third for validation, and the fourth for testing. 

We conduct ablation studies under both training setups, varying the lookback window strategy, threshold, embedding size, and stride length. For each configuration, we report the mean and variance of MAP and MRR. Based on these comparisons, we adopt a configuration with threshold 0.5, embedding dimension 128, adaptive lookback, and stride 10. This combination yields a strong balance between within-session and cross-session performance. The choice of temporal window size follows previous recommendations that windows of about 60 seconds provide a compromise between estimation stability and sensitivity to dynamic fluctuations; thus we use 100 window size (approximately 72 seconds).

We assess temporal link prediction using mean average precision (MAP) and mean reciprocal rank (MRR). For age prediction, the metrics are mean absolute error (MAE) and mean square error (MSE), with MAE interpreted in years. Five regression models are considered for evaluating the learned temporal embeddings: ElasticNet, Ridge, Lasso, Random Forest, and Support Vector Regression (SVR). Hyperparameters for each model are selected through grid search based on validation performance. Our best performance achieves an MAE of 2.8 years and an MSE of 11.68. Under the optimal configuration, our adaptive temporal modeling approach also achieves strong temporal link prediction results, obtaining MAP(0.7681) and MRR(0.6265), demonstrating the importance of flexible temporal context and neuroscience-informed structural priors for capturing dFC dynamics. It also achieves competitive or superior performance compared with classical and recent graph-based models5.

For the age estimation task, we ensure that siblings and twins are assigned to the same dataset split to avoid data leakage, guaranteeing that related individuals never appear in both training and testing partitions. A separate set of 300 subjects, not used in link prediction, is employed exclusively for age estimation to prevent information transfer between tasks. Temporal node embeddings are aggregated into subject-level representations and passed through the chosen regression model to predict chronological age.

Conclusion

We here propose an unsupervised and adaptive framework BrainATCL for predictive modelling of dFC that dynamically adjusts temporal context based on a temporal novelty index and incorporates biologically meaningful edge attributes to enhance spatial modeling. Experiments on HCP data demonstrate its strong performance and generalizability across both link prediction and age estimation tasks. One limitation of the current framework is the lack of end-to-end training between the embedding model and downstream tasks. In future work, we plan to enhance the adaptivity of the framework by aligning temporal context selection more directly with brain activity dynamics, and to explore the identification of individual-specific brain modes during the resting state.
