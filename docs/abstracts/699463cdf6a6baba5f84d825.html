Introduction

Hand gestures are an important part of nonverbal communication, making correct interpretation and performance of gestures a critical part of successful social interactions. Patients with major depressive disorders (MDD), are known to exhibit gesture deficits. For example, they use fewer gestures during social interactions1 and might interpret neutral gestures with a negative bias2, which may contributing to difficulties during social interactions. In addition, previous research showed patients with MDD perform gestures less accurate when gesture performance was assessed with the standardized test of upper limb apraxia (TULIA)3. The TULIA consists of two domains, where participants are asked to imitate a gesture demonstrated by an examiner during the imitation domain and are instructed to perform a gesture after a verbal instruction is given by an examiner during the pantomime domain4. In healthy controls, the praxis network, a combination of language and motor brain areas located in the frontal, temporal, and parietal lobe, has been implicated in gesture processing5. In patients with MDD, even though deficits in gesture performance have been shown previously, underlying neural correlates are unknown. We aimed to investigate and compare gesture performance and underlying neural correlates in patients with MDD and healthy controls. We expected less accurate gesture performance in patients compared to the controls and differences in brain activation in key areas of the praxis network between the two groups.

Methods

We examined blood-oxygenation-level-dependent (BOLD) activation during the planning and the execution of hand gestures in 30 patients and 31 healthy controls matched for age (MDD: 33.8 ± 11.3 years; controls: 28.8 ± 8.3 years; p=0.07), sex (MDD: 61.3% female; controls 67.7% female; p=0.8), and education (MDD: 15.5 ±3.0 years; controls: 16.4 ± 2.8 years; p=0.23). The event-related functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) task was based on a modified version of the TULIA consisting only of the pantomime domain. Each trial started with a written gesture command (3s), followed by a planning (3s) and execution phase (3s). Those two phases were separated by an interstimulus interval (3-10s), and each trial was concluded by another interstimulus interval (3-6s) (Figure 1). Gesture commands included 30 hand gestures: 10 novel (meaningless), 10 communicative (intransitive), and 10 tool-based (transitive). In addition, 10 control sentences were included, where participants were instructed not to plan or execute any movements. Images were acquired with a 3T Prisma MRI whole-body scanner using a 20-channel radio-frequency head coil (Siemens, Germany). Functional images were acquired with a multi-slice multi-band T2*-weighted echo planar imaging (EPI) sequence and  T1-weighted images were obtained with a 3D magnetization-prepared rapid two-gradient-echo (MP2RAGE) sequence. Group-level activation maps were estimated using one-sample t-tests, and between-group differences were assessed using two-sample t-tests (cluster-level FWE-corrected p<0.05). Preprocessing was done using Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM) implemented in MATLAB and the Computational Anatomy Toolbox 12 (CAT12) for segmentation. All statistical analysis were performed with SPM implemented in MATLAB.

Results

Gesture performance did not differ between patients with MDD and healthy controls (for all three categories p³0.07). Both groups activated areas known to be involved during the planning and execution of gestures, including the fronto-parietal praxis network, cerebellum, thalamus, and occipital lobe. Between-group comparisons showed higher activation in controls compared to patients during the planning of intransitive gestures in the left cerebellum (biggest cluster: k=128; pFWEcorr=0.002, T=4.71, [-48,-56,-46]), bilateral occipital lobe (biggest cluster right: k=513, pFWEcorr<0.001, T=5.44, [36,-88,0]; biggest cluster left: k=221, pFWEcorr<0.001, T=4.45, [-34,-94,2]), bilateral angular gyrus (biggest cluster right: k=102, pFWEcorr=0.007, T=4.66, [44,-68,32]; biggest cluster left: k=138, pFWEcorr=0.001, T=4.72, [-50,-68,22]), and left fusiform gyrus (biggest cluster: k=646, pFWRcorr<0.001, T=5.17, [-44,-48,-22]). In addition, controls activated areas in the bilateral occipital lobe (right: k=322, pFWEcorr<0.001, T=4.58, [32,-90,-2]; left: k=317, pFWEcorr<0.001, T=4.31, [-20,-92,0]) and right postcentral gyrus (k=84, pFWEcorr=0.021, T=4.04, [4,-40 ,66]) more during the planning of meaningless gestures compared to patients.

Conclusion

Even though we did not observe differences in gesture performance between patients with MDD and healthy controls, higher activation in parieto-occipital and cerebellar areas during gesture planning in controls compared to patients might still contribute to real-world gesture deficits observed in MDD. Despite the TULIA being a well-established, standardized test for the assessment of gesture performance, it does not fully capture every-day gesturing. Nevertheless, increased activation in controls compared to patients in the praxis network (angular gyrus) and other brain areas known to be implicated in motor performance and planning (e.g. cerebellum), might for example contribute to patients with MDD using fewer gestures in everyday situations compared to healthy controls, warranting further research.
