Introduction

Placebo analgesia represents a compelling psychobiological phenomenon where positive expectations of pain relief, independent of pharmacological active compounds, significantly mitigate both acute and chronic pain conditions. Individual variability in this response underscores its critical role in shaping clinical outcomes and patient prognosis. Furthermore, identifying the neural correlates of placebo analgesia is crucial for advancing the diagnosis and treatment of patients experiencing pain. Prior structural and functional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies have successfully illuminated these neural substrates, yet they are typically constrained by small sample sizes (ranging from 30-80 participants). This limitation necessitates a collaborative approach to enhance statistical power and generalizability. To overcome the data size limitations and obtain a more robust understanding, our study proposes a mega-analysis approach, pooling 13 independent placebo imaging studies to achieve a medium sample size (n=547 participants). There are two objectives of this study, 1. Rigorously delineate the structural neural correlates associated with placebo analgesia. Initially, mass-univariate analysis will be employed to identify region specific structural differences related to the magnitude of the placebo response. 2. Explore structural neural correlates of placebo analgesia as a whole-brain pattern. We hypothesize that such a comprehensive, multivariate pattern might significantly enhance the prediction and explanation of an individual’s propensity for placebo analgesia. In summary, the overarching goal of this multicentre structural neuroimaging initiative is to identify brain structural correlates that provide deeper mechanistic insights into the phenomenon of placebo analgesia, thereby informing future therapeutic strategies for pain management. 

Methods

A total of 13 different studies based on placebo were pooled for the analysis with a cumulative sample size of n = 547. The mean age and sex ratio (males to females) were 25.3 years (standard deviation=4.5) and 0.52 respectively. We obtained individual T1-weighted MRI images from all studies. Only those participants were considered with an age inter quartile threshold of 1.5. Cortical and subcortical volumes were generated using FreeSurfer 7.2.0. As a quality metrics, surface hole number was considered with a threshold of 701. That means participants above that threshold were not pursued for further analysis. This resulted in a total sample size of n=505 with mean age=24.8 (standard deviation=3.4), and sex ratio=0.5. Rather than relying on univariate measures of individual regional attributes (such as thickness, surface area, or volume), we employed a more comprehensive metric of cortical-subcortical asymmetry, which inherently integrates data from both hemispheres into a single, region-specific value. For this, we used the Desikan-Killiany atlas and extracted a total of 37 regional structural asymmetry features. We defined regional asymmetry as the normalized absolute difference: the difference between the left and right hemispheric regional volumes divided by their combined sum. Placebo Analgesia (PA) was quantified for each participant as the difference in VAS (Visual Analog Scale)-pain ratings between the placebo and control conditions. To ensure scores were comparable across different studies, all pain intensity ratings were first standardized to a 0-100 scale and then normalized by the standard deviation specific to their study of origin. For the “mass-univariate associations”, at first the asymmetry values across centres were harmonized using ComBat, to mitigate centre effects2. We used an ordinary least square (OLS) method with each brain region as the dependent variable and PA, age, sex, harmonized total intracranial volume as the independent variables. A bayes factor (BF) was also calculated for each of the regions, to be able to quantify the level of evidence both for the null and alternative hypothesis (BF01 and BF10 respectively). BF10 was calculated as the exponential of the difference between the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) for the null model (i.e., Asymmetry ~ Sex + Age + harmonized TIV) and the model with including PA as another independent variable3. Our second objective was to quantify overall predictive power of structural asymmetries towards estimating PA in a multivariate fashion. To achieve this, we used a machine learning model with kernel ridge (kernel=RBF, Radial Bias Function) with features as the brain asymmetries and target variable as the PA. The modelling pipeline was prepared using scikit-learn, and consisted of feature scaling using a robust scaler, feature selection with variable (5, 10, 15, 20) features and learning rates (alpha) with 10 values between -4 to 4 on the log space. A 10 split stratified K fold was used with centre as the stratification key. The process was repeated for a 100 shuffles to calculate the final mean predictions in this robust nested CV machine learning pipeline. The results were represented in terms of how well the predictions correlate with the harmonized PA. Crucially, both features and the target variable (PA) were harmonized using ComBat, where the harmonization model was fit and transformed only on the training set and then applied solely to the test set to prevent data leakage. Finally, a confounder test assessed whether the final mean predictions correlated with age, sex, TIV, or study centre.

Results

The results for the univariate and multivariate associations are presented with Figure 1. We found that a majority of cortical and subcortical regions did not correlate significantly with PA (Fig 1 A-B). In fact, most of the regions showed a strong evidence for the null hypothesis with BF01>10. However, there were three regions that lacked conclusive Bayesian evidence for the null hypothesis and showed weak (uncorrected) statistical significance in terms of the frequentist analysis, namely putamen (p-uncorrected=0.004, BF10=2.6), parsorbitalis (p-uncorrected=0.021, BF10=0.7) and parahippocampal gyrus (p-uncorrected=0.038, BF10=0.4). Interestingly, these regions were also involved in the unsupervised predictive modelling, where the overall features of asymmetry weakly yet significantly predicted PA with r=0.12, p =0.004 (Fig 1 C).  

Conclusion

While the mass-univariate analysis indicated that regional structural asymmetry is not a widespread, strong predictor of Placebo Analgesia (PA) – with most regions showing strong evidence for the null hypothesis (BF01>20) – Bayesian analyses were inconclusive for three specific regions (both BF01<3 and BF10<3 and p<0.05 prior to multiple comparisons correction), namely putamen, parsorbitalis, and parahippocampal gyrus. While such localized findings could offer valuable biological insights into the structural basis of placebo analgesia, their small effect size limits their direct utility as robust, standalone neuromarkers for predicting PA in clinical settings. However, our multivariate analysis suggests that overall structural asymmetry pattern in these regions holds a weak but significant predictive power for PA. This result demonstrates that the collective effect of these same structural asymmetries works better than isolated regional metrics, underscoring the importance of considering a distributed network effect for PA. 

Our findings, derived from a highly powered sample, is particularly pertinent when considering that results from smaller-scale studies may occasionally be subject to effect size inflation due to limited statistical power and selective reporting. The strong Bayes Factor for the null hypothesis observed across most regions suggests that several previous positive findings in these specific structural domain that did not replicate here should be treated with caution.

The presence of widespread negative results and the limited predictive capacity of multivariate modelling emphasizes the need for a multimodal approach in future research, integrating structural metrics like asymmetry with more nuanced aspects such as the morphological connectivity/cortical-subcortical symmetry networks (derived from the same T1-weighted data)4.
