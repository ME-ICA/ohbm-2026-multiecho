Introduction

Recent experimental and modelling work inspired by neural field theory (NFT)^1 suggests that several aspects of large-scale resting-state cortical dynamics can be modeled by wave-like propagation of neuronal activity^2. However, these models are unable to recapitulate the dominant spatial patterns seen in resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) data, commonly referred to as the cortex’s functional modes or gradients^3. For instance, the dominant functional mode of rs-fMRI data has a spatial pattern that often follows a well-known sensorimotor-association axis^3 (Figure 1A), whereas the dominant mode in standard wave-based models follows a rostro-caudal axis^2. This discrepancy may be attributed to simplifications in the modelling procedures, in which inputs to the cortex are modeled as spatially homogeneous white noise. In reality, almost all sensory input to the cortex comes from the thalamus^4. These thalamocortical afferents are spatially heterogeneous, often reciprocal, and are known to support the long-range cortico-cortical functional coupling that underpins the spatial patterns of rs-fMRI gradients^3,4. Here, we extend an established NFT model to incorporate spatially heterogeneous interactions between the cortex and the thalamus. We hypothesized that this heterogeneity would cause simulated functional gradients to align with the dominant empirical rs-fMRI gradient, identifying a plausible mechanism through which the thalamus modulates waves of excitation to support large-scale cortical functional organization.

Methods

We modified the cortico-cortical NFT wave model from Pang et al. (2023; Figure 1B), which models the evolution of cortical activity through space and time using a generic damped wave equation with one free parameter (rs; spatial length scale of the wave propagation) and one fixed parameter (γs; damping rate). In the original formulation, resting-state activity is approximated as arising from an input modeled by a spatially homogeneous Gaussian white noise. In our modification, we included an additional input related to a feedback mechanism due to thalamocortical interactions (Figure 1C). This thalamocortical feedback term introduces two additional parameters: (1) a time delay 𝜏 to represent the average time taken for activity to be sent from the cortex, through thalamic processing, and then back to the cortex; and (2) a gain parameter G that scales the level of feedback, with G=1 corresponding to feedback of the same amplitude as the original activity. Since we were primarily interested in the effect of introducing feedback, we varied G (values of 0.1, 0.5, and 1.0 are shown here) while fixing the other parameters according to prior work (𝜏=85 ms; rs=18.0 mm; γs=0.116 ms-1)^1,2.

To account for spatial heterogeneities in thalamic input, we convolved the feedback term with a group-average cortical map of Layer IV gene expression (first principal component)^5, which tracks connection strength of Core-type thalamocortical projections, known to densely innervate sensory cortex^4 (Figure 1D). Consequently, feedback is strongest in visual and somatosensory areas.

We simulated 15 minutes of activity for each model/parametrization, corresponding to the length of each rs-fMRI scan from the Human Connectome Project [3] (HCP; Figure 1E). The well-established Balloon-Windkessel haemodynamic model was applied to this activity to simulate the blood oxygen level-dependent signal characteristic of rs-fMRI. To match the HCP rs-fMRI sampling frequency, we downsampled the time series to 1.39 Hz. To extract the functional modes or gradients of the simulated data, we applied principal component analysis to the simulated time series and compared spatial patterns across parametrizations. Finally, we computed the similarity of each simulated gradient to the first rs-fMRI gradient by taking an absolute Pearson correlation, which accounts for the arbitrary signs of principal component maps.

Results

Under no feedback, the simple wave model produced gradients of coarse variation across the cortex (Figure 1F). Since the spatial component of this model involves only the Laplace-Beltrami operator (∇2), these gradients align with the geometric eigenmodes of the cortical surface, previously described by Pang et al. (2023). We observed small correlation with the first rs-fMRI gradient (|r|=.21-.31), supporting prior theoretical work^1. Under increasing levels of homogeneous feedback, these simple geometry-driven gradients were perturbed towards higher spatial frequencies, with finer variations across the cortex (Figure 1G). However, this feedback drove a negligible correlation with the first rs-fMRI gradient (|r|≤.10). Heterogeneous feedback similarly perturbed the gradients towards higher spatial frequencies, yet distinct patterns emerged for each of the three G values when compared to homogeneous feedback (Figure 1H). Contrary to our hypothesis, almost all gradients simulated by this model also displayed negligible correlation with the first rs-fMRI gradient (|r|≤.10), with the exception of the first simulated gradient for G=0.1, which remained at |r|=.21. Across both extended wave models, gradients simulated at G=0.1 show little correspondence with their counterparts at G=0, indicating that a relatively minor contribution of thalamocortical feedback is sufficient to overcome the uncorrelated noise input and shift global wave dynamics in the model.

To investigate the impact of the thalamocortical feedback on simulated dynamics, we additionally computed a spatially-averaged power spectrum for each model/parametrization. To preserve high-frequency content, we did not apply any haemodynamic model or downsampling in this analysis. For the homogeneous feedback model, all three levels of feedback induced a consistent series of resonance spikes (Figure 1I), the first of which is near 10 Hz (corresponding to 1/𝜏, the frequency of the time delay), akin to the alpha rhythm characteristic of resting-state electroencephalography signals^1. The heterogeneous feedback model induced similar resonances, albeit with weaker power in the initial 10 Hz spike and higher power in higher-frequency spikes.

Conclusion

We have developed a new framework for extending computational models of cortical dynamics to account for thalamocortical feedback, which is known to support long-range cortico-cortical functional coupling. We demonstrate that adjusting a simple gain parameter to scale the contribution of feedback in relation to noise inputs causes functional gradients to diverge from simple geometric axes to more complex spatial patterns. Notably, a small level of feedback (G=0.1) is seen to produce intermediate spatial frequencies, akin to the empirical functional gradients of rs-fMRI data derived from the HCP. Moreover, our observation that feedback induced alpha-like rhythms recapitulates prior findings from prior NFT models that involve more complex modelling and parametrizations. Although we did not find that thalamocortical feedback aligned simulated gradients with the dominant rs-fMRI gradient, it remains possible that weaker levels of feedback (i.e., G<0.1) may drive such a relationship. Our framework provides scope for future cross-validation with HCP subjects, where the level of feedback is optimized to maximize the correlation between simulated and empirical gradients. The model can then be further tested on other properties of cortical activity, such as edge-level and node-level functional connectivity, and applied to characterize shifts in functional gradient patterns across development and evolution.
