Introduction

Anger and aggression are omnipresent in contemporary society. In the information age, they saturate online platforms, media headlines, and social networks. While experiencing anger within a normal range may confer instrumental benefits – e.g., enhancing intrinsic motivation or motivating the enforcement of moral norms via punishment – excessive and uncontrolled anger becomes maladaptive and can turn into violent behaviors (e.g., verbal or physical attack, brutal violence). This dysregulation carries serious consequences for individual physical and mental health, threatens personal safety, and can disrupt social order with impacts extending to families and communities. 1

While aggression and violence represent complex and multifactorial behaviors that encompass personal, social, and societal determinants, the brain is fundamental to governing emotional experience and behavioral output. Although several studies examined anger and anger control on the brain level, 2 methodological constraints such as the lack of realistic anger induction methods and precise analytical methods have prevented a comprehensive characterization of the neural basis of anger.

Here, we leveraged recent methodological advances in naturalistic functional MRI and multivariate pattern analytic neural decoding to develop and evaluate a sensitive and robust whole-brain signature predictive of momentary self-reported subjective anger experience induced by short video clips from online media platforms. The generalizability and specificity of the signature were next tested across experiments and datasets.

Methods

The anger induction task (Fig. 1) consisted of 60 short video clips that could elicit varying levels of subjective anger experience, distributed across 4 runs. To facilitate robust results, we employed a discovery (n=80) and validation (n=30) cohort design. For each trial, a video clip was shown for approximately 25 s and participants were requested to report the intensity of their experienced anger as well as to what extent they wanted to punish the transgressor(s) in the video (separately on 5-level ratings, with one indicating the lowest level of anger or punishment and five indicating the highest level of anger or punishment, respectively).

fMRI data were collected on a 3T system (GE MR750, General Electric Medical System) at the University of Electronic Science and Technology of China. Structural images were acquired using a high-resolution T1 spoiled gradient recall sequence (TR 6 ms, TE 2 ms, flip angle 9°, field of view 256 × 256 mm, 1 × 1 × 1 mm voxels, 256 × 256 acquisition matrix, 156 slices) and were used for improving spatial normalization and excluding participants with apparent brain pathologies. Functional images were acquired with a T2*-weighted echo planar imaging sequence (TR 2,000 ms, TE 30 ms, flip angle 90°, field of view 240 × 240 mm, 3.75 × 3.75 × 4 mm voxels, 64 × 64 resolution, 39 slices).

In line with our previous practice, 3 all fMRI data were pre-processed and analyzed using SPM12 (Statistical Parametric Mapping). We designed a participant-level general linear model to generate the contrasts of interest, which included separate regressors for the different levels or anger ratings (1-5) time-logged to the onset of the videos, thus enabling us to model brain activity in response to each anger level separately. The model also included two boxcar regressors for the 4 s rating period, aiming to model effects correlated with motor activity. The fixation-cross epoch was used as an implicit baseline.

Consistent with methods evaluated in previous studies developing neuroaffective decoders, 3,4 we employed a linear support vector regression model to identify a whole-brain signature of fMRI activation predictive of self-reported anger experience elicited by short video clips using data from a discovery cohort (n=80). The performance of the resultant visually induced anger signature (VIAS) was then evaluated across multiple anger and aggression datasets: the discovery (10×10-fold cross-validation), validation (n=30), and punishment validation (n=30) cohorts. We calculated the overall and within-subject prediction-outcome correlations between the pattern expressions and the true ratings, and further derived two-alternative forced-choice classification accuracies for distinguishing the separate anger or punishment intensity levels. Two-sided binomial tests were used to test whether the classification accuracies were higher than chance level (50%). Next, we performed within-subject trial-wise prediction to examine to what extent the population-level VIAS can predict momentary variations in anger on the individual level (on a trial-by-trial basis). Moreover, we determined which brain regions and systems contribute to the whole-brain anger prediction through a bootstrap test and the computation of model encoding maps, 3-5 and we further tested whether isolated regions or networks traditionally involved in anger, such as the thalamus, 2 insula, 2 or salience network, were sufficient to predict subjective anger experience. Finally, we systematically examined the functional specificity of the VIAS by conducting predictive comparisons with established decoders for affective arousal (BAAS) 4 and multimodal negative affect (MNAS), 5 respectively.

Results

We developed a brain signature sensitive to predict subjective anger experience (VIAS) based on the discovery cohort (Fig. 2a). Next, performance of the VIAS was evaluated using cross-validation in the discovery cohort and by determining reactivity of the VIAS to anger experience in an independent validation cohort (n=30) that underwent the identical paradigm. The developed VIAS accurately predicted the level of subjective anger in the discovery cohort (within-subject prediction-outcome r=0.90±0.01 (mean±s.e.m.); overall prediction–outcome r=0.53, bootstrapped 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.46–0.60; Fig. 2b). To independently determine the robustness of the VIAS, we applied the predictive model - with no further model fitting - to the validation cohort and found comparable high prediction-outcome correlations (within-subject r=0.90 ± 0.02; overall r=0.47, 95% CI 0.35–0.59; Fig. 2c), indicating a sensitive and robust subjective anger signature. To further determine the sensitivity of the model for subjective anger, we compared pairs of activation maps within each subject by means of a two-alternative forced-choice test. In both cohorts, the VIAS accurately classified high (average of rating 4 and 5) vs moderate (average of rating 2 and 3) and moderate vs low (rating 1) anger with 90–96% accuracy (P<0.001, Cohen’s d=1.38-2.04) and high vs low with 100% accuracy (P<0.001, Cohen’s d=3.37–3.51). Moreover, the VIAS could discriminate each successive pair of anger levels (e.g., rating 1 vs 2) with at least 77% accuracy (P< 0.01) (Fig. 2b,c).

Applying the VIAS to the punishment validation cohort also yielded similar predictive performances (within-subject r=0.87±0.02; overall r=0.45, 95% CI 0.32–0.57; accuracy between low, medium and high no less than 90% (P<0.001, Cohen’s d=1.33-3.25), and accuracy between each successive pair of anger rating levels no less than 82% (P<0.001), except that it failed to predict level 4 vs 3 (accuracy=0.57, P=0.59) (Fig. 2d), indicating that the VIAS can generalize to capture anger-induced punishment behaviors.

Although anger experience differed between individuals, the VIAS could significantly predict trial-by-trial anger ratings in 100% of the individuals in two independent populations (mean prediction-outcome correlations were 0.59±0.01 and 0.52±0.02 for discovery and validation cohorts, respectively).

The subjective anger experience is represented in distributed cortical and subcortical systems, including regions such as the precuneus, insula, anterior-mid-posterior cingulate cortex, inferior/middle/medial frontal gyrus, superior/middle temporal gyrus, periaqueductal gray, hypothalamus, thalamus, precentral gyrus, inferior parietal lobule, postcentral gyrus, supplementary motor area, and cerebellum (Fig. 2e), while the regional contribution of thalamus (Fig. 2f), insula (Fig. 2g) and large-scale networks (Fig. 2h) show lower predictive accuracy compared to the VIAS.

The BAAS 4 could partly track subjective anger experience (discovery cohort: overall r=0.28, 95% CI 0.19-0.37; validation cohort: overall r=0.24, 95% CI 0.08-0.39), and corresponding multilevel mediation models revealed that VIAS partially mediated the effect of BAAS response on anger ratings and vice versa; however, the model with VIAS as the mediator had 3.56 times larger effect sizes than the model with BASS as mediator in the discovery cohort, and a similar trend was observed in the validation cohort, suggesting a stronger influence of VIAS in partially mediating the effects of BASS response on anger ratings. The multimodal negative affect signature (MNAS) 5 was not sensitive to capture anger experience (discovery cohort: overall r=0.12, 95% CI 0.02-0.21; validation cohort: overall r=0.09, 95% CI -0.08-0.25), and corresponding multilevel mediation models showed that in both cohorts the VIAS fully mediated the effect of MNAS response on anger ratings, yet the MNAS did not mediate of effect of VIAS response on anger ratings.

Conclusion

Despite long-standing discussions on the neurobiological underpinnings of anger, its potential negative effects on our mental and physical well-being, and its influence on social systems, 1 an accurate and comprehensive neurofunctional signature predictive of momentary self-reported subjective anger experience is currently lacking. In this study, we used ecologically valid anger triggers from online social media platforms (i.e., short video clips) as experimental stimuli and combined naturalistic neuroimaging with predictive models, allowing us to measure anger in close to real-life contexts and gain a thorough understanding of how anger is constructed in the brain within the context of social media use.

We established a sensitive neurofunctional model for subjective anger experience (VIAS), which accurately predicted subjective anger on the group and individual level in both the discovery and validation cohorts, and furthermore showed robust generalization in a related punishment context. Subjective anger was encoded in distributed subcortical and cortical brain systems involved in, e.g., social cognition, negative affect, arousal and salience as well as action-preparation systems, while local brain systems (isolated systems or networks) failed to reach the predictive accuracy of the whole-brain model. The VIAS showed shared yet distinct neurofunctional representations with the established affective arousal model, such that although the BAAS could predict subjective anger experience to some extent, the VIAS robustly mediated the response of the BAAS on subjective anger ratings but not vice versa. Additionally, we found that the VIAS largely exhibited separable representations with the model of (multimodal) general negative affect, despite the subjective anger experience being inherently interrelated with negative affect.

In conclusion, this study developed a comprehensive brain model for the subjective experience of anger in the context of today’s information age, verified the neurobiological link between anger and aggression, and determined the degree of neurofunctional commonalities as well as differences with affective arousal and unspecific general negative affect. The resultant subjective anger marker can be potentially useful to examine novel treatments for anger dysregulation and pathological aggression.
