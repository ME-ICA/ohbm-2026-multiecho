Introduction

Sleep is essential for maintaining full daily functionality, though if sleep is disrupted, the resulting effects can be adverse. One globally prevalent cause of disturbed sleep is obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), that affects at least a billion people worldwide (9–38 %), depending on diagnostic criteria, the majority of whom are unaware of their condition.1 OSA is a disorder characterized by repeated obstruction of airflow leading to intermittent oxygen deprivation and reduced sleep quality. This condition can significantly impact both the structure and function of the brain. Pathological changes have been observed in OSA patients, particularly in the hippocampus and frontal lobe — regions that are vital for memory and cognition.

Beyond its well‑established cardiovascular and metabolic sequelae, emerging data implicate impaired glymphatic clearance in OSA. The glymphatic system is a perivascular cerebrospinal‑fluid (CSF)–interstitial‑fluid exchange system that removes metabolic waste from the brain.2 Glymphatic flow relies on slow vasomotor, respiratory, and cardiac pulsations, which increase during sleep and act as a physiological pump.2-4

Functional near‑infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) offers bedside, high‑temporal‑resolution monitoring of cortical hemo‑ and hydrodynamics (oxygenated, deoxygenated, and total hemoglobin, as well as water ~ CSF) via four wavelengths (690–980 nm).5 The used fNIRS protocol with spectral analysis methods captures the physiological very low frequency, respiratory, and cardiac pulsations that are thought to drive glymphatic transport.

To our knowledge, no study has directly measured how OSA modifies these physiological pulsations during sleep. We hypothesize that OSA patients exhibit altered brain pulsation patterns detectable with fNIRS, revealing potential glymphatic dysfunction. This study will provide human evidence linking OSA to altered brain pulsatility and, by extension, to possible glymphatic impairment.

Methods

The study cohort comprised 20 healthy controls (HC; age =39.7 ± 8.0 years, BMI=26.2 ± 2.6, 5 females) and 12 patients with OSA (PWOSA, age = 53.0 ± 11.0 years, BMI=29.8 ± 5.6, 1 female) after exclusions for poor signal quality (7 subjects). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. OSA patients were recruited from sleep apnea patients diagnosed at the Clinical Neurophysiology Department of Oulu University Hospital. All subjects were non‑smokers aged 33–67 years without neurological diseases. Well‑controlled comorbidities, such as hypertension managed with medication, were not exclusionary.

The data were collected on hospital premises, with a member of the research team monitoring the measurements from an adjacent room. The measurement setup included a Type 3 home sleep apnea test (HSAT) device (Nox T3s, Nox Medical), fNIRS positioned over the forehead (~frontal cortex), Polar Electro sensors, and an Emfit mattress. This report focuses on the HSAT and fNIRS data. fNIRS was sampled at 250 Hz, and raw optical density from four wavelengths (690–980 nm) was converted to concentration changes using the modified Beer–Lambert law. The calculated concentration changes were oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO), deoxygenated hemoglobin (HbR), total hemoglobin (HbT) and water (H2O~CSF).5 Expert validation was employed to ensure robust, consistent data quality, so we selected a 30‑minute segment from each subject in which all signals met high‑quality standards. Respiratory events were manually scored according to the current American Academy of Sleep Medicine guidelines. The control group’s whole night HSAT‑validated apnea‑hypopnea index (AHI) was 1.6 ± 1.0, and the patients’ AHI was 18.6 ± 14.0. However, in these 30min segments equivalent AHI were in HC 0.1 ± 0.4 and in PWOSA 44.7 ± 23.7.

After calculating concentration changes, the fNIRS signal was down‑sampled from 250 Hz to 50 Hz for computational efficiency. Slow drifts were mitigated by high‑pass filtering the signal (> 0.005 Hz). Spectral entropy values for each subject and concentrations were computed over the 0.005–1.5 Hz band, which encompasses all physiological pulsation frequencies, with minimal interference from higher frequencies. In addition, spectral power density was calculated for very low frequency fluctuations (0.01–0.1 Hz), respiratory (0.1–0.5 Hz), and cardiac pulsations (0.5–1.5 Hz) across all four concentrations. All preprocessing and analysis steps were performed in MATLAB 2025b. Statistical comparisons between groups were conducted in Origin 2025b using a non‑parametric Mann‑Whitney U test, with significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

The PWOSA group exhibited significantly lower spectral entropy values for HbO, HbT, and tissue H2O relative to the healthy‑control (HC) group (0.0012 < p < 0.0033, Figure 1A). By contrast, the entropy of HbR did not differ between groups (p = 0.11, Figure 1A), suggesting that the loss of hemodynamic complexity in sleep apnea patients is primarily reflected in the arterial oxygenated hemoglobin and water signals.

Spectral power density analysis revealed a consistent increase in power within the VLF and respiratory bands across all four concentrations. For the H2O channel, the VLF‑band power increase reached significance at p = 0.034 (Figure 1B), while the respiratory‑band increase was significant at p = 0.019 (Figure 1C). Hemoglobin‑related power increases were also significant, with p‑values ranging from 0.00087 to 0.011 (Figure 1BC). In contrast, power in the cardiac band remained unchanged (Figure 1D), indicating that the pulsations (VLF and respiratory) relevant to glymphatic propulsion are selectively disrupted in OSA while the cardiac‑driven component is preserved.

Conclusion

Recurrent episodes of hypoxia and sleep fragmentation associated with obstructive sleep apnea are known to initiate a cascade of pathological processes, including alterations in hemodynamics, heightened sympathetic activity, oxidative stress, systemic- and neuroinflammation, and ultimately neuronal injury or loss. 

Our findings in this study demonstrate that OSA is associated with a marked reduction in the complexity of cortical hemodynamic oscillations, as indexed by spectral entropy measures derived from fNIRS. Specifically, patients with OSA showed significantly lower entropy in HbO, HbT, and H2O signals, whereas HbR entropy remained comparable to that of healthy controls. As the entropy reflects the irregularity of a signal, thus the observed decrease suggests a loss of physiological variability in the vascular and CSF space compartments during sleep in patients with OSA. This loss of variability likely mirrors the diminished capacity of the brain’s microvascular pump to generate the diverse pulsations that drive glymphatic exchange.

The spectral power density analysis further clarifies which components of the pulsatile drive are altered. Both the very low frequency (VLF; 0.01–0.1 Hz) and respiratory (0.1–0.5 Hz) bands exhibited increased power across all chromophores in the OSA group, whereas the cardiac band (0.5–1.5 Hz) was preserved. The amplification of respiratory pulsations is probably due to increased physiological workload against pressure, and VLF may represent a compensatory response to disturbed pulsations. However, because glymphatic flow is thought to rely on a coordinated interplay of all three drivers, slow vasomotion, respiration, and cardiac pulsation, the selective preservation of the cardiac component together with the attenuation of entropy suggests a dysregulated, less efficient pumping mechanism. Our data provide the first human evidence that OSA disrupts the very signals that underlie glymphatic propulsion, offering a plausible mechanistic bridge between OSA‑induced hypoxia, reduced waste clearance, and the heightened risk of neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease.

In summary, obstructive sleep apnea is associated with a pronounced reduction in the complexity of cortical hemodynamic oscillations and a selective amplification of very low frequency and respiratory power, while cardiac‑driven pulsations remain unchanged. These alterations likely reflect a dysregulated physiological pump that compromises glymphatic clearance during sleep, providing a mechanistic substrate for the well‑documented neurocognitive and neurodegenerative sequelae of OSA. fNIRS‑based spectral‑entropy and power analyses emerge as promising, bedside‑compatible tools for probing brain pulsatility and glymphatic health in sleep‑disordered populations, opening avenues for early detection and targeted therapeutic monitoring.
