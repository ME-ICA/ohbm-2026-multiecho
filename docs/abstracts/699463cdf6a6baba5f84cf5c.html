Introduction

A psychological trait is typically defined as a relatively stable and enduring disposition that shapes patterns of behavior, cognition, and emotion across contexts, and it can serve as an indirect marker of an individual’s characteristic functioning and vulnerability to psychopathology.

Among such traits, trait anxiety is conceptualized as an enduring disposition to experience anxiety across a wide range of situations and is closely linked to mental health symptoms such as depression and rumination. Behavioral Inhibition System (BIS) sensitivity indexes individual differences in the reactivity of a neuropsychological system that responds to signals of potential punishment, non-reward, or novelty. Higher BIS sensitivity reflects a greater propensity toward negative affective states such as anxiety and fear, and has been associated with conditions including Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD) and social anxiety disorder.

Accurately predicting these psychological traits is therefore of clear importance, because such prediction can facilitate risk stratification and early identification of emerging symptoms, help reduce clinical burden, and support the selection of appropriate interventions and treatments. 

Many neuroimaging studies have examined associations between specific brain connectivity patterns and psychological traits, and even when the primary aim has been to improve predictive accuracy, models have typically relied on a single imaging modality, capturing only a limited aspect of neuronal activity and yielding constrained representations of brain function and structure¹.

In the present study, we introduce Tri-modal fusion approaches designed to enhance prediction of trait anxiety and BIS sensitivity by integrating structural, functional, and electrophysiological connectivity, thereby overcoming the representational and informational limitations inherent in unimodal approaches.

Methods

[1] Data 

The LEMON dataset1 was used for imaging data and phenotype data. 

DWI data were preprocessed by MRtrix3, FSL, Freesurfer, ANTs. fMRI data were preprocessed by AFNI, FSL, Freesurfer, ANTs. For each MR modality, 84 x 84 connectivity matrix was generated based on T1-weighted Desikan-killiany atlas. EEG data were preprocssed using EEGLAB and source-reconstructed with eLORETA (exact low-resolution brain electromagnetic tomography) to map scalp potentials onto the cortical surface, from which an 84 × 84 connectivity matrix was constructed. (Fig.1 – A,B)

State-Trait Anxiety Inventory-Trait (STAI-T) scores and BIS scores, which index trait anxiety levels and BIS sensitivity, served as prediction targets. After all preprocessing and quality control, a total of 28 subjects with complete tri-modal data were included in the final analysis.

[2] Model 

The Graph Neural Network2 (GNN) was employed to capture complex patterns of brain connectivity. The model comprised three components: Feature Extractor, Attention Network and Readout Layer. 

Feature Extractor received each connectivity matrix and performed row-wise and column-wise convolution to learn importance and connectivity pattern of neighboring nodes. The resulting information was aggregated to establish each node feature.

In Attention Network, either a Graph Attention Network (GAT) layer or a Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) layer was applied depending on input modality to derive node-wise importance scores that guided a top-k pooling operation. This pooling retained the most informative nodes while removing noisy or less relevant ones, after which the pooled node features were reweighted by these scores so that more important nodes contributed more strongly.

In the Readout Layer, the reweighted node features were vectorized and passed through fully connected (FC) layers, which predicted psychological traits.

[3] Fusion Approaches 

Experiments were conducted to compare unimodal, bimodal, and tri-modal approaches and to show whether fusion can improve predictive performance.

1) Unimodal (Fig.1 – C)

In the Attention Network, GAT layer was used for Structural Connectivity Matrix (SCM). Since SCM represents a fixed physical structure of the brain where certain connectivity holds greater importance, attention mechanism can effectively emphasize this by assigning different weights and lead to improved performance. 

In contrast, Functional Connectivity Matrices (FCMs) derived from fMRI and EEG are based on synchronization between ROIs, representing locally coherent functional organization. Because they reflect only synchronized activity levels without a strong prior about directional importance, a GCN, which aggregates information from neighboring nodes with shared weights, was considered more suitable for FCMs.

2) Bimodal (Fig.1 – D)

As in the unimodal approach, SCM was processed with GAT, whereas fMRI and EEG FCMs were processed with GCN. The less noisy modality was then used as a gating signal to reweight the companion modality, and the reweighted representation was combined with original features. The resulting representations were then fused to predict trait anxiety levels.

3) Tri-modal (Fig.1 – E,F)

Two fusion methods, SCM-based Gated Fusion and Hybrid-Gated Fusion were developed. 

SCM-based Gated Fusion assumed that SCM and FCM are both complementary and correlated. In this scheme, noise-robust SCM was used as a gating signal to reweight each FCM. To preserve important information in the original FCM, a convex combination was performed between the original FCM and its SCM-weighted FCM. The result was concatenated with the SCM to form a fused feature. 

In Hybrid Gated Fusion, FCM edges were reweighted using a gate learned as a convex combination of a robust SCM and concatenated representation of the three modalities. This consensus-aware gating mechanism enhances feature representations by capturing both shared cross-modal information and modality-specific complementary information.

Results

All experiments were evaluated with Leave-One-Out-Cross-Validation (LOOCV), using Mean Absolute error, Pearson (r) and Spearman (ρ) correlation coefficient as predictive performance metrics.

1) Trait anxiety levels (STAI-T score)

In Fig. 2, “Trait Anxiety” panel summarizes the predictive performance for trait anxiety levels. 

[1] Unimodal: A, B, and C correspond to conditions using only the SCM, fMRI FCM, and EEG FCM, respectively. Among these, SCM-only achieved the best performance (MAE: 4.75, r: 0.681, ρ: 0.547), followed by fMRI FCM (MAE: 6.01, r: 0.437, ρ: 0.393), with EEG FCM (MAE: 8.53, r: 0.241, ρ: 0.218) showing the lowest performance. 

This pattern likely reflects modality-specific differences in signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and susceptibility to noise and artifacts. SCM encodes a stable anatomical scaffold and is less affected by transient noise, providing a robust representation of interregional coupling. In contrast, fMRI FCM is derived from low-amplitude BOLD fluctuations and is highly sensitive to motion, physiological artifacts. EEG FCM, even after source reconstruction, remains vulnerable to volume conduction and mixing of signals from multiple cortical sources, which blurs ROI-wise connectivity estimates. Consequently, SCM offer a cleaner connectivity profile than fMRI and EEG FCMs, leading to superior predictive performance, and prediction based on fMRI FCM tends to outperform prediction based on EEG FCM for similar reasons.

[2] Bimodal: D refers to the condition using SCM + fMRI FCM (MAE: 4.44, r: 0.713, ρ: 0.652), E to SCM + EEG FCM (MAE: 4.51, r: 0.717, ρ: 0.586), and F to fMRI FCM + EEG FCM (MAE: 5.91, r: 0.543, ρ: 0.502). All bimodal fusion approaches retained advanced predictive performance than unimodal baselines.

These results suggest that combining two modalities provides a richer and more informative description of brain connectivity than any single modality alone. Complementary information present in one modality can support prediction where the other is noisy or insensitive, while shared signal across modalities is effectively reinforced, yielding more robust and expressive feature representations. This complementary integration likely underlies the performance gains observed for bimodal fusion approaches relative to the unimodal approaches. 

[3] Tri-modal: G and H show the results of the Hybrid-Gated Fusion approach and SCM-based Gated Fusion approach, respectively. Both tri-modal approaches outperformed all unimodal and bimodal baselines, with the Hybrid-Gated Fusion approach (MAE: 3.94, r: 0.758, ρ: 0.709) achieved better predictive performance than SCM-based Gated Fusion approach (MAE: 4.09, r: 0.710, ρ: 0.748).

The results demonstrate that fusing a greater number of complementary modalities yields richer and more powerful representations for prediction. Especially, Hybrid-Gated Fusion approach reweights each FCM with a gating factor derived from SCM, the other FCM, and the FCM itself, thereby capturing cross-modal consensus more effectively. This suggests that the modalities not only complement one another but benefit from shared cross-modal consensus representations. 

[4] I displays the label-shuffled control result and J displays the MAE values obtained from all approaches in descending order.

The label-shuffled controls (STAI-T permuted) gave poor predictions, confirming that the reported gains were not attributable by chance.   

2) Behavioral inhibition system sensitivity (BIS score)

In Fig. 2, “BIS Sensitivity” panel shows the predictive performance for BIS sensitivity, and conditions A to J correspond to the same configurations as for trait anxiety.

The relative performances of unimodal, bimodal, and tri-modal approaches showed similar trends to those observed for trait anxiety, with tri-modal fusion approaches outperforming bimodal and unimodal baselines. 

This indicates that the modality-specific and fusion effects described above for trait anxiety generalize consistently to BIS sensitivity.

Conclusion

The proposed tri-modal brain connectivity fusion frameworks improved predictive performance for anxiety-related individual psychological traits compared with unimodal and bimodal baselines. These findings demonstrate that integrating heterogeneous structural, functional, and electrophysiological modalities yields more informative representations for predicting trait anxiety and BIS sensitivity as anxiety-related psychological states. In particular, the Hybrid-Gated Fusion approach, which employs a consensus-aware gating mechanism, produces richer feature representations by jointly modeling common cross-modal information and modality-specific complementary features. This design likely contributes to the further improvements in predictive accuracy observed in our experiments.

The results indicate the feasibility of accurately predicting individual psychological traits solely from neuroimaging data. Such neuroimaging-based models may ultimately support more precise risk assessment, facilitate the early identification of vulnerable individuals, and aid clinicians in making more informed diagnostic and treatment decisions. Importantly, self-report and interview-based assessments of internalizing symptoms are known to be vulnerable to social desirability, impression management, and underreporting, particularly in stigmatized domains such as depression and anxiety4,5. Neuroimaging-derived trait predictions could therefore serve as an objective, complementary source of information that helps to mitigate biases in self-reported symptoms, rather than replacing clinical judgment.

At the same time, several limitations must be acknowledged. 

First, the tri-modal sample size was small, and all results were obtained from a single dataset using LOOCV, so overfitting and limited generalizability remain important concerns. Although a label-shuffled control analysis suggested that the observed gains are not purely attributable to random fluctuations, this alone does not fully rule out optimistic bias in small-N settings. 

Second, we did not include strong classical baselines (e.g., regularized linear models, CPM) or simpler deep-learning architectures, which are necessary to fully quantify the added value of the proposed GNN-based fusion.

Finally, the proposed approaches were evaluated only on two anxiety-related psychological traits, namely trait anxiety and BIS sensitivity. 

To more clearly demonstrate the generalizability of the tri-modal fusion frameworks, future studies should apply them to a wider range of psychological traits, incorporate stronger baseline models and more rigorous validation protocols on larger samples (e.g., nested or repeated cross-validation, permutation testing), and evaluate whether the same architectures can reliably capture individual differences across multiple domains, ideally including external validation on independent cohorts.
