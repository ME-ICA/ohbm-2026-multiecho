Introduction

Epilepsy is a disease characterized by recurrent unprovoked seizures, with focal epilepsy, wherein the seizures originate from a single hemisphere and there is an epileptogenic focus, being the most common classification in children1. It is well known that functional networks in children with epilepsy are disturbed compared to healthy controls. Specifically alterations are seen in large-scale functional networks such as the default mode and sensorimotor networks 2. Children with epilepsy have been shown to exhibit both increases and decreases in functional connectivity across various brain regions. Taken together, this speaks to the heterogeneity of the population of those with epilepsy, but also the reorganization of brain networks within the population. Many of the existing studies which examine functional connectivity, assess static functional connectivity, assessing the relationship between areas on average. Dynamic functional connectivity wherein functional connectivity is assessed over time, may help to further characterize the reorganization of the functional networks in children with epilepsy. The current study aims to further understand the brain network reorganization in epilepsy by using Hidden Markov Models to assess dynamic functional connectivity in pediatric patients with epilepsy compared to healthy controls.

Methods

Sixty-six patients with epilepsy (age M = 12.05, SD = 3.87, sex = 60.61% female) were screened for inclusion. Epilepsy patients were recruited from an outpatient neurosurgical clinic, and healthy controls were recruited from the OurBrainsCAN Research Registry. Participants were included in the study if they completed T1 structural image and a resting state fMRI. Further, patients with epilepsy were only included if there was an appropriate age matched healthy control, wherein the ages were within one year. Resting-state fMRI images were then preprocessed using a combination of FSL, ANTs, and AFNI. Steps included slice time correction, motion correction, skull stripping, normalization, and denoising. The AAL116 atlas was then used alongside the normalized preprocessed functional images to generate a 116x116 matrix for every volume in the preprocessed functional image representing the average activation by region per volume for each participant. These matrices were then used to conduct a Hidden Markov Model analysis to explore the dynamic functional connectivity, this was completed using the GLHMM toolbox3. Fractional occupancies, reflecting differences in time spent in states, and switching rate, representing how frequently participants switched between states were assessed between participants using a t-test where a p < 0.05 was considered significant. A regression model was also used to predict time spent in states, where sex, age, and whether a patient had epilepsy or was a control were used as predictors. 

Results

In total, eighty-two participants were included in the study – 41 patients with focal epilepsy (age M = 11.13, SD = 3.31, sex = 53.66 % female) and 41 age matched healthy controls (age M = 10.79, SD = 3.35, sex = 56.10 % female). 

Four different brain states were generated with the Hidden Markov Model analyses. The first state was characterized by strong localized positive and negative functional connectivity. Positive functional connectivity was seen between: the superior, middle, and medial orbital frontal gyrus; the hippocampus and amygdala; the calcarine fissure, cuneus, and lingual gyrus; the angular gyrus and precuneus; the caudate, putamen, pallidum, and thalamus; and Heschel’s gyrus, superior temporal gyrus and temporal pole. Negative functional connectivity was seen between middle and inferior occipital gyrus, and the insula, cingulum, and caudate. The second state was characterized by moderate localized positive functional connectivity diffusely. The third state presented mainly with strong localized positive functional connectivity. This was seen between the amygdala and hippocampus, between the calcarine, cuneus, and lingual gyrus, as well as between the superior, middle, and inferior occipital gyri and fusiform gyrus. There was also negative functional connectivity between the insula and cingulum with the middle and inferior occipital gyrus. The fourth state was characterized by strong positive functional connectivity diffusely across the frontal lobe, occipital lobe and the cerebellum, increased connectivity between the amygdala and hippocampus, as well as within the parietal lobe. 

Patients with epilepsy spent more time (M = 0.35, SD = 0.36) in state four compared to healthy controls (M = 0.05, SD = 0.08, t(44) = -5.24, p < 0.001). In contrast, healthy controls spent more time in state three (M = 0.36, SD = 0.32) compared to children with epilepsy (M = 0.16, SD = 0.24, t(74) = 3.10, p = 0.0027). There were no significant differences in the times spent in state one and state two between groups (p > 0.05). There was no significant difference in switching rate between patients with epilepsy (M = 0.10, SD = 0.07) compared to healthy controls (M = 0.07, SD = 0.04); however it did approach significance (t(57) = -1.96, p = 0.0550).

Conclusion

In summary, we assessed dynamic brain connectivity in pediatric patients with epilepsy and compared them to age matched healthy controls. We assessed four functional networks generated using the Hidden Markov Model. The patients with epilepsy demonstrated changes in their dynamic functional connectivity compared to healthy controls. Significant differences between the groups were seen in which states the participants spent most of their time, however there were no significant differences seen in how often they switched between states. 

Healthy controls spent more time in state three, characterized by strong positive functional connectivity in localized regions, whereas patients with epilepsy spent more time in state four, characterized by strong positive connectivity more diffusely. The existing literature examining static models of functional connectivity have shown both increases and decreases in functional connectivity in populations with epilepsy 2. Some studies which found increases in functional connectivity have suggested that hyperconnectivity may be a compensatory mechanism, and notably, it has also been associated with decreased cognitive performance in other disease states 4,5. Overall, this demonstrates increased brain network disorganization compared to controls. While the switching rate between groups was not significantly different, it did approach significance, and a study with a larger population size may reveal an increased switching rate in patients with epilepsy which would further support brain reorganization and disorganization.   

In conclusion, pediatric patients with focal epilepsy show changes in dynamic functional connectivity characterized by more time spent in states with diffuse increased functional connectivity. The current study is limited as it does not include characteristics specific to epilepsy in our prediction models, such as epileptogenic focus, handedness, and if the patient underwent surgery at a later date. Future directions include exploring if the location of the epileptogenic foci predicts fractional occupancy and how fractional occupancy and switching rate relate to cognitive outcomes.
