Introduction

Preterm birth, defined as delivery before 37 weeks of gestation1, is associated with alterations in brain development and an increased likelihood of motor difficulties. While these developmental differences often persist into school age2,3, the precise neurophysiological mechanisms that shape motor cortical function in children born preterm remain to be fully elucidated. Neural oscillations, particularly in the high-gamma frequency band (60–90 Hz), are fundamental to voluntary motor execution4,5. In the primary motor cortex (M1), movement-related gamma synchronization reflects the precise coordination of local cortical circuits and the balance of excitatory-inhibitory activity. Critically, however, no study has yet directly linked movement-related gamma synchronization and hemispheric lateralization to the degree of perinatal maturity in school-aged children born preterm. This study aimed to characterize the developmental status of motor cortical circuits in school-aged children born preterm using child-customized magnetoencephalography (MEG). We hypothesized that these children would exhibit distinct patterns of motor-induced gamma synchronization and hemispheric organization compared to their full-term peers. Furthermore, we posited that these oscillatory profiles would be quantitatively associated with the degree of perinatal maturity, such as gestational age and birth weight, thereby serving as neural signatures of the developmental process rather than solely reflecting current functional performance. 

Methods

Thirty-seven children participated in this study. The preterm group included 18 children born before 37 weeks of gestation (mean age = 69.28 ± 3.64 months; 6 females), and the control group included 19 full-term children born at 38–42 weeks of gestation (mean age = 69.05 ± 6.92 months; 6 females), matched for age and sex. All participants were right-handed as confirmed by the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory to control for hemispheric dominance effects. General cognitive ability was assessed using the Kaufman Assessment Battery for Children (K-ABC), and no significant group differences in achievement scores were observed (p = 0.154). The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kanazawa University Hospital, and written informed consent was obtained from parents in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. 

Children performed a child-friendly, video-game-like motor response task designed to maintain attention and minimize head motion. Visual stimuli were projected onto a screen subtending approximately 21° × 26°. Each trial began with a mission cue (1200 ms), followed by a fixation cross and a variable delay (1.5–2.5 s). A fruit image then appeared as a Go signal, prompting participants to press a button with their right index finger using a non-magnetic fiber-optic response pad (LUMINA LSC400, Cedrus). Correct responses triggered an animated feedback sequence (800 ms). Each participant completed 100 successful trials across 10 blocks. 

Neuromagnetic activity was recorded using a pediatric-customized 151-channel whole-head MEG system (KIT PQ1151R) in a magnetically shielded room. Data were sampled at 2000 Hz with an online low-pass filter at 200 Hz. Structural T1-weighted MRI images were acquiredfor anatomical co-registration using a 1.5T GE SIGNA Explorer system (TR = 435.68 ms, TE = 0.024 ms, flip angle = 7°, FOV = 220 mm, matrix = 256 × 256, slice thickness = 1.7 mm). 

MEG preprocessing was performed using Brainstorm. Data were resampled to 500 Hz and band-pass filtered between 0.5 and 100 Hz; a 60-Hz notch filter was applied. Eye-blink and cardiac artifacts were removed using independent component analysis (Infomax, 20 components) combined with visual inspection. Source reconstruction was conducted using an overlapping-spheres head model and a cortical surface source space. Minimum Norm Imaging was applied with depth weighting (0.5) and noise covariance regularization (0.1). Time–frequency representations were computed using Morlet wavelets. Source-level signals were extracted from the left (contralateral) and right (ipsilateral) precentral gyri defined by the Desikan–Killiany atlas. Analyses focused on high-gamma activity (70–90 Hz) within 0–100 ms following the button press. Power changes were quantified as event-related synchronization (ERS) relative to a baseline period (−2000 to −1500 ms). Hemispheric motor dominance was quantified using a lateralization index (LI): (Contra − Ipsi) / (Contra + Ipsi). 

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 30. Independent-samples t-tests were used to compare behavioral performance and neural measures (gamma power, LI, peak frequency) between groups, with Levene’s test assessing variance equality. Pearson correlation analyses examined relationships between neural indices and perinatal factors (gestational age, birth weight) as well as standardized motor measures (K-ABC Hand Movements, Vineland-II Total Motor Score). Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05 (two-tailed). 

Results

Analysis of the button press task revealed a significant difference in processing speed between the groups. The full-term group demonstrated a mean response time of 459.0 ± 107.8 ms. In contrast, the children born preterm were significantly slower, with a mean response time of 600.6 ± 162.4 ms. An independent sample t-test confirmed this difference was statistically significant (t(35) = -3.141, p = 0.003). Correlation analysis further elucidated this finding, showing that response time was strongly negatively correlated with both Gestational Age (r = -0.511, p = 0.001) and Gestational Weight (r = -0.504, p = 0.001). This indicates that a lower degree of perinatal maturity predicts slower motor processing speed at school age. Additionally, slower response times were associated with lower scores on the K-ABC Hand Motor subtest (r = -0.511, p = 0.001) and the Vineland-II (VABS) Total Motor Score (r = -0.362, p = 0.028), validating the task's relevance to general motor functioning. 

Time-frequency analysis revealed a characteristic burst of gamma-band activity (70–90 Hz) in the primary motor cortex (M1) synchronized with the button press in both groups. However, quantitative comparison showed a distinct reduction in the preterm group. Specifically, the Event-Related Synchronization (ERS) of gamma power in the contralateral (left) M1 was 37.97 ± 25.82% for the preterm group, which was significantly lower than the 59.89 ± 28.87% observed in the full-term group (t(35) = 2.430, p = 0.020). This suggests a difference in the local recruitment of neuronal populations required for efficient motor output.The calculation of the Lateralization Index (LI) highlighted a profound difference in cortical organization. The full-term group exhibited a typical positive Lateralization Index (0.24 ± 0.21), reflecting strong contralateral dominance. Conversely, the preterm group showed a significantly reduced, negative index (-0.11 ± 0.37), indicating a shift towards bilateral or ipsilateral recruitment. This group difference was highly significant (t(35) = 3.514, p = 0.002). 

We conducted detailed correlation analyses to link these neural signatures to developmental variables. Contralateral M1 gamma power was significantly positively correlated with Gestational Age (r = 0.404, p = 0.013) and Birth Weight (r = 0.407, p = 0.012). This suggests that the capacity to generate robust gamma synchrony is tightly linked to the extent of perinatal maturity. Interestingly, contralateral gamma power did not correlate significantly with current motor performance scores (p > 0.05). In contrast, the Lateralization Index showed significant correlations with both perinatal factors and current motor skills. LI was positively correlated with Gestational Age (r = 0.476, p = 0.003) and Birth Weight (r = 0.533, p = 0.001). Furthermore, a higher (more contralateral) LI was significantly associated with better performance on the K-ABC Hand Motor Score (r = 0.415, p = 0.011). This dissociation implies that while local gamma power reflects biological maturity, the efficiency of hemispheric lateralization is more directly related to functional motor competence.

Conclusion

This study represents the first comprehensive investigation using child-customized magnetoencephalography (MEG) to elucidate the impact of preterm birth on movement-related gamma oscillations. Our findings provide compelling evidence that children born preterm exhibit a distinct neurophysiological profile characterized by two primary alterations: a significant attenuation of local gamma synchronization in the contralateral motor cortex and a marked reduction in hemispheric lateralization during voluntary movement. These results advance our understanding of the neural basis of motor difficulties in this population beyond simple behavioral observations. 

The specific reduction in contralateral gamma power, which was found to be directly proportional to gestational age and birth weight, points to a developmental alteration in the underlying cellular architecture of the motor cortex. Gamma oscillations are generated through the rhythmic interaction of excitatory pyramidal neurons and fast-spiking parvalbumin-positive GABAergic interneurons. The reduced power observed in the preterm group may therefore reflect a delay or disruption in the maturation of these interneuronal networks, which are known to be vulnerable to the complications of premature birth. Additionally, the precise timing required for high-frequency synchronization depends on the integrity of myelination. Given that white matter injury and dysmaturation are hallmarks of preterm brain development, the attenuated gamma response may also serve as a functional readout of compromised local connectivity. 

Furthermore, the observation of atypical lateralization—characterized by increased bilateral or ipsilateral recruitment—suggests a variation in interhemispheric communication. Efficient motor control typically relies on the inhibition of the ipsilateral motor cortex via the corpus callosum. The reduced Lateralization Index observed in the preterm group, which correlated with poorer fine motor skills, implies a less efficient strategy for motor execution, possibly stemming from altered callosal development. This finding aligns with diffusion tensor imaging studies showing reduced callosal integrity in preterm populations. 

Crucially, our data reveal a functional dissociation: contralateral gamma power appears to be a stable "trait" marker of perinatal maturity, while hemispheric lateralization serves as a "state" marker related to current motor proficiency. This distinction is vital for developing targeted interventions. It suggests that while the fundamental capacity for local gamma synchrony may be determined by early developmental events, the functional organization of the motor network (lateralization) is linked to behavioral outcomes and might be a more suitable target for therapeutic modulation. 

In conclusion, this study establishes motor-related gamma oscillations and their lateralization as robust, non-invasive biomarkers of cortical circuit maturation in children born preterm. By identifying these specific neural signatures, we move closer to understanding the biological constraints on motor development in this vulnerable population. Future research should focus on longitudinal tracking of these oscillatory metrics to determine whether they normalize over time or persist into adulthood, and whether they can be modified through targeted motor training or rehabilitation programs. The use of child-friendly MEG technology proves to be a powerful tool in this endeavor, offering a window into the developing brain with unparalleled temporal resolution.
