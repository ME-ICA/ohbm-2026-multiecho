Introduction

Pharmacological treatments improve motor dysfunction in Parkinson’s disease (PD) but do not halt its progression, motivating interest in complementary non-pharmacological approaches. Visual art creation may improve mood, reduce anxiety, and enhance self-expression.1 However, therapeutic responses vary widely. Recent neuroimaging advances introduced the concept of the brain fingerprint, defined as stable, individual-specific patterns of functional connectivity.2 Here, we combined functional connectomics, brain fingerprinting, and predictive modeling to test whether baseline connectivity fingerprints can predict individual responsiveness to art therapy in PD.

Methods

The sample included 23 patients with PD who underwent resting-state fMRI before art therapy and 23 age-matched healthy controls who underwent fMRI but did not receive the intervention.

The art therapy program consisted of 20 sessions over 10 weeks, based on a structured sequence of visual art projects adapted to individual abilities (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT03178786).1 Motor severity (UPDRS-III) was assessed before and after therapy, and clinical response was defined as a ≥15% reduction in score.3

Preprocessing was performed with fMRIPrep and postprocessing with XCP-D. Time series were parcellated into 400 cortical regions using the Schaefer atlas and grouped into seven networks.4

For brain fingerprinting, each resting-state time series was split into two halves, and separate FC matrices were computed using Pearson correlation. Whole-brain identifiability metrics quantified within-subject similarity relative to between-subject similarity, along with identification success rate.2,5 To assess spatial specificity of fingerprints, edgewise intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) were computed for each group using repeated random subsampling (100 iterations, 80% of subjects).

To investigate whether the spatial distribution of FC identifiability differed significantly between the two groups, differences in ICC were tested using permutation-based inference with FDR correction.

To predict art therapy response, graph-theoretical measures were computed from patient FC restricted to fingerprint-informed edge masks. Supervised models (random forest, gradient boosting, k-NN) were evaluated using leave-one-out cross-validation with parameter tuning (i.e. grid search).

Results

Patients with PD and controls did not differ in age, sex distribution and education. Following art therapy, PD patients showed significant motor improvement, with UPDRS-III scores decreasing from 37.8 ± 11.7 to 32.5 ± 11.8 (mean change −5.3; p = 0.002). Ten patients (43%) met the criterion for clinically meaningful improvement and were classified as responders.

Functional connectivity fingerprinting showed robust within-session identifiability in both groups, with within-subject similarity exceeding between-subject similarity and a 100% identification success rate. Within-subject similarity was comparable between groups (≈0.59), while normalized discriminability was higher in controls (median 7.23) than in PD (median 6.65), indicating preserved but reduced fingerprint distinctiveness in PD.

Average edgewise reliability was similar across groups, but its spatial distribution differed. Controls showed higher stability within default mode and control–attention networks, whereas PD exhibited increased stability in visual networks. These patterns were consistent at both edge and nodal levels and reflected disease-related reorganization of connectome fingerprints.

Graph-theoretical measures derived from edges with reduced stability in PD successfully classified responders versus non-responders (Figure 2). Random forest achieved the best performance (accuracy = 0.83, ROC-AUC = 0.80), with eigenvector centrality, assortativity, and rich-club organization as key predictors. Performance dropped markedly when using non-specific or PD-dominant edges, confirming the specificity of the fingerprint-informed approach.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that, although overall fingerprint stability was maintained, PD showed a redistribution of reliability from cognitive control networks toward visual and motor systems, reflecting selective network vulnerability. Importantly, fingerprint-informed network measures derived at baseline successfully predicted individual response to art therapy, linking brain organization to therapeutic benefit.

These findings support the use of brain fingerprints as stable, individualized features for predicting response to non-pharmacological interventions. Future studies should validate these results in larger cohorts, incorporate longitudinal designs, and integrate multimodal fingerprints to further advance personalized rehabilitation strategies in PD.
