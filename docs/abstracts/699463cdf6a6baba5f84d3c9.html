Introduction

The personalization of transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) in clinical applications is based on the assumptions that (i) cortical TMS exerts network effects, indirectly modulating disorder-relevant deep brain regions (e.g., subgenual anterior cingulate cortex (sgACC) in depression); (ii) more precise stimulation of individually located cortical network nodes (e.g., sgACC-anticorrelated spots in the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex) leads to more effective network modulation and, thus, better treatment outcomes; and (iii) different psychiatric symptoms (e.g., dysphoric vs. anxiosomatic symptoms in depression) are related to different functional networks, therefore requiring stimulation of different cortical entry nodes (e.g., dorsolateral vs. dorsomedial). In the present study, we set out to experimentally test these assumptions, by combining personalized TMS with concurrent functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI).

Methods

In N = 20 healthy individuals, based on their individual resting-state fMRI data, we first identified the largest clusters of negative and positive functional connectivity (FC) with the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC)/sgACC within the left dorsolateral and dorsomedial prefrontal cortex, respectively, as our stimulation targets of interest. TMS coil positions were then individually optimized using simulations of the TMS-induced electric field (E-field) to maximize on- and minimize off-target stimulation. In addition, a close-by control TMS coil placement was defined for both FC targets of interest, stimulating an area with less vmPFC/sgACC-FC but similar confounding auditory and somatosensory effects. Each participant underwent these four stimulation conditions (dorsolateral negative FC, dorsolateral negative FC-control, dorsomedial positive FC, dorsomedial positive FC-control) inside the MRI scanner, with single TMS pulses applied to each target at an E-field-adjusted intensity.

Results

Our FC- and E-field-based optimization approach for TMS coil placements resulted in very good overlap between simulated E-fields and both individual FC target clusters and the whole-brain FC topography, with minimal off-target network coverage. However, while some subjects showed specific TMS-induced BOLD modulations in both the directly stimulated, superficial target area as well as functionally connected cortical and subcortical brain regions, others exhibited more diffuse, unspecific BOLD patterns. Overall, at the group level, we did not find any statistically significant differences in BOLD modulations between any of the four experimental conditions (dorsolateral negative FC, dorsolateral negative FC-control, dorsomedial positive FC, dorsomedial positive FC-control), neither across the entire brain nor in the deeply located vmPFC/sgACC regions of interest. Rather, in all conditions, we observed widespread positive BOLD modulations in the auditory cortex (due to the loud TMS click sounds) and the cingulo-opercular network (e.g., anterior insula, dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, supramarginal gyrus, anterior middle frontal gyrus), while negative BOLD modulations were particularly evident in the default mode network (including vmPFC/sgACC). 

Conclusion

In the present study, we experimentally tested assumptions of personalized TMS, using concurrent TMS-fMRI. While we did indeed see an indirect modulation of deep, functionally connected regions when stimulating a superficial, cortical target, these effects were not specific to the stimulated target. Instead, we found no evidence for (i) stronger network modulation when precisely targeting an individual FC target versus a nearby control target and (ii) differential network responses for stimulation of nodes from different functional networks (dorsolateral vs. dorsomedial). This result provokes questions about the need for TMS personalization, actual treatment mechanisms of TMS, sensitivity and appropriateness of the employed methods, as well as dosing and brain-state dependency of TMS. In ongoing work, we will try to further elucidate the observed heterogeneity in BOLD responses, particularly focusing on results at the individual subject level.
