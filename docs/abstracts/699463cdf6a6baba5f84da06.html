Introduction

Covert attention is an important ability that enables one to focus on an object, person, or place without moving the eyes. The alpha increase on the side of the attended visual field (ipsilateral) and decrease on the ignored visual field (contralateral) greatly contribute to the success of this mechanism. Alpha lateralization index (αLI) promotes the top-down processing of stimuli, their voluntary selection according to their salience and task relevance. 

Yet, after right cerebral hemisphere damage (following a stroke, or traumatic brain injury), 30 to 50% of survivors show spatial neglect syndrome. Visuospatial attention deficits, particularly hemispatial neglect, represent a critical target for functional recovery after stroke.  Alpha abnormalities are a surface expression of connectivity breakdown, notably the white matter tracts integrity in fronto-parietal pathways. Often, right hemisphere lesion is accompanied by a pathological α increase (contralateral to the attended visual field) while it decreases in healthy population, leading to left-space processing deficit (Kerkhoff et al. 2021, Lasaponara et al. 2019).

Neurofeedback (NFB) offers a compelling perspective as a non-invasive intervention, enabling individuals to progressively modulate their own brain activity with potential for generalized cognitive rehabilitation. Alpha-based NFB is hypothesized to shift the attentional resources toward the contra lesional hemifield, re-establish the αLI unbalance, and therefore reduce the neglect symptoms. 

Alpha-based NFB validation with a healthy population is the first step, which was accomplished. Implementing NFB, and even more so combining it with fMRI, raises substantial experimental and technical challenges. This project aims to characterize the behavioural benefits of alpha-based NFB training and to identify the neural correlates of successful regulation, with the long-term goal of refining future NFB protocols for clinical rehabilitation.

Methods

Healthy older adults (9 males, 32.5 y.o +/-1.1, Oldfield laterality: 94.4+/-11, QCM anxiety: 1.9+/-0.8, MAIA: 3.15 +/-1.2, challenge: 4.5+/-1, interest: 4.55+/-0.7) participated in our experimental design consisting of four EEG neurofeedback sessions (≈30 min each) during which they learned to modulate their occipito-parietal alpha activity by applying lateralized covert attention shifts. Each session of neurofeedback consists of fifteen baseline-regulation couples, lasting 30s and 80s respectively. During the baseline, the mental state and attention of the participants are controlled by simple mental calculus during which the attention and gaze are directed straight. During the regulation runs, the participants are asked to orient their covert attention to the left while looking at the cross. Their goal is to make the wheels turn clockwise and as fast as possible (actual feedback of the real-time αLI). The quality of their regulation is translated on the screen by the turning direction of the wheels: if they turn clockwise, the alpha power is higher on the left compared with the right power (mean of P3, P7, O1 for the left versus P4, P8, O2, for the right), which is a good regulation. If the right alpha power is higher than the left alpha power, the wheels turn in the anticlockwise direction, and the participants are asked to modify their strategies. During runs 11 to 13, the participant is still asked to covertly attend the left side except that the participant does not receive any feedback (the wheels are immobile). These runs (the transfer run (TR)) evaluate the transferability of the learned skills and the dependence of visual feedback over time. To account for the inter-individual variability at rest (pseudo-neglect), the αLI measured in real-time for the neurofeedback is normalized by the one calculated during a 3min resting-state assessment eyes-open, before being fed back to the participant on the screen.  % of time regulating good (% of time turning in right direction during 80 sec run: LI ≥ 0 = good/all(good + bad)*100 = ACCURACY) and the absolute value of lateralization (αLI: Mean of lateralization during 80 sec run: (Left-Right)/(Left+Right) = SPEED in both direction: good and bad) are extracted for each run (including the TR) for the 4 sessions. Each run has been normalized by the first run of session one, and the slope, R2, and p-value are calculated per participant. Besides, learning is also estimated at the level of the session (slope and average). Sleep-related consolidation is also considered by subtracting the last run of session 1 to the first block of session 2. 

Pre–post assessments include a Posner and Visual search paradigms to quantify attentional performance and evaluate the effect of alpha-based NFB on different aspects of visuospatial attention. 

The Posner task consists of a total of 600 trials, including 420 congruent/valid trials where the cue points are where the target will appear, 90 incongruent/invalid trials, and 90 neutral trials (no predictive cue). Participants are asked to answer which gabbor has tilted as fast as possible. The tilt angle is individually calculated according to the participant's perception threshold using the Staircase method (default 6°) to adapt the difficulty level and ensure progress is observed from session 1 to session 4. Accuracy and response time (RT) are extracted per session and per condition. 

The visual search task consists of finding the target presented on the right or on the left visual part of the screen among 4, 6, or 8 items (level of difficulty/load). The Spatial Bias Index (SBI = RT_left − RT_right) in the difficult visual search condition (6–8 items) is extracted. For each participant, we computed the change in SBI across sessions 1–3 (SBI slope), such that negative slopes reflect a reduction of the rightward bias and thus improved attention towards the left hemifield.

Results

The results support the feasibility of the protocol. We observe voluntary modulation of alpha oscillations during training, behavioural indications of attentional enhancement, and successful validation of EEG correction pipelines inside the MRI environment. 

Alpha-based Neurofeedback

% of time spent regulating good per run (Accuracy) and the αLI (speed of the wheel) increased from session 1 to session 3 as shown by regression analysis: slope=0.46, R2: 0.50, p-value=0.002, with an enhancement of 15.5%+/-6 on average. Sleep-related consolidation was also significant with an increase of 13%+/-8. αLI increased during the sessions when NFB regulation run was compared with its associated baseline calculus. However, we did not observe any significant intra-session improvement measured by the slope. 

We were able to define different learning profiles. Indeed, not all the participants showed an increase in good regulation or absolute αLI from session 1 to session 3, but from session 1 to session 2 only. These results show the exact kind of complexity reported in longitudinal neurofeedback datasets: heterogeneous learning profiles, ceiling effects, session-by-session variability, motivation/fatigue fluctuations, and subjects who improve early vs late.  

Posner- behavior

The RT decreased from session 1 to session 4, and the magnitude of the difference from ses4-ses01 correlated significantly with the increase of the % of good regulation measured by the slope throughout the ses-01 and ses-03 (slope=0.121, R2=0.72, p=6.7e-02). Moreover, the sleep-related consolidation gains (Ses02 (first)-Ses01(last)) showed a significant correlation with the Posner Accuracy during session 4 (slope=2.05, R2=0.78, p=4.4e-02).

Posner-EEG

Alpha time course and theta time course showed a higher P3 for incongruent trials compared with congruent and neutral trials. ERP analysis confirmed that congruent trials-related P3 was higher in session 1 compared with session 4. Then cue-locked alpha lateralization (contralateral -ipsilateral) analysis demonstrated that alpha lateralization difference was present around 300ms post cue during session1 but absent in session 4 (post-NFB training).  

Visual Search

Across participants, the NFB learning slopes based on the lateralisation index (LI) and the Metric_M showed only weak, non-significant associations with SBI slopes (|r| ≤0.05, p ≥0.93). In contrast, the consolidation index between sessions 1 and 2 (cons1–2) exhibited a strong positive correlation with the SBI slope in difficult trials (r = 0.83, p = 0.08). Participants showing larger early consolidation of their NFB performance also exhibited the largest changes in spatial bias across sessions. Although this effect does not reach the conventional significance threshold due to the sample size, the effect magnitude is large and suggests that early consolidation of NFB learning may be a key predictor of behavioural transfer under high attentional load.

From a neurocognitive perspective, these findings suggest that alpha-based NFB does not automatically translate into a uniform reduction of spatial bias. Instead, behavioural transfer appears to depend on early consolidation of the trained pattern and on task demands. The trend-level session × difficulty interaction on SBI, combined with the strong association between the consolidation index (cons1–2) and SBI changes in difficult trials, indicates that participants who stabilise their NFB performance early are those who later show the largest reweighting of visuospatial attention when the search becomes demanding.

This is consistent with models proposing that alpha-band lateralisation implements a flexible, context-dependent gating mechanism: the trained pattern may be preferentially engaged when attentional resources are taxed, as in high-load visual search. 

Conclusion

The inter-session increase and intra-session stabilization points towards an interaction between practice and sleep contribution in the NFB training success.  

These initial findings justify further neurobehavioral and neuroimaging analyses, paving the way toward optimized NFB-based rehabilitation strategies for hemispatial neglect.
