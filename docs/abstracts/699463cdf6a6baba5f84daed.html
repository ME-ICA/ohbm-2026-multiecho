Introduction

Control of the shoulder joint demands complex coordination across cortical, spinal, and musculoskeletal systems to execute complex, multi‑degree‑of‑freedom movements like pointing. Cortical oscillations in beta (15–30 Hz) and gamma (>30 Hz) bands within primary motor cortex and premotor areas is thought to reflect partially distinct motor control processes: beta rhythms are often associated with sustained sensorimotor integration and steady force output, whereas gamma oscillations are more related to dynamic, phasic drive for rapid trajectory adjustments and proprioceptive integration. Cortico‑muscular coherence (CMC) in these bands provides a frequency specific index of coupling between cortical activity and muscle activation. Beta-band CMC is typically stronger during sustained isometric holds, whereas gamma-band CMC may increase during more dynamic phases. Intermuscular coherence (IMC) can further index shared descending neural drive across muscles, potentially reflecting time-varying synergies.

Acute nociceptive input —such as experimentally induced pain—can disrupt shoulder kinematics, increase compensatory mechanisms, alter range of motion, and alter muscle recruitment4,5. These adaptations may protect painful tissues but can reduce motor efficiency. However, the neural mechanisms underlying these changes are still unclear1. It remains unclear how transient pain reorganizes functional connectivity in brain–muscle networks (BMNs) during dynamic upper limb movements. Here, we characterise time–frequency BMNs using EEG–EMG coherence during a dynamic2, repetitive pointing task in healthy adults, focusing on beta and gamma CMC and IMC across shoulder and upper limb muscles. We explore whether acute, transient experimental shoulder pain alters these networks across the reaching cycle.

Methods

Sixteen healthy, right‑handed adults (mean (SD) age 28 (4) years; 5 women) provided informed consent under local ethics approval. Participants performed repetitive pointing with the right arm while seated, paced at 0.25 Hz using auditory cues. Baseline comprised 70 repetitions. Acute pain was induced by injecting 1 mL 6% hypertonic saline (NaCl) into the anterior deltoid muscle. Participants continued the task until self‑reported pain intensity fell below 3/10 on a numerical rating scale (NRS; 0 = no pain, 10 = worst imaginable). Pain ratings were sampled every 15 s.

EEG was recorded using a 64‑channel active electrode system (LiveAmp, Brain Vision, USA; impedances <5 kΩ). EMG was recorded from eight right shoulder and upper‑limb muscles using bipolar surface electrodes (20 mm inter‑electrode distance, microwave, Cometa, Italy): upper, middle, and lower trapezius, anterior, middle, and posterior deltoid, long head of the biceps brachii, and long head of the triceps brachii. Index finger kinematics were recorded 3D motion capture (Vicon; 10 cameras; markers on index finger, forearm, elbow, and shoulder). Each repetition was segmented from -300 ms relative to movement onset to +300 ms after target reach. Movement onset defined as the first time the index angular velocity exceeded 5% of its maximum value, computed by averaging each repetition peak value.

EMG was high‑pass filtered (4th‑order Butterworth, 20 Hz cutoff) and full‑wave rectified using the Hilbert transform. EEG preprocessing in EEGLAB included 1–100 Hz band‑pass filtering, artefact removal with independent component analysis (ICA), and rejection of trials with bad channels or prominent eye blinks. Data were down‑sampled to 250 Hz and epoched. C3 and C4 electrodes were selected to compute CMC. Segments were divided into 100 equally spaced time points. CMC and IMC were computed using Welch’s method (Hanning window, 300 ms length) in beta (15–30 Hz) and gamma (30–100 Hz) bands. Statistical significance was estimated with 95% confidence intervals based on the number of repetitions per condition, according to the following formula: CI = (1 – 0.05)^1/(N-1), with N = number of repetitions3. For consistency across participants, the minimum number of repetitions was used to define a common threshold across participants and conditions. Coherence matrices (10 × 10: 2 EEG × 8 EMG channels, across time–frequency bins for each beta and gamma band) were combined first, then decomposed using non‑negative matrix factorisation (NNMF) to extract low‑dimensional BMNs. Components explaining at least 5% of the variance were retained. All analyses were performed in MATLAB 2025a.

Results

NNMF identified four networks that together accounted for ~72% of the variance in CMC and IMC patterns across participants and conditions, suggesting that a relatively low‑dimensional set of coupling patterns explained most of the observed connectivity. Pain episodes lasted about 5 minutes on average, enabling network tracking over multiple repetitions during sustained nociceptive input (mean (SD); 6 (1)).

 Across networks, the activation profiles indicated that acute shoulder pain modulated the activation of all identified networks. Network 1 exhibited pronounced changes from movement onset through to target reaching. Network 2 showed alterations primarily before movement onset and during the arm-lifting phase. Immediately after movement initiation and near the reaching point, the timing profiles of Networks 1 and 2 were similar. In contrast, Network 4 displayed major changes around movement onset and close to target reaching, while Network 3 demonstrated similar patterns to Network 4, but with less prominence. Some networks were dominated by shoulder muscles, whereas others gave relatively higher weight to more distal muscles. Differences between beta and gamma maps were present but appeared smaller than the differences between networks, suggesting that the main organisational feature was which muscles were co‑modulated with the cortex, while frequency band provided a finer modulation. Combined channel‑weight plots indicated that some muscles (for example, specific deltoid and upper‑limb muscles) contributed strongly to several networks, consistent with shared membership in multiple control networks that could be engaged to different degrees across conditions. In contrast, other channels showed high loading in only one component, which may reflect more specialised roles within the shoulder synergy architecture. As expected, CMC was detected to a lower, yet significant, magnitude than IMC.

Conclusion

Acute experimental shoulder pain was associated with changes in brain–muscle interactions that could be captured by a small set of recurrent coupling networks. Four BMNs explained approximately 72% of the variance in corticospinal and intermuscular coherence during repetitive pointing, indicating a low-dimensional structure underlying these interactions. Pain did not exert a uniform effect across the whole movement; rather, it modulated activation profiles in a phase-specific manner, with some networks showing pronounced changes around movement onset and others during arm lifting or near target reaching. Each network exhibited distinct muscle weightings, with some dominated by shoulder muscles and others involving more distal muscles, while beta–gamma differences were present but secondary to network-specific organization. These findings suggest that nociceptive input influences the recruitment of pre-existing control networks, shifting the relative neural drive distribution to shoulder and upper limb muscles while task performance is maintained. Although descriptive and requiring formal statistical confirmation, this framework highlights BMN structure as a promising approach for studying pain-related changes in oscillatory motor control and may inform future neuro-modulatory or rehabilitative strategies.
