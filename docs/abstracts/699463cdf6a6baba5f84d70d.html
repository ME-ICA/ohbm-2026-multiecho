Introduction

As part of the Julich-Brain Atlas1, the BigBrain dataset2 provides the first ultrahigh-resolution 3D model of the human brain at 20 µm isotropic resolution, reconstructed from 7,404 histological sections. It enables cytoarchitectonic mapping at a level of cellular detail that bridges microscopic organization with macroscale imaging. While the cytoarchitectonic areas of the Julich Brain Atlas are based on the mapping of 2D images of histological sections, resulting in a spatial resolution of 1 mm in MNI and fsaverage spaces, not all of these areas had previously been mapped in BigBrain space, which had a more sparse coverage. Therefore, the goal of the study was to close this gap to fully leverage information about cortical parcellation in a coherent way.

Methods

We used a hybrid workflow that combines expert identification of cortical areas using statistical tools and image analysis with deep learning-based 3D reconstructions. Through the ATLaSUI interface3, neuroscientists annotated every 10th – 15th histological section, providing training data for the CytoNet model4. CytoNet infers cytoarchitectonically meaningful correspondences between annotated sections by explicitly modeling cortical layer continuity and laminar profiles, rather than relying on geometric interpolation. This enables the reconstruction of area boundaries that remain consistent with microstructural transitions across the cortical ribbon, even in highly folded regions. The resulting 3D maps therefore preserve both laminar organization and areal topology at 20 µm resolution, providing a biologically grounded alternative to slice-wise interpolation approaches. Large-scale model inference and reconstruction were performed on the JURECA-DC supercomputer (Jülich Supercomputing Centre, JSC).

Results

We have mapped and 3D-reconstructed additional 167 areas in both hemispheres of the BigBrain. They are available via the siibra tool suite5 and the EBRAINS Knowledge Graph (https://atlases.ebrains.eu/). This new set of areas now includes all cortical areas available in the Julich-Brain Atlas based on the mapping of ten postmortem brains. The resulting maps are made available in the EBRAINS atlas ecosystem, enabling interactive visualization and reproducible programmatic access within a unified 3D reference space. The resulting 3D maps preserve cytoarchitectonic boundaries across the full cortical extent at microscopic resolution. 

Conclusion

By substantially extending cytoarchitectonic coverage in the BigBrain reference space, an important gap in multilevel atlas frameworks has been addressed, namely the limited availability of high-resolution reconstructions of cortical areas in the BigBrain. The new areas can now be integrated into other reference systems and may serve as microstructural reference data for modeling and simulation. This new and more detailed map in the BigBrain facilitates cross-scale analyses linking the BigBrain with widely used macroscopic spaces such as MNI and FreeSurfer, thereby supporting the assignment of functional, molecular, and connectivity data to cytoarchitectonically defined regions. Integrated into multilevel atlas environments such as the siibra tool suite5, these 3D reconstructions serve as a bridge between microscopic cortical organization and macroscale neuroimaging, enabling more precise interpretation of brain signals across scales. These new cortical maps will, in a second step, be supplemented by those of subcortical nuclei.
