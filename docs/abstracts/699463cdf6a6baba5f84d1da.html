Introduction

Proprioceptive deficits have been reported to emerge in healthy aging after the age of 65, with a particularly marked impairment in the ability to discriminate movement velocity [1]. A previous fMRI study has shown reduced lateralization of primary sensorimotor activations in older adults during unilateral proprioceptive stimulation when compared to young adults [2]. Importantly, this age-related reduction in lateralized sensorimotor activations has been shown to correlate with declines in proprioceptive performance [1,2]. Although aging is known to be associated with widespread microstructural alterations in the brain, the potential link between these structural changes and proprioceptive decline remains to be established. 

In the present study, we investigated whether the relative integrity of interhemispheric structural connectivity between the hemispheres might account for individual differences in proprioceptive acuity across adulthood, including young and older adults. The integrity of interhemispheric white-matter tracts is known to support interhemispheric communication, sensory integration, and cortical modulation—particularly inhibitory interactions—between bilateral sensorimotor cortices [3].  

To address this question, we examined the relationship between white matter microstructural changes and proprioceptive performance by combining diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), with psychophysical measures obtained during proprioceptively induced hand-movement illusions. Using tractography to delineate individual interhemispheric callosal tracts, we applied multivariate analyses with cross-prediction to determine whether microstructural features of specific tract segments predict proprioceptive performance independently of chronological age. 

Methods

Participants. 
Twenty-one young adults (20–28 years) and twenty-one older adults (65–75 years), all healthy and right-handed, participated in the study.  

Proprioceptive test  

All the participants underwent a psychophysical experiment in our laboratory to determinate their ability to discriminate illusion velocity during proprioceptive stimulation. This psychophysical experiment consisted of a two-alternative forced choice task where participants underwent pairs of proprioceptive stimulation of various intensities and compared the velocity of two movement illusions. Individual psychophysical curves were used to extract the just noticeable difference that reflects the discrimination threshold. The older group exhibited higher values of the proprioceptive and tactile discriminative thresholds, which corresponded to a lower ability to discriminate the velocity of movements.  

MRI Acquisition and White Matter Analysis. 

White-matter microstructure was examined using diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) and constrained spherical deconvolution (CSD), with a specific focus on interhemispheric pathways within the corpus callosum. For each participant, a fractional anisotropy (FA) map was computed and subsequently used for tractography of seven callosal tracts reconstructed with TractSeg 4.  According to TractSeg’s subdivision, the segmented tracts included: Rostrum (CC1), Genu (CC2), Rostral Body–Premotor (CC3), Anterior Midbody–Primary Motor (CC4), Posterior Midbody–Primary Somatosensory (CC5), Isthmus (CC6), and Splenium (CC7). To make sure that the reconstruction of the tract was comparable across subjects, we made a cohort-based template for each of the tracts.  

Tractometry. 
FA values were extracted along each tract across 100 equidistant segments (tractometry). Due to high variability at tract endpoints, the first and last segments were excluded, yielding 98 segments per tract and per participant. Such segmentation suggests that segment 50 and the near-by segments reference the midline of the corpus callosum and the brain.  This procedure provided an individual-level profile of white-matter integrity for each callosal bundle. 

Group-Level Comparisons. 
To examine group differences between young and older adults, tractometry profiles were compared using the TractSeg tractometry framework. For each tract, a segment-wise t-test was performed, followed by correction for multiple comparisons. 

Machine Learning Analysis. 
To investigate the relationship between structural integrity and proprioceptive function, independent of age grouping, a multivariate machine-learning approach was employed with all participants analyzed together without imposing age-related categories. 

Dimensionality reduction was performed using recursive feature elimination (RFE) to rank and select the most informative structural features across the 98 segments of each callosal tract. Feature selection was conducted separately for two prediction targets: (i) proprioceptive performance (JND) and (ii) chronological age, considering the features ranked until the threshold of 5.  

For each prediction task, a Voting Regressor (Gradient Boost regressor and RandomForestRegressor) was used to estimate the target variable. Model performance was evaluated using the mean absolute error (MAE), the mean squared error (MSE) and R squared error between predicted and true values across 1,000 iterations, allowing assessment of the predictive contribution of each tract. 

Cross-Prediction. 
To evaluate the specificity of the selected structural predictors, the final model trained to predict JND was subsequently applied to predict age, and vice versa. The resulting MAE distributions were compared between the two prediction tasks to assess the degree to which structural features uniquely accounted for proprioceptive performance versus age. 

Results

Univariate group-based analyses of fractional anisotropy (FA) along callosal tracts revealed widespread age-related reductions in white-matter integrity. Specifically, older adults exhibited significantly lower FA values across extensive portions of all seven interhemispheric tracts. Notably, these reductions were most pronounced in CC1 and CC2, where large bilateral segments—and even the entire tract in CC2—showed reduced FA. Age-related differences were also observed in the other tracts.  In CC4, CC5, and CC6, multiple regions exhibited significantly lower FA in older adults, distributed on both sides of the tract relative to the midline (defined at segment 50). In CC7, age-related alterations were confined to the left hemisphere.  

Given that proprioceptive acuity (JND) declines with age, we applied multivariate analysis to determine whether microstructural differences in specific interhemispheric tracts could predict JND independently of age, and account for the variability in JND observed among all participants, including younger individuals. This multivariate analysis, performed without imposing a priori age-group classifications, revealed that FA values from features in the CC4 tract were stronger predictors of proprioceptive performance (JND) than of chronological age, highlighting the specificity of CC4 microstructural integrity in explaining proprioceptive acuity. 

As illustrated in Figure 2, the features selected within CC4 differed depending on whether the target variable was JND or age, and were more coherently clustered when predicting JND than when predicting age alone. Consistent with this observation, comparisons of mean squared error (MSE) between regression models showed significantly different MSE distributions for JND and age predictions based on the respective selected features. 

A similar, though weaker, association was found for the CC6 tract. Indeed, this tract revealed features that significantly predict the age and the JND, although the latter prediction showed weaker power compared to predicting age. These results of significantly different MSE distribution between model predictions show the disentanglement of proprioceptive performance from chronological age.  

In contrast, FA features from other tracts, such as CC7, reliably predicted participants' age but not proprioceptive performance, suggesting that these structural changes primarily reflect general effects of aging rather than proprioceptive-specific functional decline. 

All together, the current findings indicate that while age-related reductions in white-matter integrity are widespread across callosal tracts, only specific microstructural features—particularly within CC4—are selectively associated with proprioceptive acuity independently of age. This dissociation between general aging effects and proprioception-specific structural correlates provides a framework for interpreting the functional relevance of callosal integrity in sensorimotor perception. 

Conclusion

The present results confirm the presence of age-related structural brain alterations, characterized by reduced FA across multiple interhemispheric callosal tracts in older adults. In line with previous literature, these results support the notion that normal aging is associated with a progressive decline in the integrity of interhemispheric white-matter fibers [3]. 

Beyond these global aging effects, the multivariate analysis revealed a more selective relationship between white-matter microstructure and proprioceptive function. While chronological age could be reliably predicted from the integrity of several interhemispheric tracts, only alterations in the tract interconnecting the primary motor cortices (CC4) specifically predicted proprioceptive acuity, independently of age. This finding is consistent with previous fMRI studies showing that reduced lateralization of activity within primary sensorimotor cortices is associated with poorer proprioceptive discrimination in older adults [2]. Given that homologous motor cortices are connected via transcallosal inhibitory pathways, microstructural degradation of the motor callosal tract may lead to reduced interhemispheric inhibition, thereby contributing to diminished lateralization of sensorimotor activity and impaired proprioceptive processing. 

In addition, callosal fibers connecting the inferior parietal lobules (CC6) may also play a critical role in maintaining proprioceptive function. The inferior parietal cortices are robustly engaged during proprioceptively induced illusions of movement, and Landelle et al. [2] demonstrated that older adults with better proprioceptive discrimination exhibited stronger resting-state functional connectivity between sensorimotor and inferior parietal regions. These observations highlight the critical role of parietal interhemispheric connectivity in supporting proprioceptive perception not only in older individuals.  

Taken together, the present findings demonstrate for the first time that microstructural degeneration of motor and parietal callosal fibers contributes to altered proprioceptive processing and impaired movement perception. Importantly, these effects are not solely attributable to chronological age but reflect tract-specific structure–function relationships that underpin individual differences in proprioceptive acuity across the adult lifespan.
