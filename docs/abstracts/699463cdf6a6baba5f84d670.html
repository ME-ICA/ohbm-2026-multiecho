Introduction

The human brain exhibits intrinsic neural timescales (INTs) that vary systematically across cortical regions, reflecting how brain regions integrate incoming signals over varying temporal windows. Using functional MRI (fMRI), these timescales can be estimated through autocorrelation analysis of the time series, revealing a hierarchical organization from areas with short timescales, supporting rapid segregation of inputs, to regions with long timescales, enabling sustained integration.^1 This temporal hierarchy aligns with the brain's sensorimotor-to-association (S-A) axis, a unifying framework of brain organization that captures spatial variation in structural and functional features from unimodal sensory and motor regions to transmodal association areas.^2 However, few studies have investigated whether this organization of INTs remains stable across different cognitive states, comparing unconstrained resting-state conditions to naturalistic movie watching paradigms.^3 Such comparisons could reveal how the brain's temporal processing architecture may be modulated by unconstrained internal mentation or external sensory input.

Methods

We utilized high-resolution 7T fMRI data from the Human Connectome Project young adult dataset (HCP-YA, S1200 release) of healthy adults (N=170, mean age: 29.3 ± 3.3 years, 102 females) who performed resting-state and movie watching tasks. The data was first processed with the HCP minimal pre-processing pipelines. The vertex-wise time series were further detrended and z-scored. Finally, we averaged the time series within each parcel of the Schaefer-400 atlas (Fig. 1A). To account for signal stabilization, the first five volumes of each scan were discarded. For each parcel, we extracted the intrinsic neural timescale (INT) by calculating the area under the autocorrelation function curve, and averaged the INT for each parcel across all runs of the subject (Fig. 1B). Both for resting-state and movie watching conditions, we calculated group-level INTs for each cortical region. For each condition, we calculated the inter-regional covariance of INTs between the different cortical regions by correlating INT parcels across the cerebral cortex, resulting in a 400 by 400 covariance matrix (Fig. 1C). Then, we applied the nonlinear dimensionality reduction method of diffusion map embedding (sparsity = 0.9, alpha = 0.5) to reduce the dimensionality of the covariance matrix. This resulted in low-dimensional gradients, of which Gradient 1 (G1) and Gradient 2 (G2) explained most of the variance. We correlated the gradient loadings with the S-A axis of brain organization using spin permutation tests to understand whether these gradients align with the static functional-connectivity gradient separating primary sensory and motor functions from the default-mode network.^4

Results

Across the two different conditions, resting-state and movie watching, we observed different group-level patterns of INTs (Fig. 2A). During the resting-state condition, the highest INTs were primarily located in higher‑order association networks, including the default-mode, frontoparietal and ventral attention networks, peaking in parietal and precuneus/PCC regions. During movie watching, the highest INTs were mostly found in the visual network and temporal default-mode network. Comparing the individual-level distribution of mean INT across the 7 Yeo networks (Fig. 2B), we found a significant increase in INT from the rest to the movie condition in the visual network (p < 0.001, d > 0.8; large effect size) and a significant decrease in the ventral attention network (p < 0.001, d > 0.5; medium effect size). Smaller but significant changes (d > 0.2; small effect size) were observed, with increases in the dorsal attention and limbic networks, and decreases in the frontoparietal and default‑mode networks. The covariation gradients in INT followed known patterns of cortical organisation (Fig. 2C). Gradient 1 for covariation of INTs in resting-state condition displayed the S-A axis (r = -0.58, pspin < 0.001), while Gradient 2 spans between visual and somatomotor areas (Fig. 2D). In contrast, during movie watching, the covariation of INTs was primarily expressed in G1 between visual and somatomotor regions, while G2 showed the S-A axis pattern (r = -0.67, pspin < 0.001) (Fig. 2D). 

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that the dynamic measure of intrinsic neural timescales follows the known cortical organization patterns that have been observed in previous studies. During resting-state, most variance for spatial co-alteration of resting-state INTs is explained along the S-A axis, where regions that do not necessarily share similar INTs still vary together within their functional network, especially in the resting-state default-mode network. Interestingly, we observe a reordering of variance during movie watching, where covariation of INTs now happens primarily more coordinated between visual and somatomotor areas. Altogether, our findings highlight that intrinsic neural timescales follow cortical organization, without explicitly taking connectivity measures into account. We find a clear difference in covariation of INTs between resting-state and movie watching and are able to differentiate those fMRI conditions.
