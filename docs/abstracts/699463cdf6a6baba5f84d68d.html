Introduction

Simultaneous EEG-fMRI is a valuable tool for localizing epileptogenic networks by linking interictal epileptiform discharges (IEDs) detected in EEG with BOLD signal changes. Previous studies have demonstrated that EEG-fMRI can support localization of epileptic foci, particularly in patients with focal epilepsy and inconclusive conventional imaging, highlighting its potential role in presurgical evaluation [1, 2].  However, standard EEG-fMRI analysis relies on the generalized linear model (GLM) with a canonical hemodynamic response function (HRF), implicitly assuming a uniform and task-like neurovascular response across subjects and brain regions.

Our previous group-level work [3] demonstrated that optimizing HRF parameters for IED-driven EEG-fMRI analysis significantly increases detection sensitivity, cluster extent, and statistical strength across multiple HRF models, including physiologically informed Balloon model. While these findings establish methodological validity at the population level, their practical impact on single-subject inference and clinical interpretation remains underexplored.

Here, we present a single-case application of a dedicated HRF Optimization Toolbox (HOT) to demonstrate how subject-specific HRF modeling can alter EEG-fMRI inference at the individual level. Using data from a patient with drug-resistant focal epilepsy, we illustrate how HRF optimization affects localization of epileptogenic regions and can resolve anatomically implausible results produced by standard canonical HRF analysis. This work positions HRF optimization not as a purely statistical refinement, but as a decision-support tool for subject-level EEG-fMRI interpretation.

Methods

Patient and Data Acquisition

A 39-year-old female patient with drug-resistant focal epilepsy and hippocampal sclerosis underwent three sessions of simultaneous EEG-fMRI acquisition. EEG was recorded using a 64-channel Neuroscan SynampsRT system, while fMRI data were acquired on a 3T GE Discovery MR750w scanner (EPI, TR = 2.5 s, TE = 25 ms, voxel size 3 × 3 × 3 mm³). Structural T1-weighted images were obtained for anatomical reference.

EEG Processing and IED Identification

EEG data were corrected for MRI-related artifacts and filtered (1–30 Hz). An experienced neurologist manually identified the onset and duration of interictal epileptiform discharges. Seventeen IEDs detected across the sessions were used to construct stimulus functions for EEG-fMRI analysis.

fMRI Preprocessing and Standard Analysis

fMRI data were preprocessed in SPM12, including realignment, coregistration, normalization, and smoothing. A standard GLM analysis was performed using the canonical HRF to model BOLD responses associated with IEDs. Statistical parametric maps were thresholded using family-wise error (FWE) correction at p < 0.05.

HRF Optimization Procedure (HOT)

HRF optimization was performed using the HRF Optimization Toolbox (HOT), developed and validated in prior group-level studies [3]. Mean regional BOLD responses were extracted from statistically significant activation clusters obtained in the initial canonical HRF analysis. HRF parameters were optimized by minimizing the mean squared error between the observed regional BOLD response and the GLM-predicted response using constrained nonlinear optimization (Figure 1). The optimized HRF parameters were then reintroduced into the GLM, and the EEG-fMRI analysis was repeated.

Comparative Evaluation

Results obtained using the optimized HRF were compared qualitatively and anatomically to those derived from the standard canonical HRF, with emphasis on changes in activation location, anatomical plausibility, and consistency with clinical information.

Results

Standard EEG-fMRI analysis using the canonical HRF identified a statistically significant activation cluster localized primarily in the right lingual gyrus. This localization was considered anatomically questionable in the context of the patient’s clinical presentation and known epileptogenic profile.

Following HRF optimization with the HOT framework, reanalysis of the same EEG-fMRI data revealed a marked shift in activation patterns. The dominant activation cluster was relocated to the right precentral gyrus, a region more consistent with the patient’s seizure semiology, clinical assessment, and findings from complementary diagnostic modalities. The optimized analysis maintained FWE-corrected statistical significance while exhibiting improved alignment between neuroimaging results and clinical interpretation.

Importantly, this case demonstrated not only changes in cluster size or statistical strength, but a qualitative alteration in inferred epileptogenic localization. While previous group-level analyses showed that HRF optimization generally increases sensitivity and extent of detected activations, this single-subject example illustrates a distinct scenario in which HRF mis-modeling using the canonical response led to potential mislocalization of epileptogenic regions.

The optimization process resulted in an HRF shape characterized by an earlier peak and altered response dynamics relative to the canonical model, suggesting that the patient’s neurovascular response to IEDs deviated substantially from standard assumptions. Conclusion

This single-case study demonstrates that subject-specific optimization of the hemodynamic response function can fundamentally alter EEG-fMRI inference in focal epilepsy. While standard canonical HRF-based analyses may yield statistically significant results, they can nevertheless lead to anatomically implausible or clinically misleading localizations when neurovascular coupling deviates from assumed models.

By applying a validated HRF optimization framework as a post hoc modeling step, we show that EEG-fMRI results can be reinterpreted in a manner more consistent with clinical observations. In contrast to purely quantitative improvements reported at the group level, this case highlights a qualitative shift in inferred epileptogenic localization, underscoring the potential clinical relevance of HRF optimization at the single-subject level.

These findings support the view that HRF optimization should be considered not merely as a statistical enhancement, but as a decision-support tool for EEG-fMRI interpretation, particularly in challenging or ambiguous cases. The HOT framework enables practical implementation of subject-specific HRF modeling within standard SPM workflows and provides a bridge between advanced neurovascular modeling and clinically oriented neuroimaging.

Future work will focus on systematic evaluation of HRF optimization across larger patient cohorts to identify cases most likely to benefit from subject-specific modeling and to further establish its role in presurgical epilepsy evaluation.
