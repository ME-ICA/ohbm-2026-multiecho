Introduction

The human brain segments continuous sensory information into discrete units to navigate a dynamic world. This process is fundamental because it simplifies the continuous stream of experience into meaningful ‘chunks’, allowing the brain to efficiently memorize past experiences and to predict future events based on the current flow of chunks. By generating internal "event models," the brain maintains a stable grasp of the present. However, this stability must be balanced with flexibility; the brain must rapidly reset or update these representations whenever a spike in prediction error signals a shift in context. In the brain, this process occurs hierarchically: Lower-level sensory cortices process information at fine temporal scales, while the higher-order regions integrate information over longer timescales to form multimodal, abstract event models.(1) This nested hierarchy enables robust scene construction—the capacity to integrate sensory fragments into a coherent mental representation. The deficit in scene construction is particularly evident in Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD).(2) Indeed, the individuals with ASD often struggle to integrate sensory details into a coherent global context, a tendency theoretically described as Weak Central Coherence (WCC).(3) We propose that this fragmentation partly stems from impairment in neural event segmentation: a failure to register context shifts, resulting in resistance to the necessary transition to new processing states. We term this maladaptive resistance to state updates "Neural Inertia." Consequently, we hypothesize that this neural rigidity manifests as a specific temporal distortion: prolonged neural state duration. This temporal stagnation prevents the timely integration of dynamic sensory inputs into coherent scenes. We propose that this cascading failure drives the "insistence on sameness" observed in ASD, serving as a behavioral reflection of the brain's inability to discard outdated event models.

Methods

We analyzed fMRI data of 153 children (95 ASD, 58 TD) from the Healthy Brain Network (HBN) dataset, who watched a 10 minute clip of Despicable Me during the scan. The data were preprocessed using fMRIPrep and Ciftify, then resampled to 10k vertices. We defined spherical searchlights (radius: 9mm, step: 2 vertices) covering the entire cortex to perform hyperalignment, followed by HRF deconvolution to recover neural timing. Finally, the aligned data were group-averaged because the neural state segmentation method should rely on the high SNR data.(4) 

Next, we applied Greedy State Boundary Search (GSBS) to the hyperaligned, group-averaged movie fMRI data using a surface-based searchlight approach. This algorithm partitions the brain time series into multiple discrete segments, where each represents a stable neural state. The method is designed to detect transitions between distinct states. Specifically, GSBS iteratively searches for boundary locations that maximize the similarity between the original neural data and the time-averaged activity patterns within each event, while minimizing correlations between different states. We then computed the median brain state length within each searchlight as a measure of local processing timescales.

To statistically evaluate the group differences, we employed a non-parametric permutation test. We computed the observed difference in median state lengths between the ASD and TD groups for each searchlight. To generate a null distribution, we randomly shuffled the group labels 1,000 times and recalculated the differences at each iteration. The statistical significance (p) was derived by calculating the proportion of permuted differences that exceeded the observed difference. Given the exploratory nature of the whole-brain searchlight analysis, we report results at uncorrected thresholds to characterize the spatial extent of potential effects.

Complementarily, we employed  a bootstrap resampling procedure using the pool of identified state durations within each searchlight as input. This allowed us to estimate the 95% confidence intervals of the between-group differences in median state lengths and rigorously quantify the magnitude of deviations in processing timescales.

Results

To locally quantify group differences in brain timescales, we first computed the median state duration for each searchlight using boundaries identified via GSBS. 

Crucially, our analysis revealed a statistically robust and widespread amplification of neural inertia in the ASD group. We observed a significant global prolongation of state durations (mean difference = 0.25 s, 95% CI [0.19, 0.39], p < 0.0001), with 35.9% of cortical searchlights exhibiting significant group differences (permutation test, uncorrected p < 0.05).

Regionally, statistical mapping based on these permutation trends and bootstrap confidence intervals identified distinct deviations from the canonical hierarchy. In the TD group, sensory-input regions such as the piriform and peri-entorhinal cortices exhibited rapid state transitions with a median duration of approximately 2 seconds. In contrast, the ASD group showed significantly prolonged durations in the same regions, marked by consistent positive shifts in the statistical map, maintaining stable states for nearly 28 seconds.

Within the higher-order integration systems, the Default Mode Network (DMN) revealed a functional dissociation that suggests a maladaptive reorganization of the cortical hierarchy. We observed a breakdown in the Posterior Cingulate Cortex (PCC)—a hub for global context—where state durations were significantly fragmented (22 seconds) compared to the TD group (30 seconds). In sharp contrast, the Lateral Temporal Cortex (LTC) exhibited the most pronounced neural inertia, exhibiting the longest stability across the entire cortex at approximately 49 seconds. This divergence indicates that the autistic brain is topologically rewired: failing to sustain global context in the PCC while becoming "locked" into rigid representations in the LTC.

Conclusion

Our results offer a temporal perspective on the neurobiology of Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD), extending beyond traditional static functional connectivity approaches. We propose the concept of 'Neural Inertia' to describe the observed maladaptive dynamic, where neural states are maintained for excessively long periods, failing to synchronize with the rapid pace of a changing environment.

Critically, Neural Inertia manifests as a canonical distortion of the cortical hierarchy. We identified a severe "sensory bottleneck" in the piriform and peri-entorhinal cortices, where drastic processing delays prevent the encoding of transient cues. This sensory retention triggers a functional dissociation downstream: preventing the timely synthesis of global contexts (PCC fragmentation) while conversely entrenching the brain in over-stabilized internal representations (LTC inertia). 

This mechanistic cascade suggests that the autistic brain is functionally rewired to prioritize local details at the expense of global integration. Thus, 'Neural Inertia' provides a coherent framework for understanding why individuals with ASD may remain detached from dynamic environmental changes while exhibiting rigid cognitive behaviors.
