Introduction

The hippocampus is one of the brain’s metabolically demanding and vascularly sensitive structures, making it particularly vulnerable to fluctuations in oxygen availability1. Its core functions, novelty detection, pattern separation, and episodic memory formation, depend on precise neurovascular coupling (NVC) to rapidly adjust regional blood flow to metabolic demand2. Even brief hypoxic episodes could disrupt hippocampal perfusion, making this region a relevant model for studying adaptive versus maladaptive neurovascular responses.

Trained freedivers provide a unique human model for investigating such mechanisms: despite exposure to intermittent, acute hypoxia during breath-hold diving, they consistently show preserved structure and episodic memory performance3.

Intermittent hypoxia during apnea training may promote functional and vascular remodeling that enhances the hippocampus’s ability to operate efficiently under low-oxygen conditions. To examine these adaptations, we quantified hippocampal cerebral blood flow (CBF) using 3D pseudo-continuous arterial spin-labeling (3D pCASL) and modeled the resting-state hemodynamic response function (rs-HRF) from fMRI signals during apnea and recovery. This multimodal approach allows characterization of both static perfusion and dynamic neurovascular responsiveness.

We hypothesized that trained freedivers would exhibit CBF changes after apnea, while maintaining stable or adaptively regulated rs-HRF dynamics, reflecting strengthened NVC. We further predicted that these signatures would correlate with preserved episodic memory and that longitudinal apnea training would enhance these neurovascular responses.

Methods

Seventeen trained freedivers (FD; mean age: 47.9 ± 4.8 years) completed a protocol of four apnea blocks (2-min breath-hold + 1.5-min recovery). 3D pCASL scans were acquired pre- and post-apnea, at baseline, and after a seven-month structured apnea-training program3. Twenty age-matched controls (NC) completed the same imaging protocol without apnea.

Perfusion MRI data were processed with FSL BASIL (https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/docs/physiological/basil.html). Resting-state fMRI preprocessing followed the CONN toolbox pipeline (https://web.conn-toolbox.org/). rs-HRF parameters, response height (RH), and time-to-peak (TTP) were estimated using a blind deconvolution model (https://github.com/compneuro-da/rsHRF).

Hippocampal segmentation used the deep-learning HSF framework (https://github.com/clementpoiret/HSF) (Figure 1).

Episodic memory was assessed before and after training using the HippoPSPC task, which probes pattern separation and pattern completion3.

Group differences were evaluated using repeated-measures factorial ANOVA with post hoc multiple-comparison correction, and associations between CBF measures and memory performance were assessed using Spearman correlation analyses. Statistical significance was determined using corrected p-values < 0.05.

Results

Baseline hippocampal CBF did not differ between FD and NC (p = 0.28), indicating preserved normoxic perfusion. During apnea, FD maintained stable rs-HRF parameters, with preserved response height (RH, mean±SD) and time-to-peak (TTP, mean±SD) despite acute hypoxic stress (RH_NC = 0.27 ± 0.05 vs. RH_FD_pre = 0.23 ± 0.18 vs. RH_FD_post = 0.22 ± 0.01 all p NS; TTP_NC = 7.15 ± 0.62 s vs. TTP_FD_pre = 7.19 ± 1.34 s s. TTP_FD_post = 7.14 ± 1.37 s all p NS). This stability supports a stress-resistant neurovascular coupling profile.

At rest, trained FD showed faster rs-HRF recovery compared with their pre-training state, reflected by increased RH (RH_FD_pre = 0.29 ± 0.34 vs. RH_FD_post = 0.34 ± 0.11, all p NS) and modified TTP following training (TTP_FD_pre = 5.17 ± 1.48 s vs. TTP_FD_post = 7.28 ± 0.55 s; p=0.04), indicating enhanced neurovascular responsiveness.

Recovery was characterized by a controlled perfusion rebound that became more pronounced after training (p=0.0018), even as post-apnea CBF decreased more strongly. This pattern suggests enhanced vascular elasticity and autoregulatory efficiency developed through training. These findings align with physiological models describing oxygen debt, peripheral extraction, and delayed arterial reoxygenation during serial apneas4,5. Memory-perfusion correlations revealed that lower post-apnea CBF was associated with superior discrimination of highly similar items (r=-0.74, p=0.014), suggesting a more selective and metabolically efficient hippocampal processing mode. Episodic memory performance remained intact across sessions, consistent with an optimized neurovascular regime enabling effective functioning under reduced perfusion.

Conclusion

These preliminary multimodal results indicate that repeated apnea training induces measurable hippocampal neurovascular remodeling. FD show preserved or adaptively enhanced NVC during hypoxia and stronger perfusion recovery during reoxygenation, reflecting a resilient neurovascular phenotype. Longitudinal improvements demonstrate that these adaptations are trainable rather than static. Integrating ASL-based perfusion measures, participant-specific rs-HRF modeling, and memory performance may provide composite biomarkers of neurovascular resilience relevant for early-risk detection in cognitive and vascular brain disorders.
