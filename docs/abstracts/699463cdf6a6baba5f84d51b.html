Introduction

The hippocampal CA1 region in the medial temporal lobe (MTL) is a critical component of human brain circuits supporting cognitive performance, involved in learning, memory formation, and spatial navigation¹˒². The laminar organization of CA1 is three-layered, and previous work has demonstrated differential functional engagements of different cortical depths using 7-Tesla fMRI³. Additionally, 7-Tesla in vivo and ex vivo MRI studies have shown that the apical layers (hippocampal stratum radiatum, lacunosum, and moleculare; SRLM), which receive the majority of the entorhinal inputs via the perforant path, are particularly vulnerable to Alzheimer's Disease (AD)-related atrophy⁴. Even though CA1’s three-layered architecture has been described in ex vivo investigations, the degree to which this profile can be captured in vivo and its relation to individual differences in memory performance remain poorly characterized.
Consequently, we offer here an in vivo characterization of the laminar microstructural profile of human CA1 in younger, healthy adults using 7-Tesla MRI in combination with behavioral memory tests that capture different aspects of cognitive performance. Our aim is to explore the potential of 7-Tesla MRI to detect canonical, layer-specific myelin differences in this region in vivo and to examine their relation to behavior.

Methods

We analyzed 7T MRI and cognitive data from n = 30 healthy younger adults (26 ± 4 years). Cognitive tests: The cognitive assessment included a visuospatial object-location task (OLT), followed by a neuropsychological test battery: Rey–Osterrieth Complex Figure Test (ROCFT), Trail Making Test (TMT), Logical Memory from the Wechsler Memory Scale (LM), Digit Span (DS), Color and Word Interference Test (Stroop), and Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT). A composite memory score was computed by z-scoring and summing five memory-related measures (OLT delayed recall 1 & 2, ROCFT immediate & delayed recall, and Logical Memory delayed recall weighted ×2). MRI protocol: MRI data were acquired on a Siemens 7T MAGNETOM situated in Magdeburg, using a 32-channel head coil. The protocol included an MP2RAGE sequence at 0.5 mm isotropic resolution, a T2-weighted sequence with a region-of-interest approach (slab) focused on the medial temporal lobe (MTL) (0.44 × 0.44 × 1.1 mm), and a Time-of-Flight (ToF) angiography at 0.28 mm isotropic for vascular mapping. Analyses: qT1 and ToF images were registered to the T2 using ITK-SNAP’s manual registration tool. Hippocampal subfields were segmented using Automated Segmentation of Hippocampal Subfields (ASHS), followed by quality control and targeted manual correction of common segmentation errors. Binary CA1 masks were upsampled to 0.2 mm isotropic resolution, and qT1 images were cropped and resampled to match. Using LAYNII, CA1 was divided into 21 equidistant depth bins, which were then aggregated into three compartments: inner (apical), middle (pyramidal), and outer (basal). Layer-dependent mean qT1 values were extracted from each hemisphere for subsequent analyses. Vessel segmentations were derived from the ToF scans using the OMELETTE toolbox. Vessel distance maps (VDMs) were computed to quantify the distance from each CA1 voxel to the nearest arterial voxel. Statistical analyses: Statistical analyses were performed in Python. Linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) examined differences in qT1-derived myelination across layer compartments and hemispheres. Additional LMMs assessed whether vessel distance explained variance in layer-specific qT1 values, including VDM × Hemisphere interactions. Pairwise correlations between layer-specific qT1 values were FDR-corrected. LME models tested whether memory performance was associated with compartment-specific myelination (Memory ~ qT1 × Compartment + VDM + Hemisphere + age + sex). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

qT1 values differed significantly across CA1 depths and hemispheres. Across participants, the right CA1 showed lower qT1 values than the left CA1 (indicating higher myelination; LRT p < 0.0001; mean difference ≈ 230 ms), while depth profiles were consistent across hemispheres. Post-hoc tests confirmed a robust laminar gradient, with the outer compartment showing the highest myelination, followed by the inner and then the middle compartment (middle vs. outer: t = 4.99; inner vs. middle: t = −4.60; all p < 0.001). Age and sex showed no significant effects on microstructural variables. Vessel distance was not associated with CA1 myelination. Neither the main effect of vessel distance nor its interactions with layer compartment or hemisphere were significant (all p > 0.1), indicating that arterial proximity did not explain variability in depth-dependent qT1 values. Females showed higher memory performance than males (β = −2.57, p = 0.001), while age was not a significant predictor. Memory performance was selectively related to myelination in the left CA1. After FDR correction, higher myelination (lower qT1) across all three layer compartments of the left hemisphere was associated with better composite memory scores (r = −0.26 to −0.45, all FDR-corrected p ≤ 0.02). Exploratory analyses showed that these effects were primarily driven by performance during the first object–location retrieval, and to a lesser extent, the second. Regression models did not identify layer-specific qT1 values or vessel distance as significant predictors of memory scores (all p = 1.0).

Conclusion

Our findings provide an in vivo mesoscale characterization of human CA1 myeloarchitecture in younger adults, revealing a clear laminar gradient with the highest myelination in the inner and outer compartments and the lowest myelination in the middle compartment. This depth-dependent pattern aligns with known CA1 histology and demonstrates that ultra-high-field 7T MRI can detect signatures of laminar microstructure within hippocampal subfields in vivo. We observed a robust hemispheric asymmetry, with globally higher myelination in the right CA1, and no detectable influence of arterial distance on microstructural variation. Critically, individual differences in left CA1 myelination were associated with memory performance, particularly object–location retrieval. Although myelination did not significantly predict cognitive scores in full regression models, the consistent correlations of the left CA1 point to a pronounced role of CA1 for object location performance in younger adults. These findings support the view that the microstructural integrity of CA1 contributes to memory function in healthy young adults, potentially reflecting layer-specific involvement in retrieval pathways. Future work should determine how these laminar microstructural profiles change with aging and early AD pathology. Together, our results establish a methodological and conceptual foundation for studying depth-dependent hippocampal vulnerability and its cognitive relevance in aging and disease.
