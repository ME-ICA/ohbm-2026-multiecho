Introduction

Historically, the investigation of psychiatric disorders has focused on the unique symptomology and neurological changes that underlie distinct diagnoses. However, recent work has challenged this view following a greater understanding of the rates of comorbidity between DSM-based categorical diagnoses, a further understanding of the gradations and dimensionality across several forms of psychopathology, and more ^1. Following this thread, additional work has found large-scale profiles of cortical change that are present across disparate collections of disorders as well as general cortical progression through healthy development ^2. These investigations find consistent importance in the variation of grey matter in transmodal regions of cortex to explain a sizable proportion of overall variance in the data. Furthermore, factor analytical approaches have also added to this discussion by identifying a singular “p factor” present across a wide breadth of symptomology and classification ^4. Together, these results suggest there may be underlying, generalizable profiles that extend beyond the distinction of individual diagnoses towards a more holistic view.

Methods

In the current study, the potential specific and general components of diagnostically distinct disorders and their higher-order profiles was investigated further through a mega-analytical lens from substance use disorder datasets collated by the ENIGMA Addiction Working Group (n = 4,923, 1,630 males, 3,032 cases). We implemented a variety of methodologies including: univariate analyses for group comparisons, principal component analysis to investigate the potential presence of universal cortical profiles across our samples, multi-tracer neurotransmitter correlations to identify neurobiological systems within PET data that are captured by our cortical profiles, and elastic net classifiers to test the predictive utility of our cortical thickness data and its constituent partitions. All analyses utilized structural MRI data from ROIs in the Desikan-Killany atlas ^5.

Results

Our results identify unique patterns of difference in cortical thickness for each substance including global changes in alcohol, nicotine, and cocaine, more circumscribed alterations in cannabis use, and limited case/control differences in amphetamine-type stimulants. The effect size maps for these univariate group differences also strongly correlated with several distinct a-priori neurotransmitter systems such as acetylcholinergic and cannabinoid receptors for nicotine and cannabis respectively in addition to a wide variety of other density maps for each substance. However, despite the unique neurotransmitter correlations and group differences, principal components analysis revealed that the highest variance components across substances, even beyond the previously investigated first component, correlated very highly with principal component maps generated from only our healthy controls. This result replicates the consistent finding of an omnipresent first component but expands upon it further by finding further generalizable components. Our machine learning analyses revealed that there was predictive utility in classifying caseness using overall cortical thickness but found that even when either excluding the contribution of the pervasive, first component or by isolating the contribution of any individual component, several models still performed significantly above chance accuracy. However, the individual principal component models that were successful varied by substance lending more evidence towards a specificity present within the data despite the generalizable component maps.

Conclusion

Overall, we replicated the putative group differences across substance use disorders and found a universal generalizable profile within the widespread, first component of our data. However, additional consistent profiles across substances and unique predictive utility of these cortical profiles’ contributions to cortical thickness implicate a complex involvement of both specificity and generality underlying the complexities of disparate diagnoses and disorders.
