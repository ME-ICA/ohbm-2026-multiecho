Introduction

Understanding the relationship between brain function and behavior is essential for advancing our knowledge of human cognition. Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI) provides a powerful, noninvasive tool for studying these links in behaving humans. Naturalistic fMRI paradigms, such as movie-watching, provide an ecologically valid framework for capturing individual differences, enabling the comparison of functional responses to complex and dynamic stimuli.

Previous work has suggested that neural typicality, defined as the inter-subject correlations (ISC) between each participant’s regional fMRI time course and the group-average time course, is strongly associated with shared experiences and stimulus processing1. Neural typicality has also been linked to typicality in gaze patterns during movie viewing, an association maintained in individuals with Autism Spectrum Disorder despite lower overall typicality2. These findings suggest that neural typicality provides a sensitive measure for investigating brain-behavior relationships across diverse cognitive traits.

In the present study, we aimed to investigate the association between movie-watching-derived neural typicality and behavioral performance across distinct task domains, and to determine whether different brain regions exhibit robust, task-specific typicality-behavior relationships. Specifically, we examined typicality-behavior associations for two exemplar domains: an out-of-scanner motor task and an in-scanner working memory task. We hypothesized that neural typicality would be significantly correlated with behavioral performance across both domains and task settings, while the implicated brain regions would differ by domain and show strong correspondence with canonical functional networks and GLM-derived task activation maps relevant to each behavioral trait.

Methods

Movie-watching data from the Human Connectome Project (HCP) S1200 release were used to examine neural typicality-behavior associations. Participants with complete movie-watching data acquired on a 7T Siemens Magnetom scanner were included (N = 176). Additionally, data from all HCP participants who were not part of the movie cohort but had complete working memory (WM) and Motor task fMRI data (N = 706) were used to generate task-based GLM group activation maps. All data were minimally preprocessed by the HCP using the standard pipelines and provided in CIFTI grayordinate space comprising 91,282 vertices3. 

Each participant completed four movie-watching runs. Movies 1 and 3 consisted of independent short films from various sources, whereas Movies 2 and 4 contained excerpts from well-known Hollywood films. Each movie clip was trimmed to 808 fMRI volumes by excluding rest intervals. 

Neural typicality maps were computed separately for each movie using a leave-one-out inter-subject correlation approach: for each vertex, a participant’s fMRI time series was correlated with the mean time series of all other participants. Typicality maps were spatially smoothed with a 4 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel to match the smoothing level applied to the task fMRI data. Typicality maps were then correlated, vertex-wise across participants, with two exemplar behavioral traits provided by the HCP: (1) fine motor ability measured by the NIH Toolbox 9-Hole Pegboard Dexterity Test (out-of-scanner; http://www.nihtoolbox.org/), and (2) in-scanner working memory performance measured by N-back task accuracy4.  In both tasks, higher typicality-behavior correlations indicated that greater neural typicality at a given vertex was associated with higher performance scores.

For each movie and behavioral measure, Pearson correlation maps were thresholded at p < .05 (cluster-forming threshold) and binarized. A conjunction analysis was then performed, retaining only vertices significant in at least two movies. To correct for multiple comparisons, a permutation-based cluster-size correction (100 permutations) was applied: in each permutation, behavioral scores were shuffled, the full analysis was repeated, and the size of the largest null cluster was recorded. Clusters surviving the top 5% null threshold were considered significant typicality-behavior associations. Significant vertices were assigned to canonical large-scale functional networks using the Yeo et al. 7-network parcellation5, quantified as the fraction of significant vertices within each network. 

To assess whether typicality-behavior associations aligned with task-relevant brain regions, overlap was tested between the typicality maps and task-based fMRI activation maps. Dexterity scores were compared with the Motor Right Hand (RH) contrast, and N-back accuracy with the WM 2-back>0-back contrast. Group-level GLM activation maps were generated using vertex-wise one-sample t-tests, thresholded and binarized at p < .001 and Bonferroni corrected for 91,282 comaprisons. Spatial overlap between maps was quantified using the DICE coefficient, and overlapping vertices were similarly assigned to canonical functional networks.

Results

Neural typicality-behavior correlations were computed independently for each of the four movie-watching sessions. After applying a cluster-forming threshold of p < .05 and correcting for cluster size using a 100-permutation procedure, each movie yielded significant clusters associated with both behavioral traits: right-hand dexterity and N-back accuracy. A conjunction analysis retaining only vertices significant in at least two movies, followed by the same cluster-size correction, revealed robust and anatomically coherent clusters in task-relevant regions for both behavioral traits (Figure 1A). 

For the motor dexterity measure, significant clusters were predominantly located within three canonical functional networks. The majority of vertices mapped to the somatomotor network (39%), followed by the dorsal attention network (28%) and the default mode network (13%). This network distribution aligns with the fine motor control and attentional demands required in the Dexterity task.

In contrast, typicality-N-back accuracy correlations were distributed across multiple large-scale cognitive networks, consistent with the broad cognitive demands of working memory. Significant vertices were primarily mapped to the default mode network (29%), dorsal attention network (18%), visual network (18%), and frontoparietal control network (15%). 

To further assess whether these associations aligned with task-relevant neural responses, typicality-behavior maps were compared with task-evoked group-level GLM activation maps computed in an independent sample of 706 HCP participants. For dexterity, overlap with the Motor Right Hand contrast was modest (DICE = 0.11), which is expected given the substantial differences between the out-of-scanner dexterity task and simple fingertapping in-scanner task. However, the overlapping vertices were functionally meaningful: 52% fell within the somatomotor network and 26% within the dorsal attention network. 

In contrast, typicality-N-back correlations showed substantial overlap with the WM 2-back>0-back activation map (DICE = 0.40). Overlapping vertices were largely located in core cognitive networks that activate during WM tasks, including the frontoparietal control network (30%), dorsal attention network (27%), and default mode network (26%). 

Together, these findings demonstrate that neural typicality derived from movie-watching fMRI robustly reflects individual behavioral differences across both motor and cognitive domains.

Conclusion

Neural typicality maps derived from a naturalistic movie-watching paradigm demonstrated robust and behaviorally meaningful correlations across multiple cognitive domains. These results strongly suggest that neural typicality is sensitive to individual differences in functional brain organization that are directly relevant to behavior, making it a promising tool for studying brain-behavior associations. We further established its relevance by showing that typicality-behavior correlations consistently identified regions belonging to task-relevant canonical functional networks and overlapped with regions activated during task-based fMRI paradigms that rely on similar behavioral processes. Notably, this was accomplished without the need to introduce any explicit task during movie-watching scans, which were used to compute neural typicality.

Together, these findings support the view that neural typicality not only reflects shared processing of a stimulus but also captures meaningful inter-individual variation in brain function and its relation to behavior. This framework may be particularly useful for identifying subtypes within populations that appear homogeneous at the behavioral level. For example, clinical groups may present with similar behavioral impairments relative to controls, yet neural typicality could reveal distinct subgroups with different underlying neural patterns, potentially improving characterization of clinical profiles.

Naturalistic movie-watching-derived measures offer several practical advantages: they provide rich neural signals, are engaging for participants, require minimal compliance, and can be acquired efficiently compared to multiple task-based runs. The demonstrated associations between neural typicality and behavioral performance highlight its promise for applications in populations where task compliance is challenging, including psychiatric and neurological patients or young children. 

Overall, this work aligns with emerging perspectives emphasizing the importance of studying individual brain function in naturalistic contexts and modeling normative patterns of brain organization. Neural typicality may therefore represent a valuable and ecologically valid tool for advancing our understanding of human behavior and its neural foundations.
