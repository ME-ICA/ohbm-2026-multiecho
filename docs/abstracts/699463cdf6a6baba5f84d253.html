Introduction

While accumulating evidence shows that the human cerebellum supports a wide range of motor, cognitive and affective functions, the prevailing assumption that cerebellar signals are inaccessible to electroencephalography (EEG) and magnetoencephalography (MEG) has long limited progress in understanding cerebellar neural processing and computation. We have previously demonstrated, independently, that human cerebellar activity can be measured non-invasively using EEG1 and optically pumped magnetometer–based MEG (OP-MEG).2 Modelling work further suggests that EEG and MEG have complementary sensitivities3, therefore combined EEG/MEG recordings may enhance the detectability and spatial resolution of cerebellar activity. However, empirical evidence is lacking and modelling findings remain inconsistent regarding whether EEG and MEG differ in their sensitivity to cerebellar sources that is located deeper in the cranium.3,4 Here, we directly compare EEG and OP-MEG responses using an air-conducted sound paradigm that reliably activates the vestibulo-cerebellum on the ventral surface of the cerebellum to address these questions.

Methods

We delivered air-conducted (AC) 2-ms, 500-Hz single-cycle tone pips at 133 dB peak sound pressure level (pk SPL) to each ear, with 200 repetitions per side, using insert earphones. This stimulus is a well-established activator of the vestibulo-cerebellar pathway.5 A 2-ms, 5-kHz pip delivered at the same sound pressure level and with the same number of trials was used as an auditory control. The same protocol was administered during EEG and subsequently during OP-MEG recording on two separate days. EEG was recorded at 2048 Hz using a 96-channel BioSemi Active2 system with a custom 10% cerebellar-extension montage (EASYCAP GmbH). OP-MEG data were acquired at 1000 Hz using a whole-head, 50-sensor dual-axial system (FieldLine, Inc.), providing comparable cerebellar coverage. Each OPM sensor measures neuromagnetic fields in two directions—a radial component perpendicular to the scalp and a tangential component parallel to the scalp—resulting in a total of 100 channels. EEG data were analysed using EEGLAB and OP-MEG data using SPM25. Data were downsampled (EEG: 512 Hz; OP-MEG: 500 Hz), band-pass filtered (EEG: 0.1–200 Hz; OP-MEG: 3–160 Hz), cleaned of artefacts (EEG: independent component analysis; OP-MEG: homogeneous field correction), epoched from −50 to 300 ms and averaged. 

Results

We observed an early positive deflection at ~10 ms and a second negative deflection at ~16 ms post-pip in contralateral cerebellar EEG electrodes (N = 2; Fig. B), consistent with our previous work, which source-localised these peaks to the cerebellum. OP-MEG showed a clear 16-ms evoked peak in both radial and tangential sensor components (N = 1, Fig.D). The topography revealed the 16-ms peak at comparable spatial locations in EEG and OP-MEG, but with orthogonal orientations (Fig.C & D respectively), suggesting a shared neural generator.

Conclusion

This is the first head-to-head comparison of cerebellar EEG and MEG. Our pilot data showed the following: First, we identified convergent early evoked responses at identical latency and with orthogonal potential/field distributions in EEG and MEG, consistent with both modalities detecting cerebellar activity induced by air-conducted sound stimuli. Second, MEG detection of radial components, which would be tangential to the head surface in EEG and therefore less detectable3, provides empirical evidence that combined EEG–MEG measurements yield richer spatial information and potentially enhance signal detectability, in line with previous modelling predictions. Finally, this is also the first empirical demonstration that OP-MEG can detect vestibulo-cerebellar signals, underscoring its potential for investigating deeper cerebellar activity.
