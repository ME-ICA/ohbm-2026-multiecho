Introduction

Gliomas are common primary brain tumors that originate from glial cells and typically infiltrate surrounding tissue. Although recent work shows that glioma cells can integrate within their surrounding neuronal environment, the impact of these tumor–neuron interactions on both local circuits and distributed brain networks is still poorly understood1. Equally unclear is how the removal of the tumor may alter patterns of connectivity across the brain.

In this study, we ask how tumors affect functional organization of the hemisphere they inhabit compared to the contralateral hemisphere. We focus on regions associated with executive functions (e.g., attention, cognitive flexibility, task-switching) and examine the frontoparietal network (FPN), which supports these higher-order cognitive processes. We developed a cross-modality pipeline for integrating data from two neuroimaging modalities: electrocorticography (ECoG) and resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI). ECoG captures local meso-scale neural activity from the cortical surface with uniquely high spatiotemporal resolution, while rs-fMRI is used to characterize functional connectivity at the whole-brain scale. In prior work, we demonstrated that tumor-infiltrated cortex can remain engaged in large-scale cognitive circuits2. Here, we extend this work by examining connectivity patterns in structurally healthy-appearing cortex and quantifying hemispheric differences in connectivity. We incorporate rs-fMRI scans collected before surgery for tumor removal and three months post-operatively to further investigate how functional networks spontaneously re-organize following surgery. Together, this approach aims to advance the understanding of how gliomas reshape distributed brain networks and how these alterations may inform individualized mapping and surgical planning.

Methods

Seventeen patients with radiologically suspected diffuse low-grade glioma scheduled for awake craniotomy were recruited. In this longitudinal study, resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) scans were collected from patients before and three months after surgery. Eleven of the patients also underwent intraoperative electrocorticography (ECoG). rs-fMRI data were acquired on a Siemens Magnetom Prisma-fit 3T scanner (TR = 1060 ms, TE = 30 ms, 2 mm isotropic resolution, FOV = 192 × 192 mm², acquisition time: 9 min 10 s). Preprocessing included Independent Component Analysis to remove noise, slice-timing correction, bias-field correction, rigid-body motion correction, grand-mean scaling, and spatial smoothing (5 mm FWHM). Electrode coordinates were co-registered to each patient’s pre-operative T1-weighted MRI.

During awake surgery, ECoG was recorded from strip electrodes placed on the frontal cortex of the tumor hemisphere. Patients were asked to perform two counting tasks with increased task demand, designed to probe executive control regions. Neural activity was recorded using ECoG with 1-3 strips comprised of 4 electrodes each, sampled at 10 KHz. The recorded data underwent signal pre-processing, including re-referencing, down-sampling and noise filtering. Following pre-processing, high gamma power (70-250 Hz, associated with neural activity) from the low and high demand tasks were contrasted to yield the percent signal change (PSC) between the two tasks. These ECoG data have been used in our previous study, where we showed that this measurement identified electrodes whose activity increased with cognitive demand (i.e., task-responsive), reflecting engagement of the large-scale frontoparietal network (FPN), associated with executive control3. 

To characterize large-scale functional connectivity, seed-based correlation maps were computed on the rs-fMRI data using two complementary sets of seeds. First, for each ECoG electrode, a 2.5-mm radius sphere was centered on the MRI-derived coordinate to estimate connectivity patterns from FPN-related cortical sites. Electrodes were typically positioned at a distance from the tumor boundary on healthy-appearing cortex (N=53, average distance 28mm ± 16mm). This analysis was conducted on both rs-fMRI data before and after surgery. Second, the pre-surgery rs-fMRI were used with the tumor volume itself as a seed region, along with peritumoral expansions defined as 5-mm and 10-mm beyond the tumor boundary. These tumoral and peritumoral seeds enabled assessment of connectivity directly from tumor-infiltrated and adjacent tissue, providing a complementary analysis to the electrode-based analysis.

For all seed types, the mean rs-fMRI time series was extracted and correlated with all other brain voxels to generate whole-brain connectivity maps (Fig. 1). A Fisher’s R to Z transformation was performed on the resulting map, which was then smoothed at 5-mm full-width half maximum. A further Z-score normalization was performed within each map to compare across patients. Connectivity with canonical resting-state networks4 was quantified as the median correlation within each network. Linear mixed-effects models were used to assess hemispheric differences in network connectivity.

Results

ECoG results showed that electrodes positioned on healthy-appearing cortex exhibited task-related increases in high-gamma power, indicating engagement in executive control processes3. A large proportion of electrodes (62%; 33/53) were located within regions corresponding to the frontoparietal network (FPN), the network that supports executive control tasks.

Pre-operative rs-fMRI network connectivity was evaluated separately for each hemisphere. A clear laterality effect was observed: connectivity with the FPN, but not with other networks, was larger for the tumor hemisphere compared to the contralateral hemisphere (FPN: p < 0.0002; All other networks: p > 0.13, Fig. 2A). This effect was most pronounced for electrodes that were task-responsive or located within FPN-associated regions, suggesting tumor-related alterations in the executive-control network organization. The prominence of this effect specifically for the FPN may stem from the fact that most electrodes were positioned within FPN regions, due to the study’s focus on executive control. Importantly, three months after surgery, this hemispheric difference was no longer present (p = 0.46; Fig. 2B). Hemispheric difference in connectivity with the FPN was larger before surgery compared to 3 months after the surgery (p < 0.023). These results indicate postoperative connectivity reorganization.

We further asked whether the observed preoperative laterality effect would also appear in regions closer to the tumor. We found that the pre-operative asymmetry in FPN connectivity was distance-dependent: for tumor and peritumor seeds, connectivity with the two hemispheres was similar (Tumor: p > 0.36, Peritumor 5-mm: p > 0.19, Peritumor 10-mm: p > 0.07), in contrast to the asymmetry that was observed for electrodes located farther from the tumor. These findings suggest that tumor-related network disruptions extend beyond the immediate lesion and affect distributed cortical regions at a distance.

Conclusion

Our results demonstrate that gliomas alter the organization of whole-brain networks and exert widespread effects on functional brain architecture. These findings challenge the common assumption that tumor-related dysfunction is limited to tissue immediately surrounding the lesion and add to emerging similar evidence (e.g., ⁵). The preoperative asymmetry in connectivity we observed was absent three months after surgery, suggesting spontaneous network reorganization. This asymmetry may reflect either compensatory processes or abnormal disruption that develops as the tumor grows.

This study highlights the value of integrating ECoG and fMRI to map functional networks in glioma patients and to advance understanding of how tumors and their removal remodel brain organization. Further work is needed to illuminate how glioma-related network changes relate to cognitive outcomes and how tumor characteristics such as location and molecular properties contribute to these alterations. Ultimately, our approach and findings may promote the development of patient-tailored treatment and rehabilitation strategies.
