Introduction

From an evolutionary perspective, social anxiety can be conceptualized as an adaptive mechanism that emerged in hierarchical societies to monitor and protect social status. Humans are thought to possess a sociometer that continuously tracks one’s relative social standing within a group (Gilbert, 2006). When this sociometer detects cues indicating a potential decrease in social status, social anxiety is triggered, motivating behaviors aimed at preventing status loss or restoring social standing. Given that reductions in social status can lead to substantial and enduring adverse consequences for access to resources, social inclusion, and well-being, heightened sensitivity to status-related cues may confer evolutionary advantages (Gilboa-Schechtman et al., 2014; Azoulay and Gilboa-Schechtman, 2022). Among such cues, voice pitch represents a particularly salient and well-established indicator of social dominance and rank (Aung and Puts, 2020). Therefore, individuals with elevated social anxiety are hypothesized to be especially attuned to deviations in vocal pitch, reflecting increased vigilance toward signals of others’ social status. It also remains unclear whether this heightened sensitivity operates at an early, automatic stage of auditory processing. To address this gap, the present study uses mismatch negativity (MMN), an event-related potential that indexes pre-attentive auditory deviance detection, to examine whether online monitoring of pitch deviations differs as a function of social anxiety levels in the general population.

Methods

Sixty young adults (age range: 18-30 years old) took part in the study, comprising 30 females (13 with low social anxiety and 17 with high social anxiety) and 30 males (14 with low social anxiety and 16 with high social anxiety). Participants were classified into high and low social anxiety groups according to a predefined dichotomization criterion. Each participant completed four MMN recording sessions, with the order counterbalanced across participants. In each session, a standard auditory stimulus was presented repeatedly, with a deviant stimulus that differed from the standard only in pitch height occurring infrequently. The four standard stimuli included (1) a neutral vocal sound, consisting of a male Chinese speaker producing /wei˥/ in a TV newsreader style; (2) an angry vocal sound, consisting of the same speaker producing /wei˥˩/ with clearly angry prosody; and (3, 4) non-vocal counterparts of the two vocal stimuli. The non-vocal stimuli were synthesized using cosine waves that precisely replicated the temporal pitch contours of their corresponding vocal sounds. Standard and deviant stimuli differed systematically in pitch height, with deviants presented at a lower pitch than standards (~40 Hz). Auditory stimuli were delivered binaurally in a passive oddball paradigm, in which infrequent pitch deviants were embedded within a stream of frequent standard stimuli. Stimulus duration, presentation rate, and sound intensity were kept constant across conditions and chosen in accordance with established MMN protocols. All sounds were embedded in a continuous weak background noise (~10 dBA weaker) that was amplitude-modulated at 40 Hz. Throughout the recordings, participants were instructed to direct their attention to a silent movie (predominantly Ratatouille, Walt Disney, 2007) presented on an iPad placed in front of them and to ignore the auditory stimuli. MMN was obtained by subtracting event-related responses to standard stimuli from those to deviant stimuli; importantly, each deviant response was matched to a standard response that was preceded by the same number of standards since the previous deviant, thereby ensuring comparable sensory contexts.

Results

Irrespective of sound type, sex, or hemisphere, the peak latency of the MMN component occurred significantly earlier in socially anxious participants compared with their low social anxiety counterparts. In addition, regardless of sound type or sex, socially anxious participants exhibited significantly stronger MMN amplitudes than non-anxious participants within the 75 to 150 ms post-stimulus time window. This enhancement was observed exclusively in the right hemisphere, with no corresponding group differences detected in the left hemisphere. No interaction effects involving sound type or sex were observed for either MMN amplitude or latency.

The present findings provide neurophysiological evidence that social anxiety is associated with altered early pitch deviance processing, consistent with the proposed evolutionary account of social anxiety as a status-monitoring adaptation. Building on the notion that humans possess a sociometer that continuously tracks potential threats to social standing, the enhanced and earlier MMN observed in socially anxious individuals suggests heightened sensitivity at a pre-attentive stage of pitch processing. Notably, this effect was independent of sound type, indicating that socially anxious individuals show a generalized enhancement in pitch deviance detection rather than a selective response to explicitly social or emotional vocal cues. Such generalized sensitivity may reflect an evolutionarily advantageous strategy in hierarchical societies, where rapid detection of subtle acoustic cues, such as pitch changes that signal dominance or status, could facilitate early threat detection and adaptive behavioral responses. The right-hemisphere specificity of the MMN amplitude enhancement further aligns with the dominant role of the right hemisphere in processing pitch-relevant auditory information. Together, these findings suggest that social anxiety is associated with a hyper-responsive and temporally accelerated pitch change-monitoring system, supporting the view that social anxiety reflects heightened vigilance to potential social status threats operating at an automatic, pre-attentive level.

Conclusion

In summary, the present study demonstrates that social anxiety is associated with enhanced and temporally accelerated pre-attentive detection of pitch deviations, as indexed by mismatch negativity. Socially anxious young adults exhibited stronger right-lateralized MMN amplitudes and earlier MMN peak latencies across sound types, indicating a generalized enhancement in early pitch change monitoring. These findings support an evolutionary account of social anxiety as an adaptive system for monitoring potential threats to social status, in which heightened sensitivity to socially relevant acoustic cues operates at an automatic, pre-attentive level. By revealing alterations in early sensory prediction mechanisms rather than later, attention-dependent processes, this study extends current understanding of social anxiety beyond evaluative cognition and emotion regulation, highlighting fundamental differences in sensory change detection. Together, these results suggest that social anxiety may be characterized by a hyper-responsive pitch monitoring system that facilitates rapid detection of environmental deviations, potentially conferring adaptive advantages in social contexts while also contributing to vulnerability under modern social demands.
