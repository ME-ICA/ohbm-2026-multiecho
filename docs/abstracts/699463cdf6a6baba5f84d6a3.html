Introduction

Essential tremor (ET) is a chronic movement disorder characterized primarily by bilateral upper limb action tremor, with prevalence increasing with age and significantly impairing quality of life 1. Current neurobiological models increasingly conceptualize ET as a disorder involving distributed neural network alterations 2, 3. The integration of diffusion tensor imaging and resting-state functional MRI offers a powerful approach to probe both structural (SC) and functional connectivity (FC) in the brain. Importantly, SC and FC are intrinsically interrelated, which provides a more comprehensive understanding of brain organization than either modality alone 4. Prior work highlight that SC-FC coupling varies regionally across the brain, showing stronger integration in unimodal sensory and motor regions and greater decoupling in transmodal associative cortices 5. However, whether SC-FC coupling is systematically altered in ET remains unexplored. This study aims to investigate regional SC-FC coupling changes in ET, with the goal of identifying a convergent network underlying tremor symptomatology and uncovering its potential neurobiological basis.

Methods

Participants

A total of 160 patients with ET were recruited for this study. In addition, 175 healthy controls (HC) participants were recruited from the local community through advertisements. The HC group was matched to the ET group in terms of sex, age, and years of education. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital of Sichuan University and was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Image Processing and Network Construction

T1-weighted images, rs-fMRI, and DWI data were acquired using a 3.0 T MRI scanner (Tim Trio, Siemens Healthineers). DWI data were preprocessed and analyzed using MRtrix3, including denoising, eddy-current and motion correction, and bias-field correction. Whole-brain probabilistic tractography was performed following the official pipeline, and a 116 × 116 structural connectivity matrix was constructed based on the AAL atlas. Resting-state fMRI data were preprocessed using DPABI and SPM12. A standardized preprocessing procedure was applied. Subsequently, Pearson’s correlation coefficients were computed between the time series of all pairs of brain regions, followed by Fisher’s z-transformation to generate a 116 × 116 functional network.

Regional SC–FC Coupling

For each participant, regional SC–FC coupling was calculated as the Pearson correlation between each column of the SC matrix and the corresponding column of the FC matrix, excluding self-connections and restricting the analysis to nonzero SC edges. All nonzero SC values were normalized to follow a Gaussian distribution prior to correlation analysis. This procedure yielded 116 regional SC–FC coupling values, representing the degree of structure–function correspondence for each brain region.

Statistical Analyses

Demographic and clinical variables were analyzed using SPSS 26.0. Two-sample t-tests were applied to compare age and years of education, and χ² tests were used to compare sex distribution between groups. Group differences in regional SC–FC coupling were assessed using two-sample t-tests, with age, sex, and years of education included as covariates. Multiple comparisons were corrected using the false discovery rate (FDR) method (p < 0.05).

Associations between Clinical Scores, Gene Expression, and Neurotransmitters

Pearson’s correlation analyses were conducted to examine the associations between regions showing significant group differences and clinical characteristics, including Fahn–Tolosa–Marin tremor rating scale (TRS), MMSE scores, age at onset, and disease duration. Age, sex, and years of education were included as covariates, and FDR correction was applied to account for multiple comparisons (p < 0.05).

Gene expression data from six postmortem donors were obtained from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA). After standard preprocessing, a final gene expression matrix was generated. Partial least squares (PLS) correlation analysis was then performed to identify associations between gene expression patterns and SC–FC alterations. The first PLS component (PLS1), representing the dominant spatial covariation between gene expression and SC–FC changes, was retained for further analysis. Enrichment analyses were subsequently conducted to identify relevant biological pathways. 

To investigate the chemoarchitectonic organization underlying SC–FC alterations, spatial correlation analyses were performed using six PET-derived neurotransmitter transporter and receptor maps obtained from JuSpace (version 2.0). These included serotonin, noradrenaline transporter (NAT), dopamine receptors (D1 and D2) and dopamine transporter (DAT), metabotropic glutamate receptor 5 (mGluR5), and gamma-aminobutyric acid type A (GABAA) receptor. Statistical significance was assessed using permutation testing with 10,000 iterations, and results were corrected for multiple comparisons using FDR (p < 0.05).

Results

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics of the Sample

A total of 160 patients with ET (93 females; mean age = 49.7 ± 14.7 years) and 175 HC participants (102 females; mean age = 49.1 ± 11.8 years) were included in the analyses. There were no significant differences between the ET and HC groups in sex distribution (p = 0.915), age (p = 0.707), or years of education (p = 0.439).

Abnormalities of Regional SC-FC Coupling 

Compared with the HC group, patients with ET exhibited significantly altered regional SC–FC coupling in 16 regions of interest. Specifically, increased SC–FC coupling in the ET group was observed in regions primarily involving the left Rolandic operculum, insula, lingual gyrus, fusiform gyrus, postcentral gyrus, and superior temporal gyrus, as well as subcortical and cerebellar regions including the right caudate nucleus and the cerebellum. These regions are mainly associated with sensorimotor processing, sensory integration, and visual processing. In contrast, decreased SC–FC coupling was detected in several cortical regions, including the left superior frontal gyrus, right postcentral gyrus, and right superior temporal gyrus. These regions are predominantly located in frontal and temporal cortices and partially overlap with the default mode and sensorimotor networks.

Correlations Between SC–FC Coupling, Clinical Variables, and Neurotransmitter Profiles

No significant correlations between regional SC–FC coupling alterations and clinical variables were found. 

Spatial correlation analyses revealed that between-group differences in SC–FC coupling were significantly negatively correlated with four neurotransmitter receptor profiles, including 5-HT2a (r = −0.23, p = 0.049), 5-HT6 (r = −0.25, p = 0.036), D2R ( r = −0.29, p = 0.036), and GABAA receptor (r = −0.27, p = 0.036).

Gene Expression Profiles Related to Regional SC-FC Coupling Alterations

We found that the PLS1 scores were spatially correlated with the t value of SC-FC coupling differences. Based on the ranked normalized weights of PLS1, a total of 1,804 genes were identified as being associated with regional SC–FC coupling alterations in patients with ET. GO enrichment analysis indicated that these genes were primarily enriched in pathways related to synaptic structure, immune and inflammatory responses, and neuronal development.

Conclusion

Our study provides a comprehensive network-level characterization of the neural substrates underlying ET, moving beyond singular structural or functional connectivity approaches. We identified a distinct pattern of abnormal SC-FC coupling characterized by both increases and decreases across specific brain regions. Notably, altered coupling was predominantly observed in cerebellar areas, subcotical, sensorimotor regions, and higher-order cortical areas, aligning with the well-established circuit pathology in ET. Concurrently, increased coupling in limited regions may reflect potential compensatory mechanisms or pathological hyperactivity in parallel networks. This bidirectional disruption in SC-FC coupling, particularly affecting neural circuit integrity, likely underlies both core motor symptoms and diverse non-motor manifestations in ET. The spatial distribution of these coupling abnormalities highlights complex network reorganization in ET pathophysiology. Given the significant heritability of ET, we found that the implicated genes are enriched in biological processes related to synaptic structure, neuronal development, and immune/neuroinflammatory pathways. These interconnected mechanisms support fundamental neural functions, which may suggest that disruptions in these systems may contribute to cognitive and affective symptoms in ET.

Our study comprehensively identified SC-FC coupling abnormalities in ET patients associated with specific neurotransmitter systems and transcriptional patterns. These alterations predominantly involved the cerebellum, subcortical, cortex circuit, potentially contributing to both primary motor dysfunction and higher-order cognitive impairment underlying the diverse motor and non-motor symptoms in ET. We further elucidated the molecular underpinnings of macroscale network disruptions, providing a multiscale perspective on ET pathophysiology. With MRgFUS showing significant clinical potential, the precise localization of neural targets is essential for developing early intervention strategies in ET.
