Introduction

The integrated analysis of neuro-imaging genetics data is crucial for understanding psychiatric disorders such as Major Depressive Disorder (MDD), Attention-Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) and Bipolar disorder (BP). This field enables the study of early neurodevelopment, as captured through imaging, across diverse genetic and environmental contexts. Imaging genetic datasets typically include multi-modal imaging, tables of behavioral data, and genotyping data, with millions of Single Nucleotide Polymorphism (SNP) variables.

To study the links between genetic and imaging data, bi-multivariate correlation tools have been developed.1 A practical implementation typically involves selecting a few hundred GWAS-identified SNPs to summarize the genotyping data block. However this approach limits the ability to consider the underlying genetic correlation structure of the genetic data, including linkage disequilibrium. It is therefore more difficult to assess the influence of genetics and environmental factors in images acquired from patients suffering from psychiatric disorders. 

We propose to use Polygenic Risk Scores (PRS) to summarize the genotyping variables. The PRS for psychiatric disorders, provides a quantitative measure of genetic predisposition through weighted SNP variants for each individual. Studies show that combining PRS from different databases improves the accuracy of phenotype prediction.2 

Building on Fernandez study 3, we conduct in ABCD study a multivariate association study between imaging data and genotyping data using psychiatric PRS scores based on the Polygenic Score Catalog.4 Using Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA), we identify a strong relationship between imaging and genetics. In contrast to previous work, we show a strong correlation relying solely on psychiatric PRS, without incorporating clinical data.

Methods

Data were obtained from the ABCD Study R6.5 We collected the age, sex of each participant. In contrast to the approach of 3, we chose not to include scores from psychiatric assessment with the PRS set to avoid introducing bias that could compromise the validity of potential clinical applications.

MRI data

We used preprocessed T1-weighted structural MRI data with FreeSurfer version 7.1.1, and preprocessed diffusion MRI data. We focused on mean Cortical Thickness (CT) and Surface Area (SA) values in the 148 Regions Of Interest (ROIs) derived from the Destrieux atlas. We also considered Diffusion Tensor Imaging (DTI) derived measures within ROIs defined by the probabilistic AtlasTrack atlas, including Fractional Anisotropy (DTI-FA), Mean Diffusivity (DTI-MD), Longitudinal Diffusivity (DTI-LD), Transverse Diffusivity (DTI-TD), and total volume of fiber tracks (DTI-Vol). Finally, we considered measures derived from the Restriction Spectrum Imaging (RSI) tissue microstructure model, focusing on normalized directional restricted diffusion (RSI-Res) and normalized directional hindered diffusion (RSI-Hnd).

We selected the participants of the first session ses-00A who passed the ABCD Study's quality control for both structural and diffusion MRI acquisitions (mr_y_qc__incl__dmri_indicator &  mr_y_qc__incl__smri__t1_indicator).

We simplified the MRI preprocessing steps from 3 and the imaging data were only residualized for age, sex, scanner type and the 20 principal components (PCs) reflecting genotype-based ancestry as released by the ABCD Study.

Genetic data

The PRS weights were extracted from the PGS Catalog4 and risk scores were derived using PGSCalc on imputed genotyping data from the ABCD Study. We derived them only in the unrelated participants who self-reported to have White European ancestry. We selected a set of PRS weights related to 20 different psychiatric disorders (the PSY set).

The PSY set was constructed using a multi-term query to account for the heterogeneity of  psychiatric PRS annotations in the PGSCatalog which use the MONDO and EFO ontology. Specifically, we included PRSs associated with MONDO_0002009 (MDD), MONDO_0004985 (BP), EFO_0003888 (ADHD).

The imaging-based and genotype-based filtering yield 3605 participants.

Canonical correlation analysis

The CCA is a statistical framework for analysing the associations between two blocks of variables, here the PRSs and the imaging data. CCA estimates pairs of loadings applied to each block in order to construct two latent variables that are maximally correlated.

The latent variable space capturing shared information between datasets can be refined by applying a deflation mechanism, which generates subsequent sets of loadings to derive correlated latent variables.

We applied scikit-learn CCA to ROI-based imaging features spanning all modalities, alongside preprocessed PSY PRSs. Canonical correlation coefficients r were calculated on the pair of latents obtained before deflation (Mode 1 - M1) and after deflation (Mode 2 - M2).

Results

The CCA analysis conducted in this study (see Methods, Canonical correlation analysis) reveals an important shared variance between multi-modal imaging and the PSY set of PRSs (see fig CCA-derived results). The first row shows canonical Mode 1, and the second row shows Mode 2. Left plots illustrate feature loadings, with each MRI modality color-coded. Right plots depict PRS loadings. The estimated canonical correlation coefficients r is reported for each mode with r=0.410 for Mode 1 and r=0.397 for Mode 2, which indicates strong associations.

Regarding the imaging bloc, structural imaging modalities (CT and SA) show a wide range of loadings on both modes, suggesting a stronger association with PSY, whereas diffusion imaging modalities exhibit loadings that are predominantly unidirectional (either mostly positive or mostly negative), rather than spanning the full range.

Regarding the genetic bloc, the loadings distribution of the PRS shows that, for Mode 1, the two ADHD PRSs (002746Lahey and 003753Sato) are close together. The bipolarity PRS (002786Gui) falls near the middle of the distribution. The loadings of the remaining PRSs, each representing the MDD risk score, range from -0.6 to 0.2, demonstrating that PRSs computed for a common risk can exhibit relatively different associations with imaging data. The second mode of the CCA shows that the higher the value for the bipolar PRS, the lower the value for the ADHD PRSs.

Conclusion

Building on the work of 3, we investigated the associations between brain imaging and PRS of various psychiatric disorders, using novel derivation methods in ABCD study. We decided not to use the educational attainment PRS employed by 3, because this PRS does not reflect psychiatric risk, and may instead convey spurious information related to socio-environmental conditions. In addition, in 3, the authors mitigated the non-validated status of PRS available at that time by considering multiple scores per risk (including SNPs passing different significance thresholds). By contrast, using PGScalc with validated scores allows calculation of a single PRS per phenotype. This approach offers a substantially improved perspective on reproducibility. 

With respect to imaging, we compared the approach of 3 which used a reduced-dimension representation of regional brain features, to our method, which utilizes all regional features at the level of regions of interest. Our results show that retaining the full set of ROI features yields strong canonical correlation, suggesting that it is relevant to keep all the data to study the relationship between imaging and genetics with canonical correlation analysis.

In the ABCD cohort, we demonstrated a robust canonical correlation between regional brain imaging features and psychiatric polygenic risk scores derived from genotyping data. We showed that a high correlation can be achieved using only psychopathology-related PRS, without including clinical data or socio-environmental PRS. We also note the challenges posed by a cohort comprising subjects of mixed ancestry.
