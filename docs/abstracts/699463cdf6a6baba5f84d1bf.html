Introduction

Biological theories of sexual orientation in individuals assigned male at birth (AMAB) highlight the potential role of genetic, immunological, and endocrine mechanisms.1 Because these influences cannot be directly observed, researchers use putative developmental markers that index these pathways. Familial clustering of male nonheterosexuality is considered a marker of genetic mechanisms. Later fraternal birth order (FBO) is a correlate of male sexual orientation and is thought to reflect maternal immune responsivity to Y-linked antigens, influencing male fetal neurodevelopment. The ratio of the second-to-fourth digit (2D:4D) is a putative proxy for prenatal androgen action, with higher ratios associated with lower androgen exposure. Handedness has been associated with both genetic variation and prenatal hormonal influences, as well as cerebral lateralization. Previous research demonstrates that these markers delineate subgroups of same-sex attracted men, suggesting distinct biodevelopmental pathways.2,3 Yet, no work has examined whether such biomarker profiles correspond with subgroup differences in brain structure. Cortical thickness (CT) and surface area (SA) are sex-differentiated anatomical features and may provide insight into neurodevelopmental pathways associated with sexual orientation and gender diversity.4 Understanding whether these subgroups differ in brain structure is crucial for clarifying how multiple developmental pathways may shape neuroanatomical variation linked to sexual orientation. Thailand, with high cultural visibility of sexually and gender-diverse individuals AMAB, provides a unique context for this investigation.5 This study aimed to (1) identify latent profiles based on four biodevelopmental markers, and (2) test whether these profiles correspond to variation in CT and SA among same-sex attracted individuals AMAB. Given the possibility of multiple developmental pathways, no a priori predictions were made.

Methods

Goal 1 – Latent Profile Analysis (LPA).

Participants were recruited in Chiang Mai, Thailand (2017–2022) through sexual health clinics and network sampling. The final sample included heterosexual men (n = 317), gay men (n = 236), bisexual men (n = 30), and transfeminine individuals attracted to men (n = 229). Four biodevelopmental markers served as LPA indicators: (1) Handedness, measured with the 10-item Edinburgh Handedness Inventory; (2) Left-hand 2D:4D, measured using either direct caliper measurements or hand scans averaged across two trained raters; (3) FBO index, computed as (older brothers + 0.25)/(total siblings + 1); and (4) Familiality, defined as the proportion of nonheterosexual (relative to assigned sex) AMAB biological relatives among all AMAB biological relatives. Zero-order correlations revealed that the four biodevelopmental markers did not significantly correlate with each other, supporting their use as distinct indicators in the LPA. Standardized indicators were entered into models with 1–6 classes. Model selection was based on Bayesian Information Criterion, class interpretability, and avoiding very small classes. A four-class solution was retained (see Results for Goal 1).

Goal 2 – MRI Subsample and Neuroimaging Analysis.

For Goal 2, the latent profiles identified in Goal 1 were used as grouping variables to examine whether biodevelopmental subgroups differed in cortical structure among same-sex attracted individuals AMAB. MRI analyses included gay men, bisexual men, and transfeminine individuals attracted to men with usable MRI data and no history of gender-affirming hormone use. The smallest LPA class was excluded due to insufficient MRI sample size. T1-weighted images were acquired using a 1.5T scanner with a 16-channel head coil. CT and SA were computed using CIVET, which reconstructed the cortical ribbon’s white matter and pial surfaces and extracted 40,962 vertices per hemisphere. Age was included as a covariate. A partial least squares (PLS) analysis was used to identify multivariate latent variables (LVs) capturing maximal covariance between LPA group membership (based on the biomarkers) and vertex-wise CT and SA. Age was included as a covariate. Total brain volume was evaluated as a potential covariate using an analysis of covariance controlling for age; because it did not differ between profiles, it was not included in the PLS model. Significance of each LV was assessed using 5000 permutations. Reliability of vertex-wise saliences was estimated using 5000 bootstrap samples, with bootstrap ratios (BSRs) ±2.58 treated as stable contributors (approximate p < .01). This approach enabled identification of distributed structural patterns distinguishing latent developmental profiles.

Results

Goal 1 – Latent Profiles.

Four profiles captured distinct configurations of developmental markers, each with unique group compositions. A chi-square analysis showed that heterosexual and nonheterosexual (relative to assigned sex) participants were differentially distributed across the four profiles (χ² = 22.59, p < .00001), with nonheterosexual participants over-represented relative to chance in profiles associated with same-sex sexual orientation biomarkers.

Profile 1 – Baseline Profile (n = 513): Included heterosexual men (n = 235), gay men (n = 146), bisexual men (n = 5), and transfeminine individuals attracted to men (n = 127). This profile showed low or average scores across all four markers, representing individuals without strong evidence of any specific biodevelopmental influence.

Profile 2 – Elevated FBO profile (n = 108): Included heterosexual men (n = 37), gay men (n = 33), bisexual men (n = 6), and transfeminine individuals attracted to men (n = 32). Characterized by higher FBO index and otherwise average marker levels, this was the second largest profile, indicating that increased maternal immune responsivity may represent a common developmental pathway.

Profile 3 – Non-right-handedness profile (n = 78): Included heterosexual men (n = 30), gay men (n = 19), bisexual men (n = 1), and transfeminine individuals attracted to men (n = 28). This group showed lower laterality scores consistent with greater non-right-handedness, possibly reflecting atypical cerebral lateralization or reduced prenatal androgen effects. Due to its small MRI subsample (n = 4), it was excluded from PLS analyses.

Profile 4 – Elevated familiality profile (n = 68): Included heterosexual men (n = 4), gay men (n = 22), bisexual men (n = 13), and transfeminine individuals attracted to men (n = 29). This profile showed markedly higher familiality scores, consistent with potential genetic contributions to sexual orientation. It also contained a higher proportion of same-sex attracted participants.

Goal 2 – Brain Structural Differences.

MRI subsample sizes were: Profile 1 (baseline; n = 19), Profile 2 (FBO; n = 13), Profile 3 (non-right-handedness; n = 4, excluded), and Profile 4 (familiality; n = 42). Total brain volume did not differ by profile.

PLS revealed one significant LV (p = .007) explaining 58% of group–brain covariance. A robust structural contrast emerged between the baseline profile (Profile 1) and the familiality profile (Profile 4). Individuals in the baseline profile exhibited thicker cortex in the right precuneus and greater SA across distributed regions including frontopolar, superior temporal, angular gyrus, occipital, middle frontal, and cuneus areas, compared with those in the familiality profile. The FBO profile (Profile 2) did not reliably contribute to the latent structural pattern, suggesting that FBO-related developmental influences may not relate to cortical morphology in the same manner as familiality-related influences.

Conclusion

The findings support the existence of distinct biodevelopmental pathways underlying same-sex attraction in individuals AMAB, replicating previously observed latent profile patterns in other populations.3 In addition, these pathways were meaningfully associated with differences in cortical morphology. Integrating developmental biomarkers with neuroimaging revealed a novel association between familiality-related pathways and regional cortical organization, suggesting that genetic or familial mechanisms linked to sexual orientation are reflected in brain structure. Results highlight the heterogeneity within same-sex attracted populations and underscore the value of culturally diverse samples for advancing neurodevelopmental models of sexual and gender diversity.
