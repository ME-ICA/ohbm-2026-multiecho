Introduction

In memory clinic patients, white matter hyperintensities (WMHs) are a common manifestation of cerebral small vessel disease, occurring in >96% of individuals^1. High WMH burden has consistently been associated with cognitive impairment. The cognitive impact may even be larger for specific WMH locations^2. Yet, when assessing the factors contributing to cognitive impairment in individual patients, establishing diagnostic relevance of WMHs can be challenging. Only few studies formally quantified this diagnostic relevance, mostly only considering total WMH volume. Here, we quantify diagnostic relevance of WMHs, in terms of explained cognitive variance. We compare the relevance of sets of diagnostic markers, considering total WMH volume, WMH location in specific tracts, as well as normative modelling using age and sex-specific population norms.

Methods

We analyzed pooled and harmonized individual patient data (n=3,481) from 11 international memory clinic cohorts in the Meta-VCI-Map consortium^1. Cognitive function was quantified using harmonized z-scores adjusted for age, sex and education. We included 4 domains: attention & executive function, language, information processing speed, and verbal memory. WMHs were segmented and registered to MNI152 space. We measured total WMH volume, as well as WMH volumes in 20 white matter tracts of the John Hopkins University atlas^3. Volumes were also norm adjusted using age and sex-specific WMH norms based on 14,876 community-dwelling individuals^4. Diagnostic relevance was quantified by measuring the explained variance in cognitive function. We compared  explained variance of 4 sets of diagnostic markers: (1) demographics (age, sex and education) only, (2) demographics and total WMH volume, (3) demographics and tract-specific volumes and (4) and demographics and norm-adjusted, tract-specific z-scores. Results were validated using repeated 10-fold cross validation.


Results

The study included 3,481 patients with a mean age of 71±9 years and 50% females. The variance in cognitive function (mean [CI95%]) explained by demographics ranged between 5.4% [4.9-5.9%] (information processing speed) and 15.7% [14.7-16.8%] (language) across domains. On top of demographics, adding total WMH volume explained an additional 3.6% [3.3-4.0%] (attention and executive functioning) and 3.3% [3.0-3.6%] (verbal memory), and tract-specific WMH volumes explained a further 2.7% [2.4-3.1%] (attention and executive function) and 3.0% [2.6-3.5%] (verbal memory) when included instead of total WMH volume. In information processing speed, tract-specific volumes explained an additional 2.0% [1.5-2.5%] on top of demographics, while total WMH volume did not. Norm-adjusted WMH z-scores explained similar, but not significantly more, variance in cognition as unadjusted volumes.


Conclusion

Considering location doubled the diagnostic relevance of WMHs when explaining attention and executive function, processing speed and verbal memory, relative to total volume, with no additional benefit of norm adjusting. Nonetheless, the explained variance of WMH burden remained modest, likely because cognition in this setting is determined by many factors, particularly brain and cognitive reserve, but clearly also other pathologies, including Alzheimer’s. These results advocate that, with refinement, WMH location should be considered when explaining the cognitive impact of WMHs in individual patients.
