Introduction

The human brain and body are highly interconnected, yet aging research has often treated cerebral and peripheral systems as separate domains. Population-based aging trajectories are well-established for imaging-derived features such as cortical thickness, functional connectomes and cerebral blood flow. Age-dependent trajectories are also characterized for peripheral biomarkers, including body mass index, blood pressure and blood biochemistry measures. A central question is how these brain- and body-level measurements relate to each other.

Peripheral biomarkers are attractive candidates for tracking brain health as they are inexpensive and routinely collected in clinics. Initial efforts have reported specific brain-body associations—e.g., between reduced glucose tolerance and hippocampal atrophy,1 or between obesity and frontal cortex thinning.2 Most studies focus on single brain measurements or a compact set of physiological markers. Without incorporating comprehensive measurements from both neural and peripheral systems, we cannot build a full picture of brain-body coupling or compare brain features’ sensitivity to particular physiological signatures.

Here we ask two questions. First, to what extent can an individual’s demographic and laboratory profile inform us about their brain health? Second, among MRI-derived brain measurements spanning structural, functional and vascular domains, which are most closely associated with specific physiological phenotypes? Using multivariate pattern learning, we quantify covariance between multimodal MRI measurements and a broad set of demographics and physiological measures. We analyze two datasets with extensive neuroimaging and peripheral multi-omics: HCP-Aging and UK Biobank. We identify two generalizable axes of brain-body association—an aging axis and a metabolic axis—and determine brain features that contribute most to each. Last, we show how an individual’s position along these axes relates to their cognitive performance.

Methods

The discovery dataset is obtained from the Human Connectome Project-Aging (HCP-A).3 HCP-A data comes from 597 participants (329 females; 268 males) aged 36 to 100 years.

We use partial least squares (PLS) to assess how participants' age, blood pressure, body mass index (BMI) and plasma measurements covary with brain measurements derived from T1/T2–weighted structural imaging, arterial spin labeling (ASL) imaging, diffusion and functional magnetic resonance imaging. This multivariate analysis identified two statistically significant (assessed by permutation testing) and cross-validated latent variables (LVs) linking biomarkers to brain measurements in both males and females. In the Results section, we discuss these latent variables in detail. Note that there exist known differences between males and females in both brain and biomarker measures. For example, females typically exhibit higher cerebral blood perfusion than males5, along with distinct hormonal trajectories and notable differences in certain plasma markers (e.g., creatinine, and lipid markers). Therefore, we stratified the analysis by biological sex throughout the report.

Finally, we use the UK Biobank dataset4 as the validation dataset to replicate our findings. UK Biobank data comprises 3,013 participants (1,582 females; 1,431 males) aged 51 to 83 years.

Results

We identify two generalizable axes of brain-body associations in both datasets and in both biological sexes (males and females). The first latent variable captures 66.74% and 71.90% of the covariance between biomarkers and brain measurements in males and females, respectively (p-permutation < 0.00099). This latent variable, on the biomarker side, is driven by the dominant contribution of age (‘aging axis’). Across multiple brain measures, aging is associated with lower cortical thickness and slower cortical arterial transit time, in other words, it is mostly associated with loss of brain structural integrity and cerebral vascular alterations (Fig.1a-c).

The second latent variable captures 12.83% and 14.14% of the covariance between biomarkers and brain measurements in males and females, respectively (p-permutation < 0.00099).  The second latent variable is driven by a biomarker profile composed of low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL), elevated body mass index (BMI), mean arterial pressure (MAP), glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c), insulin, glucose, and alanine aminotransferase (ALT). On the brain side, this biomarker pattern is prominently associated with lower blood perfusion. Additional associations include lower functional connectome strength and mean diffusivity (MD); lower MD potentially reflects the presence of gliosis. This latent variable can be broadly interpreted as the ‘metabolic axis' (Fig.1d-f). Note that for both described latent variables, the correspondence between biomarker and brain scores is cross-validated and is generalizable in each biological sex group.

Next, we assess whether the pattern captured by the metabolic axis is age-driven. To test this, we regress out the non-linear effect of age from all features included in the PLS model—biomarkers and brain measurements—using generalized additive models for location, scale, and shape. We then rebuild the PLS model using age-corrected features. In this updated model, the first latent variable highlights the link between poor body metabolic profile (characterized by lower HDL and higher BMI, MAP, HbA1c, insulin, glucose, and ALT), along with lower brain blood perfusion. This result indicates that the identified metabolic axis reflects a distinct link between systemic metabolic dysfunction and vascular and functional alterations in the brain, beyond age-related effects.

What are the behavioral manifestations of the captured brain-body interaction axes in the typical aging cohort? The HCP-A dataset provides a rich array of neuroimaging, demographic, physiological and cognitive assessments that allow us to address this question. Here, we examine the link between a range of cognitive measures (fluid, crystallized, and total cognition composite scores) and participants' brain scores on the ‘aging’ and ‘metabolic’ axes. Participants—both males and females—who are younger, have greater cognitive flexibility and higher total cognition scores. Meanwhile, they perform worse on crystallized cognition tasks, indicating weaker performance in abilities heavily influenced by prior learning. We further show female participants with greater expression of multivariate brain patterns shown in Fig.1d—characterized by healthier metabolic status—express cognitive differences compared to their less metabolically healthy female counterparts and perform better in tasks requiring cognitive flexibility (Fig.2). This finding aligns with reported sex differences in the association between metabolic syndrome and dementia, with metabolic syndrome being significantly associated with cognitive impairment in women only.

To validate the reported findings, we used the UK Biobank dataset and showed that the same results can be obtained using this dataset.

Conclusion

In the present report, we analyze data of HCP-A and UK Biobank cohorts to characterize the multivariate relationship between a series of brain measurements and bodily phenomics. The PLS analysis reveals two principal axes of brain-body associations: one driven primarily by age and the other by metabolic health. In both datasets, an increase in age is associated with cortical thinning and microstructural alterations, along with slower arterial transit times. One novel and reproducible finding across this study is the identification of a metabolic axis linking peripheral physiological markers and the brain. We show that cerebral blood perfusion is the primary brain feature that potentially declines as a function of elevated metabolic risk factors (increased BMI, MAP, HbA1c, insulin, glucose and ALT, and lowered HDL). One possibility is that vascular susceptibility to metabolic risks constitutes the principal biological mechanism underlying the brain-body metabolic axis in aging populations. Elevated BMI, blood pressure and glycosylated hemoglobin, as well as low HDL cholesterol, increase atherosclerotic and cardiovascular disease risk, compromise vascular integrity, and ultimately impair brain blood perfusion.

The perfusion-centric metabolic effect can partly explain the disparate structural effects of metabolic risk factors reported to date. While most studies report reductions in frontal cortex volume and thickness in obesity, some report overall gray matter loss or even white and gray matter expansion. Given that metabolic markers covary most strongly with cerebral blood perfusion in both datasets, future neuroimaging studies of obesity and metabolic syndrome may achieve greater consistency by including blood perfusion measures when assessing brain health.

Lifestyle changes that ameliorate metabolic dysfunction can have a significant clinical relevance due to their influence on brain blood perfusion health. Hypoperfusion and vascular dysfunction impair the brain's clearance of solutes and metabolites through compromised interstitial fluid drainage leading to accumulation of toxic proteins. Simultaneously, hypoperfusion induces oxidative stress, promoting the formation of misfolded proteins and amyloid-beta precursors. In short, a potential mechanistic explanation is that obesity and metabolic dysfunction lead to cerebrovascular alterations, which in turn facilitate amyloid-beta deposition in brain tissue (leading to Alzheimer's disease) and amyloid formation in brain vasculature system (leading to cerebral amyloid angiopathy). Furthermore, reduced blood flow due to arterial narrowing promotes thrombosis and increases risk of emboli formation. Maintaining metabolic health may thus prevent or slow down neurodegenerative cascades by preserving brain perfusion.

Importantly, both aging and metabolic brain signatures have behavioral relevance across participants. We replicate well-known associations between aging and cognitive domains; in particular, we find the relationship between aging and diminished cognitive flexibility. In addition, we find that female participants with greater scores on the metabolic dysfunction axis show greater fluid cognition impairment compared to their metabolically healthier counterparts. The greater metabolic-cognitive associations observed in females may contribute to the higher Alzheimer's disease prevalence in females compared to males.

Our study resonates with a growing appreciation for studying brain aging from a wider, multivariate perspective and contributes to the development of comprehensive translatable biomarkers for brain health assessment.
