Introduction

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental condition defined by impairments in social communication alongside restricted and repetitive behaviors, affecting approximately one in 36 children in the United States, with a male-to-female diagnosis ratio ranging from 3:1 to 4:1. Most compelling theories behind this male prevalence are the Female Protective Effects and the Extreme Male Brain1. If these theories hold, they should be at least partially reflected in sex-specific neural correlates, motivating the treatment of sex as a stratifying variable rather than a nuisance factor2.

Much of our understanding of sex-specific neurobiology in autism comes from neuroimaging studies reporting disruptions in brain architecture, mainly using functional and structural connectivity signals. Beyond function and structure, brain morphology has also highlighted sex-specific differences, with evidence of thicker cortices in autistic girls early in development followed by accelerated thinning. Morphological features are thought to reflect cumulative developmental and genetic processes, yet most studies rely on region-wise analyses that may overlook spatial interdependencies across the cortex.

One promising alternative is to examine morphology-based connectivity by quantifying similarity in structural features between brain regions. Most prior work has focused on structural covariance networks based on single morphological properties, reporting sex-dependent alterations in autism, while whole-brain multivariate approaches remain limited.

To address these gaps, we characterized sex-specific connectivity patterns based on multivariate morphological information in youth with autism. Using a large multi-site dataset of participants aged 8–18 years, morphology-based connectivity was computed from features, and disrupted connections relative to typically developing controls were identified separately in males and females and related to behavioral traits.

Methods

PARTICIPANTS

337 individuals from Wave 1 of the NIH Autism Centers of Excellence (ACE) Network were included after quality control, involving visual inspection of segmentation output and exclusion of scans with preprocessing failures. The sample included 181 individuals with ASD (age: 12.73 ± 2.92 years, 84 female) and 156 typically developing controls (TDC; age: 13.20 ± 3.01 years, 78 female), collected across four sites: Harvard University, Seattle Children’s/University of Washington, University of California Los Angeles, and Yale University. Behavioral and comorbidity traits were assessed within the ASD group using scores from the Autism Diagnostic Interview–Revised (ADI-R), Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule–2nd Edition (ADOS-2), and Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL).

MRI ACQUISITION AND PREPROCESSING

Structural images were acquired using a high-resolution T1-weighted MPRAGE sequence (1×1×1 mm³). Preprocessing was performed using FreeSurfer’s recon-all pipeline (v6.0), including cortical surface reconstruction. Gray matter volume maps were generated using a voxel-based morphometry pipeline implemented in CAT12 and adapted for FreeSurfer outputs. All images were manually reviewed to ensure accurate reconstruction and parcellation.

MORPHOLOGY-BASED CONNECTIVITY

Morphology-based connectivity matrices were constructed using the Morphometric INverse Divergence framework, which estimates pairwise similarity between brain regions based on symmetrized Kullback–Leibler divergence of vertex-wise morphometric feature distributions3. Analyses focused on cortical networks.

Thickness, mean curvature, surface area, sulcal depth, and gray matter volume were extracted and sampled at the vertex level within regions of the Desikan–Killiany atlas (68 regions). Feature distributions within each region were modeled using kernel density estimation and jointly used to compute multivariate similarity between regions, yielding a 68 × 68 connectivity matrix per individual.

SEX-SPECIFIC NETWORK ALTERATIONS

Statistical differences in connectivity were identified using Network-Based Statistics (NBS)4. Test statistics were computed at each edge, suprathreshold sub-networks were identified, and significance was assessed via permutation testing with family-wise error correction. Analyses were performed separately in males and females, with diagnosis as the main effect and age, intracranial volume, and site as covariates. Results are reported for 5000 permutations, an individual edge threshold of p = 0.005, and pFWE = 0.05. Sensitivity analysis also included assessing the robustness of our findings with a more liberal (p = 0.01) and stringent individual edge threshold (p = 0.001).

IMPORTANCE OF MORPHOLOGICAL PROPERTIES

The contribution of individual morphological properties was evaluated using a leave-one-feature-out procedure combined with ROC curve analysis. Sub-network strength, defined as the sum of altered connections identified by NBS, was used as the discriminating variable. Changes in classification performance were quantified by differences in area under the ROC curve after excluding each feature.

ASSOCIATION WITH BEHAVIOR AND COMORBIDITY

Associations between altered sub-network strength and behavioral or comorbidity measures within the ASD group were examined using partial correlations, controlling for age, site, and intracranial volume. These analyses were meant to be exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive, so they were not corrected for multiple comparisons.

Results

Group-level network analysis in males identified a single cluster of 35 significantly altered connections (pFWE = 0.037), largely reflecting increased morphology-based connectivity in ASD relative to TDC. This pattern prominently involved the left fusiform and medial orbital frontal gyri, as well as the right entorhinal cortex and lingual gyrus (Figure 2A). Results were obtained using an individual edge threshold of p = 0.005 and 5000 permutations, with similar profiles observed at alternative thresholds. Within this cluster, cortical thickness was the most influential morphological property, with the AUC dropping from 0.72 to 0.54 when removed. Sulcal depth had a smaller effect, while removing surface area or volumetric properties slightly improved group separability (Figure 2B). The major sub-network of increased connectivity in ASD showed a moderate positive correlation with restricted, repetitive, and stereotyped behaviors measured by the ADI-R (r = 0.22, p = 0.037), while other behavioral and comorbidity measures exhibited weaker, non-significant associations.

In females, a single cluster of 41 significantly altered connections was identified (pFWE = 0.028). Increased connectivity in ASD was centered on a bilateral entorhinal cortex hub extending to inferior parietal, lateral occipital, and pre- and postcentral regions, whereas decreased connectivity primarily involved the banks of the superior temporal sulcus (Figure 2C). These alterations remained spatially circumscribed at more stringent thresholds and expanded at more liberal thresholds. Group separability followed a similar pattern to males, with an AUC of 0.66 using all features and a reduction to 0.58 when cortical thickness was removed (Figure 2D). Other features had minimal or no impact individually. A moderate negative correlation was observed between the sub-network of decreased connectivity in ASD and communication skills measured by the ADI-R (r = −0.25, p = 0.027), while associations with other behavioral measures were weaker and non-significant.

Overlap analysis revealed no shared edges between the male and female ASD-related network profiles at the main statistical threshold (Jaccard index J = 0). Comparison of typically developing males and females identified a separate cluster of altered connections. Overlap between this normative sex-difference network and the ASD-related profiles was minimal (J = 0 for males; J = 0.01 for females), with only a single overlapping connection. These findings indicate that the observed sex-specific network profiles are driven by diagnosis-related alterations rather than normative sex differences.

Conclusion

Here, we provided evidence for sex-specific brain connectivity profiles derived from multivariate morphological signatures in adolescents with autism. Using a network-based framework, we identified largely distinct patterns of altered morphology-based connectivity in males and females with ASD, involving different cortical hubs and, in some cases, opposite directionality. These findings suggest that divergent neural substrates may underlie core autistic traits across sexes and that morphology-based connectivity offers a complementary perspective to functional and structural approaches for characterizing ASD heterogeneity.

Across sexes, cortical thickness and sulcal depth emerged as the most influential morphological properties contributing to network-level discrimination between ASD and typically developing individuals, highlighting their relevance for capturing subtle alterations in cortical organization. In contrast, surface area and volumetric information appeared to contribute less uniquely, suggesting redundancy among certain morphometric features when examined from a network perspective.

Exploratory associations between altered networks and behavioral measures revealed distinct patterns in males and females, with increased morphology-based connectivity relating to restrictive and repetitive behaviors in males and decreased connectivity associating with communication skills in females. These findings are consistent with sex-differentiated behavioral phenotypes in autism and motivate further investigation.

Importantly, overlap analyses indicated that the identified sex-specific network profiles were not driven by normative sex differences, supporting their relevance to diagnosis-related alterations. In sum, these results demonstrate that examining brain morphology through a connectivity framework can provide novel insights into sex-specific neurobiological mechanisms in autism and may contribute to more sensitive, sex-aware models of ASD characterization and intervention.
