Introduction

Musical skill learning involves the integration of motor, perceptual and cognitive processes and shows substantial individual variability. Neuroimaging studies have reported functional brain differences between musicians and non-musicians 1,2, as well as training-related plasticity in naïve individuals following musical training, prominently in motor and auditory regions 3. However, relatively few studies have examined how baseline neural activity relates to subsequent musical learning outcomes. To address this gap, we conducted a longitudinal study in music novices who completed a 6-week piano training program. Crucially, the dataset included both monozygotic twins and non-twin individuals. This design allows us to explore not only the neural dispositions of musical skills but also the extent to which these mechanisms are shared within genetically related individuals.  

Methods

  • Participants 

A total of 102 healthy, adult, right-handed individuals (mean age: 43.1 years, range 18-65; 60 females) with less than 2 years of childhood musical training and no musical training during adulthood were included in this study. The cohort consisted of 30 monozygotic (MZ) twin pairs and unrelated individuals.

  • MRI acquisition and task

Neuroimaging data were acquired prior to training using a 3T Siemens Prisma scanner. The protocol included T1-weighted structural images and task fMRI. The fMRI session involved a piano learning (MelodyLearn) task that is similar to the subsequent piano training program, where participants learned to play custom made melodies (4 bars) by gradually combining shorter segments into longer segments following visual instructions (Fig. 1a) and auditory feedback. The task used a factorial design with three sequence length levels and four repetitions per condition (Fig. 1b). 

  • Piano training program

All participants completed a six-week piano training program consisting of 4 sessions per week. The training was divided into two parts (Fig. 1c). The first three weeks involved a standardized curriculum where participants learned to perform two study-specific eight-bar melodies by progressively combining shorter segments into full pieces. During weeks 4-6, participants maintained practice on these two melodies at full length and additionally practiced familiar Swedish standard tunes at a self-selected pace. The accumulated numbers of melodies they learned across sessions were recorded to estimate each participant’s learning slope, and all responses during training were recorded to calculate the pitch accuracies. 

  • Data preprocessing and analysis

MRI data were preprocessed and analyzed using SPM12. Multiple regression analyses were conducted to determine the relationship between functional activity and behavioral learning metrics (learning slope and pitch accuracy), with age and sex as covariates. Linear mixed-effects models were used to examine whether twin pairs exhibited stronger similarity in learning abilities compared to other relationship groups. 

Results

Multiple regression analysis revealed a significant cluster spanning the right hippocampus and amygdala whose activity during the MelodyLearn task was positively associated with individual learning slopes (k = 452; t = 4.75, p < 0.001). A similar cluster was also positively correlated with higher pitch accuracies during the initial acquisition phase (weeks 1-3) (k = 492; t = 4.53, p < 0.001; Fig. 2). Further examination of the extracted cluster activity showed decreasing activation with increasing melody length, while activity remained stable across the four repetitions. 

Linear mixed-effect models demonstrated greater similarity in pitch accuracy within monozygotic twin pairs compared to unrelated pairs (p = 0.008), supporting a genetic contribution to individual differences in musical learning ability.

Conclusion

This longitudinal study shows that baseline neural activity relates to musical skill acquisition. Pre-training activity in the right hippocampus-amygdala predicted individual differences in learning slope and early pitch accuracy, consistent with prior evidence implicating right hippocampal activity as a neural predisposition for musical learning rate 4. Furthermore, monozygotic twins exhibited greater similarity in pitch accuracy than unrelated individuals, suggesting familial contributions to variability in musical learning capacity.
