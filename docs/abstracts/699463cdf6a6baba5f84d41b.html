Introduction

Machine learning has enabled major advances in analyzing high-dimensional data, yet progress in neuroimaging remains constrained by limited data access, strict privacy regulations, and the difficulty of assembling large, well-annotated clinical cohorts. Synthetic data offers a promising solution, allowing models to be developed and evaluated without relying directly on sensitive patient information. However, generating realistic disease-specific neuroimaging data is challenging because neurological disorders induce subtle, heterogeneous changes that are easily confounded with normal variability across individuals. Standard generative models trained independently on healthy and diseased datasets often fail to preserve these nuanced group differences. 

Variational inference provides a principled framework for learning latent representations that capture structured variability in complex datasets. By parameterizing latent factors that separately model variation shared across groups and variation specific to diseased participants, variational inference enables more accurate characterization of disease-related changes. This decomposition supports controlled synthesis of both healthy and diseased samples and naturally permits generation of paired synthetic representations—counterfactual-style healthy and diseased variants of the same individual—without requiring true paired observations for model training.

In this work, we adapt a variational-inference–based generative model to synthesize neuroimaging-derived regional brain volumes derived from T1-weighted MRI (Fig.1a). Using unpaired healthy and diseased data, the model learns latent factors that reproduce realistic disease effects while preserving healthy variability. Applied to mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and Alzheimer’s disease (AD), this approach captures disease-relevant variation and supports fine-grained, individual-level synthesis of healthy features. 

Methods

We adapt a variational inference–based generative framework for synthesizing paired neuroimaging-derived features from unpaired healthy and diseased cohorts^1^2. The model is designed to learn generative factors that explain shared anatomical variability across healthy and disease datasets and differentiate them from factors that explain disease-specific effects, enabling paired data generation in the absence of longitudinal or matched acquisitions.

Each subject is represented by two latent variables: a common latent z, capturing variation shared between healthy and diseased populations, and a salient latent s, encoding disease-related^1 variation. Two probabilistic encoders approximate the posterior distributions, and a shared decoder reconstructs regional brain volumes from the concatenated latent representation. For healthy (background) samples, the salient latent is fixed to a zero vector, enforcing that disease-specific variation is not encoded in the background distribution.

Model parameters are learned by maximizing a variational lower bound on the log-likelihood of both healthy and diseased samples. The objective includes reconstruction terms, KL-divergence regularization on the latent posteriors, and an alignment penalty that enforces similarity between the common latent distributions inferred from healthy and diseased data. This alignment encourages the common latent to capture only shared anatomical variations which helps enforce paired data generation. The framework supports both unconditional sampling from the latent priors and conditional synthesis by fixing or sampling the salient latent given an inferred common latent.

Results

We first evaluated the framework using semi-synthetic neuroimaging comprising cognitively normal (CN) participants from iSTAGING^3 and synthetic disease samples simulated with 3 distinct regional atrophy patterns. The model successfully generated paired healthy and diseased samples that reproduced simulated atrophy patterns across the subtypes, including bilateral hippocampal involvement (Fig.1b). Latent space analyses showed strong alignment between healthy and diseased samples in the common latent dimensions, while the salient latent dimensions encoded subtype-specific variation. We then applied the model to real neuroimaging data from ADNI^4. Despite substantial overlap between healthy and diseased distributions after covariate correction, the model synthesized paired samples that highlighted anatomically and clinically relevant patterns in regions known to be affected in Alzheimer’s disease, including the hippocampus and amygdala. Using conditional synthesis, we generated healthy samples for each individual and quantified deviations from observed brain anatomy. The magnitude of these deviations increased monotonically from cognitively normal to mild cognitive impairment (MCI) to Alzheimer’s disease (AD), providing an individualized measure of disease burden (Fig.2).

Conclusion

This work presents preliminary results from a variational inference–based approach for generating paired and counterfactual-style neuroimaging features from unpaired data by explicitly disentangling shared and disease-specific sources of variation. The framework produces biologically plausible paired samples, captures disease heterogeneity, and generalizes to independent clinical cohorts. These properties make the method well suited for studying neurodegenerative disease mechanisms, quantifying individual-level disease effects, and enabling paired analyses in settings where matched or longitudinal neuroimaging data are unavailable. The framework provides a practical, privacy-preserving route for generating accessible neuroimaging features and holds potential to improve model generalization.
