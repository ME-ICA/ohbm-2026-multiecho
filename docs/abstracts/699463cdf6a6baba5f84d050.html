Introduction

Biophysical large-scale circuit models provide mechanistic accounts of how macroscale brain dynamics emerge from local circuitry, but parameter optimization remains prohibitively slow because each candidate parameter set requires numerically integrating stochastic differential equations and simulating fMRI time series. Consequently, most applications tune only a few parameters, limiting biological realism and preventing deployment in population-scale datasets. Recent deep neural network (DNN) surrogates for differential equations can accelerate simulation, yet they typically target deterministic systems and aim to reproduce full time courses, which is intractable for stochastic whole-brain models with hundreds of thousands of integration steps. Here we introduce DELSSOME (DEep Learning for Surrogate Statistics Optimization in MEan field modeling), a transformer-based DNN framework that directly predicts summary statistics of model realism, defined as the discrepancy between simulated and empirical functional connectivity (FC) and functional connectivity dynamics (FCD). thereby bypassing numerical integration during optimization while preserving neurobiological interpretability.

Methods

We modeled whole-brain dynamics using the feedback inhibition control (FIC) mean-field model, in which each cortical region comprises interacting excitatory (E) and inhibitory (I) neuronal populations coupled by local recurrent connections and long-range structural connectivity (SC; Figure 1a).1 For a given parameter set, the model yields time courses of excitatory (SE) and inhibitory (SI) synaptic gating variables, from which we defined the excitation–inhibition (E/I) ratio as the ratio of their temporal averages.2 Cortical regions were interconnected via an empirical SC matrix derived from diffusion MRI tractography and scaled by a global coupling parameter.

To estimate model parameters, we used covariance matrix adaptation evolution strategy (CMA-ES; Figure 1b).3 At each generation, CMA-ES sampled 100 candidate parameter sets from a multivariate Gaussian (Step 1), evaluated all candidates (Step 2), and updated the Gaussian using the best-performing 10 sets (Step 3) to guide the search. In the baseline approach, evaluation required numerically integrating the FIC model with Euler stepping to generate neural activity and simulated fMRI time series (Figure 1c). Static functional connectivity (FC) and functional connectivity dynamics (FCD) were then derived from the simulated fMRI signals and compared to empirical FC and FCD using a composite “FC+FCD cost”, which combines FC correlation cost (1 – r), mean FC difference (d), and FCD Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance (KS). Lower cost indicated more realistic simulations.

We trained DELSSOME (DEep Learning for Surrogate Statistics Optimization in MEan field modeling) cost predictors on the FIC model using Human Connectome Project (HCP) resting-state fMRI and diffusion data from 1,029 adults.4 Preprocessed fMRI time series were averaged into 68 cortical regions to construct static FC and sliding-window FCD; participant-level SC was estimated from probabilistic tractography. For group-level modeling, we repeatedly resampled 50 participants to form 64, 14, and 13 bootstrap groups in the HCP training (n = 680), validation (n = 180), and test (n = 169) splits. For each group, CMA-ES with Euler integration optimized 10 FIC parameters over 100 epochs (100 children per epoch), yielding 640,000/140,000/130,000 parameter–cost pairs for training/validation/test of the FC+FCD cost predictor.

DELSSOME replaces the numerically intensive evaluation step within CMA-ES by directly predicting FC+FCD cost from model parameters and data (Figure 1d). DELSSOME jointly embeds regional FIC parameters and SC with a transformer encoder to obtain a model embedding, and combines this with empirical FC and FCD embeddings to predict FC correlation cost (1 - r), mean FC difference (d), and FCD Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance (KS). During training, DELSSOME learned from Euler-based parameter–cost pairs; during benchmarking, we replaced Euler integration with DELSSOME inside CMA-ES and compared optimization speed and parameter quality in held-out HCP test groups.

Generalization was assessed by directly applying the HCP-trained DELSSOME cost predictor to the Philadelphia Neurodevelopmental Cohort (PNC; n = 885, 8–23 years),5 where we re-estimated cortical E/I ratio maps across age- and cognition-stratified groups.2 E/I ratio was defined from the fitted FIC models as the ratio of temporal averages of SE and SI. For individual-level modeling, we trained participant-level DELSSOME predictors across 12 additional datasets and benchmarked DELSSOME versus Euler CMA-ES on 20 held-out participants per dataset. Finally, we extended the framework to two further biophysical models, the parametric mean-field model and the Hopf model, and evaluated DELSSOME CMA-ES against Euler CMA-ES for speed and final fit.

Results

In the HCP test set, DELSSOME accurately predicted FC+FCD realism, achieving correlations of r=0.97 for static FC correlation cost (1–r), r=0.86 for mean FC difference (d), and r=0.92 for FCD KS distance relative to Euler-based ground truth. A single DELSSOME forward pass was ~2,000× faster than Euler integration when evaluating a candidate FIC parameter set, while preserving agreement with empirical FC and FCD. Embedding DELSSOME within CMA-ES yielded ~2,000× acceleration during the training phase and an overall ~50× speed-up in FIC parameter optimization when including Euler-based validation and testing (Figure 1e), with no degradation in model fit (FC+FCD cost, p=0.38 vs Euler CMA-ES; n=50 repetitions; Figure 1f). 

Applying the HCP-trained DELSSOME CMA-ES directly to the PNC dataset, without retraining, reproduced key neurodevelopmental findings2: mean cortical E/I ratio decreased with age (Figure 2a), with steeper declines in sensory–motor cortex than association cortex (Figure 2b), and DELSSOME- and Euler-derived age–E/I trajectories were highly concordant (r=0.96 across 29 age groups). In age-matched subgroups, youth with higher cognitive performance exhibited lower cortical E/I ratio than low-performance peers, with significant regional differences in 66/68 regions after FDR correction (q<0.05) for both methods and strong agreement between DELSSOME and Euler group contrasts (r=0.85 across 28 subgroup pairs). 

At the individual level, DELSSOME CMA-ES accelerated parameter fitting by ~50× across 14 datasets while achieving comparable test costs to Euler CMA-ES (paired t-tests, all p>0.05), and generalized without retraining to two unseen datasets. Leveraging this scalability, we estimated normative E/I trajectories in 11,844 individuals spanning childhood to elderly. We then fitted a GAMLSS model, revealing decreasing inter-individual variability with age and robust sex differences, with the female E/I ratio consistently higher than that of male (Figure 2c).

The DELSSOME CMA-ES was also applied to other biophysical models. When applied to the parametric mean-field model, DELSSOME CMA-ES provided ~1,500× during training and ~50× acceleration overall (Figure 2d), with statistically indistinguishable FC+FCD costs relative to Euler CMA-ES (p=0.12; Figure 2e). When applied to the Hopf model, DELSSOME CMA-ES provided ~8,000× speed-ups during training and 95× acceleration overall (Figure 2f), with statistically indistinguishable FC+FCD costs relative to Euler CMA-ES (p=0.28; Figure 2g).

Conclusion

DELSSOME provides a general deep-learning framework for accelerating mechanistic brain modeling by predicting surrogate statistics of model realism rather than simulating full time courses. Across multiple datasets, age ranges, and model classes, DELSSOME replaces the numerically intensive inner loop of evolutionary optimization while preserving parameter estimates and neurobiological inferences, including developmental reductions in cortical E/I ratio, links between E/I balance and cognition, and lifespan sex differences. The resulting 50–95× acceleration in parameter estimation, and up to 8,000× speed-up in evaluating model realism, transforms the feasibility of applying biophysical models to large cohorts and demanding analyses such as permutation testing or normative chart construction. Because DELSSOME operates directly on model parameters, structural connectivity, and empirical FC and FCD, it is readily extensible to alternative neural mass or field models and different choices of evaluation metrics. More broadly, these results illustrate how domain-informed DNN surrogates can unlock population-scale, mechanistically interpretable modeling in human neuroscience.
