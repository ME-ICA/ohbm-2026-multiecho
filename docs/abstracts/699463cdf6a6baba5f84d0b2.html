Introduction

Educational attainment is a key indicator of cognitive reserve and is known to support resilience against age-related neuropathologies. The cognitive benefits of education may vary across racial and ethnic groups due to long-standing structural inequities and differences in life-course educational opportunities.^1 Consequently, the strength of the education–cognition relationships may not be uniform across populations, and potential differences across racial and ethnic groups remain underexplored. Furthermore, prior studies rarely accounted for the presence and severity of different types of brain pathologies, including neurodegeneration, cerebrovascular disease, and Alzheimer’s disease–specific biomarkers, which can be differentially distributed across populations, limiting their ability to disentangle cognitive reserve from biological effects.

The present study examined the association between education and cognition in a large, diverse sample of aging adults from the Health and Aging Brain Study – Health Disparities (HABS-HD).^2 Our aim was to determine whether ethnoracial differences in the education-cognition relationship persist after adjusting for the presence of brain pathologies.

Methods

We analyzed cross-sectional data from 1,641 participants aged 55 and over in the HABS-HD cohort, including 598 non-Hispanic Black (NHB), 486 non-Hispanic White (NHW) and 557 Hispanic (H) individuals. Participants were selected based on the availability of MRI data and Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores. Education was quantified as years of formal schooling. Linear regression models were used to investigate the relationship between cognition (assessed using the MMSE scores) and education, including an interaction term between education and race/ethnicity to examine potential group differences.

For neuroimaging covariates, T1-weighted and FLAIR MRIs were processed using PELICAN, our extensively validated open-source pipeline.^3 Pre-processing steps included image denoising, correction of intensity non-uniformity, and intensity normalization to a common intensity range, followed by linear and nonlinear registration to the MNI-ICBM152-2009b template. Deformation-based morphometry (DBM) maps were derived from the Jacobian determinants of the nonlinear deformation fields, providing voxel-wise indices of local tissue expansion or contraction relative to the template. Lower DBM values reflect regional atrophy, whereas higher values indicate relative enlargement. Regional DBM measures were obtained by averaging DBM values within cortical and subcortical regions defined by the CerebrA atlas.^4 Mean hippocampal and ventricular DBM measures were used as markers of AD-specific and global neurodegeneration, respectively. White matter hyperintensities (WMHs) were used as markers of cerebrovascular pathology, and were quantified using BISON, our automated WMH segmentation tool.^5 Global WMH volumes were log-transformed to reduce skewness. Cortical thickness was estimated using FreeSurfer, and was provided by HABS-HD. Amyloid and tau positivity status was also extracted based on plasma markers (provided by HABS-HD). Models were adjusted for age, sex, diagnostic status, as well as neuroimaging markers of neurodegeneration and cerebrovascular pathology. The models were further repeated in the subset of 265 participants that had amyloid and tau biomarkers available, with amyloid and tau positivity included as additional covariates. Interaction terms examined whether the cognitive benefits of education differed across ethnoracial groups.

Results

Groups showed significant differences across demographic and cognitive variables, with the exception of age between Hispanic and Black participants, which did not differ statistically. NHW participants were older on average (69.0 ± 9.1 years) compared to NHB (63.2 ± 7.8) and Hispanic individuals (63.7 ± 7.4). Education (NHB: 14.8 ± 2.6; NHW: 15.4 ± 2.7; Hispanic: 9.8 ± 4.7) and MMSE scores (NHB: 27.5 ± 2.8; NHW: 28.5 ± 2.3; Hispanic: 25.5 ± 4.2) also differed significantly across groups. Most participants were cognitively normal, though diagnostic distributions differed across groups, with NHW individuals exhibiting the lowest proportion of dementia and NHB individuals the highest relative to the other groups.

Education was positively associated with MMSE across all racial/ethnic groups; however, the magnitude of this association differed significantly. The education-cognition slope was steepest in Hispanic participants (tEducation:Hispanic ≈ 5.8–8.4, p < 0.001), followed by NHB (tEducation:NHB ≈ 2.4–3.0, p = 0.01-0.001), and flattest in NHW participants, indicating smaller cognitive gains per incremental year of education in this group. Significant interaction effects supported racial/ethnic moderation of the education–MMSE relationships. These effects remained robust and showed minimal attenuation after adjusting for hippocampal and ventricular deformation, cortical thickness, WMH burden, and amyloid/tau positivity. The results indicate that structural brain pathologies do not explain the observed differences in the education-cognition relationships.

Conclusion

These findings suggest that educational attainment contributes to cognitive reserve across ethnoracial groups, with protective effects remaining even after accounting for structural brain changes and Alzheimer’s disease–related pathologies. However, the degree of cognitive benefit associated with education varied between groups. Given that these differences remain after controlling for AD-related pathology, these findings indicate that pathology alone cannot fully account for the observed effects.

Overall, our results emphasize the importance of racially diverse cohorts and equity-focused interpretations to distinguish biological and structural contributors to cognitive aging.  Given that cognitive reserve mechanisms might not be equally effective across groups, implementation of interventions and policies that mitigate the historical educational and social inequalities that carry into later life is necessary to improve cognitive health in all populations.
