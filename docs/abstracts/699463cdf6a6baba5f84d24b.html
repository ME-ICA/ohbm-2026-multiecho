Introduction

Faces are fundamental social signals, yet in everyday vision they are rarely seen in isolation. Recent ultra-high-field fMRI demonstrates that this context matters: when faces compete with scene content, the fusiform face area (FFA) exhibits hemisphere-specific response patterns [1,2]. The left FFA preserves a contralateral spatial bias for peripheral faces, whereas the right FFA loses this bias, favouring category-selective processing instead. These asymmetries are not observed in object-selective cortex, suggesting lateralised spatial sampling is a distinctive feature of face processing that emerges under naturalistic viewing conditions.

That lateralisation emerges only under competition suggests emotional faces may provide a critical test of this phenomenon. Motivational accounts link hemispheric asymmetries to action tendencies, with approach-related signals (e.g., happiness) favouring left-hemisphere processing and avoidance-related signals (e.g., fear) favouring the right. Pain expressions are theoretically critical because they can signal both threat and vulnerability, making them an ideal probe of how competition-dependent lateralisation interacts with emotion-contingent processing. Beyond the ventral stream, the posterior superior temporal sulcus (pSTS) and amygdala—regions central to decoding emotional facial cues—may reveal complementary mechanisms for prioritising expressions under competition.

Using 7T fMRI in thirty-five adults, we extended this paradigm with systematic modulation of facial emotion (happy, neutral, pain) and expanded ROIs across ventral visual and affective networks to test how competition and expression interact.

Methods

Thirty-five healthy adults participated in this study using a Siemens Magnetom 7T scanner at the University of Queensland. Functional images were acquired using a T2*-weighted multiband EPI sequence (TR = 500ms, TE = 25.2ms, 1.8mm isotropic voxels, multiband factor = 4). Each participant completed six experimental runs (560 volumes each) and two functional localizer runs (720 volumes each).

The experimental paradigm extended Taubert et al. [1] by introducing facial emotion as a factor. Stimuli consisted of face and object images presented at three visual field positions (left, centre, right) under two competition conditions: single (face/object alone) or multi-image (face/object flanked by two scene images). Face stimuli depicted three emotional expressions (pain, neutral, happy), yielding a 3 (Position) × 2 (Competition) × 3 (Emotion) factorial design.

Regions of interest were defined bilaterally from independent functional localizer data using group-level mixed-effects meta-analysis (3dMEMA) [3] with REML-derived statistics. Face-selective FFA was identified using the Faces > All contrast, and scene-selective PPA was identified using the Objects > All contrast (which included scenes as non-target stimuli). The pSTS was localised using dynamic face stimuli, and amygdala ROIs were anatomically defined.

Following Silson et al. [2] and Taubert et al. [1], we computed d' scores indexing category selectivity (face vs object responses) and four bias metrics: spatial bias (peripheral vs central responses), category bias (preferred vs non-preferred category), contralateral bias (contralateral vs ipsilateral visual field), and an overall lateralisation index. These metrics were analysed using linear mixed-effects models with hemisphere, competition, and emotion as fixed factors, and participant as a random effect.

Results

Face-selective and place-selective regions demonstrated a clear double dissociation: FFA showed positive d' scores (faces > objects; central single: left d' = 0.58, right d' = 0.68, both p < .001) while PPA showed negative d' scores (objects > faces; d' = −0.98 to −1.20, all p < .001), confirming robust category-selective processing across conditions.

Competition significantly reduced category selectivity, particularly in peripheral vision. FFA face selectivity dropped approximately 50% under competition (single d' = 0.63 → multi d' = 0.33), with the strongest suppression for contralateral presentations. Notably, right FFA lost significant face selectivity for contralateral stimuli under single presentation (d' = 0.13, p = .21) but maintained selectivity centrally under competition (d' = 0.51, p < .001). PPA showed comparable peripheral suppression (contralateral: d' = −0.96 → −0.35).

Hemispheric asymmetries in spatial processing replicated prior findings [1,4]. Left FFA maintained robust contralateral bias across single (β = 0.077, p < .001) and competitive (β = 0.024, p = .02) conditions, consistent with preserved spatial sensitivity. Right FFA showed weaker contralateral effects that were modulated by competition (single: β = 0.020, p = .04; multi: β = 0.036, p < .001), consistent with category-dominant processing. Left PPA demonstrated a competition-dependent reversal: contralateral bias when stimuli appeared alone (β = 0.085, p < .001) shifted to ipsilateral bias under competition (β = −0.030, p < .001).

Contrary to predictions, emotion (pain, neutral, happy) did not significantly modulate responses in any ROI (all ANOVAs p > .05), with the exception of a marginal Pain > Happy effect in FFA under competition (p = .04). Unexpectedly, pSTS showed object preference (d' = −0.53, p < .01) rather than the anticipated face selectivity—potentially reflecting its sensitivity to dynamic rather than static facial information. Amygdala exhibited no categorical preference (d' ≈ 0, ns) and no emotion differentiation, possibly due to the use of static rather than dynamic emotional expressions.

Conclusion

This study replicated and extended findings from Taubert et al. [1] and Silson et al. [2], demonstrating that competition-dependent hemispheric asymmetries in face processing are robust across emotional expressions. The double dissociation between FFA (face-selective) and PPA (scene-selective) confirms category-specific processing, while the ~50% reduction in selectivity under competition highlights the importance of studying face perception under naturalistic, multi-stimulus conditions.

Critically, left FFA maintained strong contralateral spatial bias regardless of competition, whereas right FFA showed weaker spatial sensitivity consistent with category-dominant processing. This lateralisation pattern generalised across pain, neutral, and happy expressions, suggesting that the fundamental spatial organisation of face-selective cortex operates independently of emotional content.

Contrary to predictions from motivational accounts of hemispheric asymmetry, emotion did not significantly modulate responses in any ROI. The marginal Pain > Happy effect in FFA under competition (p = .04) hints at potential threat-related prioritisation but requires replication. The absence of emotion effects in pSTS and amygdala was unexpected. For pSTS, this likely reflects its preferential sensitivity to dynamic rather than static facial information [4]. For amygdala, the use of static expressions or the inclusion of pain rather than fear (a more canonical amygdala driver) may explain the null findings.

These results suggest that competition-dependent lateralisation in ventral visual cortex reflects a domain-general organisational principle for face processing that is not readily modulated by the emotional significance of facial expressions. Future studies using dynamic stimuli may be better suited to capture emotion-specific effects in regions such as pSTS and amygdala.
