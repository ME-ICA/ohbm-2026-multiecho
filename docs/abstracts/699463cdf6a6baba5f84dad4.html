Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is widely recognized as a systemic condition that accelerates central nervous system aging, with patients frequently exhibiting cognitive decline and brain volume loss at rates significantly faster than healthy aging. Although cerebral atrophy and functional network disruptions are well-documented correlates of cognitive impairment in T2DM,1 the impact of the disease on the spinal cord remains a critical area that lacks sufficient research. The spinal cord serves as the essential hub for motor command transmission and sensory integration, yet it is often overlooked in neuroimaging studies of metabolic disorders. This omission is significant because patients with T2DM commonly present with diabetic frailty, which manifests as reduced grip strength, gait abnormalities, and slowing of psychomotor speed. Recent evidence from animal models indicates that hyperglycemia can induce oxidative stress and microvascular lesions that specifically damage spinal motor neurons and white matter tracts. However, it remains debated in human studies whether these spinal deficits exist centrally or are merely secondary to peripheral neuropathy.2 Furthermore, the functional coordination between the brain and spinal cord, referred to as the cerebrospinal axis, is likely compromised in T2DM. This impairment potentially drives the motor and cognitive comorbidities observed clinically. To address this gap, this study utilizes state-of-the-art simultaneous cortico-spinal cord functional and diffusion MRI (fMRI and dMRI).3 Unlike traditional separate scanning, this technique allows for the capture of physiological coupling along the entire cortex-brainstem-spinal cord pathway in real time. We assessed M1-spinal connectivity and clinical links, hypothesizing T2DM induces maladaptive plasticity via cortical inefficiency and spinal hyperexcitability.

Methods

We recruited 31 patients with confirmed T2DM (mean age 55.03 ± 9.50 years) and 27 age- and sex-matched healthy controls (mean age 51.04 ± 11.47 years). T2DM diagnosis followed World Health Organization criteria defined as fasting blood glucose of 7.0 mmol/L or higher or HbA1c of 6.5% or higher. Exclusion criteria included history of neurological disorders, severe diabetic complications such as retinopathy or neuropathy, MRI contraindications, or severe cognitive impairment defined by a MoCA score of less than 2. A comprehensive clinical battery assessed three domains including metabolic status using fasting blood glucose and Glycated Hemoglobin (HbA1c), cognition using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) for global cognition and Trail Making Test (TMT) Parts A and B for processing speed and executive function, and motor function via grip strength, the Timed Up and Go (TUG) test, and 3-meter gait speed. Subjective pain was evaluated using the McGill Pain Questionnaire (MPQ). Imaging was performed on a Siemens Prisma 3.0T scanner equipped with a 64-channel head-neck combined coil to ensure optimal signal-to-noise ratio in the cervical region. We acquired whole-brain T1-weighted MPRAGE images (TR = 2300 ms, TE = 2.28 ms, 1 mm isotropic voxel size) and high-resolution spinal cord anatomical scans. Simultaneous functional imaging employed a specialized multi-slice echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence covering the whole brain and cervical spinal cord (C1–C7) simultaneously with parameters set to TR = 2680 ms, TE = 27 ms, voxel size = 1.5 × 1.5 × 4 mm, and 70 axial slices with posterior-to-anterior phase encoding. Heart rate and respiration were recorded at 400 Hz using a pulse oximeter and respiratory belt to correct for physiological noise which is critical for spinal fMRI quality. Participants performed a block-design unilateral isometric handgrip task at a 1 Hz frequency using a split-digit gripper set to approximately 40% maximal voluntary contraction. The paradigm consisted of 12 blocks where each block included 8 seconds of task performance followed by 16 to 20 seconds of rest. Data were processed using FSL v6.0.7 and the Spinal Cord Toolbox, with preprocessing steps including motion correction, B0 field map distortion correction, and Physiological Noise Modelling to regress out cardiac and respiratory artifacts. We used a General Linear Model to contrast Grip versus Rest conditions. Group analyses used mixed-effects models with statistical thresholds set at Z > 3.1 with cluster-level FWE correction (p < 0.05) for the brain and Threshold-Free Cluster Enhancement (TFCE, p < 0.05) for the spinal cord. Psychophysiological Interaction (PPI) analysis used the peak activated cluster in the left M1 as the seed to evaluate task-modulated connectivity. DTI analysis involved tract-based spatial statistics (TBSS) to calculate Fractional Anisotropy (FA).

Results

No significant differences were found in age or sex distribution between groups. However, spinal cord morphometry revealed specific alterations in T2DM patients where the eccentricity of the C7 spinal segment was significantly lower in patients compared to controls (0.72 ± 0.04 vs. 0.79 ± 0.05; p = 0.005). Notably, this structural deviation was negatively correlated with executive function measured by TMT-B (r = -0.43, p = 0.018) suggesting a link between spinal integrity and cognitive performance. Complementing these macrostructural changes, DTI analysis revealed widespread microstructural compromise. Patients showed significantly reduced Fractional Anisotropy (FA) in cerebral tracts (specifically the corona radiata) and spinal pathways (medial reticulospinal tract). These microstructural deficits were clinically relevant: reduced spinal FA strongly correlated with poorer global cognition (MoCA, r = 0.63, p = 0.001) and slower psychomotor speed (TMT-A, r = -0.51, p = 0.012), providing direct evidence that spinal integrity contributes to cognitive-motor coupling.

Regarding task-evoked activation, both groups exhibited robust activation in the contralateral M1, cerebellum, and ipsilateral C6-C7 spinal segments during the task. However, direct group comparisons revealed widespread hyperactivation in the T2DM group. Patients showed significantly greater BOLD response in the left M1, left cerebellum, and left anterior insula (p < 0.05 corrected). Crucially, a specific cluster of hyperactivation was identified in the ventral horn of the right C7 segment (p < 0.05, TFCE corrected) which corresponds to the motor neuron pool driving the hand muscles. The magnitude of right cerebellar hyperactivation was positively correlated with both poorer executive function on TMT-B (r = 0.53, p = 0.011) and higher HbA1c levels (r = 0.61, p = 0.016) indicating that this over-recruitment is likely maladaptive and metabolically driven. 

Despite the local hyperactivation, PPI analysis revealed a breakdown in long-range functional integration. T2DM patients exhibited significantly reduced task-modulated connectivity between the left M1 and the cerebellum as well as between the M1 and putamen (p < 0.05). We found that the functional connectivity strength between M1 and the cerebellum was positively correlated with the completion time of TMT-A(r = 0.50, p = 0.018), and the connectivity strength between M1 and the putamen was positively correlated with cognitive flexibility deficits (TMT B-A) (r = 0.61, p = 0.002). In other words, stronger connectivity was associated with worse performance. In simple motor control tasks, lower functional coupling may reflect a more automated motor processing pattern, while increased coupling suggests a more effortful and feedback-dependent processing. In diabetic patients, M1 overactivation suggests a loss of cortical inhibition. In this context, increased M1-cerebellar connectivity is not adaptive compensation, but rather a form of maladaptive recruitment. Furthermore, direct M1-spinal functional connectivity did not differ significantly between groups suggesting that the primary functional disconnection occurs at the supraspinal level rather than the corticospinal tract itself. Canonical Correlation Analysis identified a significant dominant mode of population covariation linking neural and clinical profiles. The clinical phenotype was characterized by high loadings for Pain Burden and Poor Executive Function with moderate loadings for HbA1c. This clinical profile mapped onto a neural pattern characterized by enhanced M1 coupling with the spinal ventral horn and sensorimotor network but suppressed coupling with the default mode network.4 This finding suggests that patients with worse cognitive and pain symptoms rely on a high-effort network state involving excessive spinal recruitment to maintain basic task performance.

Conclusion

This study presents the first simultaneous cortico-spinal cord fMRI evidence of central nervous system reorganization in Type 2 Diabetes. Our findings describe a model of maladaptive compensation regarding T2DM neuroplasticity. The paradox of local hyperactivation in M1 and cerebellum paired with distant hypoconnectivity between M1 and cerebellum aligns with the Compensation-Related Utilization of Neural Circuits Hypothesis.5 The diabetic brain attempts to compensate for neural inefficiency by over-recruiting local resources but fails to maintain efficient long-range network integration. Furthermore, the novel finding of C7 ventral horn hyperactivation and structural deformation challenges the brain-centric view of T2DM. It suggests that central sensitization or loss of descending inhibition extends to the spinal level and contributes to motor output degradation. Finally, the multivariate analysis results powerfully demonstrate that spinal cord dysfunction is not an isolated phenomenon but is intrinsically linked to the broader pain-cognition symptom cluster. This establishes the spinal cord as a potential biomarker for disease progression. Future therapeutic strategies should consider targeting the restoration of these specific circuits through coordinated motor-cognitive training to mitigate the cascading effects of T2DM on the entire cerebrospinal axis.
