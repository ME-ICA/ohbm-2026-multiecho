Introduction

In psychology, “temperament” refers to innate individual differences in emotions and behavior, generally considered to remain unchanged throughout life. On the other hand, “personality” is said to be formed on the foundation of temperament through interaction with acquired experiences and the environment. Cloninger divides personality into temperament and character, proposing that temperament is innate and character is shaped by environment. The four dimensions of temperament are novelty seeking (NS), harm avoidance (HA), reward dependence (RD), and persistence (P), while the three dimensions of character are self-direction (SD), cooperation (C), and self-transcendence (ST). In this study, we used resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rsfMRI) of healthy adults to analyze neural networks in the brain, and evaluated and examined their association with temperament and personality based on Cloninger's theory.

Methods

The study was conducted after obtaining prior approval from the Ethics Committee of Kanazawa Medical University. The subjects were 81 healthy subjects (56 males and 25 females) with an average age of 31.3±8.8 years (20-57 years) who consented to the study after the purpose and methods were explained. Temperament Personality Inventory (TCI), a self-administered questionnaire, was used to assess the subjects’ temperament and character.

Functional images of the whole brain were captured by an EPI sequence using a 3.0T MR scanner. The captured functional images were subjected to statistical image analysis using the CONN toolbox [2]. First, independent component analysis (ICA) was performed to obtain a map of the functional connectivity (FC) of the neural network. Next, we examined the relationship between the FC of the networks obtained in the ICA and the TCI results. P<0.05 per cluster by FDR correction was considered significant.

Results

Components were extracted by ICA, and as shown in Figure 1, components were identified as five types of resting-state networks: Salience, Default mode, Language, Visual, and Sensorimotor networks. Regression analysis of the four temperaments and three characters traits associated with the degree of neural FC in the brain via these five networks revealed brain regions suggesting significant associations. Figure 2 shows the results of the regression analysis.

When the temperament trait of HA was dominant, the FC of the prefrontal cortex, mainly the left supplementary motor area (①), was decreased in the Default mode network, and the FC of the right superior and middle frontal gyrus (②) was increased. Stronger RD was positively correlated with FC in the right opercular part and insular gyri (③) of the Default mode network, and increased FC in the precuneus and cingulate cortex (④) of the Sensorimotor network. Stronger P was positively correlated with FC in the right middle temporal gyrus (⑤) and right frontal pole (⑥) of the Default mode network. When the character trait of SD was dominant, the FC of the right frontal pole (⑦) and the left orbitofrontal cortex (⑧), among the Visual network, was increased.

Conclusion

The present study allowed us to observe a representative neural network from the data of 81 healthy subjects. Individual differences in the degree of FC in the neural network may be related to the individual characteristics of the subject, such as temperament and personality.

The minor hemisphere is believed to process socioemotional competence, stress coping skills, and self-regulation. In the present study, we found a significant association with congenital temperament in the minor hemisphere. In addition, the frontal lobe of the dominant hemisphere controls important functions such as motivation, judgment, and planning. In the present study, the dominant hemisphere was found to be associated with acquired personality.

Investigation of biological factors of temperament and personality, as in the present study, will contribute to the elucidation of the pathophysiology of pre-psychiatric personality and personality.
