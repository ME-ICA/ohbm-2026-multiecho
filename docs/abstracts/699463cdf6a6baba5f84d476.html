Introduction

Complex discourse production entails the use of suprasentential, generative language and places high demands on planning, organization and cognitive flexibility, making it sensitive to subtle cognitive and neural differences.^1 Previous studies have shown that discourse-level measures are sensitive to early cognitive changes,^2 however, how acoustic features reflect discourse performance and related cognitive functions, and how this relationship is supported by underlying neural mechanisms, remain unclear. Acoustic speech features—including prosodic, articulatory, and vocal quality features—provide quantitative markers of speech production processes that may index discourse-level performance.^3 Importantly, these acoustic–behavioral relationships may not be uniform across individuals but may depend on the integrity of frontal brain systems involved in higher-order speech production.^4 This study investigated how acoustic speech features are related to discourse production performance during a complex speech task, and whether these relationships are modulated by frontal cortical activation and functional connectivity.

Methods

Ninety-three participants completed a complex elicited discourse task (“Trip to Jeju”), during which continuous speech and functional Near Infra-Red Spectroscopy (fNIRS) data were acquired simultaneously. Twenty-seven acoustic features including prosodic, articulatory, and vocal quality features were extracted from speech recordings using a feature extraction pipeline optimized for clinical speech analysis. Discourse performance was quantified using standardized ratings of length, complexity, quality, irrelevance and verbosity.^1 Partial correlation analyses were conducted to examine associations between acoustic features and discourse scores while controlling for demographic covariates, followed by Lasso regression to identify key acoustic predictors. To test whether neural factors shape these acoustic–behavioral associations, moderation analyses were performed using linear regression models including interaction terms between acoustic features and fNIRS measures. Regional activation was quantified using task-related changes in oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO) estimated using a general linear model with β coefficients extracted from bilateral dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, ventrolateral prefrontal cortex, frontopolar cortex, and orbitofrontal cortex. Functional connectivity analysis was performed using graph-theoretical metrics to examine network-level interactions among frontal regions during the discourse task.

Results

Moderation analyses revealed that frontal cortical activation significantly shaped acoustic–discourse relationships. HbO activation in the DLPFC, VLPFC, and FPC showed significant interaction effects with key acoustic features, altering both the strength and direction of their associations with discourse scores (all interaction ps < .05). Functional connectivity metrics demonstrated significant moderation effects on acoustic–discourse relationships, particularly for articulatory spectral and prosodic features. Robust interaction effects were observed for MFCC-based measures and pitch-related features, including fundamental frequency (f0) (all ps < .05), whereas moderation effects for vocal quality features such as jitter were less consistent. Higher global network efficiency attenuated the negative associations between acoustic instability and discourse performance, indicating a buffering effect of efficient frontal network organization during complex speech production.

Conclusion

These findings demonstrate that acoustic features are closely linked to discourse-level performance during complex speech production, and that these relationships are not fixed but depend on frontal cortical activation and network organization. Functional connectivity, particularly global network efficiency, modulated the impact of articulatory spectral and prosodic features—especially MFCC- and pitch-related measures—on discourse outcomes, whereas vocal quality features such as jitter showed less consistent modulation. Higher network efficiency attenuated the negative associations between acoustic instability and discourse performance, indicating a buffering effect of efficient frontal network integration. Together, these results suggest that acoustic markers of discourse performance reflect not purely peripheral speech motor processes^5 but a neural context–dependent expression shaped by higher-order frontal systems supporting the planning, monitoring, and integration of speech. Integrating speech acoustics with fNIRS-based activation and connectivity measures thus provides a multilevel framework for understanding the neural mechanisms underlying complex discourse and for improving the interpretability of speech-based cognitive markers.
