Introduction

Understanding how large-scale brain dynamics give rise to conscious experience remains a central question in neuroscience. Integrated Information Theory (IIT) provides a mathematical framework suggesting that consciousness depends on the brain’s capacity to generate information, quantified by Φ [1]. However, Φ is extremely difficult to compute directly since it requires the evaluation of every possible bipartition of the system; instead, various surrogate measures have been proposed.  Here, we apply one such approximation—geometric integrated information (Φ_G) [2]—to resting-state functional MRI data collected during wakefulness, propofol anesthesia, and recovery, a paradigm offering well-controlled and reversible transitions in conscious state.We aim to determine if the anticipated changes in Φ_G (decrease during anesthesia and increase during recovery) would be associated with disruptions in linear or in higher-order, nonlinear dynamics in the brain.

Methods

We reanalyzed an existing fMRI dataset from a prior IRB-approved study that enrolled 30 healthy adults (27 analyzed) who provided informed consent [3]. Participants completed eight 16-min scans (resting state, music listening, and squeeze-response tasks) spanning wakefulness, loss of responsiveness under propofol, and recovery. Only the resting-state scans were analyzed in the present study. MRI data were acquired on a 3T Philips scanner using high-resolution T1 imaging and multiband EPI for functional scans.

Cortical activity was modelled using a 100-parcel vector autoregressive model (VAR(1)), and Φ_G was estimated using three complementary approaches: (1) Geometric Φ_G original formulation [2]: for each possible bipartition, cross-partition interactions in the connectivity matrix were removed, the noise covariance was updated accordingly, and the increase in conditional entropy was computed. This approximates the information lost when the system is artificially “split”; (2) Time-series shuffling-derived Φ_G: time points within one side of a partition were permuted to disrupt both linear and nonlinear dependencies across the split. This provides an estimate of how integrated information changes when all statistical structure is removed; and (3) residual shuffling-derived Φ_G: after fitting the VAR(1) model, residuals within one side of a partition were shuffled while preserving the fitted linear couplings. This selectively removes beyond-linear structure, allowing us to isolate contributions that are not explained by linear dynamics alone. 

The minimum-information partition (MIP) was identified using a submodular, Queyranne-style heuristic that efficiently ranks candidate bipartitions [4]. This reduces computational complexity from IIT’s double-exponential scaling (2²^ᴺ) to a practical cubic complexity (O(N³)).

Results

Across all three methods— ΦG original formulation, residual shuffling, and time-series shuffling—Rest1, Rest2, and Rest3 showed a consistent ordering of  values –phi decreases significantly when transitioning from awake to anaesthesia and increases significantly when transitioning from anaesthesia to recovery. 

From comparing P values, residual shuffling produced the clearest and most statistically robust discrimination(Awake Vs Anaesthesia pFDR=0.006, Anaesthesia vs Recovery pFDR<0.0001) of the three states, outperforming time-series shuffling. Because residual shuffling disrupts only structure not captured by the linear model, these results suggest that the primary driver of Φ_G reductions under anesthesia is higher-order, nonlinear structure in large-scale brain dynamics. 

Although time-series shuffling also detected state differences, it introduced additional noise by disrupting both linear and nonlinear relationships simultaneously. Therefore, its P values are less robust. Still, the magnitudes of Phi Fluctuation are similar between both kinds of shuffling. This indicates the fluctuations in Phi come from a common source–in this case, we suggest that this source is the perturbation of higher-order, nonlinear structure in large-scale brain dynamics.

Conclusion

Geometric integrated information (Φ_G) reliably tracked conscious state transitions across all three algorithms, decreasing during propofol anesthesia and increasing during recovery. This implies that as consciousness fades, less information is being integrated, and the brain becomes less predictable.

Residual shuffling offered the strongest and most reliable separation between awake, anaesthetised, and recovery states, indicating that disruptions to higher-order structure beyond the linear VAR model are most relevant for explaining changes in Φ_G. Time-series shuffling revealed the same overall pattern but was less stable, likely due to the additional noise of perturbing linear activities. The similar magnitudes of Φ_G change across the two methods further support the interpretation that propofol primarily alters nonlinear information-integration mechanisms in the human cortex.
