Introduction

Distinguishing self-produced from externally generated touch is fundamental for coherent self-representation and social interaction,1 yet its neural mechanisms remain poorly understood. While predictive sensory attenuation has been well characterized in vision and audition, findings in the tactile domain—especially affective CT-targeted touch—remain inconsistent, with studies reporting attenuated, unchanged, or even enhanced neural responses during self-touch.2-3 Recent neuroimaging work suggests widespread cortical deactivation during self-administered touch, particularly in insular and salience-processing regions,1 but the modulatory factors underlying self–other tactile distinction remain largely unknown. Oxytocin (OT), a key neuropeptide regulating social salience and affiliative behavior, has been shown to enhance the hedonic value and neural correlates of CT-targeted touch. 4-5 But its modulatory effects on self- versus other-administered affective touch has not been tested. 

Methods

In a double-blind, placebo-controlled, within-subject fMRI design, 39 participants received oral OT (24 IU) or placebo (PLC, 24 IU) via a validated medicated-lollipop protocol. Forty-five minutes after administration, all subjects participated in a self–other affective touch paradigm where they were instructed to stroke their own forearm (self- touch) and receive CT-targeted stroking from a male or female experimenter (other-touch) unknown for the design aim (actually always from a female) and then rate their subjective pleasantness. Whole-brain univariate analyses (peak-level FDR-corrected, P < 0.05; and a priori mOFC ROI tested using small-volume correction with peak-level FWE-corrected, P < 0.05), task-dependent functional connectivity (gPPI; cluster-level FWE-corrected, P < 0.05), and multivoxel pattern analysis (MVPA: linear SVM with LOOCV; voxelwise bootstrapping = 5000 with FDR-corrected P < 0.05; permutation test = 10000) were used to determine how OT modulated neural responses to self- versus other-administered touch. Associations between treatment effects on behavioral and neural responses and individual autistic traits and interoception were additionally explored. 

Results

Consistent with predictions, oral OT increased the perceived pleasantness of other-administered touch (F(1, 38) = 5.30, P = 0.03, ƞ2p = 0.12), replicating prior evidence of enhanced hedonic value of CT-targeted affective touch. On the neural level, OT increased functional connectivity between orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) and anterior insula (AI) (39 / 3 / -9, t = 3.95, P FWE = 0.015), regions implicated in reward and social salience processing. Critically, OT decreased neural activations mainly in salience and interoception-related regions for other-relative to self-administered touch, including the AI (-30 / 9 / 6, t = 4.19, P FDR = 0.026), posterior insula (PI: 42 / -3 / 9, t = 4.67, P FDR = 0.026), and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC: 0 / 33 / 21, t = 3.81, P FDR = 0.031), and reduced the functional connectivity between the PI and posterior cingulate cortex (PCC, -24 /-69 / 33, t = 3.68, P FWE < 0.05). At the same time, MVPA discriminated self vs other touch with high accuracy under both OT (94.87% ± 2.50%, permutation P < 0.001) and PLC administration (96.15% ± 2.18%, permutation P < 0.001), while OT yielded fewer bootstrap-significant weighted voxels (bootstrapping = 5000; PFDR < 0.05), suggesting that OT possibly increased the efficiency of discriminating self- and other-administered touch. Exploratory analyses indicated OT-specific brain–behavior/trait associations (PLC ns), showing LAI and ACC activity differences negatively correlated with pleasantness differences (LAI: r = -0.31, P = 0.05; ACC: r = -0.34, P = 0.04); and that OT-related ACC and superior frontal gyrus (SFG) modulation associated positively with interoception (ACC: r = 0.31, P = 0.05) and autistic traits (SFG: r = 0.35, P = 0.03).

Conclusion

This study firstly showed oral OT modulated both the affective experience and neural representation of self- versus other-administered CT-targeted touch. OT enhanced other-touch pleasantness and reweighted reward/salience networks via altered mOFC–insula couping, while attenuated salience/interoception related region responses (i.e., AI, PI, ACC) to other-relative to self-touch and concurrently increasing the neural efficiency and network-level differentiation underlying tactile self–other discrimination. Together, these findings advance current models of oxytocin as a social salience modulator by demonstrating its capacity to reshape self–other boundaries in affective touch processing.
