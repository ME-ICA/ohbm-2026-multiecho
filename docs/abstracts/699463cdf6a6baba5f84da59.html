Introduction

Hypoxic-ischemic brain injury is the main contributor to death after blood flow to the brain stops during a cardiac arrest. The extent of microstructural damage and network disruption plays a central role in functional recovery, yet how these processes interact remain unclear. Apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) on MRI captures early cortical injury and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) can provide a sensitive marker of microstructural injury by detecting water diffusion in white matter. Understanding how cortical and white-matter injury overlaps and interacts may provide insights into which neural hubs are most injury after cardiac arrest and how that affects the process of neurological function recovery. This would provide a more comprehensive picture about regional cerebral resilience to hypoxic-ischemic brain injury and refine neurological prognostic models guiding earlier and more targeted interventions in cardiac arrest.

Here, we evaluate a multimodal network modeling framework linking region-wise ADC abnormalities with structural connectivity (SC) measures calculated on DTI this approach to identify which regional cortical injury and connectivity disruptions jointly explain outcome differences.

Methods

 All adult patients (n=59, age=69.2 ±19.4) were successfully resuscitated after a cardiac arrest and were in coma. The primary outcome was mortality, which was assessed at the time of hospital discharge. The brain MRI was done using a 3T Siemens Skyra scanner, and the DTI protocol included 64 gradient directions with b-values of 0 and 1000 s/mm2. For preprocessing, the raw images were converted to (.mif) format using mrconvert and denoised with dwidenoise [1]. Preprocessing included motion and eddy-current correction (dwifslpreproc) and bias field correction (dwibiascorrect). T1-weighted images were skull-stripped (mri-synthstrip) and segmented into five tissue types (5ttgen) [2]. The b0 volume was registered to T1 space using FSL FLIRT and was aligned to atlases and diffusion space. Fiber orientation distributions (FODs) were computed using MSMT-CSD, with response functions for WM, GM, and CSF estimated via the Dhollander algorithm [3]. Whole-brain tractography was performed with ACT (tckgen) seeding from the gray matter–white matter interface to generate 10 million streamlines, which were refined using SIFT2 (tcksift2) [4]. These refined streamlines were modeled using FSL BEDPOSTX, that estimates voxel-wise probability distributions following which probabilistic tractography was performed using FSL probtrax [1] by having large number of streamline samples for each voxel within the predefined automated anatomical labeling (AAL) atlas regions that was registered to diffusion space, and a ROI-to-ROI SC matrix was generated (fig 1.A) For measuring brain injury voxel-wise ADC maps were computed, it was spatially normalized to the MNI standard space using ANTs. Following normalization, regional segmentation was performed based on AAL atlas. For each anatomical region, the mean ADC value was calculated by averaging the voxel intensities within that region.

Results

Nineteen (32%) patients survived. For the surviving patients, the overall cortical injury burden was lower, i.e., the ADC values were higher (fig 1.B). Yeo-7 network-level statistical t-test comparison of ADC values shows significant spatial patterns of injury vulnerability in visual (p=9.61X10^-8) and somatomotor (SMN) regions (p=3.39X10^-7) (fig 1.B). The circular connectome diagram comparing structural connectivity patterns between patients who survived vs. those who died, showing that favorable outcomes are associated with more robust and symmetrically organized connections, particularly within the somatomotor and visual networks (VIS) (fig 2. A). The violin plots show surviving  patients exhibit consistently higher ADC (1,000-1,200 ×10⁻³ mm²/s) values compared to poor outcome patients(800-1,000 ×10⁻³ mm²/s ) across all seven functional brain networks: Ventral Attention Network (VAN), Somatomotor Network (SMN), Dorsal Attention Network (DAN), Limbic Network (LIM), Visual Network (VIS), Frontoparietal Network (FPN), and Default Mode Network (DMN) (fig 2.B). A canonical correlation analysis showed positive relationship between SC patterns and ADC measurements, indicating that preserved white matter connections overlap with healthier cortical regions, while disrupted connectivity associates with abnormal diffusion properties reflecting cortical injury (fig 2.C). Relative importance of each brain network in predicting clinical outcomes demonstrates that the SMN is the strongest predictor followed by VIS, with these two primary sensory-motor systems together accounting for most of the predictive power (fig 2.D). 

Conclusion

This multimodal brain network analysis reveals that structural connectivity patterns and regional cortical integrity overlap and are associated with survival from cardiac arrest. The significant canonical correlation between structural connectivity and ADC measurements reveals that white matter tract integrity and cortical microstructure are intrinsically coupled, demonstrating that brain injury propagates through both macrostructural network disruption (axonal damage, white matter lesions) and microstructural cellular pathology (cytotoxic edema, membrane failure). The dominance of the Somatomotor and Visual networks injury indicates that these highly metabolic regions may be more vulnerable to hypoxic-ischemic brain injury, contributing to poor outcomes. Future research should validate these findings in larger, multicenter cohorts and investigate whether longitudinal changes in ADC and structural connectivity can track recovery trajectories or identify critical time windows for intervention, ultimately enabling precision medicine approaches that tailor neuroprotective strategies and rehabilitation protocols to individual patients’ specific patterns of injury and network vulnerability.
