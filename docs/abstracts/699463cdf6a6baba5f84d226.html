Introduction

Cortical excitability reflects the readiness of neurons to respond to incoming signals, supporting the stability of neural dynamics, the tuning of perception, and efficient information processing. Its balance is maintained through regulatory mechanisms shaped by local and global biological constraints—including cortical morphology and genetic and neurochemical organization—ensuring that neither excitation nor inhibition dominates. Disruptions in this balance are implicated in several neuropsychiatric and neurological disorders, underscoring the importance of reliable excitability measurements.

Traditionally, excitability has been assessed using transcranial magnetic stimulation, which provides controlled perturbations but lacks whole-brain coverage. Auditory stimulation at 40 Hz offers a complementary proxy that engages widespread cortical regions and reflects GABAergic–glutamatergic dynamics. In parallel, stimulus-independent approaches have emerged, using intrinsic electromagnetic activity—such as aperiodic slope, entropy, and gamma-band synchronization—to estimate excitability across the cortex. However, these intrinsic measures are typically examined in isolation and rarely validated against each other or against stimulation-based metrics.

To address this gap, we systematically evaluated stimulation-based and intrinsic excitability measures in a large MEG dataset collected at rest and during 40 Hz stimulation. Our hypothesis is that these different measures can be divergent, exhibiting a regional, architecture and functional specificity. To accomplish this, we tested whether they spatially converge, whether their similarity varies across cortical regions and networks, and whether they capture distinct biological dimensions linked to structural and neurochemical features. This integrated framework clarifies what different excitability measures reflect and advances their potential as biomarkers of brain function and dysfunction.

Methods

Participants and Experimental Design

The study was approved by the Ethics Board of the Province of Venice, and all participants provided written informed consent. We recruited healthy adults with normal hearing, no MRI contraindications, no neuropsychiatric history, and no neuroactive medications. Fifty-three individuals (40 female, mean age = 29.5 ± 6.9) completed 72 whole-head MEG recordings obtained at rest and during 40 Hz auditory stimulation. All participants also underwent structural MRI. Part of this dataset was previously analyzed¹.

MEG Recording

MEG was recorded in a shielded room using a 275-channel CTF system (1200 Hz sampling). Eye movements and EKG were monitored, and head position was continuously tracked. Participants lay supine with eyes closed. Resting-state and 40 Hz stimulation runs (6 min each, randomized) were acquired. Stimuli consisted of a 40 Hz amplitude-modulated 1000 Hz tone (MATLAB-generated), delivered binaurally at 85 dB in 1-s ON / 1-s OFF trials (180 total). The sound was simultaneously recorded on an analog MEG channel to ensure precise timing for gamma synchrony analyses.

MEG Preprocessing

Preprocessing in Brainstorm included: third-order gradient noise cancellation, resampling to 600 Hz, notch filters at 50/100/150/200 Hz, 1 Hz high-pass filtering, and cardiac artifact removal via SSP. Stimulation data were epoched (−1.5 to 1.5 s) and visually inspected for artifact rejection.

Cortical Source Modelling

T1-weighted MRIs were acquired with a 1.5 T Philips Achieva scanner. Segmentation was performed with CAT12. Cortical and head surfaces were reconstructed and coregistered to MEG sensors using digitized landmarks. Forward modelling used overlapping spheres, and inverse modelling used a weighted minimum-norm estimate with dipoles constrained normal to the surface.

Excitability Measures

Ten excitability measures were computed in source space and parcellated into 100 Schaefer regions.

Stimulus-related:

- 40Hz: Inter-Trial Phase Consistency (ITPC; 39–41 Hz), higher values reflecting stronger gamma synchronization.

- 40Hz_Zscore: ITPC z-scores relative to a −500 to −200 ms baseline, averaged 300–700 ms post-stimulus.

Resting-state–based:

- Phase_Ex: High-gamma (55–95 Hz) spatial phase synchronization.

- SE: Sample entropy (lower = higher excitability).

- 1/f exponent and offset: From the aperiodic PSD (10–40 Hz); flatter slopes indicate higher excitation.

- DFA: Alpha-band detrended-fluctuation analysis; higher values correspond to increased excitability.

- Alpha-based measures: Absolute alpha power (AbsoluteAlpha), relative alpha power (AlphaRelative), and periodic alpha (FOOOF_Alpha). Higher alpha reflects lower excitability.

Figure 1 displays the group-average brain maps for each of these measures.

Clustering Analysis

Spearman correlations among all metric pairs produced a 10×10 similarity matrix per scan. After averaging across scans, a distance matrix (D = 1 − R) was clustered using average linkage. Cluster centroids were obtained by averaging ranked parcel values. ANOVA F-statistics quantified each region’s contribution to cluster separability.

Spatial Alignment with Cortical Thickness

Because cortical structure influences excitability1, we computed Spearman correlations between individual cortical-thickness maps and the individual-level expressions of each excitability cluster. A one-sample t-test assessed whether these correlation distributions differed from zero.

Receptor Density Analysis

To assess biological grounding of the observed clusters, we used whole-brain PET-derived receptor maps2, also parcellated to the 100-region Schaefer atlas, for primary neurotransmitters involved in excitability regulation. These included α4β2 and M1 acetylcholine receptors, VAChT (acetylcholine transporter), NMDA and mGluR5 glutamate receptors, and GABA_A receptors. Spatial correlations between cluster centroids and receptor maps were computed with spin-permutation testing3 (Spearman; 10,000 permutations).

Results

Hierarchical clustering of the ten excitability measures yielded a solution that appeared to be compatible with six clusters (Figure 2A). These clusters captured different families of excitability indices: (i) the 40Hz cluster, comprising all stimulus-driven measures; (ii) the Alpha cluster, including all three alpha-based metrics; (iii) the DFA cluster; (iv) the Exponent cluster, consisting of both aperiodic slope features; (v) Phase_Ex; and (vi) SE. Centroid brain maps, computed as the average spatial ranks within each cluster, were used for all subsequent analyses.

At the regional level, the largest separability values among the six clusters particularly involved regions in the centro-right temporal cortex, which are typically responsive to 40 Hz stimulation, as well as the temporal pole, the occipital cortex, and the medial and dorsolateral prefrontal cortices (Figure 2B). These regions were primarily part of the limbic, default-mode, executive function and visual-periphery network systems.

The relationship between excitability as measured by our six clusters and brain structure is depicted in Figure 2C. Significant positive correlations with cortical thickness were observed for 40Hz, Alpha, and SE clusters, suggesting that thicker cortex expressed higher excitability for these metrics. In contrast, DFA and Exponent clusters showed significant negative correlations. Phase_Ex showed no significant association (p = 0.11).

Finally, Figure 2D shows the neurochemical profile of each cluster after associating them with neurotransmitter maps typically involved in excitation/inhibition. VAChT and NMDA receptor maps significantly correlated with the clusters Phase_Ex (ρ = 0.38 and ρ = 0.54) and 40Hz (ρ = 0.59 and ρ = 0.51), indicating shared cholinergic and glutamatergic contributions. The α4β2 acetylcholine receptor showed significant but opposite correlations with Alpha (ρ = 0.65) and DFA (ρ = –0.63). No other associations survived multiple-comparison correction.

Conclusion

In this work, we demonstrated that typically used measures of cortical excitability, despite being derived from the same neural signals, can diverge markedly. We first showed this by clustering these measures based on their whole-brain excitability pattern, yielding distinct clusters with limited mutual correspondence—suggesting either limited measurement precision or, more likely, that these measures capture different and potentially complementary aspects of brain excitability.

Our separability analysis further showed that functional brain organization differentiating excitability concentrates on limbic, executive, and default-mode network systems, all of which are implicated in sensory and higher-order information processing.

In addition, no definitive conclusion can be drawn regarding whether structural underpinnings such as increased cortical thickness preferentially sustain inhibitory or excitatory responses, as this relationship depends on how excitability is defined. Interestingly, Phase_Ex, which is defined in the gamma band, appears to be the only excitability measure not supported by cortical structure.

Finally, our receptor-density analyses demonstrated that different excitability measures are supported by distinct chemoarchitectural profiles, suggesting that each may also be linked to different functional architectures.

In sum, our findings indicate that excitability measures group into biologically coherent clusters that differ in anatomical specificity, structural coupling, and receptor-level associations, providing a more nuanced understanding of excitation–inhibition organization in the human cortex.
