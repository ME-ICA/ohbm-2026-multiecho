Introduction

Anorexia nervosa (AN) is a serious psychiatric condition, in which affected individuals struggle with restrictive food intake that persists despite extreme weight loss. This cardinal feature of AN may reflect alterations in the neural mechanisms that encode the value of food reward. Traditional behavioural assays of reward-related cognition (e.g., decision-making tasks) have begun to elucidate perturbations in corresponding neural circuits in AN 1; however, these tasks lack ecological validity, and naturalistic paradigms (e.g., movies) can address this limitation by evoking complex cognition in contexts that reflect one’s daily life. Movies provide a valuable tool for examining the neural mechanisms that subserve various aspects of AN symptomatology, including altered valuation of food-related stimuli when cooking, during mealtimes, or in social situations centered around eating. Here, we tested if movie-watching can improve detection of altered brain connectivity patterns in women with AN as compared to task-based functional MRI. Women with AN were hypothesised to have altered functional connectivity (FC) of medial frontal, limbic, and default mode networks relative to controls, which would be further increased in response to naturalistic stimuli.

Methods

Women with acute AN (n = 31) and healthy women (n = 32) underwent fMRI scanning on a Siemens Vida 3 Tesla scanner following an overnight fast (see Figure 1). During the scan, participants performed a validated instrumental learning task 2 and watched two movies, depicting either food or social reward. On each trial of the learning task, participants were instructed to choose between two cues with the aim of maximising the amount of money earned throughout the task, where cues were probabilistically associated with either wins, losses or neutral outcomes. Participants then viewed two ~10-minute movies, which included scenes of either food or social reward in a counterbalanced order. After the scan, participants rated the greatest degree of positive and negative emotions that they experienced while watching the movies. Functional neuroimaging data were preprocessed in AFNI using the afni_proc.py pipeline, and we generated connectomes for each functional run per participant using the 268-node Shen atlas 3 and AFNI’s 3dNetCorr program with Fisher’s Z-transformation. Group differences in whole-brain functional connectivity were examined using Network-Based Statistics (component-determining threshold = 2.6, one-tailed, FWE-corrected, K = 1000 permutations). We tested group differences in emotion ratings using linear mixed-effects models in R (version 4.3.1, package nlme), where random intercepts for reward type (food vs social) were nested within the random effect of the participant. Behavioral results were Bonferroni corrected for ten tests (p-value = .05/10 = .005).

Results

Women with AN reported significantly greater disgust (B(SE) = 1.60(0.31), p < .001) and sadness (B(SE) = 1.00(0.34), p = .004) during movie-watching as compared to the control group, and a group-by-movie interaction effect was not significant, suggesting that emotional responses did not differ across reward types (Figure 2A). Positive affective ratings were correspondingly lower amongst patients, who felt less happy (B(SE) = -1.58(0.40), p = .0002) and calm (B(SE) = -1.26(0.43), p = .0049) relative to controls. At the neural level, the AN group had reduced connectivity during performance of the instrumental learning task across one sparse component (859 edges, p = .05), which included high-degree nodes in the left anterior cingulate cortex and bilateral insula. Moreover, the AN group had widespread reductions in functional connectivity relative to controls when viewing naturalistic food and social reward stimuli. When viewing the food movie, patients had reduced connectivity in one component (3159 edges, p = .01), with the somatomotor (38.8%), salience (13.8%), and frontoparietal (17.8%) networks comprising the majority of edges (Figure 2B). Within this component, left orbitofrontal cortex, left anterior cingulate cortext, pons, and left supramarginal gyrus had the highest nodal degree. Similarly, we observed reduced connectivity in the AN group relative to controls across one component (5121 edges, p = .008) during the social reward movie (Figure 2C). High-degree nodes in this component also spanned regions that have been previously implicated in reward processing (e.g., left anterior cingulate cortex, left insula), as well as social cognition (e.g., left posterior middle temporal gyrus).

Conclusion

Our findings extend evidence of altered neural coding of reward in patients with AN, identifying reductions in functional connectivity during performance of a validated monetary learning task and when viewing naturalistic rewards. Although emotion ratings did not differ significantly between movies, dysphoric responses to reward amongst patients were associated with qualitatively different patterns of functional (dys)connectivity relative to the control group. Women with AN had lower connectivity across a relatively sparse component when viewing scenes that involved cooking and eating, where high degree nodes converged on putative reward regions (e.g., anterior cingulate and orbitofrontal cortex). However, connectivity alterations in response to social reward spanned broad swaths of the somatomotor and salience networks, including portions of the posterior temporal lobe and superior frontal gyrus. Such alterations may explain difficulties processing advantageous social signals in individuals with AN 4, which has been linked to lower self-esteem, body dissatisfaction, and social withdrawal that can impede recovery. Both behavioural and neural results suggest that individuals with AN experience broad reductions in reward sensitivity as opposed to food-specific alterations 5, which could inform evidence-based interventions for this serious illness. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that naturalistic paradigms have the potential to delineate neural mechanisms of AN that may be undetected with traditional task-based approaches.
