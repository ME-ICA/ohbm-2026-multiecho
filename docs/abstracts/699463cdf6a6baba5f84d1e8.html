Introduction

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is critical for neurodevelopmental research, however the high cost of high-field (HF) scanners (∼$1,000,000 per Tesla)1, along with corresponding infrastructural requirements, result in significant access inequalities across the globe. Ultra-low-field (ULF) imaging systems (<0.1T) present a potential solution to issues of access inequality; by relying on large, permanent magnets, they are significantly cheaper and easier to purchase and run than HF systems. Unfortunately, these benefits come at the cost of reduced signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and resolution, which renders images suboptimal for visual reading by radiologists or for many currently available processing toolkits. Super-resolution (SR) techniques may help bridge the gap between ULF and HF, with deep learning being particularly well-suited to handle the nonlinear differences in tissue contrast between field strengths. Here we introduce GAMBAS+ (Generalised-Hilbert Mamba Super-resolution) as an extension of GAMBAS2, our earlier SR model, displaying improved performance and greater robustness to varying input images.

Methods

GAMBAS+ was trained as an adversarial model, using PatchGAN as the discriminator3. It contains the same base components as GAMBAS , however it is supplemented with two additional encoder heads and a gating network, allowing it to process up to three separate input images (Figure 1). MRI data used to train GAMBAS+ stem from a study investigating the neurodevelopment of executive function over the first 3 years of life in participants based in South Africa. A total of 87 subjects (49 Male, 38 Female; mean age 9.59±6.67 months) had isotropic HF T2w scans (1×1×1mm) acquired using a Siemens 3T scanner, and non-isotropic ULF T2w scans (1.5×1.5×5mm) acquired using a Hyperfine Swoop 64 mT system. For each subject, three ULF scans were acquired with high in-plane resolution along three orthogonal planes (axial, sagittal, coronal); as such, each matched 'pair' included three ULF and one HF scan, resulting in a total of 348 images. The data were split into training/test sets of 69/18 paired scans (i.e. ∼80/20%), balancing for age and sex, and our model was trained for 1000 epochs on a Nvidia L40S 48GB GPU. During training, one or two of the ULF scans were randomly dropped out, forcing the model to be robust to 1, 2, or all 3 inputs. To assess the quality of output images relative to ground-truth HF images, we compute structural similarity index measure (SSIM), peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR), mean absolute error (MAE) and normalised root mean squared error (NRMSE). We benchmark these results against one non-deep learning based SR technique: Multi-resolution registration (MRR)4, and two other deep learning-based SR models: SuperSynth5 and GAMBAS. Significance testing of within-subject image-quality metrics and Dice overlap is carried out via the Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

Results

From our comparative analyses, we demonstrate that GAMBAS+ significantly enhances the visual quality of MRI scans compared to both input ULF images and competing SR methods. We highlight this with improved performance in all image quality metrics, with GAMBAS+ outperforming MRR, SuperSynth, and GAMBAS regardless of the number of input images fed into the model (Figure 2). In the single-input setting, GAMBAS+ outperforms MRR and SuperSynth and has comparable performance to GAMBAS, whereas in the multi-input setting (2 or 3 scans), GAMBAS+ outperforms all competing methods.

Conclusion

Our novel GAMBAS+ network presents an effective and reliable method for translation of ULF to HF MRI, underscoring the potential of deep learning tools in widening accessibility to MRI in resource-limited settings. We demonstrate improved performance compared to other state-of-the-art methods, highlighting the benefits conferred by our revised architecture and our 'drop-out' training scheme. The latter innovation is of particular value, as tolerance to long scanning times is low in infants, making it often infeasible to acquire three separate ULF scans of satisfactory quality. Consequently, having a model capable of producing outputs with any number or combination of input images minimises the need to exclude participants if any one or two of their ULF images contain artefacts (a likely scenario in paediatric subjects).
