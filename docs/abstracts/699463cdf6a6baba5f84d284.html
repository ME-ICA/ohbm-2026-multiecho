Introduction

Driven by advancements in neuroimaging, neuroscience is undergoing a shift from traditional anatomy toward the precise exploration of functional networks and connectomics. Elucidating the brain’s organizational architecture and its evolutionary trajectories is crucial for unraveling human cognition and establishing prerequisites for the targeted treatment of neuropsychiatric disorders. Due to ethical and experimental constraints, critical invasive studies cannot be performed in humans, making non-human primate models indispensable for translational research.

The squirrel monkey, serving as a phylogenetic bridge between New and Old World monkeys, offers unique translational value due to its experimental tractability and capacity for complex behavioral training. Yet, the foundational anatomical tools for this species are critically obsolete. Current references rely solely on histological atlases from the 1960s, which critically lack essential white matter connectivity information and are incompatible with the spatial normalization required for modern 3D digital analysis. This limitation forms the core bottleneck hindering deep connectomic research in the species.

To overcome this, we established the first modernized, connectivity-driven, group-level digital brain atlas of the squirrel monkey using high-resolution Diffusion Tensor Imaging (DTI). This atlas directly addresses the deficiencies of traditional resources by providing a precise "digital coordinate system." It will serve as a robust, quantitative foundation for analyzing the squirrel monkey’s fine-grained brain structure, integrating cross-modal data (e.g., fMRI), and advancing rigorous cross-species comparative studies, thus unlocking its potential as a pivotal translational model.

Methods

Preprocessing of the diffusion MRI data was primarily implemented using the FSL software library. Initially, the raw data underwent eddy current correction using FSL’s eddy algorithms. The non-diffusion-weighted images were then extracted to generate brain masks. For skull stripping, although initial masks were derived using FSL’s Brain Extraction Tool, this automatic algorithm—optimized for human MRI—often yielded inaccuracies in the squirrel monkey brain, particularly in the orbitofrontal and ocular regions. Consequently, to ensure high-quality brain extraction, all masks were subjected to rigorous visual inspection and manual correction using ITK-SNAP. Following this procedure, diffusion tensor fitting was performed to reconstruct the tensor model, from which scalar indices, including Fractional Anisotropy (FA) and Mean Diffusivity (MD), were derived. Finally, to characterize underlying fiber populations and resolve crossing fibers, fiber orientation distributions were estimated using the Bedpostx framework (Figure 1A).

The DTI template construction process was performed on the preprocessed diffusion data. Prior to registration, it was necessary to ensure that the diffusion tensor volumes were converted from FSL’s eigenvalue/eigenvector outputs into the DTI-TK, which contains six independent components. Template creation employed an iterative, hierarchical registration strategy. First, an initial DTI template was bootstrapped, either by calculating the mean Euclidean distance or by selecting a high-quality subject. Subsequently, all subject tensor volumes underwent sequential rigid and affine alignment to this initial target. Brain masks were generated based on the affine alignment results to define the processing space. Finally, deformable alignment iteratively refined and averaged the deformation fields, ultimately producing a spatially unbiased and connectivity-driven, fine-grained DTI group template (Figure 1B).

The definition of the initial seed regions of interest (ROIs) in the squirrel monkey utilized a multi-reference protocol. Parcellation was primarily conducted on the gray matter structures of the group template, using sulci and gyri as the principal anatomical landmarks. During delineation, we referenced not only existing squirrel monkey atlases but also atlases from other species, such as macaques and marmosets, to leverage their detailed anatomical knowledge. Furthermore, previously published squirrel monkey sectional anatomical plates were employed to aid in verification and precise localization. Crucially, boundaries defined by cytoarchitectural studies were adopted as complementary evidence to finalize the accurate delineation of cortical regions (Figure 1C).

The connectivity-based parcellation workflow for the squirrel monkey was designed to generate a refined, group-level atlas. Initially, to enable individualized analysis, the predefined initial ROIs from the group template were nonlinearly registered to each subject’s native space using the ANTs software package. Subsequently, leveraging these individualized ROIs, whole-brain tractography was performed from every voxel within each seed region using FSL’s probabilistic tractography tool, thereby constructing whole brain connectivity matrices that served as clustering features (Figure 1D). Spectral clustering, implemented in the ATPP software, was then applied to parcellate the initial ROIs within each subject. To determine the optimal number of clusters K, a critical step in the clustering process, multiple validity indices provided by the ATPP software were utilized to identify the most suitable K value for each initial ROI (Figure 1E). Finally, the parcellation results from all subjects were transformed back to the template space, averaged to generate a group-level parcellation map, and subjected to a 26-neighborhood smoothing operation, resulting in the final connectivity-based atlas with fine-grained subregions (Figure 1F).

Results

The present study successfully constructed a fine-grained digital brain atlas of the squirrel monkey, encompassing both cortical and subcortical structures. The initial anatomical framework was established by defining 30 cortical initial ROIs (Figure 2A) and 14 subcortical initial ROIs (Figure 2B). Subsequently, the connectivity-based group parcellation strategy was employed, where the optimal number of clusters K was determined using multiple validity indices and by strictly considering the symmetry of parcellation results across the left and right hemispheres. This comprehensive approach yielded a refined, group-level atlas. The final connectivity-based squirrel monkey atlas comprises a total of 130 cortical subregions (Figure 2C) and 46 subcortical subregions (Figure 2D). This detailed parcellation reveals complex functional heterogeneity within the squirrel monkey's cortical mantle and subcortical nuclei, providing a robust foundation for subsequent precise neuroscientific investigations.

Conclusion

We successfully constructed the first connectivity-based parcellation of a group-level digital brain atlas for the squirrel monkey. Our rigorous methodology included MRI preprocessing with manual skull stripping, unbiased iterative template creation using DTI-TK, and initial ROI definition guided by gross anatomy and cytoarchitecture. Utilizing an optimized Connectivity-Based Parcellation strategy, the atlas was ultimately refined into 130 cortical subregions and 46 subcortical subregions (building upon 30 initial cortical regions and 14 initial subcortical regions). By overcoming the limitations of traditional histological atlases regarding digital and connectivity information, this atlas serves as a standardized, high-precision, and quantifiable framework. It is poised to significantly enhance the research value of the squirrel monkey as a translational model, particularly in comparative neuroscience, pharmacological studies, and neuropsychiatric disease modeling.

Despite the irreplaceable utility of the group template constructed from in vivo data for tissue segmentation and voxel-based statistical analysis, its inherent spatial resolution and tissue contrast remain inferior to those obtained from high-quality ex vivo imaging, thereby limiting the visibility of certain fine neuroanatomical details. Generally, imaging research in non-human primates like the squirrel monkey faces more constraints in both operational complexity and resource demands compared to human neuroimaging. Specifically, small-animal imaging presents unique acquisition challenges such as noise, motion artifacts, and anesthesia effects; furthermore, there is a persistent lack of standardized atlases, mature analytical tools, and comprehensive processing pipelines tailored for these species. While human research benefits from diverse template systems and extensive software ecosystems, the existing squirrel monkey atlases cannot yet fully satisfy advanced research needs. The atlas established here, based on current in vivo data, serves as a crucial foundation. However, achieving a level capable of resolving finer anatomy and supporting complex analytical pipelines will necessitate the accumulation of higher-resolution data, the integration of multi-modal imaging, and dedicated tool development fostered through community collaboration.
