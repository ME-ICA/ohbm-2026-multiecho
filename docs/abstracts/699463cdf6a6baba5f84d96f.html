Introduction

Word problems are essential in the transition from arithmetical to algebraic reasoning, where students must move from concrete computation to expressing relationships between quantities symbolically1. In this kind of word problems, there are errors when translating from natural language to algebraic language, the most studied being the so-called reversal error (RE). This error occurs when students correctly identify the data in comparison word problems but invert the comparative relationship when translating it into algebra, switching the variables in the equation2. Previous studies show that the RE is usually associated with bilateral activation patterns in fronto-parietal networks due to greater cognitive effort in solving the problems3 (see figure 1 in Ventura-Campos et al, 2022). In this study, we evaluate whether learning aimed at eliminating the RE, using a gamified interactive educational tool (G-IET)4 is able to modify activation patterns in participants who made this error and improve their competence in comparative word problems.

Methods

In this study, participants were 35 healthy right-handed students at the University Jaume I (Castellón, Spain) with ages ranging from 18 to 26 years. These participants were divided into two groups: the RE group, n=17 (11 women), consisting of participants who committed the reversal error and trained for 3 weeks with the 8 levels of the G-IET called RE-coach, and the NonRE group, n=18 (11 women), an active control group formed by participants without the reversal error who completed the same amount of practice time but using a different tool focused on spatial skills.

Participants performed a reversal error (RE) task3 inside the fMRI scanner both before and after training. The task comprised experimental and control conditions and included 48 trials (24 per condition) distributed across twelve randomized blocks. Each trial consisted of a fixation period (1 s), presentation of a word comparison problem in Spanish (8 s), and an equation to be judged as correct or incorrect (3 s; 50% correct). Responses were given manually using a button grip, with response–hand mapping counterbalanced across participants to control for motor-related activation.

Experimental statements manipulated three factors: operation type (additive vs. multiplicative), comparison direction (increasing vs. decreasing), and the presence or absence of contextual cues, yielding eight item types. Incorrect equations reflected reversal errors. Control statements matched the experimental structure but allowed a direct, linear translation from sentence to equation, thereby eliminating reversal errors. This subtraction-based design enabled isolation of neural activity specifically associated with reversal error processing. Participants received standardized instructions and completed a practice session with control trials prior to scanning.

Structural and functional MRI was collected with a 3T scanner (SIGNA Architect, GE Healthcare) and a 24-channel head coil. High-resolution anatomical images were acquired with T1 MP-RAGE sequence (1 mm of slice thickness; TR = 2.5 s; TE = 2 ms; FA = 8°). 

Functional T2*-weighted data was acquired with multiband gradient echo-planar imaging sequence (50 axial slices collected in ascendent interleaved order; 304 volumes; 2.5 mm of slice thickness; TR = 2 s; TE = 20.4 ms; FA = 90°; multiband acceleration factor = 2).

The data were preprocessed with fMRIPrep (citation) and statistical analyses were conducted with SPM25. At the first level, we modeled the RE-task contrast of interest (experimental > control), including motion realignment parameters derived from fMRIPrep as covariates of no interest..A one-sample t-test was performed to examine task activation for each group, and a flexible factorial analysis was used to examine longitudinal learning-related differences by comparing pre- to post-training changes between the RE-group and the Non-RE group[(RE group (post–pre) > NO_RE group (post–pre)]..  Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, with family-wise error (FWE) cluster-level correction for multiple comparisons (voxel-level uncorrected threshold p < 0.001 with a critical cluster size).

Results

Before the intervention, the RE group showed a bilateral pattern in fronto-parietal regions and marked activation in the supplementary motor area/superior frontal gyrus/cingulate gyrus, which is consistent with less automatized processing and a greater need for executive control and attention to resolve the semantic ambiguity of comparative relations in the word comparison problems. After the training with RE-Coach G-IET, the RE group exhibited a clear shift toward left lateralization in fronto-parietal areas, similar to the pattern found in the Non-RE group in previous studies.
The longitudinal analysis showed greater activation of the anterior cingulate cortex, a region involved in error detection, inhibitory control, and conflict resolution. 

Conclusion

The results show that RE-Coach G-IET not only improves performance in comparative mathematical word problems and eliminates the RE, but also modifies the brain activation patterns associated with this error. The shift from a bilateral pattern to a left lateralization in fronto-parietal areas, reflects greater specialization in semantic processing and algebraic automatization. This reorganization suggests more efficient processing based on linguistic analysis and algebraic representation. In addition to this expected change, a bilateral activation of the caudate nucleus appeared, typical of feedback-based learning, planning, and progressive response adjustment, highly consistent with the dynamics of the gamified elements of the training tool.5 
Finally, the increased activation of the anterior cingulate after the training suggests that, although they improve, participants in the RE group still employ additional executive control to avoid falling into the incorrect response.Overall, these findings support the use of RE-Coach as a useful tool for intervening in systematic errors in comparative algebraic-mathematical reasoning.
