Introduction

Food preferences and the mechanisms underlying food-related decision-making are a central topic in neuroscience, economics, and consumer research. Within the neuroeconomics framework, which integrates behavioral economics and neuroimaging, researchers aim to understand how subjective value is computed and translated into economic choices.^1 Neuroimaging studies have been particularly informative in identifying the neural substrates of food valuation.

Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) research has demonstrated that willingness-to-pay (WTP)—the maximum price an individual is willing to pay for a product—is encoded in prefrontal brain regions. Seminal studies showed that WTP for food items is associated with activity in the medial orbitofrontal cortex and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex,^2 and that both appetitive and aversive goal values during food-related decisions are represented in prefrontal valuation regions using an incentive-compatible Becker–DeGroot–Marschak auction.^3 Additional work has linked bid magnitude to ventromedial prefrontal cortex activity across valuation contexts.^4

Despite its high spatial resolution, fMRI has substantial practical limitations, motivating the evaluation of alternative neuroimaging methods. Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) offers a portable and cost-effective approach for measuring cortical hemodynamic responses in frontal brain regions and has been shown to exhibit close correspondence with fMRI across multiple cognitive tasks.^5 However, the extent to which fNIRS can replicate established fMRI findings in food-related WTP paradigms remains insufficiently explored. The present study tested whether fNIRS can capture prefrontal neural correlates of value-based food bidding previously identified using fMRI.

Methods

Forty right-handed adults (mean age 25.9 years, SD = 9.4) participated in the study. All participants had normal vision and no history of psychiatric, neurological, metabolic, or eating disorders; none were on a diet or had specialized education related to food. Participants occasionally consumed the types of food presented in the experiment. They fasted for 4 hours prior to the session and were informed that after the experiment they would remain in the laboratory for 30 minutes and could only eat food obtained during the task. All participants provided written informed consent.

Participants completed a willingness-to-pay task involving 60 trials with familiar sweet and salty snack food items. The task employed a Becker–DeGroot–Marschak auction. In the free-bid condition (30 trials), participants viewed food images for 6 seconds and placed bids ranging from 0 to 75 rubles in 5-ruble increments. In the forced-bid condition (30 trials), the same food images were presented together with a predetermined price, which participants were required to indicate on the bid scale. Trial order was randomized. At the end of the experiment, one trial was randomly selected and implemented according to standard auction rules.

fNIRS data were acquired over the frontal cortex using a NIRScout system with a 16×12 montage (41 source–detector pairs; 30-mm distance) at wavelengths of 758 and 840 nm and a sampling rate of 3.9 Hz. Data were analyzed using SPM-fNIRS following the SPM12 framework. Light intensity was converted to hemoglobin concentration changes using the modified Beer–Lambert law. Preprocessing included motion artifact correction, removal of cardiac and respiratory noise, downsampling, detrending, and filtering. Channel locations were projected to MNI space. Both whole-montage and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex region-of-interest analyses were conducted. A general linear model included predictors for all combinations of bidding condition (free vs forced) and price level (low, medium, high).

Results

General linear model analysis revealed a significant difference between free and forced bidding conditions. Free bids were associated with significantly greater prefrontal oxygenation compared to forced bids (t = 2.70, df = 37, cluster-level FWE-corrected p = .025), with peak activation at MNI coordinates [20, 75, 41]. This peak was localized to the right middle frontal gyrus, corresponding to Brodmann area 46 within the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex.

No significant effects were observed for contrasts examining differences between price levels, nor were there significant interactions between bidding condition and price level. Thus, the primary neural effect detected by fNIRS reflected the distinction between free and forced bidding rather than variation in bid magnitude.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that fNIRS can detect prefrontal cortical differences between internally generated and externally constrained food valuation decisions. The increased activation of the right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex during free relative to forced bidding closely corresponds to prefrontal effects previously reported in fMRI studies of willingness-to-pay for food. By replicating this key neural distinction using fNIRS, the present findings provide evidence that fNIRS is a viable complementary method for studying economic valuation processes in food decision-making.

Although fNIRS is limited in its ability to measure activity in deeper brain structures, such as the ventromedial prefrontal cortex, it successfully captured dorsolateral prefrontal activation patterns associated with autonomous value-based decision-making. Given its lower cost, portability, and tolerance to movement, fNIRS offers substantial advantages for consumer neuroscience and applied research contexts where fMRI is impractical.
