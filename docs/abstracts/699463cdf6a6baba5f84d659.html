Introduction

Early inter-regional connectivity in the brain progressively shapes the emergence of region-specific specialization in language processing. For example, brain connectivity at age seven predicts specialization in language-related regions at age nine.1 Therefore, the brain starts with broad functionality and then moves to specific regions over time. However, it remains unknown whether similar developmental mechanisms operate in children acquiring more than one language. Different languages place differential demands on phonological and semantic processing, which preferentially engage the dorsal and ventral neural pathways, respectively.2 Given that multiple languages coexist and interact within the same brain,3 this raises the question of whether bilingual language development involves cross-pathway influences—such that early interactions between functionally distinct pathways contribute to later neural specialization. In the present longitudinal study, we used functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) to examine whether early functional connectivity among distinct cortical regions predicts later neural specialization in Chinese–English bilingual children, and whether these developmental trajectories reflect cross-pathway influences rather than pathway-specific specialization alone.

Methods

Forty-three Chinese-English Bilingual children were recruited in the present study. They received fNIRS scans and completed a phonological task at two different waves. The first wave was conducted in their first-grade (age 6-7), and the second wave was conducted in their 3rd-grade (age 8-9) in an interval of 18-24 months. All child bilinguals had learned English before the age of 5, with a minimum of 1 year of English exposure before the experiment. 

Brain hemodynamic activity was measured using fNIRS. Based on the established language pathways,2 four Regions of Interest (ROI) were defined in the dorsal and ventral pathways. The dorsal pathway processes phonological information, including the dIFG (dorsal inferior frontal gyrus) and the superior temporal gyrus (STG). The ventral pathway processes the semantic information, including the vIFG (ventral inferior frontal gyrus) and middle temporal gyrus (MTG). To test whether early functional connectivity predicts later neural specialization, we employed hierarchical regression models to examine the additional explanatory power of connectivity measured at age 7 (wave 1) on neural specialization indexed by β values at age 9 (wave 2), including both within-pathway and cross-pathway connectivity predictors.

Results

Under the raw hierarchical regression model that did not include cross-pathway predictors, early connectivity did not provide significant additional explanatory power for predicting neural specialization at wave 2 in the bilingual group. However, when cross-pathway connectivity was incorporated into the predictive model, a different pattern emerged for the results of bilingual children.

The analysis revealed significant additional explanatory power from cross-pathway connectivity at wave 1. Specifically, connectivity between the vIFG (in the ventral pathway) and the STG (in the dorsal pathway) significantly predicted STG specialization at wave 2, t = 2.75, p = .01, indicating that stronger vIFG–STG connectivity uniquely and positively contributed to later STG specialization. 

Conclusion

This study provides longitudinal evidence that bilingual experience promotes cross-pathway neural adaptations in Chinese bilingual children. For bilingual children, stronger vIFG–STG connectivity at wave 1 positively predicted later STG activation, suggesting compensatory cross-pathway support that facilitates subsequent dorsal specialization. The cross-pathway developmental patterns observed in bilingual children point to bilingual-specific neural adaptation. These findings extend existing models of language development by demonstrating that bilingual experience recruits compensatory interactions across the dorsal and ventral pathways, highlighting cross-pathway dynamics as a key hallmark of bilingual neural specialization.
