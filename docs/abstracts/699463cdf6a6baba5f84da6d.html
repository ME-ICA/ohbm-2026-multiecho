Introduction

Artificial Intelligence (AI) combined with neuroimaging has demonstrated immense potential in characterizing the Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD), particularly in achieving high diagnostic accuracy through complex and high-dimensional features(Chen et al., 2024; Pan et al., 2025; Wen et al., 2022). However, a critical gap remains between diagnostic classification and therapeutic application. Current AI models often function as “black boxes,”(Haight & Eshaghi, 2023) lacking the localized interpretability required for targeted interventions such as Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (TMS) (Fox et al., 2012). Unlike diagnostic tasks, the primary objective of TMS is identifying specific and accessible brain regions where noninvasive neuro-modulation can normalize abnormal network dynamics (Zhou et al., 2020). To address this translational challenge, we propose a novel neuromodulation-guided screening framework that prioritizes region-specific effective connectivity. Moving beyond traditional methods that optimize for global accuracy, our approach constructs independent classification models for each brain region, utilizing inferred Granger Causality to map outgoing effective connections. By aligning feature extraction with the directional nature of TMS, this framework specifically isolates accessible cortical targets that are not only biologically reachable but also exhibit high discriminative ability. We further employ SHAP to characterize abnormal connections as excitatory or inhibitory, directly guiding direction-specific modulation protocols. Validated on the multi-site ABIDE I dataset, this biologically constrained and explainable AI pipeline provides actionable guidance for translating neuroimaging data into prioritized, individualized, and precise neuromodulation strategies for ASD.

Methods

Methods

The overall analytical pipeline employed in this study is depicted in Figure 1.

Participants

We selected 646 participants (298 ASD, 348 TD) from the ABIDE I dataset (14 sites) after excluding subjects with missing data, head motion >3mm/3°, poor image quality, or IQ <70. ASD and TD groups were balanced within sites for age, gender, and handedness (p > 0.05), and sites with <20 samples were excluded.

Data Preprocessing

Preprocessing (DPABI v5.1, SPM12) involved removing the first 10 time points, slice-timing and head motion correction, normalization to MNI space (3×3×3 mm³) with 6-mm FWHM smoothing, detrending, nuisance covariate regression (Friston 24-parameter motion, white matter, CSF, and global mean signals), and band-pass filtering (0.01-0.08 Hz).

ROI Definition and Time Series Extraction

We used a composite atlas of 416 ROIs (Schaefer 400-cortical + Harvard-Oxford subcortical) resampled to 3×3×3 mm³ MNI space. Mean time series were extracted for each ROI, yielding a 416 × time points matrix per subject.

Granger Causality Analysis

Granger causality analysis (GCA) quantified effective connectivity using linear vector autoregressive modeling (lag order 1). Positive and negative coefficients indicated excitatory and inhibitory influences, respectively. A 416×416 directed GCA matrix (zero diagonals) was generated for each subject.

Multi-site Effect Correction

We applied ComBat harmonization (https://github.com/Jfortin1/ComBatHarmonization) in MATLAB to correct site effects on GC features. Diagnosis, age, gender, FIQ, and mean framewise displacement (mFD) were preserved as biological covariates, using the default non-parametric prior method.

ROI-wise Classification Framework

The proposed framework is illustrated in Figure 1. For each of the 416 ROIs, an independent Random Forest classifier was constructed, utilizing only the directed GC connections from that specific source to the other 415 regions as features. The entire dataset was initially divided into training and test sets in a 4:1 ratio. A nested 5-fold cross-validation scheme was then implemented on the training set to ensure robust feature selection and prevent data leakage. Within the inner training loops, a two-stage feature selection process was applied: initial screening via two-sample t-tests (p < 0.05) followed by Recursive Feature Elimination with Cross-Validation (RFECV) to select the most discriminative effective connectivity. Following feature selection, classifier hyperparameters were optimized using the Optuna framework to determine optimal model parameters on the training set. The final model’s performance was then evaluated on the independent test set, quantified by the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUC). By comparing AUC scores across all 416 models, we identified the optimal modulatory target region for distinguishing ASD from TD individuals. We identified the optimal modulatory target region based on its cortical accessibility and discriminative power in distinguishing ASD from TD.

SHAP Explainability Analysis

To delineate specific downstream effector regions for therapeutic modulation, we applied SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to the top-performing ROI models using the independent test set. We computed SHAP values to quantify the contribution of each efferent effective connectivity feature, deriving two key metrics: SHAP importance (mean absolute value) to rank critical aberrant pathways, and SHAP direction (mean value) to characterize the nature of the dysregulation. Positive values indicated pathologically strengthened connections requiring inhibitory modulation, while negative values indicated weakened connections requiring excitatory modulation. This analysis generated a precise, direction-specific protocol for intervention, identifying not just where to stimulate, but how.

Results

Discriminative Power of Brain Regions

The region-wise screening across 416 classification models revealed a heterogeneous distribution of diagnostic ability (Figure 2A&B). While many regions showed limited predictive power, a specific subset of cortically accessible areas exhibited robust capacity to distinguish ASD from neurotypical controls based solely on their efferent causal connectivity. The cortically accessible ROI_236 (the right superior temporal gyrus) achieved the highest classification performance with an AUC of 0.72 (Figure 2C), significantly exceeding the average performance of 0.52 observed across other regions and demonstrated robust discriminative power. Other top-ranking regions were spatially clustered.

Explainable Causal Pathways

SHAP analysis of the top-performing ROI_236 (the right superior temporal gyrus) model identified specific efferent pathways driving the classification. Among the top-20 discriminative features, we observed a pattern of altered causal connectivity projecting from ROI_236 to widespread downstream targets, mainly including ROI_142 (left insula), ROI_371 (the right superior and middle temporal gyri), ROI_329 (the right superior temporal pole), and ROI_301 (the right precentral gyrus) (Figure 2D, 2E)

The SHAP analysis further revealed varied directionality within these specific efferent projections from ROI_236. Specifically, connections to ROI_142, ROI_294, and ROI_328 exhibited negative SHAP values, corresponding to a reduced causal influence in ASD compared to TD. In contrast, projections to ROI_371 and ROI_301 displayed positive SHAP values, corresponding to an increased causal influence in ASD compared to TD. The anatomical distribution of these key connections spanned targets including the insula, temporal gyri, and precentral gyrus.

Conclusion

This study presents a novel, neuromodulation-guided Artificial Intelligence framework that effectively translates resting-state fMRI data into actionable therapeutic targets. By shifting the analytical paradigm from maximizing global classification accuracy to evaluating local causal utility, we identified ROI_236 (the right superior temporal gyrus) as a high-priority, cortically accessible target for TMS intervention in Autism Spectrum Disorder.

Our findings demonstrate that ASD is characterized by directed causal dysregulation in specific causal circuits. The integration of Granger Causality with SHAP explainability allows for the precise mapping of these aberrations, offering a dual advantage: it identifies where to stimulate (the source node, ROI_236) and informs how to stimulate based on specific pathway deficits-for instance, requiring excitatory modulation for weakened connections to the insula (ROI_142) versus inhibitory modulation for strengthened connections to the temporal gyri (ROI_371). This approach bridges the gap between descriptive neuroimaging and interventional psychiatry, providing a rigorous, data-driven foundation for the development of personalized, circuit-based neuromodulation therapies.
