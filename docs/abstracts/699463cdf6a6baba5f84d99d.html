Introduction

In neuroimaging, it is common practice to predict discrete variables (e.g. experimental conditions) or continuous variables (e.g. stimulus ratings) from brain data obtained with functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) or electroencephalography (EEG). If such a prediction is successful, a common interpretation is that the set of signals used for predictive analysis (e.g. some region of interest, spherical searchlight or the whole brain) holds information about the predicted variable. Bayesian approaches for modelling fMRI signals^1 and for decoding from brain images^2 have been suggested in the past, but were not adopted by the neuroimaging community. Here, we propose a simple, but versatile model that enables Bayesian linear decoding from trial-wise fMRI signals, while allowing for integration of prior knowledge, inclusion of covariates and probabilistic inference on decoded variables.

Methods

We adapt multivariate Bayesian inversion (MBI)^3 for classification and regression from trial-wise fMRI data. Let Y be an n × v data matrix (number of trials n, number of voxels v) of trial-wise response amplitudes estimated from measured fMRI signals (e.g. using a trial-wise GLM with one HRF regressor per trial). Then, we specify a multivariate general linear model (MGLM)

   Y = X B + E,   E ∼ MN(0nv, V, Σ)

in which X is a known n × p design matrix, B is an unknown p × v matrix of regression coefficients, E is an n × v matrix of noise terms, V is a known n × n temporal correlation matrix, and Σ is the unknown v × v spatial covariance matrix.

For multivariate Bayesian classification (MBC), X is specified as an indicator matrix (optionally, plus covariates) which encodes the class identity of each trial (see Figure 1A). For multivariate Bayesian regression (MBR), X contains the to-be-predicted variable and a constant regressor (optionally, plus covariates) which model slope and intercept terms (see Figure 1B).

In the training data, the design matrix X is known and the MGLM parameters B and Σ have to be estimated, whereas in the test data, the MGLM parameters are known and we have to infer on the design matrix. Thus, in the first step, the parameters B and Σ are estimated from training data using a fully Bayesian treatment, and in the second step, those estimates are used to infer on the variable of interest in X using the test data. This gives a rise to a posterior distribution on the decoded variable (see Figure 1) which can be used for prediction.

In this work, we explore searchlight-based decoding, i.e. predicting variables from fMRI signals inside spherical volumes of interest, moving through the brain and centered on each in-mask voxel once^4, as well as whole-brain decoding, i.e. predicting variables from all in-mask fMRI signals, after some feature selection process in the training data.

We analyzed data (N = 108 subjects) from the Neuroimaging Analysis and Replication and Prediction Study (NARPS)^5. In this experiment, subjects were offered a mixed gamble in each trial, with certain amounts of money to win or lose, and then indicated their favorability of the bet on a four-point scale (1: strongly reject, 2: weakly reject, 3: weakly accept, 4: strongly accept)^5. In total, there were 256 trials distributed across 4 runs.

We used fMRIprep-preprocessed NARPS data, as provided on OpenNeuro. After additional slice-timing correction in SPM12, trial-wise response amplitudes were estimated using a trial-wise GLM with one HRF regressor per trial. Estimated responses from all trials and all voxels entered MBI-based prediction. Leave-one-run-out cross-validation was used for all analyses.

Results

For searchlight-based classification, we used a searchlight radius of 6 mm, calculated balanced accuracy (BA) maps and used one-sample t-tests across subjects to identify searchlights with above-chance predictive accuracy. For all discrete variables of interest (high gain vs. low gain, high loss vs. low loss, accept vs. reject, strong vs. weak, reaction time (RT) above vs. below median), we found clusters with accuracy significantly higher than chance level, indicating localized information about the decoded variables  (see Figure 2A).

For whole-brain-based regression, we predicted variables on continuous scales without dichotomization (gain & loss: 5-20 ILS^5; response: 1-4; RT: 0-4 s). For all continuous variables of interest (gain, loss, response, RT), we found that they can be decoded with above-chance predictive correlation in the majority of subjects (see Figure 2B).

Conclusion

We have shown that MBI is capable of predicting discrete and continuous variables from trial-wise fMRI data in searchlight-based and whole-brain decoding analyses. As shown in earlier work, MBI can also be applied for predicting individual differences from subject-wise MRI data in across-subject predictive analyses (e.g. brain age prediction)^3. In the future, we want to explore how inclusion of covariates and inspection of posterior probabilities for the decoded variable^3 can enhance predictive fMRI data analysis. We hope that the adoption of multivariate Bayesian inversion for functional MRI analysis will allow for more interpretable models and better quantification of uncertainty in multi-voxel pattern analysis.
