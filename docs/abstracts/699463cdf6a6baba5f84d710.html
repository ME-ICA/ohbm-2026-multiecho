Introduction

Brain age provides a powerful imaging-derived marker of neurobiological aging, often diverging from chronological age due to lifestyle, genetic, and pathological factors[1]. While structural MRI (sMRI) captures age-related atrophy[2-3], resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) reflects functional reorganization[4-5], yet most existing approaches use only one modality or rely on naïve concatenation[6], limiting their ability to model complementary mechanisms of aging. To address this, we developed AMAge-Net, an attention-guided multimodal framework integrating whole-brain functional connectivity graphs with voxel-level structural representations. The model uses hierarchical graph attention to extract functional features and a 3D DenseNet to characterize multiscale structural patterns. A cross-modal Multi-Head Cross-Attention module and Gated Fusion layer dynamically select informative interactions, improving prediction robustness and interpretability. We applied AMAge-Net to the Cam-CAN adult lifespan dataset (N=652, ages 18–89) to test whether attention-based multimodal fusion enhances brain age prediction accuracy, and to characterize structural–functional contributions to aging.

Methods

Participants were drawn from the Cam-CAN cohort (N = 652 healthy adults; 322 males). rs-fMRI preprocessing included motion/distortion correction, denoising, band-pass filtering, and AAL-based extraction of 90 ROI time series. Functional connectivity (90×90) matrices were converted into weighted graphs, where node features corresponded to region-wise connectivity profiles. Functional representations were learned via a three-layer Graph Attention Network (GAT) with hierarchical attention pooling.  sMRI (T1-weighted) images were resampled and processed through a 3D DenseNet121 to extract multiscale anatomical features. For multimodal fusion, functional vectors generated the query, while structural features served as key/value inputs to a Multi-Head Cross-Attention module. A Gated Fusion mechanism further integrated attention-derived features with global structural representations. Prediction was performed with a fully connected regression layer optimized using MSE. Model performance (MAE, RMSE, R², PCC) was evaluated using 5-fold cross-validation. Interpretability analyses quantified regional contributions using GAT attention weights (fMRI) and gradient-based saliency (sMRI). Seven state-of-the-art unimodal and multimodal baselines were re-implemented for comparison, including BrainGNN, GCN, BC-GCN, ResNet, EfficientNet, 3D-CNN, and MFFormer.

Results

AMAge-Net achieved the highest accuracy among all models tested (MAE = 5.09, RMSE = 6.52, R² = 0.87, r = 0.94), substantially outperforming unimodal and multimodal baselines. Figure 1 shows the predicted vs. chronological age scatterplots across the eight models, highlighting that AMAge-Net produces the tightest linear fit and lowest residual variance. Compared with the strongest unimodal baseline (3D-CNN: MAE = 5.76, r = 0.92), AMAge-Net achieved an 11.6% reduction in MAE, demonstrating the benefit of attention-mediated fusion. Functional-only models performed markedly worse (e.g., BrainGNN MAE=11.46), supporting the dominance of anatomical aging signals while validating the need for multimodal integration. Model interpretability revealed convergent structural–functional markers of aging. 
Several regions (posterior cingulate, precuneus, thalamus) were salient across structural and functional modalities, indicating shared structure–function aging mechanisms. Ablation experiments confirmed the necessity of each module: removing attention fusion or gating resulted in reduced accuracy; functional-only and structural-only variants exhibited substantial declines.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that attention-guided multimodal fusion substantially improves brain age estimation beyond unimodal or simple multimodal approaches. By modeling fine-grained structure–function correspondences, AMAge-Net captures complementary aging signatures and provides interpretable neurobiological insights. Convergent structural–functional aging markers highlight the posterior cingulate, precuneus, thalamus, and temporal regions as core hubs of normative aging. These findings support the use of attention-based multimodal models as powerful tools for lifespan neuroscience and future clinical applications such as detecting accelerated aging or early neurodegenerative change.
