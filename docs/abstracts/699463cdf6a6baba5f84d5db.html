Introduction

A growing number of older adults now live in cities due to rapid urbanisation and ageing population. Hence, the design of urban built environments is modifiable determinant of cognitive function and brain health in older adults1. Among the various elements of urban design, street intersection density, an objective indicator of neighbourhood connectivity, has been identified as a key feature that facilitates pedestrian movement and supports. Neighbourhoods with high street intersection density are typified by small blocks and shorter street segments, which can facilitate walking for utilitarian purposes in older adults 2. Walking in such environments may be cognitively demanding, particularly for older adults, as it requires individuals to plan routes, remember landmarks, and make decisions at multiple intersections, even if done subconsciously when navigating familiar routes. For older adults, regularly undertaking cognitively challenging walks can serve as a form of mental exercise, potentially helping to improve cognitive function. To investigate how urban neighbourhood connectivity relates to late-life hippocampal structure, this study built an end-to-end analytical framework that links MRI with city-scale Geographic Information Systems (GIS) data. Using this framework, we sought to determine whether variations in neighbourhood street connectivity are associated with differences in hippocampal subregion volumes, and whether changes in these brain structures over time are related to long-term exposure to neighbourhood connectivity.

Methods

We built a framework (Figure1) that (i) preprocesses and segments longitudinal structural MRI at hippocampal-subfield resolution, (ii) derives wave-matched, network-based neighbourhood metrics from city GIS, and (iii) integrates both streams for mixed-effects modelling of levels and trajectories.

The data was from community-dwelling adults (70–90 years at baseline) from the Sydney Memory and Ageing Study 3,4. Participants had structural MRI of the brain data at baseline (N=514, Wave 1), Wave 2 (N=247, 2-year follow-up) and Wave 4 (N=239, 6-year follow up) of the study. Structural MRI data were acquired using T1-weighted protocol (Repetition time (TR) = 6.39 ms, echo time (TE) = 2.9 ms, flip angle = 8°, matrix size = 256×256, field of view (FOV) = 256×256×190, and slice thickness = 1 mm with no gap in   between, yielding 1×1×1 mm3 isotropic voxels).  Structural MRI data underwent automated quality assessment and expert visual inspection in line with established guidelines. Scans failing image- or segmentation-level QC were excluded.  MRI data was processed to FreeSurfer (v7.1.0) longitudinal stream. The hippocampal regions segmented using the ‘segmentHA_T1_long’ 5, which uses a probabilistic atlas derived from ultra-high resolution ex vivo MRI data to generate segmentation of the hippocampal subfield regions. 

For GIS analysis, residential addresses were geocoded by wave. Exposures were calculated inside 1-km network buffers built on the pedestrian street graph to reflect walkable catchments with turns preserved. Street intersection density (SID) was computed as ≥3-way intersections per km² using PSMA Transport & Topography datasets (2006/2012/2018) time-matched to MRI waves. Additional covariates within the same buffers included population density, public transport points, % commercial land, parkland, tree canopy, blue space; NO₂ and PM₂.₅ were assigned from high-resolution land-use regression surfaces, and area-level socioeconomic status (IRSAD).

Linear mixed-effects models (lme4) was used for statistical analysis. SID was decomposed into: (a) person-mean SID (accumulated exposure across waves; between-person effect) and (b) within-person deviations (time-varying change due to moving/network updates). Models adjusted for socio-demographic covariates (sex, age, English-speaking background, education, living arrangements, residential mobility and the use of hearing aid) and environmental covariates.

The Sydney MAS study was approved by the Ethics Committees at UNSW Sydney and the South Eastern Sydney Illawarra Area Health Service.

Results

The right hippocampal tail volume was positively associated with person-level average street intersection density (β = 0.680, 95% CI [0.121, 1.238], p = 0.017), indicating that individuals living in more spatially complex neighbourhoods had larger hippocampal tail volumes. In the left hippocampal tail, a trend-level association was observed between person-level average street intersection density and baseline volume (β = 0.489, 95% CI [–0.002, 0.981], p = 0.051). No other hippocampal subfields showed this association (Figure 2).

In the right hippocampal tail, individuals living in neighbourhoods with higher street intersection density exhibited a steeper decline in volume over time, as indicated by a significant negative linear interaction with age (β = –0.083, 95% CI [–0.148, –0.018], p = 0.012). This decline was further characterized by a significant positive quadratic interaction (β = 0.00262, 95% CI [0.00011, 0.00512], p = 0.041), suggesting a potentially positive effect of street intersection density (i.e., slower decline in volume) in later ages.  A similar pattern was observed in the left hippocampal tail with significant negative linear interaction (β = –0.057, 95% CI [–0.112, –0.00198], p = 0.042) and trend-level positive quadratic interaction of age by street intersection density (p=0.06).

We found no significant associations between within-person changes in street intersection density and changes in hippocampal subfield volumes. Specifically, the time-varying component of street intersection density was not significantly related to hippocampal subfield volume at any time point, nor did it significantly moderate the linear or quadratic trajectory of volume change over time.

Conclusion

By treating the city as a graph and the hippocampus as a set of small, longitudinally tracked structures, we show that a simple, well-defined urban feature, street intersection density, relates to posterior hippocampal morphology in late life. Overall, individuals residing in areas with higher street connectivity exhibited greater hippocampal tail volume. However, this advantage was not sustained uniformly across time. Between ages 70 and 85, these individuals experienced a steeper loss of volume compared to their peers in less connected neighbourhoods. Interestingly, in the oldest age group (80+ years), the rate of decline began to decelerate, suggesting a potential rebound effect or delayed benefit of higher street connectivity in very late life. The findings suggest that the neuroprotective effect of living in neighbourhoods with higher street intersection density may vary across the ageing trajectory. This highlights the need for age-sensitive urban planning strategies that support brain health across the full spectrum of later life.
