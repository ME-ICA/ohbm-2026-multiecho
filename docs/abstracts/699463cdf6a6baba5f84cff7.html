Introduction

A healthy cerebrovascular system is essential for brain health: vessels provide nutrients and serve an important role in clearing metabolic waste. Cerebrovascular damage or dysfunction may serve as an early biomarker of cognitive decline and have been reported to predict neurodegenerative disease onset1. Additionally, individual differences in vascularization patterns, such as in the vasculature supplying the hippocampus, have been linked to differences in structural and functional decline in persons with small vessel disease2. Consequently, high resolution imaging of the vascular anatomy of the brain has important clinical and research implications.

Time-of-flight (TOF) magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) exploits the in-flow effect of blood as an endogenous contrast agent and is widely used for vascular imaging. However, acquiring high-resolution images requires long scan times, increasing motion artifacts, costs, and patient burden3. Compressed sensing (CS) accelerates TOF-MRA by incoherent k-space undersampling, enabling faster imaging of sparse structures like blood vessels4. While effective in simpler vascular regions with higher flow rates5, its performance in the brain—where vascular anatomy is complex and arterial flow slower—is less certain.

Here, we evaluated CS for TOF-MRA at 7T and 3T to image the cerebrovasculature in healthy adults, assessing whether CS acquisitions can produce images comparable to or better than conventional MRA acquisitions for clinically- and research-relevant applications, and whether that holds true at different field strengths.

Methods

Eight participants (all females, aged 23 to 40) were scanned at University College London (UCL) on a 7T Siemens Magnetom Terra MRI with a Nova Medical 8-channel transmit/32-channel receive coil, using no compression (CS0; scan time = 18:40) and a compression factor of 8 (CS8; 8:13). A separate cohort of 23 participants (12 females, aged 20 to 56) were scanned at Baycrest Academy for Research and Education (BAY) on a 3T Siemens Prisma MRI with a 64-channel coil, using no compression CS0 (27:55), and compression factors CS4 (8:38), and CS8 (4:41). A final sample of 19 remained after exclusions due to imaging artifacts, incidental findings and incomplete scans.

Vascular segmentations were automated using a custom pipeline in MATLAB. Specifically, an initial mask was generated via signal intensity thresholding, retaining only the highest percentile voxel values (>98% for 3T, >99% for 7T). Next, we performed connectivity filtering, in which segmented voxel clusters were retained only if they exceeded a prespecified size threshold of 40 connected voxels. Within the resulting mask, the number of vascular voxels were calculated as well as contrast ratios using the formula CR = μVessel - μTissue / √ σ2Tissue. Two-tailed paired t-tests were used for statistical comparisons, with Cohen's d used for calculation of effect sizes.

A separate analysis was conducted on hippocampal supplying vessels posterior cerebral artery (PCA) and anterior choroidal artery (AChA), serving as example vessels of differing sizes, both of which are relevant for research and clinical practice. Here, the automated segmentation pipeline was applied to TOF-MRA scans restricted to the hippocampal region, defined using FreeSurfer 7.1 hippocampal segmentations dilated to include surrounding tissue (see Figure 1). For this analysis, we used a 98th percentile intensity threshold for both 3T and 7T. We also individualized the connectivity threshold for each scan by identifying the elbow point in the histogram distribution of segmented cluster sizes. Within the resulting masks, a tracer familiar with hippocampal vasculature manually labeled the portions belonging to PCA and AChA. At 3T, only the PCA was labeled due to limited AChA visibility. Moreover, morphological closing of vessel structures was applied for 3T data before measurement extraction due to sporadic signal drop inside vessels, and 4 cases were excluded due to limited vessel inclusion after skull stripping and bias field inhomogeneity correction (N = 15). Diameters for the segmented structures were next estimated using functions available in MATLAB’s Image Processing Toolbox, and contrast ratios were calculated for all vessels using the same formula as the whole-image analysis.

Results

Qualitatively, 7T CS8 imaging shows image quality comparable to CS0 scans, with no noticeable differences when visualizing large vessels, such as the cerebral arteries of the Circle of Willis (see red boxes, 7T, Figure 2), or in visualizing smaller distal branches (see blue boxes, 7T, Figure 2). At 3T, there are no clear qualitative differences in CS0, CS4 and CS8 acquisitions for large vessels (see red boxes, 3T, Figure 2). However, CS0 scans appear slightly better for visualizing smaller, distal branches (see blue boxes, 3T, Figure 2).

Quantitatively, the application of CS acceleration was clearly beneficial at 7T. Compared with CS0, CS8 scans showed significantly higher contrast ratios (p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 1.51) and vessel voxel counts (p = 0.002, Cohen’s d = 0.54) in whole-image automated segmentations. Semi-automatic segmentations of PCA and AChA corroborated these findings in both hemispheres, with higher contrast ratios and median diameters for CS8 (all p < 0.01). At 3T, effects depended on CS factor: CS4 had the lowest contrast ratios and voxel count in whole-image segmentations, while CS8 outperformed both CS0 and CS4 (all p < 0.01). For PCA alone, both contrast ratios and median diameters increased linearly with CS factor, with CS8 showing the best performance (all p < 0.001).

Conclusion

This study found that CS-based acceleration applied to TOF-MRA at 7T seemed to result in comparable, or even improved, imaging compared to conventional TOF-MRA, while using only < ½ of the scan time. However, at 3T the results are dependent on the CS factor and the size of the vessels being studied, with clear benefits of CS8 seen for larger structures such as the cerebral arteries, but a potential disadvantage for the depiction of smaller, distal branches. CS8 was associated with the best performance based on our metrics, cutting the acquisition time down by five-fold, and dramatically improved the feasibility of including TOF-MRA in routine imaging workups.
