Introduction

A main focus of comparative studies of the brains of nonhuman primates, and especially chimpanzees, has been to elucidate the evolutionary foundations of human language and speech. Previous studies have reported that some captive chimpanzees intentionally produce Vocal Attention-Getting (VAG) sounds to capture the attention of an otherwise inattentive audience while some prefer Tactile Attention-Getting (TAG) [1].

The precentral gyrus (preCG) is morphologically defined posteriorly by the central sulcus (CS) and anteriorly by the superior and inferior precentral sulci (preCS). Stimulation of the precentral gyrus along the dorsal-ventral plane elicits motor activation across the body in a topographic manner [2]. We examined whether sound producing and non-sound-producing chimpanzees differed in the morphology of the precentral gyrus, involved in the control of the orofacial musculature.

Methods

T1-weighted structural MRI scans of 278 Pan troglodytes from two different sites and including 163 females and 115 males ranging in age between 3 and 54 years were acquired under the guidelines of the US Department of Health and Humans Services. Figure 1.A illustrates our process of sulcal shape comparison, using the example of the CS. First, CSs and preCSs were extracted from the MRIs using the "Morphologist 2012" pipeline [3] (fig. 1.A1). Right sulci were flipped to be compared to the left sulci (fig. 1.A2) and all sulci were then affinely normalized in Talairach space (fig. 1.A3). A shape similarity matrix (fig. 1.A4) was performed using a pairwise average quadratic distance after alignment of each pair of sulci with the ICP algorithm [4]. Isomap-based manifold learning was used to capture one-dimensional approximations of the space spanned by the sulci. We chose to keep the first 6 dimensions explaining the larger part of the variance. To exhibit the variability trait described on each isomap dimension, all sulci were rigidly aligned to the sulcus with the minimum distance to all the other sulci and local shape averages were performed at regular intervals along each one-dimensional manifold (fig. 1.B). The averages were calculated from the sulci 3D images, which were summed with weights to obtain a statistical probability anatomical map (SPAM). All the probability maps were computed at regular intervals and thresholded in order to perform 3D renderings displayed in the same orientation as the template sulcus. The isomap axes are unitless, expressing shape variability. We performed two experiments: one on the shape variability of the preCG (defined by both CS and preCS sulci) and one on the CS only for comparison. To test for associations between preCG shape and behaviors, the majority of chimpanzees were classified as either VAG+ or VAG- or TAG+ or TAG- using data reported in Taglialatella and colleagues' study [5].

We fitted linear mixed-effects models (LMM) with age, sex, VAG (or TAG) status, hemisphere and site as fixed-effect covariates, and subjects as random intercept to account for repeated measurements across hemispheres on each of the 6 preCG and 6 CS isomap dimensions.

Results

Analysis of the preCG produced 6 different isomap dimensions of variability in the shape of the gyrus located between the CS and the preCS. On isomap 1, involving a transition from a short inferior preCS and a long superior preCS to a long inferior preCS and a short superior preCS, significant differences were observed between hemispheres (p = 0.006), and a trend was observed between VAG+ and VAG- chimpanzees. On isomap 3 (fig. 2), the model also found a significant difference between VAG+ and VAG- chimpanzees (p = 0.006), and a trend between hemispheres. A Mann-Whitney U-test showed that the VAG effect was mainly in the left hemisphere. In this dimension the hand knob loses importance in favor of a long inferior preCG (fig.2 left to right). The inferior preCS takes precedence over the superior preCS and the enlargement of the inferior precentral gyrus shifts from the most ventral area to a more lateral area. There is a clear change in the curvature of the inferior preCS in this area along the axis. The effect of VAG was tested against the null model on isomap 3 (LRT:  2 =11.336 ; p=0.0008) and hemispheric asymmetry was tested on isomap 1 (LRT: 2 =11.294 ; p=0.0008 ). We note that no difference between sites was observed on these two isomaps, meaning that the VAG+/- and the hemispheric differences were reproducible and significant within the two cohorts. No significant differences were found between TAG+ and TAG- chimpanzees. 
Analysis of the CS produced 6 other isomap dimensions on which the model found no differences when comparing the position values of the left and right hemispheres between VAG+ and VAG- chimpanzees.

Conclusion

Our analyses revealed that while variation in the central sulcus was not associated with VAG production, the shape of the precentral gyrus showed a clear modification in the premotor orofacial region. The variation in preCG shape differed between VAG+ and VAG- chimpanzees on isomap 3, particularly for the left hemisphere, with VAG+ chimpanzees exhibiting a longer and larger inferior preCG, reflecting a larger surface area in the orofacial region. Furthermore, no differences were found between TAG+ and TAG- apes on any of the isomaps. Thus, the association between attention-getting communicative behavior and variation in preCG shape appears to be specific to the use of vocal, but not tactile, behavior to get attention. Our results show that a more selective use of vocalizations to get attention in the context of intentional communication is associated with a unique morphological landmark in the oro-facial premotor cortex. Given that the chimpanzee brain is relatively immature at birth and the frontal cortex matures more slowly than other regions, the results reported here might be further evidence of experience- or learning-dependent modifications in a language-related cortical region.
