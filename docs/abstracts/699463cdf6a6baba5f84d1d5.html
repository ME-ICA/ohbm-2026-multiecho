Introduction

Abnormal accumulation of amyloid-beta (Aβ) and tau proteins is a hallmark of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and related dementias (ADRD), contributing to neurodegeneration across aging populations, with glymphatic system dysfunction implicated in impaired protein clearance.1 Current methods like positron emission tomography (PET) imaging and lumbar punctures for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis are invasive, costly, or limited by radiation exposure and accessibility. As a potential strategy to address this, studies have attempted to synthesize PET images from T1-weighted (T1w) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and have shown promising results of utilizing the generative models to synthesize realistic ‘PET’ like images.2 However, their methods suffer from low accuracy (< 60%), raising the question of whether structural MRI sequences like T1w imaging can directly capture protein-related pathology. Interestingly, a diffusion MRI based, non-invasive technique called intravoxel incoherent motion (IVIM) might shine some light on it.3 IVIM is an advanced MRI technique that simultaneously assesses perfusion and diffusion in biological tissues, without the need for contrast agents. It provides three key parameters: the true diffusion coefficient (D), the pseudo-diffusion coefficient (D*), and the perfusion fraction (f), which provide insights into tissue properties and microvascular dynamics (Fig. 1A). Prior work demonstrated that IVIM metrics achieve high predictive accuracy for classifying Aβ status, with significant correlations to amyloid, tau, and cognitive scores,4 providing evidence that IVIM can detect patterns related to protein accumulation. The goal of this study is to develop a generative model to synthesize Aβ and tau PET images from IVIM MRI data. This capability would facilitate retrospective analysis of incomplete datasets and enhance longitudinal studies, while reducing reliance on invasive lumbar punctures and PET tracers, especially for patients at risk.

Methods

Participants

Data were obtained from the Stanford Alzheimer's Disease Research Center (ADRC) cohort. Ninety-three participants (mean age 71.0±9.3 years, range 50-89 years; 52% male) with paired IVIM MRI and amyloid/tau PET scans were included. The cohort comprised cognitively normal older adults (n=41, 44%), participants with Alzheimer's disease (AD, n=26, 28%), Lewy body disease (LBD, n=20, 22%), and mixed pathologies (n=6, 6%).

Image Preprocessing

Neuroimaging data were acquired on a 3-Tesla PET/MRI scanner. The imaging protocol included: (1) High-resolution 3D T1w structural images (gradient-recalled inversion recovery pulse sequence: TR/TE = 7.7/3.1 ms, inversion time = 400 ms, flip angle = 11°) with 1mm slice thickness, and 1mm spacing, (2) multi-shell diffusion-weighted imaging with 9 b-values (0, 25, 50, 75, 100, 150, 250, 500, 1000 s/mm²) to enable IVIM modeling, with 12 diffusion directions per b-value shell (TR/TE = 7000/80.3 ms, 2 mm slice thickness, 4 mm interslice spacing), (3) Amyloid PET data acquired using 18F-florbetaben (8.1 mCi), with images collected 90-110 minutes post-injection, and (4)Tau PET data acquired using 18F-PI-2620 (5-10 mCi), with images collected either 45-75 or 60-90 minutes post-injection.

An in-house developed pipeline was used to preprocess IVIM data and we adopted the IAR_LU_biexp fitting model, part of the IVIM task force 2.4, to extract D, D*, and f maps. PET data were preprocessed using the same methods described in Young et al,5 and Standard uptake volume ratio (SUVR) images were computed using the cerebellar gray matter as reference region. All images were registered to participant's native T1w space.

Model Training

We developed two independent conditional generative adversarial networks (GANs) to synthesize amyloid-PET and tau-PET SUVR images from multi-parameter intravoxel incoherent motion (IVIM) diffusion MRI (Fig. 1B). Each network comprised a U-Net generator with five encoding and five decoding levels, accepting three-channel input from IVIM-derived diffusion coefficient (D), pseudo-diffusion coefficient (D*), and perfusion fraction (f) maps. The generator employed weighted skip connections (weight=0.5) to balance detail preservation with synthetic realism, using asymmetric convolution strides (1×2×2) to preserve volumetric depth while downsampling spatial dimensions. Feature channels progressively increased from 64 to 512 through the encoding path. The discriminator implemented a PatchGAN architecture with spectral normalization applied to all convolutional layers for training stability, receiving four-channel concatenated inputs (D, D*, f, PET) to learn conditional relationships between IVIM parameters and PET appearance.

The generator was optimized using composite loss combining adversarial loss, L1 reconstruction loss (λ=100), and gradient loss (λ=10) to optimize voxel-wise accuracy and edge preservation. Both networks used Adam optimization (learning rate=0.0002, β₁=0.5, β₂=0.999) with discriminator-to-generator update ratio of 2:1. Training employed early stopping (patience=10 epochs) and learning rate scheduling based on validation loss.

Models were implemented in PyTorch 2.0.1 and trained on Stanford's Sherlock HPC cluster using NVIDIA GPUs with 64 GB RAM. Total training time was approximately 2 days.

Model Validation

We performed 10-fold cross-validation for each model (amyloid and tau), resulting in 20 total trained models. Performance was evaluated using mean absolute error (MAE), structural similarity index (SSIM), and peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) on denormalized images. Additionaly, FreeSurfer-derived regions of interest (ROIs) were used to compute mean SUVR values in real and synthetic PET images, with Pearson correlation assessing regional correspondence (Fig. 1C). Bland-Altman analysis was also performed to assess agreement between real and synthetic ROI-based SUVR values. Mean bias, 95% limits of agreement (LOA), and proportional bias were calculated.

Results

Model Performance and Image Quality Metrics

Ten-fold cross-validation demonstrated excellent synthesis performance for both models. The amyloid-PET synthesis model achieved mean absolute error of 0.0196 ± 0.0051, structural similarity index of 0.9249 ± 0.0124, and peak signal-to-noise ratio of 28.54 ± 1.64 dB. The tau-PET synthesis model showed comparable performance with mean absolute error of 0.0208 ± 0.0054, structural similarity index of 0.9226 ± 0.0121, and peak signal-to-noise ratio of 28.34 ± 1.73 dB. Both models achieved greater than 92% structural similarity to ground truth PET images, with voxel-wise reconstruction error below 2.1% in normalized intensity space. Coefficients of variation were consistently low for structural similarity (<1.5% for both models), indicating robust and consistent generalization across subjects with diverse demographic characteristics, disease stages, and anatomical variations. Performance metrics exceeded typical benchmarks for medical image synthesis (MAE < 0.03, SSIM > 0.90) and approached clinical-grade quality thresholds (PSNR ≥ 28 dB). 

Regional SUVR Correlation Analysis

Subject-wise regional quantitative validation using FreeSurfer-derived regions of interest revealed strong correlations between real and synthetic PET-derived standardized uptake value ratios (Fig. 2B). For the amyloid-PET model, 92 of 93 subjects (98.9%) displayed statistically significant correlations after false discovery rate correction for multiple comparisons, with mean Pearson correlation coefficient r = 0.73 ± 0.12 (range: 0.36-0.90). The tau-PET model demonstrated universal statistical significance, with all 93 subjects showing significant correlations (mean r = 0.65 ± 0.14, range: 0.20-0.86). These subject-level correlations demonstrate that synthetic images preserve individual-specific regional pathological patterns.

Bland-Altman Agreement Analysis

Bland-Altman analysis performed across all FreeSurfer ROI measurements from all subjects (n = 9,114 ROI pairs per model; 50 ROIs × 93 subjects) demonstrated minimal systematic bias for both models (amyloid: mean bias = 0.008 ± 0.298; tau: mean bias = 0.018 ± 0.326 SUVR units). The 95% limits of agreement were -0.577 to 0.593 for amyloid and -0.622 to 0.657 for tau, indicating that 95% of synthetic SUVR measurements fell within approximately ±0.6 SUVR units of real measurements across all regional assessments (Fig. 2C).

Conclusion

This study developed and validated multi-parameter IVIM-based GAN to synthesize amyloid-PET and tau-PET images from non-invasive diffusion MRI. Our models successfully achieved high-fidelity synthesis, achieving >92% structural similarity and strong regional SUVR correlations. Integration of three IVIM parameters (D, D*, f) as multi-channel input enabled the models to capture complex microstructural and hemodynamic features associated with pathology. Regional SUVR correlations (amyloid r=0.73±0.12, tau r=0.65±0.14) with near-universal statistical significance demonstrate preserved quantitative accuracy essential for clinical interpretation and region-based burden assessment. Bland-Altman analysis minimal systematic bias indicating well-calibrated models without consistent over- or under-estimation. 

Our models outperformed previously reported MRI-to-PET synthesis approaches despite using fewer training subjects (Fig. 2D). Recent cross-modality synthesis studies using T1w MRI as input typically report SSIM values ranging from 0.86-0.88, PSNR values of 24.8-26.5 dB, and regional correlations of r=0.61, often requiring large datasets. In contrast, our multi-parameter IVIM approach achieved substantially higher structural similarity (SSIM>0.92), superior signal quality (PSNR>28 dB), and stronger regional correlations (r=0.65-0.73) with a considerably smaller sample size (n=93). These improvements likely reflect the richer microstructural and hemodynamic information provided by three IVIM parameters compared to single-contrast structural MRI, enabling superior synthesis performance with greater data efficiency.

Important limitations warrant consideration. The relatively small sample size may limit generalization, though 10-fold cross-validation mitigates overfitting concerns. Models were trained on single-institution data; external validation on larger, multi-site datasets is needed. While cross-sectional SUVR correlations are strong, sensitivity for longitudinal changes or subtle stage distinctions remains unvalidated. Image preprocessing and training require substantial computational resources, potentially limiting accessibility. 

Future directions include several critical investigations. We plan to apply explainable AI to elucidate which IVIM features most contribute to predictions. Understanding learned relationships could reveal insights into microstructural changes accompanying protein aggregation. We aim to investigate whether models leverage IVIM-derived glymphatic function and waste clearance markers, potentially revealing MRI signatures of impaired clearance promoting pathological accumulation, advancing pathophysiology understanding beyond synthesis applications. Additional priorities include external multi-site validation, prospective longitudinal validation, extension to additional tracers, integration with other MRI sequences, uncertainty quantification, and clinical utility studies.

In conclusion, multi-parameter IVIM-based GANs represent a promising approach for radiation-free amyloid and tau-PET synthesis with high fidelity and preserved regional accuracy. While limitations require addressing through validation studies, results suggest substantial potential for advancing research and expanding molecular imaging access. Planned explainable AI investigations of glymphatic waste clearance mechanisms exemplify how these models may serve as both practical tools and scientific instruments for probing disease pathophysiology.
