Introduction

Magnetic Resonance-guided Focused Ultrasound (MRgFUS) surgery has emerged as an effective non-resective treatment for Essential Tremor (ET), targeting the ventral intermediate nucleus of the thalamus to create a focal lesion via thermal ablation. This thalamotomy disrupts the Dentato-Rubro-Thalamic Tract (DRTT) and helps reduce tremor. Understanding how MRgFUS thalamotomy alters brain network structural connectivity is thus crucial for elucidating the mechanisms of tremor reduction and optimizing treatments. Here, we use a recently developed method for longitudinal analysis of diffusion MRI to identify post-procedure changes in structural networks in seven individuals who underwent MRgFUS thalamotomy for treatment of essential tremor. 

Common tractography approaches can produce markedly different intra-subject results between imaging sessions. This has been challenging for longitudinal difference studies,  sometimes confounding methodological noise with true biological effects.1,2 Recent advances in quantitative tractography enable robust longitudinal analysis of fibre bundle connectivity (FBC).1,3 This technique establishes fixed streamline trajectories across sessions, thereby reducing non-biological variability when estimating fibre density changes.3 The present work applies this methodological development to analyze FBC changes in individual ET patients following MRgFUS thalamotomy. Importantly, the method allows quantitative comparison of effects both within and across patients. Such analyses could reveal how individual surgical factors - e.g. skull density ratio, sonication parameters, or baseline symptom severity, influence long-term brain network reorganization and tremor improvement, enabling better personalised MRgFUS treatments for ET patients. 

Methods

Data Acquisition 

Seven ET patients underwent scanning on a Siemens 3T Vida MRI scanner over 2 sessions: prior to MRgFUS surgery and at 90-day post-operation. Acquisitions included a T1-weighted volume at 1mm isotropic resolution, and a DWI dataset of 105 directions over b-values (0, 300, 1000 & 3000 s/mm²) at 2mm isotropic resolution. T1-weighted and T2-weighted FLAIR volumes were also acquired immediately after surgery to assess the lesion.  

Preprocessing 

DWI preprocessing included denoising, Gibbs ringing removal, distortion and motion correction.  DWI images were then interpolated to 1.25mm isotropic resolution. All analyses were performed using MRtrix3 (www.mrtrix.org) unless mentioned otherwise. 

Differential Quantitative Subject-level Tractography 

Fibre Orientation Distributions (FODs) were computed from each subject’s DWI dataset using multi-tissue constrained spherical deconvolution. We utilised a group-average response function across all subjects and all sessions, to allow inter-patient comparisons. T1-weighted images were intensity-normalized and coregistered to diffusion space using synthetic T1-weighted images predicted from mean b0 images. 

We then performed differential tractogram density optimization, a novel framework specifically designed for robust longitudinal analysis of structural connectivity: 

First, a temporally unbiased within-subject FOD template was constructed using rigid-body registration between timepoints. Session-specific FODs were transformed to this template space and segmented into fibre-population elements within voxels (fixels), with fixel correspondence established across sessions to a common fixel template. 

The T1w template was reconstructed in FreeSurfer to generate five-tissue-type segmentation for anatomically constrained tractography and Desikan-Killiany parcellation for connectome construction. Whole-brain tractography generated 10 million streamlines from the FOD template using dynamic seeding. Masks derived from the brain parcellation were used to exclude some biologically implausible streamlines, such as thalamic radiations crossing into the contra-lateral hemisphere via the corpus callosum. Cerebello-thalamic streamlines were unconstrained; allowing both ipsi- and contra-lateral decussation within the tegmental area. 

Differential Connectivity Analysis 

At the subject level, streamlines in the unbiased tractogram were given a weight using the SIFT2 algorithm, modified to fit fixel-wise Fibre Density (FD) averages across sessions.3 A second set of differential SIFT2 weights was then computed to reflect fixel-wise FD differences between sessions, yielding Fibre Bundle Capacity (FBC) differences for each streamline. Differential connectomes were constructed by assigning streamlines to brain region pairs and aggregating their differential FBC weights, providing edge-wise measures of longitudinal structural connectivity change between pre-surgery and 90-day post-operation timepoints. 

Results

Figure 1a shows whole-brain tractograms for Patients A and B, who respectively achieved the highest and lowest reduction in Clinical Rating Scale of Tremor (CRST) score at 90 days post-treatment (70.8% vs. 45.6%). The colour of each streamline corresponds to its differential SIFT2 weight, with stronger weights reflecting a stronger reduction of Fibre Bundle Capacity (FBC).  

Both patients exhibited FBC reduction in streamlines overlapping the surgical lesion (blue ROI overlay), with higher reduction in ipsilateral thalamic-cortical tracts (coronal view, arrows). Patient A demonstrated extremely focal, pronounced FBC reduction in precise agreement with the Dentato-Rubro Thalamic Tract (DRTT) and the surgical lesion (axial view, arrows). Patient B showed similar anatomical agreement with the lesion site and DRTT, though with substantially less extensive and weaker FBC reduction (axial and coronal views, arrows). 

Unexpectedly, both patients also showed bilateral FBC reduction extending beyond the cerebellar-thalamic-cortical tract (CTCT), notably in the occipital lobe. This bi-lateral reduction was particularly pronounced for patient A.  

Figure 1b displays differential connectomes in MNI space for patients A (top), B (bottom), and patient C (middle, with the median reduction in total CRST score following a left-lateral lesion). 

In concordance with figure 1a, patient A's connectome exhibited high-magnitude bilateral FBC reduction within the CTCT network. At 90-days post-treatment, patient A showed CRST score improvement contralateral to the treated side and notable ipsilateral improvement, aligning with the observed bilateral connectivity changes.  

In contrast, Patient B's primary FBC reduction occurred between left frontal regions with only mild reduction across the left CTCT network. Here, the differential connectome showed unexpected FBC increases across cerebellar and right CTCT network edges, with mild increases between thalamic and superior medial frontal nodes, perhaps corresponding to supplementary motor area and lower-limb motor area. Coincidentally, tremor scores for left lower extremity improved by 2-points for patient B at 90 days post-surgery (1-pt each: active and postural). 

Finally, patient C demonstrated left-lateralized CTCT FBC reduction intermediate to Patients A and B. Interestingly, changes in FBC outside of the CTCT network were across similar nodes for patients B and C, but in opposing directions.  

These indicative data in three representative participants suggest a relationship between FBC reduction within DRTT and CRST improvement. Across all seven participants in this study, we observed a statistically significant FBC reduction between the ipsilesional Thalamus and Precentral nodes, with an average decrease of –0.13 [95% CI: -0.23 , -0.02] (p=0.023). For this edge, we also observed a weak correlation of –0.16 between FBC reduction and CRST improvement, but it was not statistically significant. 

Conclusion

Individual-level analyses are challenging and rare. Several studies have investigated network changes following MRgFUS in ET patients, using functional connectivity, cortical structural changes, or DWI metrics.4,5 However, these approaches relied on group-level analyses and overlook individual variability in treatment response and mechanisms. Group-level approaches intrinsically hinder the analysis of fibre-bundle density changes at the individual-level. 

Our findings suggest that MRgFUS treatment causes network reorganization beyond the primary therapeutic target, as previously hinted by group studies5. The observed bilateral connectivity changes, particularly in Patient A, may reflect compensatory mechanisms or secondary network effects following focal lesioning. The unexpected FBC increases in Patient B, correlating with symptom deterioration in specific body regions, could represent maladaptive plasticity or reveal individual variability in neural response to intervention. While further investigation is required to rule out methodological artifacts, these bilateral effects were consistently observed across all seven patients, hinting at broad network effects. Our results also indicate that the extent and magnitude of white matter reorganization may relate to clinical outcomes: patients with greater tremor improvement exhibited more pronounced FBC reductions across the whole-brain network.  

This study demonstrates the feasibility of tracking white matter microstructural alterations at the individual patient level following MRgFUS treatment. By leveraging a recently developed longitudinal DWI analysis framework, we demonstrated patient-specific patterns of fibre bundle capacity reduction in the cerebellar-thalamic-cortical network at 90 days post-surgery. These observations, while preliminary given the small sample size, illustrate that individual-level structural connectivity analyses can complement traditional group-level approaches.
