Introduction

Age-related decline in white matter integrity impairs neural communication, affecting both cognitive and motor function1. Major interhemispheric tracts such as the genu and splenium of the corpus callosum (GCC, SCC) and projection pathways like the posterior limb of the internal capsule (TPLIC) exhibit regionally specific vulnerability, with measurable consequences for behavioural and functional outcomes. Machine learning can quantify individual deviations from normative aging through Brain Age Gap Estimation (BrainAGE)2, an approach that estimates how much older or younger brain tissue appears compared to chronological age. Higher BrainAGE values indicate accelerated biological aging.

Sleep duration represents a modifiable behavioural factor that influences brain health. Both short and long sleep durations have been associated with disrupted brain homeostasis, neuroinflammation, cerebrovascular burden, and impaired myelin maintenance, with downstream effects on white matter microstructure3. Related work suggest that sleep variability may influence brain aging processes, yet tract-specific associations and their genetic contributions remain underexplored4.

Leveraging the scale of the UK Biobank, the aim of this study was to examine whether deviations from typical sleep duration are associated with accelerated aging of white matter pathways using BrainAGE, and to determine whether these associations are modified by common genetic variation. Analyses focused on three biologically informative tracts, GCC, SCC, and TPLIC, which reflect communication networks with distinct aging characteristics. Genome wide SNP by Sleep (G×E) interactions were evaluated to assess whether genetic effects on white matter aging vary as a function of sleep behaviour.

Methods

This cross-sectional study used data from the UK Biobank neuroimaging cohort and was restricted to individuals neurologically healthy, sleep duration reports, complete model covariates, and high-quality genetic data5. Participants with neurological or neurodegenerative ICD-10 diagnoses were excluded to reduce confounding and ensure a healthy aging sample for the BrainAGE model. Sleep duration was measured using the UK Biobank touchscreen questionnaire as self-reported average hours of sleep in a 24-hour period. Responses were treated as a continuous variable and mean-centered (Sleepc), with a quadratic term (Sleepc2) included to capture potential U-shaped associations reflecting short or long sleep duration relative to the cohort average (~7-8h/day). The primary analytic sample for the multivariable analysis comprised 39,623 neurologically healthy adults aged 45 to 83 years (mean = 64.2, SD = 7.7; 52.5% female). For gene-by-environment (G×E) interaction analyses, a slightly larger subset of 40,564 participants was analyzed, reflecting minimal lifestyle and genotyping missingness across phenotypes while retaining similar demographic characteristics

BrainAGE phenotypes were derived from diffusion MRI using NODDI intra-cellular volume fraction (ICVF) features extracted separately for the GCC, SCC, and TPLIC. Each tract-specific age-prediction model was trained using Random Forest Regression with nested cross validation (10 outer folds and 5 inner folds for hyperparameter tuning), where unbiased predictions were generated from held-out folds. Systematic age bias correction was applied only to training predictions following Beheshti et al. 2019, and final BrainAGE values were defined as the residual between predicted and chronological age, where positive values indicate older appearing white matter biological aging.

Multivariable ordinary least squares regression models were fitted separately for each white matter BrainAGE phenotype. Sleep duration terms (Sleepc and Sleepc2) were entered into models that also included moderate physical activity and alcohol frequency intake as additional lifestyle predictors and were adjusted for centered age and age squared, sex, age by sex and age squared by sex interactions, head size, genetic principal components (PC1 to PC6), and scanner-position covariates (brain_pos_x, brain_pos_y, brain_pos_z, scanner_table_pos). The false discovery rate (FDR < 0.05) was used to control for multiple testing across predictors and regions. Model fit (R2) and FDR corrected q values were reported.

Genome-wide SNP×Sleep interaction tests were conducted using PLINK v1.9 to identify variants where genetic effects on BrainAGE varied by sleep duration (suggestive threshold: p < 1×10-5). For each white matter tract, interaction coefficients tested whether genotype specific associations with BrainAGE differed across sleep duration. Predicted BrainAGE values were generated for visualisation by estimating model-based effects across genotype groups (aa, Aa, AA), stratified by sleep duration categories, while holding covariates at their mean values.

Results

BrainAGE was significantly associated with sleep duration across GCC, SCC, and TPLIC (Figure 1). Both short and long sleep durations were associated with accelerated white matter aging across all three tracts, revealing a consistent U-shaped pattern. Participants sleeping at the cohort average (7-8 hours) showed the youngest appearing white matter. The quadratic sleep term Sleepc2 confirmed U-shaped associations in all tracts: SCC (β = +0.016 years/unit, q < 0.0001), TPLIC (β = +0.020, q < 0.0001), and GCC (β = +0.015, q = 0.013). Linear effects of reduced Sleepc also contributed significantly to BrainAGE in GCC (β = +0.050, q < 0.0001) and TPLIC (β = +0.032, q = 0.001). While effects were modest (R2 = 0.017–0.059), the consistency across tracts suggests sleep duration explains measurable variance of white matter aging during mid-to-late adulthood.

In G×E analyses, no genome-wide significant interaction was detected (p < 5×10-8). Nonetheless, multiple suggestive loci (p < 1×10-5) were identified for each tract without genomic inflation, suggesting subtle genetic modulation of sleep’s effects on white matter aging. Genotype stratified ribbon plots (Figure 2) demonstrated tract specific SNP by Sleep interaction patterns on white matter BrainAGE. In GCC, homozygous effect allele carriers (AA) showed higher predicted BrainAGE under long sleep duration, whereas homozygous reference carriers (aa) exhibited relatively stable or lower values, with heterozygous individuals (Aa) showing intermediate patterns. In SCC, genotype related differences were attenuated, with minimal separation between aa and Aa genotypes and modest divergence primarily among AA carriers. In TPLIC, patterns were intermediate, with AA carriers again showing greater divergence across sleep duration groups, aa carriers exhibiting opposite or weaker trends, and heterozygous individuals displaying intermediate values. Across tracts, interaction effects were modest and confidence intervals largely overlapped.

Conclusion

This study identifies sleep duration as a behavioural marker associated with white matter aging across major interhemispheric and projection pathways. Tract specific BrainAGE emerged as a sensitive phenotype for detecting these associations, capturing latent microstructural aging effects beyond conventional neuroimaging metrics. Collectively, the findings demonstrate convergence on a biologically plausible behavioural pattern, whereby sleep duration maintained near an optimal range is associated with more normative aging trajectories in white matter pathways critical for cognitive integration and motor control.

Although genome wide SNP by Sleep interaction effects did not reach conventional significance thresholds, the presence of multiple suggestive loci across tracts supports further investigation of gene behaviour interplay in shaping heterogeneity in white matter aging. Replication in studies incorporating objective sleep assessment and longitudinal neuroimaging will be essential to clarify directionality and strengthen causal inference.

From a public health perspective, these results support sleep optimization as an accessible and modifiable target for maintaining white matter integrity across aging. However, the modest effect sizes observed indicate that sleep should be considered within a broader lifestyle framework that includes additional behavioural and environmental factors influencing brain health across the lifespan.
