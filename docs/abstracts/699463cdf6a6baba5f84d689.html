Introduction

Cognitive fatigue is defined as a neurophysiological state arising from sustained and/or effortful mental exertion. From a functional perspective, fatigue is thought to signal a cost-benefit imbalance computed by domain-general brain regions, such as the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC)1. A recent mechanistic account proposed that metabolic costs would also occur in cognitive control brain circuits (e.g., task-specific regions). More specifically, higher glutamate concentration was reported in the left lateral prefrontal cortex after a strenuous day of cognitive labour2, using single-voxel MR spectroscopy. Although these findings might account for the origin of fatigue throughout the workday, cognitive fatigue with elevated feelings of exhaustion and detrimental impacts on cognitive performance also manifests at short timescales. Paradoxically, short periods of break do not lead to immediate recovery from fatigue, hinting at a longer-term neurobiological after-effect of cognitive exertion3. To date, very few studies assessed brain-wide metabolic alterations under a state of cognitive fatigue.

Given the implication of multiple brain regions under cognitive fatigue, Magnetic Resonance Spectroscopic Imaging (MRSI), allowing large field of view multivoxel measurement, may be better suited to investigate the neurobiology of fatigue than single-voxel MR spectroscopy. Of interest for our research question, the joint measure of glutamate and glutamine (GLX) can be reliably quantified in the form of a ratio (GLX:tCR) in 3D grids of voxels using free induction decay based-MRSI and concentric ring trajectory (3D-CRT-FID-MRSI)4. In this study, we used 3D-CRT-FID-MRSI at 7T to test the hypothesis that the GLX:tCR ratio increases in task-specific areas as cognitive fatigue arises and does not immediately return to baseline level during a short period of rest.

Methods

So far, we recruited and included in the analyses 36 participants (18-40 y.o., x women; total sample target, N=50) equally split in two groups. During a first visit, we calibrated individually the difficulty of a cognitive task meant for fatigue induction (Time Load Dual Back task). The task consists of a combination of a 1-back working memory task and a number parity judgment task during which participants see letters and numbers in alternation5. Group assignment was based on whether participants would be administered either the low or high cognitive load condition (LCL and HCL, respectively), with the latter meant to induce higher states of cognitive fatigue. The main difference between conditions consists of the time allowed for participants to respond to each item, the HCL version being fast paced. On a second visit, participants performed the cognitive task for 32 minutes inside the 7T-MRI scanner (Terra, Siemens, Germany). 3D-CRT-FID-MRSI acquisitions (~8 minutes) were performed several times: prior, during and after the task. Subjective experiences (i.e., fatigue) were assessed using Visual Analog Scales every 8 minutes from the beginning of the cognitive task.

MRSI allowed us to capture metabolite concentrations over a 9-cm slab selected by RF pulses that was positioned to cover most of the brain regions related to the exertion of cognitive control (i.e., frontal and parietal cortices). Sequence parameters4 were as follows: # of rings = 22; FoV = 220x220x145mm; Matrix Size = 44x44x29 at 5mm3 isotropic; Slab Volume = 220x220x90mm; TA = 8:22; TR = 600ms; Acquisition Delay = 1.3ms; FA = 42°; Spectral Bandwidth = 2778 Hz; one average. The water signal was suppressed using the WET method. For each subject, MRSI data were acquired five times: at rest before the cognitive task, at the beginning, the middle and the end of the task, and at rest after the task. T1w structural images were also acquired for spectral processing (MPRAGE; TA = 2:46; voxel-size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm).

MRSI data were processed and spectra reconstructed online through Fourier transformation of the detected FID signal (see 4 for details). Subsequently, an offline processing pipeline used FSL to derive subject-specific brain masks with T1w images registered into MRSI image space. Voxels within brain masks were fitted individually to a basis set of 19 simulated metabolites with LCModel. Total Creatine (tCR) was used as the internal concentration reference and whole-brain GLX:tCR maps were next extracted for each subject and session. Slices outside the 9-cm slab of interest were cropped and quality assurance was performed on fitted voxels. For each participant separately, we estimated the GLX absolute Cramér-Rao Lower Bounds distribution and exclude voxels above the 90th percentile. Voxels with a Signal-to-Noise ratio above 3 and a full width a half maximum (FWHM) of the NAA peak below 0.1 ppm were also excluded (see Fig. 1 for an example of final metabolite maps for one subject).

We then conducted Region-of-Interests (ROI) analyses by first extracting two binary masks over the bilateral ACC and the left dorso-lateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) using the Harvard-Oxford anatomical atlas. Second, we non-linearly aligned our ROI masks to subject’s T1-space, which allowed us to overlap the metabolite maps – containing only voxels that passed quality assurance – and the ROI masks. For each subject and session, we then extracted the median GLX:tCR value within the intersection masks, that is between ROI masks and metabolite maps. Statistics consisted of separate Linear Mixed Models (LMM) seeking the main effects of condition (HCL vs LCL) and time as well as the interaction effect. Models also included a by-subject random intercept; Kenward-Roger approximations were used to estimate p-values.

Results

The quality assurance procedure showed that, on average, 59% ± 8% of voxels are excluded from further analysis (min = 38%, max = 73%). Importantly, LMM showed that the ratio of voxel loss did not significantly change over time (β = 0.00, t(168) = -1.11, CI = [-0.01 – 0.00], p = 0.271) nor between conditions (β = 0.03, t(168) = 1.38, CI = [-0.01 – 0.08], p = 0.171). This suggests that our manipulation did not increase participants’ movements throughout the MRI acquisition. The visual inspection at the subject-level revealed that most of the excluded voxels lie in posterior parts of the brain, despite variability within our sample.

Metabolic results are displayed on Fig. 2A. LMM fitted on median GLX:tCR ratio showed significant effects of time (β = 0.04, t(168) = 2.52, CI = [0.01 – 0.07], p < .05) and condition*time interaction (β = 0.07, t(168) = 0.83, CI = [0.00 – 0.13], p < .05) in the left DLPFC. Results from post-hoc pairwise t-tests further suggested an increase in GLX ratio from the beginning of the task to the end of the task in the LCL condition only (LCL T3 – LCL T1; β = 0.11, z = -3.56, pFDR < .05). We did not observe statistical difference between conditions (β = 0.00, t(168) = -1.11, CI = [-0.02 – 0.02], p = 0.921) nor with time (β = -0.02, t(168) = -1.11, CI = [-0.09 – 0.05], p = 0.553) for GLX:tCR ratio in the bilateral ACC.

In addition, LMM fitted on behavioral data showed that cognitive fatigue significantly increased with time (β = 6.57, t(173) = 18.37, CI = [5.86 – 7.28], p < .001) despite no condition (β = 1.12, t(173) = 1.41, CI = [11.72 – 13.29], p = 0.161) or interaction effects (β = 0.84, t(173) = 1.18, CI = [-0.57 – 2.25], p = 0.241). Task accuracy was significantly lower in the HCL condition compared to the LCL condition (β = -0.16, t(134) = -7.32, CI = [-0.21 – -0.12], p < .001) but decreased over time in both conditions (β = -0.07, t(134) = -10.97, CI = [-0.08 – -0.06], p < .001). Of note, qualitative inspection of performance highlights that participants could still achieve ~80% accuracy during the last task-block of the LCL condition compared to ~65% accuracy in the HCL condition (see Fig. 2C).

Conclusion

Our findings suggest glutamatergic increases with time-on-task within a task-specific region (i.e., the left DLPFC). Unexpectedly, this effect seems to be driven by the low – and not the high – cognitive load condition. In contrast, our analysis did not reveal metabolic change within a general domain region (i.e., bilateral ACC), although activity within this region has previously been correlated with cognitive fatigue sensation1. Our study is ongoing, so our results are preliminary. Still, our preliminary results are in line with the metabolic account of cognitive fatigue which suggests that metabolic changes occur in cognitive control brain regions during effortful activities2.

Integrating behavioral and subjective data into our metabolic framework, we further hypothesize that higher cognitive load may have induced task disengagement due to excessive difficulty. In contrast, the low-load condition may have facilitated compensatory effort, enabling participants to continue meeting task demand at the expense of increased fatigue level. To further support our hypothesis, future analyses should also investigate the presence of a negative correlation between metabolic and behavioral changes (i.e., the higher the increase in DLPFC GLX:tCR and the lower the decrease in task accuracy).
