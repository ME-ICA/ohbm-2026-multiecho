Introduction

All brains are individual in structure and function. Functional differences can be captured in neural fingerprints that comprise brain features characteristic to each individual.1 Previous research efforts have shown that neural fingerprints – composed of individual brain features – can be derived from fMRI as well as from MEG signals.1–3 Neural fingerprints also explain behavioral differences, and are affected by brain diseases, such as Alzheimer’s disease.1, 2, 4 Thus, neural fingerprints are a potential tool for individual neuroimaging research and diagnostics.
    Functional connectomes as fingerprints can, however, impose problems in practical applications. Functional connectomes with more than 1000 dimensions have been used in previous studies.1, 2 The high dimensionality could increase computational cost and hinder performance of machine learning models due to the curse of dimensionality. Connectome fingerprints also only work within individuals; hence a modelling approach is needed to extract individual features generalizable to unseen individuals. Latent noise Bayesian Reduced Rank Regression (lnBRRR) is a machine-learning model that can address these issues.5 The model learns a low-dimensional latent space from observed data based on user-chosen independent variables and the structured noise within the observed data. LnBRRR has already been proven viable for neural fingerprinting,3 yet some fundamental questions remain unanswered.
    The present study aimed to uncover whether lnBRRR remains functional at typical sample sizes for neuroimaging studies (N=20-40) and if task data can be utilized to create latent fingerprints. In addition, model generalizability and similarity across task conditions was assessed. Model solutions were visualized at brain level, and the capability of the latent space to predict individual characteristics was examined by prediction of subject age and gender.

Methods

The present study utilized preprocessed Human Connectome Project (HCP) MEG data from resting-state, working memory, and language (story-math task) conditions. Story and math trials of language task were treated as separate tasks. Two recordings from each condition were used for model training and identification. 87 subjects with resting-state recordings were divided into two subsets sized 43-44, and samples of N=20-44 were selected from the subsets to train lnBRRR on low sample sizes. For task identification, 68 subjects with recordings from all conditions available were selected and divided into five cross-validation folds of N=13-14. For both data splits, the subset not used for model training served as a test set.
    Source localization was performed with LCMV beamformer, and the source signals (195s) were clustered into 62 AAL atlas ROIs by PCA (Fig. 1a). After source localization, ROI signals were divided into 45 four-second epochs with zero overlap and two seconds of real data padding for functional connectivity computation. Functional connectivity (phase-locking value [PLV], imaginary PLV, phase linearity measurement [PLM], and amplitude envelope correlation [AEC]) were computed for the canonical frequency bands (delta: 2-4Hz, theta: 4-8Hz, alpha: 8-13Hz, low beta: 13-20Hz, high beta: 20-30Hz, and gamma: 30-45Hz). Power spectral density (PSD) was computed with Welch’s method using 4s window and 50% overlap. PSD was divided into 21 frequency bins over 1-88Hz.
    LnBRRR is defined by: Y = (XΨ + Ω)Γ + E, where Y is observed data, X is the independent variables, Ψ maps the independent variables to the latent space, Ω is latent noise, Γ maps the latent space to the observed data, and E is independent noise. LnBRRR was trained on two observations per subject, and subjects were used as the independent variables, i.e. observations from each subject comprised their own group with two observations. Ten-dimensional latent space was learnt in all parts of the study. 1000 iterations (50% burn-in iterations) were used to train the model. Posterior point estimate of Γ was used to project the observed data into the latent space for identification. Cosine distance was used to measure distances in the latent space. One recording from each subject was iteratively compared to a set of second recordings from all subjects, and if subject’s own recordings were the closest, identification was successful.
    Random baseline was generated for the low sample size test accuracies to ensure that the lnBRRR accuracies were not due to chance. Point estimate of posterior Γ was bootstrapped row wise to create random Γ which was used to map Y into random space for identification. 500 repetitions were used to form a null distribution against which lnBRRR accuracies were compared. P values (p = k+1/501, where k is number of null distribution accuracies equal or higher than the lnBRRR accuracy) were computed and corrected by Benjamini-Hochberg method.
    To assess similarity of LnBRRR solutions cosine similarity between the components (orthonormalized Γ rows) of two models were computed. LnBRRR was trained with 68 participants in all conditions. To evaluate whether two solutions are similar up to rotation, similarity was measured before and after rotational alignment by orthogonal Procrustes analysis, which finds an orthogonal rotation that maximally aligns two matrices (Fig. 1b).
    To visualize lnBRRR solutions at brain level, average weights per ROI were computed for each condition from the posterior point estimate of Γ. Weights on Γ rows were standardized to zero mean and unit variance before computation of average ROI weights (Z-score).
    The resting-state latent space used for similarity comparisons was used to predict subject gender and age. Support Vector Machines (SVM) were used for the classification of the gender and regression of age. The 19 subjects not used in model training were used as a test set for classification and regression.

Results

LnBRRR was used to learn low-dimensional latent spaces from resting-state data to determine which metrics perform best in identification. Mean±SEM accuracies were computed. PLV achieved best test accuracies (Beta low: 0.89 ± 0.04, Beta high: 0.82 ± 0.02, Gamma:0.84±0.03), but gamma-band PLM (0.82±0.02) and AEC (0.78±0.03) also reached comparable performance. PSD reached test accuracy of 0.84 ± 0.03. Based on the best observed results PLV and PSD were selected for the subsequent experiments.
    Task accuracies for lnBRRR were first assessed within task. Best PLV frequency band depended on the task condition; for working memory task best accuracies were obtained with high beta (0.93±0.014) and gamma (0.91±0.026). Alpha band was pronounced in story (0.88±0.012) and math (0.82±0.087) conditions. Gamma-band PLV results are shown in Figure 2a, as it reached good performance across the tasks. PSD achieved overall comparable performance to the best PLV frequency band (working memory: 0.93±0.012, story: 0.88±0.05, math: 0.78±0.039) (Fig. 2a). Cross-condition accuracies – accuracies obtained with resting-state data and lnBRRR solutions from different conditions – were overall high and almost equal to he accuracies acquired by resting-state model (Fig. 2b).
    To evaluate lnBRRR performance with low sample sizes, the model was trained with N=20-44. Training accuracies began to increase below N=35 and N=30 for PSD and (gamma-band) PLV, respectively (Fig. 2c). Below N=35 PLV test accuracy also decreased, whereas PSD test accuracy remained stable even at N=20 (Fig. 2c). Test accuracies were compared to the random baseline, and all test accuracies surpassed the baseline statistically significantly after multiple comparisons correction.
    To measure similarity between lnBRRR solutions (training N=68) from different conditions, cosine similarity was computed between Γ matrices. Prior to rotational alignment the models were mostly dissimilar between conditions for both PLV and PSD. However, when the rotations were aligned by orthogonal Procrustes analysis, PSD model solutions from all conditions were revealed to be very similar (Fig. 2d). PLV models remained dissimilar also after the rotation. The similarity between two resting state models trained on different subjects (N=43-44) was also measured to examine model solution generalizability. PSD models had three similar components after rotational alignment and PLV models had none.
    Resting-state lnBRRR solution (N=68) was visualized at brain level. Average weights per ROI were computed and visualized. The present study focused on the PSD model as PSD models were shown to be better generalized. Figure 2 illustrates components 1-3 of the model solution. Figure 2e illustrates the average weights of the first three components from resting-state and working memory models. Although the averages were computed from non-rotated weighting matrices, Component 1 and Component 3 appear similar between the conditions (Fig. 2e).
    Age and gender of the participants were predicted based on their latent fingerprints. SVM acquired poor results on the age prediction (PLV test RMSE: 3.4y and R2=0.01, PSD test RMSE: 3.8y and R2=-0.21), but gender classification performance was notably better: PLV achieved a test accuracy of 0.63 (Matthew’s corr. coef.=0.35) and PSD a test accuracy of 0.79 (Matthew’s corr. coef.=0.65).

Conclusion

The present study assessed lnBRRR identification performance with several metrics, task data, and low sample sizes. PLV reached overall best performance in the resting-state but gamma-band PLM and AEC as well as PSD also attained good performance. The model achieved comparable accuracies in comparison to previous full connectome fingerprinting results.2 The high level of test accuracies (0.8-0.9) suggests that individual features learnt by lnBRRR also generalize beyond the training set. It should be noted that the test accuracies could have been slightly inflated due to the small test set size (N=13-14).
    LnBRRR learnt generalizable fingerprints with sample sizes N=20-44. Still, signs of overfitting were noticeable at and below N=35. It is possible that the training accuracies could have increased due to chance as the training sample sizes decreased. Still, the decreased test accuracy of gamma-band PLV is a certain sign of overfitting as the test set size was unchanged. Despite the overfitting, all models achieved statistically significant test accuracy over the random baseline, which demonstrates that lnBRRR learns generalizable latent solutions even with N=20. Nevertheless, a sample size of 35 or larger is recommended for optimal performance.
Task data lnBRRR achieved better or comparable performance to lnBRRR on resting-state data. This result suggests that task conditions may bring out more individual characteristics in brain activity, or that spontaneous activity in the resting state is more variable than task activity, partly covering the individual features. LnBRRR was observed to generalize over the task conditions, which has also been previously observed with fMRI connectomes.1 The overall high cross-conditional identification accuracies imply that the latent representation of the individual features could be similar over the task conditions.
    The cross-conditional similarity of lnBRRR solutions was examined by comparing Γ matrices with cosine similarity. Power spectra solutions were initially dissimilar but when rotation was allowed for notable similarity was observed. This further supports the notion that regardless of the task-induced brain activity the latent pattern of individual features remains stably similar.
    LnBRRR solutions from separate training samples were only modestly similar for PSD and completely dissimilar for PLV after rotational alignment. This indicates that only part of the latent representation learnt by lnBRRR at N=43-44 is generalizable when PSD is used for training and highlights the importance of larger datasets for obtaining reliable descriptions of individual characteristics from brain data.
LnBRRR weights were visualized at brain level. Component 1 contrasted frontal and temporal areas against parietal and occipital areas and Component 2 repeated a similar pattern to Component 1. Component 3 weighted solely the cingulum whereas other areas had negligible weights. The high weights on cingulum coincide with previous results that showed power spectral density of the cingulum to be important for identification of individuals.2
    Lastly, PSD latent neural fingerprints were able to predict subject gender with a high accuracy (0.79), which demonstrates that information regarding subject characteristics can be conveyed by the neural fingerprints. The narrow age range (22-35y) likely impeded age prediction.
    To summarize, the present study showed that sample sizes typical for neuroimaging suffice to learn generalizable latent fingerprints. LnBRRR was also viable with task data, and some task conditions were observed to enhance fingerprinting performance. The model generalizability and similarity between the tasks highlighted that similar patterns of individual power spectral features are retained regardless of the brain activity induced by the task condition. Altogether, the results demonstrated that lnBRRR is a potential tool for individualized neuroimaging research and diagnostics.
