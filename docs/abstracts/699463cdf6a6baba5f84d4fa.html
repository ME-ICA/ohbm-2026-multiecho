Introduction

Developmental dyslexia (DD) is a common neurodevelopmental disorder. According to the temporal sampling framework, dyslexia may arise from atypical neural oscillations and inefficient synchronization to rhythmic sensory input,1 previous studies have shown evidence of reduced neural entrainment in the auditory modality in individuals with DD2, 3, however, it is unknown why neural entrainment is reduced in DD. We hypothesize that an imbalance between excitatory and inhibitory (E/I) brain activity, leading to abnormal neural noise4, 5 may be a driving force of reduced neural entrainment in DD. 

In the present study, we used SSVEP–EEG to investigate visual neural entrainment, and E/I balance in children and adults with DD, and using recurrent neural networks (RNN) we simulated whether E/I imbalance causes reduced neural entrainment. We aim to address three key questions: (1) Is visual neural entrainment consistently altered in dyslexia across different age groups? (2) Are these entrainment differences systematically related to E/I imbalance? (3) Can an imbalance between E/I explain abnormal neural entrainment in DD?

Methods

In this study, we recruited 44 university students and 38 fifth‑grade children. Among adults, 20 were typically developing controls and 24 had developmental dyslexia; among children, 21 were controls and 17 had dyslexia. There were no significant group differences in IQ (children: z = 0.86, p = 0.195; adults: z = 0.56, p = 0.288) or age (children: t(45) = 0.42, p = 0.674; adults: t(45) = 1.55, p = 0.128) between controls and DD.

Visual stimulation consisted of rhythmic flicker in two hemifields: the left hemifield flickered at 6 Hz and the right hemifield at 7.5 Hz, thereby eliciting steady‑state responses at two fundamental frequencies (6 and 7.5 Hz) and their intermodulation frequency (f1+f2 = 13.5 Hz) in the EEG. Participants with dyslexia and age‑matched controls passively fixated three types of rhythmic visual stimuli (i.e. real characters, pseudo characters, faces) while high‑density EEG was recorded, providing objective indices of visual neural entrainment at multiple frequencies.

SSVEP amplitudes were extracted at each stimulation frequency and its harmonics, and neural entrainment strength was quantified using the signal‑to‑noise ratio (SNR), defined as the ratio of power at the target frequency and its harmonics to the average power in neighbouring, non‑stimulated frequency bins.

To quantify E/I balance, we decomposed the EEG power spectrum into periodic and aperiodic components and extracted the slope of the aperiodic 1/f component as a proxy index of E/I imbalance. These 1/f measures were computed over occipital and frontal electrode clusters showing the strongest SSVEP responses.

Finally, we implemented recurrent neural network (RNN) models informed by the empirical EEG‑derived parameters to simulate how different excitation–inhibition regimes impact network‑level dynamics and the efficiency of responses to rhythmic input, providing a mechanistic modelling perspective on the link between E/I balance, and neural entrainment.

Results

Neural entrainment abnormality. Whole‑brain SSVEP SNR at 6 Hz showed main effects of group (F(1,118.19)=96.27, p<0.001; controls>SNR) and age (F(1,62.21)=237.76, p<0.001; adults>children), as well as a Group × Age × Type interaction (F(2,186.53)=4.13, p=0.018). In children, SNR was consistently higher in controls than in DD across all stimulus types (real characters: z=10.76; pseudo‑characters: z=5.59; faces: z=8.69; all p_bonferroni<0.001), whereas in adults, group differences disappeared (all |z|≤2.06, p_bonferroni≥0.239). At 7.5 Hz, we again observed main effects of group (F(1,88.04)=58.77, p<0.001) and age (F(1,61.56)=177.66, p<0.001) plus a Group × Age × Type interaction (F(2,160.99)=8.64, p<0.001). In children, SNR was always higher in controls than in DD (all z≥8.41, p_bonferroni<0.001), whereas in adults, SNR was higher in DD than in controls for real and pseudo‑characters (real: z=5.89; pseudo: z=5.09; both p_bonferroni<0.001). At 13.5 Hz, we found main effects of group (F(1,114.49)=6.78, p=0.010) and age (F(1,116.15)=5.25, p=0.024) and a Group × Age interaction (F(1,132.18)=9.16, p=0.003) when averaging across stimulus types: the control>dyslexia difference was reliable only in children (z=3.83, p_bonferroni<0.001) but not in adults.  However, in the character condition, reduced 13.5 Hz entrainment in DD was evident in both children and adults, whereas no group difference emerged for pseudo-characters, and adults with DD even showed increased entrainment for faces

E/I and neural entrainment. Across the full sample, the aperiodic E/I exponent was not reliably associated with SSVEP SNR at 6, 7.5, or 13.5 Hz in any ROI (all |r|≤0.18, all p≥0.10). However, age‑ and group‑specific effects emerged when correlations were examined within ROIs. In the left frontal ROI, E/I was positively related to intermodulation (13.5 Hz) SNR in adults with dyslexia (r=0.44, p=0.0377) but not in adult controls (r=0.13, p=0.58), and this association became stronger after controlling for age in the full sample (partial r=0.24, p=0.0296), suggesting that higher E/I is linked to more efficient higher‑order entrainment specifically in dyslexic adults. In the right frontal ROI, E/I showed no consistent relationship with SNR at any frequency either in the full sample (all |r|≤0.05, all p≥0.67) or within individual groups, except for a trend‑level association with mean frontal SNR in adults with dyslexia (r=0.46, p=0.0282). In the occipital ROI, E/I was largely unrelated to local entrainment (all |r|≤0.12, all p≥0.30), although children with typical reading showed a moderate negative correlation between E/I and mean SNR (r=-0.52, p=0.0146), indicating that higher occipital E/I was associated with weaker posterior entrainment only in this subgroup.

RNN results. A RNN model simulation is conducted, and results will be updated to explain whether imbalanced E/I is a driving force of reduced neural entrainment.

Conclusion

These findings demonstrate that dyslexia is characterized by reduced visual neural entrainment, but only in children not adults with DD. E/I was linked to entrainment in a highly context‑dependent manner: they were positively associated with fundamental SSVEP strength in the sample as a whole, yet in adults with dyslexia higher exponents predicted reduced entrainment at 13.5 Hz in the frontal and occipital regions. Together, these results suggest that elevated E/I imbalance reflect dynamic, region‑ and age‑dependent modulation of neural entrainment rather than a unitary biomarker of the disorder.
