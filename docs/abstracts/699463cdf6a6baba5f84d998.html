Introduction

Neural representations associated with past experiences are spontaneously reinstated during offline periods such as sleep and rest, a key process thought to support memory stabilization1. We recently showed that spontaneous memory reactivation during wakeful rest is not random, but instead favors internally over externally oriented brain states (e.g., default versus task-positive networks)2. The brain is known to intermittently enter brief ‘offline’ or internally oriented states even while individuals are continuously attending to external stimulation3. However, it remains unclear whether these bouts of internal brain states preferentially promote reactivation at the expense of attention to the external environment. Moreover, spontaneous reactivation has rarely been probed using naturalistic stimuli such as video content, despite their increasing use in cognitive neuroscience studies and relevance to real-world memory. To address these questions, we examined whether reactivation of recently encoded naturalistic videos aligns with particular brain states during prolonged attention to external stimuli, and whether this coupling is inversely related to slow fluctuations in task performance.

Methods

Ten participants aged 22-45 years (mean=29.7 years, 4 females) completed multiple fMRI sessions comprising a sustained external attention task (psychomotor vigilance task, PVT) and short-form video (SFV) viewing. During the PVT, participants made button-press responses to visual targets presented at variable inter-target intervals (2-6 s). In each session, participants first performed a baseline PVT (9 min) and then underwent six successive runs of SFV viewing (7 min), with each run followed by an immediate post-encoding PVT (3 min). From the SFV viewing data, we derived a neural representation for each SFV by averaging (z-scored) voxel-wise BOLD responses across all time points within the video . We selected memory-related cortical regions of interest (ROIs) that were sensitive to SFV content based on inter-subject correlations of spatial patterns from a whole-brain searchlight analysis4. Reactivation events were defined as time points during the subsequent PVT in which activation patterns within individual ROIs were similar to those evoked by SFV viewing2. To characterize global brain states during reactivation events, we performed k-means clustering on whole-brain activity from the baseline PVT, which isolated coactivation patterns that reflect large-scale network states recurring over time5. Each time point of the post-encoding PVT was then assigned to the brain state with the highest pattern similarity. To assess whether the coupling between reactivation and brain states depends on the level of external attention, we compared reactivation event rates across brain states between time windows with high versus low response time variability.

Results

We identified three pairs of distinct brain states from the baseline PVT. The State 1A/B pair captured internal versus external processing modes, differentially weighting default versus dorsal and ventral attention networks. The State 2A/B pair highlighted a contrast in joint deactivation (2A) and activation (2B) of the dorsal attention and frontoparietal networks, which were similarly weighted relative to the low weighting of other networks. The State 3A/B pair differentially weighted activity along the sensorimotor-association axis or the principal gradient (i.e., visual and somatomotor networks versus transmodal association networks). We focused on reactivation of naturalistic SFV content in the retrosplenial and medial parietal cortices (posterior cingulate), which showed strong SFV-specific representations during video watching. Reactivation preferentially occurred during State 1A (compared to State 1B, p < .005), a state characterized by internal versus external processing. In addition, within the retrosplenial cortex, this preferential coupling with State 1A was more pronounced during time windows with high versus low response variability (p = .004).

Conclusion

We found that post-encoding reactivation preferentially occurred during a brain state characterized by default versus dorsal/ventral attention network activity, particularly when behavior became more variable. This suggests that internally oriented states may promote reactivation of recent naturalistic experiences while compromising external engagement. These findings suggest that brief, internally oriented brain states emerging during sustained attention may serve as privileged windows for spontaneous memory reactivation, highlighting a fundamental trade-off between stable external attention and ongoing internally-oriented memory processes.
