Introduction

Brain age is an in vivo neuroimaging marker of brain health. Models use imaging features to determine how old a person’s brain appears. Brain age provides biologically meaningful markers of individual aging trajectories linked to cognitive decline, cardiovascular health, and neurodegenerative risk. Because these models summarize deviation from normative aging trajectories, they provide a robust, continuous biomarker capable of detecting subtle changes long before clinical symptoms emerge.1 However, nearly all established brain age models rely primarily on T1-weighted structural MRI, which uses morphometric features such as gray matter volume and cortical thickness. As a consequence, these models insufficiently represent white matter (WM) integrity, risking incomplete characterization of whole-brain aging processes.

WM microstructure often declines earlier and more rapidly than gray matter. WM decline is strongly associated with small-vessel disease, reduced cognitive efficiency, and disruptions in large-scale network communication. Because WM pathways show the brain’s functional architecture, degradation may reflect aging-related changes that traditional brain age models cannot. Thus, diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) derived structural connectomes offer a powerful complement by enabling the assessment of how aging impacts structural connectivity. Furthermore, connectome-based models would allow for the evaluation of aging vulnerability on the network level. Networks such as the Default Mode Network (DMN), Visual Network (VIS), Sensorimotor Network (SMN), and Language Network (LAN) differ markedly in their metabolic cost and susceptibility to vascular or neurodegenerative processes.

By modeling chronological age from whole-brain structural connectomes and repeating these analyses within specific large-scale networks, we test whether particular networks disproportionately capture age-related changes and evaluate premature connectome aging as a complementary biomarker.

Methods

This study included N=367 participants (aged 20-80) from the Aging Brain Cohort (ABC) Study at the University of South Carolina. Demographic, behavioral, and neuroimaging data (T1-weighted, T2-weighted, and diffusion echo-planar imaging (EPI)) were collected from each participant. Diffusion images were preprocessed and corrected by susceptibility distortion correction (TOPUP), eddy-current and motion correction (EDDY), denoising, Gibbs ringing removal, and bias-field correction. Diffusion tensors were fit voxelwise (DTIFIT), followed by deterministic whole-brain tractography. Streamlines were mapped to a standardized anatomical parcellation to produce weighted structural connectomes. Briefly, T1-weighted images were segmented using SPM12’s unified segmentation-normalization, and gray-matter parcellations were generated using the Johns Hopkins University (JHU) 189-region atlas. Parcellations were nonlinearly registered to diffusion space. Probabilistic tractography was performed using FSL’s diffusion toolbox with BEDPOSTX modeling of voxelwise diffusion parameters, and probtrackX was used to estimate pairwise connectivity between all region pairs (5,000 samples per seed, curvature threshold = 0.2, step length = 0.5 mm, distance correction enabled). Connection strengths were defined as the number of probabilistic streamlines between each pair of regions, corrected for interregional distance and node volume, yielding a weighted 189×189 connectome for each participant.

Support Vector Regression (SVR) with a radial kernel was implemented using nested cross-validation and an exhaustive grid search over cost and gamma parameters. Models were trained on (1) the whole connectome, and (2) network-specific subsets of edges corresponding to the DMN, VIS, SMN, and LAN, as defined by previous work.2 Model performance was quantified using MAE and R² across outer folds. BAGs were calculated by subtracting chronological age from SVR calculated brain age. We also calculated BAGs in the same participants from a traditional brain age model (Volbrain’s BrainStructureAges).3 To compare connectome BAGs to this traditional model, we performed a Pearson’s r correlation test. 

Results

The whole-brain SVR model showed strong predictive accuracy (MAE=8.51, R²=.703). Network-specific models revealed substantial variability: DMN age prediction (MAE=12.63, R²=.361), and LAN age prediction (MAE=11.54, R2=.44) outperformed VIS (MAE=16.12, R²=-.031) and SMN (MAE=12.66, R²=.288). Additionally, connectome-derived BAGs only showed a modest but significant correlation with classical T1-weighted-based BAGs (r=.29, p<0.0001), indicating that our model captures partially overlapping but largely distinct biological variance. 

Conclusion

By modeling brain age directly from white matter connections, we can capture both global disconnection and network-specific vulnerability, providing a mechanistically grounded biomarker that complements and improves upon classical gray-matter brain age estimates. The relationship between traditional models and this model supports the idea that WM-based aging reflects unique aspects of disconnection and network vulnerability. Among the networks examined, the LAN and DMN provided the most accurate age predictions, aligning with evidence that their densely interconnected hub regions mature late and are the first to show age-related changes.4 These networks’ heightened vulnerability likely enhances their sensitivity to aging effects, explaining their superior predictive performance relative to VIS and SMN.
