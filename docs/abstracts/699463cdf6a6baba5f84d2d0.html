Introduction

Language lateralization can be studied using several neuroimaging techniques such as the Wada test, ECOG, MEG, or fMRI. Several studies have attempted to establish a TMS protocol for this purpose,1-2 but the results have shown only moderate agreement with other well-established techniques, especially in cases of atypical language lateralization.3 The present study aims to develop and validate a new TMS protocol specifically designed to assess language lateralization, using fMRI as the validation procedure.

We will employ a verb-generation picture task, a paradigm that reliably engages the left inferior frontal gyrus (IFG). According to Thompson-Schill et al.,4 this region plays a central role in the controlled retrieval and selection of semantic information, so its stimulation is expected to interfere with the process of generating verbs from pictures.

Our main objective is to develop a TMS protocol to explore language lateralization through a verb-generation picture task in a sample of healthy right- and left-handed participants with typical and atypical language lateralization. We expect that magnetic stimulation applied to the dominant hemisphere will induce stronger interference in language production in terms of accuracy and reaction times. Compared with previous studies, our protocol aims to provide better correspondence between TMS-induced behavioral effects and lateralization patterns derived from fMRI.

Methods

Twenty-nine participants were recruited (10 left-handers with atypical language lateralization to the right hemisphere; 10 right-handers and 9 left-handers, both with typical language lateralization to the left hemisphere).

In the first session, participants underwent a structural 3D scan on a 3T Siemens scanner, as well as a functional verb-generation5 task in which they had to say aloud an associated verb in response to a visually presented noun. Functional data were pre-processed according to the standard pipeline using SPM12. Using the LI-toolbox for SPM12, we calculated the Laterality Index (LI) for language, based on activation of the inferior frontal gyrus during the fMRI verb-generation task, applying the bootstrap method.

In the second session, stimulation was carried out using a biphasic repetitive stimulator (Super Rapid 2, Magstim, Whitland, UK). We obtained individual motor thresholds for each hemisphere in every participant, and our initial aim was to apply TMS at 100% of the motor threshold. Both hemispheres were stimulated across three anatomical regions based on the Harvard–Oxford Atlas (Region 1: x = ±49, y = 19, z = 5; Region 2: x =± 49, y = 31, z = 24; Region 3: x = ±49, y = 11, z = 24) and a fourth region derived from the peak activation of the verb-generation task in the scanner. All participants were instructed to perform a verb-generation picture task while receiving magnetic pulses. Our TMS protocol consisted of five pulses at 5 Hz, time-locked to the presentation of each stimulus over 1000 ms.

We conducted repeated-measures analyses with Hemisphere (left or right) and Region of Interest (ROI) as within-subject factors, and Language Group (Typical-Left; Atypical-Left; Typical-Right) as the between-subject factor, for each dependent variable (accuracy, errors, omissions, and naming times). Additionally, we calculated a Laterality Index for accuracy and reaction times (Left Hemisphere – Right Hemisphere) to assess the correspondence between the two techniques.

Results

We found significant Hemisphere × Language Group interactions for accuracy [F (2,24) = 36.67, P= <.001, ηp² = 0.75]; errors [F (2,24) = 29.04, P= <.001, ηp² = 0.70] and omissions [F (2,24) = 4.85, P= <.001, ηp² = 0.28], suggesting that the effect of stimulation hemisphere on task performance differed between typical and atypical language groups. As expected, the Atypical-L group showed better performance when the coil was positioned over the left hemisphere, whereas the two typical language groups (L and R) performed better when stimulation targeted the right hemisphere. These results indicate that the behavioural impact of stimulation reflects the hemispheric dominance for language production. 

In addition, our results showed a trend toward slower naming times when the dominant hemisphere was stimulated, although only found significant differences between left-handed groups [F (1,15) = 6.58, p = 0.022, ηp² =0.30], with both groups showing longer naming times when the dominant hemisphere for language was stimulated. 

Regarding the correspondence between both techniques, we obtained a concordance of 90% for Typical R, 88.9% for Typical L and 100% for atypical R for accuracy, which is substantially higher than what has been reported in previous studies.

Conclusion

Our protocol demonstrates a strong correspondence between fMRI and nTMS in determining language lateralization in both left-handers and right-handers with different patterns of language organization. These findings support the reliability and utility of our TMS-based approach for identifying the dominant language hemisphere, showing clear convergence between behavioral interference effects and fMRI activation patterns. Moreover, they contribute to strengthening a literature that has remained poorly consolidated, providing a replicable procedure that is applicable to both clinical and experimental contexts.
