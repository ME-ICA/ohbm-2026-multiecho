Introduction

test anxiety is a type of situation-specific anxiety characterized by physiological and psychological reactions when individuals are exposed to test-related stimuli. Research suggests that such anxiety can lead to inhibitory control deficits in individuals with high test anxiety (HTA), whereas those with low test anxiety (LTA) tend to maintain relatively intact corresponding functions. The Control-value theory suggest that value evaluation, as a motivational factor1, is also crucial to anxiety. Although research supports the role of motivational factors, current mainstream interventions for anxiety-related inhibitory control deficits primarily focus on attention-based training, such as cognitive control training. While these trainings can improve inhibitory control capacity, their efficacy in alleviating anxiety symptoms themselves is limited, suggesting limitations in attention-based interventions.

To address the limitations of existing attention-based interventions, this study aims to explore the effectiveness of Value Expectation Training (VET). We attempt to use VET to reshape the association between threatening stimuli and negative value expectation in HTA individuals, establishing instead a link between threat and positive value expectation, thereby ameliorating the inhibitory control deficits in HTA individuals. The study will utilize the probability learning task (focusing on the Feedback-Related Negativity, FRN) and the Flanker task (focusing on N2 and P3) combined with Event-Related Potentials (ERP) technique for verification. We hypothesize that VET will enhance HTA individuals' reward value expectation for threats, resulting in a reduced FRN amplitude; and in the Flanker task, a decreased N2 amplitude and an enhanced P3 amplitude, thus improving inhibitory control.

Methods

Participants and Design

Participants were initially screened using the Test AnxietyScale (TAS) to identify High Test Anxiety (HTA) (> 20) and Low Test Anxiety (LTA) (< 12)individuals. Confounding factors were controlled by excluding individuals with high scores on theBeck Depression Inventory-l (BDI-II < 13) and the Self-rating Anxiety Scale (SAS < 50). Of the 313 initial participants, 97 university students were finally recruited. 65 HTA individualswere randomly assigned to the HTA Experimental Group (n = 33) and the HTA Control Group(n= 32). The remaining LTA individuals (n= 32) were assigned to the LTA Control Group (notraining). The final analysis included 90 students (30 per group) after data exclusions due todropout/artifacts. All participants were right-handed, hadnormal or corrected-to-normal vision, and provided informed consent.

Procedure and Tasks

All participants completed the TAS, Flanker task (measuring inhibitory control), and Probability Learning Task (PLT, measuring value expectation) with simultaneous EEG recording at Pre-test and Post-test (Figure 1). Specifically, for the Flanker task, N2 amplitude was analyzed at electrode Fz in the 230~330 ms window, and P3 amplitude was analyzed at electrode CPz in the 300~600 ms window. Similarly, in the probability learning task, the average amplitude of the FRN was analyzed at electrode Fz in the 250~350 ms window.

Value Expectation Training

The study utilized 32 Chinese words from test anxiety databases (8 test-relevant threat, 8 test-irrelevant threat, 16 neutral), with half assigned to training and half to a probability learning task. Subjective ratings confirmed the expected distinctions: test-relevant threat words were rated as more relevant than both other categories, while both threat word types were rated as more threatening than neutral words. All words were rated similarly in familiarity.

Based on these materials, the experiment assigned participants to three groups. The HTA Experimental Group received 9 sessions of VET over three weeks. In VET, participants selected between a threat or neutral word, with feedback indicating the outcome (e.g., +10 reward or +0). Threat words were associated with a 70-90% reward probability, while neutral words had a consistent 50% probability. The HTA Control Group received a neutral training procedure, where all words had a 50% reward probability. Finally, the LTA Control Group received no training at all.Experimental Tasks

The Flanker Task measures inhibitory control by requiring participants to indicate the direction of a central arrow while ignoring surrounding flanker arrows. It compares performance in congruent (flankers and target match) and incongruent (flankers oppose target) trials. The Flanker effect—slower reaction times and higher errors in incongruent trials—reflects interference from distracting information.

The Probability Learning Task assesses decision-making and learning under uncertainty. Participants are presented with specific word stimuli (e.g., threat or neutral words) and must choose between two possible key presses ("F" or "J"). Each choice leads to immediate, probabilistic feedback in the form of a gain (reward) or a loss (punishment), presented in separate blocks. While participants are instructed to find a pattern, the outcomes are not deterministic but follow a high probability rule (e.g., 80% chance of the correct outcome). This task helps to uncover how individuals learn and adapt their choices based on uncertain reinforcement signals and how sensitive they are to positive versus negative feedback.

Results

TAS

A mixed ANOVA was conducted on the TAS with Time (Pre-test, Post-test) as a within-subjects factor and Group (HTA experimental group, HTA control group, LTA control group) as a between-subjects factor. The main effect of Group was significant, F(2,87)=69.26，p<.001，η2=.579. Further Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons revealed that the TAS score of the LTA control group (M=10.48, SD=0.85)was significantly lower than that of the HTA experimental group (M=22.23, SD=0.86, pBonf<.001, Cohen's d = 2.33) and the HTA control group (M=22.35, SD=0.86, pBonf<.001, Cohen's d = 2.56).

Flanker Task

A mixed ANOVA was conducted on the N2 amplitude with Group (HTA experimental group, HTA control group, LTA control group) × Time (Pre-test, Post-test) × Congruency (Congruent, Incongruent) as factors. The main effect of Time was significant, F(1,87)=15.118, p < 0.001, η2=.148. The N2 amplitude in the pre-test (-3.299 μv) was significantly more negative than in the post-test (-1.599 μv), t(87)=-3.89, p<.001, Cohen's d = -0.33. The main effect of Congruency was significant, F(1,87)=32.095, p < 0.001, η2=.269. The N2 amplitude in the congruent condition (-1.922 μv) was significantly less negative (more positive) than in the incongruent condition (-2.976 μv), t(87)=5.67, p<.001, Cohen's d = 0.21.

A mixed ANOVA was conducted on the P3 amplitude (Figure 2). The Group and Time interaction was significant, F(2,87)=5.55, p=.005, η2=.113. Further simple effects analysis was performed: In the HTA experimental group, the post-test P3 amplitude (8.848 μv) was significantly larger than the pre-test P3 amplitude (7.661 μv), p=.047. In the HTA control group, the post-test P3 amplitude (7.24 μv) was significantly smaller than the pre-test P3 amplitude (4.912 μv), p=.035. In the LTA control group, the post-test P3 amplitude (10.214 μv) was significantly larger than the pre-test P3 amplitude (7.596 μv), p=.017.

Probability Learning Task 

In the reward blocks, a mixed ANOVA was conducted on the FRN amplitude with Group (HTA experimental group, HTA control group, LTA control group) × Time (Pre-test, Post-test) × Word Type (Neutral Word, Test-relevant Threat Word, Test-irrelevant Threat Word) × Feedback (Reward, No Reward) as factors. The main effect of Time was significant, F(1,87)=10.741, p = 0.002, η2=.11. The Word Type and Feedback interaction was significant, F(2,174)=3.521, p = 0.032, η2=.039. Since the Reward condition was significantly greater than the No Reward condition for all word types, a difference wave (reward condition minus no reward condition) was calculated and subjected to a mixed ANOVA with Group × Time × Word Type as factors. The main effect of Word Type on the difference wave was significant, F(2,174)=3.521, p = 0.032, η2=.039. Post-hoc analysis revealed that the difference wave amplitude for the Neutral condition (2.045 μv) was significantly smaller than that for the Test-irrelevant Threat Word (2.762 μv), t(174)=-2.807, pBonf<.018, Cohen's d = -0.193. The Time, Word Type and Group interaction was significant, F(4,174)=2.523，p = 0.043，η2=.055. Simple effects analysis showed that only the post-test of Group 1 (HTA experimental group) had a significant difference across word types, F(2,87)=3.873，p=.027. In Group 1 post-test, the FRN amplitudes for the Test-relevant Threat Word (5.217 μv) and the Test-irrelevant Threat Word (4.645 μv) were significantly larger than the Neutral Word (4.122 μv) amplitude, p = .45, p = .34.

In the punishment blocks, a mixed ANOVA was conducted on the FRN amplitude. The main effect of Word Type was significant, F(2,174)=3.113, p = 0.047, η2=.035. Post-hoc analysis showed that the FRN amplitude for the neutral word (7.002 μv) was significantly larger than that for the test-irrelevant threat word (6.596 μv), t(174)=2.504, pBonf=.042, Cohen's d = 0.063.

Conclusion

The results of this study indicate that short-term VET did not significantly reduce the subjective anxiety levels of individuals with HTA. This finding suggests that the intervention effect of VET is primarily concentrated at the cognitive processing level, rather than the emotional experience level. Test anxiety itself is a multidimensional construct, encompassing both cognitive worry and emotional arousal—two dimensions that are related yet independent3. Although VET optimizes cognitive motivation and resource allocation by enhancing positive value expectations toward threat stimuli, this short-term attention-based training may be insufficient to directly alter individuals' automated emotional response patterns to threat cues or their long-established anxiety experiences. Therefore, improvements in cognitive metrics following training were not accompanied by a corresponding reduction in self-reported anxiety.

In the Flanker task, ERP results revealed the specific regulatory mechanisms of VET on the cognitive functions of individuals with high test anxiety. The HTA experimental group showed a significantly increased P3 amplitude after training, indicating enhanced motivational engagement and goal-oriented processing. The P3 component typically reflects motivation-driven attentional resource allocation and stimulus evaluation processes. This enhancement stood in sharp contrast to the decreased P3 amplitude observed in the HTA control group after training. The reduction in P3 for the control group may stem from diminished motivational investment in tasks with low-value expectations (50% reward probability), as anxious individuals are particularly sensitive to uncertainty. The experimental group's results demonstrate that high reward expectations (80%) effectively motivate HTA individuals, enabling them to mobilize more cognitive resources during conflict tasks, thereby optimizing their reward-driven attentional mechanisms. However, both the experimental and control groups exhibited reduced N2 amplitude, a component associated with early conflict monitoring, which may be attributed to practice effects. This suggests that VET did not specifically enhance conflict detection capabilities. In summary, VET primarily improves inhibitory control performance by enhancing the efficiency of motivational attentional resource allocation, rather than by strengthening early conflict monitoring.

The results from the Probability Learning Task jointly confirmed, at both behavioral and neural levels, that VET effectively reshapes the value evaluation of threat stimuli in HTA individuals. The results from the FRN component provided direct evidence for this. In the reward context, threat words elicited larger FRN amplitudes than neutral words in the post-test for the experimental group. Since the FRN reflects the degree of expectation violation, a larger amplitude indicates a smaller expectation violation—that is, the experimental group had developed higher reward expectations for threat stimuli. This strongly supports the Control-Value Theory, demonstrating that value evaluation, as a core motivational factor in anxiety, is plastic and can be recalibrated through training.
