Introduction

Despite its small size, the Locus Coeruleus (LC) constitutes the main source of norepinephrine (NE) in the brain. Its neurons have extensive monosynaptic ramifications that reach both cortical and subcortical structures, thus explaining its involvement in several brain functions.1 The LC is part of the ascending reticular formation, a set of nuclei that constitutes the sleep-wake circuitry. Therefore, it contributes to the regulation of sleep and wakefulness and of arousal,1 as well as of higher-order cognitive functions, including attentional processes2. Besides, the LC is considered to contribute to the age-related changes in sleep observed in healthy adults, while age-related alterations in the LC-NE system are believed to result in cognitive deterioration, which is exacerbated in neurodegeneration.3,4 However, much of the current understanding of LC function arises from animal research, likely due to methodological limitations in imaging of the LC in humans. As a result, translating these findings to humans is not straightforward.

As a first effort to fill this gap, we investigated whether the age-related change in LC integrity, inferred from high-resolution 7 Tesla Magnetic Resonance Imaging (7T MRI), was related to subjective sleep quality, assessed through the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index - PSQI, and sleep electroencephalography (EEG). In an attempt to better understand the association between LC and cognition, we further explored the link between the integrity of the LC and selective attention. 

Methods

All participants underwent high-resolution structural MRI at 7T (MAGNETOM Terra, Siemens, Erlangen, Germany). A T1 anatomical image was acquired using the Magnetization-Prepared with 2 RApid Gradient Echoes (MP2RAGE; 0.753 mm3 isotropic voxels) sequence, while a Magnetization Transfer – Turbo Flash sequence (MT-TFL; 0.4 × 0.4 × 0.5)mm³ voxels) was applied to image the LC. This sequence is considered to be sensitive to neuromelanin, which is specifically present in the LC, allowing its delineation. After coregistering the MT-TFL to each subject’s upsampled MP2RAGE (0.375 mm³ isotropic), the LC was manually delineated by two independent raters, and a mask was obtained from the intersection. The signal extracted from this mask was normalized with an ROI in the pons to compute the so-called LC contrast, which is considered to reflect the LC neuronal integrity.4 The final sample comprises two subsamples that differed in MRI acquisition protocol: in 54 participants the LC sequence was acquired with single transmit mode coil (sTx; 1Tx32Rx, Nova Medical, USA), while in 18 participants with parallel transmit mode (pTx; 8Tx32Rx, Nova Medical). Sequence (coil type) was therefore added as covariate in all analyses. The analyses followed two lines of investigation shown in Figure 1. 

The intermediate sleep-related analyses presented hereafter are based on 72 healthy participants (45 young, 22.5±3y; 27 late middle-aged, 61±6y; 21 Men). They completed an in-lab night of sleep, from which the sleep-related EEG parameters were extracted. Eleven EEG channels were used (F3, Fz, F4; C3, Cz, C4; P3, Pz, P4; O1, O2), and the recording was automatically staged in 30 s epochs. Based on literature findings on sleep physiology in association with the LC, we extracted the intensity of Rapid Eye Movement (REM) sleep, as reflected by REM theta energy (the cumulated power in the 4-8Hz EEG band during REM) and the average EEG power in the sigma band (12-16Hz) prior to each REM sleep episode. Moreover, we explored the density of sleep spindles, a Non-REM sleep oscillatory pattern belonging to the sigma band, computed as number of spindles per hour. Lastly, we used the PSQI as self-report metric of perceived sleep quality.

As regards cognition, these intermediate analyses are based on a sample of 45 young healthy participants (22.5 ± 3 y; M = 12). We selected the D2 test, a clinically validated paper-and-pencil task that examines selective attention. Participants are required to cross out any letter "d" with two marks either above or below it, in the presence of distractors. In particular, we characterized attentional performance using three D2 metrics, namely processing speed ₋ errors (Gz₋F), computed as total number of processed items ₋ mistakes, concentration performance (KL), computed as correct items ₋ mistakes of omission, and fluctuation of attention (SB), computed as highest number of items processed in every line ₋ lowest number of items processed in every line. 

MRI data preprocessing, LC contrast computation, and sleep metrics extraction were conducted using established as well as custom ANTs and SPM scripts, while the LC delineation was performed using ITK-SNAP. All statistical analyses were performed using R (R Core Team, 2024) and consisted of linear models and Generalized Linear Models (GLMs), selected based on the nature of the dependent variable as well as the distribution of residuals. Associations between sleep parameters and LC contrast were tested including quadratic effects of age, to explore the potential non-linear associations with LC contrast reported in previous work, as well as dichotomized age groups, each with and without interactions with LC contrast. Depending on the model specification, additional predictors were considered, namely BMI, gender, and total intracranial volume (TIV), as well as total sleep time (TST). In addition, associations between attention-related metrics and LC contrast were tested including age and gender.

Results

As regards sleep physiology (N=72), sigma power prior to REM was positively associated with LC contrast (β = –0.19, SE = 0.092, p = 0.039, Figure 2A). Gender and Sequence were also significant predictors, with females and participants in Sequence 2 (pTx acquisition) showing higher sigma activity (β = –0.025, SE = 0.0106, p = 0.023 and β = –0.026, SE = 0.00873, p = 0.0037, respectively). Besides, spindle density was also positively associated with LC contrast (β = 11637.05, SE = 5088.22, p = 0.026), with significant interactions between LC contrast and Age (β = -671.50, SE = 309.59, p = 0.034, Figure 2B), as well as between LC contrast and Age2 (β = 7.69, SE =3.65, p = 0.039). This indicates that the effect of LC contrast on spindle density varies non-linearly with age. In contrast, no significant associations were observed between LC contrast and REM theta activity (all p-values > 0.47) or subjective sleep quality (all p-values > 0.19). 

As regards cognition (N=45), Gz–F scores showed a nominally significant positive association with LC contrast (β = 5055.49, SE = 2509.23, p = 0.051) as well as a nominally significant negative interaction between LC contrast and Age (β = –238.99, SE = 117.61, p = 0.049; Figure 2C). Age and Gender were also significantly and positively associated with higher D2 scores (all p-values < 0.05). In addition, KL scores were significantly and positively associated with LC contrast (β = 3198.63, SE = 1236.09, p = 0.013) and with Age (β = 34.49, SE = 12.91, p = 0.011), with a significant LC × Age interaction (β = –148.97, SE = 57.94, p = 0.014, Figure 2D), indicating that the effect of LC contrast on KL decreases with age. No significant associations were observed between LC contrast and SB (all p-values > 0.27).

Lastly, we note that after controlling for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR) on the p-values associated to LC contrast, none of the associations between LC contrast and either sleep- or attention-related metrics remained significant (all adjusted p-values > 0.27 and > 0.08, respectively).

Conclusion

These preliminary results suggest that the integrity of the LC is related to both sleep physiology and selective attention. Regarding sleep, LC integrity was associated with two Non-REM oscillatory patterns causally related to LC function in animal models. Specifically, in animals, sleep spindles occur periodically with the periodic decrease in NE-LC tone, while sigma power increases prior to a REM episode when NE-LC tone progressively ceases5. Taken together, these findings suggest that the integrity of the NE-LC system is also involved in these oscillatory patterns in humans. Regarding cognition, we report that the integrity of the LC may be related to a better concentration performance in the attentional task, and that this effect may be varying with age. Specifically, it may decrease progressively from 18 to 30 years. Given the age-range, we surmise that the age-related association may reflect changes in brain maturation. Overall, our results suggest that LC integrity is associated with sleep and cognition through different mechanisms. To corroborate these findings, we plan to enlarge the sample size to 100 individuals and to extend the cognitive analysis to older individuals. We further plan to assess potential triadic relationships between LC, sleep, and cognition.
