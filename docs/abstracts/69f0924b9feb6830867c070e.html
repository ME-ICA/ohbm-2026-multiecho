Introduction

Parts of speech (PoS) are central to linguistic theory, yet languages differ in whether they define categories based on functional, semantic, morphological, or distributional criteria.^1 Lexical frequency can further blur PoS boundaries when particular wordforms are strongly biased toward one PoS category. These factors raise the question of whether PoS is an intrinsic lexical property or a contextual one emerging from sentence structure. Recent linguistic and cognitive theories argue that PoS are not strictly categorical but graded, allowing flexible boundaries and within-class variability. Neuropsychological studies often show noun–verb dissociations, whereas neuroimaging results vary across tasks, modalities, and languages, suggesting that PoS may not map onto sharply segregated neural circuits. Instead, they may rely on distributed networks sensitive to semantic and contextual factors. To test whether neural activity is modulated by major PoS categories (nouns, verbs, and adjectives), we examined source-localized MEG responses during naturalistic word-by-word reading and listening in a large Dutch dataset (MOUS).^2 The availability of intact and scrambled sentences allowed us to dissociate intrinsic lexical effects from contextual effects. Using encoding models, we tested whether PoS predicts unique variance in neural activity beyond lexical frequency, predictability, entropy, ordinal position, word length, and the presence or absence of sentence-level structure. Our goal was to determine whether PoS effects arise from intrinsic lexical properties, depend on syntactic and semantic context, or persist after accounting for sentence-level structure.

Methods

We analyzed MEG data from 200 native Dutch speakers (100 per modality) from the MOUS dataset. Participants were right-handed, had normal/corrected vision, no neurological or language disorders, and provided informed consent. They read or listened to 120 intact and 120 scrambled Dutch sentences presented in blocks of five, with 20% percent of trials followed by a yes/no comprehension question. MEG was recorded using a 275-channel CTF system at 1200 Hz. T1-weighted MRIs (1 mm isotropic) were acquired for anatomical coregistration. Data were filtered (0.5–20 Hz), epoched to sentence onset, artifacts were replaced with NaN. The data were downsampled to 120 Hz. Auditory data were temporally aligned to visual word-onset timing.^3. Forward models were built from individual MRIs; source activity was estimated with LCMV beamforming (8,196 dipoles) and parcellated into 382 anatomical regions (atlas conte69). PCA was applied within parcels, retaining the first five components.

Multiway canonical correlation analysis (MCCA) enhanced stimulus-locked signals and reduced interparticipant variability, with five-fold cross-validation yielding participant-specific canonical components. Encoding models were fitted to canonical components time-locked to word onsets (nouns, verbs, adjectives). For each participant, parcel, and timepoint, we estimated ridge regression models with five-fold cross-validation. The Full model included word length, lexical frequency, surprisal, entropy, index, and two dummy-coded PoS variables (Verb, Adjective). Reduced models were constructed by removing one predictor at a time. Continuous predictors were z-scored. The optimal regularization parameter λ was selected using nested cross-validation over a log-spaced grid. Predictor-specific variance explained was quantified as the coefficient of determination (COD) derived from model comparison.^4 For comparison of the results with the distribution under the null hypothesis, we permuted the design matrix 50 times.

To assess whether PoS effects persist beyond sentence-level structure, we built additional encoding models on the combined dataset (intact and scrambled items). In these combined-conditions models, the Full model included all predictors described above with an additional variable ”Context” (intact vs. scrambled). The corresponding Reduced model excluded the PoS predictors. This model comparison tested whether PoS explained unique variance in neural activity after accounting for the presence or absence of sentence context, thereby assessing whether PoS contributes to neural responses independently of contextual structure.

Permutation testing (dependent-samples t-tests) was employed to evaluate significance of effects. Timepoint-wise significance was assessed using 5,000-permutation max-statistic correction across timepoints, followed by FDR correction for the number of parcels tested. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were executed to determine which contrasts explained the main effects: Verb vs. Noun, Adjective vs. Noun, Verb vs. Adjective. We applied max-statistic timepoint correction and Bonferroni adjustment, followed by FDR across parcels.

Results

Reading

In the combined-conditions model, PoS effects emerged in a left-lateralized cortical network (53 parcels) around 250 ms in left postcentral and adjacent parietal areas involved in sensorimotor integration. By 300 ms, effects extended to ventral temporal regions implicated in visual word recognition and lexical-semantic processing. Peak sensitivity between 320–420 ms involved a broad left-lateralized network spanning left temporal, frontal, parietal, and medial regions linked to semantic, syntactic, and attentional processes (Figure 1a).^5 In intact sentences, 20 parcels showed PoS-related sensitivity between 220–480 ms, beginning in left superior temporal and ventral temporal regions, associated with phonological and semantic processing (Figure 1b), and extending to left postcentral and premotor parcels. Left associative and secondary visual regions contributed to variance explained between ~242–458 ms, alongside perirhinal parcels implicated in memory and object recognition. In scrambled sentences, 17 parcels were sensitive to PoS, with earliest effects (~160 ms) in left occipital and parietal areas, extending to left postcentral areas and angular gyrus associated with multimodal integration. A peak around 350 ms involved left-lateralized temporal, auditory, and inferior parietal areas. Post-hoc contrasts revealed one significant difference: adjectives explained more variance than verbs in left inferior temporal cortex at 750–800 ms.

Listening

In the combined-conditions model, PoS effects reached significance in 53 parcels, emerging at word onset (0 ms) in bilateral inferior frontal cortices, and extending to right temporal and parietal regions. Peak effects between 100–300 ms involved left perisylvian, orbitofrontal, and ventromedial prefrontal areas implicated in semantic and contextual integration (Figure 1d).^5 Several regions remained PoS-sensitive throughout the epoch. In intact sentences, PoS effects were more widespread (76 parcels) and sustained, peaking between 100–300 ms in left perisylvian, frontal, and temporal areas involved in lexical, syntactic, and semantic processing (Figure 1e). No PoS effects emerged for scrambled sentences. Post-hoc contrasts in intact sentences identified 23 parcels with significant differences: five showed Verb-dominant effects, two showed Noun-dominant effects, and one showed Adjective-dominant effects (Figure 1f).

Modality-independent effects

Across modalities, 16 parcels showed consistent PoS sensitivity in the combined-conditions model, including left postcentral, supramarginal, and primary auditory cortices (Figure 1g). Multiple left temporal structures, fusiform, and perirhinal regions showed sensitivity to PoS both in the combined conditions model and in intact sentences (Figure 1h), demonstrating a shared amodal network responsive to PoS information. Finally, a subset of parcels was PoS-sensitive only in intact sentences, including precentral, postcentral, and inferior temporal regions, highlighting regions specifically engaged when sentence structure is preserved.

Conclusion

Our findings provide converging evidence that neural activity reflects graded, context-dependent distinctions between major PoS categories (noun, verb, adjective) during naturalistic language comprehension. Across modalities, PoS effects emerged rapidly after word onset and engaged a distributed left-hemispheric network, including core temporal, frontal, and parietal language regions.^5 The availability of syntactic and semantic context modulated both the extent and strength of these effects: intact sentences elicited earlier onsets, broader spatial involvement, and more sustained sensitivity compared to scrambled input. Post-hoc comparisons of model coefficients further showed that PoS categories contribute unequally to these effects, with distinct parcels showing preferential sensitivity to specific PoS contrasts. Together, these results indicate that PoS is not a unitary feature but reflects a set of distributed lexical and combinatorial computations. Overall, the study demonstrates that PoS information is a robust and interpretable dimension of lexical processing in the brain, dynamically shaping neural activity during both reading and listening. The results highlight the interplay between intrinsic lexical properties, linguistic context, and modality, providing a detailed spatiotemporal account of how PoS information contributes to real-time language processing.
