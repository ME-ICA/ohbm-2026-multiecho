Introduction

Many of us feel, to varying degrees, moments of deep personal connection during narrative experiences (e.g., listening to a story, watching a film, hearing a piece of music). What makes these experiences personally meaningful is precisely what makes them challenging to study; unlike sensory experiences which can be more readily linked to external stimuli, emotional and personally relevant experiences are highly subjective and cannot be locked to specific elements in the external world. Emotional responses integrate both exogenous information from the environment and endogenous representations shaped by individual differences in internal homeostatic states, past experiences, and future goals [Ashar et al., 2017]. This integration results in highly idiosyncratic interpretations across individuals [Chang et al., 2021]. 

In this project, we tackle a fundamental methodological challenge: asking participants to report on their internal states disrupts the ongoing experience of their internal states, yet such probing remains our only window into understanding them. How do we access these private, personally meaningful experiences from a neural perspective? As a potential solution, we propose a novel computational framework that captures’ individuals’ idiosyncratic personal and affective interpretations during naturalistic movie viewing by combining collaborative filtering with advances in natural language processing.

Methods

Participants (N=118) watched 8 emotionally engaging autobiographical narratives from the Moth Radio Hour (audio + audiovisual) during fMRI. After their scan, participants reviewed transcripts of each story and indicated moments that affected them most, providing written descriptions of their internal states and thoughts during these events.

We transformed these free-response annotations into a continuous representation of each participant's evolving affective experience. First, we embedded all responses into a 384-dimensional semantic space using a BERT Sentence Transformer [Reimers & Gurevych, 2019]. Because participants annotated different moments in each story, we used collaborative filtering with a 20-second boxcar kernel to temporally smooth and impute missing timepoints [Jolly et al., 2022], creating a dense participant-by-time-by-embedding tensor that estimates each person's subjective experience throughout the entire narrative. 

For each participant, we then computed distance correlations [Székely et al., 2007] between neural and semantic representational dissimilarity matrices (RDMs) across timepoints. Specifically, we compared time-by-time RDMs derived from semantic embeddings to those derived from brain activity in each region of interest (Schaefer cerebral cortex parcellation [Schaefer et al., 2018]; Melbourne Subcortex Atlas [Tian et al., 2020]). This quantifies how well regional neural dynamics track the evolving structure of participants' idiosyncratic affective interpretations, without making specific assumptions about the embedding space itself. We assessed statistical significance using multi-level circle-shift permutation tests across stories and participants (FDR-corrected, q < 0.001). 

Results

Across our stories, we find strong evidence for associations between participants’ idiosyncratic interpretations to the narratives and dynamic fluctuations in several brain regions, including core default mode network hubs (bilateral posterior cingulate/precuneus, dorsomedial prefrontal cortex), salience network regions (bilateral anterior insula), frontoparietal areas (bilateral cingulate cortex and lateral prefrontal cortex), as well as extrastriate visual cortex and temporal pole. These effects were broadly bilateral, though with more extensive representation in the right hemisphere association cortex. 

Conclusion

This approach provides a proof of concept of how sparsely sampled subjective interpretations can be used to reconstruct a more complete picture of dynamic emotional experiences while minimizing disruption to the ongoing experience. While additional validation is needed, these findings have potential implications for understanding the neural basis of idiosyncratic emotional processing and the links between participants’ interpretations and the semantic content of the narratives themselves.
