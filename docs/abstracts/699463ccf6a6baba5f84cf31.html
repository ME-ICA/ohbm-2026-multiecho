Introduction

Semantic knowledge—our understanding of concepts and their relationships—is central to human cognition.1 Concepts can have two distinct types of relations: Taxonomic relations link concepts belonging to the same semantic category (e.g. dog and duck), while thematic relations connect concepts co-occurring in the same events (e.g. dog and leash).2 The dual-hub theory proposes that taxonomic relationships rely on the anterior temporal lobe (ATL), whereas thematic relationships rely on the temporo-parietal cortex (TPC).3 However, the dual-hub theory remains controversial. In particular, it is unclear whether taxonomic and thematic representations depend on the concurrent task, and how they change with aging. The present fMRI study addressed these gaps by jointly investigating the effects of relation type, task, and age on semantic processing.

Methods

We measured the brain activity of 41 young adults and 34 older adults using fMRI (Figure 1A-B), while they judged whether pairs of object drawings were taxonomically related (taxonomic judgments), or thematically related (thematic judgments). In both tasks, the drawings could be taxonomically related (e.g., monkey and moose), thematically related (e.g., monkey and banana), or unrelated (e.g., monkey and telephone). A visual task on scrambled images served as non-semantic control. 

       We first identified commonalities and differences in general semantic processing (semantic > non-semantic tasks) between young and older adults via whole-brain functional activation analyses based on the general linear model (thresholded at p < 0.05 FWE-corrected using threshold-free cluster enhancement). Using subject-specific functional region-of-interest (fROI) analyses4, we then investigated age and task effects on the response profiles of key semantic regions—the ATL and TPC, as well as semantic control areas—for taxonomic and thematic relations. Finally, we tested brain–behavior correlations between percent signal change in each fROI and response times or accuracies for taxonomic vs. thematic tasks within each age group (FDR-corrected).

Results

In both young and older adults (Figure 1C-E), general semantic processing (semantic > non-semantic tasks) engaged the bilateral TPC and ATL, as well as semantic control regions in left inferior and middle frontal gyri (IFG/MFG), posterior middle and inferior temporal gyri (pMTG/ITG) and pre-supplementary motor area (pre-SMA). Young adults showed stronger semantic activity than older adults mainly in semantic control areas, including left IFG/MFG, pMTG/ITG and pre-SMA (Figure 1F). No brain region was more strongly engaged in older than young adults.

Subject-specific fROI analyses revealed that the bilateral TPC was preferentially engaged for thematic (as compared to taxonomic) relationships (Figure 2A-B). However, bilateral TPC was also sensitive to taxonomic relationships, as compared to unrelated pairs. Moreover, left and right TPC showed an age-related decrease in taxonomic processing and/or an age-related increase in thematic processing. Left ATL also exhibited a task-dependent preference for thematic (as compared to taxonomic) relationships, which was stronger in older adults (Figure 2C). Right ATL was not engaged in taxonomic or thematic processing in young adults; in older adults, right ATL showed a task-dependent response to both taxonomic and thematic relations (Figure 2D).

Beyond the TPC and ATL, several semantic control regions showed task- and age-dependent responses to taxonomic and thematic relationships. In particular, pars orbitalis of the left IFG (IFGorb) and the bilateral medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) exhibited a flexible, task-dependent response to task-relevant semantic relationships (taxonomic during taxonomic judgments, thematic during thematic judgments). Semantic areas in left IFG/MFG, IPS, and PreCun showed an age-related decrease in taxonomic processing and/or an age-related increase in thematic processing. 

Brain–behavior relationships exclusively emerged in older adults (Figure 2E): Stronger activity in bilateral PreCun and left IPS was associated with higher accuracy in the taxonomic than thematic task. In contrast, stronger activity in left ATL, IFG/MFG, IFGorb, pMTG/ITG and PreCS correlated with slower response times in older adults.

Conclusion

Our results do not support the dual-hub theory: While the bilateral TPC indeed prefers thematic over taxonomic relationships, so does the left ATL. Right ATL is not engaged in young adults; in older adults, right ATL shows a task-dependent response to both taxonomic and thematic relations. Crucially, all semantic regions are also sensitive to taxonomic relationships, when compared to unrelated pairs. Therefore, the preference for thematic or taxonomic relationships is gradual, not binary. 

         Moreover, the response of several semantic areas for taxonomic and thematic relationships is task-dependent. Left TPC and ATL show an enhanced response to task-relevant thematic relationships. Semantic control regions, particularly left IFGorb and mPFC, are flexibly engaged for task-relevant semantic relations (taxonomic during taxonomic judgments, thematic during thematic judgments).

          Regarding age effects, bilateral TPC and several semantic control areas exhibit an age-related decrease in taxonomic processing and/or an age-related increase in thematic processing. In older adults, increased task-related activity for thematic vs. taxonomic processing was associated with higher accuracy but slower responses. This suggests that older adults require increased cognitive resources to maintain accuracy at a high level, but this may come at the cost of efficiency.

          Overall, our findings indicate that taxonomic and thematic representations in semantic representation hubs within TPC and ATL, as well as semantic control regions, are strongly modulated by task and age.
