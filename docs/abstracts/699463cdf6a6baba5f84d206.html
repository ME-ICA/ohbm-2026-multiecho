Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) research has traditionally assumed a single, stereotypical trajectory of cortical amyloid beta (Aβ) accumulation across individuals. This assumption underlies the use of global Aβ burden measures, such as Centiloid (a standardized scale for quantifying amyloid load), to stage disease progression and select trial participants. However, recent longitudinal Aβ positron emission tomography (PET) studies have revealed substantial spatial heterogeneity, with different individuals showing distinct regional patterns of Aβ deposition and spread, some beginning in the medial frontal cortex, others in the precuneus or occipital regions.1 This heterogeneity may reflect underlying biological differences in disease etiology, genetic risk factors, or vascular co-pathology, yet current analytic approaches reduce this complexity to a single summary statistic.

Understanding how demographic, genetic, and pathological risk factors relate to spatial patterns of Aβ deposition could improve disease modeling, enable more precise participant stratification in clinical trials, and potentially reveal distinct AD subtypes. We therefore applied spatial independent component analysis (ICA) to Aβ-PET data from two large cohorts to decompose global amyloid burden into distinct, reproducible spatial components and characterize their biological correlates.

Methods

Participants, and Aβ-PET and MRI protocols

Cross-sectional data included subjects who had  3D T1-weighted MRI, Aβ-PET, APOE-ε4 genotyping, and a global CDR rating under 1, drawn from the AMYloid imaging to Prevent Alzheimer’s Disease (AMYPAD) Prognostic & Natural History Study (PNHS) (N=1185) as well as from the A4 & LEARN studies (N=1717).2,3 Additionally, T2* and/or susceptibility weighted imaging was available for 573 participants in the AMYPAD-PNHS and 1711 participants in A4. AMYPAD-PNHS used [18F]Flutemetamol (FMM; n=649) and [18F]Florbetaben (FBB; n=536), whereas [18F]Florbetapir (FBP; n=1717) was used in A4 & LEARN. PET frames were co-registered, averaged, and aligned to the corresponding 3D T1-weighted MRI scan. SUVR images were obtained using the whole cerebellum as a reference region. Global cortical amyloid burden was evaluated using centiloid, obtained from averaged SUVR values inside the standard Global Alzheimer’s Association Interactive Network (GAAIN) target region. Cortical reconstructions were computed from the T1-weighted MRI scans using FreeSurfer v7.1.1 and visually quality controlled.

Estimation, validation and stability of independent Aβ-PET components

For FMM and FBB Aβ-PET, we performed tracer-wise probabilistic ICA (pICA) with a fixed number of n-dimensional subspaces incrementing from n=1 to n=15. For each pICA iteration, we correlated all n components of the FMM-pICA with the n components of the FBB-pICA using fslcc, and opted for the dimensionality n=9 with the largest number of largely spatially overlapping (R>0.8) components between the two pICAs. Probability maps between spatially overlapping components were then averaged and thresholded to retain voxels with an 80% likelihood of contributing to a component, yielding 3 major cortical components (Figure 1A) and non-specific binding components (Figure 1B). Subject-level component loading was estimated in all participants using spatial regression of the identified averaged thresholded components on subject-level Aβ-PET SUVR maps, yielding a subject-level loading for each identified component. Resulting component loadings were consistently significantly related to Centiloid across studies (Figure 1C), but exhibited slightly different slopes across tracers (Figure 1D), warranting tracer-wise harmonization. To harmonize these subject-level component loadings across tracers, they were z-scaled using the mean and standard deviation of the first distribution, i.e., a tracer-specific healthy reference population, of a 2-kernel Gaussian mixture model. Using available follow-up data from the AMYPAD-PNHS, stability of the identified components was quantified using a two-way random single score ICC (Figure 1E).

Biological characterization of Aβ-PET components and their pathological correlates

Only participants with a reliably detectable amount of amyloid burden were considered for further analyses; hence only participants with Centiloid > 10 were considered.4 To test the impact of participant demographics and biology on spatial heterogeneity, we calculated subject-level component dispersion as the (scaled) component loading relative to total loading in all components. Therefore, higher component dispersion indicates greater component loading relative to other components. We tested the impact of age, sex and APOE-ε4 carriership on component dispersion as prominent demographic and genetic risk factors, while (co-)pathology was assessed with microbleed count as estimated by visual reads, categorized as none, one, or more than one microbleed. For all tests, an alpha criterion of 0.05, adjusted for false discovery rate, was used.

Results

Participant Characteristics

Participants were 70.1 ± 7.1 years old, 1711 (59%) were women and 1267 (44%) were APOE-ε4 carriers. 1842 (63.5%) participants were considered Aβ-positive on the basis of a Centiloid score of at least 10; there were no significant differences in sex between Aβ-negative and Aβ-positive participants, but Aβ-positive participants were on average 3.8 years older and had a higher frequency of APOE-ε4 with 55% (n=993) of Aβ-positive compared to 26% (n=274) of Aβ-negative participants. One microbleed was present for 255 (11%) participants, while 136 (6%) participants had more than one microbleed. Microbleed frequency was also increased for Aβ-positive participants, with 13% (n=199) of Aβ-positive participants having one microbleed and 7% (n=105) having two or more microbleeds, compared to 8% (n=56) and 5% (n=31) for Aβ-negative participants, respectively.

Biological and Pathological Characterization

Results of the dispersion analyses are displayed in Figure 2. Frontal and occipital dispersion significantly increased with age, whereas parietal and projection dispersion decreased, indicating an aging-related shift towards fronto-occipito-temporal amyloid aggregation relative to parietal burden and white matter uptake. Sex had a limited impact on component dispersion, with only mildly increased frontal and projection dispersion in men compared with women. The impact of APOE-ε4 carriership was much more pronounced, with particularly decreased dispersion of projection tracts in APOE-ε4 carriers compared with non-carriers. In contrast, frontal and occipital dispersion was also strongly increased in APOE-ε4 carriers compared with non-carriers. For participants with one compared to no microbleeds, dispersion in the projection component was minimally increased, whereas for participants with two or more microbleeds compared with none, occipital dispersion was notably increased.

Conclusion

Using a spatial ICA of Aβ-PET, we identify three reproducible cortical components that capture distinct spatial patterns of amyloid deposition and relate differentially to age, APOE-ε4 carriership, and vascular co-pathology. 

Our three-component solution shows substantial overlap with previously identified patterns of Aβ heterogeneity, resembling the earliest stages of three subtypes identified by Collij et al.1 as well as frontal-parietal, inferior occipital, anterior cingulate and posterior parietal components identified previously by Pereira et al.5

By moving beyond a single global measure of amyloid burden, this approach provides a compact yet biologically informative representation of spatial heterogeneity in Aβ aggregation. Component-level measures summarize distributed pathology into continuous, low-dimensional markers that may increase sensitivity to early disease-related changes, improve effect sizes, and enable more efficient stratification of participants in observational studies and clinical trials. In particular, differential component expression may help identify subgroups with distinct risk profiles or co-pathological signatures, supporting more targeted enrolment and analysis strategies in disease-modifying trials.
