Introduction

Most people can effortlessly visualize objects or scenes in their mind, but individuals with congenital aphantasia report no voluntary mental imagery. Surprisingly, however, aphantasic individuals can accurately answer questions about the visual appearance of objects from memory, a task for which most individuals report using visual mental imagery. In a recent fMRI study [1] we showed that, during imagery attempts, aphantasics have typical activity in their high-level visual cortex, but reduced functional connectivity with the left prefrontal cortex. This pattern suggests that aphantasia may result from functional disconnection, accompanied by atypical top-down modulation via attention networks [2]. Does such functional disconnection reflect underlying anatomical differences in long-range white matter connections?

Methods

We scanned 18 individuals with congenital aphantasia and 18 age- and education-matched visualizers on a Siemens 3T Prisma, acquiring diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI; in total 20 min, 394 diffusion directions) and T1-weighted data. Data was preprocessed with QSIPrep, and fibers were reconstructed with QSIRecon. Major white-matter tracts were extracted with AFQ, and each tract was segmented into 100 equidistant nodes. Fractional anisotropy (FA) was computed at each node. Group differences were assessed in a node-wise manner, with threshold free cluster enhancement (TFCE) correction for cluster-level multiple comparisons (p < 0.05). Finally, hemispheric asymmetry was quantified via a cluster-wise laterality index, defined as (right − left) / (right + left) of FA values.

Results

Three patterns of group differences emerged: first, aphantasics showed reduced anisotropy in the uncinate fasciculus at nodes around the anterior temporal lobe (right, p = 0.039; left, marginal p = 0.097); second, reduced anisotropy in the second branch of the right superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF-II; p = 0.035) around the temporoparietal junction. Notably, the hemispheric asymmetry of FA differed between groups in these posterior SLF-II nodes (p = 0.007): FA was higher in the controls’ right hemisphere (p=0.022), but higher in aphantasics’ left hemisphere (p=0.038). Third, aphantasics had increased anisotropy in the cingulum near the anterior–posterior cingulum junction (left, p = 0.009; right, p = 0.045). No differences were observed in the inferior longitudinal fasciculus and the inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus.

Conclusion

These findings provide the first anatomical evidence of altered white-matter microstructure in aphantasia, supporting the hypothesis that it reflects atypical development of neural connections [2]. The affected tracts, the uncinate fasciculus, cingulum-cingulate bundle, and right SLF-II, mediate long-range communication among brain regions essential for attention, memory, executive control, and emotional processing. Disruptions in their microstructure and hemispheric laterality suggest an altered coordination within large-scale brain networks underlying internally generated conscious experience. This supports the view that aphantasia arises from altered large-scale connectivity and top-down modulation rather than from primary sensory cortex impairments [3].
