Introduction

Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) is one of the leading causes of disability in the world, and its incidence continues to increase. However, the anatomical underpinnings of depression remain unclear. MDD is characterized by debilitating deficits in reward processing and motivation, which have been linked with dysfunction in dopaminergic networks involving midbrain, frontal, and striatal regions. Despite this, the striatum has been under-studied in studies of depression, especially the dorsal striatum, and it is unclear if these regions play a role in depression pathophysiology. 

Synaptic plasticity mechanisms are hypothesized to underlie depression pathology, but information at the synaptic level is difficult to obtain in living patients. Existing structural neuroimaging studies often rely on macrostructural methods, are limited by lower resolution, and show conflicting results. One potential tool to address these issues is Neurite Orientation Dispersion and Density Imaging (NODDI).1 NODDI is an advanced mathematical model applied to multi-shell diffusion weighted imaging (DWI), which estimates microstructural measures such as neurite density index (NDI), i.e. the density of axons and dendrites, and orientation dispersion index (ODI), i.e. the angular complexity of dendritic structures and axonal tracts. NODDI can provide insight into plasticity-induced changes in microstructure. Although it has recently been used in some studies in MDD, it has only been used at 3T MRI. Additionally, most studies focus on white matter or cortical gray matter, so the role of crucial subcortical gray matter regions such as the striatum has not been studied. 

Here, we use ultra-high resolution brain imaging (i.e. 7T MRI), multi-shell diffusion weighted imaging, and NODDI modeling to investigate the microstructural anatomy underlying MDD, with a focus on striatal regions implicated in reward and motivation deficits of depression. 

Methods

Participants: 29 healthy controls (mean age = 30.7; 15 females) and 25 participants with Major Depressive Disorder (mean age = 28.2; 13 females) were included in this study. Eligibility criteria were as follows: aged 18 to 55, no history or current evidence of neurocognitive disorder or substance use disorder, and no active general medical problems. Patients with MDD were identified using a Structured Clinical Interview for DSM Disorders, and were required to meet the DSM-5 criteria for MDD, in a current major depressive episode. Healthy controls (HCs) were assessed with a SCID as well and could not have any lifetime history of psychiatric or neurological disorders. All participants completed a scanning session with a 7T MRI scanner, where they underwent structural T1w imaging and diffusion-weighted imaging. Participants also completed the Montgomery-Asburg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS), a clinician-rated scale assessing severity of depression symptoms. Higher MADRS scores indicate more severe symptoms. 

T1w structural MRI: Anatomical images were acquired using T1w dual-inversion magnetization prepared gradient echo (MP2RAGE, repetition time (TR)=4500ms, TE=3.37 ms, TI1=1000s and 3200ms, flip angle (FA)=4 and 5°, iPAT acceleration factor=3, bandwidth (BW)=130 Hz/pixel, 0.7mm isotropic resolution whole brain coverage). T1w images were processed and segmented using Freesurfer v6.0. 

Multi-shell diffusion weighted imaging: Multi-shell dMRI data (b=0, b=1000, b=2000) were acquired with a high-angular-resolved single shot spin echo EPI sequence with monopolar diffusion encoding (1.05mm isotropic resolution, 64 directions, TR/TE= 4000/62 ms, whole brain coverage, MB factor=2, in-plane GRAPPA acceleration R=3). Paired acquisitions with reversed phase encoding in the anterior-posterior/posterior-anterior directions were acquired. DWI preprocessing was performed in FSL to correct for susceptibility-induced distortions (topup) and eddy currents (eddy). Gradient non-linearities were corrected using an HCP routine. 

Neurite Orientation and Dispersion Imaging (NODDI): NODDI models were estimated using the AMICO toolbox. B-values were cleaned to ensure the model did not count small variations in b-values as separate shells (i.e. b=1950 was not a separate shell, but was part of the b=2000 shell). The model used the intrinsic diffusivity parameter optimized for gray matter.2 See Figure 1A for example outcome maps. Using AFNI’s 3dAllineate, each subject’s T1w image was registered to their b0 map. Transformations were applied to the freesurfer aparc.aseg segmentation file to obtain native-space ROIs for each participant: caudate, putamen, nucleus accumbens, separated by hemisphere. Using native-space ROIs, mean NODDI values were extracted: neurite density index (NDI) and orientation dispersion index (ODI). 

General linear models: In R, general linear models were run for mean NDI or ODI in each region. In the full sample (n=54), mean NDI or ODI was modeled as an effect of group, age, sex, and total intracranial volume. In health controls only, mean NDI or ODI was modeled as an effect of age, sex, and total intracranial volume. In patients with MDD, mean NDI or ODI was modeled as an effect of depression severity (MADRS score), age, sex, and total intracranial volume. FDR correction was performed to correct for multiple comparisons across regions.  

Results

Group differences: After controlling for age, sex, and intracranial volume, there are no group differences in NDI or ODI that survive FDR-correction. In the right caudate, MDD participants have lower mean orientation dispersion than healthy controls (p<0.05, uncorrected), but this does not survive correction. 

Associations with MDD symptoms: In the MDD-only sample, mean NDI in caudate and nucleus accumbens (both hemispheres) show negative associations with MADRS scores (p<0.05, FDR-corrected). Figure 1B shows Spearman correlations between NDI and MADRS for visualization purposes. ODI showed no associations with depression severity.

Age and Sex Effects: In healthy subjects, neurite density in left and right caudate and putamen was positively associated with age (p<0.05, FDR-corrected) and associated with sex (p<0.05, FDR-corrected; females had higher NDI). In left and right nucleus accumbens, orientation dispersion was positively associated with age (p<0.05, FDR-corrected). In left and right caudate and left nucleus accumbens, orientation dispersion was associated with sex (p<0.05, FDR-corrected), with females having higher ODI. In participants with Major Depressive Disorder, neither neurite density nor orientation dispersion was associated with age or sex. 

Conclusion

In this study we used ultra-high resolution MRI, multi-shell diffusion weighted imaging, and neurite orientation dispersion and density imaging to characterize the microstructure of striatal regions in depression. While we didn’t find group differences between people with depression and healthy controls, we found robust negative associations between the severity of depression symptoms and neurite density in caudate and nucleus accumbens. Lower striatal neurite density is associated with more severe depression symptoms, indicating that the microstructure of the striatum may play a role in depression pathophysiology. Interestingly, this could reflect a disruption of normal aging processes in depression. 

In healthy controls, older age was associated with higher neurite density and orientation dispersion in striatal regions (caudate, putamen, and nucleus accumbens). It is unclear what is driving this association. As this sample does not include participants older than 55, the association with age is unlikely to involve aging-related neurodegeneration. Higher neurite density could be a result of various anatomical changes, such as increased myelination, cell swelling, loss of glial cells, new neurite projections, or a loss of dendritic complexity allowing for more dense neurite packing. Higher orientation dispersion could indicate higher dendritic complexity or a loss of axonal organization. It is therefore challenging to interpret the biological mechanisms underlying these results. However, previous studies have also reported higher NDI and ODI in mid-life adults. One study of the UK BioBank reported a peak NDI at around 50 years of age, followed by a decline later in life.3 This might indicate that higher NDI reflects plasticity mechanisms associated with experience and maturity, rather than neurodegeneration. 

The association between age and NODDI measures is not present in participants with Major Depressive Disorder, which might indicate that the process of normal again is disrupted in MDD. Since lower NDI is associated with more severe depression symptoms, this would be consistent with the hypothesis that increases in NDI and ODI are protective, and MDD involves a disruption of these processes. Another possibility is that increases in NDI and ODI are in fact markers of neuronal decline, such as increased disorganization of synaptic connections or cell swelling. Rather than being protective, higher NDI and ODI could predispose individuals to experiencing depression, and worsening depression could induce compensatory decreases in NDI. This could explain why female participants show higher NDI and ODI, since MDD is known to disproportionately affect women.

This is the first study to use ultra-high resolution MRI in combination with NODDI modeling to investigate the microstructural underpinnings of Major Depressive Disorder. These data indicate that MDD is associated with a disruption of normal age-related increases in neurite density and orientation dispersion in striatal regions, and lower neurite density is associated with more severe symptoms. This could reflect one of two possibilities. The first is that higher neurite density and orientation dispersion in striatal regions are protective factors associated with experience and maturity, and decreases in striatal neurite density cause depression symptoms. The second possibility is that higher neurite density and orientation dispersion are markers of age-related decline and predispose individuals to depression, and severe depression induces compensatory decreases in neurite density. Additional studies are needed, with larger sample sizes, longitudinal data collection, and investigation of treatment effects, before definitive conclusions can be drawn, but these data implicate striatal neurite density as a potential biomarker for depression and its treatment.
