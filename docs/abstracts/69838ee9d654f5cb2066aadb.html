Introduction

Children with pharmaco-resistant epilepsy continue to experience seizures despite at least two antiseizure medications. These children are at high risk for behavioral, emotional, social, and cognitive challenges, as well as reduced quality of life. Resective surgery has shown significant quality of life improvements compared to medication alone. However, for children who are ineligible, neuromodulation treatments such as deep brain stimulation (DBS) offer an alternative. While evidence suggests that DBS modulates brain networks involved in seizure control, outcomes remain inconsistent, seizure-free patients are rare, and no consensus exists on which brain networks to target for optimal outcomes.   

We hypothesize that favorable outcomes not only depend on electrode placement but also on the networks they modulate. To test this hypothesis, we conducted a preliminary study mapping the networks modulated by centromedian nucleus (CM) DBS in children with pharmaco-resistant epilepsy and examined whether differences in whole-brain connectivity are associated with differences in seizure reduction. 

Methods

Participants. We analyzed 17 children from the CHILD-DBS registry, a North American multicenter study involving six hospitals (Sherbrooke, Montreal, Toronto, Boston, Chicago, and Houston) [1]. All children underwent bilateral CM-DBS and were under the age of 19 at the time of surgery. 

Electrode reconstruction. Using Lead-DBS [2], we reconstructed electrodes and normalized them to MNI space (ICBM 2009b Nonlinear Asymmetric) [3], with electrodes visualized on a high-resolution 7T template (Fig. 1A and C). Volumes of tissue activated (VTAs)— the regions of the brain directly affected by the electrical stimulation—were computed for the active contacts of the DBS leads in each hemisphere. These were then combined to create a single, bilateral stimulation volume per patient. 

DBS network mapping. We sought to identify the healthy brain regions that are functionally connected to each patient’s stimulation volume. We used bilateral VTAs as seed regions to generate patient-specific stimulation network maps. To compute these maps, we averaged the blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) time course across all voxels within each VTA and correlated this mean time course with every brain voxel using resting-state functional MRI data from 1,000 healthy participants (male/female: 500/500; age ± SD, 21.40 ± 2.86) from the Brain Genomics Superstruct Project (GSP) [4].

Association to DBS outcomes. We used generalized linear models [5] to compare connectivity profiles between responders and non-responders, with responders defined as those achieving a seizure reduction of ≥50% (Fig. 1C-D). Statistical significance was assessed using false discovery rate (FDR) correction.

Results

Across the cohort, patients achieved a mean seizure reduction of 35.2 ± 37.0% (range: 0–94.3%). Eight of 17 patients (47.1%) were classified as responders, while nine (52.9%) were non-responders. Patients demonstrated a mean improvement in quality-of-life scores of 17.7 ± 29.8% (range: –23.9 to 67.2%).   

Voxel-wise overlap maps of VTA showed that stimulation converged within the ventral CM, with peak overlap between stimulation volumes localized to the CM-parafascicular (Pf) border, where the maximum voxel-wise overlap reached 52.9% of patients (Fig. 2A). At the patient-level (i.e., considering whether each patient’s VTA overlapped any portion of that anatomical region), 14 of 17 patients (82.4%) showed overlap within the ventral CM, while all patients showed overlap within approximately 1mm of the CM-Pf border.

Despite this heterogeneity, VTAs in all patients were positively correlated with the insula, brainstem, and temporolimbic regions (henceforth, positive network), and negatively correlated with the lateral frontoparietal regions (henceforth, negative network; Fig 2B).

Unthresholded voxel-wise t-maps were used to compare stimulation-associated connectivity profiles between responders and non-responders. No group differences survived whole-brain correction for multiple comparisons (pFDR < 0.05), nor at pFDR < 0.1. At an exploratory level a small number of voxels in the frontal and insular regions, as well as the lateral frontoparietal regions reached puncorrected < 0.05 . Accordingly, unthresholded maps suggested distributed cortical and subcortical differences between groups (Fig. 2C).

Conclusion

In conclusion, our preliminary study characterizes the brain networks engaged by CM-DBS in children with pharmaco-resistant epilepsy. By integrating stimulation anatomy with normative connectivity mapping, we demonstrate that CM-DBS engages distributed brain networks beyond the stimulation location alone, providing insight into the variability in clinical response. Given the low sample size, larger cohorts will be required to achieve sufficient statistical power to detect robust group-level network differences associated with clinical outcome. Nevertheless, these findings suggest that network-level analysis may help interpret variability in CM-DBS outcomes by revealing the broader brain circuits engaged by stimulation, thereby laying a foundation for future work aimed at moving beyond trial-and-error DBS programming towards more personalized treatment strategies.
