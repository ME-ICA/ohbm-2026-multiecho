Introduction

Dynamic contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (DCE-MRI) provides distinctive physiological information by capturing the temporal progression of contrast uptake, facilitating the evaluation of tissue perfusion, vascular permeability, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) transport — parameters that cannot be derived from static anatomical imaging1. However, standard 3D cartesian imaging of the whole head suffers from not only poor temporal resolution but also low spatial resolution with sensitivity to motion. It also exhibits severe signal loss near air-cavity region. The PETALUTE sequence, a three-dimensional golden-angle radial petal-shaped rosette trajectory protocol featuring ultrashort echo time (UTE), offers robust central k-space oversampling, and enhanced motion tolerance, making it ideally suitable for high-temporal-resolution DCE imaging. It also enables reliable signal detection in air-cavity regions. Furthermore, as the physiological interpretation of DCE-MRI hinges on the analysis of the time-intensity curve, semi-quantitative indices such as wash-in slope, wash-out rate, time-to-peak, and area under the curve (AUC) serve as sensitive markers of microvascular and CSF dynamics. Therefore, the flexible spatial-temporal PETALUTE acquisition and reconstruction pipeline enables reliable extraction of voxel-wise and region-of-interest (ROI) enhancement curves to support subsequent physiological analysis. In this abstract, we will show how the reconstruction pipeline works and analyze those semi-quantitative indices for several ROIs, including superior sagittal sinus (SSS), lateral ventricles (LV), the pituitary gland (PG), the cortical vein (CV), the internal carotid artery (ICA), as well as two air-cavity regions: nasal cavity (NC), and left ear (LE).

Methods

Data were acquired from three healthy control adult volunteers from an ongoing research MRI study. The gadolinium-based contrast agent (Gadavist, gadobutrol, Bayer, Ludwigshaven, Germany) in a single bolus (0.1 mL or 0.1 mmol per kg of body weight) was administered via power injector (Bayer, NJ, USA) and a 22-gauge intravenous catheter at the left antecubital vein, followed by a 20 mL saline flush at 2ml / sec. After 5 minutes of pre-contrast imaging, gadolinium was administered, followed by 15 minutes of continuous DCE acquisition. All scans were acquired on a 3-Tesla Siemens Prisma (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) whole-body MRI scanner using a 32-channel head coil. Each participant has 300,000 petals, with each petal containing 216 readout points, acquired with a repetition time (TR) of 4 milliseconds, and a flip angle of 6 degrees.

PETALUTE trajectories were generated from sequence parameters using the method of Shen et al. (2023)2, with NUFFT-based forward modeling (MIRT/SigPy-E) and Pipe-Menon density compensation. 32-channel data were SVD-compressed to eight virtual coils for efficient reconstruction.

Reconstruction was performed using Extra-Dimension Golden-Angle Radial Sparse Parallel MRI (xd-grasp)3, a compressed-sensing method that exploits sparsity along a motion or contrast dimension to achieve high-temporal-resolution reconstruction from highly undersampled golden-angle data. 300,000 petals were split into 1,000 petals per temporal frame, yielding 300 time frames, using Berkeley Advanced Reconstruction Toolbox (BART). The field of view was 240×240×240 mm3 with a matrix of 256×256×256, corresponding to a nominal isotropic resolution of approximately 0.9 mm.

The reconstructed 4D images have 300 time frames as an extra dimension and each time frame represents 4 seconds of the scan (1,000 petals with a 0.4-ms TR each; Figure 1). We applied N4 bias-field correction using ANTs, an extra motion correction using FSL, and registration to 1mm MNI space using FLIRT. The anatomical ROIs described in the introduction were manually segmented and analyzed using AFNI based on their time-intensity curves. Baseline was defined as frames 0–64, and post-contrast dynamics were analyzed from frame 65 onward for wash-in, wash-out, time-to-peak, and AUC calculations. 

The animation generated directly from our reconstruction pipeline visualizes dynamic signal changes over 300 time frames (20 minutes), showing the transition from the pre-contrast baseline to post-injection enhancement. The 4-second TR minimizes motion between successive frames, improving registration reliability and enabling stable tracking of dynamic contrast changes.

Results

DCE indices in Figure 2 showed robust physiological differentiation across vascular, tissue, and CSF-associated regions. SSS demonstrated the highest peak enhancement, fastest wash-in rate, and largest AUC, consistent with results reported in several prior studies in this field 4,5. In contrast, LV exhibited minimal enhancement and a near-zero wash-out rate, reflecting slow CSF exchange and negligible contrast uptake. Parenchymal and perivascular regions — including PG, CV, NC, and LE — showed intermediate enhancement and slower wash-in dynamics, with a time-to-peak of around 2.5 minutes after injection, characteristic of tissue-level contrast arrival and diffusion. ICA exhibited variable enhancement and relatively rapid wash-in, though with greater variability due to the small ROI size and partial-volume effects.

Conclusion

We present a high spatial and temporal resolution PETALUTE DCE-MRI reconstruction and analysis pipeline that generates physiologically reliable time–intensity curves across vascular, tissue, and CSF-associated regions. The combination of PETALUTE and XD-GRASP reconstruction enabled motion-robust 4-second temporal resolution and stable extraction of semi-quantitative enhancement metrics. ROI-based DCE indices were consistent with previously reported physiological behavior, demonstrating the feasibility of PETALUTE for probing microvascular and CSF dynamics. In addition, the use of UTE enables accurate signal detection near air-cavity regions—such as the NC and LE—which are typically not well visualized with traditional MRI. Together, these advances enable robust characterization of enhancement dynamics across diverse anatomical regions, establishing a strong foundation for future glymphatic transport in health and disease.
