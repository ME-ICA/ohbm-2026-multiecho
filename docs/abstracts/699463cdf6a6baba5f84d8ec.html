Introduction

In macaque monkeys, the V1/V2 border typically lies close to a gyral ridge along most of its length, thereby reducing the distance between retinotopically corresponding locations in V1 and V2 relative to an unfolded configuration. Since the bi-directional connections between V1 and V2 are among the strongest known pathways in primates1, this observation prompted the hypothesis that mechanical tension along axons within white matter leads to cortical folding and reduces aggregate cortical wiring length.2 Whether wiring length is reduced relative to the unfolded state is not at issue, however, as this surely must be the case. A more important question is whether aggregate wiring length is near-minimal for the actual 3D configuration of a given hemisphere relative to what it would be for alternative 3D configurations that are folded to the same degree (e.g., based on a local gyrification index) and have the same connectivity in terms of their topology rather than their precise 3D location. This issue is of particular interest for human visual cortex, which is much more convoluted and variable across individuals than in the macaque. Using retinotopic fMRI, Rajimehr & Tootell3 previously showed that mean curvature in a broad swath of V1 and V2 near the vertical meridian representation was biased towards outward (gyral) folding, suggesting a qualitatively similar but quantitatively weaker relationship between areal boundaries and folding than in monkeys. Here, we address this issue in greater depth using data from the Human Connectome Project (HCP) in which the V1/V2 border, cortical folding patterns (mean curvature) and retinotopy up to ~8o eccentricity were mapped in each individual. We confirmed that the V1-V2 border tends to be closer to gyral ridges, but show that exceptions are common. We also propose a concrete approach for determining the degree to which the observed aggregate V1-V2 wiring length supports the wiring length minimization hypothesis. 

Methods

Our initial analyses focused on 21 HCP ‘Test-Retest’ subjects who were scanned in two 2-day test-retest sessions at the WashU 3T scanner separated by > 1 month, plus a separate 7T session at UMinn that included retinotopic mapping. Areas V1, V2, and other areas in the HCP_MMP parcellation were identified using a novel multimodal deep learning areal classifier algorithm).4 For each subject, a set of command-line operations (using ‘wb_command’ in Connectome Workbench) was used to generate representations of the perimeter of area V1, the portion of this perimeter that adjoins area V2, mean curvature values for these perimeter-associated vertices, vertex indices located on gyral or sulcal ‘creases’ based on mean curvature values, and a variety of analogous features. Results were displayed in Connectome Workbench on views of occipital cortex displayed on spherical maps of the left and right hemispheres. 

Results

Fig. 1 shows a map of occipital cortex for an exemplar subject, with mean curvature in grayscale (panel A) and colored for V1 and V2 (panel B).  Vertices within 3 mm geodesic distance of the V1/V2 border are yellow in panel E and colored by mean curvature in panel F. For V1 and V2 together, comparable numbers of vertices have positive and negative mean curvature in the individual (panel C) and the group average (panel D). In contrast, the mean curvature along just the V1/V2 perimeter is markedly biased towards positive curvature in the individual (panel G) and the group average (panel H). To evaluate the degree to which the human V1-V2 border runs parallel to but slightly offset from a gyral ridge, as commonly occurs in macaques, we thresholded the mean curvature maps to indicate gyral and sulcal creases (respectively red and blue in panel I). A ‘crease proximity’ measure (panel J) shows about half of V1/V2 border vertices are at least 5 mm closer to a gyral crease than to a sulcal crease in the individual (panel K) and the group (panel L). Panels M and N show maps of polar angle, and eccentricity with the V1/V2 borders overlaid for test and retest sessions (red and black). In this and most other subjects the classifier-based V1/V2 borders largely agree with one another, and both run close to the superior (green) and inferior (blue) vertical meridians in the polar angle map. Panel O shows that intersecting narrow bands of polar angle and eccentricity (from panels M, N) enable identification of corresponding sites in V1 and V2 (black dots), which can then be viewed on the 3D ‘white’ surface of the same subject (panel P).

We propose a 6-step approach to evaluate the wiring length minimization hypothesis. 1) In each subject, we use maps of polar angle (Fig. 2A) and eccentricity (Fig. 2D) to identify corresponding retinotopic locations in V1 and V2, e.g., based on intersecting isopolar and isoeccentricity contours (Fig. 2E). 2) V1-V2 vertex pairs (at the gray-white cortical surface in the individual’s 3D space) will be linked by ‘axon-like’ trajectories constrained to lie within white matter. This may be achieved by determining the shortest geodesic distance on the gray/white surface that links corresponding V1 and V2 vertices, then moving each point orthogonal to the surface until it lies entirely within the white matter segmentation.  3) Each resultant tractography-like ‘streamline’ will be treated as an elastic strand under tension, algorithmically reducing it to near-minimal length while remaining constrained to white matter. 4) The aggregate wiring length (AWL) between V1 and V2 will be computed for all linked vertices in the native 3D anatomical configuration. 5) The individual’s retinotopic map will be displaced relative to the mean curvature map in modest increments (e.g., 2 – 4 mm geodesic distance) along each of two orthogonal axes on the spherical map (e.g., azimuthal and polar angle). 6) After each displacement, steps 2 – 4 above will be repeated but using the 3D vertex coordinates associated with the shifted locations of the retinotopic vertex pairs on the mean curvature map. This is analogous to ‘spin tests’ used to shuffle cortical configurations in various fMRI analyses, but it would limit the ROI to the vicinity of V1 and V2 rather than the entire hemisphere. 7) A 3D surface fit to the V1-V2 AWL plotted along the z-axis for each displacement value in the x-y plane will represent a wiring-length landscape for that individual. If the AWL for observed retinotopy pairings consistently represents a local minimum in this parameter space, it would suggest that wiring length minimization is a major factor influencing the overall pattern of cortical folds in V1 and V2. If instead the observed AWL is rarely at or near the minimum, it would suggest that wiring length is of low relevance for determining the folding of V1 and V2. 

Conclusion

The preliminary observations reported here demonstrate that the HCP retinotopy dataset and associated parcellations and surface reconstructions constitute a valuable resource for exploring important aspects of cortical wiring, anatomy, and geometry. If the proposed analyses demonstrate that the observed aggregate wiring length between V1 and V2 is consistently shorter than alternate configurations derived by translating maps of retinotopy and folding, it would provide strong evidence that compact wiring is an important principle of 3D cortical organization, at least in human occipital cortex. Given that mechanical tension along axons in white matter is a parsimonious and general mechanism for optimizing wiring length, such an outcome would provide indirect but strong support for tension-based morphogenesis. Conversely, a negative outcome in which the aggregate wiring length of the observed V1-V2 pathway is not consistently close to a local minimum would constitute evidence against this hypothesis. In any event, axonal tension is only one of many developmental mechanisms likely to contribute to cortical folding.5 Other mechanisms particularly relevant to occipital cortex and early visual areas include an uncharacterized process that initiates an invagination of the calcarine sulcus prior to the onset of long-distance cortico-cortical connectivity, plus mechanical buckling that causes irregular folds not closely related to areal boundaries. If axonal trajectories course under buckling-induced folds as they link corresponding V1 and V2 sites that are a moderate distance apart, axonal tension might impact their depth and overall shape to incrementally reduce wiring length.
