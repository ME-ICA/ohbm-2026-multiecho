Introduction

Atlases of the human brain play a crucial role in neuroscience and serve as fundamental tools for understanding the complex structural and functional organization of the brain [1]. They serve as standardized reference systems that support the precise identification and localization of brain regions, thereby enabling detailed investigations of structure–function relationships in both healthy and pathological conditions.

The Julich Brain Atlas offers detailed maps of cytoarchitectonic brain areas based on microscopic analysis of postmortem brains. Within the EBRAINS Atlas Framework, its cytoarchitectonic probability maps (pmaps) form a resource that captures inter-individual variability by representing, for each area, the voxel-wise probability of observing the corresponding cytoarchitectonic area across the examined brains. To further provide a single parcellation maps, these are summarized into a maximum probability map (MPM) by selecting, for each voxel, the most likely area across all available pmaps. The MPM-based cortical parcellation of the Julich Brain Atlas is integrated into widely used neuroimaging software packages both in volume and surface formats, including FreeSurfer and AFNI, making it directly accessible for large-scale neuroimaging studies and standardized processing pipelines.

The atlas is continuously extended and refined by adding new cytoarchitectonic areas and improving the underlying computational workflow. Here we present the updates introduced in the upcoming version 3.2. In this release, we introduce nine newly mapped cortical areas and 26 subcortical areas, together with methodological enhancements such as improved interpolation strategies for unmapped intermediate histological sections.

Methods

Maps of cortical areas and subcortical were generated using a microscopic, observer-independent delineation method applied to serial cell-body-stained sections of a set of ten postmortem brains (5 female, 5 male), which are part of a sample of 23 postmortem brains. Each brain was fixed in 4% formalin or Bodian, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. In two of them, referred to as "BigBrains", all sections were mounted on glass slides [2], whereas in the remaining 21 brains, every 15th section was mounted and stained using a modified silver method. The sections were digitized using flatbed and later Huron Tissuescope scanners, yielding approximately 13,000 images with in-plane resolutions of 20 µm and 1 µm. Linear and nonlinear distortions introduced during sectioning and mounting were corrected using previously acquired MRI datasets of the fixed brains. Given that the spatial resolution of MRI images (≤1 mm³) is lower than that of the histology, sections were down sampled in-plane while maintaining their total number, elastically aligned to the corresponding MRI slice on a section-by-section basis, and the resulting 3D reconstructed histological postmortem dataset was subsequently registered to a standard reference brain space [4].

Cytoarchitectonic areas were mapped after identifying borders based on multivariate statistics and image analysis [2,5] in high-resolution histological sections, using closed polygon traces in every 60th or 15th section depending on curvature and geometric complexity. An in-house online tool (Online Section Tracer) supported this process by providing full access to all existing structures and storing the data in a Git repository, enabling complete traceability of all modifications. This framework facilitated the integration of newly mapped structures while ensuring consistency with previously mapped areas.

Using the previously computed transformation parameters, all mappings were independently transformed through several sequential processing steps for each area: first into the 3D reconstructed space of each individual postmortem brain, and subsequently into the reference spaces [4].

If an area was not mapped in every 15th section, intermediate sections needed to be reconstructed. In version 3.1, this was achieved by linear interpolation between the mapped sections in the 3D-reconstructed space. In the new version, however, a nonlinear interpolation strategy is employed, which more accurately captures the structural and anatomical variability present in the histological material. Specifically, the area in the most recently mapped section n(i–1) is nonlinearly transformed to match the next mapped section n(i+1). Missing areas (e.g., when only every 60th section was mapped, requiring interpolation of three intermediate sections) are then generated by applying proportional fractions of this nonlinear transformation to the mapped area in section n(i–1), resulting in anatomically more accurate reconstructions of the unmapped sections.

The various transformation and resampling steps, the independent processing of individual areas, and intersubject anatomical variability may lead to a certain overlap between areas in both the individual 3D-reconstructed brains and the reference spaces. To correct for this, a global normalization across all areas was performed. Cortical areas in the 3D-reconstructed brains could be subsequently projected onto the computed surfaces and transformed into the reference space using a surface-based method (see Greve et al.).

Using a unified framework that treats cortical and subcortical areas in a similar way, all areas transformed into the reference space via linear and nonlinear volume-based transformations were superimposed to generate 3D probability maps. From these probability maps, a maximum probability map (MPM) was computed using a winner-takes-all approach. Finally, the resulting MPM was projected onto the cortical surface.

Results

For the new release 3.2, in the cortical domain, nine new areas were added to the frontal lobe including more fine grained maps of areas 44 and 45 of Broca''s region. These additions refine the cytoarchitectonic coverage of this region and almost completely resolve the transitional “gap map” that had been introduced to cover yet unmapped cortical regions. Figure 2a illustrates the newly added areas, highlighting their spatial topography, while Figure 2b shows the full maximum probability map (MPM) projected onto the white matter surface. Furthermore, 26 new subcortical areas of the hypothalamus (see Chervonnyy et al.) were mapped based on high-resolution histological data and are now fully integrated into the atlas. Their inclusion significantly increases the anatomical detail of structures that are often underrepresented in probabilistic brain atlases, thereby improving both the coverage and the utility of the Julich Brain Atlas for future neuroimaging and computational modeling studies.

Conclusion

The new version of the Julich Brain Atlas introduces 35 new cortical and subcortical areas. As a result, the total number of areas in the atlas has increased from 227 in version 3.1 to 262 in version 3.2, i.e. an increase of 15%. In addition to the new areas, methodological improvements have been implemented, particularly in the interpolation of unmapped intermediate sections and in the quality control to further improve the overall quality of the Julich Brain Atlas. However, in the future, upcoming versions will increasingly use surface-based methods for cortical areas, which are expected to further improve the results. The new version of the probabilistic cytoarchitectonic Julich Brain Atlas offers improved capabilities for analyzing structure-function relationships at the level of cortical areas, e.g., for neuroimaging studies, modeling, and simulation and will soon be integrated to EBRAINS, easily accessible through the siibra toolsuite.
