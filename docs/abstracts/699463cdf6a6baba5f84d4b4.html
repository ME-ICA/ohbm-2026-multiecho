Introduction

The BigBrain dataset [1], a microscopic 3D reconstruction of an entire human brain, has become a cornerstone for studying cortical layers and cytoarchitectonic boundaries. But in the focus on neurons and laminar structure [2], one critical feature has remained largely unexplored: the vasculature. Threaded throughout this landmark dataset is a wealth of vascular information. Subtle, but visible to the trained eye. Here we demonstrate that with appropriate methods, the intracortical vessels can be detected (see Figure 1). After detection, the density of meso-vessels can be mapped over the entire cortical surface. This vessel-map represents the first whole-brain map of mesoscopic intracortical vessels in humans. The regional and interhemispheric variance of the meso-vessels might hold potential to delineate areal boundaries. Our meso-vessel mapping technique reframes the BigBrain dataset as more than a neuronal atlas, revealing its potential as an unexpectedly rich source of vascular information.

Methods

We used the 0.1 mm isotropic BigBrain dataset [1] together with the cortical segmentation from [2]. After initial inspection, remaining instances of kissing gyri were manually corrected to ensure topological consistency. These manual edits were then smoothed using the LayNii [3] LN2_RIM_POLISH tool to remove residual artifacts. The final segmentation was subjected to thorough quality control in both hemispheres.

Next, we computed the gradient-magnitude image of the 0.1 mm BigBrain volume using LN2_GRAMAG, defined voxel-wise as the square root of the sum of squared spatial gradients across all three axes. To enhance the visibility of intracortical vessels, specifically to make vessel interiors appear locally bright, we applied a single-step grayscale morphological dilation to the gradient-magnitude image.

Cortical depth was parameterized by computing voxel-wise equi-volume layers using LN2_LAYERS. For full-surface mapping, we adopted a chunk-wise parametrization strategy. We first identified 250 maximally distant seed voxels on the mid-thickness surface. For each seed, we computed a 30-mm geodesic disk using LN2_MULTILATERATE, generating overlapping UV parameterizations that collectively tiled the entire cortex.

Each disk was then flattened using LN2_PATCH_FLATTEN, producing a set of local 2D patches. Within each patch, we performed vessel enhancement by detecting local maxima across the stack of depth-dependent flattened images. Detected maxima were dilated using 2D morphological operations, and we quantified how frequently each spatial location was detected across all layers to form a layer-stack “peak consistency” map. Thresholding this map at >40% yielded an initial meso-vessel detection mask.

To convert detections into a biologically interpretable metric, we applied a geodesic circular kernel (X-mm diameter) to compute local vessel density, expressed as vessels per cm². These meso-vessel density maps were then back-projected to the folded cortical geometry using LN2_PATCH_UNFLATTEN.

For visualization, we reconstructed mid-thickness cortical surfaces using a marching-cubes algorithm and smoothed the resulting meshes with the “advanced surface smoothing” procedure in BrainVoyager. Surfaces were subsequently decimated from N vertices to n vertices to improve rendering performance. We sampled the meso-vessel density, cortical curvature, equi-volume thickness, and Nissl intensity (from the 0.1 mm BigBrain volume) onto these surfaces and generated inflated surface maps for visualization (see Figure 1).

Results

Figure 2 shows the comparison of meso-vessel density with other cortical maps. The meso-vessel density map exhibits several focal hotspots, with the most prominent located within the calcarine sulcus, a region consistent with primary visual cortex (V1). When compared with cortical thickness, curvature, and cell body related contrast maps, this hotspot does not directly correspond to patterns observed in the other measures. This dissociation suggests that meso-vessel density captures complementary anatomical information that is not reflected in conventional cortical metrics and may provide an additional dimension for cortical mapping.

Conclusion

Leveraging the 100 μm isotropic BigBrain reconstruction with whole-brain coverage, we demonstrate that penetrating vessel densities can be systematically extracted across the human cortex. Although BigBrain was not designed as a vascular atlas and cannot serve as a gold standard, our results establish it as a practical silver standard, the most detailed and comprehensive resource currently available for mesoscale vessel mapping at the whole-cortex level. This reframes BigBrain as more than a cellular reference atlas, revealing a substantial reservoir of vascular information that has remained largely hidden in plain sight. Our benchmark analysis is necessarily a first pass and is subject to known constraints, including staining biases, tissue shrinkage, vessel-type ambiguity, and curvature-related distortions that may ultimately require equivolume surfaces or explicit geometric modeling to correct. Addressing these limitations will require additional datasets for validation (e.g. [4, 5]), but even in its current form, the BigBrain-derived vascular maps provide a valuable open-ended resource for imaging modelers, laminar fMRI researchers, and computational neuroscientists seeking mesoscale constraints on cortical angioarchitecture.
