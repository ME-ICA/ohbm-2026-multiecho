Introduction

The anterior salience network (ASN) plays a pivotal role in daily cognitive activities, assigning stimuli salience and switching between the preferential default mode network and central executive network activity to prioritize processing of internal or external information.1 The ASN comprises multiple hub regions, each featuring a large number of connections, such as the anterior cingulate, anterior insula, and supramarginal gyrus.2 Alterations in the activity or connectivity of these hubs may influence the global structural and functional connectome, leading to large-scale changes in functional brain activity. Brain tumors, particularly high-grade gliomas, may impact activity and structural connectivity across the brain, with more serious consequences when the hub regions or their corresponding major pathways are involved. To date, a number of studies have documented a decrease in mirror correlation, disruptions in brain functional resting-state networks,  and other disconnectivity patterns in glioma patients. However, research on functional connectivity, specifically focusing on hubs and the ASN as a network of hubs, remains relatively underexplored.

The aim of the current study is to investigate resting-state functional connectivity between the regions of interest (ROIs) within the ASN in brain tumors, with respect to a tumor malignancy grade.

Methods

The study sample comprised 23 healthy subjects and 43 patients with verified brain tumors (8, 18, and 17 with low-grade, III grade, and IV grade, respectively). The data were collected at the National Centre for Neurosurgery, JSC (Astana, Kazakhstan) using a 3T GE SIGNA Architect scanner. Reference structural images were acquired via 3D fast spoiled gradient echo (FSP GR) sequence (repetition time / echo time (TR/TE) = 7.2/2.8 ms, flip angle (FA) = 12°, 0.5×0.5×0.5 mm3 voxels). Two hundreds resting-state functional volumes were recorded with echo-planar imaging (GR-EPI) sequence (TR/TE = 3000/30 ms, FA = 90°, 1.88×1.88×3 mm3 voxels).

The fMRI analysis was performed using Conn 22.v2407 toolbox for MATLAB. Ten first volumes were discarded, then functional data were realigned to the first volume, susceptibility distortion corrected, slice-timing corrected, and normalized to the MNI space with resampled voxel size 2×2×2 mm3 using the tissue segmentation of a co-registered structural image. During the segmentation of the structural image, lesion mask manually drawn by an experienced neuroradiologist was treated as a separate tissue type. The outlier scans were identified with ART (framewise displacement >0.5 mm or global BOLD signal changes >5 σ) and regressed out of the data along with linear and quadratic trends, 5 white matter and 5 cerebro-spinal fluid CompCorr components, and Friston-12 movement parameters. The first ten volumes were discarded. The data were bandpass filtered in range 0.01-0.08 Hz. ? participants were excluded for excessive head movement, 1 for poor normalization, and 1 more for not enough effective degrees of freedom after the denoising.

First-level analysis involved computation of ROI-to-ROI connectivity using the ASN regions from the HPC-ICA network ROIs atlas. Functional connectivity was quantified using bivariate correlation coefficients, which were converted into Z-scores via Fisher's transformation for use in the General Linear Model (GLM) analysis.

Group-level comparisons were conducted using the General Linear Model (GLM), where individual connectivity metrics served as dependent variables, and the defined patient groups represented the independent variables.. Cluster-level inferences utilized the Threshold Free Cluster Enhancement, with 1000 residual-randomization iterations. Results were considered significant if met p-FWE<0.05 TFCE-score threshold. Within significant clusters, we considered individual connections using p-FDR<0.05 threshold when applicable, else p<0.05 uncorrected threshold.

Results

The F-contrast comprising 4 groups (healthy controls, grade I+II, grade III, and grade IV) using the p-FWE < 0.05 TFCE threshold revealed a single significant cluster (TFCE=5.54, pFWE=0.032) of two connections, namely, between the 1) right and left SMG and 2) right SMG and right anterior insula. However, only connection 1 (F(3, 64)=6.67, pFDR=0.011) and not connection 2 (F(3, 64)=1.52, pFDR=0.631) was significant on a single connection level.

This finding was also reproduced in T-contrast between the grade III and grade IV, suggesting greater connectivity between the SMG ROIs in the grade III groups (t(35)=4.34, pFDR<0.001). Last, this single connectivity was tested accounting for 4 binary variables coding the tumor localization as covariates of no interest. While the significance of the between-group difference decreased, it still preserved at p<0.05 threshold (t(38)=2.59, p=0.014). 

Conclusion

The current study demonstrated decrease in functional connectivity between the left and right SMG in grade IV glioma, compared to grade III. We do not interpret this finding as a disconnectivity feature unique for the SMG. Instead, we consider this an instance of large hub connectivity disruption in glioblastoma. Certain localization of connectivity alterations is probably dependent on the predominant localization of tumors themselves.

Our results most probably reflect the differential tumor growth dynamics in grade III and grade IV tumors. First, grade IV tumors are typically larger, thus produce greater mass effect and more likely affect other brain regions that may covertly support the indirect functional connectivity between left and right SMG. Second, glioblastomas are notorious for intensive neovascularization processes that leads to a notable neurovascular uncoupling.3 Third, while moderate-speed growth of the grade III gliomas still does not exclude the ability for compensation, rapid volume increases in grade III gliomas exceed the limits of brain neuroplastic changes, preventing effective microstructural adaptation that could retain close-to-normal connectivity levels.4 Last, glioblastomas feature specific, destructive, pattern of interaction with the white matter fibers, compared to less damaging infiltrative pattern of anaplastic tumors.5

Collectively, these mechanisms may cause greater disruption of the direct neural transmission via the corpus callosum as well as of indirect links mediated by superior longitudinal fascicle and brainstem-level interhemispheric connectivity, which are critical for symmetric SMG functional connectivity.
