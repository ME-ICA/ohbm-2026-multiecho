Introduction

Brain white matter hyperintensities (WMHs) are indicators of cerebral small vessel disease - a condition associated with cognitive impairments that is prevalent in older age2,3 - but may also occur in younger adults.3 Higher WMH load is associated with higher cardiometabolic risk,2–4 including higher HbA1c.4 The skeletal muscle is an insulin-sensitive organ involved in the homeostasis of nutrient and energy metabolism. Dysregulation of the insulin-stimulated glucose uptake in the muscle may promote insulin resistance, increase in HbA1c (i.e., long-term blood glucose) levels, and development of type 2 diabetes, which may be reversed by muscle maintenance and exercise.1 We have previously shown that liver fat mediates the link between cardiometabolic factors (including HbA1c) and total WMH load, and that total WMH load mediates the link between liver fat and cognitive performance.4 Furthermore, we have demonstrated links between sarcopenic traits (low muscular strength and muscle fat infiltration (MFI)), brain structure, and cognitive performance.5 Here we investigate the links between HbA1c, muscular phenotypes, and brain periventricular, deep, and total WMH load in a large sample of UK Biobank participants (n=31,456) between 45 and 85 years of age at body and brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). We hypothesize that higher levels of HbA1c at baseline is associated with poorer muscle phenotypes and higher WMH load the at the follow-up imaging timepoint (approximately 10 years after baseline assessment). Further, since the skeletal muscle is insulin sensitive and related to glucose uptake,1 we expect that muscle phenotypes mediate the link between HbA1c and WMH load. 

Methods

We included 31,456 UK Biobank participants (54.8% females; mean age 63.0±7.4 years at MRI), with data on brain periventricular, deep, and total WMH load, intracranial volume (ICV), HbA1c, and muscle phenotypes (i.e., maximum handgrip strength, and MRI derived measures of thigh MFI and total thigh muscular volume (TTMV)). The muscle phenotypes capture muscular strength (i.e., handgrip strength), muscle quality (i.e., MFI), and muscle quantity (i.e., TTMV). We excluded participants with very high WMH load (i.e., outliers). HbA1c was measured at baseline, on average 9.9±2.4 years before brain and body MRI (i.e., imaging timepoint). Maximum handgrip strength was available at both timepoints. We evaluated the distributions (data not shown) and the correlation matrix (Figure 1) of the continuous variables included in the statistical analysis. 

To test our hypotheses, we implement multiple linear regression and mediation analyses on the UK Biobank Research Analysis Platform in R (version 4.4.0), using the lm function and Lavaan structural equation modelling (SEM; version 0-6.20). Initially, we used separate multiple linear regression models to investigate whether (i) HbA1c was associated with muscle phenotypes (i.e., maximum handgrip strength, MFI, and TTMV), and (ii) whether HbA1c and muscle phenotypes were associated with periventricular, deep, and total WMH load. We adjusted all regression models for age, age2, genetic sex, age-by-genetic sex, age2-by-genetic sex, ICV (only WMH models), and site, entered categorical variables as factors and standardized continuous variables. We computed the partial correlation r-effect size from the t-statistics. Subsequently, we performed SEM mediation analyses to investigate whether muscle phenotypes mediate the link between HbA1c and later WMH load, while adjusting for the same set of covariates as in the regression models except interaction terms (due to methodological limitations). For the mediation analyses, we entered categorical variables as numerical dummy 0/1 variables and standardized the continuous input and mediation variables. We applied Bonferroni multiple comparison correction (p≤0.05/N=0.001, across N=48 tests).

Results

The correlation matrix suggests that the included continuous variables are, to a varying extent, correlated (Figure 1). We observed the strongest positive correlations between measures of WMH load (r in [0.52, 0.99]) and maximum handgrip strength (baseline and imaging) and TTMV (r in [0.74, 0.80]). MFI and age showed negative correlations with TTMV and maximum handgrip strength (baseline and imaging), and positive with the remaining variables. Baseline HbA1c showed positive correlations with MFI, age, and measures of WMH load at imaging. 

The regression analyses showed that higher baseline HbA1c is significantly associated with lower handgrip strength (baseline: r=-0.04, p=9.6e-15; imaging: r=-0.03, p=4.1e-07) and higher MFI (r=0.10, p=5.7e-66) and TTMV (r=0.02, p=5.2e-05; Figure 2A) and higher periventricular (r=0.04, p=8.3e-15) and total (r=0.04, p=4.5e-14) WMH load at imaging (Figure 2B). Higher MFI is associated with higher periventricular (r=0.09, p=2.7e-15), deep (r=0.06, 1.1e-26), and total (r=0.09, p=7.5e-57) WMH load at the same timepoint. Higher baseline handgrip strength is associated with lower periventricular (r=-0.04, p=1.4e-11) and total (r=-0.04, 1.7e-10) WMH load, and similarly, higher handgrip strength at imaging is associated with lower periventricular (r=-0.04, p=5.3e-10), deep (r=-0.03, p=8.8e-06), and total (r=-0.04, p=2.2e-10) WMH load. We observed no associations between TTMV and any of the included WMH measures (Figure 2C).

The mediation analyses showed stronger mediation effects for MFI than handgrip strength (at baseline and imaging) with similar directions, with significant mediation effects (estimates in [1.0, 11.6], z-scores in [3.5, 9.9], p-values in [0.0005, 0ñ) and direct effects (estimates in [46.9, 61.9], z-scores in [6.2, 7.4], p-value in [5.9e-10, 1.2e-13) between HbA1c and both periventricular and total WMH load in separate models. The mediation analyses further showed that MFI fully mediates (estimate=1.5, z-score=8.3, p-value=2.2e-16) the link between HbA1c and deep WMH load (i.e., model showed non-significant direct effect; Figure 2DE). 

Conclusion

Our findings showed that (i) higher HbA1c at baseline is associated with lower muscular strength at baseline and follow-up, and with lower muscular quality and higher periventricular and total WMH load at follow-up; (ii) lower muscular quality is associated with higher deep, periventricular, and total WMH load at follow-up, while (iii) the opposite effects are observed for higher muscular strength; and (iv) greater mediation effects for muscular quality at follow-up than for muscular strength at baseline and follow-up between HbA1c at baseline and WMH load at follow-up. Although our study cannot establish causality, the results support that maintaining muscular health and prevention of hyperglycemia are associated with a lower risk of cerebral small vessel disease and associated cognitive decline in middle-aged and elderly adults.
