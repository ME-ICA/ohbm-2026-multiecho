Introduction

Fatigue is a multifaceted construct, referring to the subjective experience of mental or physical exhaustion, whereas fatigability describes a person’s susceptibility to fatigue and refers to the measurable decline in performance over time.1,2 Individuals with tinnitus (TSI) frequently report heightened mental fatigue, particularly after tasks requiring sustained attention or listening in adverse conditions. Despite these consistent clinical reports, investigations of fatigue and fatigability in TSI remain scarce. To date, only a few studies have examined fatigue in TSI,3 showing higher fatigue scores in TSI compared to controls. However, such work does not address moment-to-moment changes in performance or situational vulnerability to fatigue.

Current theoretical frameworks propose that the persistent perception of an internally generated sound places continuous demands on attentional and perceptual systems, potentially reducing available resources for everyday functioning.4 Fatigue may therefore influence how efficiently such resources are allocated over time, particularly under demanding conditions. This highlights the need to better understand the role of fatigue and fatigability in TSI. Moreover, as Mohamad et al.5 argue, highly isolated laboratory tasks may fail to capture the complexities of real-world (fatigue) dynamics, underscoring the need for more ecologically valid and multidimensional assessments.

In the current pilot phase, we introduce and validate a novel, multimodal paradigm to investigate fatigue and fatigability using repeated measurements across self-report, behavioral, electrophysiological, and physiological modalities in younger adults. This state-of-the-art framework enables the characterization of subjective and objective fatigue trajectories over time and provides a methodological foundation for future investigations in tinnitus, as well as broader applications in auditory and visual attention research and other clinical populations.

Methods

To assess fatigue and fatigability from multiple perspectives, participants complete an extensive set of questionnaires (demographics, quality of life, fatigue, fatiguability, sleep, stress, mood, and personality) followed by two laboratory sessions: an active, cognitively demanding fatiguing session and a passive non-fatiguing control session. Across both sessions, we obtain repeated state-fatigue ratings and multimodal objective measurements.

Each session begins and ends with resting-state EEG, a passive paradigm, and two alertness tasks (visual and auditory). During the active session’s fatiguing phase, participants perform an intensive cognitive test battery (mainly executive function tasks) in both verbal and non-verbal modalities, including Go/No-Go, Stroop, task switching, working memory, and fluency tasks. Continuous EEG, eyetracking, and physiological signals (GSR, HRV, respiration) are recorded throughout.

Data collection follows a staged validation pipeline prior to the TSI–control comparison.

  1. Study 1.1 (N=10): validation of resting-state EEG and visual alertness task.
  2. Study 1.2 (N=10): addition of the auditory alertness task.
  3. Study 1.3 (N=10): addition of passive paradigms.
  4. Study 2 (Ntin = 30, Ncon = 30): comparison of tinnitus participants to matched controls.

Data analysis: Self-report questionnaires and repeated fatigue ratings are analyzed using linear mixed-effects models to compare trajectories between active and passive sessions, controlling for motivation and CO2 levels. Behavioral performance in alertness tasks is evaluated by pre/post changes in reaction time and accuracy. EEG analyses target ERP-related changes in alertness (e.g., N200) and changes in resting-state spectral and entropy-based measures. Further, also physiological (GSR, HRV, respiration) and eyetracking data are assessed for pre/post differences and session-related changes.

Results

Data collection for Study 1.1 is complete, and data collection for Study 1.2 is nearly complete, with exploratory analyses for both currently ongoing.

Preliminary findings indicate that subjective fatigue increases more strongly during the fatiguing session compared to the passive session (session × time interaction: β = -0.01, p < 0.001). Performance in the alertness task shows a pre-post increase in reaction times in the fatiguing session, whereas reaction times slightly improve in the passive session (β = 0.02 s, p = 0.047). Electrophysiologically, N200 amplitude was reduced from pre to post in the fatiguing session at frontocentral sites (FCz: t(9) = 2.49, p = 0.034, d = 0.79), with no corresponding effects in the passive session. Resting-state analyses reveal no significant session effects; however, descriptive trends suggest increased theta and alpha power following the fatiguing session. Eyetracking results likewise show some consistent session differences, e.g. in the post fatiguing session we see an increase in eye movements and pupil diameter. Physiological measures (GSR, HRV, respiration) show no consistent patterns of change. 

Analyses of Study 1.2, including the auditory alertness task, are currently underway. Preliminary analyses further suggest subtle differences in the N1 and P2 components; however, these findings are based on initial exploratory analyses and require confirmation.

Conclusion

Our pilot results already suggest measurable subjective and objective effects of fatigue in younger adults, particularly across self-report, behavioral, and electrophysiological measures. The multimodal design captures several aspects of fatigue and fatigability. We will further refine this pipeline to enhance the feasibility of the protocol for future general and clinical, and ultimately tinnitus research, enabling a more precise examination of fatigue- and fatigability-related processes.
