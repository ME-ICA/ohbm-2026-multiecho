Introduction

The dopaminergic midbrain is associated with pathological changes in functions related to decision making, such as reward processing and motivation. Neurofeedback studies showed that volitional upregulation of the dopaminergic midbrain is possible^1,2,3,4. Apart from neurofeedback, real-time fMRI enables us to relate neural activity to behavioural outcomes in an adaptive manner. Here, we used such an adaptive paradigm to study the causal relation between self-modulated dopaminergic midbrain activity and decision-making in an effort-discounting task. The main question of this study is whether effort-related decisions are causally dependent on dopaminergic midbrain activity levels reached by self-modulation?

Methods

Seventy-four participants underwent neurofeedback training of the midbrain in a Philips Achieva 3T MRI scanner using OpenNFT toolbox^5 as neurofeedback system. They were instructed to learn volitional up-regulation by motivational self-imagery and down-regulation by demotivational self-imagery. Before this neurofeedback training, participants had been calibrated behaviorally on their willingness to exert mental effort, operationalized as n-back working memory. In a novel design, we combined these two tasks into an adaptive fMRI decision-making task. Specifically, participants had to decide whether to accept or decline an effort task when their neural activity was either in a high or low dopaminergic state. We assessed the influence of these different midbrain activity states on choice behavior and response times. We analyzed the behavioral data in a model-free fashion and with hierarchical diffusion decision models using HDDM toolbox. Furthermore, we performed fMRI analysis using SPM12 for preprocessing (slice time correction, realignment, bias-field correction, normalization, smoothing with 5 mm kernel, physiological noise correction via TAPAS toolbox) and GLM analyses. 

Results

First, the linear regression analyses of effort choices revealed that participants significantly more often accepted to perform effort when dopaminergic midbrain activity level is in a high adapted state (more than 25 % above rest activity) compared to non-adapted or low-adapted state (more than 25 % below rest activity). Response times were longer during the adaptive fMRI task compared to the non-adapted trials. 

Second, we modeled the data with a hierarchical drift diffusion model, assuming that decisions and response times result from noisy evidence sampling process. The psychometric curves revealed a leftward shift in the high dopaminergic state but not in the low dopaminergic state. That means, participants accept the same effort for less money in the high dopaminergic state. Our drift diffusion model copes very well the shift of the softmax decision function. Mainly, our analyses revealed a significantly higher drift rate for decisions in high dopaminergic state pointing towards a shift in preferences for decision-making moment.

Third, the neural pattern of the adaptive decision-making leading to acceptance of effort reveals differences in brain areas reported as valuation system of the brain, such as anterior insula, striatum and ventromedial prefrontal cortex. Exemplarily, the activity in anterior insula is addpated according to dopaminergic self-regulation preceding decision trials, which is not the case for non-adapted decision trials.

Conclusion

We find that in an upregulated neural activity state in the dopaminergic midbrain participants were more willing to exert effort. The upregulation leads to an increased accumulation of evidence in favour of accepting effort, i.e. reduced effort discounting. Both high and low dopaminergic states are associated with larger thresholds, resulting in longer response times in both regulatory conditions. In contrast to the drift rate, there is no difference between high and low dopaminergic state, suggesting that the higher thresholds may not reflect effects of the direction of regulation The model-based analysis showed a regulation effect only on choice behavior not on response times between modulation conditions and suggested that regulation effects primarily concerned choice preferences rather than attention. This is in line with findings from MR analysis showing increased neural activity patterns in brain valuation system, such as anterior insula, striatum and ventromedial prefrontal cortex, in congruence to high dopaminergic state before acceptance of effort. In conclusion, our work points to a strong causal link between human midbrain activity and the willingness to perform mental effort and opens new perspectives for disorders with reduced motivation to exert effort, such as depression and schizophrenia.
