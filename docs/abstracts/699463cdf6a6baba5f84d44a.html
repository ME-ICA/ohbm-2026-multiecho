Introduction

Advanced neuroimaging analyses have enormous clinical potential but are rarely used in practice due to the time and complexity of current research pipelines. Conventional tools can take many hours per scan and require expert intervention, making them impractical for same-day clinical use. This is especially true for diffusion MRI data.1 This gap leaves rich quantitative information untapped during routine radiology. There is a critical need for a fast, robust MRI preprocessing pipeline that can deliver research-grade results within clinical time frames.

Here we present MICAFlow, a fully automated MRI preprocessing pipeline designed to bridge the research-clinic divide. MICAFlow focuses on essential volumetric and diffusion MRI processing rather than time-intensive cortical surface analysis, to maximize practicality. It leverages modern techniques like deep learning segmentation, label-guided registration, advanced artifact corrections, and efficient parallel workflows to drastically reduce processing time while maintaining high data quality. We hypothesize that MICAFlow’s combination of speed and robustness can make quantitative MRI analysis feasible during routine clinical workflow.

Methods

Pipeline Design: MICAFlow is implemented in Python with a Snakemake-based workflow for automation and parallelization. The pipeline accepts T1-weighted, T2-FLAIR, and diffusion MRI (DWI) images. Major processing steps include bias field correction, brain extraction and segmentation using SynthSeg,2 Label-Augmented Modality-Agnostic Registration (LAMAReg), and diffusion preprocessing. LAMAReg is a two-stage label-guided registration strategy, first aligning segmentation labels of moving and target images using Advanced Normalization Tools’ (ANTs) SyNRA to obtain a coarse anatomical alignment, then refining with deformable intensity-based registration. This provides robust multi-modal alignment even in the presence of lesions or distortions by anchoring registration on common anatomical structures rather than raw intensities. We applied LAMAReg for FLAIR-to-T1 and DWI-to-T1 co-registration, as well as T1-to-MNI normalization, to ensure all outputs are accurately aligned.

Diffusion Processing: The DWI branch performs denoising and Gibbs correction (DIPY), motion/eddy-current correction (NiFreeze), and susceptibility distortion correction. If reverse-phase-encoded b=0 images are available, we use PyHySCO (physics-based unwarping) for EPI distortion correction; otherwise, we employ Synb0-DISCO, a deep-learning method that synthesizes an undistorted b=0 from the T1, to correct susceptibility distortions. After these corrections, the DWI is aligned to T1 using LAMAReg (with SynthSeg segmentations on the b=0 and T1) so that diffusion-derived maps can be mapped into T1 image space. We then compute diffusion tensor metrics (FA, MD) and propagate them, along with the aligned FLAIR, to standard space.

Evaluation: We evaluated MICAFlow on multimodal MRI exams from the MICA-MICs3 dataset (n=116 T1w scans, 72 FLAIR scans, 64 DWI scans, 54 with all scans). Key performance metrics included processing time (wall-clock runtime) on varying numbers of CPU cores, and registration accuracy for multi-modal and mono-modal alignment. For runtime, we measured total processing time on a typical dataset (T1+FLAIR+DWI) using 1, 4, 8, 12, and 16 CPU cores. For registration accuracy, we compared MICAFlow’s LAMAReg against standard methods (ANTs SyN, FSL epi_reg, FreeSurfer’s EasyReg) for aligning DWI b=0 to T1, FLAIR to T1, and T1 to MNI. Alignment quality was quantified by three metrics: Mutual Information (MI), Modality Independent Neighborhood Descriptor (MIND)4 error, and Normalized Gradient Fields (NGF).5 All comparisons used appropriate statistical tests (paired comparisons with Bonferroni correction). ANTs parameters were united across LAMAReg and ANTs tests, ensuring an equal number of registration iterations. Notably, MICAFlow completed all cases with zero failures, requiring no manual intervention.

Results

Speed: MICAFlow achieved a dramatic improvement in processing speed, meeting clinical workflow demands. On a 16-core CPU, the full multimodal pipeline (T1+FLAIR+DWI) ran in about 17 minutes compared to ~110 minutes on a single core (an ~6.5× speedup). Scaling was near-linear: e.g., 8 cores ~22 min, 4 cores ~45 min. By contrast, a conventional pipeline (MICAPipe) requires ~4–6 hours on the same data. Importantly, without any GPU, MICAFlow processed a comprehensive exam in <20 min on standard hardware. Structural-only subsets were even faster (T1+FLAIR in ~6 min, T1-only ~3 min on 16 cores). Even with 4 cores, the full pipeline finished in <45 min and the structural pipeline in ~10 min. No processing failures occurred, underscoring robustness. These results confirm that advanced MRI preprocessing can be done within a routine clinical timeframe, without specialized hardware.

Registration Accuracy: MICAFlow’s label-driven registration yielded superior alignment across modalities. For the challenging DWI–T1 co-registration, LAMAReg achieved superior mutual information, NGF, and MIND error among methods, with differences vs. FSL epi_reg and FreeSurfer EasyReg highly significant (p<0.001). Compared to ANTs SyN, LAMAReg also improved DWI–T1 alignment (MI metric, p<0.05). Similarly, FLAIR–T1 registration accuracy was significantly better with LAMAReg than with SyN or FreeSurfer (p<0.001). Even for T1–MNI normalization, LAMAReg outperformed standard SyN, notably on NGF and MIND (p<0.001), and EasyReg on all metrics. LAMAReg was also slightly better than standard SyN on T1-MNI normalization (p<0.05). The increased accuracy of LAMAReg against ANTs underscores the importance of a good initialization for SyN registration. Qualitatively, LAMAReg produced near-perfect overlap of anatomical structures (e.g., ventricles, lesions) between modalities, whereas other tools showed minor mismatches. These findings validate the advantage of our registration approach for multi-modal MRI.

Conclusion

MICAFlow is a significant step toward bringing research-grade neuroimaging into routine clinical practice. By combining cutting-edge machine learning (for segmentation and artifact correction) with proven algorithms (diffeomorphic registration, etc.) in an optimized pipeline, we achieved both high accuracy and rapid throughput. In our study, analyses that once took hours can now be completed in minutes without sacrificing quality. This enables quantitative MRI results (e.g. volumes, diffusion maps) to be available at the time of clinical review, transforming their practical utility. Furthermore, the improved multi-modal co-registration enhances the clinical trustworthiness of the outputs, giving clinicians greater confidence to base decisions on these quantitative measures.

In sum, advanced MRI preprocessing can be done quickly and robustly, closing the gap between research and clinical imaging. MICAFlow’s open-source, CPU-optimized design allows easy deployment in hospital settings (no specialized hardware needed), paving the way for broader adoption of quantitative neuroimaging. We anticipate that this tool will enable new clinical applications of MRI biomarkers and help realize the promise of personalized, data-driven neurology.
