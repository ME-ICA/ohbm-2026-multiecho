Introduction

Fetal brain Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) is crucial for assessing neurodevelopment in utero. However, analyzing this data presents significant challenges due to fetal motion, low signal-to-noise ratio, and the need for complex multi-step processing, including motion correction, super-resolution reconstruction, segmentation, and surface extraction. While various specialized tools exist for individual steps, integrating them into robust, reproducible, and user-friendly workflows that go from raw images to processed volumes is not straightforward. This lack of standardization hinders reproducibility across studies and limits the adoption of advanced analysis techniques for researchers and clinicians. To address these challenges, we introduce Fetpype, an open-source Python library designed to streamline and standardize the preprocessing and analysis of T2-weighted fetal brain MRI data as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Methods

The Fetpype library
Fetpype (https://github.com/fetpype/fetpype) is a Python package integrating established neuroimaging tools into a cohesive, extensible framework, relying on four main principles:

  1. Data Standardization: Fetpype requires input in the Brain Imaging Data Structure (BIDS) format, enhancing interoperability and data management.

  2. Containerization: Processing tools are encapsulated in Docker or Singularity containers, ensuring reproducibility and minimizing installation issues.

  3. Workflow Management: The Nipype library^1 is used to construct processing workflows: it provides a robust interface for combining different steps from different containers or packages, facilitating data caching and parallelization, and allowing pipelines to be easily shareable. 

  4. Configuration: Pipeline configuration is managed using simple YAML files and the Hydra library^2, allowing users to easily select between different modules or parameters without directly modifying the code.

Fetpype currently integrates modules for preprocessing (brain extraction, non-local means denoising, N4 bias-field correction), super-resolution reconstruction, segmentation, and cortical surface extraction. Its goal is to provide a standardized, reproducible, and flexible open-source platform, facilitating integration of community tools through modular Nipype-based containerized interfaces. The pipeline is publicly available on GitHub (https://github.com/fetpype/fetpype) and is extensively documented  (https://fetpype.github.io/fetpype).

Validation

Clinical data.

To validate Fetpype, we processed 635 fetal MRI subjects from ongoing studies at La Timone, Marseilles, France (n=216); BCNatal (Hospital Clínic and Hospital Sant Joan de Déu), Barcelona, Spain (n=91); Lausanne University Hospital (CHUV), Switzerland (n=36); and the fetal dHCP dataset (n=292, https://www.developingconnectome.org/project/). Exclusion criteria were twin pregnancies or fetal pathologies. Ethical approval was obtained from all centers (La Timone: Aix-Marseille University N°2022-04-14-003; BCNatal: HCB/2022/0533; CHUV: CER-VD 2021-00124).

MRI was acquired using 1.5T or 3T scanners with a series of T2w 2D sequences, with a varying number of stacks per acquisition. The cohort included a total of 635 subjects (261 females, 326 males, 46 unknown), with gestational age ranging from 20 to 39 weeks.


Data processing.

We processed the data using Fetpype's default pipeline. The preprocessing included brain extraction (Fetal-BET), non-local means denoising, and N4 bias-field correction. The reconstruction was done using NeSVoR (v.0.5.0)^3, and the segmentation used BOUNTI^4. The analysis then focused on eight aggregated structures: extra-cerebral CSF (eCSF), cortical gray matter (cGM), white matter (WM), lateral ventricles (LV), cerebellum, basal ganglia (BG), thalamus, and brainstem, excluding smaller regions. Measurements were  harmonized using combat-GAM^5.

Normative modeling.

We used generalized additive models for location, scale, and shape (GAMLSS) to generate normative centiles across gestational age, implemented in R’s gamlss package (https://www.gamlss.com/). We used the Sinh–Arcsinh (SHASH) distribution (location, scale, skewness, kurtosis) to model non-normal residuals. Due to our sample size (<1000), skewness and kurtosis were fixed (0 and 1). We reported the 5th, 10th, 50th, 90th, 95th centiles, and estimated model variability by bootstrapping 50 population samples for each structure that were used to fit different GAMLSS models to estimate variability. 

Results

Fetpype was successfully deployed across three sites and applied to four datasets, encompassing 635 fetal MRI scans: 216 from the MarsFet database (20–38 weeks), 292 from the fetal dHCP database (20–38 weeks), 36 from Lausanne University Hospital (21-36 weeks), and 91 from BCNatal (26–39 weeks). Processing was completed successfully for nearly all scans, demonstrating the robustness of Fetpype across heterogeneous multi-site data. The processing was highly efficient, with each scan fully pre-processed, reconstructed and segmented in 501±33 seconds (~8min 20sec) on a desktop workstation running Ubuntu 24.04 (i9-11900K processor, 64 GB DDR4 RAM, NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada GPU with 48 GB of VRAM). 

The resulting harmonized normative curves (Fig. 2) represent the largest published set of fetal brain growth trajectories to date. Centile estimates were consistent across datasets, with narrow confidence intervals indicating low variability. Fetpype effectively mitigated inter-site differences, providing a standardized framework that supports reliable cross-cohort comparisons.

Analysis of individual brain structures revealed expected growth patterns over gestation, with cortical gray matter and white matter volumes increasing steadily, while extra-cerebral CSF decreased proportionally. These normative trajectories align with prior reports, but benefit from unprecedented sample size and multi-site harmonization. 

Conclusion

Our results highlight Fetpype’s potential to standardize fetal neuroimaging research and support future large-scale clinical and scientific studies thanks to its easy-to-use, lightweight architecture. Our results showcase a first example of large-scale, multi-site studies powered by Fetpype.

In the future, we plan to supplement Fetpype with an automated reporting library containing automated quality control, subject-wise and population-wise biometry and volumetry, as well as surface-based analysis. 

Fetpype aims to advance research by improving comparability across studies, but also to foster community collaboration by facilitating the dissemination of new image processing methods for clinical applications. The Fetpype community warmly welcomes contributions from researchers desiring to integrate their method in this framework.
