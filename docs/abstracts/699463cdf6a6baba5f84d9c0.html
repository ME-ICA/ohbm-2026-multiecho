Introduction

Methamphetamine use disorder (MUD) disrupts monoamine communication, and chronic use changes the brain’s structure and function through neurotoxic cascades. These changes manifest as neural network disruptions feeding into maladaptive behaviours, complicating abstinence.1 Resting-state fMRI research has shown that networks including the default mode, salience, central executive, and attention are disrupted in MUD.2 The network-level dysfunction from MUD is associated with impaired cognitive control, executive functioning, emotional regulation, and increased compulsive behaviours.3 Beyond network dysconnectivity, structural MRI reveals cortical thinning and reduced volumes in key frontal and occipito-temporal areas.2 In the same cohort, structural alterations in cortical morphometry have been previously reported, including effects on the superior frontal, lingual, and lateral occipital regions.4

Cognitive testing shows executive function inefficiencies that persist into early MUD abstinence.4 Difficulties are often observed in domains such as working memory, cognitive flexibility, and inhibitory control. These lingering deficits may further challenge treatment engagement and increase vulnerability to relapse in the early stages of recovery.4

Relapse is a major characteristic that complicates and confounds addiction research, occurring predominantly in early abstinence. Within early abstinence, less is known about functional state of neural networks following methamphetamine cessation. By characterising early abstinence network differences in MUD, it can better inform the development of targeted treatment strategies.

To address this gap, the present study aimed to (i) characterise functional connectivity (FC) differences in individuals with MUD during early abstinence compared with healthy controls, and (ii) examine how these FC alterations relate to executive functioning and relevant demographic factors.

Methods

A total of 22 participants were included, comprising 10 participants with MUD in early abstinence (<30 days - mean age: 39.70 ± 9.13, 60% female) and 12 age- and sex-matched healthy controls (mean age: 40.75 ± 10.66, 67% female). Case was included upon a DSM-V diagnosis. Ethics approval was obtained (2022 EXP 11360) through the Health and Disability Ethics Committee (HDEC) in New Zealand.

All participants underwent an MRI scan using a 3.0 T GE SIGNA scanner with a 48-channel head coil, acquiring resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) and T1-weighted images. rs-fMRI data was acquired using a two-dimensional gradient-echo echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence with HyperBand acceleration (factor = 8). Imaging parameters included a repetition time (TR) of 431 ms, echo time (TE) of 20 flip angle of 20°, and a single echo acquisition. Images were acquired with an 80 × 80 matrix and a field of view of 24.0 cm, resulting in a slice thickness of 3.0 mm across 48 interleaved slices. Participants were instructed to remain awake with eyes open while fixating on a central crosshair, total acquisition time is 5 min 7 s. High-resolution T1-weighted images were acquired using a three-dimensional inversion-recovery prepared fast spoiled gradient-echo (IR-SPGR) sequence with a TR of 6.8 ms, TE of 2.7 ms, and a flip angle of 12°. Images were acquired with a 256 × 256 matrix, a field of view of 25.6 cm, and 320 slices. The resulting voxel size was 1 mm³, with a slice thickness of 0.5 mm. Total acquisition time was 1 min 41 s.

A computerised Tower of London (TOL) task was used to measure planning and problem solving, by matching a configuration of beads placed upon three posts upon a certain number of moves.

rs-fMRI quality control, preprocessing and subject-level analysis were completed using standardised workflows leveraging MRIQC (v23.1.0), fmriprep (v23.1.3), and Python’s nilearn library (v0.10.0), respectively (Fig. 1). Group differences between MUD and controls were tested using FSL randomise. Additionally, FSL randomise was used to correlate years of use, the TOL, and FC metrics. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 (Family-Wise Error (FWE) corrected). Using linear regression models, group differences in the TOL performance was tested in this cohort. We also extended the analysis by examining associations between significant TOL metrics and network FC using general linear modelling.

Results

This study included 22 participants; 10 cases and 12 controls. Years of education were significantly lower in the case group (12.0 ± 1.9 years) compared with the control group (14.8 ± 2.6 years; p = 0.020). Among cases, the mean abstinence duration was 8.9 ± 10.8 days, and the mean duration of methamphetamine use was 15.2 ± 5.6 years. The prevalence of smoking was higher in the case group (p = 0.043).

Relative to controls, the MUD group showed lower functional connectivity involving the default mode network (DMN) and the left ventral attention network (L-V-Att). Within the DMN, two clusters survived correction, reflecting reduced intra-network coupling of core DMN hubs and reduced inter-network coupling with the occipito-temporal association cortex (cluster 1: FWE-p = 0.0006, Peak T = 6.327; cluster 2: FWE-p = 0.005, Peak T = 5.685) (Fig. 2). For the L-V-Att, three clusters indicated weaker coupling between the attention network and the occipito-temporal cortex, alongside reduced connectivity with midline cingulate hubs (cluster 1: FWE-p = 0.0106, Peak T = 6.762; cluster 2: FWE-p = 0.0118, Peak T = 5.997; cluster 3: FWE-p = 0.0214, Peak T = 6.336)  (Fig. 2). Overall, this pattern points to disrupted integration between visual-associative regions and midline control hubs across the DMN and L-V-Att networks.

Compared to the control group, the MUD group showed significantly longer execution times (p=0.01, Cohen’s d = -1.13, 95%CI [-2.09  -0.18]), a greater total number of attempts (p=0.02, Cohen’s d = 0.95, 95%CI [0.10  1.79]), and fewer correct solutions on the first attempt (p=0.01, cohens d = -0.96, 95%CI [-1.80  -0.11]). Correlation analyses revealed no significant associations between years of use, mean execution time, total attempts, or correct solutions on first moves and neural network FC.

Conclusion

Early abstinence in MUD was marked by weaker coupling within and between large-scale networks, with the default mode and ventral attention networks showing reduced integration with posterior visual-associative areas and midline control hubs. This pattern aligns with prior reports of DMN decoupling in early methamphetamine abstinence and the antagonistic dynamics typically observed between the default-mode and attention systems5. Notably, several regions, including the posterior temporal fusiform cortex, middle temporal gyrus, and cingulate cortex - contributed to significant clusters across both the default mode and left ventral attention networks.

The functional disruptions converged with structural compromise, where cortical thinning and reduced volume - derived from the same cohort analysed in our previous publication - observed in the posterior visual-associative cortex and frontal control regions.4 This structure-function convergence suggests that attenuated network integration may be partly constrained by regionally specific cortical vulnerability, rather than reflecting isolated or transient dysconnectivity.

Behaviourally, individuals with MUD showed slower and less efficient performance on the Tower of London task, consistent with persistent executive dysfunction during early abstinence. However, these executive measures, as well as years of methamphetamine use, did not show significant associations with network functional connectivity, suggesting that the observed network disruptions may reflect a more generalised systems-level vulnerability rather than a simple linear relationship with cumulative exposure or task performance.

Overall, these findings highlight that early abstinence may exhibit convergent structural and functional alterations across large-scale networks, rather than isolated regional dysfunction. Characterising these disruptions may help identify biomarkers of relapse vulnerability and guide the development of targeted interventions.
