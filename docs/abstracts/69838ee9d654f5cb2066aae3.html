Introduction

The neocortex of most mammalian species with a large brain develops cortical folds arranged in characteristic patterns, which present a remarkable relationship with its cytoarchitectonic, connective and functional organisation.

The strong relationship between cortical folding patterns and brain organisation across mammals remains an important puzzle for neuroscientists. Additionally, these folding patterns are remarkably stable within each species1. Together, these observations have led to the hypothesis of their genetic encoding. The mechanism could be the encoding of positional information through gene expression gradients (as in Wolpert’s French Flag problem2): a reduced number of morphogen gradients established early during development would provide a unique combination of concentrations for each position, which would then regulate their commitment to different fates. In particular, such positional information would regulate the processes leading to the formation of gyri and sulci3.

During the last years it has become increasingly clear that cells can read and interpret mechanical and geometric clues with a precision equivalent to molecular clues. Here we hypothesise that geometric clues can play a role equivalent to that of molecules, providing positional information that would determine the commitment of a neocortical region to become a gyrus or a sulcus, as well as to determine their orientation.

Methods

The early fetal brain, before the formation of any fissure of fold, has a characteristic shape. At every point of the surface, two principal curvature directions can be defined, indicating the directions of maximum and minimum curvature. The average of these two curvatures – the mean curvature – provides an extrinsic measurement of local curvature: a folded paper, for example, has non-zero average curvature. The multiplication of the principal curvatures – the Gaussian curvature – provides an intrinsic measurement of curvature, the type of curvature that cannot be flattened without cuts. A folded paper has null Gaussian curvature.

We have collected MRI data from developing ferret brains and created precise, manually-edited, surface reconstructions. Unlike humans, ferrets are born with very immature, unfolded brains. During the first postnatal weeks, their neocortices expand leading to the formation of a rich and stable folding pattern. By the end of the first month, the ferret brain reaches the volume of an adult ferret. This is also the moment when they open their eyes for the first time. From this perspective, the first postnatal month in ferret brain development recapitulates the last trimester of gestation in humans.

Taking advantage of the stability of the ferret folding pattern, we have assembled cross-sectional data to reconstruct developmental trajectories from birth (postnatal day 0: P0) to adulthood, including P0, P4, P8, P16 and P32. These trajectories allow us to project each point of the P0 neocortex into a corresponding point in the P32 neocortex.

First, we tested whether the curvature of the P0 ferret brain captures the curvature of the P32 brain. Next, we tested whether the principal curvature directions of the P0 ferret brain are predictive of those of the older stages.

Results

The variations in curvature of the unfolded brain of the ferret at P0 encoded the curvature of the folded brain at P32. We computed mean curvature maps (Fig. 1a). A linear regression showed that the P0 mean curvature values captured 51.7% of the variance in curvature of the P32 brain (Fig. 1d).

The curvature at P0 allowed us to predict if a region of the cortex will become a sulcus or a gyrus in the adult (prediction accuracy: 0.68). We thresholded the mean curvature map at P32, which partitioned the brain into sulcal and gyral regions (Fig. 1b left). We then mapped this partition into the P0 brain (Fig. 1b right). The mapping was created by chaining surface-based, distortion-minimising mappings from P32 to P16, P16 to P8, P8 to P4 and P4 to P0. We used a logistic regression to classify regions into sulci (0) or gyri (1) based on Gaussian and mean curvature at P0. The logistic regression prediction captured a statistically significant part of the sulcal/gyral fate at P32 (Fig. 1e).

In addition to the curvature and the sulcal/gyral fate, the orientation of the folds in the P32 brain were also encoded by the geometry of the P0. We computed the principal curvature directions over the surface (Fig. 1c). The 1st principal curvature direction is the direction of highest curvature at each point; the 2nd principal curvature direction is the direction of lowest curvature. In the P32 brain, the 2nd principal curvature direction runs along the main axis of each fold, whereas the 1st principal curvature direction cuts along them. In the P0 brain, in absence of folding, the 1st and 2nd principal curvature directions are simply related to the global shape of the brain. We compute the angle between the 1st and 2nd principal curvature directions in the P0, with the direction of the 2nd principal curvature of the P32 projected into the P0 brain using a distortion-minimising mapping. The direction of the 2nd principal curvature at P0 is well aligned with the 2nd principal curvature in the P32 (Fig. 1f).

Conclusion

Our analyses show that the geometry of the unfolded P0 ferret brain encodes key aspects of the adult folding pattern. This hints at an important role of mechanical forces in the definition of these patterns. From a mechanical perspective, the neocortex folds due to the growth of the grey matter on top of the elastic white matter substrate which grows much less. Initially, this growth is accommodated by compression. However, after a certain critical amount of growth, the unfolded configuration becomes unstable, and further growth leads to changes in shape (shear): the brain folds. The switch from the compression regime to the shear regime is strongly determined by cortical thickness: a thin cortex will be easier to fold than a thick cortex. The orientation of these folds is determined by the geometry of the unfolded brain: depending on the curvature of the neocortical surface, some regions may be easier to fold than some others4. In addition to the regulation through geometry, the wavelength of folding – critically imposed by cortical thickness – should strongly constrain the organisation of folding patterns. The major geometric and mechanical changes introduced by brain folding could then have an influence on various developmental aspects, and play an important causal role in brain organisation5.
