Introduction

Human–AI teaming is increasingly common in high-stakes domains such as medical diagnostics, disaster relief, and autonomous systems. Effective collaboration depends not only on the AI’s objective performance, but also on the human partner’s capacity to form and adapt trust. Trust here refers to a willingness to rely on another entity under uncertainty, accepting potential vulnerability to achieve shared goals (Simpson, 2007). A key determinant of trust is perceived reliability—how consistently the AI behaves as expected and how often it violates those expectations (Mayer et al., 1995).

EEG studies show that the human brain monitors AI behaviour using neural mechanisms similar to those engaged during social or supervisory interactions, with AI errors eliciting performance-monitoring signals such as the observational error-related negativity and error positivity. However, these findings largely come from tasks with clear ground truth and unambiguous outcomes. In real-world human–AI collaboration, feedback is often uncertain or unavailable, requiring trust to be constructed and updated through internal inference. The neural mechanisms supporting trust formation under these conditions remain poorly understood.

To address this gap, we examined the neural dynamics of trust formation during a cooperative human–AI task with ambiguous feedback. Participants judged whether face images were real or AI-generated while EEG was recorded, followed by an ostensibly independent AI classification that could disagree with the participant. Trust was tracked throughout the task using repeated ratings of perceived AI reliability. By time-locking EEG to both the participant’s response and the AI’s classification, we dissociated neural signals related to internal decision formation, agreement or disagreement with the AI, and changes in perceived trust over time.

Methods

Participants

Thirty-six healthy adults (18–34 years; mean age 26; 50% female) participated. Data from two participants were excluded (non-completion, non-compliance), leaving 34 participants for analysis. All had normal or corrected vision, no neurological or psychiatric conditions, and provided informed consent. The study was approved by the QUT Human Research Ethics Committee and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Experimental Task

Participants completed a face classification task in which they judged whether each face image was real or AI-generated. After each judgment, an ostensibly independent AI agent provided its own classification 300 ms later. Unknown to participants, the AI was simulated and pre-programmed to agree on 75% of trials and disagree on 25%. The task comprised 20 blocks of 28 trials (560 trials total), preceded by a practice block.

After each block, participants rated the perceived reliability of the AI on a continuous visual analogue scale (0–100%). This provided a block-wise behavioural proxy for trust. Participants were informed that the AI could be fallible, encouraging inference under uncertainty rather than error detection.

Stimuli and Procedure

Face stimuli (50% real, 50% synthetic) were drawn from a validated dataset (Nightingale & Farid, 2022), balanced for demographic diversity. Each trial began with fixation (600–1000 ms), followed by stimulus presentation until response (max 2 s). Human and AI classification icons were then displayed sequentially. Key mappings (real/fake) were counterbalanced across participants.

EEG Acquisition

EEG was recorded using a 64-channel actiCAP system (Brain Products), sampled at 500 Hz, referenced to FCz, and grounded at Fpz. Impedances were kept below ~10 kΩ. Triggers were sent from the PsychoPy stimulus PC via a TriggerBox to ensure precise event timing.

EEG Preprocessing

Data were preprocessed in EEGLAB using a subtraction-based pipeline inspired by PREP, including line-noise removal, downsampling to 250 Hz, automated and visually confirmed channel/segment rejection, ICA-based removal of eye-blink and residual noise components, Artifact Subspace Reconstruction (ASR) for transient artifacts, average re-referencing, and spherical interpolation of rejected channels.

Data Analysis

Behavioral measures included response bias, response time, and block-wise changes in AI reliability ratings. EEG analyses focused on event-related potentials (ERPs) time-locked to both the human response and AI classification. Condition-specific responses were estimated using single-subject GLMs implemented in LIMO EEG, with cluster-based correction for multiple comparisons. Additional analyses examined covariance between ERP amplitude and trust updates, as well as modulation by individual response bias.

Results

BEHAVIOURAL PERFORMANCE AND BIAS

Participants performed the face classification task at near-chance accuracy, consistent with the ambiguity of the stimuli. Most participants showed a systematic response bias toward judging faces as either real or fake. Importantly, response times were faster for each participant’s preferred judgment, indicating consistent task engagement and stable individual decision strategies rather than random responding.

NEURAL SIGNATURES OF HUMAN DECISION FORMATION (PRE-RESPONSE ACTIVITY)

Prior to the button press, EEG revealed a centro-parietal positivity (CPP) during image viewing, peaking approximately 500 ms before the response. This signal differentiated trials judged as fake versus real, with larger amplitudes for fake judgments. The timing and topography of this activity are consistent with internal evidence accumulation and commitment dynamics, reflecting the participant’s own decision formation independently of any AI feedback.

Crucially, this pre-response activity occurred well before the AI classification was shown and therefore cannot reflect evaluation of the AI. Instead, it indexes internally generated certainty or confidence associated with the participant’s judgment.

NEURAL PROCESSING OF AI AGREEMENT AND DISAGREEMENT

Following presentation of the AI classification, disagreement (mismatch) elicited a canonical N2–P3a–P3b ERP complex, consistent with expectancy violation and evaluative processing. The magnitude and timing of these responses depended on the participant’s prior judgment, indicating that internal decision context shapes how AI feedback is processed. Individual response bias further modulated these effects, with a dissociable frontal–parietal pattern across early and late stages of feedback processing.

NEURAL PROCESSES OF ASSESSING AND UPDATING AI RELIABILITY

The central finding concerns how perceived AI reliability (trust) evolved over time. Block-wise changes in reliability ratings covaried with EEG activity in a pre-feedback time window, overlapping with—but topographically distinct from—the CPP. Specifically, ERP amplitude between approximately –680 and –350 ms relative to AI feedback (i.e., during image viewing and decision formation) predicted subsequent increases or decreases in perceived AI reliability.

This effect showed a fronto-parietal distribution, suggesting engagement of both evidence accumulation and meta-cognitive evaluation systems. Notably, the modulation emerged before the AI’s response was known and was strongest on trials where the participant judged the image as real and the AI later agreed. This indicates that trust updates are not driven solely by feedback evaluation, but draw on internally generated decision certainty, which could retrospectively be integrated into beliefs about the AI’s reliability.

Conclusion

This study provides an EEG-based account of how trust in an AI partner emerges and evolves during ongoing interaction under uncertainty. Rather than being driven solely by the AI’s observable behaviour, trust was shaped by an interplay between internally generated decision signals, evaluative processing of agreement and disagreement, and stable individual biases. Together, these findings challenge views of trust as a purely reactive process and instead highlight its grounding in endogenous cognitive dynamics.

A central result is that neural activity preceding the participant’s response—specifically during the period of internal decision formation—was predictive of subsequent changes in perceived AI reliability. EEG amplitude in a pre-response window, overlapping with but not identical to the centro-parietal positivity (CPP), co-varied with block-wise trust updates. Importantly, this effect emerged before the AI’s classification was revealed, indicating that trust updating draws on internal decision certainty rather than exclusively on external feedback. This finding suggests that participants implicitly project their own confidence onto the AI and later integrate this internal signal when revising beliefs about the AI’s reliability.

Following the AI’s classification, disagreement elicited a sequence of event-related potentials consistent with expectancy violation and evaluative processing, including N2, P3a, and P3b components. These responses were not uniform but depended on the participant’s preceding judgment, indicating that feedback processing is conditioned by internal decision context. Individual response biases further modulated these neural responses in a topographically dissociable manner, revealing how stable internal strategies shape both early conflict detection and later belief-confirmation processes.

Taken together, the results support a layered framework of trust formation in human–AI interaction. Rapid feedback-locked responses reflect evaluation of agreement and violation; slower decision-related signals index internal evidence accumulation; and meta-cognitive processes integrate these internal states over time to update trust. Trust therefore emerges not as a simple response to AI accuracy, but as a dynamic construct grounded in internal certainty, expectation, and belief integration.
