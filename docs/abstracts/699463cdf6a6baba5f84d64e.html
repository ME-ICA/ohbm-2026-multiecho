Introduction

Dynamic Causal Modelling (DCM) is a Bayesian framework for inferring effective connectivity from neuroimaging data, enabling the study of directed interactions between brain regions.¹ Spectral DCM, the most widely applied variant for resting-state fMRI, fits a linear state-space model to the observed cross-spectral density, estimating parameters of a forward model that best reproduces cross-correlations at all time lags.² Despite its adoption in thousands of studies, spectral DCM has been constrained by computational complexity to networks of approximately 36 regions, with model inversion requiring over 24 hours per scan.³ A Julia implementation achieved 10-fold speedups on small networks,⁴ but nonlinear scaling of time and memory rendered larger networks infeasible. Regression DCM offers a faster alternative,⁵ but assumes a fixed hemodynamic response across the brain and forgoes the state-space formulation central to most DCM approaches. Here, we combine theoretical and computational innovations to invert large spectral DCM models while preserving region-specific hemodynamic response functions and the state-space formulation.

Methods

Using matrix calculus, we derive analytical gradients of the cross-spectral density (the forward function), replacing slower numerical approximations and improving both speed and accuracy. On the implementation side, we employ efficient vectorised code and execute the analytical gradient computations on the GPU. We validated this approach in simulations and compared performance and accuracy against the standard SPM implementation.

Results

Our method achieves speedups that become increasingly relevant for larger networks, reaching up to 1000-fold on simulated resting-state fMRI time series. For the first time, we enable spectral DCM inversion for over 70 regions of interest (ROIs), making whole-brain spectral DCM feasible. This allows the use of standard parcellations such as Desikan-Killiany (68 ROIs), which covers both cortical and subcortical structures.

Conclusion

Whole-brain spectral DCM unlocks more realistic comparisons with existing literature, particularly studies employing functional connectivity on whole-brain parcellations. Using standard atlases for DCM studies enables direct comparison of results across studies, which has been difficult because different investigations have used different ROIs. Beyond improving comparability and reproducibility, this approach stays true to the Bayesian spirit of DCM: posterior estimates from one study can serve as priors for future studies in similar populations, based on the same parcellation. This enables the community to iteratively build reference whole-brain effective connectivity maps for both healthy populations and clinical conditions. We have started applying this method to large open-access datasets to construct these directed connectivity maps and release them to the neuroimaging community.
