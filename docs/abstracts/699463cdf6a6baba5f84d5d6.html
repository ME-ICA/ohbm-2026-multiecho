Introduction

The hallmark features of Tourette syndrome (TS) are motor and vocal tics.1 However, evidence suggests that the premonitory urge (PU) preceding tics may play a more fundamental role in symptom expression and maintenance.2 Although ~90% of individuals with TS endorse experiencing these often intrusive sensory, cognitive, and affective experiences,3 their neural basis remains poorly defined.4 Existing measures of PU only capture restricted, sensory-focused elements of PU, limiting our capacity to characterise and understand its full heterogeneity and to determine its broader clinical relevance.5 The ‘Urge-to-tic: Examining the neural and experiential bases of Tourette syndrome’ (U-TIC TS), is an NHMRC-funded project examining PU in an Australian community-based sample of children with TS using a multimodal (neuroimaging/lived experience/genetics) interdisciplinary framework. By using structural MRI, real-time urge monitoring during task-based fMRI, and phenomenological interviews, the project aims to identify the neural substrates that underlie individual variability in the urge-to-tic experience. Here, we report structural neuroimaging findings from the current dataset, alongside an overview of the integrated imaging–experience framework now underway.

Methods

High-resolution structural and functional MRI data are acquired on a research-dedicated 3-Tesla Siemens Prisma scanner at the Royal Children’s Hospital, Melbourne, Australia. Each participant completed T1, diffusion-weighted, QSM, and three functional protocols: resting-state, a tic-suppression task (TS only), and a blink-suppression task (TS and healthy controls). Both tasks use a 60-second alternating block design (suppress vs free), during which participants provide continuous real-time urge intensity ratings via an MRI-compatible trackball. Tic occurrences were timestamped using a self-report response button box. A camera mounted on the head coil monitors blinks during the blink-suppression task. The broader U-TIC framework integrates these imaging measures with detailed experiential accounts obtained through semi-structured phenomenological interviews and behavioural assessments. 

For pilot structural analyses, T1-weighted images from all TS participants scanned to date were processed using the CAT12 toolbox for SPM12. Automated segmentation and spatial normalisation were followed by a parcellation region of interest analysis, using bilateral regions from the AAL3 atlas: insula, putamen, supplementary motor area, and anterior cingulate cortex. Partial correlations between regional volume and PU severity, measured by the premonitory urge for tics scale (PUTS),5 were examined, controlling for total intracranial volume (eTIV) and for multiple comparisons (p = .0063). 

Results

Our pilot structural analysis showed a strong trend relationship between lower left and right insula volume and PU severity in TS patients (n = 9) after controlling for eTIV (r = -.70, p = .053 and .-77 respectively, p = .026). Emerging task-based functional data identifying patterns across suppression compared to free tic acitivation will be reported, to uncover functional correlates of PU. 

Conclusion

The U-TIC TS study provides a novel multimodal approach to clarifying the neural and experiential architecture of PU in TS. Structural findings from the current dataset highlight the insula, which is the area involved in interoceptive awareness, as a promising region of interest. With lower bilateral volumes associated with heightened urge severity. By integrating structural and functional imaging with real-time urge monitoring and lived-experience data, this work advances a more comprehensive model of the mechanisms underpinning PU. The emerging patterns have meaningful implications for understanding symptom maintenance, identifying neural targets for intervention, and refining behavioural treatments such as comprehsive beahvioural intervention for tics.
