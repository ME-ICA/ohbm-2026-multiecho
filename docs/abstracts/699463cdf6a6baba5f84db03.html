Introduction

Curiosity, defined as the intrinsic motivation to seek and resolve information gaps, plays a pivotal role in enhancing learning and memory across various contexts. Behavioral studies have consistently demonstrated that high states of curiosity, elicited through trivia question paradigms, lead to superior recall of target information, such as trivia answers and incidental material1,2. For instance, Gruber et al.1 found that anticipatory activity in dopaminergic regions, such as the substantia nigra/ventral tegmental area (SN/VTA) and nucleus accumbens, during high-curiosity states predicts enhanced memory for both target and incidental content.

Beyond curiosity, post-answer interest—often measured as ratings after information revelation—further modulates memory outcomes. Fastrich et al.3 reported that post-answer interest mediates the effects of pre-answer curiosity on memory, while information prediction error (IPE), calculated as the difference between pre- and post-answer interest (post minus pre), independently boosts recall, akin to error-driven learning mechanisms.

Neuroscience evidence underscores these behavioral findings, with curiosity states activating reward-related brain regions like the caudate, hippocampus, and SN/VTA during question presentation1,2. Despite these advances, significant gaps persist in understanding the neural underpinnings of interest and IPE, particularly during answer presentation. Few studies have examined interest or IPE-related activations at resolution phases, where prediction errors could drive dopaminergic updates and memory consolidation. Behavioral work highlights IPE’s role3, but neuroimaging investigations are limited.

To address these gaps, the present fMRI study investigates the neural mechanisms by which state curiosity, interest, and IPE influence memory encoding and retrieval using a trivia question paradigm.

Methods

We scanned fifty-five healthy adults using fMRI. The experiment consisted of three phases. Before enterrring to the scanner, each participant underwent a physical examination to ensure suitability for fMRI measurement and completed written informed consent. All individuals were provided with a detailed introduction to the task logic, including the initial screening session and the subsequent study phase conducted inside the fMRI scanner. Importantly, participants were not informed that a surprise memory test would be administered at the end of the study. The primary focus of the present investigation was the neural activity during the answer onset phase (i.e., the moment when the correct trivia answer was revealed), as this represents the critical resolution stage where pre-existing curiosity is satisfied, post-answer interest is generated, and information prediction error (IPE) is computed.

The screening phase aimed to select enough questions for study phase. Participants rated their knowledge, question’s valence and curiosity about a series of trivia questions, . Knowledge ratings were on a scale from 1 to 4 (‘Do you know the answer?’; 1 = ‘no idea’ and 4 = ‘pretty sure’). Valence ratings were on a scale from 1 to 4 (‘How positive or negative do you think the question is?’; 1 = ‘very negative’ and 4 = ‘very positive’). Curiosity ratings were also on a scale from 1 to 4 (‘How curious are you about the answer?’; 1 = ‘not curious at all’ and 4 = ‘very curious’). Participants’ ratings were used to categorize trivia questions into participant-specific high- and low-curiosity conditions (36 questions each). The screening phase ended once a sufficient number of items were selected for each condition. 

In the study phase, participants were shown a subset of the trivia questions (previously rated during the screening phase) along with their answers inside the scanner. Between the presentation of each question and its answer, participants were asked to rate the pleasantness of a face image. IPE can be calculated by interest rating minus previous curiosity rating in screening phase for each question. Here, we testing the effects of curiosity, interest and IPE on the learning of trivia answers and incidental face images.

Out of scanner, participants were introduced to the procedure of the unexpected memory test. In the surprise test phase, participants were tested on their memory of both the faces and the trivia answers. In the face memory test, participants were shown 72 faces from the study phase and 36 new faces. They were asked to judge whether they had seen each face before (1 = ‘definitely not seen’ and 4 = ‘definitely seen’). Accuracy for the face memory test was calculated using hit rates minus false alarm rates for each condition. After the face memory test, participants completed a trivia question memory test, in which they were asked to recall the answers to the 72 questions from the study phase and write their answers.

FMRI Analysis

A general linear model (GLM) was implemented in SPM12. The GLM was modeled with four event types corresponding to the study phase: presentation of the trivia question (duration = 5 s), face stimulus (3 s), answer presentation (3 s), and the subsequent interest rating (4 s). Each event onset was modeled separately, with parametric modulators included to capture trial-by-trial variability. Specifically, the pre-experimental curiosity rating (obtained during the screening phase) was entered as a parametric modulator for both question and face events, while the interest rating (collected during the study phase) was used as a modulator for answer and interest-rating events. IPE effects were derived via contrasts: positive IPE (interest > curiosity) versus negative/null. ROI analyses targeted spheres (8 mm radius) from Gruber et al. (2014): SN/VTA (MNI: 3, -19, -17), nucleus accumbens (nACC; 9, 10, -6), hippocampus (-21, -18, -19). Small-volume correction (SVC) applied within ROIs (p < .05 FWE). 

Results

Behavioral Results

A two-way ANOVA on question recall rates with the within-subject factors curiosity (high, low) and interest (high, low) revealed a significant main effect of curiosity, F(1,55) = 19.16, p < .001, η² = .046, with better question memory performance in high curiosity state (M = 0.616) than in low state (M =0.525). Meanwhile, the interest main effect was also significant, F(1,55) = 98.23, p <. 001, η² = .250, revealing a strong interest-enchanced memory effect. The intereaction effect was not significant (p = .371).

Another two-way ANOVA on face recognition accuracy (Hit - False Alarm) revealed a significant main effect of curiosity, F(1, 55) = 7.98, p = .007, η² = .016, with significant higher memory performance in high curiosity state (M = 0.343) than in low curiosity state (M = 0.293), confirming the curiosity-induced spillover effect on incidental face memory (Gruber et al., 2014). Neither the main effect of interest (p = .188) nor the interaction between curiosity and interest level (p = .434) was significant.

Similar to reward prediction errors, IPE was computed as the difference between post-information interest ratings and pre-information curiosity ratings, categorized into negative (disappointment), neutral (expectation-congruent), and positive (surprise) levels. In a one-way ANOVA on question memory, we found significant main effect of IPE, F(1.80, 98.75) = 4.72, p = .014, η² = .023, with positive condition (M = 0.635) significant higher than negative (M = 0.570) and neutral condition (M = 0.587).

FMRI Results: Whole-Brain Analyses

No whole-brain activations survived correction for curiosity modulators at question or face onsets, nor for interest at the rating event. At answer onset, the interest-positive modulator revealed significant clusters (cluster-level pFWE <. 05), including inferior frontal gyrus (30, 28, -8; T = 5.19, pFWE-cluster = .000; k = 661), ventral striatum (8, 4, -4; T = 4.73, pFWE-cluster = .036; k = 170), and precentral gyrus (-36, -20, 42; T = 4.45, pFWE-cluster = .019; k = 200). For IPE-positive effect, small-volume correction was applied within three ROIs. Significant clusters emerged in nucleus accumbens (8, 6, -4; T = 3.64, pFWE-cluster = .026; k = 9), and SN/VTA (14, 8, -6; T = 3.22, pFWE-cluster = .044; k = 9).

FMRI Results: ROI Analyses

To further examine the neural substrates underlying the observed behavioral effects, we conducted ROI analyses focusing on the SN/VTA, nucleus accumbens (NAcc), and hippocampus. At answer onset, interest ratings were significantly associated with activity in the SN/VTA (t = 2.89, p = 0.0058) and the NAcc (t =3.78, p < .0001), but not in the hippocampus (p = 0.936).

For information prediction error (IPE), a similar pattern emerged. IPE positive effects approached significance in the SN/VTA (t = 1.89, p = .061), were robust in the NAcc (t = 3.22, p = .0017), but were not significant in the hippocampus (t = 0.13, p = .897).

Conclusion

This study clarify the neural and behavioral mechanisms underlying curiosity, post-answer interest, and information prediction error (IPE) in modulating memory during a trivia question paradigm. Behaviorally, high curiosity and interest independently enhanced recall of target trivia answers, with positive IPE further boosting performance relative to negative or neutral conditions, akin to reward prediction error mechanisms. Curiosity also spilled over to improve incidental face recognition, though interest and IPE did not significantly influence this. Neurally, during answer revelation, interest parametrically modulated activity in whole-brain regions including the inferior frontal gyrus and ventral striatum, and in a priori ROIs such as the substantia nigra/ventral tegmental area (SN/VTA) and nucleus accumbens (NAcc), but not the hippocampus. Positive IPE showed a similar, though less robust, pattern, with significant activations in the NAcc and SN/VTA under small-volume correction. These findings highlight the resolution phase as a critical window for dopaminergic reward processes in epistemic learning, extending anticipatory curiosity effects to post-information valuation.
