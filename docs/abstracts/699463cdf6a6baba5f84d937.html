Introduction

Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (TMS) targeting the primary motor cortex (M1) evokes motor responses in the corresponding muscles, typically recorded as Motor Evoked Potentials (MEPs) using surface electromyography (sEMG). The peak-to-peak amplitude of MEPs represents corticospinal excitability and reflects the synchronous recruitment of motor units.1 However, conventional bipolar sEMG lacks spatial resolution, offering limited insight into recruitment patterns or the distribution of muscle activation. The recorded MEPs are highly sensitive to electrode placement and cross-talk from neighboring co-activated motor units can significantly affect amplitude. Furthermore, factors such as TMS pulse width, stimulation intensity, induced current direction over the cortex and muscle state can generate distinct spatial activation patterns contributing to the high degree of variability observed in TMS-evoked responses. Finally, the spatial differences between TMS-evoked recruitment and that of voluntary contraction remain under-explored due to limited spatial information. High-Density sEMG (HDsEMG) overcomes these limitations by non-invasively capturing spatial activation maps,2 enabling a detailed investigation of corticospinal output variability. In this study, we utilized HDsEMG to characterize the spatial activation profile of TMS-evoked responses in the forearm extensor muscles. We systematically investigated the effects of varying TMS parameters and muscle states on spatial distribution and compared TMS-evoked muscle activity with physiological voluntary contraction.

Methods

We recruited sixteen healthy volunteers (10 females; mean age: 30.12 ± 4.72 years) with no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders. All participants provided written informed consent. We combined HDsEMG with neuronavigated multi-locus TMS (mTMS) to map motor activation profiles of the forearm extensors. Muscle activity was recorded using a 128-channel HDsEMG grid (16 × 8 array, 10 mm inter-electrode distance) positioned over the Extensor Carpi Radialis (ECR) and surrounding muscles. The grid was aligned in the proximal-distal and ulnar-radial directions with ground and reference electrodes placed over the adjacent ulnar bone. We used a neuronavigated mTMS system to electronically control pulse shape, intensity and current direction. We used monophasic trapezoidal pulses to target the ECR cortical hotspot guided by individual T1-weighted MRIs. Resting Motor Threshold (RMT) was defined as the minimum intensity evoking an MEP ≥ 50 µV in 5 out of 10 consecutive trials in the relaxed muscle. RMT was determined separately for each pulse width. The resting state protocol comprised blocks of 20 trials with randomized inter-trial intervals (2.5–3 s). We modulated pulse width (60 µs, 90 µs, 120 µs) and intensity (110%, 120%, 130% RMT) resulting in 9 blocks in randomized order. Two additional blocks assessed current direction (Posterior-Anterior vs. Anterior-Posterior) at a fixed 90 µs pulse width. Active Motor Threshold (AMT) was defined as the minimum intensity evoking an MEP ≥ twice the background EMG amplitude in 5 out of 10 trials. During active protocol blocks, participants maintained a constant isometric forearm extension at 10% Maximum Voluntary Contraction (MVC) with visual feedback. We delivered three blocks (20 trials each) at 110%, 120% and 130% AMT with a 90 µs pulse width, triggered upon reaching the target force. Baseline voluntary contractions without TMS were recorded at 10%, 50% and 100% MVC (20, 10 and 5 trials respectively) to compare physiological recruitment with TMS-evoked patterns. HDsEMG signals were pre-processed to generate activation maps using the median MEP amplitude (TMS trials) or root-mean-square amplitude (voluntary trials; 100 ms window). We calculated shifts in the Center of Gravity (COG) relative to the COG of the individual's 10% MVC map, which served as the reference (REF). Overall map similarity was quantified using the Earthmover's Distance (EMD) metric.3 We compared EMD scores between pooled TMS maps vs. REF and voluntary contraction (VC) vs. REF. We used unsupervised K-means clustering to identify Dominant Clusters (DCs) with the highest average activity. From these clusters, we extracted area, volume, peak spread, mean amplitude, peak amplitude position and weighted centroid positions. We performed one- or two-way Repeated Measures ANOVA for comparisons. Sphericity was tested using Mauchly’s test and Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied on p-value when sphericity was violated. For post hoc comparisons we used Student’s t-test and Wilcoxon signed-rank test for parametric and non-parametric data respectively.

Results

Quantitative analysis revealed that the spatial distribution of motor output is significantly modulated by specific stimulation parameters and muscle states. We observed that TMS parameters and MVC levels drive distinct shifts in muscle activity distribution by shifting the COG position. A two-way repeated measures ANOVA demonstrated a significant main effect of pulse width (F(2,14) = 9.86, p = 0.002) on the ulnar-radial COG shift relative to REF. The COG shift was not significant with increasing stimulation intensity, and no significant interaction between pulse width and intensity was observed (F(4,28) = 1.47, p = 0.24). The direction of induced current significantly influenced the COG shift (F(1,9) = 5.53, p = 0.043), as did the MVC level (F(2,20) = 4.74, p = 0.021). No significant shifts were observed in the proximal-distal direction, suggesting that spatial variability of the corticospinal projection to the forearm is primarily organized along the ulnar-radial axis. Regarding topological similarity, a Friedman test showed a significant difference in EMD scores across pooled comparison groups (REF vs. REF, REF vs. TMS, and REF vs. VC; χ2(2) = 9.75, p = 0.0076). Post-hoc Wilcoxon signed-rank tests demonstrated that dissimilarity between REF and TMS-evoked maps was significantly higher than dissimilarity between REF and VC maps (z = 2.38, p = 0.017). K-means segmentation revealed that while the overall area and peak spread of DCs remained stable, their ulnar-radial location and intensity were parameter-dependent. Peak position within the DC was sensitive to pulse width (F(2,14) = 6.24, p = 0.012), while the weighted centroid was not. Conversely, the weighted centroid was sensitive to pulse direction (F(1,9) = 5.53, p = 0.043) where peak position was not. Increasing MVC levels altered both weighted centroids and peak positions (F(2,20) = 4.74, p = 0.021; F(2,30) = 3.04, p = 0.087, GG corrected). Volume and mean amplitude of the DCs were highly sensitive to all tested conditions: pulse width, intensity, direction, muscle state and MVC levels.

Conclusion

Our findings provide strong evidence that TMS-evoked spatial activation patterns are distinct from those generated during physiological voluntary contraction. The significant difference in EMD scores between voluntary MC and TMS-evoked maps indicate that TMS-driven recruitment is not analogous to physiological activation. We identified that TMS-evoked spatial activity is dependent on pulse width and the direction of the induced electric field. The significant ulnar-radial shift in the locus of activity observed in both full-map COG and isolated DCs with varying pulse widths suggests that altering pulse duration recruits distinct populations of cortical neurons. This is likely driven by the strength-duration time constant properties of the targeted neural population leading to the activation of spatially distinct motor units.4 Similarly, changes in induced current direction activate different neuronal populations, which in turn modulate spatial output. In contrast, increasing stimulation intensity failed to induce significant spatial shifts in the COG or dominant cluster position. This suggests that while higher intensities recruit larger neuronal populations, the spatial recruitment pattern remains stable, primarily increasing only the volume and mean intensity of the activation map. This aligns with the theory that surrounding neurons are recruited progressively as intensity increases due to loss of focality, but the targeted neurons continue to contribute majorly to the activation locus.5 These parameter-specific spatial shifts expose a critical limitation in conventional TMS-EMG studies as standard bipolar recordings are blind to such topological changes. Our findings highlight the advantage of HDsEMG for measuring TMS-evoked muscle activity. Future protocols could use baseline HDsEMG mapping to personalize electrode positions guided by individual-specific activation topology. Such spatial optimization would minimize intra-individual variability, enhance the reliability of TMS biomarkers and provide deeper insights into the distinct corticospinal pathways accessed by different neuromodulatory interventions.
