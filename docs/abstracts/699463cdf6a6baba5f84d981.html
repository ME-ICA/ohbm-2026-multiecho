Introduction

Aperiodic (1/f) EEG activity has recently been proposed as a marker of neural noise, cortical excitation:inhibition (E:I) balance, and cognitive ability. However, most studies rely on static resting-state estimates or task-averaged measures, leaving open whether spectral slope – the primary index of aperiodic activity – reflects a stable, global neural regime or dynamic control processes engaged during ongoing cognition. Importantly, different cognitive domains may rely on distinct mechanisms of aperiodic modulation1. Here, we test whether trial-level dynamics of spectral slope during reasoning performance predict behavior, and whether resting-state and task-evoked spectral slopes differentially relate to cognition, addressing trait- vs. state-based interpretations of aperiodic activity. Using time-resolved slope estimates and mixed-effects modeling, we dissociate tonic regime shifts from dynamic, state-dependent slope fluctuations and examine their associations with latent cognitive abilities.

Methods

EEG was recorded from 64 BioSemi electrodes at a sampling rate of 256 Hz during three eyes-open resting-state conditions and a Raven-type reasoning task (36 matrices presented in randomized order) in 156 healthy young adults (Mage= 23.0 years, SD = 3.8; 86 females). After preprocessing, including band-pass filtering (0.05–40 Hz) and artifact rejection, power spectra were computed using Welch’s method from 2.5-s nonoverlapping windows. These windows spanned either the resting-state recordings (360 s total) or the fixed interval between Raven matrix appearance and the response bank (36 trials × 30 s each), with the first 2.5 s after Raven matrix onset excluded to avoid potential event-related potential confounds. Spectral slope was estimated over the 2–33 Hz frequency range, excluding the alpha band (7–14 Hz), from Welch power spectra (50% overlap) in log space and averaged across 12 fronto-central electrodes; this approach was used because it yielded the most reliable aperiodic estimates in our previous work2. The analyses were replicated using a broader frequency range (1–35 Hz) and the fooof/specparam algorithm3, a commonly used tool for separating periodic and aperiodic components, with peak width limits set to 2–12 Hz and a maximum of two periodic peaks.

For Raven trials, linear mixed-effects models were first fitted to slope values to characterize task-related aperiodic dynamics as a function of task difficulty (Trial Type: Easy [Raven’s Advanced Progressive Matrices, items 1–18] vs. Difficult [items 19–36]), time within processing epochs (Time Window), and epoch number (Epoch Number). Slope was then decomposed via subject-mean centering into between-subject (subject-level means) and within-subject (trial-level) components (hereafter Between- and Within-Subject Slope). Generalized linear mixed-effects models predicted trial accuracy from task difficulty, time within processing epochs, epoch number, and both Between- and Within-Subject Slope components.

Subject-level EEG indices were derived to capture multiple aspects of aperiodic dynamics: (i) within-trial change, indexing the rate of change in slope over time within Raven task epochs; (ii) task-level change, indexing slower changes in slope across successive Raven task epochs; (iii) state-dependent coupling, defined as the strength with which momentary within-subject deviations in slope (Within-Subject Slope) predicted trial accuracy; and (iv) a task-evoked shift, quantified as the deviation of mean Raven-task slope from resting-state slope. These EEG indices were correlated with latent cognitive factors derived from confirmatory factor analysis of reasoning (R; 6 tasks), working memory (WM; 5 tasks), processing speed (PS; 3 tasks), inspection time (IT; 3 tasks), and perceptual discrimination (PD; 3 tasks). For all cognitive factors, higher values indicated better performance.

Results

Spectral slope dynamics during reasoning performance. Linear mixed-effects models of spectral slope (restricted to correct trials) revealed robust within-trial neural dynamics. Across conditions, slope systematically became less negative over time within epochs (Time Window effect: β = 0.012, p < .001), indicating a progressive flattening of the aperiodic spectrum as processing unfolded. In addition, slope differed by task difficulty (Trial Type effect: β = −0.010, p < .001), with steeper slopes observed in Difficult relative to Easy trials. Importantly, task difficulty modulated the temporal evolution of slope: although slope flattened over time in both conditions, this flattening was attenuated in Difficult compared to Easy trials, as reflected by a negative Trial Type × Time Window interaction (β = −0.0036, p < .01). Thus, under higher demands, the rate of within-trial slope flattening was reduced. In contrast, epoch-wise changes in slope across the task were weak and did not reach significance (p = .07), suggesting that, at the group level, the observed effects primarily reflect within-trial dynamics rather than gradual changes over the course of the task. When resting-state slope was included in the model, the pattern of task-related aperiodic dynamics remained largely unchanged.

Spectral slope and trial accuracy in the reasoning task. Generalized linear mixed-effects models predicting trial accuracy revealed a strong effect of task difficulty, with lower accuracy on Difficult relative to Easy trials (Trial Type effect: β = −1.40, p < .001). Beyond this dominant effect, momentary within-subject deviations in spectral slope significantly predicted performance: when slope was transiently less negative than an individual’s own mean, the probability of a correct response increased (Within-Subject Slope effect: β = 0.043, p < .001). This state–performance coupling strengthened over time within processing epochs (Within-Participant Slope × Time Window: β = 0.026, p < .05), indicating that momentary neural state became increasingly relevant as processing unfolded. In addition, this coupling was further modulated at longer task timescales, as reflected by a significant Epoch Number × Trial Type × Within-Participant Slope interaction (β = 0.039, p < .01), suggesting that the relationship between momentary slope fluctuations and accuracy differed across epochs and task difficulty, with stronger coupling emerging in Difficult trials at later stages of the task. In contrast, Between-Subject differences in average task-related slope did not predict overall accuracy (p = .30). However, Between-Subject Slope significantly modulated sensitivity to task difficulty (Trial Type × Between-Subject Slope interaction: β = −0.073, p < .001), such that individuals with steeper average slopes showed lower accuracy on Difficult relative to Easy trials. Including resting-state slope in the accuracy models did not alter the magnitude or significance of any of these effects.

Interindividual differences in aperiodic dynamics and cognition. At the individual-differences level, task-level changes in spectral slope showed broad associations with cognition, correlating with WM (r = .18), PD (r = .23), IT (r = .19), and PS (r = .23; all ps < .05), but not R (p > .05). In contrast, individual differences in state–performance coupling (indexing how strongly momentary slope fluctuations predicted trial accuracy) were selectively related to R (r = .30, p < .05) and WM (r = .20, p < .05). Finally, the magnitude of task-evoked deviation from resting-state slope (task-evoked shift) correlated with IT (r = .18, p < .05) and PS (r = .25, p < .05), but not with R or WM (ps > .05), indicating a tonic mechanism linked to perceptual efficiency and processing speed rather than complex cognition. No other slope-derived metrics (e.g., average Raven slope or average resting-state slope) showed significant associations with cognitive abilities.

Conclusion

Across three complementary analyses, the present findings demonstrate that aperiodic EEG slope during reasoning reflects multiple dissociable mechanisms operating at different timescales rather than a single static neural property. Models fitted to slope values showed robust within-trial dynamics: spectral slope progressively flattened as reasoning unfolded, with this flattening attenuated under higher task demands, indicating demand-dependent constraints on dynamic neural adjustment.

Linking these dynamics to behavior revealed that momentary, within-subject deviations in slope were behaviorally relevant. Transient shifts toward flatter slopes increased the probability of a correct response, and this state–performance coupling strengthened over time within trials and across epochs, particularly for difficult trials. In contrast, between-subject differences in average slope did not predict overall accuracy, but instead modulated sensitivity to task difficulty, suggesting a tonic influence on performance vulnerability rather than reasoning success per se.

At the individual-differences level, a clear dissociation emerged across cognitive domains. Slow, epoch-wise changes in aperiodic slope during task performance showed broad associations with working memory, perceptual discrimination, inspection time, and processing speed, suggesting a relatively domain-general mechanism related to sustained engagement, vigilance, or gradual task adaptation4. In contrast, individual differences in state–performance coupling—capturing how strongly momentary within-subject slope fluctuations predicted trial accuracy—were selectively related to higher-order cognition, correlating with reasoning and working memory but not with perceptual or speed-based measures. Finally, tonic task-evoked deviations of aperiodic slope from resting state were associated with inspection time and processing speed, but not with reasoning, indicating a distinct mechanism linked to perceptual efficiency and processing speed rather than complex cognitive integration. Together, these findings challenge unitary interpretations of spectral slope as a static neural noise marker and highlight the importance of time-resolved, within-subject approaches for distinguishing state-dependent and tonic contributions of aperiodic EEG activity to cognition.
