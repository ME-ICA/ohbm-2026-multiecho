Introduction

Bipolar Disorder (BD) and Unipolar Depression (UD) rank among the most debilitating mental disorders worldwide, with the significant challenge of their clinical differentiation representing a core dilemma in contemporary psychiatric practice. A particularly critical issue is that BD patients most frequently present with depressive episodes at initial diagnosis, and the core symptom clusters during these episodes substantially overlap with those of UD[1]. It is estimated that up to 10% of BD patients are misdiagnosed with UD for extended periods, with an average diagnostic delay of 5-8 years. This misdiagnosis directly leads to severe treatment deviations. Structural MRI studies suggest that BD is associated with widespread cortical thinning and reduced gray matter density (GMD), whereas alterations in UD are more localized, though overlap exists in certain brain regions[2,3]. While classification models based on cortical thickness (CT) or GMD alone have shown some discriminatory potential, the integration of both features within a multi-model machine learning framework to distinguish BD depression from UD remains unexplored.

Overall, while CT and GMD alterations in BD and UD have been separately reported, the effectiveness of integrating these two metrics to construct diverse machine learning models for classifying BD depression and UD remains unknown. Therefore, based on the exploration of CT and GMD alterations in BD depression and UD, this study employed multiple machine learning algorithms to evaluate their performance in distinguishing BD depression from UD, aiming to explore their potential as objective neuroimaging biomarkers while also comparing the classification efficacy across different models.

Methods

Participants

A total of 203 participants were enrolled, including 63 patients with BD depression, 70 patients with UD, and 70 age‑ and sex‑matched healthy controls (HCs). Recruitment was conducted through Shandong Daizhuang Psychiatric Hospital, all participants provided written informed consent, and the study was approved by the local ethics committee.

MRI Acquisition and Preprocessing

T1‑weighted structural images were acquired on a 3.0 T Siemens Lumina scanner. CT was measured using FreeSurfer (v7.3.2) across 68 DK atlas‑based regions. Brain volumetric analysis was derived using the standardized recon-all pipeline, encompassing key stages such as motion correction, intensity normalization, automated Talairach transformation, tessellation of gray and white matter boundaries, motion artifact removal, and topological defect correction. GMD was assessed using VBM as implemented in the Computational Anatomy Toolbox (CAT12) within SPM12 to obtain GMD maps. Following whole-brain VBM analysis of GMD, five regions of interest (ROIs) showing statistically significant between-group differences (FDR-corrected p < 0.05) were extracted for further analysis.

Machine Learning Classification

Machine learning technology is widely utilized for its robust modeling capabilities. All analyses were implemented in Python (v3.7.8) with the scikit-learn package (v0.24.1). We developed and compared six machine learning classifiers: k-Nearest Neighbors (kNN), Logistic Regression (LR), Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), Naïve Bayes (NB), Random Forest (RF), and Support Vector Machine (SVM). As shown in Fig.1, we conducted a supervised classification task with a structured workflow: (1) Feature construction: Based on the statistical analyses of CT and GMD, 23 brain regions exhibiting significant differences were identified and constituted the initial feature set. (2) Feature preprocessing and selection: Firstly, CT and GMD features were standardized to ensure uniformity in scale and distribution. Subsequently, to eliminate redundant features, and achieve optimal prediction performance, dimensionality reduction was performed using the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO). This method not only selected the most discriminative features for the six classifiers but also assigned relative weights to these features based on their linear coefficients. This process retained informative features and optimized predictive performance. (3) Model evaluation: The reduced feature set was randomly split into training and testing subsets to ensure external validity. While a 10-fold cross-validation procedure was implemented on the training set to optimize model stability and prevent overfitting. (4) Feature importance assessment: The contribution of each input feature to classifier performance was quantified by computing permutation-based feature importance.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in SPSS 25.0. Categorical variables were compared with the Pearson’s chi‑square test; continuous variables were assessed using ANCOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test depending on distributional assumptions. Post‑hoc Two Sample T tests were applied for brain regions showing significant group differences. Partial correlations between CT/GMD and clinical scores were examined within patient groups. All statistical analyses were controlled for gender, age, years of education, and total intracranial volume (TIV), with statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 after false discovery rate (FDR) correction.

Results

Clinical and Neuroimaging Findings

No significant demographic differences were observed among BD depression, UD, and HCs groups, though patient groups scored significantly higher scores on HAMD and HAMA scales (all p<0.001). CT analysis revealed widespread cortical thinning in BD depression compared to both HCs and UD, particularly in temporal (e.g., bilateral inferior/middle/superior temporal gyri) and frontal (e.g., orbitofrontal, pars triangularis) regions, whereas UD did not differ from HCs. GMD analysis identified five regions with significant group differences (left parahippocampal gyrus (PHG.L), left superior temporal pole (STP.L), left rectal gyrus (REC.L), right insula (INS.R), right precentral gyrus (PC.R)), showing more extensive reductions in BD depression.

Partial correlation analysis

Significant clinical-imaging correlations were found in both patient groups. In UD, HAMA scores negatively correlated with CT in the left pars orbitalis and right medial orbitofrontal cortex, and HAMD scores correlated negatively with CT in the right medial orbitofrontal cortex. In BD depression, the number of episodes was negatively associated with GMD in the right insula.

Feature Selection and Machine Learning Classification

Lasso regression on 23 initial features yielded 7 discriminative CT features (Set A, Fig.2). Permutation based importance assessment further refined this set to 4 core CT features (Set B, Fig.2): left fusiform gyrus (L_fusiform), left pars triangularis (L_parstriangularis), right pars triangularis (R_parstriangularis), and right superior temporal gyrus (R_superiortemporal).

Using the refined 4-feature subset, we retrained and evaluated all six classifiers. The classification performance of most algorithms showed notable improvement compared to the results obtained with the initial 7-feature set, confirming the effectiveness of removing redundant and non-contributory features through the two-stage selection pipeline. As shown in Fig.2, the feature set B led to improved accuracy in five of the six classifiers. MLP and NB achieved the highest accuracies (87.9% and 84.6%, respectively), with MLP also showing balanced sensitivity (83.3%), specificity (87.5%), and AUC (0.85). RF attained 81.8% accuracy and the highest specificity (92.8%). SVM, which completely failed on Set A (sensitivity=100%, specificity=0%), performed robustly on Set B (accuracy=80.5%, sensitivity=77.8%, specificity=100%, AUC=0.89). Feature weight analysis highlighted CT of the L_parstriangularis as the most influential feature across all classifiers, consistently exhibiting the highest weight values (range 0.119–0.259).

Summary of Key Classification Outcomes

Through systematic feature selection, we identified an optimal set of 4 CT features, L_fusiform, L_parstriangularis, R_parstriangularis, and R_superiortemporal, for distinguishing BD depression from UD. Among the 6 classifiers evaluated, MLP and NB delivered the highest and most stable performance. Notably, the L_parstriangularis emerged as the most consistently influential feature across all models. Furthermore, the marked improvement in SVM’s classification efficacy upon transitioning from feature Set A to the refined Set B underscores the critical impact of feature selection on model suitability and highlights that classifier performance is strongly dependent on feature quality rather than any universally optimal algorithm.

Conclusion

Cortical Thinning as a Discriminative Marker of BD Depression

The study identified a distinct pattern of widespread cortical thinning in BD depression patients, particularly affecting frontal, temporal and parietal regions involved in emotion regulation, executive control, and socio-cognitive processing functions consistently implicated in BD pathophysiology. In contrast, UD patients did not show significant cortical thinning relative to HCs. This supports the view that widespread cortical atrophy is more characteristic of BD than UD, even during depressive episodes[4]. Moreover, in UD, the observed cortical thinning correlated with higher symptom severity, suggesting a state-dependent relationship.

Spatially Distinct GMD Alterations in BD Depression versus UD

GMD analysis revealed distinct spatial alterations between BD depression and UD, with prominent group differences in the left parahippocampal gyrus, left superior temporal pole, left rectal gyrus, right insula and right precentral gyrus. BD depression showed more extensive GMD reductions across these regions, possibly reflecting the cumulative neurobiological impact of recurrent mood episodes, while UD alterations were more circumscribed, consistent with a divergent neuroanatomical substrate between the two disorders.

sMRI effectively distinguished BD depression and UD

A two-stage feature-selection pipeline refined 7 initial structural features to a concise 4-feature subset comprising CT in the L_fusiform, L_parstriangularis, R_parstriangularis, and R_superiortemporal. This subset not only captured key neuroanatomical distinctions but also significantly improved classifier performance. Among 6 algorithms, MLP and NB achieved the highest and most stable discrimination (accuracies up to 87.9%). Notably, SVM, which failed completely on the initial feature set, showed robust performance on the refined set, underscoring that model efficacy depends critically on feature quality rather than any universally optimal algorithm[5].

The L_parstriangularis emerged as the most influential feature across all models, supporting its central role in the neuroanatomical divergence between BD depression and UD. Methodologically, the study demonstrates the value of combining embedded selection (LASSO) with permutation-based importance assessment to eliminate non-contributory features and enhance both model performance and interpretability.

Limitations

The study is limited by its moderate sample size and the lack of control for medication effects, which may influence structural measures. Future studies with larger, medication-naïve cohorts and longitudinal designs are needed to validate the generalizability and pathological specificity of the identified features.

Conclusion

This study employed a machine learning framework to integrate CT and GMD features for distinguishing BD depression from UD. Notably, following feature selection and dimensionality reduction, frontotemporal CT features not only demonstrated stronger discriminative power than GMD features but were also consistently assigned higher contribution weights in the integrated classification models, highlighting their greater diagnostic value in differentiating bipolar from unipolar depression. The most discriminative features were concentrated in frontal and temporal cortical regions, indicating that abnormalities in these cortico-limbic circuits are central to the neuropathological divergence between BD depression and UD. Future studies may further validate the clinical applicability of frontal-temporal cortical measures to support differential diagnosis in clinical practice.
