Introduction

Adolescence is a pivotal neurodevelopmental period marked by substantial cortical reorganization and heightened plasticity, during which emotional and behavioral difficulties often emerge and precede psychiatric disorders1. This vulnerability reflects biological maturation, lifestyle factors, early adversity, and broader environmental exposures1. Although brain maturation has been linked to psychopathology, findings remain inconsistent: the ABCD study reports associations between smaller cortical metrics or negative age-expected deviations and higher symptoms2, whereas other studies found no significant relationships3. These discrepancies likely reflect reliance on cross-sectional or single time-point measures that fail to capture within-individual developmental change, masking meaningful variation in the timing and pace of maturation that contributes to mental-health vulnerability. Normative modeling offers a framework for identifying individual deviations from expected neurodevelopmental trajectories by comparing brain measures to population norms3. However, most applications are cross-sectional and cannot characterize developmental change3. The recently introduced z-difference metric addresses this limitation by estimating person-specific rates of change relative to normative trajectories4, yet it has been used only once in psychosis risk and never in adolescence or in relation to multidimensional psychopathology or ecological risk. Here, we apply a longitudinal normative modeling framework to quantify deviations in the pace of cortical and subcortical maturation during adolescence and test whether ecological risk factors predict atypical development. We hypothesized that greater risk exposure would be associated with accelerated brain volume reduction, that these deviations would prospectively predict mental health symptoms beyond cross-sectional metrics, and that neurodevelopmental pace would mediate the association between risk exposure and psychopathology. 

Methods

Structural MRI data were obtained from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study (Release 6.0), a harmonized multi-site cohort spanning ages ~9–18 years. Regional cortical and subcortical volumes were used from the ABCD study. Scans with motion artifacts, segmentation errors, or missing metadata were excluded using ABCD quality-control criteria5. Participants were divided into three subsets. Individuals with ≥1 psychiatric diagnosis were classified as atypically developing (ATD; N = 5,252; 32% female). Typically developing (TD) participants were split into (i) a held-out evaluation set (20% of TD; N = 6,511; 50% female), stratified by age, sex, site, and race/ethnicity, and (ii) a normative modelling pool (N = 17,749). One scan per participant was randomly selected for training (N = 6,481; 51% female) to ensure independence. As a sensitivity test, analyses were repeated using alternative timepoints to confirm that results were not dependent on the specific training scan. Normative models were constructed using Bayesian linear regression (BLR) implemented in PCNToolkit. Separate models were trained for each brain region, including age and sex as covariates and scanning site as a batch effect. Model performance was evaluated using explained variance (EXPV), mean standardized log-loss (MSLL), and the correlation (Rho) between predicted and observed values. For each participant and visit, region-wise deviation scores (z-scores) were estimated from the posterior predictive distribution, yielding individualized deviation from age-specific expectations. To quantify within-person developmental change, we used the z-difference (z-diff) framework, which computes the standardized difference between two posterior predictive distributions from the same participant. Unlike subtracting z-scores directly, z-diff incorporates predictive uncertainty, covariance structure, and measurement noise, providing an unbiased estimate of maturation pace. Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied separately to regional z-scores and z-diff scores. The first component of the z-scores was defined as brain status (cross-sectional deviation from normative structure), and the first component of the z-diff scores was defined as brain pace (rate of deviation relative to normative maturation).

 To examine biological relevance, PCA loadings were correlated with cortical gene-expression gradients derived from the ABAGEN framework in neuromaps. Spatial correlations were evaluated against 10,000 BrainSMASH null maps to control for spatial autocorrelation. To investigate the unique relationships between brain status, developmental pace, and psychopathology, we constructed a two-dimensional neurodevelopmental space with brain pace on the x-axis and brain status on the y-axis. Lower (negative) values indicate reduced developmental pace (faster decline) and reduced brain status (less mature brain structure). Individuals were assigned to four quadrants representing distinct neurodevelopmental profiles. Psychopathology scores (total, internalizing, externalizing) were compared across quadrants using Mann–Whitney U tests validated with 10,000 permutations (FDR-corrected p < 0.05). To determine whether brain pace confers vulnerability beyond static brain structure, two parallel association models were performed using 38 environmental/biological risk factors and 25 psychological measures. Models were adjusted for age, sex, baseline symptoms, and brain status (when predicting with brain pace). Finally, mediation analyses were performed to test whether brain pace mediated associations between risk factors and total mental health problems, using 10,000 permutation tests with false-discovery-rate correction (FDR < 0.05).

Results

The normative models demonstrated robust prediction accuracy across both cortical and subcortical regions. Highest explained variance and correlations were observed in association cortices, including frontal, temporal, and parietal regions, which undergo protracted maturation during adolescence. Limbic and deep gray matter structures such as the amygdala, hippocampus, and striatum also showed reliable performance, indicating that both static and dynamic developmental markers can be modelled across the whole brain (Figure 2, Panel A). To differentiate structural deviation from developmental timing, we compared inter-regional relationships in z-scores (static deviation) versus z-diff scores (developmental pace). The z-diff correlation matrix revealed markedly lower inter-regional similarity, indicating heterogeneous maturation rates across brain regions. In contrast, z-scores showed high global correlation, suggesting that structural deviation is more coordinated across the brain (Figure 2, Panel B). PCA reduced these measures into two principal neurodevelopmental indices: brain status, reflecting overall structural deviation, and brain pace, representing individual differences in maturation rate. Although the two indices showed moderate spatial similarity (r = 0.51, p < 0.01), they retained partially distinct spatial organization, indicating separable biological mechanisms (Figure 2, Panel B). To examine molecular relevance, we correlated brain status and brain pace maps with cortical gene-expression gradients derived from the first principal component of the Abagen atlas. Brain pace showed a strong alignment with gene expression (r = 0.47, p < 0.01), particularly in association cortices enriched for genes involved in synaptic remodeling, metabolic demand, and neuroplasticity. Brain status also correlated with gene expression (r = 0.38, p < 0.01), but the stronger alignment of pace suggests that dynamic maturation captures molecular hierarchy more faithfully than static morphology (Figure 2, Panel C).

We next projected each participant into a two-dimensional neurodevelopmental space defined by brain pace (x-axis) and brain status (y-axis). Adolescents showing both faster-than-expected decline and lower structural status (negative–negative quadrant) exhibited significantly higher total psychopathology and elevated internalizing and externalizing symptoms (FDR-corrected p < 0.05). This pattern suggests that accelerated maturation combined with low structural status represents a vulnerability phenotype for emerging mental health difficulties. Risk-factor analyses revealed distinct etiological profiles for brain status and pace. Brain status was more strongly associated with early-life experience including birth characteristics, parental age, prenatal tobacco exposure, breastfeeding duration, and household income, parental education, and sleep disturbance (0.05 < r < 0.20, p < 0.01; Figure 2, Panel D). In contrast, brain pace showed stronger associations with lifestyle and behavioural variables. Faster decline was linked to greater screen time, higher BMI and waist circumference, and impaired sleep initiation and maintenance (0.05 < r < 0.20, p < 0.01; Figure , Panel E). Brain pace was also robustly associated with anxiety/depression, somatic complaints, rule-breaking, and total CBCL psychopathology. Finally, mediation analyses showed that brain pace partially mediated the effects of lifestyle and environmental risks on mental health. Specifically, brain pace mediated associations between elevated screen time (r = 0.16, p < 0.01), sleep initiation problems (r = 0.17, p < 0.01), higher BMI (r = 0.14, p < 0.01), and lower household income (r = 0.12, p < 0.01) with increased psychopathology (Figure 2, Panel F). These findings indicate that dynamic brain maturation serves as a mechanistic pathway linking environmental risk to emerging mental health problems during adolescence.

Conclusion

This study introduces a large-scale longitudinal normative modeling framework designed to quantify individualized deviations in adolescent brain maturation. By separating cross-sectional status from the rate of deviation over time, our approach distinguishes how atypical a brain is (brain status) from how atypically it is changing (brain pace). This distinction overcomes the fundamental limitation of cross-sectional and single timepoint methods, which cannot capture developmental pace or its clinical importance. We found that brain status and brain pace reflect complementary neurodevelopmental features. Brain status showed strong inter-regional collinearity, consistent with previous findings that structural differences tend to covary across cortical hierarchies. In contrast, brain pace exhibited weaker collinearity, revealing more heterogeneous regional maturation rates. This suggests that longitudinal divergence is not simply an extension of static deviation, but a distinct marker of developmental plasticity and vulnerability. Supporting a biological basis for these findings, cortical pace patterns correlated with transcriptional gradients from the Allen Human Brain Atlas. Regions with prolonged volumetric change showed enrichment for genes related to metabolic demand, circuit refinement, and neuroplasticity processes known to peak during adolescence. This molecular alignment suggests that brain pace captures developmental processes embedded in cortical hierarchy and gene expression architecture, providing biological validation rarely demonstrated in normative modeling. 

Quadrant-based classification revealed that adolescents characterized by both reduced brain status and reduced developmental pace, reflecting mature brain structure alongside accelerated volumetric decline, exhibited the highest symptom burden across internalizing, externalizing, and general psychopathology domains. This pattern suggests that the joint presence of atypical brain status and accelerated developmental trajectories may confer heightened vulnerability to broad mental health difficulties during adolescence. To further disentangle the unique contributions of brain status and developmental pace, we conducted association analyses that explicitly isolated the role of pace. These analyses demonstrated that brain developmental pace uniquely predicted later mental health outcomes even after accounting for baseline symptom severity and static measures of brain structure. In contrast, brain status within the same analytical framework showed greater sensitivity to concurrent or historical factors but was less predictive of future psychopathology. This dissociation underscores the particular importance of developmental pace, rather than static brain status, as a prognostic biomarker for future mental health risk during adolescence. Importantly, brain developmental pace and brain status showed distinct associations with environmental exposures. Brain pace was more strongly associated with lifestyle and socioeconomic factors, whereas brain status was more sensitive to early-life exposures, including prenatal and perinatal conditions as well as certain lifestyle factors. These findings suggest that developmental pace may be especially responsive to ongoing, potentially modifiable exposures during adolescence, whereas brain status may more strongly reflect earlier developmental constraints. Finally, mediation analyses provided further mechanistic insight by showing that lifestyle factors such as screen time, sleep patterns, and body mass index were associated with increased psychopathology indirectly through accelerated reductions in brain volume. This pathway supports a developmental mechanism whereby modifiable lifestyle factors influence mental health risk via their impact on the pace of brain maturation, rather than solely through static structural differences.
