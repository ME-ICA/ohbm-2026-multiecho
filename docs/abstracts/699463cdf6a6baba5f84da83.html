Introduction

The integration of structural and functional connectivity measures offers a comprehensive understanding of how the brain supports communication across distributed neural systems. Structural connectivity (SC), derived from white matter tracts, reflects the anatomical framework enabling interregional communication, whereas functional connectivity (FC) captures the temporal synchronization of neural activity across those regions. While these two dimensions are closely related, the strength and variability of their relationship remain areas of active research – particularly in conditions where structural integrity is compromised by neurological disease.

Multiple sclerosis (MS), is characterized by demyelination and axonal loss which disrupts both SC and FC, leading to network inefficiencies and altered information flow. Such disruptions may weaken or reorganize the coupling between structural and functional pathways, a process thought to underlie cognitive and behavioral changes in MS. Understanding how these relationships differ between healthy controls (HC) and individuals with MS can reveal mechanisms of neural adaptation and compensation.

This study investigated the correlation between SC and FC across six key resting–state networks: the dorsal and ventral default mode networks (dDMN, vDMN), left and right executive control networks (lECN, rECN), and anterior and posterior salience networks (aSN, pSN). By comparing structure–function coupling within and between these networks across groups, the analysis aimed to characterize disease–related alterations in network integration and to identify potential patterns of connectivity reorganization associated with MS.

Methods

This study was approved by the University of Manitoba Health Research Ethics Board and all participants provided written informed consent. Structure–function correlations were computed following HARDI and resting state fMRI (rs–fMRI) analyses using baseline MRI data from 102 persons with MS (pwMS) (83 female; 19 male; mean age = 49.02 ± 12.63 years; 85 relapsing–remitting MS; 17 progressive MS; mean disease duration = 19.26 ± 11.26 years; median [range] Expanded Disability Status Scale = 3.5 [0–6.5]) in the MS cohort of the Comorbidity and Cognition in Multiple Sclerosis (CCOMS) Study1. These results were then compared to previously computed structure–function relationships from 94 neurologically healthy participants in the CCOMS Study HC cohort (63 female; 31 male; mean age = 38.09 ± 15.55 years).

All MRI data in this study were acquired using a 3T Siemens Magnetom TIM Trio whole–body scanner equipped with a 32–channel head coil. HARDI scans were performed using the Human Connectome Project (HCP) spin echo multi–band echo planar imaging (MB–EPI) “cmrr_mbep2d_diff” sequence with a 3D field of view (FOV) of 212 × 204 × 160 mm3 and a spatial resolution of 2.00 × 2.00 × 2.00 mm3. This sequence captured 50 diffusion–encoded images at a b–value of 1500 s/mm2 and 5 non–diffusion–weighted images at b = 0 s/mm2, totaling 100 diffusion–weighted and 10 non–diffusion–weighted images over two acquisitions with opposite phase encoding directions. The rs–fMRI data were acquired using the gradient–echo HCP MB–EPI “cmrr_mbep2d_bold” sequence with a 3D FOV of 220 × 171.6 × 176 mm3 and a spatial resolution of 2.20 × 2.20 × 2.20 mm3. This sequence collected 420 volumes in a total acquisition time of 7:12 minutes1.

The SPM12 Artifact Correction in Diffusion MRI (ACID) Toolbox (version beta 02) was used to apply motion, eddy current, and echo planar imaging (EPI) distortion correction, and compute participant–specific whole brain mean diffusivity (MD) maps, which were then nonlinearly normalized to MNI152 space using the CAT12 (version r1318) Toolbox, as previously reported1. However, since elevated MD values are associated with brain tissue degradation, such as decreased cellularity, demyelination and edema, SC was defined as the additive inverse of MD (i.e., −MD). Therefore, region of interest (ROI) analyses were conducted to extract average SC (−MD) values from each of the individual tracts within the dDMN, vDMN, lECN, rECN, aSN, and pSN networks in the UManitoba–JHU Functionally–Defined Human White Matter Atlases2,3 to serve as a tract–specific measure of SC strength for each participant.

For each participant, SPM12 and the CONN Toolbox were used to remove the first 10 non–steady state rs–fMRI timepoints, align all of the remaining GE–EPI images, coregister these to the T1w anatomical image, apply skull–stripping, correct for EPI distortions, and apply spatial smoothing using a 4.0mm 3D Gaussian kernel. The Artifact Detection Tool (ART) was used to identify and exclude time points with volume–to–volume intensity changes of z > 3.0, rotational motion > 0.05 degrees, and/or translational motion > 0.50 mm. Finally, a temporal band–pass filter (0.008–0.09 Hz) was applied to isolate low–frequency fluctuations before using a ROI–ROI analysis to measure FC (i.e., temporal bivariate correlations) between each pair of ROIs within the dDMN, vDMN, lECN, rECN, aSN, and pSN networks distributed in the Stanford Resting State Connectivity Atlas.

Correlations within both the HC cohort (n=94) and MS cohort (n=102) were run, yielding both Pearson coefficients and p–values for all SC and FC ROI–ROI pairings within each network (Figure 1). Element–wise statistical comparisons of the SC–FC correlation coefficients from the HC vs. MS cohorts were then formally examined using the R software CoCor package4.

Results

Given the relatively large number of comparisons performed in the CoCor analysis, Fisher z–scores (i.e., differences between SC–FC correlations in the HC cohort and SC–FC correlations in the MS cohort) were organized into separate color–coded matrices for each of the six networks (Figure 2). In this figure, positive values (i.e., SC–FC correlations in HC > MS) are plotted using a warm colour scale, negative values (i.e., SC–FC correlations in HC < MS) are plotted using a cool colour scale, and the largest differences in either direction (p < 0.05) are highlighted using dashed white border outlines. 

Despite the relatively large number, the highly dependent nature of individual statistical tests violates key assumptions underlying conventional family–wise error correction methods (e.g., Bonferroni, Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate, etc.). Therefore, individual results for any particular SC–FC pairing should be interpreted with caution. However, the Fisher z–score matrices demonstrate patterns that suggest SC–FC coupling differences between groups in the six networks. 

First, the predominantly warm colors throughout the dDMN and vDMN (Figure 2A–B) suggest that, on average, these “task negative” networks exhibit higher SC–FC coupling among HCs compared to pwMS. Conversely, the predominantly warm colors throughout the lECN, rECN, aSN, and (to a lesser degree) pSN (Figure 2C–F) suggest that, on average, these “task positive” networks exhibit higher SC–FC coupling among pwMS compared to HCs. This suggests MS–related SC–FC changes at the network level.

Second, a consistent observation across all six network matrices (Figure 2) is that the positive and negative Fisher z–scores do not appear randomly distributed. Instead, with relatively few exceptions, the matrices contain columns of either predominantly warm (HC > MS) or predominantly cool (HC < MS) colors, suggesting that in addition to network–level alterations, there are also consistent within–network MS–related SC–FC changes.

Conclusion

Between–group differences in SC–FC coupling suggest that MS disrupts the relationship between structural and functional connectivity throughout large–scale networks that support internally directed cognition, cognitive control, and salience processing.

Interestingly, several SC–FC pairings (across all networks) exhibited correlations in different directions between the two cohorts – i.e., a positive correlation in the HC cohort and a negative correlation in the MS cohort, or vice versa. This suggests that SC–FC coupling within the cohort of pwMS is not just an extension of the normal variability observed within the HC cohort, but rather that there are fundamental differences between HC and pwMS that represent structural and/or functional network reconfigurations.

For the DMNs, parahippocampal SC and FC covaried with other DMN regions for both the HC and MS cohorts (not shown), and between groups the dDMN SC–FC correlations were generally higher in the HC cohort. Interestingly, the largest of these differences (p < 0.05 uncorrected) all involve at least one rich–club hub (superior frontal gyrus, inferior parietal cortex, precuneus, thalamus, or insula), suggesting that there may be a particular vulnerability of high–degree, integrative nodes that normally anchor global communication in pwMS. In the dDMN, the largest effects involved FC between a rich–club node (medial superior frontal gyrus or medial precuneus) and medial temporal structures (bilateral parahippocampal gyri), paired with SC between the precuneus and right cingulate gyrus or between left and right parahippocampal gyri.

For the ECNs, within–group SC–FC couplings in both HC and MS cohorts were concentrated in cerebellar connections (not shown), underscoring the cerebellum’s established role in cognitive control. However, these cerebellar SC–FC relationships did not differ significantly between groups, implying relative preservation of cerebellar structure–function alignment, despite known cerebellar involvement in MS5. Indeed, the only group–wise SC–FC difference in the ECN with p < 0.05 (uncorrected) involved FC between the left middle frontal gyrus and left middle temporal gyrus, paired with SC between the left middle frontal gyrus and left inferior/superior parietal regions. The predominance of large SC–FC couplings within each group combined with the sparsity of large ECN differences between groups, suggests that altered structure–function coupling may not be the primary substrate of executive impairment in MS.

The SNs showed a different and more convergent pattern. In the aSN, two large group differences with higher SC–FC coupling in the MS cohort involved FC of the medial superior frontal gyrus with the right insula, coupled with SC between the medial superior frontal cortex and left cerebellum and also between the left and right cerebellum. In the pSN, four large differences – also with higher SC–FC coupling in the MS cohort – all involved FC between the left cerebellum and medial thalamus, but with distinct SC pairings linking right postcentral gyrus, thalamus, and insula. Given that these SNs integrate external stimuli with interoceptive and affective states, these findings point to network–specific reorganization at the level of cerebello–thalamo–insular circuitry. Indeed, cerebellar pathology is common in MS and is implicated in both motor and cognitive deficits5. The present pattern – with higher SC–FC coupling observed in the MS cohort, especially centered on cerebellar and thalamic nodes – suggests that MS may shift how these hubs constrain and coordinate cortical processing, particularly in the SNs role in switching between DMN and ECN states.

The results support a model in which MS perturbs structure–function coupling, particularly in rich–club nodes in the DMN and SN systems, with relatively limited effects in the ECN. This approach may elucidate the underlying basis of the heterogeneity of impacts of MS on cognitive and behavioral outcomes.
