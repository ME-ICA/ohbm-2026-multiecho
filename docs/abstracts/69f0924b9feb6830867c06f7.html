Introduction

Superficial white matter (SWM) fibers form the majority of cortico-cortical white matter brain connections and play a key role in supporting local computations across the cortical sheet. Yet, despite their importance for cortical specialization, SWM bundles organization has rarely been compared across species within a neuro-evolutionary framework1. In this study, we applied a new sulcus-based labeling tool to two independent SWM bundle atlases, reconstructed from diffusion MRI tractography: a human atlas2 and a chimpanzee atlas3, to quantify how SWM bundles relate to local folding patterns across hemispheres and species. 

By extracting, for each bundle, the number and identity of crossed sulci, we characterized (1) the global distribution of sulcal-crossing complexity and (2) species differences in hemispheric asymmetry. We then examined two evolutionarily relevant sulcal territories: the central sulcus (C.S.) and the anterior ramus of the lateral (Sylvian) fissure  (F.C.L.r.ant), to assess how local sulcal geometry influences short-range fiber repertoires in motor-related and language-related regions.

This sulcus-centric approach provides a new comparative framework linking cortical folding and the architecture of superficial white-matter connections across two species of primates, allowing us to investigate how evolutionary pressures may have shaped the emergence, organization, and functional roles of short-range association fibers in the primate lineage.

Methods

Datasets and short-range fiber atlases - We analyzed SWM bundles in humans and chimpanzees using two existing atlases. For humans, the atlas was defined in the MNI template space and built from anatomical and diffusion MRI data from the entire HCP-YA cohort (1065 subjects)2. The chimpanzee atlas3 was defined based on a cohort of 39 subjects and available in the Juna.chimp template space4. Both atlases provide whole-hemisphere sets of SWM bundles (see fig. 1).

Sulcal extraction and quality control - Cortical sulci were extracted from the template surfaces of both species using the Morphologist pipeline from Brainvisa ( https://brainvisa.info/web/morphologist.html). This framework provides automated sulcal segmentation and labeling using a consistent nomenclature across hemispheres.

To ensure high-quality cross-species comparability, all sulcal labels underwent visual inspection and manual correction. Sulci with inconsistent definitions, ambiguous boundaries, or lacking clear homology across species were excluded. This resulted in a harmonized sulcal set enabling direct between-species comparisons (see fig. 1).

Sulcus-based labeling of short association fibers - We evaluated SWM connectivity using the Bundle-Sulci Labeling (BSL) tool from the Ginkgo toolbox5, which assigns to each fiber bundle the identity and number of the sulci it intersects. For every bundle in each atlas, we extracted two quantitative measures: 1) Sulcal-crossing count i.e. the total number of distinct sulci crossed along the trajectory; 2) Sulcus identity list i.e. the specific sulci intersected, enabling local analyses.

This yielded a sulcal “topology signature” for each bundle, directly linking fine-scale white-matter trajectories to cortical folding geometry. All computations were performed using identical BSL parameters for humans and chimpanzees to guarantee methodological symmetry.

Global topology analysis - To characterize the overall architectural profile of each species, we constructed the distribution of sulcal-crossing counts (i.e., number of bundles crossing 1, 2, 3, 4, or more sulci, called sulci complexity). Because the human atlas contains approximately four times more bundles than the chimpanzee atlas, all distributions were normalized by the total number of bundles per hemisphere to allow direct comparison of proportions.

We then used a Chi-square test (χ²) to statistically compare the shape of the distributions between species in each hemisphere. The test evaluated whether the proportion of bundles in each complexity category differed significantly across species.

Hemispheric asymmetry analysis - To quantify hemispheric differences in sulcal-crossing complexity, we computed the Laterality Index (LI) =  (R-L)/(R+L) using the mean sulcal-crossing count per hemisphere. Positive LI values reflect rightward asymmetry; negative values indicate leftward asymmetry. To localize the specific sulci contributing to hemispheric biases, we computed the Absolute Asymmetry Index (AAI) for each sulcus as: AAI = R-L/( R+L) using bundle counts assigned to each sulcus.

Sulcus-specific analyses of the central sulcus and inferior frontal sulcus - To investigate how sulcal morphology shapes local short-range connectivity, we focused on two evolutionarily meaningful sulci for which clear cross-species homology exists: the Central Sulcus (C.S.), a conserved landmark of the primary motor system, and anterior ramus of the lateral (Sylvian) fissure (F.C.L.r.ant) separating in the inferior frontal region Brodmann areas 44 and 55 - also called Broca areas,  which are implicated in language-related functions in humans.

For each sulcus, we quantified: (1) the absolute number of SAF bundles crossing it, (2) the proportion relative to the whole atlas, and (3) the hemispheric laterality index for local representation. This allowed us to contextualize global topology differences within specific functional territories.

Results

1) Global sulcus-based topology 

Normalized topology profiles revealed two fundamentally different organizational patterns. In humans, SWM bundles were strongly concentrated within the 1-2-sulcus range, reflecting predominantly local, short trajectories. In contrast, chimpanzees exhibited a distribution shifted toward higher complexity, with a peak at 3-4 sulci and a markedly larger proportion of bundles crossing ≥5 sulci (≈40% vs. ≈7% in humans). χ² tests confirmed highly significant species differences in both hemispheres (LH: χ² ≈ 2843, p < 0.0001; RH: χ² ≈ 3560, p < 0.0001).

2) Hemispheric asymmetry 

Laterality indices (LI) computed for the mean sulcal-crossing count revealed differences in hemispheric asymmetry. Mean sulcal-crossing counts showed a subtle rightward bias in humans (LI = +0.038; RH mean = 2.20 vs. LH = 2.04), indicating slightly more convoluted SWM bundles trajectories in the right hemisphere. Chimpanzees showed near symmetry (LI = +0.004; RH = 4.11 vs. LH = 4.08). This indicates that the average human SWM bundles possess a subtle right-hemisphere bias toward connecting more distant local areas than the average SWM bundles in the left hemisphere.

Sulcus-wise asymmetry maps revealed different “drivers” of hemispheric imbalance. In humans, the strongest asymmetries were found in anterior and limbic–orbitofrontal regions, with the Posterior Orbital Sulcus (S.O.p.; AAI ≈ 0.68), the Rhinal Sulcus (S.Rh.; AAI ≈ 0.61), and the Superior Frontal Polar Transverse Sulcus (S.F.polaire.tr.; AAI ≈ 0.57) all showing pronounced rightward biases (LI > 0). In contrast, chimpanzees displayed a different asymmetry profile: their most lateralized sulcus was the Callosal Sulcus (S.Call.; AAI ≈ 0.71, rightward bias), whereas high asymmetry in associative cortices, such as the Anterior Inferior Temporal Sulcus (S.T.i.ant.; AAI ≈ 0.42) and the Intraparietal Fissure (F.I.P.; AAI ≈ 0.26), was predominantly left-lateralized (LI < 0).

3) Local sulcal profiles: C.S. and F.C.L.r.ant. 

Despite the larger overall number of bundles in the human atlas, proportional analyses revealed that both species exhibit distinct local organizational biases. The C.S. accounted for ≈7.5% of all bundles in humans but ≈21–22% in chimpanzees, indicating a proportionally enlarged short-range motor periphery in chimpanzees. Humans showed a clear rightward asymmetry at the C.S. (LI = +0.111), whereas none was found in chimpanzees.

Conversely, the F.C.L.r.ant. was proportionally much more represented in chimpanzees with a higher number of crossing bundles (≈14–16%) compared to humans (≈1.5–2.3%), with both species showing leftward lateralization, stronger in chimpanzees (LI = –0.106 vs. –0.076 in humans). These findings highlight species-specific specializations in local connectivity around motor and inferior-frontal territories.

Conclusion

Sulcus-based mapping of SWM bundles revealed profound cross-species differences in both global topology and local hemispheric organization. Humans display predominantly SWM bundles with subtle right-hemisphere biases, whereas chimpanzees show a broader complexity range of crossed sulci, stronger representation of high-complexity bundles, and a distinct pattern of local asymmetries. Region-specific analyses further indicate that the central sulcus and the anterior ramus of the lateral fissure support disproportionately larger short-range repertoires in chimpanzees than in humans. These results show that local folding geometry and superficial white-matter architecture have evolved in species-specific ways, highlighting the value of sulcus-centric approaches for comparative neuroanatomy. Although these atlases were designed to be representative of their respective species, future work applying sulcus-based labeling directly to larger cohorts of human and chimpanzee subjects will be essential to determine whether the organizational principles identified here are consistent across individuals within each species.
