Introduction

Previous fMRI studies have reported changes in brain activity, connectivity, and intrinsic networks associated with decision-making about risky options (Chung et al., 2015; Lauharatanahirun et al., 2018). A parallel literature indicates that whole-brain multivariate analyses of neural responses during decision-making tasks yield effective neuromodulation targets whereby participants can learn to modulate distributed brain patterns associated with cognitive processes (Kim et al., 2024; LaConte et al., 2005; LaConte et al., 2007). This study combines these data to examine in adolescents whether real-time fMRI (rtfMRI) neurofeedback based on whole-brain activation classification modulates neural and behavioral responses during a risky decision-making task.

Methods

A prior dataset including 143 adolescents (non-rtfMRI) making choices about riskier versus safer gamble options (Lauharatanahirun et al., 2018) was analyzed to develop a whole-brain classifier for use in the current rtfMRI experiment. Condition-wise beta images associated with riskier and safer choices were estimated using a general linear model, and a nu-support vector machine with a non-linear kernel was trained using a 10-fold nested cross-validation scheme. A new sample of 30 adolescents was recruited for rtfMRI. During rtfMRI, percentage BOLD signal changes were estimated in real-time and the distance to the model hyperplane was presented via a slider bar every TR (every 2s). As shown in Figure 1, the paradigm consisted of 5 visits during which participants made a series of choices between riskier and safer gambles, with and without neurofeedback. The neurofeedback was a slider bar that moved toward the right or left side and reflected the distance to the hyperplane toward brain patterns associated with riskier (right) and safer (left) gamble choices (Figure 1C). Participants were instructed to move the slider bar to the right-hand side (reflecting brain patterns associated with riskier gamble choices) without receiving any explicit information about the meaning of the slider position. The first and last visits consisted of 72 decision trials without neurofeedback. In the second through fourth visits, six runs were completed including two non-real-time runs (36 trials each at the beginning and end of the visit) and four real-time runs (18 trials each). For offline analysis, the percentage BOLD signal was calculated to evaluate classifier performance for distinguishing choices between riskier and safer gambles. Individual classification was performed using a nu-support vector machine with a non-linear kernel based on a 10-fold nested cross-validation. The prediction ratio was reported for each of risky (positive) and safe (negative) choices. Risky prediction was calculated as true positive (TP) plus false positive (FP) dividing by the total samples (# of percentage BOLD) and safe prediction was estimated as true negative (TN) and false negative (FN) dividing by the total samples. In addition, the neural features from the classifier were used to measure the association with behavioral choices from comparison of the first and last visits.

Results

The model used to provide neurofeedback had a test accuracy of 79.93% (Figure 2A). Spatial patterns of the pretrained whole-brain classifier from 143 participants revealed contributions from bilateral insula, medial orbitofrontal cortex, medial superior frontal gyrus, caudate, superior temporal pole, dorsal lateral prefrontal cortex, anterior/posterior cingulate cortex, and inferior parietal lobule (Figure 2B). Adolescents’ choices of the riskier gamble decreased from the first visit to the last visit (Figure 2C). Whole-brain classification for both non-rt- and rt-fMRI runs showed increased and decreased prediction accuracy of brain patterns associated with riskier and safer choices, respectively (Figure 2D-E). The difference in risky choice ratio between the last and first visits showed a significantly negative association with the difference in false positives (i.e., incorrect prediction of risky choice) across all the subjects (Figure 2F). This association was stronger among subjects who decreased their risky choice ratio between the last and first visits but no association was observed for subjects who increased their risky choice ratio between the last and first visits. A significantly positive association was identified between the difference in risky choice ratio and the difference in true positives (i.e., correct prediction of risky choice) across all the subjects (Figure 2G).

Conclusion

These findings suggest the potential for successfully porting group data into a real-time fMRI setting to modulate brain processes associated with decision-making under risk in adolescents. In a context requiring choices between risky and safe options, adolescents regulated their brain features toward patterns associated with increased risk which resulted in increased behavioral choices of the safer gambles. This successful regulation differentiated between subjects showing increased versus decreased risky ratio over multiple visits.  Repeated training with rtfMRI neurofeedback induced changes in neural patterns and corresponding shifts toward safer or riskier decision-processes, highlighting its potential as a mechanism for modulating decision-making behaviors. Future studies may be warranted to evaluate alternative neurofeedback strategies, such as providing explicit instructions or modulating brain features associated with safer choices, to determine approaches that effectively modulate adolescent decision-making.
