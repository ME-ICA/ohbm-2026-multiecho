Introduction

Intravenous anesthetics are routinely used across surgical procedures, interventions, and critical care settings. These agents induce loss of consciousness through various receptor mechanisms. Propofol, a widely used anesthetic, acts primarily as a GABA_A receptor agonist. However, the pathway from molecular pharmacology to network-level effects and ultimately to loss of consciousness remains poorly understood. The fundamental challenge is to connect molecular-level receptor actions to network-level dynamics to understand how loss of consciousness emerges. Emerging evidence suggests that loss of consciousness arises from disruptions at the network level, including reduced information integration, breakdown of long-range frontoparietal feedback signaling, and altered thalamocortical connectivity patterns1. However, mechanistic explanations remain debated. Central to this debate is the thalamus, a key hub structure shaping cortical dynamics. Theories diverge on whether thalamic suppression drives cortical changes or results from them. Recent studies have found that during propofol anesthesia, diffusely projecting thalamic regions exhibit stronger reductions in connectivity than specialized nuclei. This pattern may reflect enhanced GABAergic inhibition of diffuse projections due to their stronger subcortical inhibitory input2. We test this proposed mechanism using a computational brain network model (BNM).

Methods

We employ the Larter-Breakspear neural mass model3, which describes the dynamics of an excitatory and an inhibitory neuronal population. Its parameters allow the implementation of propofol’s GABAergic effect, while its complex, chaotic dynamics can reproduce diverse temporal patterns characteristic of brain activity. To construct a BNM, we derive a thalamocortical structural connectome (SC) from diffusion imaging data, integrating a cortical parcellation4 with a fine-grained thalamic segmentation5 to enable detailed analysis of thalamocortical connectivity. In the BNM, brain regions are represented by Larter-Breakspear models that interact through long-range connections defined by the SC. Propofol-induced effects are investigated systematically, starting with an analysis of the Larter-Breakspear model in isolation to characterize local inhibitory modulation. Subsequently, a full-scale BNM comprising 68 cortical and 46 thalamic regions is constructed and optimized against empirical neuronal activity to establish a biologically constrained baseline.

Results

An exploration of the Larter-Breakspear model in isolation reveals that increasing local inhibitory coupling produces similar effects to prolonging inhibitory postsynaptic potentials. Both manipulations lead to decreased alpha-band power (8-12 Hz) and increased slow-frequency power (< 8 Hz), consistent with empirical findings during propofol anesthesia. Quantitative analysis of the structural connectome reveals that connection strengths of specific nuclei are characterized by a 50% larger standard deviation as compared to non-specific nuclei. This is consistent with the fact that specific nuclei project more heterogeneously than non-specific nuclei. Additionally, the two types of nuclei are associated with distinct connectivity patterns to the frontal and parietal regions. While specific nuclei project to frontal and parietal regions to the same extent (ratio: 1.2:1), non-specific nuclei predominantly exhibit frontal connectivity (ratio: 3.7:1). For the BNM, two parameters were optimized to reproduce empirical signatures of healthy resting-state brain activity, specifically fMRI-derived functional connectivity patterns and EEG spectral properties. We find a correlation r = 0.41 between empirical and simulated functional connectivity, as well as a peak in alpha power, characteristic of eyes-closed resting state EEG. Furthermore, the optimization places the BNM in a regime of chaotic dynamics. With increased implementation of propofol’s mechanism of action, we observe a slowed and less complex stimulus propagation through the network, as well as a gradual reduction of the Perturbational Complexity Index, signalling a decrease in the system’s ability to integrate information. This is in line with both empirical findings as well as previous computational approaches. 

Conclusion

The optimized brain network model successfully reproduces key characteristics of propofol-induced unconsciousness, including spectral slowing and decreased information integration as measured by the Perturbational Complexity Index. Structural connectome analysis reveals that non-specific thalamic nuclei exhibit predominantly frontal connectivity while specific nuclei show balanced projections, suggesting that preferential inhibition of non-specific nuclei during propofol anesthesia could selectively disrupt frontal activity and frontoparietal integration consistent with empirical observations during loss of consciousness. Having validated a model that reproduces key anesthetic signatures, this framework enables systematic investigation of underlying mechanisms, including whether differential thalamic suppression drives selective cortical disruptions. Integration with patient-specific connectomes could enable personalized anesthetic optimization, advancing toward precision medicine in perioperative care through "virtual patient" simulations.
