Introduction

The stability of adult primary somatosensory (SI) cortical maps in the face of altered bodily experience remains controversial. On one hand, SI finger maps in upper-limb amputees1,2 and tetraplegic individuals3 are strikingly similar to those of healthy controls, suggesting limited change in body representation despite severe sensory loss. However, habitual motor patterns are thought to be the main driver of SI organisation, as motor activity determines the timing of tactile inputs4. Given this, substantial changes in motor experience may drive body map plasticity in a way sensory deprivation alone does not. Motor Neuron Disease (MND) offers a compelling model to test this, as progressive motor degeneration and impairment alter movement patterns and consequently the afferent sensory inputs to SI.

Methods

Here, we used ultra-high-field (7T) functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) to map finger representations in SI in 8 MND patients and 15 controls. We complemented imaging with a battery of tactile perception, proprioceptive, and motor tests. This multimodal approach allowed us to investigate whether MND is associated with alterations in somatosensory maps and to assess whether impairments of somatosensory or motor function may underpin potential map changes.

Participants completed two vibrotactile stimulation tasks during a ~1.25-hour scan session, using MR-safe fingertip stimulators. A phase-encoded localiser defined an individual SI region of interest, and a blocked design provided the data for representational comparisons. Pre-processing followed a standard AFNI pipeline, and data were projected to the cortical surface for analysis. One patient and one control were removed due to excessive motion.

We applied representational similarity analysis (RSA) to quantify the pattern structure elicited by stimulation of each fingertip within the SI ROI.

Results

Preliminary results indicate that finger representations were generally more similar to each other in patients relative to controls (i.e., lower dissimilarity values; see Fig 1). When dissimilarity values were averaged across all ten finger pairings, most patients showed reduced differentiation between fingers compared with controls (Fig 2). One patient showed the opposite pattern, possibly related to their Flail Limb subtype, but this is speculative and remains under investigation.

Conclusion

These findings suggest that substantial changes to habitual motor behaviour—as occurs in MND—may reduce the distinctiveness of cortical finger representations. More broadly, the results support the idea that altered movement patterns, and the consequent changes in afferent sensory input, can erode the fine-grained structure of the adult body map. The reduced individuation of finger representations observed in patients may also reflect a breakdown in lateral inhibition between digits, potentially arising from altered excitation–inhibition balance in MND.
