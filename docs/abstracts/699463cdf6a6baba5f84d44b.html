Introduction

Human auditory perception relies on integrating information over multiple temporal windows, from fast acoustic cues to slower modulations supporting auditory object formation and meaning1,3. Neurophysiological evidence indicates that the auditory cortex operates through discrete temporal integration regimes (approximately 50, 200, and 400 ms) that shape how sound structure is transformed across cortical hierarchies3. These temporal scales are grounded in empirical evidence from human auditory cortex, offering a biologically motivated foundation for modelling how representations unfold across time.

Recent work on deep neural networks (DNNs) - brain alignment has shown that large-scale DNNs trained on sound recognition can partially mirror auditory cortical transformations, capturing structured trajectories through representational space5. However, the extent to which models that explicitly implement multiscale temporal integration capture the time-varying structure of magnetoencephalography (MEG) representations remains unknown. 

Here, we compare human MEG responses to natural sounds with two multistream auditory computational models, trained on environmental sound classification, sharing the same architecture but differing in temporal window sizes: a neurophysiologically motivated model (50/200/400 ms) identified initially in speech processing as cortical integration windows3 and a data-driven optimised model (20/40/80 ms) discovered via systematic hyperparameter search aimed at solely maximising sound-classification performance.

Using a model comparison framework, we test whether temporal integration windows derived from auditory neuroscience better account for MEG responses than those identified through a hyperparameter optimisation process that optimised only sound-classification accuracy. We then examine, within the best-performing model, which processing stages align most closely with distinct temporal phases of the MEG representational dynamics.

Methods

MEG Dataset.  Twenty-one participants (N females = 11; median age = 25yr) listened to a set of 150 sounds (duration = 2 s), eight times across two MEG sessions, while performing a one-back repetition detection task. Stimuli covered diverse categories (animals, tools, human actions, natural environments, mechanical events), were RMS-normalised, and presented binaurally.

MEG Acquisition and Preprocessing. MEG data were recorded with a 306-channel whole-head system at 1000 Hz sampling rate. Preprocessing included temporal signal space separation, band-pass filtering (0.05–70 Hz), artefact rejection, epoching (−1.0 to 3.75 s), and resampling to 200 Hz. MEG representational dissimilarity matrices (RDMs) were computed using cross-validated squared Mahalanobis distance (crossnobis), with noise covariance estimated via the Ledoit–Wolf method based on pre-stimulus baseline, and theoretical dependencies between distances removed through RDM whitening4.

Model Architecture. Both models were trained to perform sound classification. They were first pretrained on a large-scale environmental sound corpus (~1 million non-overlapping sounds) to learn general acoustic representations, then fine-tuned on 1,600 curated sounds and evaluated on a held-out test set of 400 sounds. Time-wise F1-score performance was used to select the best-performing optimised model for subsequent MEG alignment analyses2.

Either model processes the waveform input by converting it into a log-mel spectrogram and passing it through three parallel temporal streams that differ only in their temporal buffer sizes: 50, 200, and 400 ms in the neurophysiological model (reflecting cortical temporal integration windows)3,  and 20, 40, and 80 ms in the optimised model (obtained via hyperparameter search). Each stream applies successive 2-D convolutional layers that extract local spectrotemporal structure by learning filters tuned to energy transitions across time and frequency. The resulting representations are then fed into bidirectional Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) layers, whose gating mechanisms determine how much past and future context to retain. After recurrent processing, all streams are temporally aligned and concatenated, merging fast, mid, and slow temporal information into a unified multiscale feature space. Finally, time-distributed dense layers serve as the classification head, producing a category prediction at each time step during training; the hidden activations from these layers and all preceding layers form the basis for representational analyses.

Model Activations and RDM Computation. For each of the 150 sounds, activations from all layers were extracted, vectorised, downsampled (stride=200ms), and converted into model RDMs using Euclidean and Cosine distances. Model RDMs were whitened and z-scored to match MEG processing4.

RSA and Cross-Validation. We performed time-resolved Representational Similarity Analysis, following established approaches for aligning MEG representational geometries with deep neural network models5. For each participant and time point, we computed MEG representational dissimilarity matrices (RDMs) and compared them with model RDMs extracted from every layer. Using Leave-One-Participant-Out (LOPO) regression, model RDMs were used to predict MEG RDMs for all training participants; the resulting regression weights were then used to predict the held-out participant's RDM. This procedure produced cross-validated explained-variance (R2cv) time courses for each layer, providing a millisecond-resolved estimate of brain–model correspondence.

Model Comparison. To evaluate whether temporal-window selection shapes brain–model correspondence, we compared the overall predictive performance (R²CV time courses) of the optimised and neurophysiological models. Layer-by-layer analyses were then performed only for the optimised model to characterise how representational alignment evolves across its convolutional, recurrent, and post-concatenation stages.

Results

The optimised temporal-window model (20/40/80 ms) consistently outperformed the neurophysiological temporal-window model (50/200/400 ms) across participants and time (Figure 2, left). This advantage was broad and sustained, indicating that canonical integration windows derived from neurophysiology do not necessarily maximise alignment with MEG population-level representational geometry.

A clear hierarchical pattern emerged from the layer-by-layer analysis of the winning network. Early convolutional layers showed brief predictivity peaks shortly after sound onset (~70–120 ms), consistent with encoding of local spectrotemporal structure and matching the expected timing of early acoustic responses in auditory cortex.

In contrast, the strongest and most temporally extended alignment arose in the first bidirectional GRU layer (GRU0) of Stream 1 (40 ms), with a similarly pronounced but shorter peak in Stream 0 (20 ms). These layers integrate information over intermediate temporal ranges (longer than convolutional receptive fields yet shorter than the broader temporal spans of Stream 2, 80ms) and appear to capture transitions in auditory structure that are especially salient for MEG.

Stream-wise comparisons revealed a complementary pattern. Stream 0 (20 ms) contributed with some sharp peaks; Stream 1 (40 ms) generated sustained high-amplitude alignment, in particular in the recurrent layers; whereas Stream 2 (80 ms) followed the same temporal profile but did not exhibit a distinct peak above the overall layer-wise trend, suggesting that broader temporal windows did not bring additional predictive power at the MEG sensor level. Importantly, across convolutional and recurrent layers alike, temporal profiles showed similar overarching dynamics, indicating that multiple processing stages track the same underlying representational transitions with different temporal resolutions.

Overall, the data-driven model achieved markedly better alignment with MEG representational dynamics. This likely reflects that the temporal scales most relevant for cortical population responses to environmental sounds lie in the intermediate range, whereas the broader 50/200/400-ms windows were originally identified in the context of speech, where linguistic units such as phonemes, syllables, and words organise temporal integration.

Conclusion

These findings indicate that intermediate temporal integration mechanisms are key to capturing the evolving representational geometry of human auditory cortex. Although the temporal windows used in the neurophysiological model reflect canonical integration scales observed in neurophysiological recordings3, they did not provide optimal alignment with MEG. Instead, the optimised 20/40/80-ms windows suggest that the temporal structure most informative for population-level cortical representations lies at a finer and more dynamic scale than previously assumed.

The robust GRU0 peak in Streams 0 and 1 indicates that recurrent integration at intermediate latencies performs a key representational transformation, reorganising rapid acoustic features into more stable auditory dimensions that align well with MEG. Conversely, the limited contribution of Stream 2 suggests that excessively broad temporal pooling may obscure informative temporal structure rather than amplify it.

Together, these results highlight that the brain’s effective temporal coding regime (at least as reflected in MEG representational geometry) may prioritise short windows, and that models tuned via optimisation can reveal this preference more clearly than models built from canonical physiological assumptions. This underscores the value of combining neuroscientific hypotheses with data-driven model selection to uncover the temporal scales most relevant for human auditory computation.
