Introduction

Given their diagnostic and prognostic potential, age-related changes in spectral neurophysiology have been the subject of scientific enquiry for several decades.^1 However, most prior studies rely on cross-sectional data and traditional, independent analysis of pre-specified power bands. Such analyses ignore effects outside those bands, and more importantly, ignore covariance between bands (frequencies). In this study, we investigated the potential of higher-order tensor decomposition methods to identify meaningful components in resting-state MEG data, with dimensions of frequency, time, channel and participant. We then related the participant loadings to both cross-sectional and longitudinal measures of age and cognition.

Methods

The MEG data were approximately 9mins of eyes-closed, resting-state MEG data from Elekta VectorView system and a stratified sample of 130 individuals participating in Phase 2 (2011-13) and Phase 5 (2023-25, approximately 12 years later) of the CamCAN dataset (www.cam-can.org). The temporal extension of Signal Source Separation in MaxFilter2.12 was used to remove environmental noise, interpolate bad channels, and adjust for head movement. The data were subsequently high- and low-pass filtered at 0.1 Hz and 145 Hz, respectively, downsampled to 300 Hz, and segmented into 2s epochs. After bad epochs were excluded and cardiac and ocular artefacts were identified and removed using ICA, power spectra between 0.5 and 145 Hz were estimated for each epoch. These spectra were then averaged over up to 15 consecutive epochs (depending on number of bad epochs) to give 30s sampling of time. The amplitude data were then L2-normed separately for each participant, channel-type (102 magnetometers and 204 planar gradiometers) and phase. A 4-dimensional tensor was constructed, consisting of 306 channels, 96 frequencies (from 2-48Hz in steps of .5), 17 timepoints and 260 participants (concatenated across the two phases). We used a sparse, nonnegative CANDECOMP / PARAFAC decomposition (the PROX-ANQP algorithm of (^2)), for which the proximal regularisation was fixed to the default of 1e-4, and several values were tested for the sparsity regularisation, including the lowest two default values (0 and 1e5) as well as a very low intermediate value (.01) to evaluate the consistency of results. The decomposition was repeated for a range of component numbers [3, 4, 5, 6], with the maximum determined by inflection points in the relative error of the decomposition. The maximal correlation of the frequency mode was used to match components across different decompositions. 

The effect of age on each component was analysed with linear mixed effects models, predicting the loadings on the participant mode by a second-order polynomial expansion of age at baseline (Phase 2), change in age across phases, and their interaction. The longitudinal change in each component was further related to the longitudinal change in cognition, indexed by the slope of scores on the Cattell test of fluid intelligence. To control for the influence of outliers, a robust regression approach was implemented, predicting the slope in cognition by the slope of change in each MEG component.

Results

The three-component tensor decomposition with sparsity set to .01 revealed components that seemed to reflect a high Alpha component (#1), Beta component (#2) and low Alpha component (#3), with the low Alpha component potentially including aperiodic 1/f activity (Figure 1a). The high Alpha component decreased across time, potentially reflecting increased drowsiness (Figure 1b). The gradiometer topographies showed a distribution consistent with visual activity for the high Alpha component (#1), motor cortex activity for the Beta component (#2), and a broader visual/temporal component for the low Alpha / aperiodic component (#3, Figure 1c-e). 

The loadings for the high Alpha component (#1) decreased with age, while that for the low Alpha component (#3) increased with age (Figure 1f, h), consistent with a reduction of the peak frequency of Alpha with age that has been observed previously. Indeed, the high alpha component showed a significant decrease with cross-sectional age (estimate = -1.278, t = -2.934, p = .004), longitudinal age (estimate = -.005, t = -2.649, p = .009) and an interaction (estimate = -0.069, t = -2.239, p = .027). The low alpha component also showed a main effect of cross-sectional age (estimate = 0.567, t = 2.196, p = .030) and longitudinal age (estimate = .003, t = 4.531, p < .001) in the opposite direction, but no interaction. The Beta component did not show significant change across age (Figure 1g). 

The correlation between the change in each component and the change in cognition is shown in Figure 1i-k, though none reached significance. 

These results were reasonably consistent across the number of components and the sparsity regularisation parameter.

Conclusion

Tensor decomposition methods can reveal meaningful components in neurophysiological power spectra beyond those reflected in traditional band-specific analyses. This advancement allows for the identification of both cross-sectional and longitudinal relationships between age, neurophysiology and cognition.
