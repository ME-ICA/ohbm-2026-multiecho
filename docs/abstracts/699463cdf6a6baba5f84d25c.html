Introduction

Aging is associated with arterial stiffening, which affects the propagation of cardiac pulsation in the cerebral vasculature, potentially compromising brain function1,2. Furthermore, altered sleep physiology in aging is linked to cardiovascular and neurovascular health3 and the risk of neurodegenerative diseases, such as Parkinson’s disease. However, few studies have inspected these factors on cerebrovascular pulsatility. Resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) is traditionally used to map neural connectivity, with cardiac pulsation regarded as a physiological artifact. Recent studies demonstrated that cardiac pulsatility can be extracted from rs-fMRI BOLD signals to characterize vascular topography4. This study utilizes a cardiac-aligned rs-fMRI to estimate whole-brain pulsatility and investigate its associations with aging and sleep quality.

Methods

Resting-state fMRI and simultaneous photoplethysmography (PPG) data were acquired on 3T MRI from 116 healthy participants (77 younger adults, 18-35y; 39 older adults, 55-80y) from the baseline data of the public domain HRV-ER dataset5. Cardiac pulsatility was analyzed using a voxel-wise signal extraction pipeline. Preprocessing included second-order polynomial detrending to remove low-frequency scanner drift. Subsequently, BOLD signals were reordered based on PPG-derived cardiac cycles. The pulsatile waveform was modeled using a 3rd order Fourier series to capture the cyclic cardiac influence. The pulsatility was quantified as the coefficient of determination (R2) in native EPI space. Individual maps were normalized to MNI space via a multi-stage registration (Rigid→Affine→SyN) using Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs). Mean R2 values were extracted from 116 regions using the AAL atlas. Statistical analyses included Bonferroni-corrected independent t-tests for age effects and multiple linear regression models (adjusting for age group) for sleep associations, utilizing False Discovery Rate (FDR) to ensure robust findings.

Results

Figure 1A illustrates the spatial heterogeneity of BOLD pulsatility, revealing a posterior-to-anterior gradient. The Vermis and Right Cerebellum III exhibited the highest pulsatility (R2 = 0.31 ± 0.10 & 0.30 ± 0.09, mean ± S.D., respectively), significantly exceeding frontal cortical regions (R2 < 0.05). Regarding age-related alterations (Figures 1B), Bonferroni-corrected analysis identified significant hyper-coupling in older adults (OA) across six regions. Specifically, the Right Parahippocampal Gyrus exhibited significantly elevated pulsatility in OA (0.13 ± 0.03) compared to Younger Adults (0.10 ± 0.02), t (114) = -4.70, p < 0.001, yielding a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.92). This pattern suggests that arterial stiffening may disproportionately impact regions critical for memory.

Independent of these age effects, Figure 1C depicts that the Right Thalamus demonstrated the strongest positive association with sleep duration (standardized β = 0.45, p < 0.001, FDR q < 0.05; Figure 2A). Comparable associations were identified in the Left Superior Temporal Gyrus (β = 0.44; Figure 2B). As shown in these scatter plots, positive associations indicate that individuals with longer sleep duration maintain stronger pulsatility in subcortical integration hubs, supporting the role of sleep in regulating cerebrovascular dynamics.

Conclusion

This study characterized the association between vascular pulsation and age/sleep across 116 brain regions using a retrospective fMRI signal extraction approach. While the hindbrain exhibited the strongest pulsatile baseline, the analysis revealed a distinct intensification of pulsatility within the parahippocampal gyrus in older adults, suggesting age-related vascular vulnerability in memory networks. In contrast, the thalamus exhibited an age-independent positive association with sleep duration, implicating a potential protective neurovascular link. Collectively, these results suggest the utility of BOLD pulsatility as a non-invasive biomarker for monitoring cerebrovascular health and detecting early hemodynamic changes in aging.
