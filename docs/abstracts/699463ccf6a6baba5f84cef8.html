Introduction

Brain activity unfolds as a succession of recurrent states across time scales1,2. EEG and fMRI provide complementary temporal and spatial views of these dynamics3, yet how their brain states correspond remains unclear. Here, we examine how fast, band-specific EEG states relate to the expression of slower fMRI states during simultaneous EEG–fMRI. Using Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) applied separately to fMRI and EEG, we identified recurring fMRI brain states and EEG brain states characterized by both the EEG broadband and frequency band–limited powers. We then investigate how band-limited EEG state expression contributes to the broadband state structure and also relates to fMRI state activation. Our analyses indicate that broadband EEG states can be partially explained by a combination of band-specific EEG state dynamics, and that multiple frequency components may jointly contribute, though not uniquely, to the activation of specific resting fMRI states. Together, this work provides a band-resolved, state-based framework for probing how millisecond-scale electrophysiological state organization maps onto recurring BOLD amplitude patterns, while highlighting the need for further evidence to establish a definitive temporal or causal correspondence between modalities.

Methods

In this study, resting state data from 12 participants was recorded simultaneously on a 3T fMRI and a 10-20 EEG system for 8 minutes. fMRI data was preprocessed using a standard fmriprep pipeline, while EEG data underwent gradient artifact removal in EEGLAB and ICA-based cleaning in mne-python. EEG data were further source-localised, and both data sets were parcellated using the 100-region Schaefer atlas. We applied Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) separately to each modality. In fMRI, HMM states were defined as recurring spatial activation maps (state-specific patterns of BOLD amplitude) with probabilistic switching over time. In EEG, we estimated a sequence of recurrent HMM states characterized by the broadband EEG signal, as well as the beta, alpha, theta and delta bands. We then compared mean activations and temporal distributions across modalities and frequency bands. In addition, we evaluated whether state occurrences demonstrated cyclical behaviour. 

Results

We identified 10 recurrent states in the EEG data, and 5 in the fMRI data. Band-specific EEG states exhibited low to moderate correlations with the most closely matching broadband EEG and fMRI states (rmean(EEG) = 0.26, σ(EEG) = 0.12, rmean = 0.2, σ = 0.09). At lower frequencies, a small number of EEG states were active for a majority of time points, but different activity patterns were dominant across the frequency bands. EEG states did not occur at different rates between fMRI states. We identified multiple combinations of EEG states across frequency bands which were significantly more or less likely to occur than chance (padj < 0.05). Cyclical dynamics were significant in high-frequency and broadband EEG (padj < 0.05), but not low-frequency EEG or fMRI. Both cycle order and cycle rate differed between EEG frequencies.

Conclusion

Our findings show that brain activity dynamics differ substantially between fMRI and EEG, as well as across EEG frequency bands. While recurrent patterns showed some spatial overlap between frequency bands and modalities, EEG dynamics were dominated by distinct patterns in each frequency band, and EEG state distributions were independent of fMRI state activation. However, we also found that the occurrence of EEG brain states is not independent across frequency bands, indicating a common mechanism for synchronizing processing tasks.
