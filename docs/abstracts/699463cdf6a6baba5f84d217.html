Introduction

Humans present a robust sensitivity to geometry. A key signature of this capacity is the geometric regularity effect, where individuals show higher accuracy and faster responses to regular shapes, such as squares and rectangles, than to irregular ones1. This effect is absent in baboons, whose behavior is explained by object-recognition models1, whereas human performance requires an extra model incorporating discrete geometric features1,2. This leads to the hypothesis that humans may deploy an extra cognitive process for encoding geometric properties supported by brain regions implicated in mathematical reasoning.

Geometry is closely intertwined with other branches of mathematics. Neuroimaging shows that geometry and number representations both engage the anterior intraparietal sulcus (aIPS) in children and adults2,3. Yet the specificity of geometry’s relation to mathematics remains untested as prior research has not accounted for spatial abilities. The intraparietal sulcus (IPS) is also engaged during spatial tasks, including visuospatial working memory (WM), spatial reasoning4 and mental rotation5. Because the IPS comprises multiple cytoarchitectonic subregions, group-level analyses may blur nearby regions and create overlaps that may not exist at the individual level.

We investigated whether sensitivity to geometric regularity is uniquely tied to mathematical reasoning or if instead reflects broader spatial cognition. Two online behavioral experiments (N = 150; N = 200) and a high-density intra-individual fMRI study (N = 3) were conducted. Participants completed geometry, mathematics, and spatial tasks. We hypothesized (i) a behavioral interaction between mathematics and geometric regularity, and (ii) selective engagement of aIPS during mathematics and geometry but not during spatial tasks.

Methods

Experiment 1: Online experiment

A total of 150 participants were recruited via Prolific and completed an online study using the Gorilla platform. The experiment included a number comparison (NC) task and a battery of spatial tasks: sequential order (SO), Boolean reasoning, mental rotation, and WM (Fig. 1A). All tasks, except the math task, included either regular or irregular trials, defined by the type of shapes presented or, in the case of the WM task, by the structure of the sequences. Regular trials used squares and rectangles while irregular trials used all other shapes (Fig. 1B). The experiment exhibited several limitations. Neither the geometric regularity effect nor its interaction with mathematics was significant. Additionally, many participants had poor performance on the NC task, which only measured one dimension of mathematical ability. For these reasons, a new version of the online experiment was developed. 

In the second version, 200 participants were recruited. This version retained all tasks from the original setup and added a geometry-intruder task plus three additional mathematical tasks: addition, subtraction, and number line (NL) (Fig. 1A). Because each mathematical task targeted a distinct component of mathematics, a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted using task accuracies to derive a composite score reflecting overall mathematical ability.

Separate linear mixed-effects models were fitted for each task, using either log-transformed RT or accuracy as the dependent variable. For the WM task, only accuracy-based models were constructed. In the first version of the experiment, math ability was indexed by performance on the NC task (RT or accuracy), while in the second one, it was specified using a CFA math score. To assess task-general effects, RT and accuracy values were first standardized within each task and then pooled. Each model included fixed effects for math ability, trial type (regular or irregular), their interaction. Age and sex were included as covariates. A random intercept for each participant accounted for individual baseline differences. 

Experiment 2: fMRI experiment

The second experiment was an intra-individual fMRI study in which three participants completed two scanning sessions on the same day. Participants performed a set of cognitive tasks similar to those used in the online experiment, including NC (math), WM, SO, mental rotation and an oddball task. The oddball task was used as a visual localizer and involved the presentation of miniblocks of regular shapes, irregular shapes, houses, faces, tools and math stimuli (Fig. 1C). Participants were instructed to maintain fixation on a central green dot and press a button when it turned red (Fig. 1E). All tasks had either regular or irregular trials, except the math task, and control blocks. 

A geometry-sensitive area was defined for each individual using the oddball task from the contrast (irregular + regular) > (faces + tools + houses), with the search restricted to the parietal lobe and the frontal eye field (Fig. 1D). 

The right anterior IPS coordinate [35.5,−48.5,55.5] reported by Sablé-Meyer2 and its mirrored left-hemisphere counterpart served as references. Two IPS peaks were defined per subject and required to lie within 15 mm of these coordinates. If multiple local maxima met the criterion, the peak with the highest t-value was selected. 

Bilateral peaks around the middle occipital gyrus (MOG) were consistently present in all subjects and were therefore included. Final ROIs were 5-mm-radius spheres centered on the selected peak coordinates. After ROI definition, significant activation by the other tasks within each ROI was assessed using voxelwise FWE correction at p < 0.05.

Finally, an IPS task-activation map was generated to visualize subregional engagement across tasks. IPS subdivisions were taken from the Julich atlas, which parcellates IPS into eight cytoarchitectonic subregions.

Results

Experiment 1: Online experiment

In the first version of the experiment, a linear mixed-effects model across all tasks revealed a robust main effect of math ability for both RT (β = 1.084, p < 0.001) and accuracy (β = 3.285, p < 0.001). However, the geometric regularity effect was not significant (RT: β = 0.711, p = 0.429; accuracy: β = 0.527, p = 0.117), and there was no significant interaction between math ability and trial type (RT: β = −0.136, p = 0.217; accuracy: β = −0.025, p = 0.951). Task-specific models followed the same general pattern, with math ability positively predicting both RT and accuracy. The only exception was the WM accuracy model, where regular trials were significantly more accurate than irregular ones (β = 2.070, p < 0.001). 

In the second version, the CFA showed excellent fit (CFI = 0.995; RMSEA = 0.049). In the linear mixed-effects model using RT as the outcome, there was no significant main effect of math ability (β = −0.066, p = 0.368). Additionally, regular trials were significantly faster (β = −0.581, p < 0.001), and the interaction between math ability and trial type was significant and negative (β = −0.155, p = 0.003). In the accuracy model, math ability positively predicted accuracy (β = 0.260, p < 0.001), and regular trials were more accurate overall (β = 0.568, p < 0.001). The interaction term was not significant (β = −0.045, p = 0.429). Generally, individual task models were consistent with the global pattern. However, in the accuracy models, the math coefficient for rotation was not significant (β = 0.148, p = 0.151) and there was a significant interaction in geometry (β = -0.252, p = 0.013). The significant interaction likely reflects a ceiling effect in regular trials, where mean accuracy was 0.932 compared to 0.723 in irregular trials. Additionally, after correcting for multiple comparisons, the interaction term of the RT models for Boolean and rotation did not survive. 

Experiment 2: fMRI experiment

The geometry-sensitive ROIs were identified in all three subjects based on the oddball task (Fig. 2). Four clusters were observed bilaterally in aIPS and MOG for all subjects, except that the left aIPS (laIPS) was absent in subject 2. The peak coordinates were localized using the Julich atlas and all individual aIPS coordinates lied in hIP3 within the IPS. 

Next, we assessed the level of activity in the cognitive spatial and mathematical tasks vs control conditions within these ROIs. Both laIPS and raIPS showed significant activation across all spatial cognitive tasks and math in all subjects (Fig. 2, FEW at p<0.05). MOG clusters did not exhibit consistent activation. 

When comparing activation magnitudes within each ROI, mental rotation showed the strongest response in the raIPS, whereas the SO task elicited the weakest activation (Fig. 2). No other ROI demonstrated a consistent order of activation magnitudes across subjects.

The geometrical regularity effect (irregular > regular) was tested within each ROI and task using FWE correction at p < 0.05. The effect was present in laIPS for SO in subjects 2 and 3, and for rotation in subject 3. In raIPS, it appeared for the geometry task in subjects 1 and 3, and for rotation in subject 3. No ROI showed a geometric regularity effect for the WM task in any participant (Fig. 2). 

Using the Julich atlas, we quantified the percentage of significantly activated voxels within IPS subregions (FWE at p < 0.05). Across tasks and subjects, hIP1 and hIP3 (aIPS) showed consistently higher activation than other subregions. In the geometry task, few voxels survived the primary statistical threshold. After lowering the threshold (p < 0.001 and p < 0.01, both uncorrected), subject 2 showed bilateral activation in hIP1; subject 3 showed activation in left hIP1 and right hIP3. Subject 1 did not exhibit systematic activation in the geometry task.

Conclusion

The objective of this work was to determine whether sensitivity to geometric regularities is uniquely supported by mathematical reasoning or if instead reflects broader spatial cognition. 

In the first online experiment, no geometric regularity effect emerged in any of the models. This motivated the design of a second version, which included three extra mathematical assessments and a geometry task. In the second version, a robust geometric regularity effect was observed across all models. Although mathematical accuracy was related to spatial ability, no significant relationship was found between mathematical performance and accuracy to detect geometric regularities. Thus, we found no evidence that accuracy in detecting geometric regularities depends on mathematical ability.

In the neuroimaging study, ROIs were defined in areas sensitive to geometric perception in bilateral aIPS and bilateral MOG, replicating the results obtained by Sablé-Meyer et al2. The aIPS was also activated during mathematics, but also in all other spatial cognitive tasks. No region exhibited selective activation for mathematics and geometry alone. 

Further analyses localized the aIPS ROIs within the hIP3 subregion of the IPS. Moreover, all spatial and mathematical tasks consistently activated bilateral aIPS, specifically subregions hIP1 and hIP3. These findings support the hypothesis that geometric processing relies on domain-general spatial resources.

Consequently, none of our initial hypotheses were supported. The combined behavioral and neuroimaging results indicate that geometry and mathematics draw on shared neural mechanisms, but their relationship appears to reflect a general spatial foundation rather than uniquely mathematical.
