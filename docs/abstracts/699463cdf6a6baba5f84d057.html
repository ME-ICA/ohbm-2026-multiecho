Introduction

Targeted central nervous system (CNS) drug development remains a challenge, in part because brain disorders present with complex and heterogeneous behavioral symptoms. Addressing these symptoms effectively requires understanding not only which aspects of behavior are influenced by a given drug, but also which drugs are plausible candidates for targeting specific behavioral domains. Although molecular targets, e.g. receptors and their corresponding genes, are known for many drugs, it is often difficult to link these to particular behavioral profiles. Consequently, this lack of mechanistic insight hampers the development of selective and effective CNS therapeutics, underscoring the need for frameworks that more directly link molecular action to behavioral outcomes.

Here, we present an analytical framework that derives behavioral profiles directly from a drug’s mechanism of action by leveraging vertex-level cortical gene-expression data provided by the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA1). These high-resolution spatial signatures allow us to characterize where in the cortex drug-target genes are most strongly expressed2. We then link these spatial gene expression signatures to behavioral constructs defined by the Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) framework via spatial correlation analyses with domain-associated functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) meta-analytic maps. This strategy, first demonstrated by Ecker et al.2, enables a principled mapping between molecular targets and behavioral domains. We demonstrate the utility of this framework based on methylphenidate (MPH). MPH is a Food and Drug Administration (FDA)‑approved treatment used in Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) to increase sustained attention and working memory while reducing movement and impulsivity. The mechanism of action of MPH is well established, thereby serving as a positive control to validate the method and demonstrate its capacity in predicting drug–behavior relationships.

Methods

To characterize how MPH’s molecular targets relate to behavioral domains, we developed a three-step workflow. First, based on MPH’s mechanism of action, we identified a set of cortically expressed genes, forming the gene set for subsequent analyses. Second, we leveraged publicly available gene-expression data to derive a cortical expression pattern that best represents where drug effects are likely localized across the cortex. Last, we correlated this expression pattern with meta-analytic fMRI maps representing the RDoC domains, enabling us to link MPH’s molecular targets to a specific behavioral profile.

The gene set associated with MPH’s mechanism of action was identified using the DrugBank database (https://go.drugbank.com). DrugBank is a curated repository containing molecular data for approximately 20,000 drugs. Three primary targets were identified for MPH: the dopamine transporter (SLC6A3), the noradrenaline transporter (SLC6A2), and the serotonin 1A receptor (HTR1A). The genes encoding these target proteins –  referred to as “target genes” – were used as inputs for the subsequent cortical expression mapping.

Cortical expression maps were generated from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA1), an open-access post‑mortem dataset quantifying mRNA expression across the human brain. AHBA data were initially quality‑controlled and normalized, and subsequently spatially interpolated across the cortex using Ordinary Kriging2. The resulting maps provided high‑resolution expression profiles on the fsaverage6 template (40,962 vertices per hemisphere). Because SLC6A3 and HTR1A were the only targets robustly expressed in cortex, their average expression provided a single high‑resolution expression signature for MPH. This map provided the molecular basis for subsequent spatial correlations with RDOC domain-specific functional activation patterns.

Behavioral (i.e., functional) activation patterns were derived using NeuroQuery3, an automated meta-analytic tool predicting functional patterns of activation based on search terms. We generated functional maps for 37 terms reflecting Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) constructs (https://www.nimh.nih.gov/research/research-funded-by-nimh/rdoc/constructs) each assigned to one of six broader behavioral domains. The search terms were consistent with a set of seed terms used in a previously published RDoC-related study generating activation maps from Neurosynth4. The generated target fMRI patterns were projected to the fsaverage6 surface  to ensure spatial alignment with the target gene expression signature for MPH. 

The spatial correspondence between the target gene expression signature and fMRI patterns were assessed using Spearman rank correlations. To focus on the magnitude of association, we evaluated absolute correlations, as MPH’s opposing agonist and inhibitory actions on its targets preclude interpreting the direction of correlation. To assess statistical significance, we generated a null distribution using 5,000 ‘eigenstrapped’ surrogate maps with automatic selection of the optimal number of Laplacian eigenmodes5. Because our focus was on the overall behavioral profile across RDoC domains rather than on the significant association with an individual domain, we interpreted correlations at a nominal (uncorrected) threshold of peigen-value < 0.05 (two-tailed). This approach resulted in a behavioral profile for MPH derived from spatial associations between its target gene expression signature and functional activation patterns linked to RDoC constructs.

Results

The spatial distribution of MPH’s cortical expression signature of the SLC6A3 and HTR1A genes showed a systematic gradient across the temporal and occipital lobes, which is displayed in Figure 1A. This pattern served as the reference pattern for subsequent association analyses with the RDoC behavioral domains, including the fMRI activation map for RDoC construct Attention that is displayed in Figure 1B. 

Spatial correlations between gene expression and functional activation patterns revealed a distinct profile of behavioral domains most strongly associated with MPH’s mechanism of action (Figure 1C). As expected, the strongest associations emerged within the Cognitive Systems domain, where the expression pattern aligned most closely with constructs related to attentional control, goal-directed behavior, and inhibitory processes. These included Attention (ρ = 0.45, peigen = 0.03), Goal Selection (ρ = 0.42, peigen = 0.01), Inhibition/Suppression (ρ = 0.39, peigen = 0.01), and Working Memory – Active Maintenance (ρ = 0.23, peigen = 0.02). Additionally, we observed significant associations for all constructs in the Negative Valence domain: Acute Threat (‘Fear’; ρ = 0.29, peigen = 0.03), Loss (ρ = 0.24, peigen = 0.02), and Potential Threat (‘Anxiety’; ρ = 0.27, peigen = 0.05). A significant association was observed for the behavioral domain Systems for Social Processes with construct Animacy Perception (ρ = 0.41, peigen = 0.01), indicating sensitivity to socially relevant visual cues. No significant associations were observed for Positive Valence and Arousal/Regulation, and the spatial correlations within Sensorimotor Systems also did not reach significance. Together, these findings indicate a distinct behavioral profile for MPH, which is marked by the strongest spatial alignment with cognitive control, attentional, and threat-related processes.

Conclusion

This study aimed to link MPH’s mechanism of action to a specific behavioral profile by comparing gene expression signatures to fMRI activation patterns for 37 behavioral constructs in the RDoC framework. By deriving a behavioral profile solely from the cortical gene expression patterns encoding MPH’s molecular targets, we were able to relate molecular target–derived predictions to known behavioral effects reported for MPH administration in ADHD. The strongest target-derived associations emerged for cognitive processes, which are central to ADHD symptomatology, i.e. attention, goal selection, inhibition/suppression, and working memory. These findings align well with MPH’s established efficacy in reducing core ADHD symptoms. We also observed significant associations with the Negative Valence System, including fear, loss, and anxiety, which is in line with anxiety and nervousness being common side effects of MPH treatment. The notable association with animacy perception likely reflects an involvement of the ventral visual pathway, where the expression of MPH target genes differs from their average cortical expression. Together, these findings show that the spatial alignment between a drug’s target gene expression pattern and functional activation maps might be used to predict both its therapeutic and side-effect–related behavioral effects.

These findings thus offer an initial ‘proof-of-concept’; i.e., that behavioral profiles of pharmacological agents may be predicted based on their molecular targets and corresponding cortical expression patterns, and/or vice versa. However, rather than replacing experimental or clinical investigations, we highlight that this approach may provide a complementary framework for predicting behavioral domains that may be targeted by a drug based on mechanistic insights. As such, this framework may serve as a cost-efficient and accessible starting point for exploring possible drug effects or identifying candidate agents for further preclinical and clinical evaluation.

Despite several limitations, the approach presented here provides a promising step toward data-driven behavioral inference in CNS drug research. First, the current analysis focused exclusively on cortical expression patterns and did not incorporate subcortical targets, which are relevant for many CNS-active agents. Moreover, not all molecular targets of MPH exhibit robust cortical expression, constraining the completeness of the expression-derived profile. The observed correlations, while statistically significant, fall within weak-to-moderate ranges, and the use of absolute coefficients precludes differentiating whether MPH’s agonist or inhibitory actions on specific targets would enhance or suppress a given behavior. Nonetheless, these limitations highlight opportunities for methodological refinement while underscoring the potential utility of this approach for generating initial behavioral predictions to inform targeted and mechanism-informed CNS drug development. Ultimately, by integrating molecular targets with large-scale functional activation patterns, this framework opens a new avenue for anticipating behavioral effects of CNS-active agents and supports more rational, mechanism-based strategies in early-stage drug discovery.
