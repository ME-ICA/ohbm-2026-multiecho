Introduction

International neuroimaging collaborations depend on the lawful and ethical movement of neurodata across institutions and jurisdictions. However, cross-border transfers are governed by highly heterogeneous legal regimes that define personal and sensitive data differently, impose divergent consent requirements, and condition international data flows on adequacy decisions, standard contractual clauses (SCC), or localised data transfer agreements.1 These inconsistencies create structural bottlenecks that delay or constrain global brain-mapping initiatives globally. 

To address these governance challenges, the BRIDGE Project conducted a systematic, doctrine-based comparative legal analysis of neurodata-related regulatory frameworks across Africa, Latin America and the Caribbean, Europe, and North America. Using a standardised ontology, the study mapped how more than 96 laws and regulations conceptualise neurodata, regulate its processing, and enable or restrict international transfers. This work sought to identify critical friction points, such as mismatched consent standards, inconsistent definitions of sensitive data, and divergent transfer mechanisms, as well as legal enablers that can facilitate responsible and interoperable neuroimaging data sharing.

The goal of this analysis is to generate practical insights for neuroscientists, data stewards, consortia leads, and neuroinformatics teams seeking to design compliant, future-proof global research workflows. By integrating legal-comparative rigour with operational relevance for neuroscience practice, the study provides the empirical foundation for harmonised transfer tools, including global model Data Transfer Agreements (DTA) and automated governance platforms.

Methods

We employed a structured, comparative doctrinal–qualitative methodology aligned with the BRIDGE six-layer legal ontology. Legal materials were identified using a multi-tiered regional search strategy and strict inclusion criteria covering constitutions, data protection laws, health research statutes, consumer protection frameworks, and authoritative regulatory interpretations.

Each instrument was analysed according to six predefined dimensions: 1) scope and definitions of neurodata and sensitivity classifications; 2) consent models and lawful processing bases; 3) data subject rights; 4) rules governing cross-border data transfers; 5) cybersecurity and oversight mechanisms; and 6) ethics review requirements. Coding was performed by regionally qualified legal researchers using standardised templates linked to verbatim citations. Inter-coder reliability achieved Cohen’s κ ≥ 0.70, with discrepancies resolved through consensus review.

Transfer-specific variables were mapped across jurisdictions, including: a) availability and scope of adequacy decisions; b) recognition of standard contractual clauses or equivalent instruments; c) mandatory content for DTAs; d) supplementary measures for high-risk transfers; e) localisation or data-residency restrictions; and f) exceptions for scientific research or public interest. Analytical outputs were converted into machine-readable matrices and cross-regional comparison tables.

Finally, bottlenecks and enabling conditions were identified through pattern analysis. Bottlenecks were defined as legal conditions that materially restrict, delay, or complicate international data movement in neuroimaging. Enablers were defined as legal structures or flexibilities that promote interoperability, risk-proportionate governance, or cross-border research integration.

Results

Across regions, neurodata is inconsistently defined and classified, with major consequences for transfer governance. Europe uniformly treats most neuroimaging data as sensitive personal data subject to heightened safeguards, whereas African and Latin American frameworks vary widely in their sensitivity classifications and exemptions for scientific research. North American regimes, particularly the United States, apply a fragmented, sector-specific approach that creates uncertainty for multinational data flows.

Consent requirements differ markedly.2,3 The EU and many African jurisdictions require explicit consent for processing special categories of data; several Latin American laws recognise broad consent for future use; and the US commonly relies on HIPAA authorisations or institutional agreements. These divergences complicate interoperability and re-use in pooled neuroimaging studies.

Cross-border transfer rules showed the greatest barriers.4,5 Adequacy mechanisms exist primarily in the EU but are absent or limited elsewhere. SCC-analogous clauses exist in several African and Latin American statutes but vary in structure, legal force, and mandatory safeguards. Some jurisdictions impose stringent localisation rules, while others permit researcher-driven DTAs if accompanied by supplementary measures such as encryption, anonymisation, and impact assessments.

Despite these bottlenecks, several enabling trends emerged: convergence around core data subject rights, including access, correction, and erasure; growing recognition of scientific research exemptions, particularly under GDPR analogues; widespread acceptance of contractual transfer instruments, though often with jurisdiction-specific adaptation; emergent global norms on transparency, cybersecurity, and proportional risk assessment.

These findings point toward the feasibility of a global model DTA and harmonised governance workflows for neuroimaging collaborations.

Conclusion

This comparative analysis reveals that the principal barriers to global neuroimaging data sharing arise not from a lack of legal safeguards but from misalignment across jurisdictions in sensitivity classifications, consent standards, transfer mechanisms, and oversight structures. However, the study also identifies a growing set of legal enablers, such as research exemptions, flexible contractual mechanisms, and convergent data rights, that can support harmonised, ethically robust cross-border neuroimaging collaborations.

By systematically mapping these conditions across 4 world regions, this work provides the empirical foundation for developing interoperable governance tools, including global model DTAs, cross-jurisdictional transfer matrices, and digital compliance platforms such as ezGov. These tools can help neuroscientists navigate regulatory complexity, reduce delays in multinational studies, and promote responsible, equitable, and secure neurodata sharing. Future work will integrate these findings into machine-readable ontologies and automated decision-support systems to further streamline compliance within international neuroimaging research ecosystems.
