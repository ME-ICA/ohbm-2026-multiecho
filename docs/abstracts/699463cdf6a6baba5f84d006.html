Introduction

Epilepsy is characterized by abnormal, rapidly fluctuating interactions across large-scale brain networks—dynamics that static connectivity methods cannot adequately capture. Magnetoencephalography (MEG), with its millisecond temporal resolution, provides unique access to these fast-changing neural interactions. However, commonly used dynamic functional connectivity (DFC) approaches—such as sliding-window correlations or time-varying covariance analyses—often fail to detect the transient bursts, causal interactions, and event-driven dependencies that underpin epileptiform activity propagation.

To overcome these limitations, we introduce a modeling framework based on the multivariate Hawkes process, a self-exciting point process model that quantifies directed, history-dependent neural interactions with fine temporal resolution. MEG signals were preprocessed, source-localized, and converted into event sequences reflecting high-amplitude transient activations across cortical regions. The Hawkes model was then applied to estimate directed excitation dynamics, enabling inference of how activity in one region modulates the likelihood of subsequent events elsewhere in the network.

This framework reveals fine-grained, temporally precise interactions that remain obscured in conventional DFC analyses. In particular, epilepsy networks exhibited heightened recurrent excitation and abnormal cross-temporal lobe interactions, especially within hippocampal and medial temporal circuits. Together, these findings demonstrate the suitability of Hawkes models for uncovering event-driven network mechanisms in epilepsy and their potential utility in seizure prediction and network-targeted interventions.

Methods

The MEG dataset was collected at Seoul National University Hospital (SNUH) and includes 44 mesial temporal lobe epilepsy (mTLE) patients with hippocampal sclerosis (HS) who achieved post-surgical seizure freedom between 2005 and 2011, as well as 46 age-matched, right-handed healthy controls (HC). Of the patients, 22 underwent left temporal (LT) resections and 22 underwent right temporal (RT) resections, forming clinically homogeneous subgroups with respect to pathology and outcomes (Jin et al., 2015).

Hawkes processes provide a principled framework for modeling MEG activity as a multivariate point process. In this formulation, the brain is represented as a system of discrete neural events whose likelihood depends on past events according to excitation kernels. This history-dependent structure is particularly appropriate for epilepsy, where pathological networks transition through rapid bursts of excitation, cross-regional triggering, and short-lived hypersynchronous states. MEG is thus an ideal modality for Hawkes modeling due to its high temporal sensitivity to neuronal population dynamics.

Preprocessed and source-localized MEG time series were transformed into event sequences using amplitude thresholding. We first fit univariate Hawkes models with exponential kernels to estimate spectral radius as a global connectivity measure for group comparison. Next, multivariate Hawkes models were applied to event sequences derived from 72 ROIs across 100 time points, integrating graph-theoretic features such as betweenness to refine the representation of functional interactions. Exponential kernels were used to model the directed temporal dependencies between ROIs. To enhance flexibility and capture more complex excitation patterns, we additionally employed piecewise-defined kernel functions as a general nonparametric alternative.

Given the high dimensionality of the multivariate model, we additionally applied a block-structured dictionary approach with LASSO regularization (Tibshirani, 1996) to reduce parameter complexity and enhance interpretability.

Time-rescaling residuals, QQ-plots, and Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistics collectively confirmed that Hawkes-derived intensities captured the observed event timing structure in both patient and control groups.

Results

Across LT, RT, and HC groups, Hawkes modeling revealed systematic differences in excitation dynamics. Epilepsy groups showed increased recurrent excitation within temporal and limbic networks, reflecting pathological hyperexcitability. Distinct hemispheric patterns emerged: LT patients exhibited stronger ipsilateral-to-contralateral propagation along left temporal pathways, whereas RT patients showed the opposite pattern. In contrast, healthy controls displayed lower long-range excitation and more balanced excitation–inhibition dynamics, consistent with intact regulatory control.

Conclusion

The Hawkes framework uncovered subtle, directed, and temporally specific dependencies that conventional windowed DFC methods fail to detect. These results highlight the value of Hawkes process modeling for characterizing pathological network dynamics in epilepsy and provide insight into group-specific differences in excitation, inhibition, hemispheric asymmetry, and temporal network evolution.
