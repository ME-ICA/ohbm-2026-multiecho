Introduction

The hippocampus is one of the most complex structures in the human brain, playing a vital role in memory, navigation, and is one of the most vulnerable loci of neurological diseases. Its organization comprises intricate laminar architecture, subfields, and neural circuits at the micron scale. While MRI provides nondestructive, 3D visualization of the brain, its resolution is far behind the scale that neuroscientists typically look for in histology. Consequently, there is an urgent need to bridge the substantial resolution gap between in vivo MRI at the macroscale and histology at the microscale, aiming for a "3D virtual histology" that combines the benefits of both modalities.

Ex vivo MRI at ultrahigh-field (UHF, ≥7 T) has emerged as a powerful tool for bridging this gap, benefiting from the near-linear gain in signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) with field strength.1 Diffusion MRI (dMRI) is uniquely valuable for mapping microstructure yet challenging to push to microscopic scales due to intrinsic signal attenuation. Under UHF, the resolution of ex vivo dMRI has largely plateaued around the 100 µm isotropic.2 Nevertheless, it remains insufficient for resolving cellular-scale microstructures. For instance, the soma of a human pyramidal neuron is approximately 10–50 μm, and the entire granule cell layer in the hippocampus is as thin as ~60 μm.3 Therefore, a further enhanced resolution approaching a cellular scale would be ideal for building MRI towards virtual histology.

Here, we present a specialized Diffusion MR Microscopy (dMRM) framework that achieves an unprecedented 50 µm resolution in the ex vivo human hippocampus using a 14.1 Tesla scanner. At a resolution that approaches the size of individual neuronal cells, we were able to directly correlate the dMRM-based microstructural maps and tractography with histopathology. This work establishes 50 µm ex vivo dMRM as a powerful tool for 3D volumetric virtual histopathology of the human brain and neurodegenerative disorders.

Methods

Brain tissue preparation: Human brain tissue was obtained from the National Health and Disease Human Brain Resource Center, Medical College of Zhejiang University, Hangzhou, China. All participants signed an informed consent, an anatomical gift act for organ donation, and a repository consent to allow the data to be shared. Diagnosis of AD was confirmed at autopsy, and the control was selected from cases with no vascular or other neurological complications. The study included one control specimen (female, 50 years) without known brain pathology and one Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) specimen (male, 91 years). For this study, the mid-hippocampal region (approx. 5 mm thick) was carefully dissected. To enhance MR signal relaxation properties and SNR at UHF, specimens were incubated in phosphate-buffered saline containing 1 mM gadolinium-based contrast agent for 7 days at 4°C. Prior to scanning, samples were transferred to 50 mL polypropylene centrifuge tubes and immersed in Fomblin, a perfluoropolyether, to prevent tissue dehydration and eliminate magnetic susceptibility artifacts at the tissue boundaries.

UHF dMRI Acquisition: Ex vivo human hippocampal specimens were imaged on a 14.1T Bruker Avance III HD scanner (Fig. 1). The system was equipped with a high-performance Micro 2.5 gradient system, capable of a maximum gradient strength of 1.5 T/m and a slew rate of 15,000 T/m/s, utilizing a 30 mm diameter volume transceiver coil. We utilized a 3D echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence with the following parameters: TR/TE=1000/24 ms, b=3000 s/mm², 20 diffusion directions, 2 additional b0 volumes, and a resolution of 50×50×100 µm³ (interpolated to 50 µm isotropic). Scan time was approximately 78 hours per sample.

MRI Data Processing: Data were processed using MRtrix3, including denoising and eddy current correction. Fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity (MD), radial diffusivity (RD), axial diffusivity (AD), and color-coded FA (colorFA) maps were computed using diffusion tensor reconstruction. Moreover, fiber orientation density (FOD) was obtained by the constrained spherical deconvolution (CSD) algorithm. Probabilistic tractography was performed using a tensor-based algorithm with the following parameters: one seed per voxel with random positioning, a FA threshold of 0.02, an angular threshold of 60°, and a step size equal to 1/10 of the voxel size (5 µm for a 50 µm resolution). The minimum and maximum tract length were set to 0.1 mm (twice the voxel size) and 20 mm, respectively. Super-resolution Tract Density Imaging (TDI) maps were generated at 10 µm resolution.

Histopathology and Validation: Following MRI acquisition, the hippocampal blocks were sectioned and stained for Neurofilament (NF), Microtubule-associated protein 2 (MAP2), hyperphosphorylated tau (p-tau) and amyloid-β (Aβ). To validate dMRI orientations, the structural orientation in MAP2 and NF sections was analyzed by structure tensor analysis using ImageJ.4 Briefly, the original immunohistochemical image was first decomposed into three channels using the color deconvolution tool: the first channel represented hematoxylin-stained nuclei, the second channel contained the positive signal of interest (e.g., MAP2-labeled dendrites), and the third channel corresponded to the background. Subsequently, structure tensor analysis was performed on the second channel using the OrientationJ plugin, which generated an HSB-formatted map of local fiber orientation, where hue corresponds to orientation, saturation to anisotropy, and brightness to the original signal intensity.

Results

Diffusion MR microscopy revealed layer-specific microstructural organization of the human hippocampus and detected disrupted laminar architecture in AD. dMRM successfully delineated six distinct hippocampal layers: the alveus (Alv), pyramidal cell layer (PCL), stratum radiatum (SR), stratum lacunosum/moleculare (SL/M), molecular layer (ML), granule cell layer (GCL), results which were validated by hematoxylin staining (Fig. 2A). At an isotropic resolution of 50 µm, layer boundaries were sharply defined, whereas when we downsampled the data to 100 µm isotropic resolution, the layers appeared blurred, e.g., the boundary between the SR and SL/M, and the thinnest GCL almost disappeared, emphasizing the strength of ultra-high resolution. When the resolution was further reduced, most layers became indistinguishable, leaving only a coarse gray–white matter contrast. Furthermore, in the AD hippocampus, the SR and SL/M layers appeared disrupted on the MD map with discontinuities in the SR and SL/M layers (as indicated by the triangle in Fig. 2A), and the overall inter-layer contrast was markedly reduced. Corresponding NF staining confirmed these structural alterations. Additionally, the PCL in CA1 was notably thinner in the AD sample than that in the control sample, as validated by MAP2 dendritic staining.

Tractography and immunohistochemistry revealed detailed hippocampal neuronal projections. Based on the 50 µm tractography results, we generated TDI maps at 10 µm isotropic resolution, achieving a level of detail comparable to histology (Fig. 2B). The resulting TDI closely matched the color-coded orientation maps derived from structure-tensor analysis of MAP2 (stains for dendrites). In the CA and ML regions, radially oriented dendrites were consistently observed in TDI and MAP2 staining. Beyond 2D visualization, we successfully reconstructed the classic tri-synaptic hippocampal circuit in 3D. The tri-synaptic pathway consists of: (1) the perforant path (PP) from the entorhinal cortex to the DG, (2) mossy fibers projecting from the DG to CA3, and (3) Schaffer collaterals extending from CA3 to CA1. Specifically, the PP entered the hippocampus tangentially and traversed the CA region, forming synaptic connections with dendrites in the SL/M layer and propagating tangentially along this layer toward CA3. In Fig. 2C, densely converging tangential fibers were clearly observed. The FOD reconstruction revealed dominant tangential orientations, which were confirmed by NF staining and its color coded maps. The mossy fiber pathway was distinctly visible, projecting from the DG to CA3 with excellent agreement between tractography and NF staining results. The Schaffer collaterals originated from CA3 pyramidal neuron axons and projected to CA1, connecting with dendrites in the SR layer. The tangential tracts within the SR-layer corresponding to Schaffer collaterals were clearly resolved, which was not visualized in previous ex vivo dMRI studies.1

Conclusion

In this study, we achieved an unprecedented dMRM resolution of 50 µm in the ex vivo human hippocampus using a 14.1 T ultrahigh-field scanner, offering a level of detail not previously reported. While previous attempts to achieve 3D histology relied on co-registering serial histological sections to MRI volumes—a labor-intensive process prone to geometric distortions from tissue cutting and mounting—dMRM provides an intrinsically 3D, distortion-free representation that preserves spatial continuity.5 Importantly, immunohistochemical validation confirmed that these ultrahigh-resolution dMRM maps faithfully reflected underlying neural architecture. Consequently, our work establishes a robust technological framework for “3D Virtual Histology,” bridging the long-standing gap between macroscopic MRI and microscopic histology with a continuous, nondestructive capability for characterization of tissue microstructure, neuronal projections, and pathology.

At the microscopic scale, dMRM characterized the underlying cellular environment with high specificity. We observed that while neuron- and dendrite-dense regions (e.g., PCL and ML) generally exhibited higher MD compared to glial/myelin-rich regions (e.g., alveus), the GCL was a notable exception. Despite being densely populated with cell bodies like the PCL, the GCL displayed significantly lower MD. This discrepancy reflects distinct cellular morphologies: pyramidal neurons in the PCL are approximately ten times larger than granule cells, and the GCL exhibits a much higher packing density, creating a highly restricted diffusion environment. 

At the connectivity level, dMRM demonstrated superior potential in resolving complex 3D circuits difficult to capture with conventional histology. We successfully visualized the perforant path forming synaptic relays in the SL/M layer and the Schaffer collaterals traversing the SR layer—pathways historically challenging to resolve in ex vivo dMRI. These 3D projections were validated by super-resolution TDI maps, which produced microstructural patterns closely mirroring MAP2 immunohistochemistry.

This study has several limitations. First, as ex vivo tissue properties differ from in vivo conditions due to fixation, future work is needed to establish transfer functions to clinical MRI. Second, this feasibility study was limited to a mid-hippocampal block from one control and one AD specimen; larger cohorts covering more brain regions are requisite for generalization. Third, fiber-tracking accuracy was constrained by the directional resolution (n=20) due to scan time limits. Finally, while 2D histology served as the gold standard, it inherently truncates 3D fibers, suggesting that future validation should integrate 3D polarized light imaging or 3D microscopy. Looking ahead, combining ultrahigh-resolution multimodal MRI with deep learning offers the potential to directly predict histological maps, ultimately pushing toward high-resolution in vivo “virtual histology” of the human brain.
