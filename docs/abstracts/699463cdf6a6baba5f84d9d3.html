Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) and Parkinson’s disease (PD) disrupt the central nervous system. Epidural electrical stimulation (EES) coupled with rehabilitation has shown potential to improve function in individuals with SCI 1 and PD2.

A major challenge for EES is determining the optimal implantation site along the spinal cord due to substantial inter-individual anatomical variability 1. The spinal cord is segmentally organized into spinal levels. The size and localization of spinal levels expose increasing variability in the caudal direction in humans 1,3. Accurate, personalized localization remains essential for EES and clinical rehabilitation 1,2.

Resting-state spinal cord functional MRI captures spontaneous BOLD fluctuations related to neuronal activity. Data-driven methods such as ICA and Hierarchical Clustering reveal intrinsic organization but lack temporal resolution. Innovation-driven co-Activation Patterns (iCAPs) model transient co-activations. Their spinal variant, SPiCiCAP, aligns with cervical 4 and lumbosacral 5 spinal levels in healthy participants, suggesting the potential to identify functional organization corresponding to anatomical boundaries.

In this work, we investigate through the SPiCiCAP framework resting-state fMRI of individuals with complete SCI (N=3;lumbosacral region), incomplete SCI (N=2;cervical, N=1;thoracic and N=1;lumbosacral regions), and PD (N=1;lumbosacral region). We identify personalized co-activation patterns, with spinal-level alignment, probing that residual spinal activity persists independently of brain input in individuals with complete SCI. We assess the potential of SPiCiCAPs for functional spinal level mapping and personalized therapeutic strategies.

Methods

Participants

We studied 7 individuals with SCI (clinical trials NCT06845904, NCT05665998, NCT05111093, NCT06243952) and 1 participant with PD (clinical trial NCT06295614). Lumbosacral spinal cord MRI was acquired for 3 participants with chronic complete SCI, for 1 participant with chronic incomplete SCI and 1 participant with PD. Cervical spinal cord MRI was acquired for 2 participants with chronic incomplete SCI. Lower thoracic spinal cord MRI was acquired for 1 participant with chronic incomplete SCI. Demographic and lesion characteristics were documented for each participant.

Data Acquisition and Pre-Processing

Imaging was performed on a 3T Siemens PrismaFit scanner using multi-channel body, spine, head, and neck coils, depending on the spinal region. High-resolution structural images were acquired using T2-weighted SPACE and TrueFISP sequences for accurate spinal anatomy visualization 1. Functional images were obtained using T2*-weighted echo-planar imaging with selective field-of-view (ZOOMit), covering lumbar, lower thoracic, or cervical regions 1,4,5. Imaging parameters were adapted to avoid aliasing and optimize coverage of the spinal cord enlargements. Manual shimming was performed to maximize field homogeneity. Resting-state fMRI (15 min, 360 volumes) was acquired with eyes open.

Preprocessing included slice-timing correction, slice-wise motion correction, and multi-modal denoising using motion, cerebrospinal fluid, and physiological regressors when available 1,4,5. Spatial smoothing was applied anisotropically along the spinal cord to improve signal-to-noise ratio 1,4,5. Images were registered to the PAM50 template using subject-specific structural landmarks for group analysis; subject-level analyses remained in native space 4,5.

Spinal Cord Innovation-Driven Co-Activation Patterns (SPiCiCAP)

SPiCiCAPs identify patterns of transient activity in spinal cord fMRI. Denoised time courses were deconvolved using Total Activation to obtain innovation signals. Significant voxels exceeding phase-randomized surrogate thresholds were retained and temporally clustered using K-means to identify spatially coherent patterns 4,5. Low- and high-granularity decompositions (components of components K) were generated based on consensus clustering, with higher K capturing finer rostrocaudal, dorsoventral, and left-right organization.

Data Analysis

Spatial similarity between SPiCiCAPs and spinal levels was assessed using Dice or cosine similarity with anatomically-defined masks 1. Laterality indices quantified left-right and dorsoventral asymmetry. Cluster stability was evaluated through consensus across subsamples, temporal halves of fMRI, and varying K values 4,5. Temporal dynamics were reconstructed by back-projecting significant innovation frames onto SPiCiCAP spatial maps, thresholded to identify active and de-active timepoints 4. Temporal overlap and anti-coupling between SPiCiCAPs were assessed using Jaccard similarity.

Exploratory Spinal Level Identification

To explore finer spinal segmentation, SPiCiCAPs were optimized to match high-confidence spinal levels from structural MRI 4,5. Granularity was reduced, and suprathreshold contiguous clusters along the rostrocaudal axis were merged based on Dice similarity, asymmetric containment, and stability. Centers of mass and boundaries between clusters provided a functional segmentation, integrating structural and functional information to reconstruct spinal levels below complete lesions.

Results

We applied the SPiCiCAP framework to resting-state fMRI from individuals with disrupted CNS function.

Complete SCI - Lumbosacral Imaging

Two participants with complete thoracic injuries showed high tSNR after preprocessing (156.30 and 70.18 after spatial smoothing), yielding quality signals. A third participant was excluded due to severe metal artifacts (final tSNR=8.16). Consensus clustering identified K=6 as most robust for both participants. SPiCiCAPs demonstrated high stability (>0.6, most ~0.8) across resampling iterations and were distributed rostrocaudally in highly localized patterns thresholded at |Z|>2.3. Dice coefficients confirmed close alignment with anatomically-defined spinal levels, indicating preserved level-specific spontaneous activity despite complete thoracic disruption.

Temporal stability analysis showed SPiCiCAPs were reproducible across recording halves. Stability across K values revealed certain regions (S3-S5, T12 in one participant; L3, L4, L5-S1 in another) were consistently detected. Temporal dynamics showed moderate coupling via Jaccard distance, with patterns at the same or adjacent levels exhibiting higher overlap.

Fine-grained decomposition (K=24) revealed robust clusters with high anatomical specificity. Laterality indices quantified asymmetry: strongly dorsal patterns (DV=0.87), ventral (DV=-0.89), left-lateralized (LR=1.00), and right-lateralized (LR=-0.92) activity demonstrated spatially specialized organization. Some patterns spanned multiple distant levels, suggesting co-activation networks.

Incomplete SCI - Cervical Imaging

Two participants demonstrated high tSNR (107.62 and 111.27 after smoothing) despite metal in one case. Consensus clustering identified K=7 and K=5, respectively, with high cluster stability (>0.6). SPiCiCAPs showed clear rostrocaudal distribution (C5-T3 and C8-T4) aligned with specific segments at |Z|>2.3. Dice coefficients confirmed spatial overlap with anatomically-defined levels.

PD - Lumbosacral Imaging

Despite neurodegenerative pathology, tSNR reached 66.40 after preprocessing. At K=6, SPiCiCAPs displayed clear rostrocaudal organization with moderately high consensus stability. Dice coefficients showed strong correspondence for specific lumbar segments (L2, L4), indicating preserved intrinsic functional organization despite Parkinsonian degeneration.

Incomplete SCI - Lower Thoracic Imaging

Despite nearby sources of cardiac/respiratory noise, tSNR increased to 74.79 after denoising. At K=5, SPiCiCAPs demonstrated robust rostrocaudal distribution that matched segmental organization (T9-L1), with very high cluster stability (>0.8). Patterns showed strong alignment with lower thoracic levels at |Z|>2.3.

Incomplete SCI - Lumbosacral Imaging

tSNR reached 100.49 after preprocessing. At K=5, SPiCiCAPs showed high stability (>0.8) with clear rostrocaudal organization, supporting that patterns reflect underlying segmental anatomy.

Group Analysis

Normalized analysis of complete SCI participants imaged on the lumbosacral region (n=2) revealed K=8 as optimal, with SPiCiCAPs spanning T11-S5. For incomplete SCI participants imaged on the cervical region (n=2), K=5 showed rostrocaudal organization aligned with C8-T3 levels.

Exploratory Spinal Level Identification

At K=28, a merging workflow identified functional segmentation in a single complete SCI participant, revealing distinct segments within the sacral region, though some neighboring spinal levels seem to remain merged.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this is the first characterization of spontaneous spinal cord activity in SCI (complete and incomplete) populations across different spinal regions.

Applying the SPiCiCAP framework to resting-state BOLD signals below complete SCI demonstrates that rostrocaudally structured activity, closely resembling spinal level organization, occurs independently of the brain. This suggests that spontaneous activity is encoded and maintained by local spinal circuits, potentially involving loops through sensory afferents that engage interneurons, without supraspinal involvement. These local circuits, preserved after complete SCI, can be captured by SPiCiCAPs as organized functional structures revolving around spinal levels, offering possibilities for clinical application in personalized therapeutic strategies.

The optimal number of clusters (K) does not always match the exact number of spinal levels in the fMRI field-of-view and varies between individuals. Larger spinal levels are well represented by SPiCiCAPs, whereas smaller ones are difficult to resolve. In the lumbar cord, K=6 is often optimal and aligns with observed lumbar levels, but sacral levels cannot be reliably separated due to their small size and limited fMRI spatial resolution. Even at high granularity, sacral levels remain difficult to discriminate. In cervical regions, optimal K varied between participants but approximated observed level counts. This inter-individual variability reinforces the need to determine K at the individual level.

Some SPiCiCAPs span two adjacent levels, an effect that may reflect genuine individual variability revealed by participant-wise analysis. Additionally, spinal reorganization after chronic injury must be considered, as the spinal cord is plastic, and long-term brain disconnection could lead to functional reconfiguration. Our structural spinal-level definitions based on dorsal root entry zones on structural MRI may introduce boundary uncertainty and bias, as actual anatomical borders between levels are not always sharply defined. This underscores the need for data-driven methods combining functional and structural information.

The Dice coefficient measuring spatial overlap between SPiCiCAPs (thresholded at  |Z|>2.3, =0.05) and anatomical masks may be conservative in our dataset. The thresholding substantially reduces apparent SPiCiCAP size, and our smaller dataset (360 volumes per participant) compared to healthy group studies makes reproducibility more challenging. Nevertheless, SPiCiCAPs demonstrated high stability (>0.6, often >0.8) across resampling iterations and showed correspondence with anatomically-defined levels, particularly for larger spinal levels.

Temporal analysis revealed that SPiCiCAPs at the same spinal level exhibit greater temporal overlap via Jaccard distance, extending observations from healthy cohorts to the complete-SCI population at the individual level. This supports that spontaneous activity organization is strongly shaped by underlying anatomy, even in the absence of brain input. At high granularity (K=24), SPiCiCAPs showed spatial segregation within single levels with distinct laterality patterns, reflecting previously reported spatial organization in the healthy population 4,5.

We explored a framework integrating structural and functional information to localize spinal levels. At K=28, merging SPiCiCAPs based on spatial similarity and stability showed promise in identifying functional segmentation, particularly in challenging, smaller sacral regions.

Spinal cord resting-state fMRI provides a window into intrinsic spontaneous cord (cervical, thoracic, and lumbosacral) activity that persists independently of supraspinal input across complete SCI, incomplete SCI, and PD populations, with potential clinical applications including spinal-level identification for EES implantation guidance.
