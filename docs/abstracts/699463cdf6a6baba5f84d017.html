Introduction

Visual mental imagery refers to the feat of our mind to voluntarily create sensory-like visual information in the “mind’s eye” without external stimulation. The neural underpinnings of visual mental imagery resemble those involved in visual perception of external stimuli including domain-specific regions.1

Recent work by Liu et al. showed that domain-specific higher visual areas were similarly active in mental imagery and perception of faces and places.2,3 Moreover, the fusiform imagery node (FIN) was proposed as a domain-general hub of imagery. The FIN was associated with imagery vividness, and aphantasic individuals showed reduced FIN connectivity with frontal regions.2

The findings raise the possibility that imagery vividness depends on the integration of signals from the frontal cortex to the FIN, which may then activate domain-specific areas like the fusiform face area (FFA) & parahippocampal place area (PPA) via feedback pathways.

To investigate these proposed feedback mechanisms, ultra-high field fMRI with sub-millimeter resolution can be used. It enables the distinction of feedforward and feedback information flow, as activation can be localized within different cortical depths. Deep and superficial laminar compartments primarily reflect feedback information, while middle layers are associated with feedforward signals.4

Carricarte et al. used this approach to investigate mental imagery and perception in FFA and PPA using a faces-places task similar to the classic O’Craven and Kanwisher study.3,5 They observed stronger activation in deep cortical layers of the domain-specific regions in imagery compared to perception, consistent with the idea of enhanced feedback signals.

However, despite its potential role as a hub of mental imagery, the FIN has not yet been examined at the laminar level. Here, we aim to bridge these findings by comparing laminar activation profiles during imagery and perception in the domain-specific FFA and PPA, and the domain-general FIN.

Methods

Three subjects (1 female, mean age = 27 years, SD = 1.3) were recruited and provided informed consent, with ongoing data collection targeting a minimum of 10 participants.

For each subject, we obtained anatomical (MP2RAGE, 0.7mm isotropic) and functional data using a Siemens 7T Magnetom Plus MRI scanner.  Participants completed 2 tasks while functional data was collected – a functional localizer and a mental imagery task of faces and places.

In the session, 2 runs of a functional localizer containing faces, places and objects were conducted at 1mm isotropic resolution (EPI BOLD, AP, R3MB2, TR=2, TE=23.6, pF6/8, 56 slices, TA=6:20), as well as 6 runs of the main mental imagery task at 0.8mm (EPI BOLD, AP, R3MB2, TR=2.5, TE=28.6, pF6/8, 62 slices, TA=7:20).

Each run of the main task comprised images of 6 famous faces and 6 famous places.  Each trial had a vision and imagery component. First, a given face or place was shown for 3 seconds, then, following a 7.5-12.5s variable interstimulus interval, the fixation cross turned to red indicating the start of the 8s imagery component of the trial where subjects imagined the previously shown face or place. An unchanging black border was present for the entirety of each run which framed the images when shown and indicated where subjects should project their mental images when imagining. The fixation cross was also present for the entirety of each run and was black except for when an image was either being shown or imagined. Therefore, the visual input was matched between the vision and imagery components except that the face or place image was absent and to be imagined during the imagery component (Figure 1C).

The data was preprocessed using AFNI, FreeSurfer and BrainVoyager. The functional localizer was used to define the FFA and PPA as regions of interest with a conjunction analysis, identifying voxels that responded selectively to faces for the FFA or places for the PPA. Only statistically significant clusters at the threshold of p<.005 were selected that were located in the appropriate anatomical locations (i.e. Fusiform/Parahippocampal). To define the FIN, we applied a conjunction of the activation of all perception and imagery conditions of the imagery task and again selected on the basis of anatomy informed by prior work.2

FreeSurfer and BrainVoyager were used to segment grey matter, white matter and CSF, using anatomical data upsampled to 0.35mm isotropic resolution. Refinements were done manually around the regions of interest in ITK-SNAP. Seven cortical layers and columns spanning on average 900 ± 1100 voxels were generated with LayNii (Figure 1A). The ROIs were transformed into masks including all columns that contained 30% of the given binary ROI mask. Laminar profiles were extracted with the resulting masks and beta maps of all face and place perception and imagery conditions of the main task (Figure 1B).

In addition, to support further investigation of the layer activity, we also generated a 3 cortical layers parcellation. Within subjects, for each layer and ROI, we estimated the single trial responses for each condition using the average ROI-layer time course. First, the within-subject selectivity of FFA and PPA, per layer, for face and place imagery was tested with a 2-sample t test, with p-values derived via null distributions (10,000 permutations). Second, we examined the functional connectivity, defined as the beta series correlations, between the layers of the FIN to the layers of the FFA and PPA. Significance was determined within subjects by comparing to the null based on repeating the correlations with shuffled orders (10,000 permutations).

Results

We replicated the previous findings of a clear distinction of faces and places in FFA and PPA across both perception and mental imagery trials. The laminar profiles underline this differentiation. Within the PPA, the mean signal intensity of mental imagery of places is consistently stronger than both the imagery and perception of faces, while the FFA reflects a similar trend, though less pronounced (Figure 1B). While the presented laminar profiles are uncorrected for the draining-vein effect, they reveal a trend for the profiles in the imagery condition to not rise as steeply towards the pial surface as the profiles for the perception condition. The FIN’s laminar profiles do not differ between the conditions.

We confirm the domain selectivity of imagery in a single trial analysis with significance testing: the FFA is significantly selective for imagery of faces across all layers (subjs 1&3), and the PPA is significantly selective for imagery of places in layers 2 and 3 (subjs 1&3) as well as in layer 1 in one of the subjects. This domain-specific selectivity was greatest is layer 3 in both FFA and PPA (observable for all subjects in PPA, significant for subj. 1 & 3 in FFA and PPA) (Figure 2A).

Moreover, an area likely corresponding to the FIN was defined in all subjects, demonstrating domain-general activity across all conditions and strong imagery response. Preliminary correlations suggest that the FIN has preferential correlations with FFA and PPA during imagery of faces and places, respectively (Figure 2B, C). Considering the domain specific matrices (face in FFA, place in PPA), the correlation between layer 3 and FIN-layer 3 was significant for both FFA and PPA. However, the overall pattern of significant correlations across all layers differed between FFA and PPA.

Conclusion

Overall, the finding that faces and places are distinguishable in imagery and perception was replicated at ultra-high resolution, and the difference was reflected in the laminar profiles of FFA and PPA. The selectivity of FFA and PPA in face and place imagery was highest in the superficial layer. While this is somewhat inconsistent with prior work 5, it is nevertheless consistent with the feedback signals expected during imagery which are thought to target both deep and superficial layers. Two differences should be highlighted, however. First, we examined the relative contribution of faces versus places (rather than versus perception) and also we have not applied corrections for the draining vein bias. Future work will examine this in greater detail.

The FIN’s engagement in both imagery and perception across domains was shown here as well, in line with previous findings.2 We attempted to bridge this finding with the layer profile findings during imagery in domain-specific areas by correlating each layer of the FIN with each layer of the FFA and PPA. Our results suggest that while the FIN may be domain-general, it's connectivity to other up-stream areas appears to be domain-specific, with a stronger connectivity to FFA during imagery of faces and to PPA during imagery of places. The connectivity to high visual areas as well as the frontal cortex will be the focus of future work to explore the relation of the FIN to mental imagery vividness.

Although preliminary with n=3, these observations provide a framework for understanding mental imagery on a laminar level, with a total planned study enrollment of 10 subjects.  Despite the challenges of ultra-high field fMRI, the signal here was strong enough to differentiate the conditions within FFA and PPA on a laminar level without the usage of any denoising steps or modeling of the draining vein bias.
