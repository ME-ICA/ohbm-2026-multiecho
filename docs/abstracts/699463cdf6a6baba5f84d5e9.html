Introduction

While there is growing interest in proactive brain health monitoring using objective digital biomarkers, the substantial within- and between-individual variability in brain activity poses a major challenge.1 This variability complicates the separation of stable, trait-level signatures of brain health from transient, context-dependent fluctuations. Most existing analytical frameworks mitigate this challenge by averaging or otherwise removing temporal variability across both short and long timescales, potentially discarding information that is critical for understanding individual functional brain health. Moreover, characterizing high-dimensional brain data as it evolves over time remains difficult, particularly when attempting to link short-timescale neural dynamics to inter-individual differences in behavior.2 These challenges contribute to the persistent disconnect between observable behavioral characteristics, including clinical symptoms, and the underlying evolution of brain activity measured by neuroimaging.

Electroencephalography (EEG) offers a scalable and non-invasive method of capturing the temporal dynamics of large-scale brain networks. Here, we propose a probabilistic framework for modeling EEG dynamics as trajectories in a high-dimensional neural state space, where each dimension corresponds to a recording channel and each point represents the instantaneous scalp electric field arising from the summation of synchronous postsynaptic potentials across large neuronal populations. By integrating von Mises–Fisher (vMF) mixture modeling with Recurrence Quantification Analysis (RQA), our approach captures the full spatiotemporal structure of EEG dynamics. We apply this framework to a large dataset to test the hypothesis that latent profiles of continuous neural dynamics can disentangle stable subtypes of mental health vulnerability from transient neural fluctuations.

Methods

Dataset: Healthy Brain Network EEG dataset is used3 (N = 2,108, age: 5-22 years). Data are recorded using a 128-channel Magnetism-EGI system, across three paradigms: resting state, a surround suppression visual task, and movie watching for approximately 30 minutes. Psychological trait dimensions available per participant are derived by projecting parent-reported Child Behavior Checklist responses onto four latent factors (p-factor, attention, internalizing and externalizing). For preprocessing, we concatenated data across all tasks and preprocessed using a standardized pipeline. Signals were band-pass filtered (0.5–50 Hz), notch-filtered at 60 Hz, montage changed to a 32-channel BioSemi montage and re-referenced to the common average. Artifacts were removed using independent component analysis (ICA), retaining only components classified as “brain” by ICLabel.

vMF mixture modeling: We modeled the high dimensional EEG trajectory over time as samples from a mixture of von Mises-Fisher (vMF) distributions (Fig 1A). This framework refines traditional microstate analysis by replacing discrete, "winner-takes-all" labels with a probabilistic measure of state assignment. By capturing the uncertainty of state persistence, we model neural transitions as continuous and smooth rather than abrupt steps. To compare trajectories across the population, we needed a shared geometric framework. We therefore used the 7-map template from Tarailis et al. (2021) as fixed centroids to define the "attractors" of the state space. These canonical maps provide a stable common ground, while individual differences are captured by subject-specific concentration parameters and mixture weights. We recovered standard temporal metrics (duration, occurrence, coverage) by identifying "stable" periods where the posterior probability of a state exceeded 90%. Time points falling below this confidence threshold were analyzed as transitional states.

Recurrence quantification analysis (RQA): To capture non-linear temporal structure beyond simple state timing metrics, we augmented the mixture model with RQA. We constructed recurrence matrices based on the cosine similarity between points in the trajectory over time. From these matrices, we extracted four key dynamical metrics: Trapping Time (TT) and Laminarity (LAM) to quantify the stability of dwelling phases; and Determinism (DET) and Entropy (ENTR) to characterize the predictability and complexity of the system’s recurrent patterns.

Latent Profile Analysis (LPA): While the above vMF and RQA measures summarize the evolving multidimensional trajectory over time, we attempted to identify whether stable, trait-level subgroups exist within these dynamical patterns (Fig 1B). To isolate these neural traits from developmental effects, we first binned participants by age and sex (2 sex and 5 age bins) and z-scored all trajectory features within bins. We then constructed a feature vector for each participant comprising the vMF parameters (concentration, mixture weights, transitions, and state timing metrics) and RQA metrics. Using the StepMix library for LPA, a 4-profile solution was selected as the optimal model based on AIC and BIC criteria (Fig 2A), and these profiles were then validated against mental health dimensions to assess their behavioral relevance.

Statistical test: We validated the clinical relevance of the identified profiles using one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold  across four dimensional measures of mental health risk. Post-hoc Tukey pairwise comparisons were subsequently conducted to identify specific subgroup differences in scores.

Although the unsupervised clustering method uses all the dynamical features of the trajectory to identify the subgroups, we were interested in understanding the distinct neural mechanisms of the identified behavioral extremes. Hence, we compared the features of the trajectory between subgroups using a similar statistical approach as explained above.

Results

LPA identified four distinct subgroups of participants exhibiting unique EEG dynamical profiles. These subgroups showed no significant differences in age and sex distributions, confirming that the identified profiles reflect differences in neural dynamics rather than developmental stages (Fig 2B). Validation against behavioral dimensions revealed strong relevance for these unsupervised profiles. ANOVA showed a significant main effect for p-factor (F = 33.48, p<<0.001), attention (F = 148.3, p<<0.001), internalizing (F = 50.57, p<<0.001), and externalizing scores (F = 31.29, p<<0.001).

Subgroup 1 exhibited significantly lower scores compared to all other subgroups on attention (p<<0.001) and internalizing (p<0.001). This subgroup also showed significantly lower scores than subgroups 3 and 4 on the p-factor (p<<0.001), and significantly higher score than subgroups 3 and 4 on externalizing (p<<0.001).

Subgroup 2 showed significantly lower p-factor score and higher externalizing score compared to 3 and 4 (p<<0.001). This subgroup also exhibited higher attention score than subgroup 1 and lower attention score compared to subgroup 4. For Internalizing, this subgroup was lower than 3 and 4 and higher than 1 (p<0.001). 

Subgroup 3 showed significantly higher p-factor scores compared to subgroups 1 and 2 (p<<0.001), significantly higher attention scores than subgroup 1 (p<<0.001), and significantly higher externalizing symptoms relative to subgroups 1 and 2 (p<<0.001). Internalizing symptoms in subgroup 3 differed significantly from all other subgroups (p<0.001).

Lastly, Subgroup 4 exhibited the highest overall symptom score, with significantly higher p-factor scores than subgroups 1 and 2 (p<<0.001), significantly higher attention  and internalizing  scores compared to all other subgroups (p<<0.001). However, externalizing scores in subgroup 4 were significantly lower than those of subgroup 1 and 2 (p<<0.001). Figure 2C visualizes these behavioral dimensions per profile, where asterisks indicate the number of statistically significant pairwise differences for each profile.

In terms of EEG dynamic features, transition timing metrics (computed for states with <90% assignment confidence) differed significantly across subgroups (transition duration: F = 142.75; occurrence: F = 140.9; time coverage: F = 45.25; p<<0.001 for all). For RQA measures, significant subgroup effects were observed (ENTR: F = 88.77; LAM: F = 162.80; TT: F = 49.63; DET: F = 161.63, p<<0.001 for all). For mixture weights and concentration parameters, all microstates exhibited highly significant subgroup effects (weights: F = 61.5-236.2; concentration: F = 117.33-224.70; p<<0.001 for all). Lastly, state durations also showed significant subgroup differences for MS1–MS6 (F = 12.58-245.62, p<0.001).

Post hoc analysis on EEG measures (p<<0.001) showed that subgroup 1 was characterized by longer but sparse transitions compared to all other subgroups. This subgroup also showed significantly lower ENTR, LAM and DET compared to all other subgroups. Across microstate features, this subgroup showed lower mixture weights across all states and lower concentration parameters for states 1-3.

Subgroup 4 was distinguished by significantly higher rate of transition occurrence compared to other groups. This subgroup also showed significantly shorter dwell time and time coverage in microstates 4-6 compared to all other subgroups and higher mixture weights for microstates 1-3. 

Conclusion

Our results demonstrate that objective risk stratification can be achieved solely from the dynamical properties of EEG trajectories. By modeling the continuous, multidimensional evolution of neural activity, we identified latent “high-risk” and “low-risk” phenotypes. Importantly, these risk profiles were identified in a fully unsupervised and behaviorally blinded manner, without using any symptom information during model training.

Subgroup 4 emerged as a distinct high-risk phenotype, characterized by the greatest symptom severity across p-factor, attention, and internalizing domains, whereas subgroup 1 represented a low-risk phenotype, exhibiting the lowest symptom burden across these same dimensions.

The low-risk profile (subgroup 1) was characterized by lower determinism, laminarity, and entropy relative to age- and sex-matched peers, alongside reduced mixture weights and increased time spent in transitional periods. Together, these features suggest that cognitive and emotional resilience may depend on dynamic flexibility—the capacity of the neural system to remain fluid, spend time in transitions, and avoid rigid adherence to strong attractors or prototypical states. The coexistence of low determinism and low entropy is not contradictory. Determinism reflects the tendency of the system to revisit similar trajectories, whereas entropy quantifies the diversity of these repeated patterns. When both are low, the system rarely becomes trapped in repetitive trajectories, and when repetition does occur, the resulting patterns are simple and transient. In contrast, elevated determinism and laminarity are commonly interpreted as markers of hypersynchronous or rigid neural dynamics with reduced adaptability. Accordingly, the low-risk profile likely reflects a system that preserves a broad repertoire of accessible states without becoming confined to any single configuration.

In contrast, the high-risk profile (subgroup 4) was marked by elevated internalizing and attention deficit related symptoms, alongside pronounced imbalances in functional state utilization, assuming EEG microstates reflect distinct large-scale functional networks. This subgroup over-occupied microstates 1, 2, and 3 related to sensory processing, and arousal,4 while under-engaging networks associated with microstates 4, 5, and 6 associated with processing emotional information, executive functioning and default mode network,4 suggesting that symptom burden may arise from a rigid over-reliance on a restricted subset of neural configurations at the expense of others. This interpretation aligns with prior findings linking internalizing disorders, such as depression, to increased prevalence and duration of specific microstates that are often associated with default mode network activity reflecting difficulty disengaging from internally focused, self-referential processing.5 Similarly, attentional impairments have been associated with reduced flexibility in transitioning between resting-state networks, leading to prolonged occupancy of sensory-dominant states and insufficient engagement of task-positive configurations5.

Taken together, these findings suggest that an individual’s symptom burden can be assessed by characterizing how their brain activity evolves over time as measured by EEG. The ability to recover behaviorally meaningful risk phenotypes from EEG dynamics alone represents a novel advancement beyond traditional symptom-driven or case–control frameworks.

A primary limitation of this work is the cross-sectional design of the data, which prevents assessing how profiles evolve within individuals or predicting future symptom onset. Additionally, concatenating diverse paradigms (rest, movie, task) may obscure context-specific dynamics. Future longitudinal work is required to validate these latent profiles as stable, predictive biomarkers of mental health risk.
