Introduction

Maternal welfare is an important factor in predicting child neurodevelopment outcomes, and perinatal maternal depression (PMD) is one of the most common maternal risk factors affecting child brain development. Structural differences in sub-cortical regions, particularly the amygdala and hippocampus, have been reported in PMD-exposed children, although findings are inconsistent and sometimes sex-specific1,2. Most neuroimaging studies of PMD have been conducted in high-income countries (HICs), despite PMD prevalence in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) being approximately double that of high-income settings.

Ultra-low field (ULF) MRI offers a low-cost, scalable alternative to conventional high-field (HF) MRI, but its validity for infant brain volumetry and compatibility with infant image processing pipelines remains largely untested. This study aimed (1) to test the validity of ULF MRI in measuring volumes of sub-cortical regions associated with PMD exposure in infant brains and (2) to explore the effects of PMD on sub-cortical regional volumes of infants from a South African birth cohort.

Methods

Infants from Gugulethu, South Africa, were scanned during their first year of life (nsubjects = 296, nsessions = 625) as part of the Khula longitudinal birth cohort3. After expert visual quality control, a sample of (nsubjects = 277, nsessions = 514) remained, including (nsubjects = 79, nsessions = 85) sets of paired HF (3T Siemens Skyra) and ULF (64mT Hyperfine Swoop®) scans. Paired scans were acquired within ≤ 90 days of each other at approximate time points of 3 months, 6 months and 12 months. ULF data was first treated using the GAMBAS Image Quality Transfer tool for enhanced resolution, before both ULF and HF T2w scans were processed with the BIBSNet infant image segmentation network containerised within Flywheel4,5. 

Agreement between HF and ULF volumetric estimates of the amygdala, hippocampus, caudate nucleus, and putamen was assessed using Lin’s concordance correlation coefficient (CCC). Generalised-linear-hypothesis-tests (GLHT) were also run to analyse statistical deviation of HF-ULF relationships from the line of identity (X=Y) (figure 1a). To characterise the timing and magnitude of any deviations in agreement, proportional residuals between HF and ULF volumetric estimates were modelled as a function of age. In addition, systematic percentage differences between HF and ULF volumes were calculated at each time point for each sub-cortical region (figure 1b). Associations between PMD exposure (Edinburgh prenatal depression score (EPDS) >12) and regional brain volumes were examined using linear mixed-effects models, adjusted for age, sex, total brain volume, gestational age, alcohol exposure, and socioeconomic status, with interaction terms to test sex- and region-specific PMD effects. Models were run separately for the ULF and HF datasets.

Results

Agreement between HF and ULF MRI-derived volumetric estimates was strong across all regions (figure 2). In paired scans (n = 85), Pearson correlations ranged from r = 0.88–0.94 and Lin’s concordance correlation coefficients ranged from CCC = 0.82–0.93. Concordance was highest for the amygdala and putamen, whilst lower agreement was observed for the caudate nucleus, particularly in the left hemisphere (CCC = 0.82).

Generalised linear hypothesis testing demonstrated significant deviation from the line of identity for hippocampal, caudate, and amygdala volumes (all p < 0.001), indicating proportional bias between HF and ULF estimates. Putamen volumes showed weaker evidence of deviation, reaching significance only in the right hemisphere (p = 0.03). Age-stratified analyses of proportional error further characterised the direction of this bias. Caudate volumes transitioned from relative ULF overestimation at earlier time points (T1 = −2.74%) to increasing ULF underestimation by 12 months (T3 = +7.04%) (figure 1b). In contrast, hippocampal volumes showed little bias at earlier ages (T1 = -0.67, T2 = 0.02%), with a tendency towards ULF overestimation emerging at 12 months (T3 = −2.94%).

 In linear mixed-effects models, no significant main effects of PMD exposure or PMD × ROI interactions were observed in either HF or ULF datasets. Although significant main effects of sex and ROI × sex interactions were observed, there was no evidence that sex moderated the association between PMD exposure and regional brain volumes (PMD × sex and PMD × ROI × sex interactions: all p > 0.05).

Conclusion

Strong agreement between ULF and HF MRI supports the validity of ULF neuroimaging for estimating infant sub-cortical volumes relevant to PMD research. Infant image processing is complicated by movement artefacts, small brain regions, and rapid developmental changes including myelination. However, these results indicate that, with an appropriate image processing pipeline, ULF MRI can provide a viable measure of early brain development. Systematic divergences between HF and ULF estimates emerged at specific developmental time points, likely reflecting age-related changes in tissue contrast that may be differentially captured at ultra-low field strengths; such effects may be mitigated through region- and age-specific calibration in future analyses.

No evidence was found for altered sub-cortical volumetry associated with PMD exposure between 3–12 months of age. Models were fitted using the unpaired, fully powered ULF dataset, as the HF clinical sample was underpowered (PMD: nsubjects = 13, nsessions = 16). Although few studies have examined this specific age range, the null findings are consistent with the mixed literature across both earlier and later developmental stages.
