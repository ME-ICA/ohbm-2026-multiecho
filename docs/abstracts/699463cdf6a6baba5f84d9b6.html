Introduction

Optically pumped magnetometers (OP-MEGs) provide high spatial and temporal resolution and enable measurement of magnetoencephalography (MEG) signals directly at the scalp surface. In contrast to conventional cryogenic MEG (cryo-MEG) systems, in which sensors are fixed within a rigid helmet designed to fit approximately 95% of adult male head sizes, OP-MEG sensors can be positioned on the scalp. This on-scalp configuration increases signal-to-noise ratio, a benefit that is particularly important for children, as adult-sized cryo-MEG systems often place sensors more than 3 cm from the child’s scalp. OP-MEGs offer the advantage of being cryogen-free, potentially lowering operational costs and simplifying the setup compared to traditional cryo-MEG systems, which require superconducting materials and cryogenic cooling. Although several research sites have already begun using wearable OP-MEG systems in both children and adults with epilepsy (Feys et al., 2022; Hillebrand et al., 2023; Pedersen et al., 2022; Sequeiros & Hamandi, 2024; Vivekananda et al., 2020), the technology has not yet been systematically tested in larger groups.

HEDscan is a high-density on-scalp MEG system utilizing optically pumped magnetometer technology. We present results of an ongoing cross-validation study with patients with epilepsy. Patients undergoing routine cryo-MEG/EEG examination for their epilepsy were invited to stay an additional 1 hour to be rescanned with a HEDscan on-scalp OP-MEG/EEG. To date over 35 patients (adults and children) have been scanned with both scanners and simultaneously with 10/20 EEG. In addition, sensory-evoked activity was recorded and localized in the patients and a set of healthy controls. The goal of the study is to compare the data quality between the HEDscan OP-MEG and conventional cryo-MEG, using simultaneous EEG and MEG recordings. 

Methods

Patients underwent simultaneously EEG using a 10-20 montage and MEG resting-state recordings on a 248-channel cryogenic MEG system (4D Neuroimaging, San Diego, CA) and an on-scalp MEG system with 80+ sensors/160+ channels (HEDscan from FieldLine Medical, Louisville CO) (Alem eta al., 2023) for 30 minutes each. In most cases, both sets of scans were done on the same day with the same HPI coils and EEG electrodes. No panel coils were used during the OPM data collections because the fields in the MSR did not exceed the +/- 150 nT dynamic range of the HEDscan system. Consequently the HEDscan helmet could be swapped for the cryo-MEG with little disruption to the routine clinical cryo-MEG scans. Patients were taken out of the MSR after the cryo-MEG scanning and given a 15-minute break. The HEDscan smart helmet was attached to the end of the bed. The patient’s head with EEG and HPI still attached from the cryo-MEG was placed in the HEDscan smart helmet. Sensors were pushed in until they touched the scalp surface. Co-registration with the subject's anatomy was performed by digitizing the positions of five head-position indicator (HPI) coils with respect to three fiducials and localizing them with the cryo-MEG and OP-MEG respectively. The HPI coils were re-localized and digitized with respect to the OPM sensors. Since the OPM sensors were in direct contact with the scalp, head shape was inferred from the sensor locations aiding the co-registration process. The resting data from both scanners were filtered with a bandpass filter from 3 – 70 Hz or 20 – 70 and a notch filter at 60 Hz. The OP-MEG data was down sampled to 250 Hz to match the sample rate of the cryo-MEG scan. Bad segments containing muscle activity were marked, and a kurtosisbeamformer was calculated (Hall et al., 2018; Scott et al., 2016; Ukai et al., 2004). Thresholds in the volumetric images were used to identify the peak locations of high kurtosis, where virtual channels were computed. Peaks were marked for comparison with the EEG signals and the peaks were used to perform dipole fits at time points over a 30 ms window around the peak. These dipole fits were independent from the kurtosis peak outcomes and served to validate the kurtosis peaks. The dipole clusters were plotted and compared to the kurtosis thresholded peaks and the two scanners’ results compared against each other. The EEG was also independently marked by a neurologist. The identified peaks were averaged to form a single averaged EEG and MEG peaks for the cryo-MEG and this was repeated for the OP-MEG. EEG and MEG peaks underwent dipole fit (independently) and results compared. For the sensory mapping study, auditory, visual, somatosensory, and motor areas were localized both by performing dipole fits and an event-related beamformer (Jobst et al., 2018) for both scanners independently and compared.

Results

Results are presented from 3 patients. Figure 1 shows the topologies for patient A between a typical peak from the cryo-MEG, OP-MEG and EEG. The EEG is simultaneous with the OP-MEG. The on-scalp OP-MEG kurtosis beamformer are displayed in red/orange activity and an equivalent current dipole for a corresponding spike is shown as a red circle overlaid on the patient’s MRI. Three data segments are also shown to the right of these figures: the on-scalp OP-MEG data, the simultaneous EEG data, and the time course of the virtual electrode placed at the location of the maximum of the kurtosis.

Figure 2 shows two additional patients comparing the cyro-MEG and OP-MEG results. For patient B, the cryo-MEG kurtosis results are plotted overlaid with a cryo-MEG dipole fit of a typical spike above and below the OP-MEG kurtosis results are plotted overlaid with the same cryo-MEG dipole. To the right of these MRI’s, three data segments depicting the MEG data, the simultaneous EEG data and the time course of the virtual electrode placed at the location of the maximum kurtosis for a typical spike is plotted for each scanner. For patient C, we show a patient with a VNS, which adds noise to the recordings. However, with appropriate filtering, we see similar equivalencies between the cryo-MEG, OP-MEG and EEG results. As well, as a point of comparison, we show the event-related beamformer results of the somatosensory stimulation for the right hand. The filtered beamformer is sufficient to correct the additional noise. The spatial location is consistent with somatosensory activity. Independent analysis using 5 spatial separation projectors show good temporal morphology of the average time course and a consistent left lateralized topography.

Conclusion

Clear interictal spikes were recorded at the same time points and exhibited similar morphology between the EEG and OPM recordings, consistent with the equivalent EEG and cryogenic MEG results. There was also good agreement between the OPM and cryogenic MEG resting data spike locations.The kurtosis beamformer presents a convenient method for localization and identification of interictal activity and agreed well spatially with the dipole fits. The sensory study showed that the OPM HEDscan system localized sensory-evoked fields reliably and yielded similar data quality to cryogenic MEG systems even when VNS was present. The results of the sensory study agreed closely between both the OPM and cryogenic MEG recordings and were consistent with results found in literature. We continue to recruit and scan additional patients in our continued efforts to systematically validate the system.
