Introduction

Peripheral inflammation has been shown to trigger neuroinflammation through various mechanisms, such as disrupting the blood-brain barrier and activating neuroglial cells, which can lead to several neurological diseases, including depression (MDD). In fact, evidence indicates an association between elevated peripheral cytokines and depression—for example, increased levels of interleukin (IL)-6, IL-10, IL-12, IL-13, C-reactive protein (CRP), tumour necrosis factor (TNF)-α, along with decreased interferon (IFN)-γ.1 The state of neuroinflammation may impair neurotransmitters involved in mood regulation, manifesting as depression symptoms.2 

Neuroimaging signatures such as resting-state functional MRI (rsfMRI) metrics could serve as an additional gateway to understanding the relationship between neuroinflammation and depression. Previous research has demonstrated the associations of rsfMRI metrics with both depression and inflammation. Specifically, there is a reduction in local functional connectivity density (lFCD) in individuals with MDD compared to healthy controls.3 In turn, the amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF), a standard fMRI metric, was decreased in several regions following mild inflammation induced by vaccination.4 These associations may be explained by the fact that rsfMRI metrics depend on cerebrovascular reactivity, which could be affected by neuroinflammatory consequences, such as disruption of the blood-brain barrier (BBB). Therefore, our study aims to explore the relationship among rsfMRI metrics, inflammatory markers, and depression symptoms, potentially providing a more comprehensive understanding of how inflammation contributes to the functional changes underlying depressive symptoms. 

Methods

We employed the Canadian Biomarker Integration Network in Depression (CANBIND-1) dataset. The cohort data, including individuals with MDD (age = 35.5 ± 12.9) and healthy controls (age =33.4 ± 11.1), were summarized in Figure 1a. The peripheral inflammatory data includes 29 blood markers of cytokines, chemokines and growth factors. These blood markers were clustered into inflammatory modules using the weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) workflow, implemented in R. The neuroimaging markers were derived from rsfMRI data, including ALFF, local (lFCD), and global FCD (gFCD). ALFF was calculated as the square root of the power spectrum over the 0.01–0.1 Hz frequency range. lFCD and gFCD were determined by the number of voxel-wise connections with a correlation coefficient greater than 0.6, with other voxels in the local cluster or in the entire brain, respectively. Brain parcellation for each metric was performed using the multi-modal HCP-MMP1.0 atlas. The inflammatory-neuroimaging relationships were established through correlation analysis of these regional rsfMRI metrics with the inflammatory module “eigen-genes”, separated by sex. Significant correlations (FDR corrected, p < 0.05) were mapped onto the HCP atlas. Additionally, we conducted mediation analysis using partial least squares – structural equation modelling (PLS-SEM) to examine the mediating effect of inflammation on the relationship between rsfMRI metrics (of significantly correlated ROIs) and depression symptoms, indicated by overall MADRS scores (Figure 1c). 

Results

Based on the WGCNA analysis, we identified seven modules of inflammatory markers, with the Brown and Yellow modules showing a significant positive correlation with MADRS scores. These associations are mapped to the corresponding brain parcellations in Figure 2a, separated by sex. The female group demonstrates more inflammation-related rsfMRI associations compared to males. The Brown and Yellow modules (which do not contain any anti-inflammatory markers) are linked to decreased gFCD in the ventral visual regions. The turquoise module, which includes the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10, shows a notable association with increased ALFF in the parietal regions and lFCD in the cingulate cortex. The PLS-SEM mediation analysis (Figure 2b) reveals a significant negative relationship between gFCD and the pro-inflammation modules (β  = -0.452, p < 0.001), and a positive association between these pro-inflammation modules and the overall MADRS score (β  = 0.282, p < 0.01). There is a significant negative indirect effect of gFCD, mediated through the Brown and Yellow modules, on the overall MADRS score (β  = -0.11, p < 0.05), while the direct relationship between gFCD and MADRS score is not statistically significant (β  = 0.05, p = 0.3). Other rsfMRI metrics (ALFF and gFCD) do not demonstrate direct or indirect associations with MADRS, although they do show significant positive correlations with other inflammation modules, including Turquoise.  

Conclusion

Compared to males, females show stronger links between blood inflammatory markers and rsfMRI metrics. Specifically, we found that the Turquoise inflammatory module (which contains the anti-inflammatory IL-10) was associated with increased ALFF and local FCD in parietal and cingulate regions, which are parts of the default-mode network. However, these changes did not reflect in symptom severity. In contrast, the Brown and Yellow modules, which contain the pro-inflammatory  IL-9, IL-17, RANTES and TNF-a, were significantly linked to both decreased global FCD in ventral visual regions and higher depression severity (MADRS score). These markers have also been previously associated with MDD.5 Additionally, mediation analysis using PLS-SEM revealed a significant indirect pathway where reductions in gFCD in ventral visual networks contribute to depression severity, mainly through the mediation of the Brown and Yellow modules. Moreover, the lack of a direct significant correlation between gFCD and MADRS suggests that systemic pro-inflammatory status fully mediates the connection between these neuroimaging changes and depressive symptoms. Overall, these findings support the involvement of neuroinflammation in MDD symptoms, but also a unique link between inflammation and rsfMRI that is not immediately reflected in MDD symptoms. Future work will need to clarify the etiological implications of this mismatch.
