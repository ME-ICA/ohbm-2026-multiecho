Introduction

In social environments, we form expectations from minimal information that produce lasting effects on perception and behavior. While expectations in the human domain have been extensively studied via behavioral and indirect measures of neural activity, the temporal and anatomical precision of when and where expectations arise remains unclear. 

Using intracranial electroencephalography (iEEG), we investigate multimodal settings with narrative-based expectations and its impact on face perception, and explore the temporal and anatomical precision of when and where expectations arise. 

Recent work suggests broadband aperiodic dynamics, rather than narrow-band oscillations, may better capture contextual processes. Therefore, we tested whether similar dynamics underlie social expectations by examining high-frequency activity (HFA; 70-170 Hz) and aperiodic exponents (1/f slopes) during processing of positive, negative, and neutral face narratives.

Methods

  • Participants: 20 participants with epilepsy volunteered to participate in the study (9 female; 11 male; μage = 33.3, SDage = 11.78). All participants provided informed consent. The study protocol was approved by Stanford University Institutional Review Board (IRB), and the experiment was conducted in accordance with Stanford IRB's guidelines in each patient's own hospital room.
  • Experimental design: To investigate multimodal expectation modulation, we examined face processing and its dynamics as a function of prior narratives. For one trial, participants listened to short audio clips that succinctly described a face's characteristics ("audio"), then viewed the face for 1 second ("face") and provided subjective ratings ("rating"). Each participant completed 150 trials in total.
  • Epochs of interest: We primarily focused our analysis on three epochs of interest: 1) early audio epoch (0-500 ms from audio onset), 2) late audio epoch (-500-0 ms from audio offset), 3) face epoch (0-1000 ms from face image onset).
  • Preprocessing: Time series from each electrode were preprocessed with the following steps: (1) downsampled to 1000 Hz, (2) decomposed time-frequency spectra, (3) applied notch filter for 60 Hz and harmonics, (4) flagged outlier channels based on power spectral density, (5) applied common average referencing. For spectra data, we used log-spaced frequencies (1-256 Hz); for HFA, we used a narrow band of frequencies (70-170 Hz).
  • Aperiodic analysis: For aperiodic exponents, we parameterized aperiodic exponents and offsets using the FOOOF toolbox. We then epoched, smoothed, baseline-corrected, and removed outliers for analyses on aperiodic exponents.
  • Statistical approach: To identify which neural components are involved in expectancy modulation, we extracted two measures: (1) HFA and (2) aperiodic exponents, and statistically assessed the effect of narrative valence on face perception. We first clustered the temporal profile of each measure (HFA and 1/f) during each epoch of interest (early audio, late audio, face epoch) using non-negative matrix factorization (NMF). Next, we tested whether HFA and aperiodic exponents within a given epoch differed as a function of affective category. We corrected for multiple comparisons (i.e., accounting for electrodes and epochs within a patient).

Results

Result 1. HFA is not modulated by affective information, therefore, not showing evidence of expectancy modulation: There was no statistically significant modulation for HFA clusters (N.S.). The clusters, however, did replicate past studies, in which we found two audio clusters – early transient and late sustained profiles in the caudal and rostral zones of superior temporal gyrus respectively – as well as a transient face cluster in the fusiform gyrus and early visual cortex. 

Result 2. Aperiodic slopes during late audio and face epochs were statistically modulated by affect type (CCT-corrected p < .05); In other words, the broadband dynamics differed depending on affectFurther investigating the time-frequency spectrograms provide insight - positive vs. negative narratives have different profiles in high frequency activity during the late audio epoch; this affect modulation persists into the early stages of the face epoch (onset to 500 msec), where the absence of low frequency activity during the negative epochs are different from the positive conditions. 

In sum, we conclude that periodic exponents, not HFA, are sensitive to valence information; HFA tracks sensory-modality specific information, with corresponding anatomical substrates. Unlike HFA which tracks fast, sensory-specific responses, aperiodic exponents reflect slower, broadband dynamics suited to capturing sustained expectancy effects.

Conclusion

  • Our results shed light on affective modulation, captured by aperiodic broadband dynamics (slopes), effects potentially missed by traditional narrow band analyses. 
  • While none of the high frequency activity across different epochs were modulated by affect, we found that visual and auditory profiles have robust replicable findings.
  • This approach of broadband dynamics may explain why prior face perception studies found no valence effects (Müsch et al., 2014; Cortex), possibly due to primary focus on narrow band analyses. Our findings are in line with previous work where aperiodic components capture cue-based expectation effects, beyond those detected by oscillatory components (Weber et al., 2024; Nat. Comm).
  • Unlike transient sensory representations, expectation modulation may operate on a longer time scale, presumably involving coordination across different broadband, sustaining across a long duration.
