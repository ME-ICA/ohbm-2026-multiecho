Introduction

Much of the variance in resting-state functional connectivity (rsFC) can be captured by simple statistical models relying on spatial autocorrelation. Spatial decay models, for instance, predict pairwise connectivity as an exponentially decaying function of inter-regional distance and account for many properties of brain network topology.1 More recently, deep learning-based models leveraging the spatial distributions of gene expression have demonstrated improved performance in reconstructing brain-wide connectivity beyond statistical and linear approaches.2 Here, we ask which aspects of functional connectivity can be reconstructed from gene expression, testing whether simple attention-based models predict both short-range connections explained by spatial decay and residual long-range connections.

Methods

Resting-state fMRI from the Human Connectome Project Young Adult cohort (HCP-YA; n=1065; ages 22-35; 54% female) was processed with XCP-D and parcellated using the Schaefer 400 atlas supplemented with 56 subcortical structures.3,4 rsFC matrices were calculated for each individual, then averaged, yielding a single population-average rsFC matrix (FC-μ). To fit a spatial decay model for FC-μ, we first extracted region-wise centroid coordinates. Lateral MNI coordinates were defined in a single hemisphere to account for bilateral symmetry. For each region, a ‘focal’ decay curve was estimated, parameterized by SA-λ, defining the rate at which FC decreases exponentially with distance, and SA-∞, the asymptotic level to which FC decays (Fig. 1A). These parameters were learned on a per region ‘focal’ basis to capture realistic neuroanatomical variance in the spatial decay model. The full focal rsFC matrix (FC-focal) was subsequently generated by computing per edge connectivity strength as a function of pairwise distances (Fig. 2A). Residual connections not explained by spatial decay were isolated by subtracting FC-focal from FC-μ, yielding an FC-residual matrix. We then tested whether a previously proposed attention-based model (Fig. 1B) could reconstruct FC-focal and FC-residual from the regional distributions of gene expression.2 Per region population-average gene expression measures were extracted from the Allen Human Brain Atlas, processed with a stringent differential stability threshold, followed by subsetting to the 100 genes with the highest brain-wide variance.5 Predictive performance was evaluated over 40 train test splits on spatially distinct test sets.6 Models were refitted using a stringent, computationally efficient null spin test approach to evaluate the effect of spatial autocorrelation.2

Results

Attention-based models reconstruct connectivity patterns of held-out brain regions for both FC-focal (true r = 0.8±.08; null r = 0.52±.11) and FC-residual (true r= 0.48±.13; null r = 0.12±.08). While prediction performance is higher for the short range FC-focal connections, this performance is accompanied by a high null estimate (r ≈ 0.5), suggesting that spatial autocorrelation patterns in gene expression, even when spatially permuted, can predict short range connectivity patterns. For FC-residual, the null estimate is substantially lower (r ≈ 0.1), pointing towards the opposite trend that long range connections are not recoverable by spatial autocorrelation patterns that differ from that of the true brain. Alongside this result, models trained on the longer-range connections of FC-residual occasionally make strong predictions for shorter-range FC-focal connections that were residualized out (see Fig. 2 FC-residual). This ‘false positive’ trend suggests that the patterns learned from long-range connections may extend to some short-range connections. Combining predictions from FC-focal and FC-decay yields a global prediction of r = 0.62, in line with prior findings that simple attention-based models can recover brain-wide functional connectivity patterns from gene expression alone (Fig. 2).2

Conclusion

Our results suggest that multi-range connectivity can be decoded from the spatial distributions of gene expression across the brain. Using a robust attention-based model, we show that short-range connections are highly predictable from spatial autocorrelation patterns, whereas long-range connections may be encoded by more complex patterns specific to the brain's spatiomolecular organization. Although this study examines resting-state functional connectivity in healthy adults, it provides a foundation for predictive approaches relating gene expression to alternate connectivity targets, such as clinically relevant connectivity differences.
