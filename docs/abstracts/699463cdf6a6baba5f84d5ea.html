Introduction

Increased levels of Amyloid-β (Aβ) in the brain are a key early hallmark of Alzheimer's disease (AD) that precedes cognitive decline and dementia. Glymphatic pathways play a major role in clearing Aβ. Thereby, the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) helps carrying metabolic waste out of the subarachnoid space1. Low-frequency global neural activity, measured by blood oxygen level–dependent (BOLD) signal, fluctuations correlate with CSF inflow via the fourth ventricle2,3. The coupling between gBOLD and CSF has been established as a candidate biomarker and may index CSF dynamics relevant to waste clearance. However, most prior work has relied on a single cross-correlation function to assess CSF-gBOLD coupling strength over a period time spanning several minutes2,3. Yet, a recent study has shown that ventricle-brain BOLD dynamics can manifest in multiple modes, pointing to richer temporal structure4. The aim of this study is to introduce a time-resolved, state-based analysis of CSF-gBOLD coupling in resting-state functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI).

Methods

We analysed 10 min resting-state fMRI from 143 participants (aged 51–79, 88 females, 133 with normal cognition/10 with early cognitive decline) from the Australian Dementia Network study. Participants were scanned on a 3T Siemens Prisma scanner with a 64-channel head coil with the following parameters; TR/TE: 820/33 ms, flip angle = 53°, Multi-band (SMS) acceleration factor of x6, Voxel size: 2.4 mm isotropic, 60 slices. In addition, structural T1 scans were acquired (TR/TE: 2300/2.91 ms, TI: 900 ms, flip angle = 9°, voxel size: 1 mm isotropic, FOV: 256 mm, and 192 sagittal slices. Aβ burden was quantified via Amyloid Positron Emission Tomography imaging and expressed as centiloid values. From fMRI, timeseries of gBOLD was extracted via a grey matter mask and CSF signal was extracted from the fourth-lowest slice to obtain high sensitivity to the CSF inflow effect. Both timeseries were z-normalised and bandpass filtered (Fig. 1A).

Results

Using windowed cross-correlation of CSF and gBOLD signals (Fig. 1B) combined with a hidden semi-Markov model5, we first identified five recurrent coupling states (Fig. 1C), each characterized by distinct lag and correlation profiles (Fig. 1D). This key finding demonstrates that CSF–gBOLD coupling in rest is not static but organized into transient, discrete physiological signatures. To establish that these identified states represent truly distinct patterns, we employed Linear Discriminant Analysis on the mean windowed cross-correlation features, which confirmed a significant separability between the five states. Having robustly validated these transient coupling modes, we then showed that the proportion of time spent in the distinct CSF–BOLD coupling states differed significantly by Aβ status, i.e., Aβ negative or positive, and gender (Fig. 1E).

Conclusion

These results collectively demonstrate the temporal complexity of CSF–gBOLD coupling. The time-resolved analysis reveals a previously hidden temporal structure that is sensitive to pathological burden. By showing that these CSF–gBOLD states are influenced by gender and Aβ burden, our findings highlight potential pathways through which clearance-related dynamics could contribute to dementia risk.
