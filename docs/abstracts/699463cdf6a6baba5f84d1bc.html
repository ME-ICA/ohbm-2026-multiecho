Introduction

Deciphering how functional dynamics emerge from structural constraints is a central challenge in neuroscience. While human brain mapping characterizes these relationships at the macroscale, advances in Synthetic Biological Intelligence (SBI) now allow us to investigate them in controllable human iPSC-derived neural systems. This paradigm, often termed Biological Neurocomputation, enables modelling how intrinsic plasticity and connectivity support information processing within an embodied feedback loop. To probe these mesoscale dynamics, we used the CL1 platform, which provides programmable in vitro networks with precise spatiotemporal stimulation and real-time functional readouts [1].

Here, we assess the population-level encoding capacity of these networks by treating neuronal cultures as physical reservoirs—a framework known as Reservoir Computing (RC). Inputs drive a complex, non-linear dynamical system (the reservoir), transforming stimuli into high-dimensional states that can be decoded via linear classifiers (Fig. 1A). Although physical RC has been shown in non-biological substrates, its validation in human iPSC-derived networks remains limited [3].

Critically, the human connectome relies on a balance of segregation and integration. Evidence from animal models suggests that structural modularity improves trajectory separation and enhances pattern classification [4,5]. Extending this principle to human biology, we hypothesize that engineered iPSC-derived cultures with enforced modular topology—via microfluidic constraints—will show superior computational capacity compared to unstructured monolayers. We evaluate this across spatial binary classification, temporal sequence recognition (Morse code), and complex spatiotemporal pattern recognition (MNIST).

Methods

Neural Culture & Modular Topology Engineering:

Human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) were differentiated into distinct cortical and hippocampal populations to model diverse neural architectures. To investigate the impact of mesoscale topology on computational capacity, we compared standard unstructured 2D monolayers against engineered modular networks. Modular confinement was achieved using custom Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) devices fabricated via soft lithography (Fig 1.B).

Two distinct PDMS geometries were utilized to control functional connectivity:

  1. Octagonal Structures: Comprising 150 µm diameter wells connected to eight neighbors via microfluidic channels (5 µm width). Each well was aligned to constrain a single electrode node.
  2. Square Structures: Comprising 300 µm × 300 µm wells containing four electrode nodes each, with peripheral wells connected via a surrounding loop to facilitate re-entrant signaling pathways.

The PDMS film thickness was maintained at 100 µm for all configurations.

Electrophysiological Recording & Spike Sorting:

Neural activity was recorded using the CL1 biological neurocomputation platform (Cortical Labs). Spikes were detected in real-time at a sampling rate of 25 kHz. For each event, a 75-sample waveform cutout (1 ms pre-event, 2 ms post-event) was extracted. To ensure classification was based on physiological dynamics rather than stimulation artifacts, waveforms were sorted using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM). Clusters identified as noise or artifacts were manually removed prior to Reservoir Computing (RC) analysis.

Reservoir Computing Tasks:

We implemented three tasks of increasing spatiotemporal complexity to benchmark the reservoir's encoding capacity:

  1. Spatial Binary Classification: To test spatial separability, we stimulated single electrodes separated by five varying spatial distances. The network's response to each location was recorded for 1 second. A total of 300 trials were conducted (60 trials per distance).
  2. Temporal Binary Classification (Morse Code): To test temporal integration, we encoded the letters 'S' (...) and 'O' (---) into bi-phasic AC pulse sequences. A "dot" was represented by a single pulse at 10 Hz, while a "dash" was a burst of five pulses. We presented 50 instances of each letter to fixed input locations.
  3. Spatio-Temporal Classification (MNIST): To test complex pattern recognition, 60,000 static MNIST digits were converted into spatio-temporal spike tensors (N=16 input channels, t=5 timesteps) using a fully-spiking autoencoder with 3-layer Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) for both encoding and decoding. A subset of 1,000 digits was fed into the culture via 16 electrodes, and responses were recorded for 1 second post-stimulation.

Readout & Classification:

The reservoir output layer was implemented as a simple linear logistic regression model trained on the neural state vectors x(t). Post-sorting spike trains were binned temporally:

  • Spatial & MNIST Tasks: Activity was grouped into 15 uniform temporal bins before classification.
  • Morse Code: Activity was grouped into finer 10 ms bins to capture rapid temporal dynamics before performing classification.

Spike counts were summed per bin per channel to generate the state matrix x(t). Model performance was evaluated using 5-fold cross-validation. For the Spatial task, splits utilized 240 training and 60 testing samples; for Morse Code, 80 training and 20 testing samples; and for MNIST, 800 training and 200 testing samples.

To validate that performance was driven by biological neural dynamics, we compared "Real" neural data against two rigorous controls:

  1. Shuffled Control: Spike times were randomly shuffled across channels and bins to destroy spatiotemporal correlations while preserving culture-wide firing rates.
  2. Test-Chip Control: Identical stimulation protocols were applied to a non-biological test chip to rule out signal leakage or hardware artifacts.

Results

Spatial Encoding: 

We first evaluated the capacity of the neuronal reservoirs to linearly separate distinct spatial inputs. All biological networks—regardless of topology—demonstrated the ability to distinguish between stimulation sources with accuracy significantly exceeding chance levels (p < 0.05). However, modular architectures exhibited superior performance. Specifically, mixed cortical-hippocampal cultures plated in modular PDMS devices achieved the highest classification fidelity, reaching a median accuracy of 96% on the binary discrimination task. This suggests that while basic spatial mapping is an intrinsic property of neuronal populations, the structured connectivity provided by modular scaffolding enhances the signal-to-noise ratio for spatial encoding (Fig 2.A).

Temporal Encoding: 

In the Morse Code task, which requires the reservoir to integrate information over time, performance varied significantly by culture type. While most biological networks trended above chance, only PDMS cultures with high spontaneous activity levels achieved accuracy significantly higher than randomly shuffled controls (p < 0.01). Notably, standard 2D hippocampal monolayers failed to perform better than chance, indicating that unstructured networks may lack the requisite recurrent dynamics to maintain temporal representations. This finding underscores that high-level network activity, combined with modular recurrence, is critical for distinguishing purely temporal features like sequence duration and frequency (Fig 2.B).

Spatio-Temporal Encoding (MNIST):

The MNIST task, requiring the simultaneous integration of spatial inputs and temporal sequences, revealed the most striking divergence in computational capacity.

- Single-Trial vs. Population Averaging: Initial decoding of individual digit presentations yielded low accuracy due to high trial-to-trial variability. However, summing network responses across trials significantly reduced noise, revealing a robust population code.

- Impact of Topology: Standard 2D monolayers performed poorly on this complex benchmark. In strong contrast, modular PDMS cultures demonstrated significant learning capacity. The PDMS High Activity and PDMS Hippocampal High Activity groups emerged as the top performers, achieving median accuracies of 69% and 75%, with maximum accuracies reaching 88% and 82%, respectively. These results were statistically superior to shuffled controls (p < 0.001), which remained at chance levels (~10%).

- Latent State Space Analysis: To investigate the mechanism underlying this performance, we performed Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on the reservoir's output states. The projection of "Real" neural data onto the first two principal components revealed distinct, linearly separable clusters corresponding to digit classes. Conversely, PCA of shuffled spike trains and raw input signals failed to show such structure, resulting in overlapping trajectories (Fig 2.C-D).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that biological modularity functions as a regularizer that expands the functional dimensionality of the neural reservoir. While unstructured monolayers can handle simple spatial mappings, the complex non-linear projection required to disentangle spatio-temporal patterns (like handwritten digits) appears to strictly depend on the reverberating dynamics supported by modular, mesoscale architectures.

Conclusion

We successfully demonstrated that human iPSC-derived neuronal networks can function as robust physical reservoirs, capable of encoding high-dimensional information within their latent state space. Our results provide empirical validation for the hypothesis that biological computation is not solely a property of synaptic weights, but an emergent property of population-level dynamics constrained by topology. While simple spatial mappings can be achieved by isotropic monolayers, we show that the complex non-linear separation required for spatio-temporal recognition (MNIST) is strictly dependent on the rich dynamical repertoire supported by modular architectures.

This study establishes that structural modularity serves as a critical functional regularizer in biological neural networks. By physically constraining connectivity via PDMS microchannels, we likely enhanced the network’s "fading memory" capacity—the ability to maintain temporal representations of past inputs without descending into chaotic attractors. This suggests that the brain’s modular organization is not merely a metabolic constraint, but a computational necessity for disentangling complex spatiotemporal signal streams.

The superior performance of hippocampal-cortical co-cultures in modular devices highlights a synergistic relationship between physical topology and biological identity. The inclusion of hippocampal cells—biologically specialized for pattern separation—within a modular scaffold yielded the highest classification accuracy. This implies that to elicit complex function in vitro, we must recapitulate biological complexity at multiple scales: the cellular scale (appropriate diversity) and the mesoscale (hierarchical modularity).

These findings establish the CL1 platform as a viable system for "programmable biological intelligence," enabling the rapid prototyping of neural architectures. Beyond biocomputing, this approach offers a bottom-up model for human brain mapping. By engineering specific topologies and observing the resulting computational deficits or enhancements, we can better understand how structural dysconnectivity in human disorders (e.g., schizophrenia) might lead to functional cognitive failures. Future work will explore closed-loop training paradigms to further optimize these biological reservoirs for dynamic, goal-directed behavior.
