Introduction

Recent attention has focused on the small effect sizes in brain-behavior association studies. It has been suggested that large samples are required to detect and obtain reproducible brain-behavior relationships1. However, a potential explanation for the small effect sizes and non-reproducible findings is the use of imprecise behavioral measures (i.e., poor validity and reliability) for the psychological constructs being linked to brain structure and function2. The Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development study provides researchers with a unique opportunity to investigate the neurobiological substrates of developmental psychopathology in a large, community-based sample (N=11,800)3. One widely used measure within this dataset is the Child Behavior Checklist4, which measures developmental psychopathology with 8 subscales that assess empirically distinct syndromes: Anxious/Depressed, Withdrawn/Depressed, Somatic Complaints, Social Problems, Thought Problems, Rule-Breaking Behavior, and Aggressive Behavior; and 3 composite scales: Internalizing, which combines the first 3 subscales and reflects internalized anxious and mood states, and somatic symptoms; Externalizing, which combines the last 2 subscales, and represents manifest maladaptive behavior; and a Total Problems score; which combines all 8 subscales. The Child Behavior Checklist was designed as a clinical screening instrument to be maximally sensitive in identifying youth requiring referral for clinical follow-up4. Previous approaches to evaluating the construct validly and reliability of the Child Behavior Checklist have been suboptimal. Here, we used a suite of psychometric approaches to evaluate the construct validity and reliability of the Child Behavior Checklist, and assess its suitability as a dimensional measure of psychopathology for brain-behavior analyses in the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development study cohort.

Methods

Analysis Stage 1: Scale Construct Validity

Using baseline data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development study (N=11,800), we tested the construct validity (i.e., factorial validity) of the Child Behavior Checklist, using a combination of maximum likelihood, Bayesian confirmatory factor analysis, and factor mixture modelling. The fit of a 1) bifactor model; 2) two-factor model; and 3) a unidimensional model was evaluated. Confirmatory factor analysis tests whether a model-implied covariance matrix reproduces an observed covariance matrix. First, we tested the fit of the theory-based and most frequently used scoring structure of the 8 subscales and 3 composite scales three composite scales in a bifactor model, in which all 8 subscales load on a general psychopathology (‘p’) factor with residual Internalizing and Externalizing group factors capturing residual variance shared across the Anxious/Depressed, Withdrawn/Depressed, Somatic Complaints, and the Rule-Breaking Behavior and Aggressive Behavior subscales, respectively. Although we note that the empirical independence of these two composite subscales is not assumed, and Internalizing and Externalizing problems exhibit high comorbidity. Next, we tested a two-factor model with just correlated Internalizing and Externalizing factors, and then a parsimonious one-factor model. 

Confirmatory factor analysis imposes rigid constraints on model parameters, which can cause an otherwise well-fitting model to fail fit tests due to the omission of unmodelled minor parameters, such as covariances between the unexplained variance of the subscales (i.e., error covariances). Thus, we then used Bayesian confirmatory factor analysis, which enables the specification of model priors to accommodate minor model misspecifications. Model priors can be systematically varied to obtain an optimally fitting model. 

It was also possible that the sample may be heterogeneous and comprised of quantitatively different subgroups, either divisible by observed characteristics (i.e., ethnicity, sex) or latent (unobserved) subtypes. This can lead to biased, or uninterpretable factor solutions, which is a major consideration for studies of this cohort, which was specifically designed to sample individuals from diverse sociodemographic and cultural backgrounds. For this purpose, we used factor mixture modelling, which combines the data-centred approach of factor analysis, with the person-centred approach of latent class analysis, to identify any subgroups. The models included zero-inflated factor mixture models to accommodate the large number of community-based participants in the sample with low levels of psychopathology. The approach allows for partially overlapping classes and varying levels of discrepancy (e.g., differences in factor means, factor variances, observed variable intercepts, factor loadings).

Analysis Stage 2: Subscale Construct Validity and Reliability

We evaluated the assumed unidimensional structure of the 8 subscales using confirmatory factor analysis. Item Response Theory is a collection of statistical tools used to model item level data and its relationship with the underlying latent trait continuum (i.e., construct). We used this approach with the graded response model, appropriate for multiple choice data, to assess the psychometric performance of individual items, in terms of their overlap/redundancy, ability to discriminate between different levels of the latent trait, and the level of the latent trait at which they were most informative. We also evaluated the overall reliability of the subscales in measuring the target construct along the latent trait continuum. The zero-inflated graded response model was additionally applied to accommodate the large zero-inflated component of the study cohort who scored low on each of the subscales of the Child Behavior Checklist. 

Results

Scale Confirmatory Factor Analysis

All three models either failed global and/or local fit statistics, indicating that the model-reproduced covariance matrix did not closely reproduce the observed covariance matrix. In the case of the bifactor model (χ² (16) = 1041.73, p < .001, RMSEA = 0.074 (95%CI = 0.070 - 0.077), CFI = 0.976, SRMR = 0.024), which passed local fit statistics (i.e., correlation residuals <+.10), it had factors with average variance extracted below the recommended threshold (<.500; 50% of the variance explained in the observed variables); p factor (.482), Internalizing (.129), and Externalizing (.171). The reliability (ωH & ωHS) of the corresponding scales was .857, .028, and .0195. The explained common variance (.841) and percentage of uncontaminated correlations (85.7%) indicated that multidimensionality could be ignored in favour of a unidimensional scale5. The unidimensional model (χ² (20) = 3063.662, p < .001, RMSEA = 0.113 (90%CI = 0.11 - 0.117), CFI = 0.930, SRMR = 0.041), showed near-acceptable local fit (i.e., 3/28 correlation residuals >+.10). The average variance extracted of the p factor was just subthreshold 0.495. 

Bayesian Confirmatory Factor Analysis

No combination of Bayesian priors for the factor loadings and error covariances produced a positive number of effective parameters (𝑝𝐷), indicating that the bifactor model specification was incompatible with the observed data structure. The best-fitting admissible unidimensional model (i.e., the lowest Deviance Information Criterion, and a positive predictive p-value value closest to 0.5), consisting of a combination of normal distribution priors for the factor loadings (M=0.7, σ2= 0.1), and inverse Wishart priors for the error covariances (M=0.7, σ2=14), had a general factor with a low average variance extracted (.270), and large error covariances, highlighting a misfit between the observed and model-estimated covariance matrix. 

Factor Mixture Modelling 

The best-fitting bifactor model (BIC=158438.1, LL= -78989.24), a 7-class factor mixture model-1 (i.e., classes with differences in latent means only), yielded a negative variance, indicating an improper solution, as did the next best fitting model, the 3-class zero-inflated factor mixture model-3 (i.e., differences in observed variable thresholds/intercepts and the factor variance–covariance matrix across classes). The best-fitting unidimensional model (BIC=142065.2, LL= -7032.41, Entropy=1, Smallest class=5.33%), a 5-class zero-inflated factor mixture model-3, yielded factors with low average variance extracted (i.e., 0.303 - 0.379). 

Subscale Confirmatory Factor Analysis

6/8 subscales did not demonstrate adequate global and local fit and average variance extracted based on confirmatory factor analysis of item-level data. Only the Withdrawn/Depressed subscale with 1/28 correlations residuals >+.10 and an average variance extracted of .507, as well as the Attention Problems subscale with 6/55 correlation residuals >+.10 and an average variance extracted of .551, exhibited close to acceptable construct validity.

Item Response Theory for Scale Reliability

            Option response functions showed that item slope parameters were all clustered around 3 SDs above the M of the latent trait. The slope parameters, representing endorsement of the different response categories (0 = Not True; 1 = Somewhat or Sometimes True; 2 = Very True or Often True), were highly clustered, indicating that they did not effectively differentiate between severity levels. Reliability of each of the subscales was high (rxx=.86 – .96) at high levels of the latent trait (1.5–3.0 SD above the M), but below minimum (rxx=.60) for all 8 subscales between the mean and 0.75 SD below the M (Figure 2). Zero-inflated graded response models, shifted many of the item option response functions further towards the M, but they remained redundant and provided information over a limited range of the latent trait continuum.

Conclusion

The Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development study provides the neuroscience community with an unprecedented opportunity to probe the neurobiological substrates of developmental psychopathology. The Child Behavior Checklist is one of the most frequently used measures of developmental psychopathology for brain-behaviour association studies. These studies predominantly test correlations between scores on the 8 subscales and/or 3 composites scales of the measure with chosen metrics of brain structure and function across the entire sample of youth in the cohort. Thus, the Child Behavior Checklist is frequently utilised as a dimensional measure of psychopathology.

Here, we tested the fundamental psychometric properties of the Child Behavior Checklist in the full Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development study cohort (N=11,800), specifically its construct validity and reliability, to evaluate its suitability as a dimensional measure of developmental psychopathology in this community-based sample. We found that the subscales largely do not meet the basic requirements of construct validity and reliability required for use as a dimensional measure in this cohort. Confirmatory factor analysis failed to converge on unidimensional structures for most of these subscales, with large numbers of error covariances, and low variance extracted. This suggests that the items are not coalescing as empirically cohesive constructs, and there may be some presence of multidimensionality. Furthermore, we demonstrated that the reliability of the 8  subscales dropped below the minimum (rxx = .6) between the M and 0.75 SDs below the M of the latent trait continuum. Measurement error is the inverse of reliability, and leads to downward bias in effect sizes, in addition to reduced power, and less efficiency of estimation2. Increasing sample size will only decrease sampling variability; the estimates will remain biased and inconsistent2. 

            We also tested the hierarchical structure of the Child Behavior Checklist to assess the construct validity of its 3 composite scales. We found that the Internalizing and Externalizing scales captured just 12.9% and 17.1% once variance common to all 8 subscales had been removed, and their reliability, as measured by omega hierarchical subscale (ωHS)5 was .028, and .019. This indicates that these scales are primarily capturing total variance, rather than variance specific to their constituent subscales. Finally, we found that the p factor, representing total scores was a good representation of the latent structure of the Child Behavior Checklist data. First, the p factor in the bifactor model, which provided a good fit to the data, had high reliability, suggesting that 85.7% of variance in the Total Problems score can be attributed to this general factor. Our factor mixture models showed that there are no substantive latent subtypes embedded within the data for this model. 

It should be noted that our study has limitations. We used stringent criteria in assessing construct validity. Specifically, we used the exact fit test (χ2 test statistic), which is overly sensitive to minor model misspecification in large samples. We complemented this with a measure of local fit by examining the matrix of correlation residuals, which tests whether the model reproduces the bivariate relationships between the variables. However, it is not uncommon for there to be correlated error variances, and these can be freely estimated where theoretically justified to obtain adequate fit. Similarly, we used average variance extracted as a measure of convergent validity. However, more liberal criteria have been used, such as standardized loadings >.400, of which the bifactor and unidimensional models both passed. 

Together, our results demonstrate that the Child Behavior Checklist has several limitations as a dimensional measure of psychopathology. We caution that results of brain-behavior association studies using this measure should be interpreted with caution.
