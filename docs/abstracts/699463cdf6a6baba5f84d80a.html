Introduction

Huntington’s disease (HD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by motor impairment accompanied by early cognitive and neuropsychiatric symptoms (Tabrizi et al., 2013). Among these, apathy,defined as reduced motivation and goal-directed behavior is the most prevalent and disabling psychiatric manifestation, affecting up to 70% of patients across disease stages. Neuropathologically, striatal degeneration follows a dorsomedial-to-ventrolateral gradient, with the ventral striatum (VS) remaining relatively preserved during early disease stages. Modulated by the mesolimbic dopamine system, the VS is known to regulate reward processing, outcome valuation, and the establishment of stimulus outcome associations. This encoding serves as a reinforcement learning signal, guiding motivation via approach and avoidance behavior.

The VS plays a central role in reward processing, outcome valuation, and reinforcement learning through dopaminergic modulation and integration of limbic and prefrontal inputs. Altered reward processing has been reported in early HD, yet neuroimaging findings related to VS function remain inconsistent, with evidence for both hypo- and hyperactivation.

While the dorsal striatal deficits have been extensively studied, impairments in the VS require further investigation. Given its critical role in motivation, reward, and decision-making, we aim to explore how functional regulation by the VS may link between reward processing and motivation. The present study adopts an effort-based framework highlighting the VS as a functional interface for reward and motivation processing. This perspective may explain some of the impairments in the emergence of apathy in terms of reward processing in addition to executive dysfunctions.

Methods

Thirty-nine Huntington’s disease gene expansion carriers (HDGECs) and twenty-six age-, sex-, and education-matched healthy controls participated. Apathy was assessed using the short-form Lille Apathy Rating Scale (LARS-s), allowing classification of participants as apathetic or non-apathetic and dimensional assessment across emotional, executive, and auto-activation subdomains (Dujardin et al., 2013). Disease burden and clinical status were indexed using the CAG-Age Product (CAP), UHDRS motor and cognitive scores, and Total Functional Capacity. Depressive symptoms were controlled using the PBA-s.

Participants underwent fMRI while performing a monetary gambling task adapted from Gehring and Willoughby (2002), designed to probe reward and loss valuation. Choices between low and high monetary bets were followed by gain or loss feedback, with occasional unexpected high-magnitude outcomes to enhance reward salience.

Functional images were acquired on a 3T Siemens scanner and analyzed using SPM12. First-level models contrasted gain and loss outcomes against baseline. Group-level whole-brain analyses compared controls and HDGECs. Region-of-interest analyses focused on ventral striatal clusters identified in whole-brain contrasts. Spearman correlations examined associations between VS activity and apathy severity and subdomains, controlling for age, CAP, motor and cognitive scores, and depression.

Results

Across all participants, gain and loss outcomes engage a reward network including the ventral striatum, anterior cingulate cortex, insula, amygdala, and prefrontal cortex. Whole-brain analyses revealed significantly reduced activation in the left ventral striatum during gain processing in HDGECs compared with controls. No significant group differences were observed during loss processing.

ROI analyses demonstrated a significant group effect within the left VS. Clinically apathetic HDGECs exhibited markedly reduced VS activation during gain outcomes compared with both non-apathetic HDGECs and controls, whereas non-apathetic HDGECs did not differ from controls.

Further analyses revealed significant negative association between left VS activation and global apathy severity. Subdomain analyses showed that reduced VS activity was specifically associated with emotional and auto-activation apathy, but not executive apathy. These findings align with prior structural and behavioral evidence linking motivational apathy to limbic fronto-striatal dysfunction in HD (De Paepe et al., 2019).

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that ventral striatal dysfunction during reward processing is a key neural correlate of apathy in Huntington’s disease. Reduced left VS responsiveness to gains was selectively associated with emotional and auto-activation apathy, supporting contemporary models that conceptualize apathy as a deficit in reward-based motivation rather than generalized executive failure (Le Heron et al., 2019).

The absence of group differences during loss processing suggests a selective impairment in reward sensitivity, consistent with recent evidence from HD and other neurodegenerative disorders linking apathy to reduced benefit sensitivity rather than increased cost sensitivity (Le Heron et al., 2019; Wong et al., 2023). By extending prior structural findings  to functional reward valuation, this study identifies the ventral striatum as a critical neural substrate underlying motivational deficits in HD.

These results highlight ventral striatal reward processing as a potential functional biomarker of apathy and a promising target for incentivization-based behavioral and pharmacological interventions aimed at improving motivation and quality of life in HD.
