Introduction

Adverse perinatal factors, such as low birth weight (LBW) and preterm birth, can influence health outcomes throughout the lifespan. Children born with LWB and earlier gestational age (GA) remain at higher risk for cognitive difficulties and psychiatric illness later in life.1 This neurodevelopmental risk may manifest in atypical brain growth and maturational trajectories that are detectable using anatomical brain imaging. Prior studies examining regional brain volumes, cortical thickness, and surface area have shown links between effects of perinatal factors on brain growth and cognitive functioning of children years after birth.2 

However, existing research on the relationship between perinatal factors and brain maturation has been limited by several methodological challenges. Prior efforts have been constrained by small sample sizes, narrow age ranges, and the additional difficulty of recruiting individuals with a history of adverse perinatal factors. Self-selection bias and eligibility criteria may further constrain the population represented in brain imaging research, and retrospective reporting of perinatal information may increase uncertainty in key variables of interest. 

To overcome these challenges, we propose that retrospective analyses utilizing clinically acquired MRI scans present a complementary methodological framework to smaller, costly prospective research studies.3 Harnessing clinically acquired scans not only taps into a large anatomical MRI archives across a wide age range but additionally allows us to incorporate clinically collected perinatal information to study their relationship with brain development. Here, we use a normative modeling approach to benchmark anatomical brain phenotypes against large-scale brain growth charts. Initial findings suggest robust relationships between perinatal factors and regional brain volumes, cortical thickness, and surface area derived from clinical scans.

Methods

Clinically acquired MRI scans at the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia were deidentified and curated using methods previously shared to identify a cohort with no or limited imaging pathology.3 Briefly, reports of brain scans were evaluated by trained graders, who excluded reports of scans indicating serious pathology or artifacts likely to interfere with downstream image processing (including but not limited to intracranial masses, hemorrhages, surgical interventions, motion or dental artifacts;Fig1A). 

For included scan sessions, Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) images were accessed via the clinical pictures archiving and communication system (PACS). DICOMs were curated into the brain imaging data structures (BIDS), followed by both manual and automated quality control steps reported in detail in Zimmerman et al (2025).3 Next, global and regional measures based on the Desikan-Killiany parcellation were extracted via Synthseg+ and FreeSurfer’s recon-all-clinical pipelines, which were developed for clinically acquired scans.4 Following segmentation, scans were excluded based on automated quality metrics (mean Euler number <-60 or any regional Synthseq QC score <0.65), and the median of segmented features for each scan session from all remaining anatomical scans was calculated. BW and GA were derived from deidentified clinical records. After removing BW records >1000kg to account for data entry errors, BW under or above four standard deviations from the mean were excluded (N = 10). Similarly, GA <22 weeks, and >42 weeks were excluded (N = 4). 

7,205 individuals (0-36 years age at scan) with MRI scans were included in the brain growth chart models, and 7,192 individuals under 21 years were included in downstream analyses (54% female, median age = 13 years, IQR-age = [8 years – 17 years]). 3,214 had BW and 3,622 had GA available (Fig1B). Among those with GA 16.5% were preterm (< 37 weeks’ gestation), and among those with BW 13.4% were LWB (< 2.5kg) per World Health Organization definitions (Fig1C-D). Preterm and LWB individuals were distributed across the age range of the sample (Fig1E-F). For all reported analyses, age at scan was adjusted for GA, and missing GA assigned 40 weeks. Growth charts were also fit using only participants with known GA, showing consistent findings.

To construct growth charts as previously described,5 generalized additive models for location, scale, and shape (GAMLSS) were fit to each anatomical feature, with nonlinear terms for age, a fixed term for sex, and a random effects term for scanner. Using these models, individual brain centile scores were calculated for each feature. To quantify the divergence of individual brains separately for morphological qualities, the Centile Mahalanobis Distance (CMD) was calculated using centile scores for cortical volume (CMDVol), surface area (CMDSA), and thickness (CMDCTh), resulting in a single cumulative deviation score for each morphological domain.5

Linear regression tested the relationship between perinatal factors and brain centile scores, with Euler number included as a quality control covariate. Additional regressions with volumetric and surface area features included global measures as a covariate to explore regional specificity of the findings. Exploratory analyses examined linear interactions between perinatal factors and age at scan to probe how the perinatal influence on brain development may vary with age. To unpack possible interactions with age, sample was split at mean age and associations between perinatal factors and brain centiles were examined in younger and older halves. For all regressions, variables were converted to z-scores, and effect sizes reported are standardized. Pearson’s correlation was used to test the association between CMDs and perinatal factors. All analyses reported as significant were corrected using false discovery rate (FDR) correction for multiple comparisons for the number of brain regions tested.

Results

Both higher BW and higher GA were associated with larger global brain measures, including intracranial volume, cortical grey matter volume, subcortical gray matter volume, cerebral white matter volume, and total surface area (BW Beta Range = [0.23, 0.24], GA Beta Range = [0.055, 0.096], pFDR < 0.05). Only GA was negatively associated with total ventricular volume, such that children with earlier GA had larger ventricles (Beta = -0.052) (Fig2A). The largely positive associations extended to regional volumes and surface area across cortical and subcortical regions (BW Beta Range = [0.038, 0.28], GA Beta Range = [-0.057, 0.14], pFDR < 0.05; Fig2B top panel). Associations with regional volumes also showed robustness to controlling for intracranial volume and total surface area, though smaller effect sizes were observed (Fig2B bottom panel). 

Despite having no significant associations with mean cortical thickness, both BW and GA were associated with regional cortical thickness in both directions (BW Beta Range = [-0.090, 0.093], GA Beta Range [-0.087, 0.088], pFDR < 0.05; Fig2C left panel). Moreover, exploratory analyses showed significant interactions between perinatal factors and age at scan for cortical thickness in multiple regions (BW x Age Beta Range = [-0.073, -0.042], GA x Age Beta Range = [-0.068, -0.042], pFDR < 0.05; Fig2C right panel). Supporting an interaction effect with age, when splitting the sample in half based on age at scan, the younger sample (0-12 years) showed mostly positive, significant relationships between perinatal factors and cortical thickness in several regions (BWyoung Beta Range = [0.059, 0.13], GAyoung Beta Range = [-0.057, 0.12], pFDR < 0.05; Fig2D, left). Conversely, the older sample (12-21 years) showed mostly negative relationships for fewer regions (BWold Beta Range = [-0.16, -0.075], GAold Beta Range = [-0.11, 0.079], pFDR < 0.05; Fig2D, right). There were no significant interactions between perinatal factors and age at scan for volumetric or surface area phenotypes. CMD, a measure of cumulative atypicality of regional brain centiles, showed significant associations with perinatal factors in the cortical thickness domain, but not in the cortical volume or cortical surface area domains (rBW-CMDcth = -0.091, pBW-CMDcth = 2.7x10-7; rGA-CMDcth = -0.063, pBW-CMDcth = 1.3 x10-4) (Fig2E).

Conclusion

In a large and well-characterized anatomical MRI cohort we found perinatal factors to be associated with multiple measures of brain growth. These findings, using clinically acquired neuroimaging scans are consistent with prior studies that relied on smaller samples and retrospective reporting of perinatal factors. Specifically, we report that lower BW and earlier GA are associated with reduced brain volume and surface area, across a wide age span from early childhood to young adulthood. We also found that the relationship between cortical thickness and perinatal factors was more region-dependent, with adverse lower BW and earlier GA associated with increased cortical thickness for some regions and reduced cortical thickness in others. The region-specific directionality of these associations explains our null finding between perinatal factors and the mean cortical thickness measure.  

Interestingly, our exploratory analyses suggested an age-varying relationship between perinatal factors and cortical thickness, but not for surface area or volume measurements. Similarly lower BW and early GA appear to be associated with bigger CMDs for cortical thickness, indicating a more atypical distribution of cortical thickness centile scores across brain regions, compared to the reference distribution. These preliminary findings may suggest a potentially different mechanism through which perinatal factors influence cortical thickness, compared to surface area of cortical regions. They may also be due to the nature of cortical thickness trajectories during brain development: synaptic pruning and myelination at the gray-white junction lead to decreasing cortical thickness starting in early childhood, after a period of rapid increase during infancy driven by neuropil expansion. Importantly, these processes do not occur simultaneously across all brain regions but have been found to follow a cortical hierarchical pattern. If adverse perinatal factors were to alter neuropil expansion, myelination, and synaptic pruning mechanisms at both stages, this could be observed as a higher atypicality index, reflecting the different mechanisms influenced by perinatal factors for a given cortical region. 

An alternative explanation is that the age-varying relationship with cortical thickness may be related to the nature of our sample, where a higher proportion of children at younger ages have records of perinatal information. This could lead to observation of an age-varying relationship, due to stronger relationships at younger ages due to increased sampling. It is also possible that indications for clinical imaging differ by age, leading to differential selection biases for our sample based on age at scan. Nonetheless, the possibility of age-varying influence of perinatal factors and brain development is a valuable area of further investigation, as it could provide clues into the underpinnings of increased neurodevelopmental and psychiatric risk. In future work, we aim to increase the sampling of low birthweight and prematurely born children across the full age range and further explore the clinical and environmental phenotyping in our dataset to assess the factors that may differentially shape the brain growth trajectories of children with adverse perinatal factors.
