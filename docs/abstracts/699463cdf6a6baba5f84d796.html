Introduction

Recent advancements in generative artificial intelligence have catalyzed a paradigm shift in medical imaging, particularly for modeling the progression of neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s Disease (AD). Conditional generative models have emerged as a powerful framework capable of synthesizing future brain images by incorporating individual-level constraints. This capability offers a significant advantage over traditional population-based statistical models by preserving personalized anatomical features. However, current generative approaches largely prioritize technical fidelity—such as image resolution and textural realism—often at the expense of biological interpretability. Most existing models condition generation solely on basic demographics (e.g., age and sex), which fails to capture the intrinsic genetic heterogeneity that drives the variable rates and patterns of brain atrophy observed across individuals. Given that the Apolipoprotein E (ApoE) genotype is a critical genetic modifier of AD pathogenesis and structural neurodegeneration, we hypothesize that incorporating this genetic prior is essential for accurate longitudinal modeling. In this study, we aim to overcome the limitations of unimodal demographic conditioning by demonstrating that integrating ApoE genotype into a latent diffusion framework significantly enhances the predictive precision and structural realism of simulated brain aging trajectories.

Methods

We utilized a large-scale longitudinal dataset from the Gwangju Alzheimer’s & Related Dementias (GARD) cohort in the Republic of Korea.1 The final dataset included 1,935 unique individuals spanning the cognitive spectrum from healthy aging to mild cognitive impairment and dementia (61.5% female; mean age 74.3 years), yielding a total of 4,176 longitudinal T1-weighted MRI scans. To simulate personalized disease progression, we adapted the Brain Latent Progression (BrLP) framework, a state-of-the-art architecture combining a Latent Diffusion Model for high-fidelity image synthesis with a ControlNet adapter to predict future brain scans.2 We designed a comparative experiment with two conditioning strategies: (1) baseline model: conditioned solely on demographic variables (age, sex) and (2) proposed biological model: conditioned on age, sex, and ApoE genotype. All models were trained to predict a follow-up scan given a baseline image and target condition. Performance was rigorously evaluated using quantitative metrics assessing structural fidelity: Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM), Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), and Mean Absolute Error (MAE).

Results

Quantitative evaluation revealed that the biological model conditioned on ApoE genotype yielded superior predictive performance compared to the demographic-only baseline across all evaluated metrics. The biological model achieved a mean SSIM of 0.94 (vs. 0.89 for baseline) and a PSNR of 30.01 dB (vs. 26.72 dB), alongside a significant reduction in reconstruction error (MAE: 0.01 vs. 0.02). We further assessed model robustness by analyzing performance across different difficulty levels. In the top-performing cases, the model achieved near-ground-truth fidelity (mean SSIM: 0.973 ± 0.003; PSNR: 33.5 ± 1.19 dB; MAE: 0.007 ± 0.002). Even in the most challenging worst case scenarios, the model maintained substantial structural integrity (mean SSIM: 0.835 ± 0.025), preventing catastrophic hallucinations common in generative tasks. Additionally, we analyzed the impact of the projection interval on prediction accuracy. While a weak negative correlation was observed between SSIM and the time interval (r = -0.19, p < .001), indicating increased difficulty for long-term predictions (>7 years), the model demonstrated consistent and stable performance within the 1–4 year follow-up range, which corresponds to the typical duration of clinical trials.

Conclusion

Our findings confirm that integrating ApoE genotype into generative frameworks enables more precise modeling of individual brain trajectories than demographic data alone. The significant improvement in reconstruction metrics suggests that genetic factors provide critical signal for predicting structural atrophy patterns. By moving beyond simple demographic conditioning, this approach establishes a more biologically grounded framework for simulating personalized risks of structural brain changes, bridging the gap between technical image synthesis and precision medicine.
