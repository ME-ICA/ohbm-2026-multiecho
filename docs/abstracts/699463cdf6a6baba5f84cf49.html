Introduction

Structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is an essential tool for non-invasive investigation of brain anatomy and pathology. MRI-derived metrics play a key role in understanding atypical development. A growing application of MRI-derived metrics is brain age prediction, in which machine and deep learning algorithms estimate biological brain age from structural or functional MRI features. The difference between predicted brain age and chronological age is often interpreted as a marker of atypical aging and a potential disease predictor.

However, translating raw MRI data into clinically meaningful brain age estimates is challenging. Image quality is a critical factor, as artifacts, scanner-related distortions, or protocol variability can compromise the accuracy of downstream analyses. It remains unknown how scan quality impacts brain age prediction, despite evidence that artifacts may influence algorithm accuracy and stability. 

Brain age algorithms are typically trained and tested on research grade data, which is typically high-quality and artifact-free data, but used for real-world, clinical-grade data, which is more heterogeneous and typically contains more artifacts. The field lacks annotated datasets spanning the full spectrum of image quality, hampering the ability to determine when and how artifacts meaningfully bias brain age estimates. 

This study systematically assesses how MRI image quality affects deep learning–based brain age prediction across four common artifact types: motion, ghosting, blurring, and noise. We assess how increasing artifact severity alters 1) deviations from artifact-free brain age predictions, 2) prediction accuracy, defined as the accuracy of predicted brain age relative to chronological age, and 3) prediction precision, defined as the consistency of predictions across varying artifact levels. Analyses were performed using three widely used brain age models trained on either research-grade or heterogeneous clinical-grade datasets.

Methods

Participants

Data were drawn from the open-access cross-sectional dataset Advanced Brain Imaging on Ageing and Memory (ABRIM) dataset.1 For the present work, we included 293 out of the 295 participants (median age 52 years, range 18–85 years) with available high-resolution T1-weighted MPRAGE scans (0.8x0.8x0.8mm) acquired on a 3T Siemens Prisma system using a 32-channel head coil (TR=2200 ms, TE=2.64 ms). 

Artifact simulation

To systematically examine the effects of scan quality on brain age prediction, four artifact types, motion, ghosting, blurring, and noise, were simulated using TorchIO (available at https://github.com/TorchIO-project/torchio). In addition to the artifact-free images (severity = 0), we generated ten severity levels per artifact (levels 1–10). Parameter selection was informed by visual QC ratings obtained with the PondrAI QC framework, which scores image quality on a 1–6 scale (1=perfect, 6=terrible) (available at https://github.com/pondrai/PondrAIQC). Parameters were tuned such that the severity levels spanned the full PondrAI QC quality spectrum. To enable better evaluation of the effects of subtle artifacts, severity levels were non-linearly distributed using a power function (Figure 1A). This yielded eleven levels per artifact type.

Brain age prediction algorithms

Brain age was estimated using three widely used deep learning algorithms with distinct training data and preprocessing pipelines (Figure 1B). Using each algorithm’s original image preprocessing pipeline and pretrained algorithms may ensure a realistic comparison on the performance of how these brain age algorithms are typically deployed.

Pyment2: a Simple Fully Convolutional Network (SFCN) trained on >34,000 healthy individuals (ages 3–95) from 21 public datasets from different scanners. Code of used commit 00acac5 (v2.0.0) is available at: http://www.github.com/estenhl/pyment-public.  

MIDI3: a DenseNet-based model trained on >7,000 heterogeneous clinical scans acquired during routine clinical practice on GE 3T scanners. MIDI did not exclude low-quality images. The code of used commit 028f4c6 is available at: https://github.com/MIDIconsortium/BrainAge.  

MCCQR4: a multi-channel 3D CNN with Monte Carlo dropout and quantile regression. The model was trained on >10,000 healthy individuals from multiple datasets, including harmonized multi-scanner acquisitions. Used code of commit 4a00072 and model details are available at: https://github.com/wwu-mmll/uncertainty-brain-age. 

Statistical analysis

Deviations from artifact-free brain-age predictions: To quantify how artifacts influence predicted brain age, we fitted linear mixed-effects (LME) models using participant as a random intercept. Artifact type (motion, ghosting, blurring, noise), severity level (0–10), and algorithm (Pyment, MIDI, MCCQR) were entered as fixed factors. The primary model included a three-way interaction (algorithm × artifact × severity) to test whether severity-related brain age performance varied across algorithms and artifacts. LME models were fitted separately for each algorithm. Post-hoc comparisons were performed using FDR correction. Cohen’s d was computed for the post-hoc pairwise comparisons, interpreted using the following bins: negligible (< 0.20), small (0.20-0.50), medium (0.50-0.80), large (0.80-1.30), very large (> 1.30).

Prediction accuracy: Prediction accuracy for each artifact type and severity level was compared using Pearson’s correlation coefficient (R), and mean absolute error (MAE). To enable comparisons across algorithms, accuracy metrics were expressed as percentage change relative to the artifact-free condition (severity = 0).

Prediction precision: Precision of brain age estimates across severity levels as compared to severity 0 was defined as high intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC, two-way random-effects, absolute agreement) and low within-subject coefficient of variation (wsCV).

Results

Artifact-free performance and pre-processing failures

All three algorithms showed high performance on artifact-free scans (Pyment: R=0.92, MAE=7.1 years; MIDI: R=0.90, MAE=9.1 years; MCCQR: R=0.96, MAE=4.6 years). Preprocessing failed mainly for Pyment (N=194 for motion, N=240 for ghosting, N=255 for blurring, and N=321 for noise) and minimally for MCCQR (ghosting only N=11), whereas for MIDI all images were successfully processed.

Deviations from artifact-free brain-age predictions

The three-way LME model revealed an interaction between artifact severity, algorithm, and artifact type (F(60,37179)=34.9, p<.001), indicating algorithm-specific sensitivity to different artifacts (Figure 2A). Post-hoc LME models showed that for all algorithms the interaction between severity and artifact was significant (Pyment: F(30,11498)=87.8, p<.001; MIDI: F(30,12556)=58.7, p<.001, MCCQR: F(30,12545)=38.2, p<.001). Motion and ghosting produced the strongest deviations across severities (medium-to-large effects), while blurring and noise showed smaller effects, mostly at higher artifact severities (Figure 2B-D).

Prediction accuracy

Motion artifacts led to progressive deterioration in prediction accuracy. For Pyment, R decreased with 27% and MAE increased with 110%. For MIDI, R decreased with 12% and  MAE increased with 16%. For MCCQR, we found R decreased with 17% and MAE increased with 112%. 

Ghosting artifacts exerted the strongest influence on accuracy. Pyment’s R decreased with 21%, and MAE increased with 76%. For MIDI, R decreased with 42% and MAE increased with 35%. For MCCQR we observed a decrease in R of 25% and an increase in MAE of 135%.

Blurring artifacts had minimal impact at low severity levels but became progressively disruptive at higher severity levels. For Pyment we observed a decline in R of 14% and a MAE increase of 26%. MIDI remained largely stable across all metrics, indicating robustness to blurring. MCCQR experienced R decreases of 10%, and MAE increases of 137%.

Noise artifacts produced algorithm-specific patterns. Pyment was minimally affected, with an  R increase of 4% and MAE increase of 17%. For MIDI we observed bigger performance declines, with R declining with 33% and MAE increasing 30%. MCCQR remained largely stable, with negligible changes in R and MAE.

Prediction precision

Motion artifacts progressively reduced prediction precision. For Pyment, ICC values declined from excellent to poor, with wsCV rising from 3.4% to 21.1%. MIDI remained highly stable in terms of prediction precision, with ICC consistently excellent or good and wsCV below 7%. MCCQR demonstrated excellent ICC at low severity levels, and moderate ICC at severity 9 and 10, with wsCV increasing from 0.7% to 12.9%.

Ghosting artifacts similarly impacted predicted precision. Pyment exhibited excellent ICC at low severity, decreasing to moderate at severity 10, with wsCV rising from 1.1% to 16.2%. MIDI started with excellent ICC, and dropped to moderate with increasing severity, and wsCV increased from 0.8% to 11%. MCCQR showed excellent ICC initially, declining to moderate at severity 10, and wsCV increased from 0.6% to 13%.

Blurring artifacts minimally affected performance precision at low severity. Pyment retained excellent ICC and low wsCV (<7%) up to severity level 9. At severity level 10, ICC declined to 0.87 (good) and wsCV increased to 7.5%. For MIDI, ICC stayed excellent and low wsCV until severity level 8. At the highest severity levels, ICC declined to 0.67 and wsCV increased up to 11.8%. MCCQR retained excellent ICC at mild blur, but moderate ICC (0.62) and wsCV 12.6% at highest severity. 

Noise artifacts generally had minimal impact on Pyment’s brain age prediction precision, with ICC >0.92 across severities and wsCV <6.2%. MIDI exhibited more sensitivity at extreme noise, ICC decreased to 0.53 and wsCV increased to 10.5% at severity 10. MCCQR was largely robust to noise, maintaining excellent ICC and low wsCV (<4%) across severities.

Conclusion

Our study systematically assessed how common MRI artifacts affect deep learning–based brain age prediction by evaluating deviations from artifact-free predictions, prediction performance, and prediction stability across algorithms. This systematic evaluation yielded three main findings. First, motion and ghosting artifacts induced substantial deviations from artifact-free brain age predictions, severely degrading accuracy and prediction precision even at low-to-moderate severities. For blurring and noise, only high artifact severities had a meaningful impact on brain age predictions.  Second, algorithms trained on heterogeneous clinical datasets, such as MIDI, seemed to result in better resilience to low-to-moderate artifact severities compared to those trained on high-quality research data, like Pyment. Third, preprocessing pipelines exerted a major influence on data inclusion.  

These differential effects of the four artifact types likely reflect the spatial characteristics of each artifact: motion and ghosting introduce coherent distortions that can mimic or obscure age-related anatomical patterns, misleading algorithms trained to detect subtle structural variations, whereas blurring and noise primarily perturb voxel intensities without displacing structures.

Algorithm-specific vulnerability mirrored training data characteristics and preprocessing. Pyment, trained on high-quality research scans, showed pronounced sensitivity to motion and ghosting, with higher data loss due to preprocessing failures, particularly for noise and blurring. MIDI, trained on heterogeneous clinical scans, maintained robust performance and stability across low-to-moderate artifact levels, highlighting the benefit of diverse training data for real-world resilience. MCCQR, trained on multi-site research and clinical datasets with uncertainty modeling, showed intermediate sensitivity, suggesting that quantile regression and Monte Carlo dropout can enhance robustness against prediction errors introduced by artifacts. Architectural differences may also contribute; DenseNet architectures, as in MIDI, propagate low-level features to deeper layers, potentially mitigating the effects of localized distortions, whereas simpler SFCN models may be more susceptible to subtle structural perturbations.

Preprocessing pipelines strongly influenced both data inclusion and downstream robustness in our dataset. Pyment’s Freesurfer-based preprocessing exhibited higher failure rates for degraded images, whereas CAT12 (MCCQR) and HD-BET (MIDI) handled artifact-laden images more reliably. These findings underscore the importance of artifact-resilient preprocessing for maximizing usable data and maintaining model stability, particularly in clinical settings5.

Limitations include the use of simulated artifacts, which, while enabling controlled comparisons, may not capture the full complexity of real-world distortions. Future work should extend evaluations to multi-site clinical datasets with annotated real-world artifacts, include additional algorithms with varied architectures and multimodal inputs, and investigate longitudinal impacts on BA consistency.

In conclusion, different MRI artifacts and severities differentially affect different brain age prediction algorithms, with motion and ghosting producing the largest deviations from artifact-free brain age predictions. Rather than assuming uniform robustness, our results indicate that brain age estimates become unreliable under specific artifact conditions that are frequent in routine clinical imaging. While training on diverse clinical data and employing robust preprocessing can improve the resilience to artifacts, reliable clinical use of brain age requires explicit assessment and management of MRI image quality.
