Introduction

Neuropeptides are a fundamental molecular signaling system both in the nervous system and the periphery. These neuronal messengers are thought to be mainly synthesized and secreted in a small set of structures, such as the hypothalamus, although there is increasing evidence that both neuropeptides and their receptors are considerably more widespread throughout the brain and body 1. Once released, neuropeptides undergo slow enzymatic breakdown, unlike the fast reabsorption associated with classical neurotransmitters 1. Like metabotropic neurotransmitters, however, neuropeptides support inter-cellular communication via G protein-coupled receptors 1.

Despite their importance, how neuropeptide signaling maps onto the organization of the brain remains poorly understood 2. Here, we comprehensively map neuropeptide systems in the human brain. Using gene transcription as a proxy, we chart the spatial distribution of 38 neuropeptide receptors across 14 different neuropeptide families in both cortex and subcortex. We then study how neuropeptide receptors relate to other biological systems in the brain, from molecular and cellular features to their fine-grained embedding in hypothalamus, to whole-brain functional specialization. At the microscale, we study how neuropeptide systems co-localize with classical neurotransmitter systems. At the mesoscale, we associate neuropeptide receptor gene expression with hypothalamic nuclear organization. At the macroscale, we map neuropeptide systems onto patterns of whole-brain functional specialization and trace their emergence across phylogeny. Altogether, we demonstrate that the spatial patterning of neuropeptide receptors is highly organized across the brain and has multiple functional consequences.

Methods

Neuropeptide systems data

Neuropeptide system data was derived from their corresponding receptor-encoding gene expression obtained from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (n=6, 1 female, ages 24-57) 3. Microarray probes were filtered based on intensity, differential stability and similarity to RNA-seq data from the same individuals. Tissue samples were non-linearly registered to MNI space and assigned to 455 brain regions in the Schaefer 400, Melbourne Subcortex, and CIT168 atlases. Expression values were normalized using robust sigmoid and min-max scaling. Individual donor data was averaged to yield a mean receptor expression matrix.

Hypothalamic nuclei data

We sampled neuropeptide receptor genes in eight hypothalamic nuclei: the lateral hypothalamus, ventromedial nucleus, dorsomedial nucleus, perifornical nucleus, posterior hypothalamus, medial mammillary, lateral mammillary and tuberomammillary nucleus. Using Principal Component Analysis, we collapsed the nuclei gene expression into two spatial axes of variance, with PC1 explaining 45.77% of the variance, and PC2 explaining 34.87% of the variance across nuclei.

Co-localization to neurotransmitter systems

PET tracer density maps for 16 neurotransmitter systems were collected from the neuromaps repository. We used dominance analysis to calculate the co-localization of neurotransmitter with neuropeptide systems, expressed as a percentage of total spatial variance shared between the two. Mean co-localization was then stratified into ionotropic and metabotropic neurotransmitter signaling types.

Meta-analytic cognitive decoding

We obtained term-specific brain activations using Neurosynth and focused on 125 terms that were cross-referenced in the Cognitive Atlas. These terms ranged from broad categories like attention to specific states like sleep.

We used PLS Correlation to relate cognitive term maps and neuropeptide gene expression profiles and found one significant latent variable after contrasting our result with random matched genes and spatial nulls. To ensure generalizability, we randomly split observations into training and test sets 1,000 times and evaluated how well our model performed on unseen data.

Evolutionary pressure

We compared positive selection between ionotropic and metabotropic neurotransmitter, and neuropeptide signaling using orthologous genes across 13 vertebrate species representing human lineage. Amino acid and coding sequences were aligned and formatted for positive selection analysis using HyPhy. We focused on substitution rates (dN/dS) to identify evolutionary pressures across the three signaling types.

Results

Figure A: Neuropeptide systems organize into a cortical-subcortical gradient

We mapped 38 neuropeptide systems across 14 families using their corresponding receptor gene expression from the Allen Human Brain Atlas 3. The spatial distributions showed considerable heterogeneity, with some receptors preferentially expressed in cortex while others concentrate in hypothalamus and subcortical structures. For example, VIP receptors showed high expression in cortex, opioid receptors in nucleus accumbens, and leptin receptors in hypothalamus and hippocampus.

Figure B: Developmental trajectory of hypothalamus reflected in neuropeptide expression  

Principal component analysis of receptor expression in eight hypothalamic nuclei revealed two dominant organizational axes. PC1 (45.77% variance) captured a tubero-mammillary gradient from anterior to posterior regions, reflecting developmental programs where tuberal nuclei differentiate earlier in embryogenesis 4. PC2 (34.87% variance) separated medial (periventricular) from lateral hypothalamic territories, mirroring cytoarchitectural differences 4. Together, we find that neuropeptide receptor expression recapitulates fundamental anatomical divisions of the hypothalamus.

Figure C: Spatial overlap with neurotransmitter systems

Here we compared the spatial distribution 38 neuropeptide receptors with 16 neurotransmitter receptors from PET imaging. On average, neurotransmitter receptors shared 60% spatial variance with neuropeptide receptors. Critically, metabotropic receptors showed significantly greater co-localization with neuropeptide receptors than ionotropic receptors (t(74)=2.76, P=0.007), suggesting neuropeptides preferentially occupy environments with slow-acting metabotropic signaling 1.

Figure D: Regional cognitive decoding of neuropeptide systems using meta-analysis 

Using partial least squares correlation to map receptors onto 125 cognitive terms from Neurosynth, we identified one dominant mapping (81.17% covariance explained). This revealed a cortex-subcortex division: cortically expressed receptors (VIPR1/2, MCHR1/2, RXFP1, CCKBR) covaried with sensory-cognitive terms like "visual attention," "object recognition," and "reading." Subcortically expressed receptors (OXTR, EDNRB, NTSR2, LEPR, OPRM1) covaried with terms related to reward ("addiction," "anticipation"), emotional regulation ("stress," "anxiety"), and bodily functions ("eating," "sleep"). This demonstrates that neuropeptide anatomical layout corresponds to a spectrum from basal bodily to higher cognitive function 5.

Figure E: Evolutionary pressure for neuropeptide signaling

Evolutionary analysis across 12 vertebrate species examined positive selection of neurotransmitter (ionotropic/ metabotropic) and neuropeptide signaling systems. Using an aBSREL model, we found significant effects of signaling type on substitution rates (Friedman χ²(2)=7.54, P=0.023). Maximum positive selection occurred around the transition from amniotes (chicken) to mammals (platypus). Neuropeptide and metabotropic receptors showed more protracted positive selection than ionotropic genes during early mammalian evolution. Notably, genes positively selected in earlier evolutionary branches encode predominantly subcortical receptors (e.g., APLNR, EDNRB), while more recently selected genes are expressed primarily in cortex (e.g., MCHR1, VIPR2). This suggests the cortex-subcortex differentiation may have evolutionary origins coinciding with neocortex emergence and higher cognitive function development.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study provides a comprehensive mapping of 38 neuropeptide systems in the human brain, revealing an organization of neuropeptide receptors along a cortex-subcortex gradient. We demonstrate that neuropeptide receptor expression recapitulates fundamental hypothalamic divisions and preferentially co-localizes with metabotropic neurotransmitter systems, suggesting coordinated slow-acting modulatory signaling. Critically, we associate neuropeptide systems to meta-analytical maps of cognitive specialization, with cortical receptors related with sensory-cognitive functions and subcortical receptors related to reward, emotional regulation, and homeostatic processes. Evolutionary analysis reveals extended positive selection for neuropeptides during the amniote-to-mammal transition, with earlier-selected genes now predominantly subcortical and later-selected genes cortical. Collectively, these results establish neuropeptides as fundamental orchestrators of brain function across multiple scales, from molecular co-transmission to whole-brain cognitive architecture, with evolutionary roots tied to neocortical emergence.
