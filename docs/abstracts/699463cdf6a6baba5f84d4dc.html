Introduction

Musical reward emerges from dynamic cortico–subcortical interactions centered on the nucleus accumbens (NAcc) and is accompanied by coordinated peripheral physiological responses. While previous work has implicated dopaminergic and affective circuits in musical pleasure, the neural and computational mechanisms governing dynamic reward processing—particularly during pleasure ascent and pleasure descent—and their coordination with autonomic physiology remain poorly understood. Most prior studies rely on static contrasts and neglect brain–body interactions, limiting mechanistic accounts of naturalistic musical reward. Here, we integrate central neural dynamics with peripheral physiological signals to characterize the computational pathways underlying dynamic music reward processing.

Methods

Thirty healthy participants underwent fMRI scanning while passively listening to self-selected pleasurable music (eliciting chills) and neutral music. Peripheral physiological signals, including photoplethysmography (PPG) and electrodermal activity (EDA), were recorded concurrently to index autonomic arousal (Figure 1A). Immediately following scanning, participants completed a replay-based continuous pleasure rating task (1–4 scale) using the identical stimuli, enabling segmentation of pleasure ascent and pleasure descent epochs (Figure 1B).

Event-related analyses of skin conductance responses (SCRs) were conducted to capture transient autonomic dynamics associated with pleasure transitions. Univariate fMRI analyses contrasted pleasurable versus neutral music and pleasure ascent versus descent. Multivoxel pattern analysis (MVPA) was used to identify distributed representations discriminating the two pleasure phases. Dynamic causal modeling (DCM) was applied to formally test computational models of cortico–subcortical information flow, quantifying state-dependent changes in effective connectivity and regional self-inhibition during pleasure ascent and descent.

Results

Pleasurable music elicited robust autonomic engagement relative to neutral music, reflected by increased PPG rate (Figure 2A), PPG amplitude (Figure 2B), skin conductance level (SCL; Figure 2C), and skin conductance response (SCR; Figure 2D). Critically, pleasure ascent and descent exhibited dissociable autonomic dynamics: pleasure ascent was associated with rapid SCR responses, including a pre-activation trend (~300 ms), whereas pleasure descent showed delayed SCR effects (1.6–3.5 s post-event; Figure 2E), indicating phase-specific brain–body coupling.

Univariate analyses revealed stronger right NAcc (rNAcc) activation during pleasure ascent than descent (Figure 2F). MVPA identified a cortico–subcortical network—including right superior temporal gyrus (rSTG), left anterior cingulate cortex (lACC), right orbitofrontal cortex (rOFC), insula, and amygdala—that reliably discriminated between pleasure ascent and descent (Figure 2G), suggesting distributed encoding of dynamic reward states.

DCM results revealed computationally distinct pathway configurations across pleasure phases. During pleasure ascent, the direct rSTG→rNAcc pathway was weakened, while the indirect rSTG→rOFC→rNAcc pathway was strengthened (Figure 2H), consistent with integrative cortical valuation preceding subcortical reward encoding. In contrast, pleasure descent enhanced the direct rSTG→rNAcc pathway and increased information flow through the lACC→rNAcc pathway (Figure 2I), suggesting more immediate affective and regulatory control. Direct comparisons confirmed phase-specific modulation of cortico–subcortical circuits, accompanied by state-dependent adjustments in regional self-inhibition (Figure 2J).

Conclusion

This study delineates the dynamic neural and peripheral physiological mechanisms underlying musical reward by dissociating pleasure ascent from pleasure descent. Our findings reveal distinct cortico–subcortical information flow patterns and temporally specific autonomic responses that jointly shape the experience of musical pleasure. These results are consistent with a predictive control account in which pleasure ascent and descent reflect distinct computational states, implemented via phase-specific cortico–subcortical pathway reconfiguration and coupled autonomic dynamics. By integrating central and peripheral dynamics, this work advances a mechanistic framework for understanding the spatiotemporal organization of aesthetic reward in the human brain.
