Introduction

Single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) is a highly specialized, useful imaging modality that allows researchers to measure perfusion flow in the brain1. Recent technological advancements in SPECT imaging have enabled SPECT to be a robust measure of perfusion, specifically resting cerebral blood flow (rCBF). Multivariate techniques such as independent component analysis (ICA)2 have been used to estimate covarying functional brain networks. Previous approaches have been fully data-driven (i.e., blind ICA). More recently, a hybrid approach, spatially constrained ICA, has been used in fMRI data (called the NeuroMark pipeline) to allow for full automation while also enabling adaptation to individual datasets. Using this approach requires a set of spatial priors, typically established by identifying components which replicate across data sets 3. A SPECT template would be helpful to automatically estimate subject specific functional SPECT networks that correspond across subjects and studies. This would allow for seamless and comparable analysis of SPECT network findings to study clinical groups across various psychiatric disorders. The current study evaluates the replicability of blind ICA decomposition across different sets of subjects. Results show that SPECT ICA does show evidence of estimating replicable (canonical networks) and motivates the development of a NeuroMark SPECT template. 

Methods

Two subsets of subjects from a 22,733 depressed subject pool were selected. 2001 patients total were selected across data subsets, with 1000 patients split across dataset 1 and 1001 for dataset 2. Each patient participated in a resting SPECT brain scan with patients scanned across fourteen clinical imaging sites. SPECT scans were acquired using Picker (Philips) Prism XP 3000 triple-headed gamma cameras (Picker Int. Inc., Ohio Nuclear Medicine Division, Bedford Hills, OH, USA) or InterMedical MultiCam 3000 eco triple-headed gamma cameras (Intermedical Medizintechnik GmbH, Lubbecke,  D-32312, Germany) with low energy high resolution fan beam collimators. 

For each procedure, an age- and weight-appropriate dose of 99mTc–hexamethylpropyleneamine oxime (HMPAO) was administered intravenously at rest. For the rest scans, patients were injected while they sat in a dimly lit room with their eyes open. Patients were scanned for approximately 30 minutes after injection. Data acquisition yielded 120 images per scan with each image separated by three degrees, spanning 360 degrees. A low pass filter was applied with a high cutoff and Chang attenuation correction performed4. The resulting reconstructed image matrices were 128x128x78 with voxel sizes of 2.5mm3. For voxel-based analyses, images were aligned to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space with the Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs version 2.2.0; RRID:SCR_004757) using a SPECT template, resulting in an image matrix size of 79´96´68 with isotropic voxel sizes of 2.0mm3. SPECT images were scaled to the within-scan maximum voxel and noise outside of the brain was removed using a 50% of maximum threshold, prior to registration. After the thresholded images were aligned to the MNI space and the transformation applied to the un-thresholded images, a brain mask derived from the MNI 152 template5 was used to remove noise outside the brain from the un-thresholded images for use in the statistical models. Registered SPECT scans were visually checked for the absence of severe functional abnormalities or artifacts and proper registration to the MNI space.  

Blind ICA was run subsequently on both datasets using the Group ICA of fMRI toolbox (GIFT; https://trendscenter.org/software/gift/). A spatial correlation heatmap was generated to identify components of importance after running the two blind ICAs (defined as components with correlations ³ 0.4) were selected. Afterwards, component pairs were identified, and all 100 components were visually inspected for artifacts, edges, or abnormalities (i.e., not related white matter components or features, or noise/motion artifacts). Finally, using the GIFT autolabeller function, components were subsequently labelled using the Peterson-Yeo Buckner atlas networks as a reference.

Results

Out of the 100 components, 80 component-component pairs were found to have spatial correlations above 0.4. Visual inspection of all 100 components from datasets 1 and 2 eliminated any components that were found to have white matter or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) activity. For dataset 1, 44 components were labelled as being a part of nine networks including the basal ganglia, cerebellum, dorsal attention, default mode network, frontoparietal, somatomotor, subcortical, ventral attention, and visual networks. For dataset 2, 41 components were labelled across the same networks.

Conclusion

Results show that over 70 of ICA components estimated from separate SPECT data sets are labelled as intrinsic networks and are highly replicable. These networks/components motivate a pipeline for estimating canonical brain networks from SPECT data. This work sets the foundation for the use of these templates in a spatially constrained ICA approach, i.e. NeuroMark SPECT. Future work will continue to refine the template and then evaluate with spatially constrained ICA on an independent dataset to show replicability and stability of components and their sensitivity to clinical groups.
