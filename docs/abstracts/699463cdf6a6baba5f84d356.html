Introduction

As a structured psychosocial intervention, reminiscence therapy involves guiding individuals to systematically review, reconstruct, and integrate positive life experiences and has been shown to effectively alleviate depressive symptoms in older adults.1 However, there is marked heterogeneity in older women with depressive symptoms responses to this intervention: individuals with higher levels of extraversion, openness, and conscientiousness tend to engage in more extensive and positive reminiscence.2 Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) provides an important insight into the functional mechanisms of the brain in neuropsychiatric disorders by analyzing spontaneous brain activity during rest. Previous studies have shown abnormal functional connectivity(FC) at the large-scale brain network level in patients with depression, including weakened prefrontal-limbic connectivity and aberrant connectivity between the default mode network and the salience network.3 Some studies have utilized baseline features of brain functional networks—such as intra- or inter-network connectivity of the default mode network, sensorimotor network, and subcortical networks—to predict the outcomes of pharmacological treatment in patients with depression.4 These findings suggest that baseline brain FC patterns may serve as potential biomarkers for predicting treatment outcomes. And, the variability of treatment outcomes may cause from heterogeneity of functional brain networks in older women with depressive symptoms. Therefore, this study aims to construct a machine learning prediction model using baseline resting-state FC to predict outcomes of reminiscence therapy in older women with depressive symptoms.

Methods

Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) data and behavioral scale assessments were collected from older women with depressive symptoms in the Fourth People’s Hospital of Chengdu. The study was approved by the local ethics board, and all participants provided written informed consent. All fMRI images were preprocessed using SPM as implemented in the Neuroscience Information Toolbox (http://www.neuro.uestc.edu.cn/NIT.html). The first 5 scans were deleted. Then the images were preprocessed using the following steps: realignment, slice time correction, spatial normalization (2*2*2mm3) and smoothing (6-mm full width at half maximum kernel, FWHM). Following this, the cortex was parcellated into 400 functional regions based on the Schaefer-Yeo7 functional atlas, which correspond to 7 brain networks. Additionally, the Melbourne Subcortical Atlas was applied to segment subcortical structures into 50 regions. Mean time series for each region were then extracted using the GRETNA toolbox, and a 450×450 functional connectivity matrix was constructed and subjected to Fisher z transformation. The upper triangular elements of the baseline functional connectivity matrix were used as initial features. A two-stage feature selection procedure (Figure 1) was employed to reduce feature dimensionality.5

Specifically, In the first stage, a double-nested leave-one-out cross-validation was implemented. The inner loop integrated Pearson correlation analysis (p<0.01) with Elastic Net(E-Net) for feature selection, while the outer loop employed unweighted contribution(Score_N) and weighted contribution(Score_W) to select features. In the second stage, key brain regions were identified based on the frequency of occurrence across the selected features. Only FCs between these identified key regions were retained as final features. Finally, the retained key features were used to build predictive models, such as ridge regression, to predict ΔGDS. The models were evaluated using leave-one-out cross-validation, with Mean Absolute Error and R-Square as evaluation metrics.

Results

This study developed a predictive model based on baseline functional connectivity, employing four methods: ElasticNet, Lasso, Ridge, and SVR. The results indicate that the SVR model performed best at a 9% feature selection threshold, with an R² of 0.87 (Figure 2a) and an MAE of 0.09 (Figure 2b). In contrast, the ElasticNet and Lasso models achieved optimal predictive performance at an 8% threshold: ElasticNet yielded an R² of 0.82 and an MAE of 0.12, while Lasso yielded an R² of 0.84 and an MAE of 0.11. Figure 2c displays the scatter plot of predicted versus actual values for the Lasso model at the 8% threshold, showing a significant correlation (r = 0.914, p < 0.01). After two-stage feature selection at the 8% threshold, the retained features involve multiple brain networks, including the visual network, default mode network, sensorimotor network, control network, and subcortical network (Figure 2d).

Conclusion

In summary, this study preliminarily established an prediction model for reminiscence therapy outcomes using baseline resting-state functional connectivity in older women with depressive symptoms. This framework may provide neuroimaging evidence for understanding heterogeneity in older women with depressive symptoms in reminiscence therapy, and also offers a feasible approach for personalized intervention strategies in older women with depressive symptoms.
