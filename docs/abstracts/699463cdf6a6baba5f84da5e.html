Introduction

Numerous studies have demonstrated that schizophrenia is associated with diverse anomalies in brain structure and function.1 However,  the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying these neuroanatomical alterations remain poorly understood. Although a few recent studies have mapped the single-nucleus transcriptomic gene sets to brain abnormalities linked to schizophrenia, these efforts are restricted to cortical regions.2 Therefore, how cellular and molecular architecture relates to whole brain abnormalities in schizophrenia remains largely unexplored.  

In this study, we have integrated schizophrenia related structural MRI (sMRI) components with a comprehensive set of molecular and cellular annotation maps which include (i) brain region based individual gene expression profiles, (ii) brain cell-type specific expression profiles, (iii) neurotransmitter concentration maps, (iv) mitochondrial phenotypes to better understand the biological mechanisms underlying these neuroanatomical abnormalities. By leveraging the recent scientific advances in post-mortem whole brain tissue gene expression from Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA), cell type specific gene markers from single nucleoid transcriptomics, a comprehensive atlas of neurotransmitter receptors and transporters, and brain-wide maps of mitochondrial density and enzymatic activities of the respiratory chain, we mapped them to neuroimaging components and identified multiple schizophrenic structural alterations linked to specific cell types, genetic pathways and neurotransmitter receptors, which may explain the biological process contributing to these brain anomalies.

Methods

We have analyzed the T1-weighted sMRI data acquired from five cohorts (BSNIP, BSNIP2, FBIRN, COBRE and PK), comprising 2029 participants (1147 healthy controls and 882 schizophrenia patients). Details of data acquisition and preprocessing are provided in.3 Applying a constrained Independent Component Analysis (ICA), we extracted 66 replicable components defined by Neuromark framework.4 Afterwards, we performed linear modeling on the loadings of each independent component (IC), including age, gender and site as covariates, followed by Bonferroni correction. This analysis identified the 25 ICs that showed significant group differences, which were subsequently used to investigate their cellular and molecular correlates.

For genetic expression analysis, genetic correlates were identified by two approaches : (i) regional gene expression profiles and (ii) cell type specific gene expression profiles. First, gene expression data from AHBA were processed using the abagen pipeline 5 and mapped to a whole brain atlas (including Schaefer 400 cortical regions, and 54 subcortical regions and 32 cerebellar regions), yielding regional gene expression profiles for approximately 15,000 genes across 486 brain regions. We then derived human brain cell type specific expression profiles for 29 cell types (includes 21 neuronal and 8 non-neuronal cell types) by integrating curated cell type specific gene sets (https://www.proteinatlas.org/humanproteome/single+cell/single+nuclei+brain) with the resulting regional gene expression profiles. Transcriptomic principal component (PC) maps for each cell type were generated, and the 2nd PC maps were used to present cell type, while the 1st PC maps indicated the cerebellar vs. cerebral differences.   

For molecular analyses, we incorporated two complementary sets of molecular maps: (i) neurotransmitter density maps comprising 20 receptors and transmitters, and (ii) mitochondrial phenotype maps consisting of six spatial measures obtained from NeuroVault.

Finally, we have computed spatial correlations between 25 structural brain components and four sets of biological annotations: (i) regional gene expression profiles (ii) regional cell type specific profiles (iii) neurotransmitter density maps and (iv) mitochondrial phenotype maps. Then, empirical p-values were derived using permutation-based spatial null distributions and subsequently corrected using FDR correction to identify the significant associations between brain components and the biological annotations.

Results

Among 25 schizophrenia related structural components, there are four components in cerebellum,  and also five components in subcortical regions.  In the brain component–gene association analysis, 22 of the 25 ICs showed significant correlations with gene-expression profiles, encompassing a total of 7,199 unique genes. The number of significantly associated genes varied markedly across components, ranging from 3 to 5,754 genes per IC. Notably, ICs corresponding to cerebellar regions exhibited the strongest gene associations, involving the highest number of genes.

Among the 25 ICs showing significant schizophrenia effects, six ICs were found to be associated with multiple cell types (Fig. 1a), explaining 7% – 59% of the variance in the corresponding brain ICs. These associations predominantly highlighted subcortical brain regions and involved the cell types: LAMP5_LHX6 chandelier cells (Fig. 1c), Upper_Layer_Intratelencephalic neurons, MGE interneurons, and CGE interneurons. Overall the neuronal cell types showed stronger associations than the nonneuronal cell types. 

For neurotransmitter density maps, two brain ICs showed strong associations with multiple receptor and transporter systems (Fig. 1b). Collectively, these neurotransmitter markers explained approximately 58.4% and 54.3% of the variance in the two corresponding ICs. The strongest associations involved dopaminergic and serotonergic systems, including D2 (Fig. 1c), VAChT, and 5-HT4 receptors, with prominent expression in the caudate region. In contrast, mitochondrial phenotype maps did not show strong associations with the brain ICs.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we identified multifactorial genetic signatures at both the cellular and regional gene-expression levels using data from AHBA for schizophrenia related brain structural alterations. Our results clearly confirmed the cerebellar involvement in the pathology of schizophrenia consistent with previous reports in literature. Beyond the gray matter structural alterations in patients with schizophrenia, we also identified genes differentially expressed between cerebellum and the rest of the brain, potentially playing a role in schizophrenia. Notably, schizophrenia-related brain components showed strong associations with specific interneuron populations, including LAMP5_LHX6 chandelier cells, with GAD2, SLC32A1, PCP4L1, TAC1, and MYB emerging as the top contributing genes (Fig. 1d). These findings align with prior single-nucleus transcriptomic studies demonstrating chandelier cell dysfunction and GABAergic interneuron abnormalities as core mechanisms in schizophrenia.4 In addition, we observed strong associations with D2, VAChT, and 5-HT4 receptors, supporting the involvement of dopaminergic, cholinergic, and serotonergic dysregulation in schizophrenia. Furthermore, by extending brain–genetic associations beyond cortical regions, this work provides a broader perspective on the biological processes underlying schizophrenia-related neuroanatomical alteration.
