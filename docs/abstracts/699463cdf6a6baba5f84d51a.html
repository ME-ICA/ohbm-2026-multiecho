Introduction

Understanding how the human brain’s physical wiring shapes its dynamic brain activity is fundamental, as this relationship underpins cognitive performance, ageing, and various neurodegenerative disorders. To quantify this relationship, brain structure and activity are commonly represented as networks based on graph theory. Structural networks are derived from diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (dMRI) via white matter tract reconstruction and are referred to as the structural connectome, while activity networks are typically derived from blood-oxygen-level-dependent functional MRI (BOLD-fMRI) based on interregional co-activation patterns and are referred to as the functional connectome. Previous studies have shown that functional connectome is constrained by the underlying structural connectome,1 and that this structure-function relationship varies with cognition, ageing, and neurodegenerative disorders.2

A critical limitation of BOLD-fMRI is that the BOLD signal reflects both neuronal and cerebrovascular processes, making it vulnerable to haemodynamic confounders, particularly problematic in ageing or neurodegenerative research. In contrast, the [18F]-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG-PET) offers a more direct and quantitative measure of brain activity via glucose metabolism and is less affected by haemodynamic variability. Recent advance in functional FDG-PET (fPET) using hybrid bolus/infusion protocol enables monitoring brain glucose metabolism over time, making it possible to construct metabolic connectome.3 Notably, metabolic connectome has been shown to be more sensitive to ageing and cognitive differences than functional connectome.4 However, it remains unclear to what extent metabolic activity is constrained by the brain structure and whether the structure-metabolism relationship can serve as a biomarker for ageing and cognitive performance.

Methods

This study used the Monash MetConn simultaneous resting-state PET-MR dataset.4 The current sample includes 85 healthy participants divided into two age groups: the younger group included 40 participants (mean age 27.9 years, 22 females, age range 20-42), and the older group included 45 participants (mean age 75.7 years, 23 females, age range 66-89).

The dMRI data were preprocessed using MRtrix3. We used a single-shell multi-tissue constrained spherical deconvolution (CSD) model to estimate the orientation distribution function (ODF), and a probabilistic (iFOD2), anatomically constrained (ACT) algorithm to generate individual tractography. Streamline weights computed by the SIFT2 algorithm were entailed to construct the structural connectome (SC). Resting-state fPET data were reconstructed and motion-corrected using FSL, and partial volume corrected in PETSurfer. The CONN toolbox in Matlab was used to denoise and filter out high-frequency PET signals using a low-pass filter (0.0625 Hz). The metabolic connectome (MC) was then generated using Fisher-z transformed Pearson’s correlation across time frames. All connectomes presented were mainly based on the Schaefer 100 atlas.

Two analyses were conducted to examine the SC-MC relationship. First, to explore to what extent metabolic co-variation can be predicted by structural connections, we performed a regional SC-MC coupling analysis. SC-MC coupling was calculated using partial Spearman-rank correlation between a column of the SC matrix and its corresponding column of the MC matrix, controlling for Euclidean distance between centroids of brain regions. We then investigated age differences in SC-MC coupling using linear models. Second, to further explore SC-MC cross-modality integration and segregation based on graph theory, we performed a multilayer network analysis. SC and MC were considered as two interconnected layers of the brain. Matrices were density-thresholded and binarised, and multilayer network metrics were averaged across densities from 10-30% in steps of 1%. The main multilayer network analysis metrics included: multiplex participation coefficient, which measures cross-layer integration, and multiplex clustering coefficient, which measures cross-layer segregation. Linear models were then used to examine age effects.

Results

Across all participants, regional SC-MC coupling was consistently positive but exhibited substantial spatial variability across the cortex, with partial Spearman correlation coefficients ranging from 0.012 to 0.255. The lateral prefrontal regions showed the strongest coupling, whereas motor and sensory areas showed relatively weaker coupling. For most regions, older adults showed weaker SC-MC coupling than younger adults. Specifically, older age is associated with significantly lower SC-MC coupling in left dorsal prefrontal cortex, left lateral prefrontal cortex, left medial parietal cortex, and left extra striate cortex (all p < .05).

Multilayer network analyses further revealed age-related differences in cross-modal network organisation. At the global level, older age was associated with a significantly higher multiplex participation coefficient (p = .031) and a significantly lower multiplex clustering coefficient (p < .001). At the nodal level, significantly higher multiplex participation coefficients were found in the right temporal parietal (β = 0.160, p = .033) and right central (β = 0.134, p = .041) region in older adults. In contrast, older adults showed widespread reductions in multiplex clustering coefficient across 21 regions, with the most pronounced decreases in prefrontal cortices, and additional reductions in the precuneus and surrounding posterior midline regions, insula, intraparietal sulcus, and a subset of parietal and occipital areas.

Conclusion

This study provides the first individual-level characterisation of the structure-metabolism relationship in the human brain. We showed that regional SC-MC coupling is strongest in left prefrontal regions, which also exhibit the largest age-related reductions, indicating that structural wiring strength in these regions becomes less predictive of glucose metabolism co-variation in older adults. In contrast, when connection weights were not considered, older adults showed more similar connectivity patterns between structural and metabolic networks, alongside reduced cross-modality consistency in locally specialised processing. Together, these findings suggest an age-related network reorganisation from more flexible, locally segregated patterns toward more rigid, globally integrated patterns. Overall, our results provide initial evidence that metabolic activity is constrained by white matter structure, and that structure-metabolism relationship may serve as a potential biomarker of ageing. Ongoing work will examine how these network changes relate to cognitive performance.
