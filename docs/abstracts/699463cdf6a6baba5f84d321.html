Introduction

The glymphatic system, a recently elucidated pathway associated with glial cells in the brain, plays a critical role in intracranial waste clearance and immunomodulation, processes potentially associated with the pathological underpinnings of neurodegeneration.1 The function of glymphatic system can be non-invasively assessed using the diffusion tensor image analysis along the perivascular space (DTI-ALPS) index in human. With this index, recent studies have reported normal glymphatic function in episodic migraine patients compared with healthy controls,2 while decreased function was found in chronic migraine (CM) patients compared with episodic migraine patients and healthy controls.3 Given that chronic migraine exhibits decreased glymphatic function compared to episodic migraine, we hypothesized that the long-term recurrent nociceptive insult experienced by primary dysmenorrhea patients might likewise compromise glymphatic function. Furthermore, preclinical data indicates that acupuncture intervention can modulate glymphatic function, enhancing clearance in animal models of Alzheimer’s disease.4 Thus, in the present study, we aimed to aimed to determine if long-term recurrent menstrual pain alters the DTI-ALPS index in primary dysmenorrhea patients, and to evaluate the modulatory effect of acupuncture intervention on this index.

Methods

Twenty-one primary dysmenorrhea patients (verified by abdominal sonography) who completed all DTI scans were drawn from our previous larger single-blinded, sham-controlled study. Subjects were randomized to receive an 8-week intervention (30 min/session, 2 sessions/week) of either verum (n = 12) or non-penetration sham (n = 9) acupuncture on the bilateral SP6 acupoint in our previous study with 1:1 ratio. Menstrual pain experience was assessed using the McGill Pain Questionnaire for recalled general pain experience, and at baseline, during, and post-intervention. Participants underwent a single shell (b = 1000 s/mm2), 50-directions DTI scan at these same three time points. The DTI-ALPS index was generated using DSI-studio version 2025 following the standardized protocol .5 Briefly, images were firstly corrected for eddy current and head movement, then fiber orientations were reconstructed using the Q-space diffeomorphic reconstruction function to convert the fiber orientations from native space into standard MNI space. Four spherical ROIs (2-mm radius) were placed at the coordinates x=-38.5/-26.5/29.5/41.5, y=-12.5, z=26, respectively. The diffusivity values in the direction of the x-, y-, and z-axes in each ROI were extracted to generate the DTI-ALPS index. 

The statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS v21. To test the possible association between long-term recurrent menstrual pain and glymphatic system function, a Spearman correlation was performed between the baseline DTI-ALPS index and clinical characteristics of menstrual pain, namely length of menstrual pain history, menstrual pain duration per cycle in general, menstrual pain experience in general, and menstrual pain intensity in general. For the possible modulation effect of acupuncture intervention on glymphatic system function, two-sample t test were conducted on demographic data and clinical characteristics of menstrual pain at baseline, while the effect of group (verum vs. sham) and time (pre-, during-, post-) on the menstrual pain experience, menstrual pain intensity, and DTI-ALPS index were assessed using a two-way mixed-design analysis of variance. Significance was set at p < 0.05

Results

All patients had a long-term history of recurrent menstrual pain (mean: 12.14 ± 5.29 years; range: 5 to 21 years) with moderate baseline pain intensity (mean score: 3.23 ± 1.09, on a 0-5 scale). Compared with the DTI-ALPS index of healthy controls from a separate study,2 the DTI-ALPS index in primary dysmenorrhea patients remained similar (bi-hemisphere: 1.66 ± 0.11; right hemisphere: 1.52 ± 0.14; left hemisphere: 1.81 ± 0.14). Crucially, the DTI-ALPS index at baseline was positively correlated with general menstrual pain intensity (ρ = 0.479, p = 0.028). However, no significant correlation was found between the baseline DTI-ALPS index and the length of menstrual pain history (ρ = 0.002, p = 0.993), general menstrual pain duration (ρ = -0.313, p = 0.167), or general menstrual pain experience (ρ = 0.155, p = 0.502).

Regarding the potential modulatory effects of the acupuncture intervention, there were no significant differences between the verum and sham acupuncture groups in demographic data or baseline clinical characteristics. Across the 8-week intervention, no significant group by time interaction was observed for either menstrual pain experience (F[2, 38] = 0.691, p = 0.507) or menstrual pain intensity (F[2, 38] = 0.417, p = 0.662). Similarly, there was no significant main effect of group for either variable (menstrual pain experience: F[1,19] = 0.211, p = 0.651; menstrual pain intensity: F[1,19] = 0.813, p = 0.379). However, a significant main effect of time was found for menstrual pain experience (F[2, 38] = 7.424, p = 0.002); post-hoc tests with Sidak correction revealed significant decreases between the pre- and during-intervention time points (p = 0.005) and the pre- and post-intervention time points (p = 0.043). Also, a significant main effect of time was also observed for menstrual pain intensity (F[2, 38] = 3.905, p = 0.029); post-hoc tests with Sidak correction revealed a decreasing trend between pre- and during-intervention assessments (p = 0.068). However, neither a significant group by time interaction effect (F[2,38] = 1.278, p = 0.29) nor a main effect of group (F[1,19] = 0.584, p = 0.454) and time (F[2,38] = 0.613, p = 0.547) was found on the DTI-ALPS index.

Conclusion

In the current study, patients with primary dysmenorrhea exhibited DTI-ALPS index similar to those of healthy controls reported in previous literature,2 suggesting that long-term, recurrent, yet short-lasting pain attacks may not lead to global impairment of the brain's glymphatic system, a finding analogous to observations in episodic migraine patients.2 However, unlike the lack of association with duration or history, the DTI-ALPS index was positively correlated with general menstrual pain intensity, implying a potential physiological link between pain severity and perivascular diffusivity. Furthermore, the acupuncture intervention did not produce a significant change in the DTI-ALPS index, indicating that its modulatory effect on glymphatic function in this population may be subtle or undetectable by this metric. Future studies are needed to further explore the relationship between glymphatic function and pain characteristics, as well as the mechanisms of acupuncture modulation.
