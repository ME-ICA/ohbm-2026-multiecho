Introduction

Intermodal correspondence tests allow us to understand the coupling between brain features, such as structural and functional connectivity or gene expression and imaging biomarkers. Multiple methods to test intermodal correspondence exist, which differ in their assumptions and null hypotheses. The simple permutation-based inter-modal correspondence (SPICE) test1 was developed to utilize subject-level information without requiring strong statistical assumptions. Namely, it tests for the null hypothesis that the distribution of intermodal correspondence within a subject is no different than that between subjects

A key assumption of this permutation analysis is that subjects are exchangeable under the null. This assumption and the effects of its violation in SPICE have not been evaluated. However, it is known that many neuroimaging findings vary with development, sex, or disease state. In this work, we set out to understand how additional covariates impact the results of SPICE. Using neuroimaging data from the Philadelphia Neurodevelopmental Cohort (PNC)2 and simulated data, we identify this problem in some settings with SPICE analogous to “Simpson’s paradox”. That is, the degree of intermodal correspondence within groups may differ from an overall group effect (e.g., by age) in level of intermodal correspondence. 

We demonstrate how analyses naïve to such confounding may lead to confusing or improper results. To address such misleading test results, we propose and evaluate two approaches for performing SPICE that account for measured confounding: (1) restricted permutation, where exchangeability is only assumed within each stratum of the confounding covariate; (2) Fisher’s method, where SPICE is applied to each group separately, after which the p-values are aggregated and tested with a parametric distribution. Overall, these results represent an important evaluation and extension of SPICE that increases its rigor and generalizability.

Methods

Datasets. We used magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data from the Philadelphia Neurodevelopmental Cohort (PNC) collected at the University of Pennsylvania. Data were available for 741 children and young adults (ages 8-23) for all modalities considered: cortical thickness, sulcal depth maps, and the 2-back > 0-back contrast from a fractal n-back working memory task (Frac2back). Data were analyzed in fsaverage space; structural data was derived from T1 structural images, available already processed through FreeSurfer and sMRIPrep.

SPICE. In its original form, SPICE tests the null hypothesis H0: , where  are the brain maps of two modalities for individual ,  is any correspondence measure (e.g., Pearson correlation) that maps a pair of maps to a scalar value. The null is rejected when the correspondence measure is more extreme between the same individual than between any two individuals. This model assumes homogeneity of the null distribution with respect to other covariates (e.g., age; Figure 1A-B).

Demonstrating Type-I and Type-II errors. Consider the scenario where we are interested in whether the two modalities of the same subject correspond in a given population. Here, we define Type-I error as rejecting the null when SPICE is performed on the aggregated samples, while there is no effect each subgroup; and vice versa for Type-II error.

First, we simulated data from two pseudo-modalities to illustrate both types of errors. By applying shuffling and/or smoothing operations to the cortical thickness and/or sulcal depth maps from the PNC, we constructed groups of map pairs that yielded Type-I and Type-II error, respectively.

Then, to demonstrate the effects of confounding in the PNC data, we examined the effect of the discrete variable age (in years) when comparing (1) cortical thickness versus sulcal depth and (2) cortical thickness versus frac2back. For each pair of modalities, we performed SPICE (with Pearson correlation as the test statistic) both on the complete dataset and on each age stratum (in two-year increments) separately. As in Weinstein et al., we also performed SPICE on each parcel defined by the Yeo17 atlas.

Unravelling age effects in the PNC dataset. To understand the mechanism through which age affects the conclusion of SPICE, we examined the computation of p-values for each age subgroup. For every SPICE test, we plotted the histogram of the test statistics obtained from each permutation, with the observed value overlaid.

Methods to account for age effects. We propose two generalizations to SPICE to account for the more realistic scenario where a third variable may lead to Simpson’s paradox. (1) Following the methods of Winkler et al.,4 we define each age stratum as a permutation block and restrict permutations to maintain these blocks, justified by within-block exchangeability (Figure 1C). (2) We performed SPICE separately on each age stratum independently and combined the resulting p-values with Fisher’s method originally developed for meta-analysis (Figure 1D).

We compared the performance of these methods through simulations in terms of their ability to produce the correct decision when a group effect is present. Specifically, we computed the average maps of cortical thickness and Frac2back, respectively, for age groups 8-9 and 22-23, whose underlying null distributions of correlation are relatively far apart, introducing an age effect. For each age group independently, we generated individual-level maps following the data generation process in Weinstein et al.,1 with parameters controlling the strength of the true correspondence within a group, and the level of independent random noise. We ran 1000 simulations for a grid of parameter values using the proposed methods and the original SPICE separately and calculated the proportion of times the method rejected the null hypothesis on the combined data to yield Type-I error and power.

Results

Understanding age effect through null distribution of correspondence statistic using PNC data . We performed SPICE on each of the 8 age groups separately and on the entire population of 741 subjects, collecting the null distributions of the correspondence statistic in each test. Consistent with the results from Weinstein et al.,1 we found significant negative correspondence between cortical thickness and sulcal depth in all age groups (ϕ=-0.094, -0.122, -0.141, -0.149, -0.153, -0.165, -0.166, -0.164) and in the whole sample (ϕ=-0.147). Importantly, we observed a clear age effect wherein as age increased, this negative correlation increased (Figure 2A). In this case, the subject-level association of the two modalities is such that the null hypothesis is rejected for each age group individually (p=0.001 for each test) and for the entire population tested en bloc (p=0.001), and neither Type-I nor Type-II error is present (although the final naïve p-value may be biased).

Conversely, the correspondence between cortical thickness and Frac2back was not significant in the entire population nor in any subgroup (p=0.051, 0.211, 0.880, 0.142, 0.491, 0.640, 0.710, 0.061, 0.061; 0.550). However, it is clear that the null distribution is not identical across all ages (Figure 2B).

SPICE produces Type-I and Type-II error in simulated data when group effects are not accounted for. Using simulated data where intermodal correspondence varied between constructed groups, we showed that both Type-I and Type-II errors could theoretically arise when applying the SPICE test without adjusting for confounding group effects. For example, with two groups within which the observed statistic is not extreme relative to the null (p=0.920 and 0.809, respectively), but with a group effect on the intermodal correspondence, Type-I error (p=0.001) is possible for the combined data (Figure 2C). Type-II errors were also found (details omitted). These examples verify that the SPICE test is vulnerable to covariates that strongly affect the relationship being tested, if not properly adjusted.

Age effect leads to Type-I error in PNC data. In order to find situations where Type-I error occurred in real brain maps, we performed SPICE test between cortical thickness and Frac2back on each of the 17 functional networks defined by Yeo et al.3 after obtaining pairwise combinations of age groups. Examining the correspondence between cortical thickness and Frac2back in the visual central network on two consecutive age groups (16-17, 18-19), Type-I error was found wherein the SPICE test blindly returned a spuriously small p-value despite no subject-level correspondence being found within either group (Figure 2D)

SPICE test with restricted permutations best captures intermodal correspondence given a confounding effect . To compare the performance of the proposed approaches that address the confounding effect, we simulated brain maps for two modalities with varying correspondence signal and noise level by controlling the variances, respectively. When there is no correspondence signal, naively mixing groups without adjustment led to highly inflated Type-I error, especially as data became less noisy. When there was a true correspondence, SPICE on mixed data always showed highest rejection rate (albeit uninterpretable due to false positives), whereas Fisher’s method was most conservative across all settings. For each method, increasing true correspondence effect of each group or reducing noise both led to higher power.

Conclusion

Testing for correspondence between brain maps of different modalities is a difficult statistical question, and researchers selecting a test must be aware of the assumptions, null hypothesis, and weaknesses of each method. Compared to other methods such as the Spin Test or BrainSMASH, SPICE requires less strict assumptions about spatial autocorrelation and is able to incorporate subject-level information. However, our results demonstrate that SPICE, like many statistical tests, suffers Simpson’s paradox when confounding variables are not properly adjusted for. Thus, SPICE must be applied with care. We showed for the first time that violation of the exchangeability assumption can occur with the SPICE test, leading to Type-I error. We demonstrate that these effects can be mitigated through the use of restricted permutations within blocks. In the future, we aim to incorporate SPICE into a more general statistical model to allow where subject-level and covariate-level effects can be simultaneously tested.
