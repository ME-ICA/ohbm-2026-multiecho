Introduction

The perturbational complexity index (PCI), a measure derived from transcranial magnetic stimulation and electroencephalography (TMS-EEG) recordings, shows a strong association with conscious awareness in a broad spectrum of experimental and clinical conditions, including arousal from sleep, awakening from anesthesia, and recovery from severe brain injury1. PCI’s excellent performance in distinguishing conscious from unconscious patients positions TMS-EEG to be a key diagnostic tool for establishing the capacity for conscious awareness in patients with severe brain injuries whose behavioral examination suggests a disorder of consciousness (DoC). However, TMS-EEG is not widely available and not feasible to perform in some clinical settings (e.g., in critically ill patients who have undergone a hemicraniectomy), a methodological barrier that inspired efforts to develop alternative biomarkers of brain function that correlate with PCI.  Furthermore, there are gaps in knowledge about the underlying brain network properties that generate PCI in the human brain.  Emerging evidence from animal models suggests that PCI is modulated by thalamocortical connectivity2, but there is no consensus as to the anatomical basis for the generation of this complexity measure within the cortex in humans. Understanding the regions related to driving cortical complexity within the brain may give us insights into the neural correlates of consciousness and elucidate why injuries to some regions within the subcortex pose an increased risk of developing DoC. The development of a readily available biomarker that is closely correlated with PCI also has the potential to improve clinical care when it is not feasible to obtain a PCI measurement.

Methods

We studied 18 healthy controls who provided informed consent to participate in a multimodal brain mapping study, in accordance with a research protocol approved by the Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board. Each subject underwent resting-state functional MRI on a Siemens Prisma Fit 3T scanner, and a separate TMS-EEG session during wakefulness consisting of 3-8 stimulation sites spanning three regions (occipital, parietal, and premotor) (n = 116 total spots; Fig. 1). Biphasic TMS pulses were delivered to the cortex using a neuronavigational system (Nexstim NBT 2.2, Nexstim, Finland) which allowed for precise registration of the T1 image and TMS coil. Simultaneous EEG signals were recorded via a 62-electrode cap (EASYCap) connected to BrainAmp DC amplifiers (Brain Products GmbH, Germany) at a sampling frequency of 5 kHz. PCI was calculated at each cortical site following the procedure described in Casarotto et al 2016 Rs-fMRI scans were preprocessed using fMRIPrep3, to perform seed-based correlation measures between each stimulation site and regions within the subcortex. This stimulation-site to subcortex functional connectivity was then correlated with PCI across all stimulation sites and all subjects (Fig. 1, yielding a subcortical map where the value at each voxel represents the predictability of the PCI using the resting-state functional connection (Fig. 1).

We generated two subcortical maps, one for the posterior regions (n = 72 for Parietal + Occipital spots) and one for all stimulation sites (n = 116). To control for multiple comparisons across all voxels, we applied a false discovery rate (FDR) correction and identified significant voxels (p < 0.05) for both conditions (posterior and all stimulation sites). We used the resting-state functional connectivity at the significant voxels, either individually or combined, as features to predict PCI. To avoid overfitting given the relatively small sample size, we performed a leave-one-out cross validation (LOOCV) strategy during prediction. We iterated over all sites/subjects and at each iteration, we left out one site and fit a linear regress model using the remaining data points, then predicted the PCI value for the left-out site. We accumulated the results from every iteration and scatter plotted the predicted PCIs against the measured PCIs (Fig. 2B).

Results

Fig. 2a shows the subcortical voxels that are strongly correlated with the PCI. We found two significant clusters through visual examination of the subcortical maps: the central lateral nucleus within the thalamus as defined by the Iglesias et al 2018 thalamic atlas4 (region of interest (ROI-1) and the caudal pontine tegmentum (ROI-2). The resting-state functional connectivity showed significant weak to moderate correlations with the PCIs (All Sites ROI-1: r = 0.33, FDR corrected p = 0.042; All Sites ROI-2: r = 0.46, FDR corrected p = 0.0028; Posterior Sites ROI-1: r = 0.51, FDR corrected p = 0.0064; Posterior Sites ROI-2: r = 0.45, FDR corrected p = 0.016). Fig. 2b shows the results of predicting PCI with resting state connectivity values from these ROIs using linear models. The plots are the result of a LOOCV showing the predicted PCI value plotted against the actual PCI for models generated using ROI-1 only (row 1), ROI-2 only (row 2), and ROI-1+ ROI-2 combined (row 3). In both conditions we found that mean squared error (MSE) of predicted values improved when combining the features from both regions (Fig. 2B)

Conclusion

We identified regions in the central thalamus and pontine tegmentum whose resting-state functional connectivity to TMS stimulation sites significantly correlates with PCI value. Furthermore, we showed that multiple imaging features may be combined in a linear model to optimize prediction of PCI. Future experiments will test if a more complex model using resting state features across multiple ROIs and multimodal data such as white matter structural integrity will further improve PCI prediction. The ability to model what anatomical regions, and white matter integrity measures relate to the generation of a complex signal across the cortex would broaden our understanding of the role the subcortex plays in sustaining conscious awareness. Furthermore, as TMS-EEG is not currently accessible for many patients, generating an rs-fMRI-based proxy for PCI has the potential to improve the accuracy of detecting consciousness in patients with severe brain injuries.
