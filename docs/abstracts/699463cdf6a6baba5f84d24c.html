Introduction

Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR), an 8-week training course to incubate mindfulness, has been associated with the improvement of well-being of practitioners and the alterations of brain functionality, such as Default-Mode Network (DMN), Salience Network (SN) and Fronto-Parietal Network (FPN). However, two limitations were found in the literature: (1) most studies focused on the pre-/post-training comparisons in lack of the long-term training effect, (2) MBSR was usually taken as a whole for the pre-post comparison but each practice in MBSR may lead to different brain functionality1. The body-scan practice works by shifting attention to monitor body parts and be aware of the present. At the same time, practitioners uphold the principles of non-judgmental presence and experiencing acceptance. In this study, we looked at how the brain functionality under body-scan practices differs from that under resting state in a longitudinal design (e.g., pre-MBSR, post-MBSR, six months and twelve months after post-MBSR). We hypothesized that the compared with the resting state, the body-scan practices changed voxel-wise brain functionality (ALFF and local connectivity) linearly along the long-term training period.

Methods

MBSR Taiwanese participants aged 33 to 63 (N = 17, 12 females) enrolled in the training to be a mindfulness facilitator and had no prior mindfulness experience. The initial sample was reduced to N = 10 to retain within-subject measures across four timepoints: before (Pre), after (Post), six months after (SixPost), and twelve months after (TwPost) MBSR. Prior to each timepoint of experiment, a mindfulness training workshop was given to the participants. Questionnaires translated in Chinese Language were given in each timepoint, and among of interests were Adult Executive Functioning Inventory (ADEXI), BriefSelfControl, MindWandering, and Five Facet Mindfulness Questionnaire (FFMQ).

In each timepoint, fMRI data were acquired using a 3T SIEMENS MAGNETOM Prisma scanner (TR = 2000ms, TE = 32ms, voxel size = 3.4×3.4×3.4mm, flip angle = 77deg, 33 interleaved bottom-up slices, 3.4mm thickness) with 20-channel head coil. The dataset was acquired from the first two sessions of a larger experiment design. The current design started with 5 minutes of resting, followed by 5 minutes of body-scan mindfulness in the scanner. During resting, participants were told to close eyes, rest, and not overly fixated on any thoughts. During body-scan, participants were told to (as taught in MBSR) attend to body-parts in a stream like manner, be aware of any sensation, and to return to the awareness once mind wandered.

Data were pre-processed using the default parameters of CONN and normalized ALFF and normalized LCOR. These ROIs were selected from Harvard-Oxford and network atlas of ICA-HCP.

We conducted whole-brain comparison for body-scan and timepoint main effect using AFNI 3dMVM to obtain significance clusters (uncorrected p < .001, FWE p = .05). We chose 15 ROIs from DMN, SN and FPN in the ICA-HCP atlas as masks to be fitted onto the whole-brain clusters. Any significant cluster present in the ROI mask allowed the ROI to be taken for further analysis. We compared body-scan and resting states at each timepoint using repeated-measures ANOVA for each ROI. Multiple comparison was done using LSD and a corrected significance threshold with FDR. We further included a questionnaire as a predictor in a linear mixed-effect model, ROI ~ Condition * Timepoint + Questionnaire + (1|Subject), and fitted it into ANOVA.

Results

Significant clusters were present in ROI masks (See Fig. 1A) of DMN Right Lateral Parietal Cortex (RLP, 3 voxels), DMN Posterior Cingulate Cortex (PCC, 226 voxels), and FPN left Lateral Prefrontal Cortex (LLPFC, 13 voxels) in ALFF and SN Left Anterior Insula (LAI) in LCOR with 2 voxels.

We compared body-scan and rest across four timepoints. Repeated-measure ANOVA of fMRI revealed a significant body-scan main effect (body-scan – rest), in which normalized ALFF of the PCC was lower and the LLPFC was higher compared to rest (F(1,9) = 20.06, p = .002, q = .005, Fig. 1B; F(1,9) = 17.71, p = .002, q = .005, Fig. 1C), but not for the LPR and LAI (Fig. 1D, E). In normalized LCOR, a body-scan effect was only found in the LLPFC at the uncorrected p threshold (F(1,9) = 5.16, p = .050, q = .197, Fig. 1C), which showed less local connectivity than rest. Post-hoc LSD revealed that the body-scan effect occurred in post-MBSR timepoints, but some did not survive FDR correction. For instance, normalized ALFF of PCC in body scan was lower in Impost (p = .015, q = .078), Sixpost (p = .006, q = .05), and Twpost (p < .001, q = .004) compared to Pre. Higher body-scan ALFF of the LLPFC was found in Impost (p = .021, q = .085), and less negative LCOR (p = .009, q = .037) compared to Pre. No ROIs in Pre passed the uncorrected threshold in any main effects.

We next tested the association of ROIs with questionnaires. Questionnaire analysis revealed only FFMQ Acting with Awareness was significantly different in the Friedman test (FFMQ_Aware: χ²(3) = 10.61, p = 0.014, q = .006, W = 0.354; FFMQ Total Score, TTL: p = 0.112). FFMQ_Aware in post-hoc revealed significance at Pre (M = 27, SD = 3.56) and Impost (M = 30.4, SD = 4.88; p = .034). Hence, we used FFMQ_Aware and FFMQ_TTL for further analysis. Using a linear mixed-effects model, normalized ALFF of the PCC showed a body-scan main effect with both FFMQ_TTL (F(1,71) = 19.95, p < .001, q < .001) and FFMQ_Aware as predictors (F(1,71) = 24.18, p < .001, q < .001), whereas body-scan and timepoint main effects were found in the LLPFC with both FFMQ_TTL (body-scan: F(1,71) = 6.58, p = .011, q = .016; timepoint: F(3,71) = 3.843, p = .013, q = .038) and FFMQ_Aware predictors (body-scan: F(1,71) = 11.74, p = .001, q = .002; timepoint: F(3,71) = 2.79, p = .047, q = .056). Similarly, no main effect differences were found in the normalized ALFF of the LPR and LAI. For normalized LCOR, only the model with the FFMQ_Aware predictor showed significance, that is, a body-scan main effect in the LLPFC (F(1,71) = 5.05, p = .028, q = .083) and a timepoint main effect in the LAI (F(3,71) = 2.98, p = .038, q = .204). The LPR and PCC did not differ between body-scan and rest in any main effects.

Conclusion

We showed that body-scan mindfulness, as practiced by MBSR participants, reduced PCC functions and enhanced LLPFC functions along the longitudinal trace. However, the body-scan effect was inconsistent across post-MBSR timepoints as seen in LPR and LLPFC. Additionally, after accounting for the mindfulness questionnaire, the timepoint main effect was found in the normalized LCOR of LAI, further complicating the relationship between the body-scan effect and brain regions. Although the body-scan effect was evident (uncorrected p-level) in post-MBSR sessions, the overall result did not provide sufficient evidence to reject the hypothesis for all ROIs, as the effect of body-scan was non-linear, especially in LPR, LLPFC and LAI. Yet, among all, a consistent decrement in body-scan compared to rest in all three post-MBSR timepoints and showed a main effect in body-scan after accounting for questionnaires was seen in PCC. This decrement in activity is consistent with other types of meditation techniques, such as Loving-Kindness and Concentration, in experienced meditators2. After accounting for questionnaires, main effects were found in LLPFC. Both LLPFC and PCC, which was related to goal-directed behaviour and the control of focus attention3, thereby aligning with the cognitive demands of the body-scanning in controlling attention shifts. The current finding was consistent with the finding of low gamma and beta waves during body-scan in EEG4, implicating that body-scan modulates internal synchronization and requires cognitive allocation of attention towards shifting attention. A small sample size limited the power of the current results. This exploratory study revealed the non-linear effect of body-scan in the local dynamic of ROIs, suggesting body-scan mindfulness induced a complex brain functionality.
