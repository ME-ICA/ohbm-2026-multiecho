Introduction

Functional language lateralisation is commonly characterised by left-hemisphere dominance, a pattern largely established on the basis of non-tonal, alphabetic languages. Findings from tonal languages, however, remain mixed. Mandarin Chinese encodes lexical meaning through pitch contours, a feature that may recruit additional auditory and perceptual mechanisms associated with the right hemisphere. Some clinical observations in speakers of Chinese report a higher incidence of atypical and crossed aphasia, suggesting that language processing in this population may not be exclusively left-lateralised.^1

     Variation in hemispheric organisation has also been linked to bilingual experience. A meta-analysis of behavioural laterality studies demonstrated that age of acquisition and proficiency modulate language lateralisation, with more bilateral patterns observed primarily in early or highly proficient bilinguals, and stronger left-hemisphere dominance typically maintained in later or less proficient learners.^2 This suggests that if differences in lateralisation are driven by language-specific features rather than innate population-level factors, comparable shifts might be expected in non-native speakers of tonal languages, albeit weaker or absent at lower proficiency levels.

     Language lateralisation can be examined behaviourally using the processing of action words, which engages sensorimotor networks and exhibits hemispheric asymmetries. The visual half-field (VHF) paradigm provides a well-established method for assessing such asymmetries.^3^4

     The present study adapted a VHF word-reading paradigm to examine hemispheric lateralisation during action-word processing in Mandarin-English bilinguals with different dominant language backgrounds. Both groups completed the same task in both languages. Accuracy, reaction times, and laterality indices were analysed to assess whether tonal language experience is associated with variability in functional lateralisation across speakers.

Methods

Participants

Fifteen healthy adults participated in the study: seven in the European group and eight in the Mandarin group. The European group consisted of native Polish speakers (3 females; mean age = 26 years, SD = 6), for whom English served as a non-tonal, alphabetic second language. The Mandarin group consisted of native Mandarin speakers (5 females; mean age = 29 years, SD = 6), for whom English served as a non-tonal, alphabetic second language acquired later in life. All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision. 

     To address potential floor effects, analyses were conducted both on the full sample and on a qualified subsample defined as participants achieving more than 50 percent accuracy in at least one visual half-field.

Design and task

The study employed a visual half-field (VHF) paradigm to assess hemispheric lateralisation during visual word processing. The VHF method is considered a valid measure of cerebral language dominance when implemented in accordance with established methodological constraints.^3 Accuracy (ACC) and reaction times (RT) were recorded, and laterality indices were computed separately for both measures.

      Participants were seated approximately 57 cm from the monitor, such that 1 cm on the screen corresponded to 1 degree of visual angle, in line with current consensus recommendations for visual half-field experiments.^4 Each trial began with a central fixation cross presented for 1000 ms. Subsequently, two words were displayed simultaneously, one in the left visual field (LVF) and one in the right visual field (RVF), accompanied by a central arrow indicating the target stimulus. Participants were instructed to read the target word aloud while ignoring the distractor.

      Stimuli were presented for 180 ms and were then masked with hash symbols of equal length.^4 The arrow remained on screen until a vocal response was detected. Inter-trial intervals varied pseudorandomly between 1250, 1500, and 1750 ms.

Procedure

      Each participant completed two pseudorandomised experimental blocks, one per language (English and Mandarin), each preceded by a training session using non-experimental stimuli. Each block consisted of 40 trials. In the European group, the English block was presented first, followed by the Mandarin block; in the Mandarin group, the order was reversed to balance task difficulty across groups. Instructions were presented in the language used in the respective block.

Stimuli

Stimuli consisted of action verbs adapted from a previous VHF study.^3 The Polish stimuli were replaced with Mandarin verbs written as single Hanzi characters, while the English list was adjusted to include only monosyllabic verbs. English verbs were matched to Mandarin stimuli for syllable length and initial phonation (voiced, voiceless, vowel-initial) to minimise articulation-related differences in reaction times across languages. The final stimulus set comprised 20 English and 20 Mandarin action verbs, plus four additional training items.

Apparatus and data acquisition

The experiment was programmed and administered using SuperLab version 6.4.3 on a Windows 11 system (Lenovo Legion laptop; Intel i7 processor; 16 GB RAM; screen resolution 2560 × 1600; refresh rate 165 Hz). Vocal reaction times were recorded via the built-in microphone. Stimuli were displayed in black font on a white background (Microsoft YaHei; font size 60 for English, 85 for Mandarin).

Data processing and analysis

Responses exceeding plus or minus two standard deviations from the mean were excluded at both the individual and group levels. Statistical analyses were conducted using jamovi (version 2.4.14.0). Alpha was set at 0.05.

Results

Laterality indices

Laterality indices (LIs) were calculated separately for response accuracy (LI ACC) and reaction times (LI RT) using standard formulas^5:

LI ACC = (R ACC − L ACC) / (R ACC + L ACC) × 100
LI RT = (L RT − R RT) / (L RT + R RT) × 100

For accuracy, R (right) ACC and L (left) ACC refer to the percentage of correct responses to stimuli presented in the right (RVF) and left visual field (LVF), respectively. For reaction times, R RT and L RT denote mean response times (in milliseconds) for correct responses to stimuli presented in the RVF and LVF. Positive LI values indicate a right visual field advantage (left-hemisphere dominance), whereas negative values indicate a left visual field advantage (right-hemisphere dominance).

     Across all participants, mean LI ACC values indicated left-hemisphere dominance for both languages in both groups, with stronger lateralisation for English (Mandarin group: mean = 21.1; European group: mean = 17.5) than for Mandarin (means = 4.7 and 7.2, respectively). This pattern was not consistently observed for LI RT, which showed weaker and more variable lateralisation.

Accuracy

A 2 × 2 × 2 mixed ANOVA with repeated measures (Language: English, Mandarin; Visual half-field: left, right; Group: Mandarin, European) revealed a main effect of Language, F(1,13) = 21.99, p < .001, partial eta squared = 0.63, indicating that reading accuracy was lower for English than for Mandarin overall. A significant main effect of Visual half-field was also observed, F(1,13) = 12.98, p = .003, partial eta squared = 0.50, such that accuracy was lower for stimuli presented in the left visual field than in the right visual field.

     Importantly, these effects were qualified by a strong Language × Group interaction, F(1,13) = 118.33, p < .001, partial eta squared = 0.90. Post-hoc Tukey tests showed that in the Mandarin group, English words were read significantly less accurately than Mandarin words (p < .001), whereas in the European group the opposite pattern was observed, with higher accuracy for English than for Mandarin (p < .005).

When the analysis was repeated in a qualified subsample (participants achieving more than 50 percent accuracy in at least one visual half-field), the overall pattern of results remained similar, although the main effect of visual half-field no longer reached statistical significance.

Reaction times

An analogous ANOVA conducted on reaction times for correctly read words revealed a main effect of Language, F(1,13) = 25.67, p < .001, partial eta squared = 0.66, indicating faster responses for English than for Mandarin overall. No main effect of Visual half-field was observed, F(1,13) < 1.

     A significant Language × Group interaction was again found, F(1,13) = 100.78, p < .001, partial eta squared = 0.89. In the Mandarin group, responses to English words were slower than to Mandarin words (p < .014), whereas in the European group English words were read substantially faster (by more than 500 ms) than Mandarin words (p < .001). A three-way Language × Visual half-field × Group interaction approached significance, F(1,13) = 4.06, p = .065, partial eta squared = 0.24, suggesting divergent hemispheric processing patterns between the two groups.

Laterality indices analysis

ANOVA conducted on laterality indices (excluding visual half-field as a factor) revealed a main effect of LI type, F(1,13) = 7.82, p = .015, partial eta squared = 0.38, indicating that accuracy-based laterality indices showed stronger lateralisation than reaction-time-based indices. A significant Language × LI type interaction was also observed, F(1,13) = 5.04, p = .043, partial eta squared = 0.28, demonstrating that differences in lateralisation between English and Mandarin were driven primarily by accuracy-based measures.

Conclusion

The results indicate a predominantly left-lateralised pattern of action-word processing at the group level, accompanied by variability in the degree of hemispheric lateralisation. Participants differing in dominant language background showed differences in lateralisation indices, with Mandarin-dominant speakers exhibiting reduced leftward lateralisation relative to non-tonal-dominant speakers. These effects were most evident in reaction time-based measures and were characterised by substantial inter-individual variability.

     The findings suggest that experience with a tonal language may influence the strength of hemispheric dominance during action-word processing, rather than producing a categorical shift in lateralisation. This pattern is consistent with an experience-dependent modulation of functional lateralisation.

     These results should be interpreted in light of limitations including the modest sample size and variability in individual bilingual language histories. Consequently, the present findings primarily inform differences in the degree of lateralisation and highlight the need for further investigation using larger samples and more detailed measures of language experience.
