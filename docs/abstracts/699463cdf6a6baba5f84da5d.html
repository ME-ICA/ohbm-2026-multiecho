Introduction

Subjective emotional experiences refer to how emotions are consciously felt, such as the qualitative differences between fear, joy, or calmness. Although emotions have been extensively studied at physiological, behavioral and neural levels, their subjective qualities still remain difficult to characterize and regulate experimentally. Contemporary theories emphasize that emotions arise from distributed neural processes integrating bodily signals, cognitive appraisals, and conscious awareness, rather than from isolated limbic structures alone.1

Neurofeedback approaches offer a promising avenue for modulating emotional processes, but conventional fMRI neurofeedback typically relies on univariate signals from predefined regions (e.g., amygdala or insula), limiting specificity and precision.2 Decoded Neurofeedback (DecNef) overcomes these limitations by targeting multivoxel activity patterns associated with specific mental states,3 allowing modulation of neural representations without explicit emotion induction or conscious strategy use.4

Here, we investigate whether whole-brain multivariate patterns can reliably decode emotional valence and serve as targets for DecNef training. More critically, we examine whether distributed neural representations of emotional valence can be modulated by DecNef in a way that influences subsequent subjective emotional experience.5 This work aims to advance mechanistic understanding of emotional awareness and regulation, with potential relevance for conditions characterized by impaired emotional insight, such as alexithymia.

Methods

Participants: We are recruiting 32 healthy young adults (18-35 years, normal or corrected-to-normal vision). Sample size is consistent with prior DecNef studies and powered to detect medium effect sizes.

Stimuli: 170 images (85 positive, 85 negative valence) from the International Affective Picture System (IAPS) are used for decoder construction and pre-post behavioral assessments.

fMRI Acquisition: Data acquired on a Siemens 3T scanner using T2*-weighted EPI (TR=2000ms, TE=28ms, 3×3×3.5mm³ voxels, 34 slices) and high-resolution T1-weighted MP-RAGE (1mm isotropic).

Decoder Construction: Participants viewed 16-second blocks of emotional images (4 images per block, alternating positive/negative valence). Whole-brain logistic regression classifiers were trained on fMRI volumes acquired 5-25s after block onset to discriminate positive versus negative valence, using leave-one-run-out cross-validation. Searchlight analyses (6mm radius) identified regions contributing to decoding performance.

DecNef Training: Participants complete three DecNef sessions with monetary reinforcement. Visual feedback (disc size) reflects decoder-derived likelihood that brain patterns match the target valence state. The study uses a double-blind, between-subjects design where neither participants nor experimenters running sessions know whether positive or negative valence is being reinforced. Participants are instructed to use any mental strategy to increase disc size but receive no explicit emotion-related instructions.

Assessments: Pre-post behavioural measures include subjective valence/arousal ratings (9-point Likert scales), affective priming tasks, and neural measures include univariate pattern analysis of emotion appraisal task, and physiological monitoring (electrodermal activity, photoplethysmography, respiration) during all fMRI sessions.

Results

Whole-brain Emotional Valence Decoding: Individual whole-brain logistic regression classifiers successfully discriminated positive versus negative emotional valence across participants tested to date (n=5). Cross-validated performance using leave-one-run-out validation yielded strong classification metrics: mean AUC = 0.90 (range: 0.75-0.95) and mean accuracy = 0.90 (range: 0.75-0.95), demonstrating robust individual-level decoding of emotional valence from distributed brain patterns.

Spatial Contribution Analysis: Searchlight analyses identified prefrontal cortex (PFC) regions as primary contributors to decoding performance in the majority of participants (mean searchlight accuracy = 0.74, range: 0.63-0.90). The distributed nature of informative regions supports targeting prefrontal representations, which are implicated in emotion regulation and higher-order emotional processing, as DecNef targets.

Implications for DecNef Targeting: These results demonstrate the feasibility of whole-brain valence decoding at the individual level and motivate use of distributed prefrontal representations as DecNef targets. Ongoing data collection will test whether reinforcing these patterns through DecNef leads to systematic changes in subjective ratings, affective priming, and emotion regulation performance.

Ongoing Analysis: Given the double-blind nature of the experiment, DecNef training results cannot yet be disclosed. Planned analyses will assess whether DecNef induces shifts in prefrontal-limbic neural patterns, subjective emotional ratings, affective priming performance, physiological responses, and prefrontal engagement during emotional tasks. Post-training awareness assessments will evaluate whether learning occurred implicitly.

Conclusion

Our preliminary results demonstrate that emotional valence can be decoded with high accuracy using whole-brain fMRI patterns,3 with dominant contributions from prefrontal regions associated with emotional regulation. This supports a view of emotions as distributed representations shaped by higher-order cortical processes rather than solely by subcortical affective circuits.1

By leveraging these representations, decoded neurofeedback offers a method for implicitly modulating emotional brain states without explicit emotion induction or conscious strategy use.4 This approach may overcome key limitations of traditional neurofeedback2 by enabling fine-grained, content-specific modulation of emotional experiences.

If successful, this work will advance our understanding of the neural basis of subjective emotions and open new translational avenues for disorders characterized by impaired emotional awareness and regulation, such as alexithymia. More broadly, the findings contribute to ongoing debates in affective neuroscience and consciousness research regarding how emotions are represented, accessed, and regulated in the human brain.
