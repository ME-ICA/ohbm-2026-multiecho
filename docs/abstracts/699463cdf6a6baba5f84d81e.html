Introduction

Opioid use disorder (OUD) is a chronic condition characterized by compulsive drug-seeking and dysregulation of reward neurocircuitry1. Despite advances in medication-assisted treatment combined with behavioral interventions, relapse rates remain high (50-70%)1,2. The nucleus accumbens (NAc), situated in the ventral striatum, plays a central role in the neurobiology of addiction by integrating inputs that modulate motivational and emotional responses to rewarding stimuli1-3. Chronic substance exposure leads to maladaptive changes in synaptic plasticity within this region, contributing to impaired executive function and inhibitory control1. Beyond static connectivity changes, addiction may involve dysregulated temporal dynamics in reward circuits, with transient brain states potentially driving fluctuations in craving intensity. Low-intensity focused ultrasound (FUS) is a novel, non-invasive neuromodulation technique capable of precisely targeting deep subcortical structures4. A recent open-label trial demonstrated that FUS neuromodulation of the bilateral Nac and internal capsule produced immediate and sustained reductions in opioid craving (mean 91%) with corresponding increases in abstinence over 90 days2. However, the neural mechanisms underlying these clinical improvements remain poorly characterized. We applied micro co-activation pattern (μCAP) analysis to longitudinal resting-state fMRI data to investigate how striatal functional connectivity dynamics relate to craving and abstinence following low-intensity FUS neuromodulation.

Methods

Thirty-five participants with OUD completed a baseline resting-state fMRI scan after which a subgroup of 12 participants (mean age 35.5±8.2; 3 women) underwent FUS neuromodulation of the bilateral NAc and internal capsule (220 kHz, 20 minutes). This subgroup completed follow-up visits of resting-state fMRI at 7, 30, and 90 days post-FUS. Sample sizes at follow-up varied due to excessive head motion (n=1 excluded at day 7 and n=2 at day 90). Cue-induced craving (0-10 scale) was assessed while viewing drug imagery, and abstinence was confirmed via urine toxicology. We applied μCAP analysis to characterize striatal functional connectivity dynamics5. Unlike conventional CAP analysis which treats seeds uniformly, μCAPs iteratively refine seed weight maps to identify functionally distinct subregions within a seed and their associated whole-brain co-activation patterns (Figure 1). To maximize statistical power for μCAP derivation, we determined the CAPs using all baseline fMRI volumes from thirty-five OUD participants. 

Using bilateral striatum as our seed region, the analysis proceeded as follows: First, we extracted and z-scored the mean BOLD signal across seed voxels to identify timepoints of significant activation. fMRI volumes exceeding an activation threshold were selected and pooled across subjects, then clustered using K-means with correlation distance. The optimal number of clusters (K=4) was determined via test-retest reliability by splitting subjects into training and testing groups and tracking cluster stability. The key innovation of μCAPs is the iterative refinement of seed weight maps. After initial clustering, each resulting co-activation pattern (CAP) is restricted to the seed region. A winner-takes-all strategy is then applied: for each voxel within the striatum, only the CAP with the highest activation value at that voxel is retained, with other values set to zero. This produces refined seed weight maps capturing intra-seed spatial variability—effectively parcellating the striatum based on its distinct whole-brain connectivity patterns. These updated weights generate new seed time courses for each μCAP, which are thresholded to select a new set of fMRI volumes for clustering. This iterative process continues until convergence, monitored by comparing seed patterns between successive iterations using the Hungarian algorithm to match μCAPs across iterations; convergence is reached when the average cosine distance falls below 0.005. 

For each participant, we computed state occurrence (proportion of time in each state) and mean dwell time (MDT; average consecutive frames per state). To relate neural dynamics to clinical outcomes while accounting for our longitudinal design and sample size, we employed Bayesian multilevel models. Critically, we decomposed effects into between-person components (do individuals with generally longer MDT have higher craving?) and within-person components (when a participant dwells longer than their own average, do they also crave more?). Models controlled for time since baseline, age, and sex.

Results

Following FUS, opioid craving decreased significantly over time (baseline: 6.5±3.8 to 90-day: 0.7±1.0) while while days of abstinence increased (baseline: 22.9±17.8 days to 90-day follow-up: 87.2±56.9 days). 

μCAP analysis identified four striatal connectivity states: two dorsal striatum-centered states (States 1, 4) and two ventral striatum-centered states. State 2 showed coupling with core default mode network (DMN) hubs including medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), posterior cingulate cortex (PCC), lateral temporal cortex, and temporoparietal junction (TPJ). State 3 displayed a more restricted pattern of ventral striatum–frontal DMN connectivity, primarily involving ventromedial PFC and PCC (Figure 2, left). State 3 (ventral striatum-DMN) showed decreased occurrence (β=-0.008, p=0.008) and MDT (β=-0.162, p<0.001) over the follow-up period. Omnibus tests revealed that MDT, but not occurrence, significantly predicted craving beyond time alone (ΔAIC=-3.0, χ²(4)=10.95, p=0.027). 

Bayesian multilevel models decomposing within- and between-person effects showed that within-person increases in State 2 MDT (ventral striatum-DMN) were associated with higher craving (β=0.43, 95% CI [0.056, 0.814], Pd=98.7%) and lower abstinence (β=-0.231, 95% CI [-0.453, -0.006], Pd=97.7%) (Figure 2, right). State 3 MDT showed similar within-person associations with craving (β=0.21, 95% CI [-0.018, 0.432], Pd=96.6%). In the context of OUD, prolonged engagement in these ventral striatum-DMN states may reflect craving-related rumination or drug-related autobiographical memory retrieval.

Conclusion

These findings suggest that FUS neuromodulation of the NAc and internal capsule is associated with reduced temporal engagement of ventral striatum-DMN connectivity states. Critically, the temporal stability of these states (MDT), rather than their prevalence, predicted clinical outcomes. Within-person fluctuations in striatal-DMN coupling tracked day-to-day variations in craving, potentially reflecting internally-directed processes such as drug-related rumination. Although limited by the open-label design and absence of a sham-control condition, this pattern may serve as a potential mechanistic biomarker for treatment response in OUD.
