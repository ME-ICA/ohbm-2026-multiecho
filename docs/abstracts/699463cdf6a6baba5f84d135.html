Introduction

Motor performance declines progressively with aging, even in healthy adults, as reflected in reduced balance, coordination, and fine motor control1. These functional changes parallel structural and physiological alterations in the primary motor cortex (M1) and its major efferent pathway, the corticospinal tract (CST). M1 is somatotopically organized into face, upper-limb, and lower-limb representations2, a layout that continues through CST projections to the spinal cord. Given this functional specialization, different M1–CST subdivisions may exhibit distinct neurovascular and microstructural characteristics. For instance, vascular supply varies along M1’s medial–lateral axis: the lower-limb region is primarily supplied by the anterior cerebral artery, whereas upper-limb and face regions depend largely on the middle cerebral artery3. Such heterogeneity may lead to region-specific hemodynamic differences and differential vulnerability to aging. Microstructural factors, including intracortical myelin in M1 and axonal organization within the CST, further contribute to motor system integrity, yet it remains unclear whether somatotopic subdivisions follow similar or divergent aging trajectories.

The Human Connectome Project in Aging (HCP-Aging) provides high-resolution structural MRI, multi-delay arterial spin labeling, and multi-shell diffusion imaging, coupled with detailed motor assessments across the adult lifespan4,5. This enables comprehensive evaluation of cortical regions and their efferent white-matter pathways. We examine (i) somatotopic gradients in M1 hemodynamics, intracortical myelin proxies, and CST microstructural integrity; (ii) age-related changes across these measures; and (iii) their associations with motor performance. We hypothesize that M1 and CST subdivisions show distinct neurovascular and microstructural profiles, exhibit region-specific aging patterns, and relate differentially to somatic motor abilities.

Methods

Participants:  We analyzed 339 adults from the HCP-Aging Lifespan 2.0 dataset. All participants provided written informed consent under IRB-approved protocols. Individuals with neurological disorders associated with cognitive decline were excluded. Only right-handed participants (Edinburgh score ≥ 50) were included, and those older than 90 years were removed due to potential age-report inaccuracies. Participants were categorized into younger (36–65 years, n = 213) and older (66–90 years, n = 126) groups. Motor function was assessed across hand grip strength, endurance (two-minute walk test), and locomotion (four-meter walk). Higher normative scores indicated better performance. All participants underwent structural T1w/T2w MRI, multi-delay pseudo-continuous ASL (pCASL), and multi-shell diffusion MRI.

MRI Acquisition:  Data were acquired across four sites on harmonized Siemens Prisma 3T scanners with 32-channel head coils. T1w and T2w images were collected at 0.8 mm isotropic resolution. Multi-delay pCASL included a 1500 ms labeling duration and five post-labeling delays (PLDs) (200–2200 ms), with 2.5 mm isotropic multi-band EPI readout. Two M₀ images and reversed-phase spin-echo EPI pairs were acquired for calibration and distortion correction. Diffusion MRI used 1.5 mm isotropic multi-band EPI, two b-values (1500/3000 s/mm²), ~92–93 directions per shell, and reversed-phase images for geometric correction.

Structural MRI Processing: T1w data were processed with FreeSurfer for surface reconstruction and cortical parcellation. The precentral gyrus was first defined using the Desikan–Killiany atlas, then subdivided into six segments along the face–foot axis using coordinate-based SVD (mrGrad). Segments were grouped into three somatotopic subregions: face (1–2), upper-limb (3–4), and lower-limb (5–6). T1/T2 ratio maps were generated by voxelwise division of T1w and T2w images, and mean values were extracted from each M1 subregion. 

ASL Processing: ASL data were processed with the HCP-ASL pipeline, including banding-artifact and slice-wise fluctuation correction, iterative motion correction, and TOPUP distortion correction. Cerebral blood flow (CBF) and arterial transit time (ATT) were estimated in the native ASL grid using BASIL with partial-volume correction. The ASL-derived low-resolution T1w image was registered to the native T1w image, and the resulting transform was applied to CBF and ATT maps for M1 sampling.

Diffusion MRI and Tractography:  Diffusion data were preprocessed following the DESIGNER framework, including denoising, Gibbs ringing removal, motion/eddy-current correction, and bias-field correction. Diffusion tensors were fit using weighted least-squares, and fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity (MD), axial diffusivity (AD), and radial diffusivity (RD) maps were computed. Bilateral corticospinal tracts (CSTs) were reconstructed using probabilistic tractography (MRtrix3). Seeds were placed in the precentral gyrus and targets in the ipsilateral cerebral peduncle. Streamlines intersecting M1 somatotopic subregions were extracted to generate face, upper-limb, and lower-limb CST subdivisions. Diffusion metrics were sampled along streamlines and averaged within each subdivision.

Statistical Analysis:  Group differences in demographics and motor performance were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test (continuous variables), chi-square test (gender), and general linear model (GLM) for motor scores with gender as a covariate. Somatotopic differences within M1 and CST were evaluated using paired t-tests. Age effects on imaging metrics were tested using GLM controlling for gender. Linear versus quadratic age–metric relationships were compared using extra-sum-of-squares F tests, with model fit summarized by r or R². Partial correlations (adjusting for gender) assessed associations among imaging metrics and motor performance. Statistical significance was defined as an FDR-adjusted p < 0.05. 

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics of participants: 

      The study included 339 right-handed participants aged 36–90 years (mean 59.63 years; 212 females, 127 males). Gender distribution did not differ between younger and older groups (P = 0.780). Older adults exhibited significantly poorer motor performance, with lower scores in grip strength (dominant: P < 0.001; non-dominant: P < 0.001), 2-minute walk (P < 0.001), and 4-meter walk tests (P < 0.001). Dominant-hand grip strength decreased linearly with age (r = –0.381, P < 0.001), whereas non-dominant grip strength (R² = 0.508, P = 0.011), endurance (R² = 0.357, P = 0.004), and locomotion (R² = 0.092, P = 0.025) followed quadratic trajectories. 

Somatotopic gradients in M1 structure and hemodynamics: 

In younger adults, ATT increased from the face to the lower-limb representations in M1 (all pairwise comparisons: Pleft < 0.001, Pright < 0.001; Fig. 1F). CBF was highest in the upper-limb region and lowest in the lower-limb region (all pairwise comparisons: Pleft < 0.001, Pright < 0.001; Fig. 1G), which may reflect the greater metabolic demand of frequent and fine hand movements. The T1/T2 ratio also increased along the face-to-feet axis (all pairwise comparisons: Pleft < 0.001, Pright < 0.001; Fig. 1H). 

      Older adults showed similar patterns (Fig. 1I–K), although ATT differences between upper- and lower-limb regions on the left were non-significant (P = 0.210). 

Somatotopic gradients in CST microstructural integrity

In younger adults, FA increased from the face to the lower-limb subdivisions of the left CST, whereas in the right CST FA was lowest in the upper-limb subdivision and highest in the lower-limb subdivision (all pairwise comparisons: Pleft < 0.05, Pright < 0.001; Fig. 2C). MD was highest in the face subdivision and lowest in the upper-limb subdivision (all pairwise comparisons: Pleft < 0.001, Pright < 0.001; Fig. 2F). AD was lowest in the upper-limb subdivision and highest in the lower-limb subdivision (all pairwise comparisons: Pleft < 0.001, Pright < 0.001; Fig. 2I). RD decreased along the face-to-feet axis (all pairwise comparisons: Pleft < 0.001, Pright < 0.001; Fig. 2L). These microstructural patterns likely support effective long-distance corticospinal communication underlying lower-limb motor function. 

      Older adults showed similar somatotopic gradients of FA (Fig. 2D), MD (Fig. 2G), AD (Fig. 2J), and RD (Fig. 2M) (all subregion comparisons: Pleft < 0.001, Pright ≤ 0.001), except that FA did not differ between the right face and upper-limb subdivisions (P = 0.499), and MD did not differ between the right upper and lower-limb subdivisions (P = 0.196).

Aging trajectories of ATT, CBF, and T1/T2 ratio in M1 subregions: 

      ATT increased linearly with age in face (r = 0.482, Pleft < 0.001; r = 0.497, Pright < 0.001), upper-limb (r = 0.415, Pleft < 0.001; r = 0.429, Pright < 0.001), and lower-limb regions (r = 0.306, Pleft < 0.001; r = 0.329, Pright < 0.001). CBF declined linearly in all subregions (r = –0.293 to –0.230, P < 0.001). T1/T2 ratio in the face increased linearly (r = 0.298, Pleft < 0.001; r = 0.308, Pright < 0.001), whereas upper- and lower-limb ratios followed quadratic trajectories (R² = 0.035–0.076, P = 0.005–0.015).

Aging trajectories of FA, MD, AD, and RD in the CST subdivisions:

      FA declined linearly in bilateral face (r = –0.212, Pleft < 0.001; r = –0.183, Pright < 0.001) and left lower-limb CST (r = –0.119, P = 0.031). No significant FA changes were observed in upper-limb CST. MD, AD, and RD increased quadratically with age across all CST subdivisions (P ≤ 0.003).

Relationship of M1 and CST imaging metrics with motor performance: 

      Grip strength correlated with ATT and CBF in upper-limb M1 (P ≤ 0.008) and with CST MD and AD (P ≤ 0.015). ATT in lower-limb M1 negatively correlated with locomotion (P = 0.041). Endurance was associated with M1 lower-limb CBF and T1/T2 ratio (P ≤ 0.006) and corresponding CST MD and RD (P ≤ 0.006).

Conclusion

M1 and CST exhibit distinct somatotopic structural and functional gradients. Aging leads to widespread declines in M1 hemodynamics and alterations in myelin integrity, while the upper-limb CST shows relatively preserved microstructure. Importantly, these microstructural and hemodynamic changes are associated with somatic motor decline, offering critical insights into region-specific mechanisms of motor aging and their impact on somatotopic motor functions in older adults. By studying how aging affects these homunculus regions and their connections, we can detect subtle, region-specific vulnerabilities before overt motor impairment appears.
