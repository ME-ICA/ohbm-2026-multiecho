Introduction

Overlapping occipito-parietal and premotor areas are consistently recruited during different action domains, such as execution, imagery and observation1. While this suggests that action simulation and movement execution rely on a shared network, it remains unclear whether these regions encode actions through a unified representational code or preserve domain-specific representational geometries. One hypothesis proposes that hand actions are represented according to their kinematic synergies, low-dimensional embeddings of joint movements based on biomechanical regularities2. Synergic models outperform alternatives in predicting cortical activity during grasping2 and action observation3. Others argue that action representations may be shaped by low-level visual features of movements or objects4, or by higher-order semantic-linguistic properties, such as action meaning or verb5. However, it remains unresolved whether these principles are represented in the action network, and whether one exerts a dominant influence in areas showing domain-independent representational architecture.

To address these questions, we combined two fMRI experiments involving execution, imagery and observation of hand actions. We first isolated cortical regions encoding action-specific information across domains via decoding and conjunction analysis. To test for a shared geometry, we computed classification-based representational dissimilarity matrices (RDMs) and correlated them across domains. We then assessed which representational principles accounted for their geometry by comparing neural RDMs with three computational models capturing kinematic synergies, low-level visual features and high-level semantic relations. Finally, we projected these effects onto cortical representational gradients derived from gesture execution, so to identify where, and on which computational basis, hand-action representations remain stable across domains.

Methods

Experimental procedures

Two independent fMRI experiments were conducted after approval by local Ethical Committees. Experiment 1 (Exp. 1; 3T, TR=2 s; voxel 3 mm iso) included 9 (5F) participants in the execution and 9 (5F) in the imagery condition. Participants executed or imagined virtual grasping of 20 common objects, each prompted by an object picture and repeated 5 times. Experiment 2 (Exp. 2; 3T, TR=1.1 s; voxel 2.4 mm iso) enrolled 24 (14F) participants who underwent both execution and observation conditions. During action execution, participants performed virtual grasping of 8 objects and 8 intransitive hand actions, all cued by a text. During action observation, videos of a hand reproducing the gestures with the participants own kinematics were shown, and each action was repeated 3 times.

Voxel selection

After standard preprocessing, single-gesture beta coefficients were estimated via GLMs. For each experiment, condition and subject, a geodesic-distance searchlight (3 mm radius) was centered on each voxel. Multivoxel beta patterns were classified with leave-one-repetition-out cross-validation using a nearest neighbor classifier based on Euclidean-distance, yielding chance-normalized accuracy for each voxel and action. One-sample t-tests and Fisher combination across subjects identified voxels with significant decoding (q<0.05). A conjunction across all conditions and experiments defined the action-coding voxels included in subsequent analyses.

Representational similarity analysis

To characterize the neural representational geometry of hand actions, we computed a group-level classification-based RDMs for each voxel and condition. To test whether action-coding voxels shared a similar representational structure across domains, Spearman correlations were computed between voxel-wise RDMs for execution-imagery (Exp. 1) and execution-observation (Exp. 2). P-values were combined across experiments using Fisher’s method, yielding to a set of domain-independent voxels with significant representational similarity across all conditions (q<0.05).

Computational models

Three models captured distinct principles underlying hand-action representations. The sinergic model described each action by its kinematic synergies, derived via PCA (5 components) applied to posture data (from 2 in Exp. 1, and acquired via CyberGlove in Exp. 2). Pairwise Euclidean distances were computed in this synergy space generating the model RDMs (Fig.1A). The visual model captured low-level visual features extracted with spatiotemporal Gabor filters applied to object images (Exp. 1) or action videos (Exp. 2). The model RDMs were generated based on pairwise Euclidean distances between visual feature vectors (Fig.1B). The semantic model represented high-level conceptual relations of textual action instructions using OpenAI’s text-embedding models. Pairwise cosine distances between these embeddings yielded the semantic RDMs (Fig.1C).
Models RDMs were rank-normalized and used as predictors in a voxelwise GLM to explain neural RDMs in each condition. For each action-coding voxel, single-predictor R² values quantified variance uniquely explained by each model. For each model, p-values were combined across experimental conditions using Fisher’s method (q<0.05).
To identify the dominant coding principle within domain-independent voxels (i.e., with stable representational structure across action domains), paired t-tests (q<0.05) compared model-specific R² distributions restricted to this subset of voxels.

Cortical gradients

To derive the principal cortical gradients reflecting dominant axes of representational variation, a similarity matrix was computed from execution RDMs and used for diffusion-map embedding. The resulting 2D coordinates were aligned via rigid rotation to maximize correspondence with decoding maps, enabling visualization of effects distribution along these functional gradients (Fig.1 D, E).

Results

Across experiments and domains, individual hand actions were reliably discriminated from single-gesture activity patterns. In Exp. 1, peak decoding emerged in the somatosensory cortex during execution (accuracy=0.59, chance=0.50) and in occipital regions during imagery (accuracy=0.57). In Exp. 2, execution decoding peaked in the left supramarginal gyrus (accuracy=0.58), while observation decoding peaked in the lateral occipital cortex (accuracy=0.58). A conjunction analysis across the four conditions revealed that 1.7% of tested voxels showed significant above-chance decoding (q<0.05) in every condition. These domain-independent action-coding voxels were located in widespread occipito-parietal areas, including dorsal extrastriate cortex, superior parietal lobule, intraparietal sulcus (IPS) and postcentral gyrus. 

To obtain a compact representation of this ensemble of domain-independent areas, we derived two principal cortical gradients based on representational organization during execution, capturing a vision-to-action axis and a secondary dominant-to-contralateral somatomotor axis (Fig.1 D,E). Projecting voxelwise decoding onto cortical gradients revealed non-uniform and domain-dependent organization. Execution decoding was highest toward somatomotor regions, observation toward visual regions, and imagery displayed an intermediate profile. Thus, although the same domain-independent voxels encoded action-specific information across conditions, the distribution of decoding strength varied substantially across functional gradients.

Despite broad overlap in decoding, only a small fraction of domain-independent voxels exhibited a shared representational geometry. Correlations of voxelwise RDMs indicated that 0.7% of domain-independent voxels showed significant (q<0.05) execution–imagery similarity in Exp. 1, and 7.7% displayed execution–observation similarity in Exp. 2. Combining evidence across experiments identified 5.3% of domain-independent voxels with significantly stable representational-similarity across domains. These voxels were predominantly located in the right IPS and adjacent dorsal parietal cortex, suggesting that domain-independent representational stability is spatially circumscribed within the broader action-coding network (Fig.2A).

All three computational models explained significant (q<0.05) portions of the neural representational geometry, but with distinct cortical distributions (Fig.2D). The synergic model captured 33.7% of action-coding voxels, primarily in parietal and somatomotor areas. The visual model was significant in 40% of voxels, mostly in occipital regions. The semantic model explained significantly 27.8% of voxels, located predominantly in lateral temporal areas. Only 3.7% of voxels were jointly explained by all models, whereas 29.2% were not accounted for by any tested model, indicating distributed and partially independent representational principles of hand actions.

Crucially, when the analysis was restricted from all above-chance voxels to the subset exhibiting stable representational geometry across domains, each model still accounted for a significant proportion of variance; however, the synergistic model produced the strongest and most widespread effects. Mean R² was 0.022 for the synergic model, significantly higher than both the visual (R²=0.016) and semantic models (R²=0.016) (paired t-tests, q<0.05) (Fig.2B). The synergic model was significant in 55.7% of these voxels, outperforming both visual (31.5%) and semantic (40.3%) models (Fig.2C). These results indicate that the representational structure preserved across action execution, imagery and observation is primarily grounded in kinematic synergies, with visual and semantic influences playing secondary yet robust roles.

Conclusion

Our findings show that only a restricted portion of the cortical areas that discriminates hand actions during execution, imagery and observation maintains a stable representational geometry across these domains. These domain-independent voxels were confined to a circumscribed region of the right intraparietal and dorsal parietal cortex, indicating that domain-independent action coding is spatially limited within the broader set of action-responsive regions. When testing candidate coding principles, all three computational models (synergic, visual and semantic) contributed significantly to neural representational structure, each with a distinct topographic distribution along the functional gradients. The synergic model showed its strongest effects toward the motor end of the vision-to-action gradient, the visual model peaked at its occipital-visual extreme, and the semantic model was most prominent in intermediate portions of the gradient linking visual and parietal territories. Crucially, within voxels exhibiting domain-independent representational stability, kinematic synergies explained the largest share of variance and outperformed both visual and semantic models. Together, these results suggest that although multiple representational principles jointly shape cortical encoding of hand actions, the portion of cortex that preserves a consistent representational geometry across multiple action domains relies mostly on biomechanical regularities captured by hand synergies. This highlights a dominant kinematic basis for higher-order action coding in dorsal parietal cortex, bridging execution, imagery and observation.
