Introduction

Manual dexterity is referred to as the skill to perform fine motor movements, typically resulting in the ability to write with a pencil, pick up small items, cut with scissors, and other actions requiring precise movements. A method for evaluating manual dexterity is the Nine-Hole Peg Test (9-HPT), according to which a subject is asked to take nine pegs from a container, one by one, place them into nine holes on a board as quickly as possible, and then remove the pegs from the holes, one by one, replacing them back into the container. The score is given by the total time required to complete the test, including both pegs placement and removal phases, recorded by the operator with a stopwatch. The 9-HPT is used in clinical settings in patients with various neurological diagnoses, such as stroke, Parkinson’s disease and multiple sclerosis (MS). Particularly, it has been proposed as a standard measure in MS.^1

Notwithstanding manual dexterity is usually addressed in the assessment of the sensorimotor domain, it is a complex skill that encompasses cognitive components, potentially engaging prefrontal circuits, in line with an extensive definition of manual dexterity, as it requires the ability to cognitively plan and execute a task.^2 Specifically, the 9-HPT could involve cognitive aspects due to the strategy adopted to fill and empty the nine holes as fast as possible. In this vein, the 9-HPT can be considered an optimal tool for assessing manual dexterity, taking into account the cognitive component which coexists with and could overcome the sensorimotor one.

Methods

Methods are based on a recent study in which we investigated the role of cortical areas related to cognitive and sensorimotor functions during the right 9-HPT in twenty-eight right-handed healthy participants. Functional Near-Infrared Spectroscopy (fNIRS) allowed us to assess the hemodynamic response during task performance in an ecological setting, with individuals seated at a table performing the test as in a clinical environment.^3

To discern between sensorimotor and cognitive aspects in manual dexterity, we compared an action that can be related to executive functions required to plan movements based on a choice (9-HPT) with a new control condition, i.e., the One-Hole Peg Test (1-HPT). In the 1-HPT a mask covered all the holes except the central one, and participants were instructed to pick up nine pegs one at a time, place each peg into the only hole, remove it, and place it into a container near the board.

A block-design paradigm was adopted, with alternating 25-s periods of task (i.e., 9-HPT or 1-HPT) and rest (participants remained still with both hands on the table). During the task periods, participants started the test after a “go” signal. Each cycle was repeated three times.

They performed more slowly on the 9-HPT than the 1-HPT. Although both tasks activated sensorimotor areas related to reaching and grasping, participants exhibiting a higher execution time difference (ΔHPT, calculated as 9-HPT - 1-HPT) showed greater activation in right prefrontal regions (Brodmann Area (BA) 10 and BA 11). A linear relationship between ΔHPT and right BA 10 activity suggested that individuals with greater performance discrepancies recruited prefrontal regions for processes such as planning and control, though this recruitment did not translate into improved speed.

Here, we conducted a similar fNIRS study in MS. Twenty-two right-handed people with MS (PwMS) (16 relapsing-remitting and 6 secondary progressive, median Expanded Disability Status Scale = 4) were asked to perform the 9-HPT and the 1-HPT as fast as possible with the right hand. As in the typical clinical context, after one familiarization trial, the time required to insert and remove the nine pegs was averaged over two trials.

fNIRS (NIRSport, NIRx) was used to study cortical activity in bilateral prefrontal and sensorimotor areas. Two 8x8 source and detector systems with 760 & 850 nm LED were used in tandem mode. A customized montage was implemented to obtain 40 standard (source-detector distance: 3 cm) and 8 short-separation (source-detector distance: 8 mm) channels. The standard channels were assigned to BAs with fOLD (fNIRS Optodes’ Location Decider). Data pre-processing was performed in MATLAB (MathWorks, MA, USA) through in-house scripts and some of the Homer3 NIRS processing package functions. Channels with SNR<2 in the optical density time-courses were discarded, motion artifacts were identified by applying the function hmrR_MotionArtifactByChannel on changes in optical density data and corrected by applying a combination of spline and wavelet motion correction techniques, a band-pass filter (0.01-1.5 Hz) was applied to remove slow drifts and high frequencies.

A General Linear Model (GLM) was performed to estimate the hemodynamic response function (HRF) at the individual level, with regression of short separation channels to reduce the physiological noise. Block average analysis was performed over a time interval from -5 to 40 s relative to task onset. Given the variability in task duration among participants, a new procedure was implemented to extract the average values of the oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO) concentration changes, used for statistical analysis. A temporal smoothing was applied to the individual HRF (cut-off frequency: 0.08 Hz) to identify the peak, defined as the maximum value of the smoothed response. A 6-s time window was then centered around the detected peak on the original HRF, and the average HbO value within that window was computed (Fig. 1).

Results

On average, the time to complete the 9-HPT was longer with respect to the 1-HPT (23.80±0.96 s vs. 21.83±0.86 s, p<0.001). Then, we considered two subgroups based on the median value of the difference in execution times, i.e., ΔHPT (9-HPT - 1-HPT): High Difference (HD) and Low Difference (LD). In the HD group, 9-HPT completion time was significantly longer than 1-HPT, whereas in the LD group no difference emerged between the two tasks. However, there were no significant performance differences between the HD and LD groups, and both groups showed longer completion times compared with healthy participants from the previous study (on average, 9-HPT = 17.18 ± 0.27 s; 1-HPT = 16.36 ± 0.25 s). This indicated that, overall, PwMS performed worse than healthy controls, and that in the LD group the 1-HPT was already as slow as the 9-HPT.

In the whole PwMS group, the two tasks elicited increased HbO concentration changes in similar cortical areas. With respect to healthy participants, PwMS showed a pattern suggestive of a compensatory network, characterized by bilateral activation involving prefrontal regions (BA 9, 11, and 46) and sensorimotor regions (BA 3, 4, and 6).

Interestingly, during the 9-HPT a significant difference between the two groups emerged in the left BA 10, with higher activation in the HD group: PwMS who were substantially slower on 9-HPT relative to 1-HPT showed greater activation in this area (Fig. 2). This finding may indicate a shift in cognitive strategy during the 9-HPT with respect to the control condition, leading to increased engagement of prefrontal areas.

Furthermore, the LD group showed significantly higher Modified Fatigue Impact Scale (MFIS, assessing the impact of subjective fatigue on physical, cognitive, and psychosocial functioning over the past four weeks) scores than the HD group, both in total score (47.1±22.5 vs. 28.4±14.2, p = 0.03) and in the cognitive sub-scale (20.8±11.2 vs. 8.5±7.3 p = 0.006).

Conclusion

Overall, differently from healthy subjects, PwMS engaged prefrontal regions (BA 9 and 11) to support the execution of manual dexterity tasks. When considering two groups of PwMS on the basis of the time difference between the standard test and the control condition, individuals belonging to the Low Difference (LD) group did not perform as well as healthy controls on fine motor tasks: their performance in the 1-HPT was similar to the 9-HPT, suggesting reduced selectivity and precision of fine motor control (i.e., difficulty in isolating the movement needed to insert the peg into the only hole). In addition, the LD group reported higher cognitive MFIS scores, suggesting greater perceived cognitive fatigue, which may further impact motor planning and execution.

The LD group could not plan, due to inefficient recruitment of the left BA 10, which is part of the Cognitive Reserve network.^4 BA 10 corresponds to the rostral frontal cortex and has a role in prospective memory, in the evaluation of alternative action plans and in planning processes about sequences of actions. This area may become essential to control processes especially in an aging or pathological populations during challenging tasks.

In conclusion, attention should be paid to the cognitive domain when assessing manual dexterity, particularly in clinical populations in which both motor and cognitive functions may be affected, such as in multiple sclerosis.
