Introduction

Urban environments are known to influence cognitive and mental health, yet most research treats urban morphology as static, leaving its long-term dynamics largely unexamined. Here, we delineated dynamic urban morphology using a taxonomy of urban characteristics and investigated how trajectories of morphological change relate to cognition, mental health, and brain structure and function.

Methods

Using supervised classification, rule-based filtering, and urban change detection, we reconstructed lifetime urban morphology for 5,848 Chinese Imaging Genetics (CHIMGEN) participants with geocoded residential histories from birth.^1 Recent and dynamic influences of urban morphology were evaluated across nine cognitive domains, nine mental health indicators, and 1,084 imaging-derived phenotypes. Trajectory patterns were identified using K-means clustering and growth mixture modeling. We further applied a distributed lag non-linear model to quantify sensitive windows and used convergent cross mapping to assess phenotypes potentially driven by urban morphological dynamics.

Results

Dynamic profiles captured individual differences in lifetime exposure to urban morphology. Urban morphology was associated with cognitive performance and behavioral measures, including language learning and working memory, and was also linked to variations across multiple neuroimaging modalities. Distinct trajectory types showed long-term associations with brain phenotypes. Individuals experiencing more frequent morphological change exhibited increased ASL signal, and those moving toward areas with higher urban morphology scores showed greater gray matter volume. Sensitive windows varied across phenotypes, and traits related to the social environment showed stronger evidence of being shaped by urban dynamic exposure.

Conclusion

Urban morphology evolves across the lifespan, and these trajectories relate to individual differences in cognition, mental health, and brain organization. Several questions remain open, and further refinement of dynamic exposure modeling may help uncover additional mechanisms.
