Introduction

Intracranial volume (ICV) is a fundamental marker in neuroimaging, as it enables the normalization of brain volumes and allows accurate comparisons of morphology across individuals. Its proper estimation is particularly relevant in studies of development, aging, and neurodegeneration.1 However, existing ICV models have been built predominantly from samples almost exclusively focused on adult populations from WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic) countries. This lack of representativeness introduces biases that compromise the validity of reference values and restricts our understanding of human anatomical variability. Moreover, previous studies2 have demonstrated meaningful differences across populations, underscoring the importance of including minority and historically underrepresented groups to reliably characterize the full spectrum of brain morphometric diversity.

In this context, the inclusion of underrepresented populations is essential for advancing an equitable neuroanatomical science and for improving the design of personalized, population-specific clinical treatments. Our work addresses these limitations by leveraging an exceptionally large, heterogeneous, and globally representative dataset that includes T1w MR scans from 99 countries and spans the entire human lifespan, from birth to 100 years. This broad coverage is particularly noteworthy, as many previous studies exclude children and rarely include individuals older than 60 years. Additionally, the used dataset maintains a nearly equal distribution of males and females, enabling more stable and comparable sex-specific estimations.

Using this diverse cohort, we developed a robust ICV model for males and females across the lifespan, along with region-specific models for seven major world global areas to characterize population-level differences. This study provides the most comprehensive foundation to date for volumetric normalization and global-scale brain morphometric analysis.

Methods

Data

The data used in this study originate from the volBrain platform (volbrain.net), a widely used neuroimaging system for segmenting and quantifying brain structures from MR scans. The volBrain platform has processed more than 770,000 cases worldwide in the last 10 years. From those cases, a subset of 423,226 cases was kept in our database with the permission of the users for research purposes. The dataset used in this study includes 106,481 unique T1-weighted MRI scans with full demographic information (age, sex and country). All images were processed using one of the volBrain pipelines, each providing ICV estimates. Note that our ICV delineation includes only brain tissues (grey and white matter) and cerebrospinal fluid (i.e., excluding the meninges).  

To ensure quality, we excluded scans processed with diagnostic pipelines such as cognitiveTree. Additionally, we incorporated 422 scans from children aged 9-14 years from The Queensland Twin Adolescent Brain Project3, to mitigate the lack of data for this age range in Oceania.

The final dataset comprises 98,099 unique cases (52.7% female, 47.3% male), ranging from birth to nearly 100 years and representing individuals from 99 countries distributed across five continents (excluding Antarctica).

Model Selection and Outlier Detection

To model the lifespan ICV evolution, several models were tested. We used the model selection procedure followed by Coupé et al. 2016.4 First, six regression models were fitted: linear, quadratic, cubic, and their hybrid variants, including an exponential term for early-life growth dynamics. In this initial step, only centred age was included as a covariate, and robust statistics were applied to limit outlier influence.

Candidate models were identified using ANOVA, based on the F-statistic and coefficient statistical significance. Among the selected candidates, the final model was chosen according to the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Sex was then added as a covariate.

Outliers were detected through an iterative procedure: a model was fitted to all cases, residual standard deviations were computed separately for males and females, and observations with absolute residuals exceeding three standard deviations were flagged as outliers. A new model was fitted using inliers, and the process was repeated iteratively until the residual standard deviation was not decreasing by more than 0.5% over two consecutive iterations (evaluated for males and females independently). After removing outliers, a final regression model (see  Figure 1) was fitted including pipeline information as dummy variables.

ICV Analysis by Geographic Region

To investigate ICV across geographic regions, data from 99 countries were grouped into seven major areas (Africa, Asia, Europe, North America, South America, Central America, and Oceania) following the regional divisions defined by the United Nations. The number of cases for these regions is 3,816; 38,836; 24,742; 9,920; 10,365; 8,436 and 1,984 respectively.

Independent models were obtained for each region using a bootstrapping-based approach. Specifically, 200 iterations were generated, and in each iteration, a sample of 4,000 cases was selected through replacement-based sampling balanced by sex and age. Each bootstrap sample included 200 women and 200 men per 10-year age interval, ensuring demographic comparability between regions.

From these models, the bootstrap distributions of regression coefficients were obtained. Subsequently, we computed the correlations between the mean coefficient values (estimated for each demographic region) and reference anthropometric and socioeconomic variables using a Leave-One-Out approach. Region anthropometric values were calculated as the average of the countries represented within each region in our dataset, based on available population statistics.

Results

Using the iterative outlier removal method, a total of 2,212 outliers were identified; therefore, the final model was fitted using 95,887 cases. The selected model for ICV was the Cubic Hybrid Model (1), where sex is coded as -1 for females and +1 for males, and age is expressed as centred age (i.e., each subject’s age minus the mean age of the dataset, which is 44 years). Coefficient values are 1393.19, –0.52, 5.7×10-3, –2×10-4, 2.2×10-4, 2.96, 79.62, and 0.31 for β0, β1, β2, β3, β4, β5, β6, and β7, respectively.

The estimated mean ICV for a middle-aged woman was 1,313.6 cm3, whereas for a middle-aged man it was 1,472.8 cm3. This difference primarily reflects the larger cranial size typically observed in men. The resulting male-to-female ratio (1.12) is consistent with values reported in previous studies.1

During early life, ICV increases exponentially until reaching a plateau, around age 15 in females and age 17 in males. Furthermore, the model reveals a decline in ICV with advancing age for both sexes, in agreement with previous findings.5 This decrease accelerates progressively over the lifespan and is more pronounced in women. Specifically, the reduction reaches about 2 cm3/year in women and 1.4 cm3/year in men, being the total loss of 94 cm3 and 41 cm3 for females and males respectively.

Several age-related mechanisms may contribute to this ICV reduction. First, it has been shown that the subarachnoid space and dural sinuses undergo calcification with age, and when combined with thickening of the dura mater, these changes may reduce intracranial volume.5 Additionally, increased intracranial pressure and age-associated dehydration could further accelerate the decline in the intracranial compartment over time.

Regarding the analysis of ICV across the proposed geographic regions, the results indicate statistically significant differences between them, as shown in Figure 2, with intercept values consistent with those reported in previous studies.1 These variations may be attributed to several factors, for instance, greater exposure to sunlight increases vitamin D levels, which can lead to an earlier closure of the fontanelles and consequently a smaller intracranial volume. Likewise, more favourable socioeconomic conditions are associated with improved growth and development, potentially resulting in larger ICV values.

In fact, β0, that is ICV for a middle-aged person, shows a strong correlation with height (r = 0.69 ± 0.08), which is expected, given that taller individuals typically have larger head sizes and therefore a greater intracranial volume. Furthermore, the correlation of β0 with socioeconomic variables such as Gender Inequality Index (r = -0.79 ± 0.07) or Population with at least some secondary education (rmales = 0.86 ± 0.05, rfemales = 0.83 ± 0.06) become statistically significant. 

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, the demographic information is self-reported by users. In addition, we cannot ensure that all cases are cognitive normal, since that information is not collected. Lastly, we assume that the observed variability reflects individual differences, but it could also arise from differences between scanners or acquisition protocols. Nonetheless, it is worth noting that all pipelines on the volBrain platform perform an extensive preprocessing specifically designed to minimize potential differences arising from the use of different scanners and/or acquisition protocols.

Conclusion

In this study, we generated and analysed what is probably the largest, most diverse, and geographically representative dataset available to date for estimating intracranial volume, comprising over 98,000 individuals from 99 countries, with ages ranging from birth to 100 years. From this global cohort, which is highly balanced by sex and exceptionally heterogeneous in demographic characteristics, we developed a robust ICV model based on a cubic hybrid regression approach combined with iterative and robust outlier detection methods.

The results confirm well-established patterns of brain growth and aging, showing a rapid increase in ICV during the first years of life, followed by stabilization in adolescence and a small but gradual decline with age. A clear sex difference is also observed, with higher ICV values in males, consistent with previous studies.

Regional analysis revealed statistically significant differences in ICV across continents. These variations likely reflect a combination of biological, environmental, and socioeconomic factors, including differences in average height, and sunlight exposure, all of which may influence cranial morphology. The strong correlation observed between socioeconomic variables and ICV further underscores the interdependence between these factors and intracranial volume on a global scale.

Overall, these findings highlight the critical importance of including diverse and traditionally underrepresented populations in neuroimaging studies. Incorporating a truly international cohort allows for a more accurate characterization of human brain morphometric variability and represents an essential step toward building normative models that are fairer, more precise, and broadly generalizable. This work provides a solid foundation for future research on neurodevelopment, aging, and volumetric normalization, and paves the way for more personalized and culturally inclusive clinical approaches.
