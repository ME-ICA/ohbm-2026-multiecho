Introduction

Several findings suggest an aberrant cerebrospinal (CSF)-glymphatic system (CSF-GLYM) in schizophrenia-spectrum disorders. For instance, schizophrenia or psychotic symptoms have been linked with choroid plexus alterations, proinflammatory markers and aberrant AQ4, all elements of the CSF-GLYM.1 Moreover, recent investigations of the coupling between global grey matter fMRI signal fluctuations and fourth ventricle-CSF signals in patients during early psychosis indicate lower coupling strength within patients with more severe symptoms, likely indicating impaired CSF flow dynamics.2

Perivascular spaces (PVS) represent CSF-filled spaces surrounding arterial and venous vessels that penetrate the brain tissue. They are viewed as part of the CSF-GLYM, taking up a pivotal role in the clearance of toxic and waste metabolites. Fluid in PVS can be visualized broadly in healthy subjects and patients with brain disorders by using 3T structural MRI, particularly in the basal ganglia and centrum semiovale. So far, visual rating scales have been commonly used for PVS MRI analysis. Furthermore, deep learning approaches have recently emerged as a promising method for PVS segmentation, with the convolutional neural network, specifically U-Net, becoming a gold standard.3 

Given the involvement of CSF in schizophrenia-spectrum disorders and the role of PVS within the CSF-glymphatic system, examining PVS characteristics in schizophrenia and first episode psychosis (FEP) is of particular interest. Accordingly, this project aims to investigate whether PVS volumes in patients with schizophrenia or during FEP, respectively, are impaired. 

Methods

We included T2-weighted 3T MRI data from four datasets comprising individuals with schizophrenia, FEP and healthy controls. For the schizophrenia cohort, data were obtained from the open-source database OpenNeuro (The Neural Basis of Visual Shape Completion in Schizophrenia and Bipolar Disorder as well as from the corresponding healthy-control study Brain network mechanisms of visual shape completion), the REWARD data record retrieved from the public repository SchizConnect, and the ongoing Schizophrenia Long study, currently being conducted at the University Hospital Klinikum Rechts der Isar TUM in Munich (unpublished). Regarding first episode psychosis, data were obtained from the HCP Early Psychosis Release 1.1 by the Human Connectome Project. In total, the schizophrenia dataset included 48 schizophrenic patients and 50 healthy controls, distributed across three scanners, and the early-psychosis dataset included 89 patients and 57 controls, also distributed across three scanners.

All data were visually inspected for common MRI artefacts, and affected images were excluded. After brain-extraction and registration to MNI space of each subject’s data, PVS were segmented using an nnU-Net model,4 which had been trained on 30 manually segmented T2w images using ITK-Snap (Figure 1A–C). T1w images from all subjects were processed using Freesurfer to create white matter parcellation masks and to determine the whole brain volume. 

Next, the PVS volumes were determined in 11 anatomical regions based on the Desikan-Killiany atlas: frontal lobe, parietal lobe, temporal lobe, occipital lobe, cingulate, insula, white matter, unsegmented white matter, total white matter (white matter & unsegmented white matter), basal ganglia with thalamus and whole brain (Figure 1D).  Apart from volume, PVS count served as an additional metric. FSL’s cluster tool with connectivity 26 was used to calculate cluster counts for the whole brain, as well as for each region (right hemisphere, left hemisphere, and total). 

All statistical analyses were performed using Python and SPSS. To ensure the reliability of the chosen datasets, 25 brain regions commonly affected by alterations in volume and size were examined. After scanner harmonization and exclusion of outliers, both parametric t-tests and non-parametric Mann-Whitney U tests were performed while controlling for sex, eTIV and age. FDR correction after Benjamini-Hochberg (α = 0.05) was applied. For PVS analysis, PVS volumes were normalized by dividing the PVS volume by the corresponding regional volume. After scanner harmonization and exclusion of outliers, a Mann-Whitney U test was performed while controlling for covariates sex and age, followed by FDR correction. 

Results

To assess data reliability, group comparisons were performed between patients with schizophrenia and controls. Both Mann-Whitney U tests and parametric t-tests revealed a significant difference in volume in the insula and hippocampus, which remained significant after FDR correction (pFDR = 0.025 for each region). These findings correspond with regions commonly altered, thus supporting the reliability of the dataset.5

Similarly, group comparisons between patients with early psychosis and controls revealed significant differences in structural measures in the fourth ventricle and mean cortical thickness (consistent across Mann–Whitney U and parametric t-tests), which remained significant even after FDR correction (pFDR = 0.05 for the fourth ventricle and pFDR = 0.025 for mean cortical thickness), supporting the reliability of this dataset as well. 

The comparison of PVS volumes between patients with schizophrenia and controls did not reveal a significant difference in any region after FDR-correction. Concerning PVS-counts in this cohort, the comparison did not reveal a significant difference in any region after FDR-correction. 

Regarding the early-psychosis vs. control cohort, PVS volume comparisons did not reveal a significant difference in any region after FDR-correction. In terms of PVS counts, the comparison between patients with psychosis and controls revealed differences in three regions: the parietal lobe (p = 0.038), the temporal lobe (p = 0.030), and the cingulate gyrus (p = 0.033), but none of these group differences survived FDR correction. 

Conclusion

Our findings do not suggest impaired PVS volume or count in patients with early psychosis or chronic schizophrenia. Initial uncorrected tendencies in the early psychosis cohort did not withstand FDR correction. Larger cohorts or single-scanner studies may be required to reveal significant PVS alterations and further clarify the role of PVS in early psychosis and schizophrenia.
