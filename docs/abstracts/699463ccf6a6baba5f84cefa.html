Introduction

Brain network interactions can be characterized using undirected (functional relations between nodes) and directed models.1 The latter class of models can provide some insights into the nature of the “flow” of information across brain networks. Several classes of such directed models exist. Effective connectivity versions of directed models include Dynamic Causal Models which rely on a priori model architectures (subsequently compared in a Bayesian framework) and incorporates biophysical models mapping the neuronal response into fMRI signals.2 Functional connectivity versions of directed models are based on analyses of statistical relationships between time series, such as the established method of Wiener-Granger Causality (WGC), which estimates causal directionality between pairs of regions (r1, r2), based on principles of temporal precedence between their time series.3 We applied WGC to explore task-evoked fluctuations in the causal relationships between brain regions and recover plausible patterns of information “flow”. We further adopted a graph-theoretical analysis approach to observe directional connectivity between nodes during visuo-motor task performance (sustained excitation vs. occasional inhibition) and characterize the roles that these nodes play in the brain network, by quantifying their incoming and outgoing connections to other nodes.

Methods

For our data collection, we used a basic visuo-motor integration task to drive information “flow” in the brain. The task (Figure 1) required participants to respond to a presented visual stimulus (red or green square) by finger tapping, implying that the directional flow of information followed a sensorimotor direction (visual sensation → motor action). Specifically, we evoked contextual responding using two conditions: 1) During sustained response excitation, participants were instructed to respond irrespective of the stimulus color (red or green square); 2) During occasional response inhibition, participants were instructed to inhibit responses to red squares (20%). These task conditions were curated specifically on account of the differing classes of demands they place on brain network activity and are therefore likely to have non-additive effects. The sustained excitation condition therefore evokes ongoing, visuo-motor coupling with minimal demands on cognitive control. The occasional inhibition condition, conversely, is designed to explicitly break visuo-motor coupling. We acquired fMRI data from the task in two groups of participants: OCD (n=36) and healthy controls (HC, n=26) [Gradient-echo echo-planar imaging (EPI) fMRI data were obtained using a 3 Tesla Siemens Verio system; 12-channel volume head coil (TR: 2.6 s, TE: 29 ms, FOV: 256×256mm2, acquisition matrix: 128×128, 36 axial slices, voxel dimensions: 2×2 x 3 mm3); 3D T1-weighted anatomical MRI image was also acquired (TR: 2200 ms, TI: 778 ms, TE: 3 ms, flip angle = 13°, FOV: 256×256mm2, 256 axial slices of thickness = 1.0 mm, matrix = 256×256)]. After pre-processing the fMRI data and extracting time series from a functionally-defined cerebral atlas, we used WGC to measure the strength of directed functional connectivity (r1 → r2 vs. r2 → r1) which measure the dynamic causal relationship between two time series.4 Our initial method included the use of inferential statistics to test significant effects related to Group (HC ≠ OCD) and Direction (MVAR(r1 → r2) ≠ MVAR(r2 → r1)). We further extended our analyses through use of graph theoretical measures (in- and out-degree centrality) to characterize local network organization. For each node, we computed in- and out-degree centrality quantify a node’s cumulative (as opposed to pairwise) role across the functional connectome, representing each node as both an information “sink” and an information “source”. High in-degree centrality may be interpreted as a region that receives a large amount of information from its functional neighbors, while high out-degree centrality suggests that a region is a source of information dissemination.5

Results

In our primary analysis high out-degree centrality was observed in frontal paracentral lobes, temporal superior, inferior, and posterior lobes, parietal superior and precuneus lobes, subcortical amygdala and thalamus regions (regardless of group). High in-degree centrality was observed in thalamic nodes, inferior parietal lobe (IPL), parietal postcentral gyrus, insular, limbic cingulate lobes, and subcortical basal ganglia (regardless of group). Secondary analyses (Figure 2) were constructed to capture relative inter-group differences in in- and out-degree centrality. Here, most regions (excluding middle temporal gyrus, fusiform gyrus, posterior superior temporal gyrus, insular gyrus, and both cortices in the occipital lobe) demonstrated lower out-degree centrality for OCD. The paracentral lobule and superior frontal gyrus, along with the superior parietal lobule, postcentral gyrus, and amygdala, demonstrated particularly high out-degree centrality in HC groups during sustained excitation. During occasional inhibition, there was greater out-degree centrality in OCD groups for all regions excluding the parahippocampal gyrus, precuneus, cingulate gyrus, hippocampus, and basal ganglia. Out-degree centrality in OCD group was particularly greater in the medioventral occipital cortex during occasional inhibition. We observed similar patterns for in-degree centrality measures, where, during sustained excitation, in-degree centrality was HC greater in all regions excluding the superior frontal gyrus, paracentral lobule, postcentral gyrus, and hippocampus. In-degree centrality measures during occasional inhibition yielded similar results to out-degree centrality during the same task condition, with greater degree centrality being observed in OCD groups for most regions. 

Conclusion

In our results we identified regions exhibiting high degree centrality. Initially, our pairwise WGC analyses did not identify significant group differences between OCD and HC participants in the directionality of neural interactions. As aforementioned, given the known pathology of OCD (functional connectivity disruptions within the FST circuit) this finding was unexpected. However, the absence of group differences may suggest that fundamental directional information flow during basic motor control tasks remains largely intact in OCD patients. Our graph-theoretical analyses suggested inter-group differences in degree central in multiple regions suggesting subtle differences in the flow of information across the brain during task-based function. Our findings advance understanding of directed brain network interactions during motor control in OCD and suggest that a combination of task design, and multiple analytic methods may be better suited to understanding the nature of functional brain deficits in the illness.
