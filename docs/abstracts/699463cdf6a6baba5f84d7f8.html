Introduction

Metacognition, the ability to monitor and evaluate one’s own cognitive processes, is central to adaptive decision-making and learning. By estimating the reliability of their perceptions and judgments, individuals can regulate behavior accordingly. Beyond individual performance, metacognition also plays a key role in collective decision-making, enabling confidence coordination, efficient information sharing, and improved joint outcomes1.

Although collaborative decision-making has been widely studied in adults, far less is known about how collaboration shapes metacognition during childhood, a developmental period marked by ongoing maturation of both social cognition and self-evaluation. Adult studies demonstrate that metacognitive judgments are socially influenced: individuals integrate others’ confidence1 and interacting partners often converge toward similar confidence levels, a phenomenon termed confidence matching2. Whether children show comparable social modulation of metacognition, and how this ability changes with age, remains largely unexplored.

Building on research on collective intelligence and the “wisdom of crowds”3, this study investigates how social interaction influences metacognitive ability in children. Using a perceptual decision-making task with confidence judgments, we assess individual and joint metacognitive performance within child dyads.

Research questions: 1- Does collaboration enhance metacognitive ability?; 2- Does the metacognitive benefit of collaboration differ between younger and older children, indicating developmental changes in social metacognitive integration?; and 3- Does individual metacognitive ability change over the course of the task, as assessed by early versus late trial comparisons, and does this differ by age group?

Methods

A total of 84 children (42 dyads) were recruited from a local urban school in Vitoria, Spain. The final sample comprised 38 younger children aged 8–9(Grade 3) and 38 older children aged 11–12(Grade 5), with an equal distribution of females and males. All participants were native Spanish speakers with normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no reported history of reading or learning disorders. Participation was voluntary, written informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians, and all procedures were approved by the BCBL Ethics Board. Children received a small prize for participation.

The experiment was programmed in OpenSesame(v3.3.12) and run on a Dell computer connected to two identical Dell LCD monitors(1920×1080 resolution) that mirrored each other. Participants sat at right angles, each facing their own screen at a viewing distance of approximately 59 cm, with physical occluders ensuring that each child could only see their own display. Stimuli consisted of centrally presented Gabor patches (sinusoidal grating with Gaussian envelope), tilted slightly leftward or rightward relative to vertical. Task difficulty was individually calibrated using a 2-down-1-up adaptive staircase targeting ~70% accuracy. This was done to match the level of type-1 performance across dyads and thereby any effect of collaboration in metacognitive performance(meta-d’) cannot be attributed to differences in type-1 performance. Calibration followed a two-stage procedure, beginning with a coarse staircase (starting at 21° with 2° steps) and followed by a finer staircase (starting at their final degree with 1° steps), ensuring comparable perceptual difficulty across dyad members while maintaining variability necessary for metacognitive analyses. Responses were collected via two separate keyboards: the “left” participant used the Z/X keys and number keys 1–6, and the “right” participant used the N/M keys and the numeric keypad 1–6.

The task consisted of repeated perceptual judgments completed jointly by each dyad. Each trial followed a fixed four-phase sequence: individual decision, individual confidence rating, dyadic discussion and joint decision, and joint confidence rating. On each trial, both children viewed the same Gabor stimulus simultaneously and first made a private left/right judgment, followed by a private confidence rating on a 6-point scale illustrated as betting candies (1= very unsure; 6= very sure). After both private responses were recorded, children were shown each other’s choices and confidence ratings and were allowed to discuss freely before reaching a single joint decision, entered by the experimenter. Dyads then provided a joint confidence rating using the same scale, reflecting their collective certainty. To avoid trial-by-trial feedback, accumulated points were revealed every ten trials. Each trial yielded four primary measures: individual choice, individual confidence, joint choice, and joint confidence.

Analyses were conducted separately for younger children, older children, and joint decisions. First, we quantified basic perceptual performance using type-1 accuracy and d′. Second, we examined confidence–performance relationships. Third, we assessed metacognitive sensitivity using meta-d’ derived from signal detection theory. To test whether collaboration enhances metacognitive sensitivity, and whether any collaborative benefit differs by age group, we fitted linear mixed-effects models with agent (individual vs. joint) as a within-dyad factor and age group as a between-dyad factor, including random intercepts for dyads and children nested within dyads to account for non-independence. To assess potential learning effects in Collaboratory decision making over time, we additionally tested whether individual metacognitive sensitivity changed across the task by splitting trials into early and late bins and modelling meta-d′ as a function of Time, Age group, and their interaction using linear mixed-effects models.

Results

Mean type-1 sensitivity (d′) was 1.07 for younger children, 1.22 for older children, and 1.40 for joint decisions. Joint decisions showed significantly higher metacognitive sensitivity (meta-d′) than individual decisions made by both younger and older children. Specifically, joint meta-d′ exceeded that of younger individuals (t = −3.47, p = .0009) and older individuals (t = −2.17, p = .033) and was higher than individual performance overall (t = 3.24, p = .0018). This pattern of results suggests that collaboration increased metacognitive ability, namely, how well confidence tracked accuracy.

Analyses of time-on-task revealed no change in metacognitive efficiency across early versus late phases of the task, either overall or as a function of age group, and no differential improvement between individual and joint decisions over time. Together, these findings suggest that while collaboration enhances children’s metacognitive sensitivity, it does not lead to progressive metacognitive learning across the task.

Conclusion

When collaborating, dyads outperform both younger and older children in discriminating correct from incorrect decisions using confidence, despite equivalent levels of individual type-1 perceptual performance. Hence any effect of collaboration on metacognitive ability can’t be explained by differences in type-1 sensitivity across the different members of the dyad. Crucially, this pattern was stable across the task. Analyses comparing early and late phases revealed no change in metacognitive sensitivity (meta-d′) over time, either overall or as a function of age group, and no differential improvement between individual and joint decisions across the sessions. Although null effects should be interpreted with caution, this pattern of results suggests that neither individual practice nor continued interaction led to progressive metacognitive learning or calibration within the time scale of the experiment. Importantly, this stability is consistent with the limited exposure to collaboration, as each dyad completed only two to three sessions. Detecting additional improvements in metacognitive ability during collaborative decision making may therefore require longer-term or longitudinal designs that allow children sufficient time to internalize and refine confidence-based strategies through repeated social interaction. Instead, the present findings suggest that collaborative advantages in metacognitive ability arise very quickly and are sustained.

The present results indicating benefits in metacognitive ability through collaboration raises an important question: how do children integrate and adapt to each other’s confidence during interaction? To address this, we will examine the dynamics of confidence matching, asking whether children align their confidence with their partner over time, whether this process differs between younger and older children, and how confidence integration shapes joint judgments. By focusing on trial-by-trial confidence adjustments, these analyses aim to identify the social mechanisms through which collaboration enhances metacognitive sensitivity.

Beyond behavior, the present findings motivate future investigation into the neural mechanisms underlying social metacognition during development. The observed collaborative benefits likely arise from decision-level integration or evidence pooling, rather than from changes in the efficiency of individual confidence computations. An important next step is therefore to examine how the reported effects are instantiated in the brain during neural development and social interactions. For instance, future work is needed to investigate the neural correlates of social metacognition using developmentally appropriate hyperscanning approaches. In particular, how behavioral signatures of confidence integration and partner influence are associated with prefrontal cortical activation and inter-brain synchrony between interacting children during collaborative decision-making. Prior research has shown that prefrontal regions4 play a central role in metacognitive monitoring and control, and that coordinated neural activity emerges between individuals during cooperative and communicative tasks5. Linking behavioral confidence alignment to such neural coupling would help clarify how social interaction shapes metacognitive processes across development.
