Introduction

The human brain undergoes a prolonged and complex process of structural and functional maturation, beginning in pregnancy and continuing through infancy and adolescence into adulthood. Accurately quantifying these age-related changes through appropriate trajectory modeling is crucial for understanding healthy brain development and for detecting and characterizing atypical patterns, such as those observed in autism spectrum disorders.

In this study, we assessed non-invasive, quantitative regional measures of resting cerebral blood flow (CBF) in 399 participants aged 2 to 31 years using Arterial Spin Labeling (ASL). Our objectives were threefold: (1) to precisely model growth trajectories for age-normed quantification of resting CBF; (2) to characterize atypical brain development in individuals with autism spectrum disorders (ASD); and (3) to develop and provide an interactive, open-source online tool for exploring these statistical models.

Methods

292 typically developing (TD) subjects (160males, 132 females, age = 12.4 ± 7.5 years, range: 2.15–31.28 years) and 107 with a clinical diagnosis of ASD (89 males, 18 females; age = 9.9 ± 6.6 years; range: 2.32–29.46 years) participated in this study. All MRI exams included T1-weighted and ASL sequences and were acquired on General Electric Signa 1.5T and Discovery 3 T MRI scanners. MRI images were preprocessed using Statistical Parametric Mapping 12 (SPM12; Welcome Department of Cognitive Neurology. London, UK, www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm). Mean resting CBF (ml/100 g/min) was extracted from the 128 brain regions of the AAL atlas (Rolls et al. 2020). For each brain region, we applied generalized additive models for location, scale, and shape (GAMLSS) with a generalized gamma distribution to model non-linear age-related changes in cerebral blood flow (CBF). The models simultaneously estimated mean (μ), variability (σ), and skewness (ν) as smooth functions of age, while accounting for group (TD vs. ASD), their interaction with age, sex, and random effects of scanner. Robust inference was obtained via 10,000 bootstrap replications, providing age-specific predictions, percentiles, and empirical p-values. Results were implemented in an open-access Shiny platform for transparent and interactive visualization.

Results

The interactive online platform (https://brainappy.shinyapps.io/perfbrainmap/) enables the investigation of age-related cerebral blood flow (CBF) trajectories in both neurotypical and atypical populations across cortical and subcortical regions. The tool provides (1) percentile-based growth curves and (2) distribution parameter estimates for location (μ), scale (σ), and shape (ν), thereby allowing a detailed characterization of normative and atypical developmental patterns. In addition, it identifies the spatial (regional) and temporal (age-related) points at which the two groups significantly diverge for each of these distribution parameters. Notably, one of the significant age x group interactions was observed in the right superior temporal cortex (μ component, first term: Z = 2.13, p = 0.033), with group separations in location detected between 2–7 years, 12–15.5 years, and 25.5–26 years at a 99% confidence level (see figure 1).

Conclusion

In this study, we established precise normative trajectories of regional resting cerebral blood flow (CBF) across the first decades of life in a large cohort by applying advanced generalized additive models for location, scale, and shape (GAMLSS). Beyond defining normative growth charts, this framework enabled stratification between typically developing (TD) and autism spectrum disorder (ASD) participants, thereby highlighting brain regions with divergent age-related CBF trajectories, such as the right superior temporal cortex, and identifying age-specific developmental stages where such divergences emerge. This quantitative approach provides a powerful tool to delineate region-specific critical windows of brain development and to advance our understanding of the dynamic processes underlying atypical trajectories in neurodevelopmental disorders.
