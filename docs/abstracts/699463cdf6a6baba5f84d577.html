Introduction

The basal ganglia are central to dopaminergic signaling, which is known to be disrupted in schizophrenia spectrum disorders (SSD) and contributes to deficits in cognitive and emotional regulation. Prior studies have consistently reported structural abnormalities in basal ganglia nuclei in SSD,1 such as enlarged globus pallidus, but the underlying microstructural and biochemical mechanisms remain poorly understood.

Recent work has highlighted brain iron abnormalities as a potential mechanistic framework for interpreting basal ganglia alterations in SSD. Brain iron is essential for dopamine synthesis, mitochondrial metabolism, and myelin production in the brain, and the basal ganglia contain disproportionately high iron concentrations relative to the rest of the brain. Emerging evidence suggests that peripheral iron deficiency and brain iron dysregulation may contribute to cognitive vulnerability during development,2 with preliminary MRI findings showing an association between reduced basal ganglia iron levels and more severe symptoms in SSD.3 However, due to the complex nature of iron metabolism and storage mechanisms in the brain, in vivo characterization of brain iron in SSD remains challenging.

Here, we use multimodal quantitative MRI (qMRI) to noninvasively probe iron- and microstructure-sensitive tissue properties in young adults with SSD. By mapping transverse relaxation rates R2*, R2, and R2’, we characterize distinct aspects of iron accumulation across basal ganglia structures. Combined with macromolecular proton fraction (MPF) and structure volume, we aim to derive comprehensive qMRI signatures of basal ganglia abnormalities in SSD. Finally, we test associations of these MRI metrics with clinical measures and peripheral blood markers related to iron metabolism to provide contextual information for interpreting brain MRI findings.

Methods

Sixty-eight participants (30 healthy controls: 19 Male/11 Female, mean age 26.4 [SD 4.7]; 38 SSD: 25M/13F, mean age 27.5 [SD 5.4]) underwent a diagnostic interview, fasted bloodwork (iron panel and C-reactive protein, CRP), and whole-brain MRI. T1w MPRAGE images were obtained for anatomical reference. To estimate transverse relaxation parameters, we acquired a multi-echo gradient-echo sequence (mGRE; TR=92ms, 14 echoes at TE=1.90-32.98ms, 1.25x1.25x1.3mm3 resolution) and a multi-echo turbo-spin-echo sequence (TSE; TR=11s, 3 echoes at TE=25-108ms, turbo-factor=5, 1.2x1.2x2.5mm3 resolution). 

For MPF mapping, we employed a quantitative magnetization transfer (qMT) protocol that includes one MT-weighted (TR=29ms, TE=2.43ms, FA-10°, off-resonance saturation pulse duration=12.3ms, FA=560°, offset-frequency=4kHz) and two non-MT-weighted gradient-echo sequences (FA=4°/25°, TR=21ms, TE=2.43ms) with 1.25x1.25x1.3mm3 resolution. A B1+ field map was acquired using the Siemens product turboFLASH B1+ mapping sequence.

R2* and R2, or effective and irreversible transverse relaxation rates, were fitted voxel-wise to a mono-exponential function using the mGRE and TSE magnitude images. The reversible relaxation rate, R2’ was calculated as R2’ = R2* - R2. MPF was fitted from the qMT images using in-house developed scripts with B1+ and B0 field correction. Volumes of caudate, putamen, globus pallidus, and accumbens were extracted, along with regional-average MRI metrics based on the FreeSurfer Aseg atlas (Figure 1). Structure volumes were normalized by total brain volume to account for head size bias. 

We used independent sample t-tests to compare MRI metrics and blood biomarkers between groups. Highly skewed blood biomarkers (e.g., CRP) were log-transformed before statistical analysis. Pearson’s correlation and regression models were used to test relationships between MRI metrics, blood biomarkers, and psychotic symptom severity measured by the Scale for the Assessment of Positive and Negative Symptoms (SANS and SAPS). All statistical tests were controlled for subjects’ age and sex. Models that included all subjects also controlled for group membership. Findings were considered significant at p<0.05.

Results

Significant group differences were found across basal ganglia structures (Figure 2A), most prominently in the globus pallidus, where SSD showed reduced R2*, R2, R2’, and MPF, and increased volume (p-values range between p<0.001 and p=0.015). Similar changes in qMRI metrics were also observed, particularly in the putamen (p-values range between p<0.001 and p=0.046), but no additional volumetric differences were noted. The two groups did not differ significantly in age (p=0.38) or sex distribution (p=0.81).

In terms of blood biomarkers, no major iron index (i.e., ferritin, transferrin, and iron) was significantly different between groups. However, SSD compared to healthy controls had significantly higher CRP (p=0.006; Figure 2B), which was also associated with ferritin (Pearson’s r=0.39, p=0.020).

Across groups, higher CRP was associated with lower transverse relaxation rates across caudate, putamen, and pallidum, particularly lower R2* and R2 (p-values range between p<0.001 and p=0.045). Within the SSD group, more prominent negative symptoms were associated with higher volume in the bilateral globus pallidus and lower transverse relaxation rates in the left pallidum and accumbens area (p-values range between p<0.001 and p=0.038; Figure 2C).

Conclusion

Using a multimodal qMRI approach with metrics sensitive to iron content, water-macromolecular fraction, and morphology, we show distinct basal ganglia abnormalities in SSD, where the globus pallidus demonstrates the most widespread alterations and the caudate is relatively preserved. The pattern of change is consistent with reduced brain iron content and increased water fraction, suggesting potential disruptions in brain iron metabolism and microstructural integrity within key dopaminergic nuclei that may contribute to SSD symptomatology.

In the globus pallidus, SSD demonstrated significant alterations in all MRI metrics examined, including the volumetric enlargement widely reported in prior literature.1 In the putamen and accumbens area, similar reductions in MPF and transverse relaxation rates were present in the absence of volumetric differences. This observation may indicate that qMRI markers capture microstructural or biochemical changes that precede or occur independently of macrostructural alterations. Within patients, these qMRI alterations were further linked to more prominent clinical symptoms, supporting the view that iron-related microstructural disruptions within basal ganglia circuits contribute to motivational and affective impairments.

Although R2, R2*, and R2’ are broadly sensitive to iron-induced susceptibility, they are governed by different biophysical processes and may thus probe distinct aspects of tissue iron, such as the spatial distribution and size of iron clusters.4 Combined with MPF, they provide a comprehensive tissue signature, which demonstrates that the nature of brain iron abnormalities in SSD varies among basal ganglia structures and is likely not a simple reduction in total iron concentration.

Interestingly, peripheral iron markers did not differ between groups, suggesting that brain iron regulation remains independent of short-term circulating iron pools. In contrast, the SSD group had elevated CRP, and across all participants, higher CRP was related to lower transverse relaxation rates in several regions of interest. Since inflammatory conditions are known to influence iron status biomarkers,5 our findings suggest that systemic inflammation is associated with brain iron-related changes in SSD and may potentially obscure peripheral iron indices. 

Overall, this work underscores the value of multimodal qMRI for understanding the biophysical underpinnings of basal ganglia abnormalities in SSD and suggests that brain iron and related microstructural changes may represent a promising target for future mechanistic studies.
