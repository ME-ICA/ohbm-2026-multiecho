Introduction

Interhemispheric functional connectivity (ihFC)—the temporal correlation of neural activity between homologous regions across hemispheres—is a fundamental feature of the human brain. Disruptions in ihFC are linked to neurodevelopmental and psychopathological conditions, including autism spectrum disorder (ASD), attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), schizophrenia (SZ), and depression ¹. 

Psychopathological conditions are complex and heterogeneous, prompting growing interest in identifying mechanisms shared across diagnostic categories. Transdiagnostic, multimodal developmental studies—focused on psychological dimensions rather than categorical diagnoses—offer a more nuanced understanding of how these conditions manifest. While ihFC alterations appear across disorders and relate to symptom severity, it is unclear whether they reflect shared transdiagnostic variation or how they relate to genetic factors. Clarifying these links is crucial for evaluating ihFC as a biomarker and for mapping brain–behavior–gene pathways. Such approaches may ultimately enable more precise, individualized models of psychopathological disorders and inform earlier, targeted interventions ² ³. 

The present study aims to identify associations between ihFC, psychological outcomes, and genetic variation, using retrospective data from the Philadelphia Neurodevelopmental Cohort (PNC; N = 1,308)⁴, a publicly available large-scale neurodevelopmental dataset. Adopting a transdiagnostic and data-driven framework, we used graph neural network (GNN) modeling to explore how patterns of ihFC predict differences in psychological functioning, including anxiety, psychosis, depression, and obsessive-compulsive symptoms. Finally, a candidate-gene association analysis was conducted to link genetic variation to the ihFC features most strongly related to psychological dimensions, providing an integrative view of brain–behavior–gene relationships. 

Methods

Data

Multi-modal data from the Philadelphia Neurodevelopmental Cohort (PNC, Satterthwaite et al., 2026, N=1,308, aged 8 to 21 years), a large-scale public dataset on neurodevelopment, was used. Participants in that cohort underwent different sets of measurement: MRI images, psychological assessment, and whole genome genotyping through blood samples. A total of 422 participants were excluded due to excessive motion, cropped images, or an MRI–assessment age gap greater than six months. 

Behavioral data preprocessing

Psychopathology was assessed using a computerized structured interview (GOASSESS), which evaluates symptoms of anxiety, mood disorders, and psychosis. Factor analysis was then used to group symptom scores into seven dimensions: psychosis, attention, depression, anxiety, bipolarity, conduct, and obsession/compulsion. 

rs-fMRI data preprocessing

Resting-state fMRI images (TR=3000 ms, TE = 32 ms, voxel-size = 3x3x3 mm, acquisition time = 6 min) were preprocessed using fMRIPrep (Esteban et al., 2019), ensuring standardized methods. FC matrices were computed using the brain atlas homotopic Markov Random Field (hMRF) 400. Pearson's correlation was used to estimate connectivity at both the region of interest (ROI) level (atlas-based) and at the functional network (FN) level (Yeo networks: visual (VisN), default-mode (DMN), ventral attention (VAN), dorsal attention (DAN), somatosensory (SMN), limbic (LBN), and control  (ContN). 

Graph Neural Network

To model associations between interhemispheric functional connectivity and psychological dimensions, we trained a graph neural network (GNN) using individual functional connectivity matrices as inputs and the seven psychological dimensions as simultaneous prediction targets. The GNN was implemented as a multi-output model with all outcomes equally weighted in a mean squared error loss function, and hyperparameters were optimized using a Bayesian grid search. Node features were each ROI, and edge features were the FC. To identify the most influential connectivity patterns, we conducted an edge-focused explainability analysis, focusing on connections rather than node-level features. We first performed global ablations by removing all interhemispheric edges or all intrahemispheric edges. We then performed FN-level ablations, testing three conditions for each FN: removal of all edges, removal of only interhemispheric edges, and removal of only intrahemispheric edges. 

The influence of each subset was quantified by comparing performance between the full and ablated models using Wilcoxon Signed-Rank test, FDR-corrected for multiple comparisons. Significant performance drops indicate a greater contribution of the corresponding FN to the brain–behavior prediction. 

Genetic data preprocessing

Genotype data were obtained from multiple platforms (Axiom, Affymetrix, Omni, Illumina HumanHap) and underwent extensive quality control. SNPs or individuals with call rates <95%, variants with MAF <1%, or those deviating from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (p < 1 × 10⁻⁶) were excluded. Alleles were converted to nucleotide format, standardized to GRCh37/hg19, harmonized with the 1000 Genomes Phase 3 reference, and phased for imputation. Only variants with imputation quality >0.8 were retained, resulting in 59,624,535 high-quality variants across 22 pairs of autosomes. 

A candidate gene association analysis (CGAS) examined the relationship between mean interhemispheric functional connectivity in psychopathology‑related networks and ion channel genes regulating neuronal excitability and signaling. Cross‑disorder genetic studies show shared psychiatric risk loci are enriched in excitatory neuron genes, supporting a focus on excitability and connectivity mechanisms¹. Candidate genes—GLRA3, KCNA1, KCNA3, and KCNA5—were selected for their roles in action potentials, network oscillations, and synaptic transmission. Analyses controlled for sex, age, family ID, and ancestry. 

Results

In 4-fold cross-validation, the best-performing graph neural network (GNN) achieved the following coefficients of determination (R²)—which quantify the proportion of variance in each psychological dimension explained by the model, are independent of score scaling, and allow direct comparison across dimensions—across seven psychological dimensions: psychosis (0.21 ± 0.03), attention (0.04 ± 0.02), depression (0.11 ± 0.02), anxiety (0.07 ± 0.01), bipolarity (−0.0006 ± 0.003), conduct (−0.01 ± 0.007), and obsession/compulsion (0.14 ± 0.02). Performance was consistent across folds, and the reported R² values represent the mean across held-out validation folds. 

Comparison with a null model revealed a significant reduction in predictive accuracy for all dimensions except conduct. Given the minimal predictive contributions of attention and bipolarity, these dimensions were excluded from further analysis. Subsequent investigations therefore focused on psychosis, depression, anxiety, and obsession/compulsion. 

Ablation of entire matrix components (complete interhemispheric or intrahemispheric) did not significantly affect model performance. In contrast, targeted removal of interhemispheric edges revealed contributions of specific network connections. For psychosis, significant reductions in predictive accuracy were observed following removal of interhemispheric edges within the DAN (effect size r = 0.49, FDR-corrected p = 0.003), LBN (r = 0.51, p = 0.03), VN (r = 0.48, p < 0.001), and DMN (r = 0.45, p < 0.001). 

For depression, removal of either interhemispheric or intrahemispheric edges alone did not affect prediction performance; however, ablation of the entire DAN led to a marked decrease. For anxiety, edge removal within the DMN and LBN substantially reduced predictive accuracy. 

For obsession/compulsion, predictive performance declined significantly following removal of interhemispheric edges in the DAN and DMN, as well as intrahemispheric edges within the LBN. These results are summarized in Figure 2A, which illustrates the impact of network-specific edge removal across psychological dimensions. 

We examined the association between candidate SNPs in selected genes and mean interhemispheric functional connectivity (ihFC) in large-scale networks having influence on psychopathological predictions; DAN, LBN, VN, and DMN. All reported associations survived Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (adjusted significance threshold = 0.005). Only the values of the most significant SNP for each network are reported. In the DAN, intronic SNPs in GLRA3 (rs9999152 (effect size: 0.03, p-value: 1e-5)), KCNA5 (rs6489602, rs6489601), and CCBE1 (rs891312, rs749998) showed significant correlations with mean ihFC. In the LBN, SNPs in CCBE1 (rs10871791 (0.02, 7e-4)), GLRA3 (rs2251316, rs2555629), SCN1B (rs1125867), and KCNA1/KCNA5 (rs11063484, rs2239507) were associated with mean ihFC. In the DMN, SNPs in KCNA5 (rs11063476, rs4766329), CCBE1 (rs3114281 (-0.02, 9e-4)), and NECAB2 (rs925331) showed significant associations with mean ihFC. All identified SNPs were located in intronic regions of their respective genes. 

Conclusion

Using a GNN, we showed that whole-brain functional connectivity carries psychological dimension-specific predictive information. Predictive performance was strongest for psychosis and obsession/compulsion, moderate for anxiety, and negligible for attention and bipolarity. Importantly, our cohort consisted of young participants aged 8 to 21, a critical developmental period during which interhemispheric and intrahemispheric brain networks undergo substantial maturation. This developmental context may shape both the emergence of functional connectivity patterns and the expression of psychiatric symptoms. 

Network-level analyses revealed that interhemispheric connections in the DMN were particularly critical for predicting psychosis, anxiety, and obsession/compulsion, while intrahemispheric connectivity within the LBN also contributed across these dimensions. Psychosis relied on the broadest set of interhemispheric networks, suggesting extensive bilateral integration as a hallmark of this dimension. Anxiety and obsession/compulsion shared overlapping network patterns with psychosis, indicating partially shared neural substrates and suggesting that some functional connectivity patterns may contribute to multiple forms of psychopathology. 

In contrast, depression exhibited a distinct profile. Unlike the other dimensions, we did not observe consistent contributions from DMN or LBN connectivity, although subtle effects may have gone undetected. Prediction performance for depression decreased primarily when the DAN was removed, which may indicate reliance on distributed attentional networks rather than bilateral DMN integration. Given that depression often emerges later in adolescence and early adulthood, its neural substrates may depend on the ongoing maturation of networks such as the DAN and associated circuits. In our young cohort, this developmental heterogenity could contribute to more diffuse or variable connectivity patterns, partially explaining the moderate prediction of accuracy for depression. Overall, these observations suggest that while some dimensions share convergent network substrates, others, such as depression, may depend on distinct or developmentally evolving network configurations, warranting further investigation5. 

At the genetic level, intronic variants in GLRA3, KCNA1, KCNA5, CCBE1, SCN1B, and NECAB2 were associated with mean interhemispheric connectivity across several resting-state networks (DAN, LBN, VisN, DMN). Although these variants have not been previously linked to specific psychopathological dimensions, our findings suggest that they may influence network integration and coordination more generally. By modulating interhemispheric communication, these genetic variants could shape the connectivity patterns that underlie predictive modeling of psychosis, anxiety, and obsession/compulsion, providing a potential mechanistic link between gene-level variation and large-scale brain network function. 

Together, these results provide a comprehensive view of the network and genetic mechanisms underlying psychiatric symptom dimensions. Interhemispheric DMN integration and intrahemispheric LBN connectivity emerge as convergent network substrates supporting multiple dimensions of psychopathology, while depression appears to rely on a distinct, developmentally evolving attentional-network profile. By combining functional connectivity patterns, predictive modeling, and genetic variation, this work supports a dimensional, network-specific understanding of psychiatric dimensions.  While this study was conducted in a developmental cohort, age-related variability in network-based models of psychopathology was not directly examined, representing both a limitation and an important avenue for future research.
