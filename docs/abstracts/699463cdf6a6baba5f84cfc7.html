Introduction

Conscious perception of internal signals plays a crucial role in satisfying basic needs and maintaining homeostasis, yet these internal urges often conflict with external contextual demands. Preference, a core psychological construct that reflects internal states, is known to fluctuate with physiological or affective conditions. Despite extensive evidence linking internal states to preference-based decisions, direct neural evidence for the role of interoception in preference processing is still limited. The present study aimed to investigate how interoception relates to both task-relevant and task-irrelevant preference information processing, using task-based EEG and fMRI. We hypothesized that stronger cortical responses to heartbeats would be associated with higher subjective preference rating, and that preference-related signals—regardless of task relevance—would be encoded in interoception-related regions such as the anterior insula. Moreover, we expected that the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) would play an important role in regulating task-irrelevant preference information.

Methods

39 Participants (20 females; mean age = 22.9 years) and 42 participants (17 females; mean age = 24.5 years) completed the EEG and fMRI study, respectively. Across both studies, interoceptive accuracy (IAcc), sensibility (IS), and metacognition (metaIA) were measured via the Heartbeat Counting Task1. 

In the EEG study, participants performed Movie Rating Task while 64-channel EEG and ECG were recorded (Figure 1A). They viewed 50 movie titles and indicated whether they had previously seen each movie. For the movies they had watched, participants recalled key scenes and silently rated the movies before providing an explicit rating relative to their most and least favorite movies. EEG signals were time-locked to R-peaks during movie recall to extract heartbeat-evoked potentials (HEPs)2. We tested whether subjective preference levels modulated HEP amplitude and whether interoceptive sensitivity measures predicted these effects. 

In the fMRI study, participants performed Preference and Luminance Decision Task during scanning3 (Figure 1B). Depending on the cue, they chose between two movie titles based either on subjective preference or on luminance of the printed characters. On half of the trials, task-irrelevant features conflicted with task-relevant ones. In the luminance task, reduced decision efficiency in incongruent versus congruent trials indexed interference from task-irrelevant preference information (Preference Interference Effect; PIE). We examined how interoceptive sensitivity relates to individual differences in processing and regulating task-relevant and task-irrelevant preference feature information. 

Results

In the EEG study, A cluster-based permutation test found no significant HEP clusters associated with parametric preference levels. However, interoceptive sensitivity modulated HEP–preference associations: individuals with higher IS and better metaIA exhibited stronger relationships between HEP modulations and preference ratings—positive effects at fronto-central sites and negative effects at parieto-occipital sites (Figure 2A). No significant effects were observed for IAcc.

In the fMRI study, hierarchical GLM analyses revealed that the anterior insula showed greater activation during preference-based decisions than luminance-based decisions. The anterior insula also encoded task-irrelevant preference information. Correlation analyses revealed that individuals with lower metaIA showed larger PIE. Inter-subject representational similarity analysis further showed that those with larger PIE group had more similar anterior insula activation patterns between preference and luminance trials, suggesting increased intrusion of preference signals (Figure 2B). Generalized psychophysiological interactions (gPPI) analyses using the anterior insula as the seed region identified stronger connectivity with the rostral mPFC (rmPFC) as a key mechanism. Importantly, insula–rmPFC connectivity significantly mediated the relationship between metaIA and PIE (Figure 2C).

Conclusion

Using complementary EEG and fMRI approaches, this study provides converging neural evidence that interoceptive metacognition shapes both the processing and regulation of subjective preference information. Individuals with better interoceptive metacognition exhibited enhanced cortical responses to heartbeats during preference decisions and showed more effective suppression of task-irrelevant preference information during luminance decisions. Together, these findings highlight the central role of insula-mPFC connectivity in linking interoception to the processing and control of subjective preferences.
