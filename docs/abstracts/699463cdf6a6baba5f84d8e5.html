Introduction

The UK Biobank (UKB) has now acquired cross-sectional population imaging, including multimodal brain MRI, from 100,000 participants1. From these scans, thousands of image-derived phenotypes (IDPs; 3,935 in the BIG40 release) have been produced, allowing researchers to undertake large-scale studies of brain structure and function without having to deal with the raw imaging data directly2. UKB is now inviting up to 60,000 individuals to return for repeat imaging 2–7 years later, allowing us to directly track how these IDPs change with time.

The simplest way to quantify longitudinal change is to compute the same IDP at each timepoint and take the difference. This treats the problem cross-sectionally, and measurement error at both timepoints increases noise in the estimated change. For morphological measures (e.g. tissue or regional volumes), residual geometric distortion and segmentation variability can further bias or obscure subtle effects.

Longitudinal pipelines attempt to reduce this unwanted variability by explicitly linking the two acquisitions from the same participant. SIENA accounts for geometric distortions by constraining affine alignment by assuming a rigid skull3]. FreeSurfer provides a dedicated longitudinal stream which initialises surface fitting at each timepoint from the same within-subject template.

However, most established longitudinal approaches (apart from a few, such as CAT12) rely only on affine alignment during processing, limiting the spatial accuracy with which subtle changes can be localised. Here we present an FSL-based pipeline that uses symmetric nonlinear registration to build within-subject midspace templates to be used for subsequent longitudinal IDP estimation. We test this on the 4,564 of the first UKB repeat-imaging participants, with a preliminary comparison of cross-sectional versus registration-based longitudinal SynthSeg4 volume IDP changes.

Methods

We applied the pipeline to the T1-weighted whole-head images from 4,564 of the first UKB participants with repeat imaging. First, we applied an additional FSL-FAST bias-field correction over-and-above the standard UKB processing, as we found that even small residual intensity inhomogeneity negatively affected the pipeline. All images were intensity normalised to a mean of 1000.

Longitudinal alignment was initialised using a slightly modified SIENA workflow, where brain-extraction was performed using SynthStrip. Running SIENA on the two T1-weighted images provided (i) robust affine registration between timepoints, constrained by the assumption of a rigid skull, and (ii) a subject-specific halfway space that treats both timepoints symmetrically. In addition to SIENA’s global percentage brain volume change output, we used the SIENA transforms to resample both timepoints into a subject-specific halfway space and create an average T1 image.

The halfway-average image was brain-extracted and rigidly aligned to the Oxford-MM-1 (OMM-1) T1 template using FSL-FLIRT. This allowed us to ensure that the nonlinear registration was always taking place in the same space for all subjects in order to minimise the influence of between-subject field of view differences on warp estimation. Concatenating the OMM-1 transform with the SIENA halfway transforms allowed resampling of both timepoints into a common space for all subjects.

Nonlinear longitudinal registration was then performed with FSL-MMORF5 in a scalar-only configuration, using the two (affinely aligned) T1-weighted images as the input and reference modalities and OMM-1 as the warp space. Timepoint-specific brain masks with dilated exclusion regions based on FSL-BIANCA white matter lesion segmentations were used to down-weight non-brain and lesioned regions, encouraging the deformation to be driven by healthy tissue only. The result was that MMORF produced a dense, diffeomorphic displacement field between timepoints in OMM-1 coordinates. To avoid bias towards either timepoint, we ran the registration a second time with the input and reference roles of the two timepoints reversed, and created symmetric half-warps to map each timepoint into a nonlinear subject-specific midspace. The two midspace-resampled T1-weighted images were then averaged to produce a high-SNR, longitudinally unbiased, nonlinear midspace template. Voxelwise Jacobian determinant maps were computed from the two nonlinear half-warps, producing a voxelwise measure of local volume change.

SynthSeg was then run on (i) the nonlinear midspace template and (ii) each timepoint resampled into midspace using affines only. For each labelled ROI we computed: (a) “cross-sectional” volumes from the affine-resampled timepoint segmentations; and (b) “longitudinal” volumes by scaling the midspace ROI volume by the mean Jacobian within that ROI for each timepoint. This latter measure explicitly exploits the volumetric changes estimated during nonlinear registration, while requiring only a single segmentation per subject. The resulting cross-sectional and longitudinal measures of volume change for the SynthSeg ROIs were compared.

Results

Across subcortical ROIs, cross-sectional and longitudinal volume estimates were broadly similar, with comparable median percentage volume changes and little overall bias. However, there was still substantial variability in individual IDP estimates.

Two subcortical ROIs showed clear method-dependent effects, namely Cerebral White Matter and Lateral Ventricles. For Cerebral White Matter, the longitudinal approach showed little to no median volume loss, whereas the cross-sectional approach showed ~1% median atrophy. Since some white-matter atrophy is expected over typical UKB re-scan intervals, this discrepancy suggests that some aspect of the longitudinal approach is making it insensitive to that change. A possible contributor is the handling of white-matter hyperintensities: lesion masks used to down-weight pathology during registration can alter how boundary-driven changes are attributed to white matter versus neighbouring tissue. For the Lateral Ventricles, the longitudinal approach showed smaller median dilation than cross-sectional. This was traced to overly liberal periventricular lesion masks overlapping the ventricles, which down-weighted true ventricular boundary change during registration and therefore reduced the Jacobian-derived expansion estimate.

Across cortical ROIs, a consistent bias was observed: longitudinal estimates showed higher median atrophy than cross-sectional. This was most evident in the frontal and temporal poles, which showed near-zero median atrophy cross-sectionally but ~1–2% median atrophy longitudinally. These regions are known to have poorer segmentation reproducibility and are particularly sensitive to small geometric inconsistencies - the longitudinal approach appears to mitigate this by relying on a single midspace segmentation combined with Jacobian-based change.

Conclusion

A longitudinal, registration-based approach to estimating regional brain volume change appears better able to capture expected patterns of age-related cortical atrophy in UKB than a conventional cross-sectional differencing approach. The largest differences occur in cortical regions with poorer repeat segmentation stability, suggesting that reducing the problem to a single ROI definition (in an unbiased midspace) plus Jacobian-based scaling can reduce noise and residual geometric bias.

At the same time, the unexpected absence of white-matter atrophy in the longitudinal estimates highlights an important trade-off: while longitudinal processing can reduce segmentation noise, it can also introduce biases through registration and masking choices. Ongoing work is therefore focused on (i) diagnosing the source of the Cortical White Matter discrepancy, including the interaction between lesion masks and tissue boundaries, and (ii) defining a robust strategy for incorporating white-matter hyperintensity information without suppressing true ventricular or periventricular change.

Overall, these results support the use of nonlinear longitudinal processing in generating IDPs for the UKB reimaging study. Our next steps will be to investigate whether the observed differences between cross-sectionally estimated and longitudinally estimated IDP changes translate into differences in their associations with non-imaging phenotypes and genetic data withing UKB.
