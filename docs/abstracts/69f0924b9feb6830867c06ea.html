Introduction

Slowly (<0.1 Hz) alternating internal and external attentional modes are a prevalent theme across imaging modalities and species. For instance, fMRI studies have shown anti-correlated activity in the Default Mode Network and the Task Positive Network. Activity within these networks is correlated with self reported internal or external awareness, which alternate at 0.05 Hz1.  A similar time-scale was observed by Bastian and Sackur2, who observed that transitions to mind-wandering occurred once every 18 seconds on average. In the context of sustained attention, studies have shown that power in the alpha band fluctuates at ~0.07 Hz within area A1 in macaques3 and over fronto-central electrodes in human EEG4. These fluctuations are anti-correlated with entrainment towards the stimulus and hit rate. Slow fluctuations have also been observed in the modularity of neural dynamics. Low modularity or “Integration” was related to working memory performance while high modularity or “segregation” was observed during simple motor tasks5.

In this work, we engage with this topic by presenting a dynamical systems mechanism for dual processing modes. We propose that integrative windows correspond to internal attention, while segregative windows correspond to external attention. Through extensive whole-brain simulations we demonstrate that dual attentional modes can naturally emerge from the interactions of coupled alpha oscillators. We show that that Slow Coherence Oscillations (SCOs) arise from the modular topology of the human connectome. These coherence oscillations modulate attention by suppressing the entrainment of sensory cortices to external stimuli in the coherent phase. This mechanism was used to model the findings of Lakatos et al3 and Kasten et al4. Crucially, the changes in coherence and alpha power occur at rates consistent with experimental observations in both humans and non-human primates, approximately 0.07 Hz.

Methods

We modelled large-scale brain dynamics using networks of coupled phase oscillators governed by the Kuramoto model. Each oscillator represents a brain region whose phase evolves according to its intrinsic frequency and coupling to other regions through a structurally derived connectivity matrix. Time-delayed interactions were included to account for finite conduction velocities. Whole-brain models were constructed for both humans and macaques. For humans, the brain was parcellated into 1000 regions using the Schaefer atlas, with regions grouped into seven functional networks. Structural connectivity matrices were derived from diffusion MRI tractography. For macaques an 82-region parcellation and publicly available connectivity matrices based on tract-tracing data were used. Functional analogues of temporo-parietal and default mode networks were identified from prior literature. Intrinsic frequencies were assigned within the alpha band (8-12 Hz) based on anatomical node strength, such that stronger nodes oscillated at lower frequencies.

Global coupling strength and conduction delays were homogeneous across the network and systematically varied to explore the parameter space. Network dynamics were simulated using Euler integration with a timestep of 1 ms. Network synchronization was quantified using the Kuramoto Order Parameter (KOP), defined as the magnitude of the normalized vector sum of oscillator phases. The KOP serves as the primary measure of network integration, with values near one indicating high synchrony and values near zero indicating desynchronization. For each parameter combination, 100 seconds of activity were generated and the KOP time series was computed for the whole brain and for individual subnetworks in the human and macaque connectome. Slow Coherence Oscillation (SCO) frequency was defined as the dominant non-zero peak in the Fourier spectrum of the KOP time series. This yielded a robust characterisation of the parameter space, from which regions that supported coherence oscillations could be identified (see Figure 1). The set of parameters that maximised the variance in the KOP (metastability) was used for the following analysis.

To relate coherence fluctuations to attentional modes, a competitive entrainment model was implemented. A simulated sensory node with an intrinsic frequency of 5 Hz was coupled both to the temporo-parietal network and to an external oscillatory input representing a stimulus. Power at the alpha frequency and stimulus frequency was estimated using continuous wavelet transforms for both the human and macaque connectomes. This enabled the examination of how fluctuations in network coherence modulate the relative influence of internal network dynamics versus external sensory forcing.

Gain modulation was used to model the effects of cholinergic neuromodulation. Multiplicative gain was applied by scaling all structural connections within a target subnetwork, effectively increasing intra-network coupling. Simulations were conducted both with and without conduction delays to test their role in enabling gain-dependent modulation of SCO frequency.

Finally, to assess the dynamical basis for SCO generation, simulations were also performed on surrogate networks. These included fully connected networks with heterogeneous intrinsic frequencies drawn from a Lorentzian distribution, as well as randomized human and macaque connectomes created by shuffling connection weights while preserving symmetry. Analytical results for all-to-all networks without delays were used to show that oscillatory order parameters cannot arise in such systems, a result confirmed by numerical simulations.

Results

We found that slow oscillations in coherence emerge across a wide range of parameters in our connectome based whole brain model. When the variance in coherence is maximised, coherence oscillations occur at 0.07 Hz in the whole brain as well as in subnetworks, matching the experimentally observed rates of attentional mode switching. Similar coherence oscillations were observed in the macaque connectome and alternative parcellations of the human connectome, indicating that the phenomenon is not specific to a particular atlas or species. Importantly, coherence oscillations were present not only at the whole-brain level but also within individual subnetworks, including the temporo-parietal and default mode networks, and these local oscillations were phase-locked to the global oscillation.

These coherence oscillations are suggested to modulate the representation of external stimuli at sensory nodes through competitive entrainment (See Figure 2). During periods of high temporo-parietal coherence, network input is synchronised and more effectively entrains the sensory node, leading to increased alpha power. During low-coherence periods external oscillatory input is able to entrain the sensory node at the stimulus frequency. These regimes alternated at the SCO frequency and reproduced experimentally observed anti-correlations between alpha power and stimulus entrainment. Multiplicative gain applied to either the left or right tempor-parietal network, altered the intramodular synchronization frequency, shifted the frequency difference between subnetworks and thereby modulated the SCO frequency. When conduction delays were removed, gain had no effect on SCO frequency. These results indicate that conduction delays enable gain-dependent tuning of attentional rhythms and assign a functional role to delayed interactions in large-scale brain dynamics.

To understand the origin of these slow coherence oscillations (SCOs), we examined a simplified model consisting of two coupled oscillator populations. When intra-network coupling was strong and inter-network coupling was weaker, the populations synchronized locally but not globally, a regime known as frustrated synchronization. If the two populations synchronized at slightly different frequencies the global order parameter oscillated at a frequency equal to the difference between the synchronization frequencies of the populations. Analytical results showed that both coupling strength and conduction delays independently modulate synchronization frequency, providing a mechanistic basis for the emergence of SCOs in modular networks. 

The human connectome satisfies the structural requirements for this mechanism. Its modular structure leads to prolonged partial synchronization regimes in which subnetworks synchronize locally at different frequencies. Within functional networks, locally synchronized modules were divided along hemispheric lines, such that left and right subnetworks synchronized at distinct frequencies, producing oscillatory coherence within the network. Hierarchical modularity further enabled nested coherence oscillations across multiple spatial scales. Fully connected networks with heterogeneous intrinsic frequencies did not exhibit oscillatory order parameters, consistent with analytical predictions. Similarly, randomized human and macaque connectomes, generated by shuffling connection weights while preserving symmetry, failed to produce SCOs. These results demonstrate that modular topology is essential for the emergence of slow coherence oscillations.

Conclusion

Periodic alternation between internal and external attentional modes is a prevalent theme in the literature that lacks a comprehensive explanation. The present work supports the hypothesis that switching between integrative and segregative states is the dynamical principle behind this phenomenon, and that competitive entrainment is the mechanism relating the two. We demonstrate that both human and macaque connectome based whole-brain models can support slow oscillations in coherence (SCOs) at frequencies matching attentional mode switching, and that for a given sensory node periodic changes in sensory entrainment emerge out of competition between the sensory network and an external stimulus. Hierarchical modularity is revealed to be the structural basis for SCOs and conduction delays are found to play an adaptive role in enabling the modulation of SCO frequency by cholinergic gain. These results bring a wide variety of findings under the same theoretical framework and suggest exciting new directions for the computational modelling of attention.
