Introduction

Increasingly, the complexity of the brain is mirrored by the complexity of the neuroscientific literature: no individual mind can grasp it fully.  Manually-curated meta-analysis is extremely

laborious, and increasingly unfeasible given the exponential growth of the literature. However automated engines for coordinate-based meta-analysis such as NeuroSynth or NeuroQuery are largely or entirely restricted to MRI or PET, and only available for human studies. These restrictions preclude translational discovery from key non-human model organisms, and from huge swaths of the literature using other methods for recording, imaging, and causally manipulating brain function.

Where human experts flag, the latest AI models excel: Large Language Models (LLMs) are trained on vast corpora of text that include the entire scientific literature, effectively making them ‘AI experts’ across multiple domains of knowledge. Indeed, LLMs even outperform human experts by a wide margin at predicting the outcome of neuroscience experiments 1.

Here we introduce automated meta-analysis of AI experts for neuroscience, combining the key advantages of automation and expert curation. 

Crucially, our framework for AI-powered synthesis of the neuroscientific literature unlocks the possibility of extending meta-analysis into the domain of other organisms. We derive meta-analytic maps of cognition in two fundamental model organisms: macaque, and mouse.

We extensively benchmark brain maps generated by LLMs against species-specific functional, structural, connectomic, transcriptomic, and chemo-architectonic databases. To demonstrate causal relevance beyond to modality-specific approaches for data synthesis, we extend to human intracranial electrical stimulation 3 and Lesion Symptom Mapping 4. 

We make this resource openly available for the neuroscience community at https://github.com/Hana-Ali/neuroLLM.

Methods

We use LLMs to systematically synthesise literature-wide neuroscientific knowledge about how cognitive functions and dysfunctions relate to brain regions. We meta-analyse 5 of the most popular AI models: OpenAI's GPT-4, Llama, DeepSeek, Mistral, and Gemini. 

Our framework supports multiple species, demonstrated in human, macaque (Macaca mulatta), and mouse (Mus musculus). Analysis is performed on a per-region basis: the LLM

is queried about a specific region using its anatomical name as per the corresponding atlas (Desikan-Killiany, Regional Mapping and Allen Mouse Brain Atlas respectively for human, macaque, and mouse). 

For each cognitive function or disorder of choice, each LLM is prompted to estimate the probability that a specific brain region is involved in that specific function. Responses are parsed to extract decimal probability values between 0 and 1. We prompt the LLMs to ‘behave as neuroscience experts and only rely on peer-reviewed articles, textbooks, and reputable sources’ in their simulated review of the literature. By querying each LLM about the same cognitive function systematically for each brain region, we assemble quantitative brain maps for specific cognitive domains and disorders. 

We validate and contextualise LLM-generated summary maps of the literature against multimodal databases in human, macaque, and mouse (Figure 1): (i) similarity of fMRI activity (haemodynamic co-fluctuation, also known as functional connectivity); (ii) gene co-expression (correlation of transcriptomic fingerprints); (iii) structural connectivity from in vivo diffusion MRI tractography (in human) or ex vivo tract-tracing (for macaque and mouse); (iv) anatomical cortical hierarchy from in vivo T1w:T2w myelination.

For the human, we also consider additional functional databases: (v) similarity of MEG activity (electrophysiological co-fluctuation); (vi) similarity of FDG-PET activity (metabolic co-fluctuation); (vii) similarity of subjective experience upon electrical stimulation (electrical co-elicitation). Likewise, we consider additional multimodal anatomical databases: (viii) laminar similarity (similarity of histologically derived cellular distributions across the cortical layers); (ix) receptor co-expression (correlation across 19 in vivo PET maps). For completeness, we also include (x) the Euclidean distance between regions, to account for the brain’s inherent spatial autocorrelation. 

We benchmark LLM’s neuroscientific knowledge against current reference solutions in the neuroscience community: (i) NeuroSynth and NeuroQuery for meta-analysis of human neuroimaging, covering >14,000 studies; (ii) disease-related cortical thickness abnormality from the ENIGMA consortium (ADHD, autism spectrum disorder, bipolar, depression, and schizophrenia), encompassing >17,000 patients and >22,000 healthy controls; (iii) GWAS-derived risk genes for over 30 brain-related disorders, encompassing psychiatric, neurodevelopmental, neurodegenerative, neurological, and brain tumors. 

Finally (iv) we include recent publicly available maps from Lesion Network Mapping studies: a psychosis circuit, derived from regions whose lesion induces psychosis symptoms (N=152 patients); and a depression circuit, derived from the functional convergence of regions whose lesion induces depressive symptoms, and whose brain stimulation (with DBS or TMS) alleviates depressive symptoms (14 studies; N=461 lesion cases and N=251 stimulation cases). In mouse, we also include the dataset of cortical maps reconstructed using in vivo electrophysiological recordings of 621,733 neurons recorded during a decision-making task with 699 Neuropixel probes in 139 mice, recently released by the International Brain Laboratory consortium 2.

Our workflow for AI expert summary of the neuroscientific literature is publicly available and easy to use, supporting many popular LLMs (both paid and free) to maximise accessibility.

Results

LLM AI experts seamlessly integrate knowledge across multiple technologies and heterogeneous studies, going beyond traditional human- and modality-specific meta-analytic engines that are blind to causality. 

Consistently across all three species, we find that fMRI co-fluctuation (and in human, similarity of subjective experience upon direct electrical stimulation) displays the highest relative importance for predicting the automated expert consensus of functional similarity, outperforming the anatomical and molecular ones: synchronous activity predicts similarity of cognitive function (Figure 1d).

Crucially, LLMs are only ever queried about one region at a time. However, we find that when combined, LLMs’ characterisations of different regions’ cognitive functions delineate a unimodal-transmodal functional hierarchy in each of human, macaque, and mouse. In each species, we find significant spatial correlations between the putative LLM functional hierarchy, and the empirical anatomical hierarchy of intracortical myelination from T1w:T2w MRI (Figure 1e). These emergent results suggest that the LLM experts can discover features of brain organization in that are latent in the neuroscientific literature.

Brain maps of specific cognitive functions assembled through consensus of AI experts bear significant spatial overlap with conventional meta-analytic maps, while also going beyond them, capturing maps of direct electrical stimulation or lesion network mapping that are not

available from current meta-analytic engines. On one hand, for the human neuroimaging literature LLM-generated cognitive maps recapitulate NeuroSynth meta-analysis (Figure 2a). On the other hand, LLMs outperform traditional frameworks for functional or anatomical meta-analysis like NeuroSynth and ENIGMA at predicting the effects of causal interventions from intracranial electrical stimulation 3 (Figure 2e-g) and Lesion Network Mapping 4.  Additionally, LLM-generated cortical patterns of disease vulnerability reflect known symptoms comorbidities better than reference solutions in the neuroscience community (cortical thinning from the ENIGMA consortium, and GWAS-derived risk genes for over 30 brain-related disorders), revealing disorder-disorder associations that were latent in the scientific literature but invisible to modality-specific approaches to data synthesis.

Crucially, we can also obtain analogous maps for other species, showcased here with macaque and mouse (Figure 2b-d). For the mouse, we further show that the LLM-derived maps can successfully perform blind decoding of the recently-released flagship resource of Neuropixels recordings from the International Brain Laboratory consortium 2 (Figure 2c) - opening entirely new avenues for a powerful analytic approach that up to now was the sole domain of human neuroscience. Integration with species-specific connectomic and transcriptomic databases allows us to extend to macaque and mouse the results of NeuroSynth-based studies that were previously only possible for the human brain 5, revealing a shared molecular circuit for cognition across mammalian species, demonstrating LLM’s power to integrate and find convergence among entirely separate data modalities.

Finally, our extensive robustness checks indicate that results are highly consistent not

only across different LLMs, but also within each individual LLM upon repeated queries. LLMs indicate appropriate levels of confidence in their answers for different terms and for different regions, differentiating absence of evidence from evidence of absence of a structure-function association. 

Conclusion

We developed and made openly available a fully automated computational workflow to synthesise the knowledge of the scientific literature embedded in large language models’ training corpora.

LLMs’ extensive neuroscientific knowledge-base and linguistic proficiency enables them to recognise different names for the same brain region, much like a human expert would–thereby enabling automated, systematic mapping of function to anatomy at scale like coordinate-based meta-analytic engines, while overcoming the latter’s need for exact coordinates that restricts them to human MRI.

The extensive evaluations performed here suggest that widely used LLMs have great value as automated experts for synthesising the functions of specific regions, and the neural circuits underlying specific cognitive functions and disfunctions.

Automated meta-analysis of AI experts goes beyond traditional coordinate-based meta-analysis, extending its reach from a single imaging modality to the broader literature on direct interventions, lesions, and disease. In parallel, use of AI experts to summarise the literature extends to macaque and mouse analytic approaches that were previously the sole domain of human neuroscience. This approach sheds light on brain-disorder and disorder-disorder associations that were latent in the scientific literature, and reveals common molecular signatures of cognition and affect across multiple species.

The automated interrogation of AI experts introduced here offers a quantitative overview of the literature, akin to what may be obtained from aggregating human experts’ opinions - –though it is worth bearing in mind that the literature itself is not free from bias, including WEIRD participants, confirmation bias, and publication bias among others. Although fine-tuning may further improve performance, we expect that as LLMs’ rapid evolution continues, performance will improve even for general-purpose models, which already outperform human neuroscientists’ knowledge of the literature by a wide margin  1.

Unlike coordinate-based meta-analyses, but more akin to human experts, LLMs supplement evidence with (educated) guesswork and extrapolation. A clear example is language: for macaque and mouse, LLMs consistently provided maps that are analogous to the respective

species’ maps for ‘listening’ – while also appropriately expressing significantly lower confidence in their answers.

Crucially, cases when LLMs' answers consistently diverge from human expectation --- or from modality-specific meta-analyses --- may offer valuable clues about latent patterns and missing links in the literature, providing the opportunity for automated hypothesis-generation and highlighting follow-up experiments and analyses.

Altogether, meta-analysis of AI experts can fundamentally catalyze integrative and translational discovery across species and across data modalities, finally bringing together a scattered literature to identify emergent patterns and latent insights across disparate subfields, modalities, and species. At a time of exciting technological, analytical and data sharing advances, the present work showcases how the use of widely accessible AI technologies can synthesize knowledge about the brain in a way that is readily useful for generating quantitative insight and asking fundamentally new questions about brain function and dysfunction.
