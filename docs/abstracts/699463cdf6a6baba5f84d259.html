Introduction

Language allows humans to convey infinite meanings based on finite linguistic units and syntactic structures in natural contexts1-2. Successful understanding of language requires incremental encoding of lexical content and syntactic features to accurately represent the conveyed meaning from ongoing speech streams3-5. This capacity is underpinned by complex neural coordination in the human brain, which requires flexible, resilient and effective cortical coding to balance the constrained neural resources and the huge computational burden imposed by the infinite amount of possible linguistic information. Despite these advances, the exact mechanism which entails the representation of rich, interwoven linguistic information under biological constraints, remains unclear. 

Methods

In this study, we investigated how the human neocortex performs natural language comprehension, especially the dynamic integration of lexical and syntactic information from continuous speech, by examining neural trajectories in latent spaces. For this end, we used a published multimodal neuroimaging dataset (SMN4Lang) containing magnetoencephalography (MEG) recordings from twelve participants. During the MEG acquisition, they listened to 60 stories over six hours and naturally engaged in continuous speech comprehension. First, we examined whether neural latent trajectories capture individual words from naturalistic stories, using an encoding framework based on deep learning. Next, we tested the associations of lexical and syntactic features with neural latent geometry through neural decoding and representational similarity analysis (RSA). We then investigated whether different coding strategies are used to represent lexical content and syntactic relations through a model-based test. Namely, we compared the logarithmic model, associated with robust coding, with the first-order polynomial model, representing efficient coding, for lexical and syntactic patterns, respectively. Finally, we explored the computational principles underlying the brain-like representation by investigating whether specific connectome-based reservoir computing systems, which better store and integrate more long-range information, are more likely to generate observed neural trajectories. 

Results

Using nonlinear dimensionality reduction, we generated neural trajectories in latent spaces from region-level MEG data of participants listening to 60 naturalistic stories with a total duration of approximately six hours. We demonstrate that these trajectories are language-relevant by showing that they capture consistent components across participants and discriminate single-word representations. We then reveal the associations of lexical and syntactic features with neural latent geometry using multivariate decoding and representational similarity analysis (RSA). Compared to lexical features, neural latent geometry is primarily associated with contexual syntactic information. We further show that the human cortex orchestrates distinct coding strategies to facilitate the comprehension of natural language, in which syntactic relations are associated with cortical coding with more efficient compression compared to lexical features. Furthermore, our simulation using brain-inspired RC systems demonstrates that observed neural trajectories can be simulated with near-critical dynamics and a preference for historical information. 

Conclusion

We investigated how the human brain flexibly coordinates cortical coding to comprehend natural language via neural trajectories in latent spaces. Our findings demonstrate that contextual syntactic information plays a dominant role by enabling more efficient compression than lexical features. Syntactic relationships in latent spaces benefit from the representational advantage of storing and preferentially processing long-range information within the connectome. Our work reveals how the human cortex uses finite resources to handle the infinite nature of language through compressive flexibility and connectome constraints.
