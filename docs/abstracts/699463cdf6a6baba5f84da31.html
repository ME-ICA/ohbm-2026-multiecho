Introduction

Obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD) has been linked to dysregulation within cortico–striato–thalamo–cortical (CSTC) circuitry, with converging evidence implicating supplementary motor area (SMA)–centered networks in symptom expression and control.1 Low-frequency (1 Hz) repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) over SMA has shown clinical benefit in OCD, yet its circuit-level functional connectivity (FC) effects and the extent to which medication status moderates these effects remain incompletely characterized.2 Here we tested whether an SMA-targeted rTMS intervention induces longitudinal changes in resting-state FC (rs-FC) within CSTC-related networks, and whether FC changes track symptom improvement.3 We hypothesized that active SMA rTMS would shift SMA rs-FC with striatal and medial frontal nodes in a direction consistent with normalization, and that FC change magnitudes would be associated with changes in Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS) severity.

Methods

Participants: We analyzed rs-fMRI and clinical data from N=27 individuals with OCD (age: mean±SD = [30.67]±[9.34] years; [15]female), allocated to three treatment arms: active rTMS + SSRI (CISRS, n=8), active rTMS without SSRI (SISRS, n=12), and sham rTMS (SHAM, n=7). Symptoms were assessed weekly using the Y-BOCS.

rTMS protocol: Participants received inhibitory low-frequency rTMS targeting SMA following a Mantovani-style approach (1 Hz; [pulses/session=1800], [sessions=15]).

MRI acquisition: Resting-state fMRI was collected at Pre and Post intervention on a General Electric Discovery MR750 3 Teslas system (EPI: TR/TE = [2000]/[40] ms, voxel size [4] mm isotropic. 

Preprocessing: Data were preprocessed using standardized pipelines (motion correction, susceptibility distortion handling if available, coregistration to T1w, normalization to MNI space, nuisance regression including motion parameters and physiological/WM/CSF signals where applicable, spatial smoothing [4] mm FWHM). Quality control included censoring frames with excessive motion (FD > [0.2] mm) and excluding runs/participants not meeting predefined QC thresholds.

Connectivity analysis: Seed-based rs-FC was computed using an anatomically/functional SMA seed defined in MNI space (coordinates: 0, -6, 52). Voxelwise Fisher-z transformed correlation maps were generated for each participant and timepoint.

Statistics: Longitudinal group effects were tested with voxelwise linear mixed-effects models (LMM; random intercept for subject) specifying Group × Time and covariates Age, Sex, and baseline severity Y-BOCS:

Model: FC ~ Group*Time + Age + Sex (+ baseline Y-BOCS) + (1|Subject).

Planned contrasts tested 

  1. Overall Pre→Post change within each group and 
  2. Differential change (Active vs Sham; CISRS vs SISRS). 

Multiple comparisons were controlled using [cluster-wise correction / FDR] with voxelwise threshold p<[0.001] and cluster α 0.05 (two-sided). Effect sizes and peak coordinates were extracted from significant clusters. Associations between FC change (Δz) and symptom change (ΔY-BOCS) were assessed using robust correlation or mixed models controlling for covariates.

Results

Clinical outcomes: Y-BOCS scores decreased significantly over time (main effect of Time: t = −8.35, p = 5.1 × 10⁻¹³), with a significant Group×Time interaction indicating differential symptom trajectories across treatment arms. Mean Pre→Post Y-BOCS change was −13.3 points (−51.4%) in the active rTMS + SSRI group (CISRS), −8.8 points (−29.9%) in the active rTMS without SSRI group (SISRS), and −6.4 points (−27.5%) in the sham group. Post-hoc contrasts revealed significantly lower post-treatment Y-BOCS scores in CISRS compared with SISRS (p = 0.0032, Tukey-corrected), while differences between CISRS and SHAM at PostTMS were not significant. These findings indicate overall symptom improvement across groups, with the largest magnitude of change observed in the medicated active rTMS group.

Connectivity outcomes: Voxelwise longitudinal analyses revealed significant Pre→Post changes in SMA-seeded resting-state functional connectivity in the active rTMS groups, while no significant clusters were observed in the sham condition (cluster-corrected p < 0.05). 

In the active rTMS + SSRI group (CISRS), decreased SMA connectivity was observed primarily in cerebellar and posterior midline regions, including a cerebellar cluster (k = 59 voxels, peak MNI x = 18, y = 38, z = −32) and a posterior cingulate cortex cluster (k = 44 voxels, peak MNI x = −22, y = −62, z = 0).

In contrast, the active rTMS without SSRI group (SISRS) showed a prominent reduction of SMA connectivity with striatal regions, centered on the putamen (k = 357 voxels, peak MNI x = −22, y = 2, z = 12).

No significant Pre→Post connectivity changes were detected in the sham group, indicating that observed network modulations were specific to active stimulation. Together, these findings suggest that SMA-targeted rTMS induces distinct patterns of CSTC-related connectivity reconfiguration depending on medication status, with cerebellar and striatal circuits differentially involved across treatment arms.

Conclusion

Active low-frequency SMA rTMS produced measurable longitudinal changes in resting-state functional connectivity within CSTC-relevant networks in OCD, with connectivity shifts differing from sham stimulation and partially tracking symptom improvement. These results support SMA-centered circuit modulation as a plausible systems-level mechanism of rTMS in OCD and suggest that medication status may influence the magnitude and/or topology of FC changes.
