Introduction

Linearized encoding analysis is a popular tool for testing the alignment between representations of neural networks and the human brain. However, it is not well known that a classical neuroscience design (i.e., repetition of stimuli across trials and subjects) may introduce data leakage in the cross-validation (CV) of encoding models.

For example, three subjects were presented with two stimuli (Figure 1). The CV design in (a; “subject-wise models”) does not repeat any stimuli across partitions, whereas the CV design in (b; “stimulus-wise models”) repeats the same stimulus across partitions. Design (b) may seem like a valid “leave-one-subject-out” design with independent noise, which is often used for classification. However, a signal evoked by the same stimulus leaks through all partitions, spuriously inflating the prediction accuracy of arbitrary predictors. This data leakage is well known in the machine learning community as “leakage in training examples” (LTE) [1], yet it remains prevalent in various scientific fields, including neuroimaging [2].

The current work [3] uses formal analysis, numerical simulations, and real-data analyses to explain the mechanism of LTE in encoding analysis and to demonstrate realistic instances of it. In particular, this work focuses on ridge regression with finite impulse response modeling of dynamic stimulus features and neural response time series, as often utilized in naturalistic neuroimaging studies involving natural speech, music, and movies.

Methods

In a nutshell, the formal analysis reveals that LTE occurs as follows:

(1) A repeated signal disables regularization in an otherwise seemingly valid optimization process, even with independent noise.

(2) As the regularization hyperparameter approaches zero, the projection matrix becomes positive definite, whereas it would be null when properly regularized.

(3) Because of the repeated signal, the expected prediction accuracy under random noise is proportional to a bilinear form involving a nonzero vector and a positive-definite square matrix.

Simulations explored the influence of various factors on the inflation of prediction accuracy due to LTE, including the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), model complexity, data dimensionality, and the spatial and temporal autocorrelation structures of predictors and noise. The effect of LTE—i.e., spuriously high predictive performance with null predictors—was defined as the inflation in prediction accuracy of encoding models with null predictors (i.e., uniform random noise) when stimuli were repeated across CV partitions, compared to when all stimuli were independently generated from the same distribution.

For real-data demonstrations, we used EEG, fMRI, and emotion-rating time series recorded while participants listened to naturalistic music, drawn from open-access datasets (https://purl.stanford.edu/sd922db3535; https://openneuro.org/datasets/ds003085). For simplicity, the true predictors were the broadband envelopes of the presented auditory stimuli, downsampled to match the respective neural response sampling rates. Null predictors were generated as (1) uniform noise, (2) Gaussian noise, or (3) phase-randomized envelopes.

All analysis code and data are available on Zenodo (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.15100830). An executable simulation demo is available on CodeOcean (https://codeocean.com/capsule/4591394/).

Results

A striking demonstration is shown in Figure 2. Both rows appear similar. However, the top row displays prediction accuracies of subject-wise models with true predictors, whereas the bottom row shows prediction accuracies of stimulus-wise models with null predictors (i.e., phase-randomized envelopes). Due to their highly plausible spatial patterns, the spurious results may be misinterpreted as evidence for neural encoding of null predictors.

Conclusion

Encoding modeling is a flexible and powerful tool that enables rigorous analysis of dynamic neural responses to naturalistic stimuli. As naturalistic neuroimaging gains increasing traction, more researchers without prior experience in predictive modeling are likely to adopt this approach. Consequently, the risk of data leakage must be more clearly communicated within the field.
