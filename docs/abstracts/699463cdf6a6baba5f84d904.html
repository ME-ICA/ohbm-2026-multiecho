Introduction

By measuring changes in blood oxygenation levels, fMRI provides an indirect measure of brain activity. The time-series data collected from fMRI scans offer a window into dynamic brain processes, but the frequency of data collection significantly influences the quality of the resulting information. Faster sampling rates are typically associated with higher temporal resolution, enabling researchers to capture rapid changes in brain activity (Yang 2021). However, collecting data at fast sampling rates requires advanced scanner technology, larger storage capacity, and higher costs as a result. Consequently, the use of slower sampling rates is still prevalent, especially in legacy datasets, raising the question of how to maximize the utility of such data (Biswal et al. 2010}. Still, existing approaches continue to prioritize faster sampling rates since they provide more fine-grained temporal information (Dowdle et al., 2021). Since causal processes in the brain likely occur several orders of magnitude faster than any non-invasive measurement devices could detect  (Perri et al., 2014), leveraging the complementary information from multiple sampling rates could reveal patterns that may be obscured when considering a single rates in isolation.

Our work challenges the assumption that faster measurements are always better, proposing instead that slower sampling rates can actually be used to enhance the estimation of causal brain dynamics. This perspective is supported by prior work showing that multi-resolution analysis is effective across domains such as signal processing and image super-resolution, where combining coarse-resolution images reveals details not apparent at any single scale (Desco 2001; Liu 2018; Chen 2018; Ledig 2017). Echoing this idea, (Solovyeva 2023) present a counterintuitive finding: rather than introducing more uncertainty, incorporating slower measurements alongside faster ones can actually reduce ambiguity in identifying causal structure. 

Methods

This study investigated whether combining fMRI signals acquired at different temporal resolutions can improve predictive modeling performance. Complementary analyses were conducted using two primary datasets: the Human Connectome Project (HCP) (Van Essen et al., 2013) dataset and the OULU dataset (Huotari et al., 2019) which provide either native or multi-rate fMRI acquisitions. Separately, the Function Biomedical Informatics Research Network (FBIRN) (Keator et al., 2016) and Center for Biomedical Research Excellence (Cetin et al., 2014) datasets were used solely for experiments involving synthetic noise augmentation. For empirical validation with genuine multi-rate acquisitions where sequence parameters naturally vary with temporal resolution, we processed the Emory Multiband dataset containing systematic multiband factor variations (MB2–MB12) producing temporal resolutions spanning three orders of magnitude (0.512s–5.67s) from 32 participants in standardized resting-state conditions. In the controlled simulation experiments, FBIRN and COBRE datasets were used to generate structured colored noise via Cholesky decomposition of permuted functional network connectivity matrices. This resulted in a structured noise matrix for each subject, each of which was used as a seed with which to corrupt the Oulu (multi-scale) or HCP (single-scale) data at on a range of noise levels (signal to noise ratio [1.5, 2.5]). Note, multi-scale data was rsFMRI captured at a high-speed TR=100ms and slow TR=2150ms, whereas HCP was cautpured at one TR=720ms and deliberately undersampled to simulate a slower TR. Pearson correlation matrices were constructed from either clean or corrupted data from both single and multiscale data. The upper triangular entries of fast and slow correlation matrices were then taken as features with the label being noise or clean. Fast and slow from single and multiscale datasets were fused by element-wise addition or concatenation and all features were fed to a variety of machine learning algorithms to predict noise on a range of noise strengths. For the Emory dataset, another multi-scale dataset, a similar method was employed, with fast and slow data processed to ROI timeseries, a sliding window approach was used to cut overlapping windows of dFNC, and features from fast, slow and fused were supplied to machine learning algorithms to predict gender. The methods pipeline consisted of the following: (1) standardized spatial preprocessing and temporal filtering applied uniformly across datasets; (2) constrained independent component analysis extracting 53 resting-state networks using the NeuroMark template; (3) dynamic functional connectivity matrix computation via Pearson correlation within windows;  (4) sliding-window approach to cut overlapping windows of component timecourses with dataset-specific window sizes preserving comparable temporal coverage; (5) feature vector extraction from upper-triangular connectivity matrices; (6) composite feature construction through element-wise summation (ADD) and concatenation (CONCAT) of paired fast-slow features; (7) noise corruption at varied signal-to-noise ratios using external subject covariance patterns (OULU and HCP); and (8) supervised classification with cross-validation ensuring subject-level holdout.

Results

Our results include an examination of sampling rate effects on ICA spatial maps, performance of our highly controlled noise prediction experiments across SNR levels, extension to gender prediction for multi- and single- rate data, experiments for the choice of undersampling rate of the single scale dataset, and an examination of the top influential features on model predictions. First, we examine spatial map quality differences between fast and slow sampling rates through independent component analysis, finding that generally, the faster TR brain networks were found to be clearer than those of the slower TR, conforming with the results of (Huotari et al., 2019). The faster TR, though captured at a rate which inevitably includes some physiological noise, still resulted in smoother ICA components for the faster TR data. Second, we report noise classification performance across signal-to-noise ratio levels, comparing how well models trained on single-rate features (fast only, slow only) versus composite multi-rate features (element-wise summation, concatenation) distinguish clean from corrupted signals. Across all models and datasets, the AUC exhibits the expected decrease with increasing SNR. Corrupted features with higher SNR contain more signal and less noise, making them extremely similar to original “clean” features and difficult to discriminate. With decreasing SNR, corrupted features become distinct from “clean” features, making them more distinguish- able. Classification performance drops from near-perfect accuracy at SNR 1.5 to near random at SNR 2.5, where the signal is over twice the noise level. The bottom row of the figure illustrates this effect in the single-scale HCP dataset: the highest AUC scores arise from the fastest sampling rate, with combined features performing slightly worse and the manually undersampled rate showing the lowest accuracy due to temporal information loss. Consequently, combining the artificially undersampled and original rates did not reproduce the information gain seen in the multiscale OULU dataset. Third, we extend these findings to gender prediction using the Emory Multiband dataset, evaluating whether composite features from genuine multi-rate acquisitions outperform single-rate features for demographic inference. We found promising advantages for Add and Concat composite features in the multi-rate Emory experiments, with Add consistently achieving the highest performance across classifiers. However, Concat features generally underperformed relative to Add in the Emory dataset, with this likely due to concatenation doubling the feature dimensionality, which can be problematic given Emory’s relatively small sample size. Fourth, we investigate the optimal undersampling factor for creating artificial slow-rate data from the HCP dataset, testing whether specific temporal resolution ratios maximize combined feature performance in simulated multi-rate scenarios. Fifth, we analyze logistic regression model weights to identify which functional network connections most strongly influence predictions, examining how these influential features differ between single-rate and multi-rate conditions and between datasets with authentic versus simulated temporal diversity. Together, these analyses address whether multi-rate temporal sampling provides complementary information that enhances predictive modeling beyond what single-rate acquisitions alone can achieve, with particular attention to distinguishing effects arising from genuine multi-rate acquisition versus those from computational manipulation of single-rate data.

Conclusion

Experiments with the OULU dataset in which models were trained on combined multi-rate features, outperformed those trained on single-rate data. In contrast, no apparent advantage was observed for combined features in the HCP dataset, with the leading AUC score coming from models trained on the fully sampled data. Models trained on composite features from manually undersampled HCP data yielded lower accuracy, consistent with informa- tion loss. Unlike HCP, the OULU dataset provided genuinely distinct acquisitions at two TRs, enabling complementary temporal information to be captured. In HCP, the slow sampling rate was simulated through uniform subsampling of fast data, a process that may have discarded high-frequency signal content without contributing new information. These observations suggest that the benefits of multi-rate combination depend on the authen- ticity of the acquisition. Beyond performance differences, examining logistic regression weights provides further perspective on how multi-rate features shape model predictions. The analysis of model weights in Section 5.2.2 demonstrates that dif- ferent sampling rates highlight distinct functional connections as most predictive of noise corruption, emphasizing the sensitivity of logistic regression models to the temporal resolution of the data. In the OULU dataset, the most important edge weights for composite features (Add, Concat) consisted of connections that were not as prominent in single-rate models, with shared connections among single and multi-rate features. This shared structure of similar weights with new connections in multi-scale features suggests that combined multi-rate information can yield complementary predictive structure. Importantly, at moderate noise levels (e.g., SNR 2.2 in OULU), the dissimilarity in model structure between composite and other feature types corresponded to the largest observed differences in AUC, indicating that the network connections most sensitive to noise vary depending on how No- ticeable differences in connections between SLOW and CONCAT+SLOW for OULU suggest that concatenation style fusion can enable cross feature interactions not present in the single-rate slow features. Models trained on manually-undersampled HCP data were found to have important edge weights largely overlapping with those identified in single-rate models, suggesting that combining information after information loss did not introduce additional predictive structure. Markedly missing are differences in weights for ADD and CONCAT features observed in OULU. These results suggest that influential connections are not fixed but adapt to both data quality and complementarity leading to more diverse model representations from composite multi-rate features. Having established that multi-rate features alter both performance and influential connections in noise clas- sification, the utility of composite features was next evaluated for gender prediction on the Emory Multiband (multi-rate) and HCP (single-rate, control) datasets. Key observations from Figure 9 highlight significant advan- tages in the Emory Multiband dataset, with highest AUC scores from the composite features. Add is consistently the top AUC across classifiers compared to single-rate features SR1 (Fast TR) and SR 2 (Slow TR) alone. Multi- rate Concat scores were generally higher in performance compared to single rate features, with scores suffering in the multi-layer perceptron, potentially due to the larger input size introducing overfitting risk and increasing learning difficulty. In gender prediction with the single-rate dataset (HCP), variation in performance decreased, with results being fairly indistinguishable across sampling rates and no significant advantages for the composite features. HCP manual undersampling (Slow TR) showed degraded performance which is consistent with information loss and highest performances are observed in the original sampling rate (Fast TR).
