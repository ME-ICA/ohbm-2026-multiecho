Introduction

Sickle Cell Disease (SCD) is a genetic blood disorder caused by a mutation in the beta-globin gene, impairing oxygen delivery and neurovascular function1. This dysfunction contributes to neurological complications, including stroke, silent cerebral infarcts, and progressive cognitive decline1. Deficits in executive domains such as cognitive flexibility, processing speed, and memory have been documented in individuals with SCD, yet the neural mechanisms driving these impairments remain unclear2. Prior work suggests that the frontoparietal network, particularly the prefrontal cortex, supports cognitive flexibility, but how SCD affects the structure and function of these circuits is still not well understood3. Research has shown a correlation between damage to white matter structural integrity, such as reduced fractional anisotropy, and slower processing speed in patients with SCD4. Furthermore, resting state studies similarly link aberrant functional connectivity in networks such as the default mode to cognitive difficulties in SCD5. A major gap is our limited understanding of how these structural abnormalities and functional disruptions interact during cognitive processing in SCD. To address this, we employed structural–functional connectivity (SC–FC) coupling to quantify how strongly a region's functional connectivity profile is predicted by its DTI-derived structural connections, offering a framework to link these modalities5. This task-based approach represents the first direct test of how chronic vasculopathy constrains network dynamics during active cognitive task in SCD. In this study, we computed node-wise SC–FC coupling using DTI and task-based fMRI while participants completed a customized Digit Symbol Substitution Task (DSST), a measure of processing speed and cognitive flexibility5. We hypothesize that patients with SCD will exhibit weakened SC–FC coupling in the frontoparietal network, which will explain their deficits in response time and accuracy.

Methods

Participants included 5 healthy controls (aged 25 ± 4.39; 3F/2M) and 8 individuals with SCD (aged 35 ± 12.36; 5F/3M), all of whom completed demographic, pain, and psychological assessments prior to scanning. During our customized Digit Symbol Substitution Task (DSST; Fig. 1), a reference table paired digits with symbols, and participants viewed a digit–symbol prompt and indicated via keypress whether the pair was congruent or incongruent, engaging cognitive flexibility through variable digit–symbol combinations. Behavioral accuracy and response times were analyzed in Python using descriptive statistics and t-tests. All MRI data were collected on a Siemens 3T Prisma system. A T1-weighted MPRAGE scan was acquired for anatomical reference. Diffusion MRI was obtained using a multiband EPI sequence (MB = 4) with 1.5 mm isotropic voxels, TR = 3.23 s, TE = 90.6 ms, a 140 × 140 matrix, and monopolar diffusion encoding; the multishell scheme used b-values of 300, 1000, 2000, and 3000 s/mm² with 6, 24, 40, and 66 directions. Susceptibility distortion correction was performed using FSL topup with 7 PA and 1 AP b0 images, followed by applytopup, bias field correction from the b0 image, and motion/eddy correction with b-table rotation. Restricted diffusion was quantified using restricted diffusion imaging (Yeh, F., 2017), and reconstruction used generalized q-sampling imaging with a diffusion sampling length ratio of 1.25. Deterministic tractography with augmented tracking strategies employed a randomly selected anisotropy threshold (0.5–0.7 Otsu), angular threshold (45°–90°), step size equal to voxel spacing, and length constraints of 30–200 mm. Diffusion analyses and tract-shape metrics were performed in DSI Studio6. Structural connectivity matrices were derived using the Brainnetome atlas, and number of tracts was used as the structural metric. For task fMRI, one run of the DSST was acquired (TR = 1.031 s, TE = 0.03 s, slice thickness = 2 mm, spacing = 2 mm, duration = 6:15 min), and preprocessing was completed with fMRIPrep (motion correction, normalization, smoothing). Physiological data—including cardiac and respiratory signals—were collected using an EKG and a respiratory belt. These recordings were processed using the niphlem package to generate RETROICOR-based nuisance regressors, which were included as covariates during functional connectivity estimation to reduce physiological noise7. Functional connectivity matrices were generated using custom Python code by computing pairwise correlations among the 246 Brainnetome atlas regions8.

To quantify structure–function relationships at high spatial resolution, we implemented a node-wise SC–FC coupling approach5. After constructing each participant’s 246 × 246 structural connectivity matrix and corresponding 246 × 246 functional connectivity matrix, we treated each column as the connectivity “fingerprint” of an individual brain region5. For each node, we computed the Pearson correlation between its structural connectivity fingerprint and its functional connectivity fingerprint, yielding a coupling coefficient that reflects how strongly that region’s functional interactions align with its underlying white-matter architecture. This procedure resulted in a 246-element SC–FC coupling vector for each participant, providing a fine-grained spatial map of structure–function alignment across the brain. We then aggregated these coupling values from the individual nodes into 24 major gyral-based regions as defined by the Brainnetome atlas hierarchy, allowing us to characterize coupling deficits at a clinically interpretable anatomical scale. Group comparisons and visualizations (violin plots, spatial difference maps, and network-level heatmaps) were then used to identify cortical and subcortical systems showing altered coupling in SCD.

Results

Across behavioral performance, both groups completed the DSST with high accuracy and showed comparable response times (Fig. 1c). Adults with SCD consistently demonstrated higher accuracy than controls across all trial types: combined accuracy was 96.24% ± 2.41 in SCD and 90.04% ± 8.88 in controls; congruent accuracy was 96.31% ± 2.18 in SCD and 91.82% ± 10.31 in controls; and incongruent accuracy was 96.23% ± 3.27 in SCD and 88.27% ± 10.11 in controls. Response times were similar between groups. For combined trials, SCD averaged 1.60 ± 0.65 s and controls averaged 1.60 ± 0.71 s; for congruent trials, SCD averaged 1.50 ± 0.63 s and controls 1.44 ± 0.61 s; for incongruent trials, SCD averaged 1.70 ± 0.65 s and controls 1.79 ± 0.76 s.

Node -wise SC–FC coupling analysis revealed clear group-level differences in the distribution of coupling values across the 246 Brainnetome regions. Controls showed a higher mean coupling coefficient (0.1907 ± 0.1013) compared to individuals with SCD (0.1524 ± 0.0957). As shown in Fig. 2a, the distribution of coupling values in controls (blue) is shifted upward relative to the distribution in SCD (red), indicating a greater number of nodes with higher coupling values in the control group. To examine the spatial layout of these differences, a regional contrast map (Control – SCD) was generated (Fig. 2b). In this map, red regions denote areas where coupling was numerically higher in controls, while blue regions denote areas where coupling was numerically higher in SCD. Red-dominated areas were primarily located in the frontoparietal cortex, visual cortex, and posterior association regions. Blue regions, reflecting higher coupling in SCD, appeared in localized portions of the prefrontal and temporal cortices.

As shown in Fig. 2c, aggregation of mean SC–FC coupling differences to the gyral level revealed a descending hierarchy of deficits. The largest positive group differences (Control > SCD) were observed in the medioventral occipital cortex and paracentral lobule, followed by prominent reductions in patients with SCD in the orbital gyrus, hippocampus, and precuneus. Subcortical and limbic structures, including the thalamus and basal ganglia, also showed significantly higher coupling in controls. Additional positive differences extended to the lateral occipital cortex, postcentral gyrus, fusiform gyrus, and cingulate gyrus. In contrast, several frontal and parietal regions, such as the superior frontal gyrus, precentral gyrus, and superior parietal lobule, exhibited small or near-zero group differences. A limited set of gyri demonstrated negative differences (SCD > Control), most notably within the inferior parietal lobule and superior temporal gyrus. Together, these findings provide a quantitative mapping of spatially localized coupling contrasts and gyrus-level deficits in patients with SCD.

Conclusion

Behavioral performance on the DSST showed that adults with SCD maintained higher accuracy and response times comparable to controls. This pattern suggests that cognitive flexibility and processing speed during the task were largely preserved in the SCD group. The higher accuracy in SCD may indicate that participants approached the task more cautiously and placed greater emphasis on avoiding errors. This behavioral pattern contrasts with the neural findings. SC–FC coupling was lower overall in SCD, and spatial analyses indicated that controls showed higher coupling across broad regions of the frontal, parietal, occipital, and subcortical systems. In comparison, regions with higher coupling in SCD were limited and appeared mainly within prefrontal and temporal areas. Gyrus-level comparisons supported this distribution. Controls showed larger differences in visual and subcortical gyri, while many frontal and parietal gyri displayed similar coupling values across groups.

Two distinct explanations may account for how the SCD group achieved behavioral performance similar to controls despite reduced SC–FC coupling. One explanation focuses on compensatory recruitment. Individuals with SCD may engage brain regions that show weaker coupling in controls. These regions may become active to support task completion when structural constraints are reduced. This process aligns with the concept of cognitive reserve, which allows individuals to maintain performance by relying on additional neural resources. A second explanation points to functional reorganization. Lower coupling in SCD may reflect a more flexible or adaptive pattern of communication. This pattern may allow participants to reroute information and bypass structurally vulnerable white-matter pathways. The preservation of task performance would then arise from the ability of the brain to shift communication patterns rather than from the engagement of additional regions alone.

These findings should be interpreted in light of several limitations, most notably that age and SCD subtype were not controlled as covariates. Since both factors are known to influence cognitive performance, they likely contributed to the observed group variability and warrant consideration in future analyses of clinical heterogeneity. Additionally, the relatively small sample size limits statistical power, and ongoing enrollment will help address this issue. Another limitation relates to the pattern observed in controls, where SC–FC coupling was high and widespread. This pattern, combined with their behavioral performance, raises the possibility that the task demands may not have fully engaged their attention. Reduced engagement could result in coupling patterns that resemble resting-state rather than task-specific connectivity. Future analyses will compare task and resting-state coupling within the same participants to test this possibility. "Finally, while the structural and functional metrics used in this study were selected to align with existing literature, they may not capture subtle microstructural or directional nuances. Relying on streamline count for structural connectivity and Pearson correlation for functional connectivity limits our sensitivity to white matter integrity changes better captured by fractional anisotropy or mean diffusivity, and directional influences revealed by effective connectivity methods like dynamic causal modeling. Thus, we plan to evaluate SC–FC coupling using these alternative metrics in future work to provide a more comprehensive characterization of network dynamics.
