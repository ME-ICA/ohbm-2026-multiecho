Introduction

Automated seizure detection from electroencephalography (EEG) is a critical need in both clinical and preclinical epilepsy research, particularly for long-duration recordings where manual annotation is labor-intensive and subjective. In rodent models of epilepsy, reliable detection algorithms are essential for quantifying seizure burden, characterizing disease mechanisms, and evaluating therapeutic interventions. However, seizure-related EEG patterns are highly nonstationary and heterogeneous, posing challenges for traditional feature-based approaches.^1

Recent advances in deep learning have enabled data-driven seizure detection methods that leverage time–frequency representations of EEG signals. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs), in particular, have shown strong performance in learning discriminative spectral–temporal features directly from spectrograms, reducing reliance on handcrafted features.^2 In preclinical EEG, seizure activity is often dominated by low-frequency components, motivating focused analysis in the delta–theta–alpha range.^3

In this study, we present a 2D CNN–based seizure detection framework using spectrograms derived from rat EEG recordings acquired in an NMDAR encephalitis model. The primary aim was to establish a robust baseline detector in a data-limited setting and to quantify its performance using standard classification metrics.  

Methods

Experiment Design IgG purified from the peripheral blood of patients with anti-NMDAR encephalitis (n=3) and healthy controls (HC) (n=4) was chronically administered into the CA3 region of the hippocampus in 2-3-month-old male Wistar rats using osmotic pumps with a brain infusion kit over 14 days. Continuous wireless video-EEG and EMG recordings were performed throughout the infusion period.

Data and Annotations EEG recordings were obtained from rats in an NMDAR encephalitis model. A total of 141 seizure events were manually annotated from recordings of three subjects and used as ground truth labels for supervised learning.

Preprocessing and Segmentation Continuous EEG signals were segmented into 10-second non-overlapping windows. Each window was labeled as seizure or non-seizure based on 50% temporal overlap with annotated seizure events. This window length was selected to capture characteristic seizure dynamics while maintaining manageable input dimensionality. Windows that include signal loss were excluded.

Time–Frequency Representation For each EEG segment, a time–frequency spectrogram was computed to characterize spectral content over time. Analysis was restricted to the 0.5–15 Hz frequency range, encompassing delta, theta, and alpha bands, which are known to contain prominent seizure-related activity in rodent EEG. This frequency selection reduced dimensionality and emphasized physiologically relevant features.

Dataset Construction EEG recordings from three rats (Rat 1: 120 seizures, Rat 2: 12 seizures, Rat 3: 11 seizures) were processed into 10-second spectrograms (0.5-15 Hz) with 50% overlap. To prevent temporal data leakage, each rat's data was chronologically split (60% training, 20% validation, 20% test) at the seizure event level. Subject-aware augmentation was applied to balance representation: Rat 1 received no augmentation, while Rats 2 and 3 were augmented ten times using Gaussian noise, frequency masking, and time masking techniques. The training set was balanced to a 1:3 seizure-to-non-seizure ratio through downsampling of the non-seizure data, yielding 10,168 training spectrograms (2,542 seizure, 7,626 non-seizure). Validation (17,310 spectrograms) and test sets (17,377 spectrograms, 32 seizure events) maintained natural class imbalance to reflect real-world conditions.

CNN Architecture and Training A custom 3-block 2D CNN was designed with increasing filter depth (32, 64, 128 filters; 3 x 3 kernels). Each convolutional block incorporated Batch Normalization to stabilize learning and Dropout (0.3–0.5) to prevent overfitting, concluding with Global Average Pooling to reduce spatial dimensions without dense layer parameters. To address the severe class imbalance, the model was trained using Focal Loss (gamma=2.0, alpha=0.5), which dynamically down-weights easy negatives to focus learning on hard-to-classify seizure examples. Training employed the Adam optimizer with an adaptive learning rate scheduler and early stopping. A subject-aware data augmentation pipeline, applying Gaussian noise and frequency/time masking, was selectively utilized on data-sparse subjects to improve cross-subject generalization.

Evaluation Metrics Performance was assessed using a dual-metric framework: Window-Level metrics (Precision, Recall, F1, Specificity, Accuracy) quantified temporal segmentation quality, while Event-Level metrics (Sensitivity, False Positives per hour) evaluated clinical utility by defining a seizure as detected if any constituent window was classified positively.

Results

The model was trained for 50 epochs with early stopping (patience=15), achieving convergence at epoch 47. Performance was evaluated on a held-out test set spanning 24.1 hours and containing 32 seizure events with natural class imbalance. Regarding the primary clinical metric, the model achieved an event-level sensitivity of 90.6% (29/32 seizures detected). The three missed seizures corresponded to sparse subjects (Rat 3 and Rat 4), reflecting the challenge of limited subject-specific training data. The system maintained a false positive rate of 5.47 per hour. At the window level, the model demonstrated a precision of 53.4%, recall of 79.9%, and an F1 score of 0.640, alongside a high specificity of 99.2% and an overall accuracy of 99.0%.

Context on Data Challenges and Real-World Implications

This study confronted substantial methodological challenges inherent to preclinical seizure detection. First, severe inter-subject heterogeneity: Rat 2 dominated the dataset with 120 seizures (85% of total events), while Rats 3 and 4 contributed only 8 and 13 seizures, respectively. This extreme imbalance is common in rodent epilepsy models, where seizure frequency varies widely across animals. Second, cross-subject generalization: differences in electrode impedance, skull thickness, and individual seizure semiology create distinct amplitude and spectral profiles, necessitating subject-adaptive preprocessing strategies (per-window normalization) over traditional file-level z-scoring.

Despite these constraints, the model achieved 90.6% event-level sensitivity—clinically meaningful performance for automated screening applications. Notably, the 5.47 FP/hour rate, while modest in precision-focused applications, translates to approximately 131 false alarms over 24 hours. However, in the context of long-duration preclinical recordings (often spanning days to weeks), this represents a >95% reduction in manual review burden compared to unaided annotation. For a 7-day continuous recording (~168 hours), the model would flag ~920 events for review versus ~120,000 raw 10-second windows—an efficiency gain enabling high-throughput phenotyping in drug screening or genetic studies.

The three missed seizures (9.4% false negatives) occurred in sparse subjects, suggesting that subject-aware augmentation, partially but incompletely compensated for limited training examples. Future work incorporating semi-supervised learning or active learning strategies may further improve minority-class performance.

Conclusion

In this study, a spectrogram-based deep learning model was developed for automated seizure detection using EEG recordings from an NMDAR dataset. Despite the relatively limited number of seizure events, the model demonstrated robust performance, achieving a balanced trade-off between sensitivity and specificity. Consequently, subject-specific patterns may have influenced performance. In future work, the generalizability and robustness of the model will be systematically evaluated using EEG recordings from additional NMDAR rats, with the aim of assessing cross-subject performance and improving translational relevance. Overall, these results highlight the potential of time–frequency representations combined with deep learning for detecting seizure activity in autoimmune epilepsy models.
