Introduction

Task-based functional MRI (tb-fMRI) is a core method for probing the neural mechanisms underlying human cognition, behavior, and psychopathology. By quantifying task-evoked changes in BOLD activity, tb-fMRI enables the characterization of neural responses that vary across individuals, developmental stages, and clinical populations. Despite its potential to capture such variation, most tb-fMRI studies still rely on traditional case–control designs. While these designs have yielded valuable insights into group-level activation patterns, they inherently overlook the substantial inter-individual heterogeneity present in both healthy and clinical populations1. This heterogeneity is further compounded by variability in task paradigms, task parameters, and acquisition protocols across independent studies; factors that are rarely modeled explicitly2,3. Normative modeling1 has emerged as a powerful framework for quantifying this variability at the individual level. By mapping between brain measures and biological, cognitive, task-related, and acquisition-related variables and binding different studies to a common reference, normative models capture the expected range of variation in large healthy and clinical cohorts. Beyond structural and resting-state MRI, normative modeling has been successfully applied to understand voxel-level variability in task paradigms such as the Emotional Face Matching Task2 and Fear Conditioning3, demonstrating its utility for disentangling biological from non-biological sources of variability. Here, we extend normative modeling to the domains of working memory and reward processing by estimating large-scale lifespan normative models.

Methods

We estimated large-scale lifespan normative models of task-evoked brain activation during working memory (WM) and reward processing using task-based fMRI data from up to 7,238 healthy individuals aggregated across multiple datasets with harmonized processing pipelines (Table 1). Although they activate similar brain regions, various task paradigms were used to assess WM and reward processing. All individual-level tb-fMRI data were preprocessed using the standardized ENIGMA HALFpipe4 pipeline (v1.2.3) to minimize site-related variability. Processed images underwent manual quality control by multiple raters, and participants with poor image quality or excessive motion (mean framewise displacement > 0.5) were excluded. First-level analyses in HALFpipe produced individual z-statistic maps for the WM contrast (2-back > 0-back) and the reward contrasts (hit > miss; anticipation reward > anticipation control). For participants with multiple task runs, contrast estimates were combined using fixed-effects analysis (nilearn.glm.compute_fixed_effects, v0.12.1). For each contrast, the resulting z-maps served as the response variables in the normative models. WM and reward models were estimated as a function of age, sex, mean FD, site, phase-encoding direction, and task-related parameters (e.g., task length, number of trials, target stimuli, task design, task type, gain type, where applicable). We used Bayesian Linear Regression with cubic B-splines (knots = 5) to model contrast-specific activation patterns. Model generalizability was assessed using a half-split train–test evaluation, with performance quantified using explained variance (EXPV) and mean standardized log loss (MSLL). After model fitting, we computed normative probability maps (NPMs) indexing voxelwise individual deviations. Z-scores were calculated as the difference between observed and model-predicted activation scaled by the prediction variance. NPMs were thresholded at |Z| ≥ 2.6 (p < .005), and the number of significantly deviating voxels was summarized per individual and across the sample. The distributional properties of voxelwise deviations were evaluated via skewness and kurtosis. All model training and evaluation were conducted using the Predictive Clinical Neuroscience toolkit (PCNtoolkit) software (v1.1.1; https://github.com/amarquand/PCNtoolkit).

Results

Figure 1 summarizes the explained variance of the normative models and presents the corresponding normative probability maps (NPMs) for each task contrast. In brief, the estimated normative model for the WM load > control contrast showed a good fit, with an EXPV reaching .34 and an MSLL reaching −1.49. Local maxima were observed in canonical WM regions, including the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), cerebellum, superior parietal gyrus (SPG), occipito-parietal junction (OPJ), posterior cingulate cortex (PCC), and ventral PFC. The model for the reward hit > miss contrast also performed well (EXPV = .332; MSLL = −1.020), with the highest explained variance in the striatum, visual cortex, mPFC, and superior temporal sulcus (STS). In contrast, the model for the reward anticipation (reward > anticipation-control) contrast yielded more modest performance (EXPV = .205; MSLL = −.758). The strongest fit was detected in the visual cortex (lingual and calcarine areas), putamen, insula, supplementary motor area (SMA), and ventral PFC. Across all contrasts, voxelwise skewness and kurtosis largely fell within acceptable limits (−1 < skewness < 1; kurtosis ≈ 0), indicating that individual deviations from the estimated norms were approximately Gaussian and therefore interpretable. However, we found a poor fit and high positive kurtosis in the most ventral areas of the brain (e.g., vmPFC, ventral temporal areas, as well as ventral cerebellum), which can be explained by varying degrees of signal dropout. Therefore, we advise the readers to interpret individual deviations within these areas with caution. The NPM for WM load > control revealed substantial inter-individual variability across the cortex, especially in working-memory-associated regions such as the mPFC, dorsolateral PFC, SPG, and OPJ, up to ~2% of the sample. The largest apparent deviations (3–3.5%) were in the ventral temporal cortex and the ventral cerebellum; however, given the poor model fit in these areas, these values are not considered meaningful. For the reward contrasts, inter-individual variability was generally lower and primarily localized to ventral brain regions (i.e., due to signal dropout), with the paracingulate gyrus emerging in the anticipation contrast.

Conclusion

This study provides the largest lifespan analysis to date of the neural correlates of WM and reward processing, with a specific focus on inter-individual heterogeneity. While we identify robust and reproducible activation patterns across both domains, substantial variability persists at the level of individuals, underscoring the limitations of traditional case–control approaches. Normative modeling offers a principled framework for characterizing this variability and for linking individual deviations in task-evoked activation to cognitive differences and symptom expression. As such, it holds considerable promise for advancing mechanistic and clinically meaningful models of brain–behavior relationships in mental disorders.
