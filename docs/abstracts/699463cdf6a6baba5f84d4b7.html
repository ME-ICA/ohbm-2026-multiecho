Introduction

Aggression, involving hostile or harmful behaviors directed at another individual with the intent to cause harm, can manifest along a continuum from nonphysical forms (e.g., verbal aggression) to extreme violence (e.g., severe injuries or death). Different forms of aggression pose a major public health and safety concern associated with various social and psychological problems, particularly in adolescents and young adults. Aggressive behaviors can be categorized in numerous ways, but the most common distinction focuses on two types: reactive (RA) and proactive (PA) aggression. RA is characterized by an unplanned, retaliatory response to provocation, whereas PA refers to unprovoked, goal-oriented behavior aimed at achieving some personal gain1. These aggression subtypes have been widely investigated in neuroimaging and genetic studies, highlighting the contribution of distinct neural circuits2 and molecular pathways3. Evidence suggests that RA is associated with heightened emotional reactivity and limbic system structure and functioning, whereas PA is linked to altered prefrontal control and reward-processing mechanisms. However, how the neural systems underpinning aggression are shaped by genetic and molecular mechanisms remains poorly understood. In this study, we aimed to address this research question by (i) identifying the neuroanatomical systems associated with RA and PA, and (ii) linking these patterns to genes and molecular pathways using an imaging transcriptomics approach.

Methods

This study included 670 participants from the FemNAT-CD cohort4 (391 neurotypical individuals and 279 with conduct disorder; mean age = 14.21 ± 2.49 years; 51% female; mean IQ = 100 ± 13) with available structural MRI (sMRI) data and behavioral assessments from the Reactive-Proactive Aggression Questionnaire (RPQ)1. Measures of cortical thickness (CT) were computed using Freesurfer 6.0.0 with a 15 mm full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel for spatial smoothing. Vertex-wise CT was modeled using generalized linear models (GLMs) to assess the main effects of RA and PA, as measured by the RPQ, while controlling for linear and quadratic age terms, sex, full-scale IQ, MRI acquisition site, and mean CT across the cortex. Corrections for multiple comparisons were performed using random-field-theory-based cluster analysis for non-isotropic images with a cluster-based significance threshold (pclust) of 0.05 (two-tailed). To identify genes with a cortical pattern of expression that resembles aggression-related imaging patterns, we performed imaging transcriptomics analyses as described in Ecker et al.5. Specifically, the analyses were performed using spatial autocorrelation-preserving Spearman correlations with surface representations of normalized gene expression maps from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA), setting a significance level at pperm<0.05. Genes transcriptomically associated with the RA and PA patterns, as well as their overlap, were subsequently analyzed using Metascape’s multi-gene list analysis for functional enrichment and pathway clustering. Metascape (www.metascape.org) integrates multiple annotation resources (including Gene Ontology, KEGG, Reactome, CORUM, and WikiPathways) and performs over-representation analysis using the hypergeometric test, with p-values corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (pfdr < 0.05). In addition, protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed from the STRING and BioGRID databases, and densely connected subnetworks were identified using the MCODE algorithm. Each MCODE cluster was subsequently annotated according to its most significant enriched term, enabling biological interpretation of shared and distinct functional modules across the three gene sets. Analyses were conducted using Metascape (accessed November 2025).

Results

Vertex-wise GLM analysis based on CT identified distinct significant RFT-corrected clusters for the main effects of RA and PA, after controlling for confounders (age, sex, IQ, imaging site, and mean CT) and their interactions (Figure 1A-B). Higher RA scores were associated with reduced cortical thickness in posterior occipito–temporo–parietal association cortex (e.g., cuneus, angular and superior temporal gyri) but increased thickness in sensorimotor, lateral prefrontal executive-control, auditory–temporal and paralimbic regions (including pre-/supplementary and primary motor cortex, postcentral gyrus, dorsolateral and ventrolateral prefrontal cortex, temporal pole, anterior cingulate cortex, and primary/association auditory–temporal cortex). In contrast, higher PA scores were associated with increased cortical thickness in occipito–parietal and auditory–temporal association cortex (including cuneus, angular and superior parietal gyri, occipitotemporal and superior temporal regions) but reduced thickness in paralimbic, medial temporal and somatosensory regions (including temporal pole, dorsal anterior cingulate, hippocampal/parahippocampal areas and primary somatosensory cortex).

The imaging transcriptomics analysis of the main effects patterns (unthresholded t-maps) identified 229 unique genes with significant transcriptomic association with RA, and 87 for PA, with 53 overlapping genes - a significant overlap by Fisher’s exact test (OR = 122.57, 95% CI: [76.1, 201.15], p < 0.001, Figure 1C-D). PPI network analysis of the shared genes identified clusters significantly enriched for synaptic-related processes, such as synaptic membrane (GO:0097060, -log10(p) = 5.6), Neurexins and neuroligins (R-HSA-6794361, -log10(p) = 5.6), and positive regulation of synapse assembly (GO:0051965, -log10(p) = 5.1), highlighting convergence on synaptic organization mechanisms (Figure 1E). Moreover, gene enrichment analysis using Metascape revealed that genes associated with the reactive axis were predominantly enriched for response to stimulus (GO:0050896, -log10(p) = 5.69), cellular (GO:0009987, -log10(p) = 4.38) and biological regulation (GO:0065007, -log10(p) = 3.66) processes, indicating activation of adaptive and regulatory mechanisms underlying cellular responsiveness and environmental interaction. In contrast, genes associated with the proactive axis showed significant enrichment for developmental (GO:0032502, -log10(p) = 3.93), response to stimulus (GO:0050896, -log10(p) = 3.44) and positive regulation of biological processes (GO:0048518, -log10(p) = 3.29), indicating engagement of developmental and modulatory programs supporting functional organization and plasticity (Figure 1F). Thus, RA and PA measures of aggression not only mapped onto distinct neurocognitive networks but were also associated with different genes and genetic networks.

Conclusion

Our investigation of the cortical correlates of RA and PA indicates that these distinct aggression subtypes implicate partly overlapping brain regions as well as spatially distinct substrates. Lower-order posterior regions linked to sensory processing and integration of external stimuli were implicated in both RA and PA but showed opposite association with CT, consistent with divergent neurobiological within partially shared sensory-integration circuits. In contrast, RA shows a strong association with higher-order frontal and motor related regions, generally related to impulsivity, action initiation and regulation. Conversely, we observed that PA is associated with decreased CT in regulatory but also memory-related regions, e.g., medial-temporal regions, indicating alterations in decision-making and empathy circuits.  These findings align well with the behavioural manifestations of both RA and PA. Furthermore, the gene decoding and enrichment results suggest that while shared genes converge on synaptic organization pathways, the reactive and proactive subtypes capture distinct biological programs related to adaptive cellular regulation and developmental modulation, respectively.
