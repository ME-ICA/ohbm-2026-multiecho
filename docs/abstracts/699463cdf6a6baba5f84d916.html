Introduction

Neuroplasticity of the immature brain is considered essential for optimal neurodevelopment. However, early brain focal injury can lead to lifelong neurocognitive deficits, suggesting that its impact often extends beyond the primary lesion site. The considerable inter-individual variability in the long term outcomes remains insufficiently understood.

Neonatal arterial ischemic stroke (NAIS) provides a valuable model for studying post-lesional plasticity in early development. In approximately two-thirds of cases, lesions affect the left hemisphere, most often within the middle cerebral artery territory. This lesion pattern typically damages one or several regions of the language network, particularly areas in the inferior frontal, parietal (dorsal stream), and superior temporal gyri (ventral stream). Remarkably, many affected children nonetheless achieve near-typical language abilities, supported by homotopic language reorganization in the right hemisphere ¹. This atypical early right-hemispheric specialization has been proposed as a potential explanation for the subtle but widespread non linguistic impairments reported in this population, commonly referred to as a functional “crowding” effect. ² 

The AVCnnADO project extends into adolescence the neuropsychological and MRI multimodal assessment of individuals with neonatal stroke (n = 100 at recruitment). We investigated the spatial relationships between language and calculation-related functional activation in the adolescents according to their hemispheric language specialization (typical left , or atypical right or bilateral). We further explore how these functional patterns relate to clinical performance. 

Methods

The AVCnnADO project relies on a prospective cohort of subjects with neonatal stroke (n= 100 newborns at recruitment) followed until adolescence (n = 32, 14 F, 18M), who performed extensive language and neuropsychological testing and multimodal MRI at 16 years of age. After exclusions based on lesion territory, 31 patients remained. Age-matched controls (N=30, 14 F, 16 M) underwent identical MRI but simplified clinical assessments.

MRI data at 16 years of age were acquired on a Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma Fit system equipped with a 64-channel head coil. Anatomical imaging included T1-weighted MPRAGE (TR = 2300 ms, TE = 3.05 ms, TI = 248 ms, voxel size = 0.9 mm isotropic, 176 slices) and T2-FLAIR (TR = 5000 ms, TE = 396 ms, voxel size = 0.9 mm isotropic, 176 slices). Functional MRI tasks were acquired with identical parameters across paradigms (2-mm isotropic voxels, 69 slices, TR = 1.81 s, TE = 30.4 ms,).

A Sentence Generation Task (SGT) block-design paradigm was used to determine the individual language hemispheric specialization. This robust paradigm supports assessment of language processing even in cognitively impaired subjects. 

A slowed version of a ‘localizer’ paradigm ³ was adapted to be used in adolescents with suboptimal abilities. Participants received a continuous succession of intermingled auditory and visual stimuli involving sentence comprehension, simple subtraction and motor responses. 

As all tasks were performed silently in the scanner, task comprehension and compliance were verified during pre-scan training.

Quality control was done with MRIQC, and preprocessing with fMRIPrep 22.1.1. One control and one patient were excluded based on SGT poor quality and motion related artifacts. The final sample included 30 controls (14F/16M), and 30 patients (17M/13F) 

After detrending, smoothing and accounting for confounds, individual Z-maps were generated using a GLM with a Glover hemodynamic response function

Language Lateralization Index (LI) were computed from individual SGT Z-maps using the SPM12 LI toolbox ⁴, LI values approaching +1 for left-hemisphere dominance and −1 for right-hemisphere dominance. Patients were stratified into Typical (> 0.2) and Atypical language representation (< 0.2) groups for subsequent analyses. 

Correlations with clinical performance: As a first approach, the global score of the language performance scale CELF was plotted against LI values. This score reflects global language performance and is highly correlated with IQ. When a group-specific trend was visually apparent, Pearson correlations were computed.

Group-level GLMs were computed in Nilearn to characterize functional organization in controls, typically lateralized patients, and atypically lateralized patients. For spatial comparability, all patient Z-maps were registered to MNI ICBM152 2009c symmetric space. fMRIPrep ensured accurate structural–functional alignment. Group analyses used 8-mm FWHM smoothing and non-parametric inference with Threshold-Free Cluster Enhancement (TFCE, 500 permutations). Resulting p-value maps were thresholded at p < 0.05, FDR-corrected. Contrasts examined were: right vs. left hand motor activation, audio vs. video stimulation, and here we focused on language vs. calculation tasks in both visual and auditory modalities. Task-related BOLD signals were extracted and averaged within the resulting activation clusters as an additional validation step.

Finally, effect-size maps from first-level analyses were processed in SPM12 to compute conjunction maps for language- and calculation-related activations. 

Results

Language lateralization during the SGT revealed a clear inversion of the typical hemispheric specialization in most patients with left-hemisphere lesions, who showed right-hemisphere cortical (-0.4 LI +/-0.3) and left cerebellar (mean 0.5 +/-0.35) dominance. Patients with right-hemisphere lesions exhibited the expected left-lateralized pattern similar to controls ( mean 0.6 +/- 0.25 for both groups). Based on hemispheric lateralization indices, the cohort was stratified into typical (left-dominant) and atypical (bilateral/right-dominant) subgroups. All controls showed typical lateralization (mean LI = 0.58), and among patients, 14 were typically lateralized (5F/9M), and 16 atypically lateralized (8F/8M).

Second-level activation maps confirmed the known patterns: controls displayed the expected left perisylvian, frontal, basal ganglia, and right cerebellar network; typically lateralized patients showed a more restricted but highly asymmetric left-dominant network; atypical patients showed a mirrored right-hemisphere pattern accompanied by left cerebellar involvement.

Clinical–behavioral correlations, shown in figure 1a indicated no significant associations between lateralization and cognitive performance in patients with right-hemisphere lesions. In contrast, patients with left-hemisphere lesions showed better CELF scores (subscores are not shown but were all significantly correlated) with more right-lateralized activation.

In the language–calculation contrast, controls showed canonical left-dominant temporal language activations and a bilateral fronto-parietal calculation network centered on the intraparietal sulcus. Both patient groups showed preserved temporal activations during language processing but no significant group-level activation for calculation, likely reflecting increased inter-subject variability, reduced statistical power, or reduced task compliance.

Direct comparisons between typical and atypical patients revealed no significant differences, prompting the generation of a combined patient activation map for language vs calculation contrast, shown in Figure 1b , 1. 

Across all patients, language activations mostly involved the superior temporal gyrus supramarginal and superior postcentral area. Calculation-related activity was markedly reduced and shifted toward left inferior temporal and fusiform regions, with no clear engagement of the intraparietal sulcus.

To further probe the relationship between hemispheric specialization and calculation networks, activation maps of atypical language patients were symmetrically flipped to align dominant hemispheres (Figure 1b 2). This analysis showed that language activations were consistently stronger in the dominant hemisphere, involving anterior and posterior temporal regions, thalamus, hippocampus, and superior parietal cortex. Calculation-related activations were also lateralized to the dominant hemisphere, engaging the supramarginal gyrus and lateral temporo-occipital cortex, with no detectable activation in the non-dominant hemisphere. BOLD responses in these regions were comparable in amplitude to those of the left hemisphere in controls.

Supplementary conjunction analysis, not shown in this abstract, as preliminary results, revealed a lateralization in the language-dominant hemisphere in both typical and atypical patient groups. This atypical lateralization was stronger in regions facing the lesional area, as expected, but also in the cerebellum and occipital lobe, which were not lesional in our cohort. 

Conclusion

This ongoing study examined patterns of hemispheric language organization following neonatal arterial ischemic stroke (NAIS) and the impact of atypical language organization on cognitive outcomes and functional specialization of calculation. We confirmed that patients with left-hemisphere lesions most often recruit homotopic right-hemisphere regions for language, accompanied by an inversion of cerebellar lateralization. While the cortical reorganization has been frequently reported in the literature, cerebellar inversion is described far less often. This reorganization was most consistently observed in left perisylvian lesions involving the dorsal stream. Importantly, 20% of left-lesion patients retained typical language dominance, highlighting the heterogeneity of post-stroke plasticity.

Surprisingly, stronger right-hemisphere language dominance in patients with left-hemisphere lesions was associated with both better language and cognitive performance (WAIS scale mais scores), although these scores seem highly correlated, challenging the traditional “crowding effect” hypothesis, which posits detrimental competition when multiple functions reorganize within the same hemisphere. Instead, our findings suggest that strong homotopic reorganization may play a positive role, while long-term developmental and social factors may modulate cognitive outcomes. However, these results remain insufficient to fully refute crowding, more targeted studies are still to be performed.

Calculation tasks did not yield significant group-level activation in the two patient subgroups separately, but consistent clusters emerged when pooling all patients. These clusters localized primarily within the language-dominant hemisphere, notably in the intraparietal sulcus, suggest spatial covariance between language and calculation networks. A persistent activation in the left postero-inferior temporal gyrus across groups suggested that not all components of the calculation network realign with language reorganization. Overall, calculation-related activation was weaker and more variable in patients than in controls, likely reflecting cognitive heterogeneity or reduced task compliance.

Collectively, these findings do not provide evidence for neural cortical competition underlying a crowding effect. Instead, they suggest that atypical right-hemisphere language dominance can support, and sometimes enhance cognitive and linguistic outcomes following early left-hemisphere injury. Conjunction analyses support these findings, showing a high hemispheric integration of language and calculation tasks, consistent with a shared computational–linguistic architecture that reorganizes coherently after early lesions.

Nevertheless, the present analyses remain insufficient to definitively rule out crowding, and future work should include lateralization measures for non-language tasks, overlap analyses across language and calculation networks, connectivity analyses and resting-state investigations to reduce task-compliance confounds.
