Introduction

Sedative music and white noise both serve as a non-pharmacological intervention for insomnia symptoms or sleep deficiency.1,2,3 However, beyond lowering the sleep onset latency, does the music listening before bedtime change the follow-up sleep architecture. Compared with the effect of listening to the white noise, we collected the resting-state EEG signal with auditory stimulation before sleeping, and then analyzed the power spectral density (PSD) to assess the spectral slope (1/f) with aperiodic analysis,4 which was a marker of neural excitation-inhibition balance.5 This study aimed to: (1) characterize aperiodic slope dynamics during auditory stimulation, NREM and REM sleep; (2) differentiate the aperiodic signatures of music intervention versus white noise; and (3) correlate these dynamics with musical sophistication, insomnia severity.

Methods

We recruited 31 adults (17 females, 14 males; aged 29.90 ± 6.47 years) to experience two sleep sessions on different days receiving 30 minutes of auditory stimulation: sedative music,6 or white noise. (Figure 1.) Each session was separated by a minimum one-day washout interval. Prior to the experiment, participants completed 7-day sleep diaries, Goldsmiths Musical Sophistication Index (Gold-MSI) and Insomnia Severity Index (ISI). Experiment schedules were aligned with participants’ sleep routines with control of room temperature and dim light. Sleep scoring was carried out using YASA Python code with C4 channel.7 Aperiodic dynamics were extracted from resting epochs and sleep stages using the FOOOF algorithm (R2 ≥ 0.8) in Python.4 Statistically, two-way repeated-measures ANOVA and paired t-test with significance level of 0.05 were performed to evaluate the differences between the music and white noise. Spearman correlations were measured to examine relationships between spectral slope, ISI, and Gold-MSI. For sleep analysis, we targeted the N3 stage due to its established link with maximal neural inhibition and the highest spectral slope.8

Results

Filtering process of R2 threshold in the aperiodic analysis yielded valid datasets for the resting state (n = 20) and sleep stages (N1: n = 17; N2: n = 26; N3: n = 23; REM: n = 18). Resting-state aperiodic slopes exhibited insignificant main effects for Music (F(1,19) = 1.89, p = 0.19), or Time (F(1,19) = 0.27, p = 0.61), nor Interaction (F(1,19) = 0.94, p = 0.35). In sleep, we found marginal significance in N3 (t = -1.88, p = 0.07) sleep, while REM sleep showed a nominal trend (t=2.50, p=0.02). (Figure 1.) Furthermore, Spearman correlation analyses yielded no significant associations between aperiodic slope dynamics and either subjective traits ISI, Gold-MSI. Nevertheless, the music listening exhibited more pronounced slope changes in proportion to the Gold-MSI than white noise (r = 0.32 vs. -0.04; Fig. 2A) and a negative correlation with insomnia severity (r = -0.21 vs. -0.08). In N3 sleep, a positive association between the spectral slope with musical sophistication (r =0.18 vs. -0.03; Fig. 2B) and a negative association with insomnia severity (r = -0.10 vs. -0.05). Additionally, a consistent negative correlation was found between musical sophistication and insomnia across both music (r = -0.26) and noise (r = -0.23) conditions.

Conclusion

Even though most findings were insignificant, the results in REM sleep suggests that music induces a shift toward enhanced neural inhibition. Furthermore, the correlations suggest that higher musical sophistication and lower insomnia severity may be associated with a steeper aperiodic slope, reflecting increased inhibition during music intervention. This implies that the efficacy of music-facilitated sleep may rely on individual neural receptivity, where the brain's excitation-inhibition balance can be modulated by one's musical traits and sleep health.
