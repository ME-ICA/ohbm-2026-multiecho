Introduction

Efficient cognitive functioning relies on the brain’s ability to coordinate neural activity across distributed regions. Neural phase synchronization supports this process by regulating timing relationships among oscillatory signals, enabling dynamic integration and segregation of information across large-scale networks [1]. Most prior synchronization studies have emphasized static properties, such as average synchrony strength or stable network topology, assuming that stronger synchrony reflects more efficient neural communication.

However, many complex systems, including the human brain, do not converge on a single stable coordination pattern but instead exhibit metastable dynamics, transiently dwelling near particular network organizations before transitioning to others [2,3]. From this perspective, temporal variability in synchronization is a fundamental feature of neural coordination, yet EEG research has predominantly focused on static features of synchrony, while the temporal dynamics underlying ongoing state transitions remain less explored.

Metastable dynamics are thought to support a balance between stability and flexibility in brain networks, with the degree of this balance varying across individuals. Such variability reflects diverse cognitive styles and neural strategies and can be considered within a broader framework of neurocognitive diversity. Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) represents one dimension of this diversity, characterized by distinct patterns of information processing and network organization. However, how metastable phase synchronization relates to individual differences in autistic traits remains unclear.

To address this gap, we examined whether metastable synchronization dynamics of resting-state EEG reflect autistic traits assessed using the Autism-Spectrum Quotient (AQ) and the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule, Second Edition (ADOS-2), using complementary measures of network-level metastability.

Methods

We analyzed EEG data from 35 adults with ASD (38.1±11.1 years old, 28 males) and 28 neurotypical adults (30.1±11.2 years old, 19 males) during 3 minutes of eyes-closed resting state. Autism-Spectrum Quotient （AQ） and the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule-Second Edition (ADOS-2) were assessed for each individual [4,5].

The distribution of AQ scores did not significantly deviate from normality (W = 0.97, p = 0.24), whereas ADOS-2 scores exhibited a significant deviation from normality (W = 0.95, p = 0.04). Given that some key variables did not satisfy the normality assumption, all subsequent statistical analyses were performed using non-parametric methods.

EEG was recorded from 64 scalp electrodes, band-pass filtered at 1–50 Hz, and preprocessed using independent component analysis for artifact removal, current source density transformation to reduce volume conduction effects, and the Gabor wavelet transform to extract instantaneous phase at 1 Hz resolution. 

We computed the instantaneous phase difference across multiple brain areas, which indicates whether these brain regions are momentarily synchronized. To quantify the temporal dynamics of large-scale coordination in the brain, we used two complementary metrics of metastability: MSI reflects how strongly the entire brain network switches between periods of high and low synchronization. High MSI indicates greater variability in coordinating information across widespread regions. SCE was calculated from pairwise patterns of phase difference over time, indicating the diversity of transient synchrony coalition — i.e., how many different spatial synchrony patterns the brain explores. In other words, SCE reflects spatially structured network diversity in network coordination.

Together, MSI and SCE capture global variability and spatial diversity in network aspects of metastability. ASD measures—neural associations were evaluated by cluster-based permutation testing for Spearman correlations.

Results

First, for comparing metastability measures between the ASD and typically developing (TD) group, nonparametric Mann–Whitney U tests revealed group differences in metastability dynamics: MSI in the ASD group showed significantly lower global metastability at 42–44 Hz (p < .05), which suggests reduced high-frequency large-scale variability in ASD. SCE in the ASD group exhibited lower spatial coalition diversity at a wide range (4, 7–9, 17–21, and 26–48 Hz, p < .05),  suggesting widespread alterations in metastable coalition diversity across from theta to gamma bands in ASD. These findings indicate that atypical metastable network dynamics are present in ASD even during rest, reflecting reduced flexibility in both global and spatially structured neural coordination.

Second, we found frequency-specific associations between metastability measures and autistic traits in integrated data for ASD and TD. MSI, a global measure of whole-brain synchronization variability, showed negative correlations with the AQ subscore “Attention Switching” at the high-beta to gamma range (25–50 Hz, cluster-t = -80.58, p = 0.004) and “Attention to Detail” at the gamma (29–50 Hz, cluster-t = -61.25, p = 0.01). These findings suggest that excessive stability and reduced switching in global network coordination are related to attentional behavioral characteristics. 

Finally, SCE in integrated data for ASD and TD, indexing the diversity of transient spatial coalitions, exhibited dissociable neural signatures across traits. AQ “Attention Switching” showed a significant negative correlation with SCE in widespread front-parieto-occipital networks at beta (18–24 Hz, cluster-t = -610.6, p = 0.0006), indicating reduced flexibility in high-frequency network organization for individuals with greater attentional inflexibility. AQ “Communication” also showed a significant negative correlation with SCE in the front-parietal networks at beta and gamma (21–49 Hz, cluster-t = -1623.154), which indicated the frontal lobe is working at full capacity to perform communication functions.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that metastable dynamics of EEG phase synchronization networks robustly reflect autistic tendencies across both group differences (ASD vs. TD) and individual differences in autistic traits. We showed that adults with ASD exhibited reduced metastability in the gamma band for global synchronization (MSI) and widespread reductions in network synchrony coalition diversity across theta-to-gamma bands for SCE. These findings indicate that atypical fluctuations in large-scale coordination are intrinsic properties of the autistic brain even during resting-state conditions.

Beyond group-level abnormalities, specific autistic traits were linked to distinct metastability components. Negative correlations between MSI and Attention Switching / Attention to Detail in high-beta and gamma frequencies suggest that reduced global attractor switching reflects rigidity and a narrowed attentional focus. Conversely, decreased SCE associated with poorer attentional switching indicates a lack of flexible reorganization in spatial coalitions, pointing to stability-biased neural dynamics underlying attentional inflexibility. Together, these results suggest that the balance between stability and flexibility — a hallmark of metastable coordination — is disrupted differently for each cognitive dimension of autistic traits.

These observations support theoretical models proposing that ASD is characterized by a shift from flexible neural configuration toward over-stabilized, less exploratory dynamics, limiting transitions between synchronized states. Crucially, MSI and SCE captured complementary aspects of metastability—global variability versus spatially distributed diversity—highlighting the multidimensional nature of neural coordination that underlies cognitive flexibility and rigidity.

Overall, quantifying spontaneous metastable dynamics offers a mechanistic framework to explain how moment-to-moment coordination of neural networks—if biased away from optimal balance—can lead to the behavioral rigidity observed in autism spectrum conditions.
