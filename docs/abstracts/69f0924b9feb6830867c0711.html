Introduction

The human visual system is organized into multiple retinotopically mapped cortical areas whose functional properties are well established. However, the structural connectivity that links these maps remains partially understood. Long-range axons interconnecting visual areas support perception, yet no standardized method exists for reconstructing these connections at retinotopic resolution in vivo. As a result, we know the functional organization of the early visual cortex in detail, but lack a corresponding structural model of how visual maps communicate.

Diffusion MRI (dMRI) tractography is the only noninvasive tool capable of estimating the trajectories of white-matter pathways in living humans. While widely used to identify major tracts and area-to-area connections, conventional tractography does not resolve connectivity as a function of polar angle or eccentricity. Previous attempts required bespoke pipelines available only in specialized labs and were restricted to a limited set of tract classes. Consequently, foundational principles of visual connectivity, such as “like-to-like” wiring between homologous retinotopic locations, have remained largely untested in humans.

Here, we introduce VISCONN-T (VISion CONNectivity Toolbox), an open-source, containerized pipeline for mapping retinotopic white-matter connectivity across 12 early and mid-level visual areas. VISCONN-T merges population-receptive-field-based retinotopic maps (pRFs) with anatomically constrained diffusion tractography, enabling the estimation of streamline connections between iso-eccentric polar-angle locations across visual areas. We benchmarked the pipeline across 3 datasets and over 1,800 participants spanning ages 2–88. Results demonstrate the ability to estimate fundamental organizational principles of the human visual connectome, including evidence for like-to-like connectivity1 and structural correlates of perceptual field asymmetries2.

Methods

VISCONN-T integrates established MRI processing tools (MRtrix3, FSL, FreeSurfer) within a modular pipeline deployed on brainlife.io4. pRF models are estimated from structural MRI using automated retinotopic mapping methods, yielding polar-angle and eccentricity maps across 12 visual areas (V1–V3, V3A/B, hV4, LO1/2, TO1/2, VO1/2)3. Continuous retinotopic maps are discretized into iso-eccentric bins (0–2°, 2–4°, 4–6°, 6–8°, 8–90°) and canonical polar-angle meridians (horizontal, upper and lower vertical, diagonals) to create retinotopic parcels. Data were analyzed from three large-scale neuroimaging cohorts spanning the human lifespan: the Human Connectome Project (HCP; n = 1,061), the Cambridge Centre for Ageing and Neuroscience (Cam-CAN; n = 585), and the Pediatric Imaging, Neurocognition, and Genetics study (PING; n = 106).

For all experiments, diffusion MRI preprocessing included denoising, Gibbs-ringing correction, susceptibility and motion correction (FSL topup/eddy), bias correction (ANTs N4), and registration to T1w images (ANTs). Constrained spherical deconvolution was used to estimate fiber orientation distributions. Whole-brain tractograms were generated using a combination of probabilistic, particle filtering, and deterministic tractography, then filtered using anatomical priors (i.e., anatomically constrained tractography) and geometric criteria to reduce false positives.

Streamline endpoints were intersected with retinotopic parcels to compute connectivity density (streamline count normalized by the mean volume of connected regions) between visual-field segments. Short-range connections were defined as streamlines with both endpoints within retinotopic parcels; long-range connections had one endpoint within the visual cortex and one outside. Statistical analyses included repeated-measures ANOVA and post-hoc t-tests to quantify horizontal–vertical asymmetry (HVA) and vertical-meridian asymmetry (VMA). 

We extended these analyses to examine lifespan trajectories of short-range visual connections using data from HCP, Cam-CAN, and PING by computing normalized meridian asymmetry indices across age. Specifically, we quantified the horizontal–vertical asymmetry (HVA = 100 × (SDHM − SDVM) / mean(SDHM, SDVM)) and the vertical-meridian asymmetry (VMA = 100 × (SDLVM − SDUVM) / mean(SDLVM, SDUVM)), where SD denotes streamline density. We further assessed the potential influence of cortical folding on tractography-derived connectivity by explicitly evaluating gyral-bias effects, examining how local curvature relates to streamline termination patterns across retinotopically defined regions. 

Complementary to these polar-angle-based analysis, we quantified like-to-like retinotopic alignment between visual areas along the eccentricity dimension. Streamline density was computed between all pairs of eccentricity bins for each area pair, yielding eccentricity-by-eccentricity connectivity matrices. Matrix elements represent streamline density between a specific eccentricity segment in a source area and a specific eccentricity segment in a target area. Like-to-like connectivity was defined as matrix elements along the main diagonal, corresponding to homologous eccentricity bins. To assess how connectivity varies with retinotopic mismatch, off-diagonal elements were grouped by absolute eccentricity difference (in degrees of visual angle, DVA), and streamline densities were averaged within each group.

To provide a normative population-level reference for these analyses, we generated a retinotopic connectivity atlas by aggregating streamline densities across 1,061 participants.

Results

VISCONN-T reliably reconstructed retinotopic connectivity across early and mid-level visual areas. Streamline density scaled with segment size while maintaining proportional connectivity across eccentricity bins, validating the pipeline’s anatomical specificity. 

Across 1,800 participants, we observed robust like-to-like connectivity¹: streamlines preferentially linked homologous retinotopic locations between visual areas. Eccentricity-by-eccentricity connectivity matrices revealed maximal streamline density along the diagonal, corresponding to homologous eccentricity bins, with a graded decrease as retinotopic mismatch increased (Fig. 2a). Connectivity was strongest for same-eccentricity and adjacent bins (±1 DVA) and declined sharply beyond ±2 DVA, approaching minimal values for separations ≥6 DVA (Fig. 2a). This organizational principle, previously reported only in invasive rodent studies, was robust across all three human datasets and extended beyond V1–V2 connections. Eccentricity-by-eccentricity connectivity matrices between V2 and V3 (Fig. 2b) exhibited a pronounced diagonal structure, indicating preferential coupling between homologous eccentricity representations. Compared with V1–V2, V2–V3 connectivity showed a broader diagonal band and relatively elevated coupling between neighboring eccentricities, consistent with increased integration across retinotopic space while preserving specificity across the early visual hierarchy.

Connectivity patterns mirrored well-established perceptual asymmetries of the human visual field2. Visual performance is known to be superior along the horizontal meridian relative to the vertical meridians (horizontal–vertical asymmetry, HVA), and superior along the lower relative to the upper vertical meridian (vertical-meridian asymmetry, VMA). Consistent with these behavioral effects, analyses in 1,061 subjects of the HCP dataset revealed significantly greater streamline density along the horizontal meridian than along the vertical meridians across all three datasets (HVA; p < 0.001), and greater along the lower compared to the upper vertical meridian (VMA; p < 0.001) (Fig. 2c). The magnitude of these asymmetries varied systematically across visual areas, being strongest in early visual cortex (V1–V3) and progressively attenuated in later visual areas. Importantly, these effects were robust to normalization for local gyral curvature, indicating that they are not driven by tractography-related gyral bias. Notably, meridional asymmetries were observed exclusively in short-range connections and were absent in long-range connections, suggesting that local visual circuitry plays a primary role in shaping functional HVA and VMA (Fig. 2d). Critically, these patterns were replicated in the Cam-CAN and PING datasets, demonstrating robustness across independent cohorts and age ranges (Fig. 2e).

Lifespan analyses demonstrated divergent age-related trajectories. VMA showed minimal expression in childhood, increased markedly during adolescence, plateaued in adulthood, and declined with aging. HVA followed an opposite path, exhibiting its greatest magnitude in childhood, decreasing steadily across the lifespan, and exhibiting a slight increase in older adulthood (Fig. 2f). These structural trends align with behavioral reports of visual field asymmetries5.

Finally, we generated a retinotopic structural connectivity atlas summarizing population-level visual pathways, including eccentricity- and polar-angle–specific connectivity maps across V1–hV4, LO/TO complexes, and V3A/B. This atlas provides a normative reference for future studies and supports individualized connectome profiling.

Conclusion

We present a scalable pipeline for mapping the human retinotopic visual connectome in vivo. VISCONN-T unifies diffusion MRI tractography with pRF-based segmentation, enabling the reconstruction of white-matter pathways as a function of polar angle and eccentricity across multiple visual areas.

By revealing like-to-like connectivity between homologous retinotopic locations and structural correlates of perceptual field asymmetries, this work establishes fundamental principles of human visual white-matter organization that were previously inaccessible using noninvasive techniques. The dissociation between short- and long-range connectivity asymmetries further suggests that local circuitry plays a central role in shaping functional visual field biases.

VISCONN-T is openly released as a reproducible, containerized workflow together with a population-derived atlas spanning childhood to late adulthood. This resource enables systematic investigation of visual network development, aging, and disease, and provides a foundation for linking individual differences in white-matter architecture to visual behavior and clinical outcomes. Code, documentation, and a fully reproducible end-to-end pipeline are openly shared to support future investigations into the structural foundations of human vision.
