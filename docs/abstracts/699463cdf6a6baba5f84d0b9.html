Introduction

FAIR data principles are considered the gold standard for ensuring that data is findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable. The principles have been widely promoted as a means to democratize research in domains such as neuroscience.1 However, these principles were originally developed in the Global North. As of today, no empirical studies have evaluated the FAIRness of tools and datasets across low-, medium-, and high-resource settings. For example, previous work on the availability of data has concentrated on datasets from high-resource settings,2 and focus has been mainly on their existence rather than on their real-world accessibility. Additionally, previous work has examined the capability and consistency of researcher workflows,3 but it has not assessed global variability or the influence of resource settings on these capabilities. Our project seeks to address this gap by examining the accessibility and usability of neuroimaging data across regions with markedly varying economic conditions, with particular attention to global disparities in technical expertise, training opportunities, and the infrastructural support available to researchers. 

Methods

In study 1, a single research group in North America attempted to access a large number of datasets from 6 different global regions. Our objective was to 1) measure access, and 2) develop a guide to accessing global neuroimaging datasets from low-, middle-, and high-resource settings. Datasets were identified through the researcher's prior knowledge, scientific networks, published review articles, and broad literature searches. Datasets had to have at least one neuroimaging modality and have a basic or clinical application to mental health research to be included in study 1. We evaluated measures of data governance for datasets and documented barriers we encountered while attempting to access each dataset.

In study 2, we recruited a diverse group of researchers to participate in a volunteer-based research study where they utilized neuroimaging data. Our objective was to understand the unique challenges researchers from low-, middle-, and high-resource regions face when utilizing neuroimaging data. We recruited volunteers through conferences and scientific networks, and minimal training in neuroimaging was required so that we could examine practical barriers to neuroimaging research capacity. We recruited over 100 volunteers from 34 countries, balanced across resource settings, and assigned two neuroimaging datasets from diverse regions to each volunteer and distributed research questions. Volunteers were mostly early-career researchers, such as graduate students and postdoctoral fellows. Volunteers reported their experiences accessing and analyzing the data, allowing us to examine differences in researcher experiences and barriers between indicators of resource-setting, including the country's Human Development Index and World Bank Income Classification.

Results

For study 1, we identified 49 datasets from 6 global regions. The datasets represent a broad mix of neurological, psychiatric, developmental, and healthy-control populations, along with some infection- or substance-related cohorts. We characterized every dataset, including information about licensing (27 datasets had a CC license), data use agreements (26 datasets had a data use agreement), and access level (ranging from open access to private), so we could understand not only what data exists, but how easily and fairly they can be used. While attempting to access each dataset, we also documented challenges we encountered. Challenges ranged widely, but some of the most common ones included: 1) requiring a full research proposal, 2) lack of email response, and 3) lack of data access instructions. We then took these challenges and conducted a qualitative analysis to identify categories: administrative, legal, technical, communication, and ethical challenges. When looking at the frequency of these challenges across these categories, we saw that administrative challenges were most common, followed by legal, then technical, communication, and ethical. 

For study 2, the work is still ongoing. 52 volunteers have begun accessing and working with their assigned datasets. We will use text-embedding models and large language models to transform open-ended volunteer reports on their experience accessing, downloading, preprocessing, and analyzing international neuroimaging data into numerical representations. From these representations, we will derive a volunteer capacity score that summarizes the researcher’s capacity. We then use correlation analyses to relate this score to our indicators of resource-setting, such as the Human Development Index and the World Bank Income Classification. We anticipate that results will reveal pronounced differences in capacity across resource settings, which could highlight critical inequities in the global neuroscience ecosystem and provide a roadmap for more inclusive and representative research.

Conclusion

By attempting to access a large number of datasets from around the world and documenting both their access frameworks and the challenges we encountered in study 1, we hope to provide a more open and global guide for accessing neuroimaging data, one that supports fair use by a diverse community of neuroimaging researchers. For Study 2, we believe that by capturing the real-world experiences of researchers as they attempt to access and use data from around the world, this work can highlight systemic disparities in research capacity across countries with different resource settings, underscoring that FAIR in principle does not always translate to FAIR in practice. We believe this work could help guide and inform future open-science policy, shed light on the need for training programs and global capacity building, and highlight the importance of expanded infrastructure and resource support around the world.
