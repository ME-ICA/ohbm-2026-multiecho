Introduction

Human cortical-dominated functional network reorganization and interaction are considered key mechanisms for the emergence and maturation of complex human cognition1. Yet, the thalamus, as a bridge for cortical and subcortical communication and a core driver of neural development2, its crucial role has been less understood.    

    Recently, both human and animal model studies have gradually discovered that the dorsal and occipital regions of the thalamus broadly support various cognitive functions such as executive control, attention, memory, and reward processing3, with different brain regions receiving input from specific thalamic neuron groups4. However, to date, most researchers have relied on single basic cognitive paradigms, which in many ways limits our understanding of how different thalamic subregions precisely encode and support various emotional-cognitive functions in humans. More importantly, how this mechanism manifests in higher-order human emotions remains unclear.

    From a developmental perspective, previous studies have found that the thalamus accelerates synapse formation and shapes functional specificity through the transmission and interaction of sensory stimuli5. However, how different thalamic nuclei collaborate with the brain to drive the maturation and differentiation of early cognitive functions in multi-dimensional emotional-cognitive processing remains an open question. 

    To address these two core scientific issues, this study is based on a newly recruited large cohort of children and adolescents (824 participants), each performing a multi-task paradigm composed of four classic cognitive functions. It covers two major areas previously focused on in research: working memory and attention, as well as expanding to two less explored cognitive functions—emotion and incentive. Ultimately, we selected four tasks: N-back, ANT, negative emotion face matching, and BART.

Methods

At the activation level, we first performed voxel-based multivariate modeling and used Partial Least Squares Regression (PLS-R) to build both common and task-specific predictive models of behavioral performance from thalamic activation across four cognitive domains (Fig. 1a & b). We mapped the voxel-level beta coefficients to 15 thalamic subregions and reported the percentage of voxels in each thalamic subregion that significantly predicted task performance, after correction. We used the latest human thalamus template (AAL3) with 15 fine subdivisions. We further validated the subregions of the thalamus that support generalized cognitive abilities by constructing the similarity between behavioral representation patterns and neural standard patterns at the group level (Inter-subject RSA). This approach allows us to incorporate a wider range of behavioral measures. 

     Given the brain's continuous adaptation to various tasks through flexible reorganization and coordination patterns, we quantified neural flexibility (NF)  by integrating the differences in functional connectivity (FC) patterns between thalamic subregions and the whole brain across four different domains (cross-domain) or across different subdimensions within a domain (within-domain) (Fig. 1e). At the whole-brain level, we defined the 7 cortical networks and 5 subcortical nuclei as target brain ROIs from Tian'template. Given that we found thalamic NF exhibited a gradient distribution along the anterior-posterior axis, we subsequently performed repeated measures ANOVA and conducted a correlation analysis between the ranks of NF and the ranks of topological thalamic subregions to validate this finding. 

     To examine developmental effects, we employed a Generalized Additive Model (GAM) implemented in the R package "mgcv" (Fig. 1f), and plotted the trajectory of thalamic neural flexibility (NF) development across age. To further validate the relationship between NF and cognitive function, we calculated the Pearson correlation between within-domain NF for each task and behavioral performance, while controlling for covariates such as gender, age, and number of acquisitions (Fig. 1d).

    Finally, we investigated how thalamocortical connectivity shapes NF by performing dynamic functional connectivity (FC) analysis with a sliding window approach and K-means clustering, which revealed two meta-states: integration and separation (Fig. 1d). Building on this, we explored the potential neurotransmitter basis of dynamic connectivity's influence on NF by relating FC maps to neurotransmitter maps from public databases. To more precisely characterize thalamocortical connectivity patterns, we used the Human Brainnetome Atlas template (200 regions) for cortical analysis.

Results

Understanding how humans achieve multiple emotional and cognitive functions within a limited structure, and how these mechanisms develop, is a key question in neuroscience. Current mainstream theories emphasize adaptive reorganization mechanisms driven by the frontal-parietal network and the neocortex. This study, however, reveals the critical role of the thalamus as a hub connecting the cortex and subcortical nuclei, and as a core driver of neural development, in supporting the emergence and development of various cognitive functions in humans.

    Through a multi-task paradigm, we investigated the activation of different thalamic subregions and their functional connectivity (FC) reorganization patterns supporting multi-domain cognitive functions, regional heterogeneity, developmental effects, and shaping mechanisms in a large human sample (N=824). The results show that, in classic cognitive functions, the thalamus exhibits a gradient effect along the anterior-posterior axis (Fig. 2a), with increasing generalization and flexibility (NF) in both task performance across cognitive domains and whole-brain adaptive reorganization. PLS-R and RSA analyses indicate that the posterior thalamus serves as the core region supporting generalized multi-domain emotional-cognitive functions, while the anterior thalamus supports higher cognitive functions in a more specific manner.

    In terms of dynamic thalamocortical connectivity, NF continues to develop and differentiate during childhood and adolescence (Fig. 2e), with NF in tasks such as BART and ANT strongly correlating with behavioral performance (Fig. 2b). This suggests that the development of the thalamus and its dynamic reorganization with the whole brain in response to different demands facilitates the maturation of diverse cognitive functions. Further analysis revealed differential effects of thalamic connectivity with the cortex and subcortical regions on NF: thalamocortical and thalamo-basal ganglia connectivity were negatively correlated with NF, while thalamic connectivity with other subcortical nuclei was positively correlated with NF (Fig. 2c). We further elucidated that this heterogeneity is closely related to its underlying neurotransmitter basis (Fig. 2d).

    In conclusion, this study not only reveals the gradient-supported heterogeneous distribution of cognitive functions across thalamic subregions but also emphasizes how its cooperation with the whole-brain network drives the differentiation and maturation of multi-level emotional and cognitive functions in humans.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study reveals how different thalamic subregions support diverse cognitive functions through an anterior-specific, posterior-generalized encoding strategy. The pulvinar, along with the traditionally recognized primary sensory nuclei (LGN, MGN) and the emerging RE region, plays a key role in the flexible cooperation with the whole brain, facilitating the maturation and differentiation of multi-domain cognitive functions in humans. We further uncovered the driving mechanisms of specific connectivity patterns, neurotransmitters in this process. Our findings provide a new perspective on the fine-grained functional architecture and organizational principles of the thalamus in human complex emotional-cognitive processing, with important implications for future advancements and simulations of human intelligence.
