Introduction

The field of neuroscience requires understanding how brain structure affects brain function. Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) structural connectomes assess anatomical relationships, while functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) functional connectomes record coordinated brain activity. Although these two modalities are related, evaluating their coupling is challenging due to their high dimensionality and distinct regional organization. Most existing methods rely on edge-wise correlations between structural and functional connectivity matrices, which may miss latent linkages contained in their shared network
geometry1. Recent research suggests that structure-function interaction is better understood through the use of multivariate models2. To capture regionally heterogeneous patterns within the connectomes, the authors3 proposed an eigen decomposition-based approach. In this work, we present a spectrum-driven framework for characterizing structure-function coupling via joint network modes shared by structural and functional connectomes. This method produces a subject-level coupling index that reflects the proportion of structural network components matched with functional organization, resulting in an interpretable and global measure of multimodal brain network coupling.

Methods

Each subject’s structural and functional brain networks were created using DTI and fMRI. Structural connectomes reflected inter-regional white-matter connections, whereas functional connectomes collected statistical relationships between regional BOLD time series. To measure structure-function coupling beyond traditional edge-wise correlations, we performed joint diagonalization4. We used a joint spectral decomposition framework that detects shared network modes across both modalities. This approach extracts a common set of eigenmodes that describe both structural and functional connectivity patterns, enabling the separation of functionally connected and decoupled components within the structural network. Joint modes were rated according to their functional relevance, and a subset representing strongly coupled components was determined using a data-driven criterion. A subject-level structure-function coupling index (SFCI) was then calculated to reflect the relative importance of these connected structural components. The approach was tested on a group of healthy persons who had matching DTI and fMRI connectomes.

Results

Functionally connected and decoupled structural network components were clearly separated by joint spectral decomposition. Joint eigenvalues clustered into two groups across participants, showing that only a subset of structural network modes contributes to functional organization. Functionally important joint eigenvalues distinguish coupled and decoupled components in Fig. 1. Visualization of the dominant joint mode showed spatially coherent patterns aligned with functional brain systems, validating the framework’s biological plausibility (Fig. 1b). Reconstructing the structural connectome using only functionally related modes preserved major network features and suppressed weakly relevant components. A sample of 30 subjects5 yielded consistent results for the SFCI, a summary of subject-level coupling strength. Structure–function relationships may be governed by a low-dimensional common subspace rather than uniform edge-wise correspondence.

Conclusion

We propose a spectral approach for assessing structure-function coupling that goes beyond edge-wise correlation to discover shared network modes between structural and functional connectomes. The proposed structure-function coupling index is an accessible subject-level measure of multimodal brain network alignment. This technique, which isolates functionally significant structural components, provides a systematic foundation for researching individual variability and has a high potential for application to neurological and psychiatric illnesses.
