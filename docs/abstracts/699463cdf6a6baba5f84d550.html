Introduction

Creativity is central to innovation and cultural progress, yet its neurobiological basis remains fragmented due to small samples, heterogeneous creativity assessments, and a focus on isolated regions or single imaging modalities1. Converging theories2 propose that individual creativity is multi-component, spanning creative cognition (e.g., divergent thinking), creative personality (e.g., openness, curiosity, creative self-efficacy), and behavior/achievement (real-life creative activities and accomplishments). A key challenge is to place these components within a common, brain-wide organizational framework. 

Here, we test whether human brain connectome gradients3, which capture the principal low-dimensional patterns of human brain organization, can provide such a framework for the full domains of individual creativity. We derive gradients from (i) resting-state functional connectivity (FC), (ii) morphometric similarity networks (MIND)4 capturing coordinated variation in cortical morphology, and (iii) a fused FC–MIND connectome integrating structural and functional information. We ask three questions: (1) Do multimodal gradients show stronger biological grounding by aligning with independent maps of gene expression, intracortical myelination, and neurotransmitter receptor/transporter distributions? (2) Do unimodal versus fused gradients differentially embed individual differences in creative cognition, personality, and behavior across large-scale cortical systems? (3) Do gradient-based regional features predict these creativity dimensions and generalize to an independent cohort? By combining large samples, data-driven creativity dimensions, and multimodal gradient mapping, this work aims to provide an interpretable coordinate system linking creativity to cortical hierarchies across molecular, microstructural, and systems-level organization.

Methods

We analyzed a large discovery cohort of healthy young adults (n=1367, age=21.15 ± 1.00 years) and an independent age-matched validation cohort scanned at the same site (n=553, age=19.27 ± 1.56 years). Individual creativity was measured with 12 indices spanning divergent thinking tasks (e.g., Alternate Uses Task), personality scales (e.g., Williams Creativity Aptitude Scale), and self-reported creative activities across the arts and sciences (e.g., Creative Achievement Questionnaire). To obtain data-driven creativity dimensions, we constructed a behavioral association network across all creativity indicators and applied graphical LASSO followed by Louvain community detection and consensus clustering. This yielded three communities corresponding to cognition, personality, and behavior (Fig.1), and the first principal component (PC1) score within each community served as the sub-domain score. 

For neuroimaging data, we parcellated the cortex using the Schaefer-1000 atlas and computed: (1) resting-state FC matrices; (2) MIND using five cortical features (thickness, surface area, gray matter volume, curvature, sulcal depth); and (3) a multimodal fused connectome (FC–MIND) using Similarity Network Fusion (SNF)5. For each connectome, we derived the first three gradients using diffusion map embedding and aligned individual gradients via Procrustes rotation. To interpret what the gradients represent biologically, we related group-level gradients (first three) to reference cortical maps of gene-expression PC1 (Allen Human Brain Atlas), intracortical myelination (HCP T1w/T2w), and PET-derived receptor/transporter maps, using spin permutations and FDR correction.

To link gradients to creativity, we quantified global and network-level gradient metrics, including explained variance ratio, range, variability, and dispersion, and related them to creativity sub-domain PC1 scores, as well as predicted creativity sub-domain scores from regional gradient values using linear regression models with 50-fold cross-validation and 1,000 times permutation test. 

Results

Across modalities, the first three gradients explained 73% (FC), 81% (MIND), and 67% (FC–MIND) of connectome variance. Fused gradients recapitulated the unimodal–transmodal hierarchy and additional control/attention axes, and showed significant correspondence with gene-expression PC1, intracortical myelination, and multiple PET neurotransmitter maps (Fig.2). Brain–behavior analyses showed domain-selective patterns. Unimodal FC gradients are mainly related to creative behavior, whereas MIND gradients showed associations with personality and behavior, with prominent effects in the limbic network, and unimodal global metrics showed no robust links to creative cognition. In contrast, fused FC–MIND gradients showed broader associations with behavior, personality, and cognition across multiple large-scale networks. Moreover, regional fused-gradient scores yielded small but reliable predictions for all three creativity sub-domains (Fig.2), outperforming unimodal gradients. Lastly but not least, key gradient motifs and predictive patterns were generalized to the validation cohort.

Conclusion

By integrating FC and MIND, fused FC–MIND gradients provide a whole-brain, biologically anchored coordinate system that links creativity to cortical organization across molecular, microstructural, and neurochemical axes. Relative to unimodal gradients, multimodal gradients more consistently map and modestly predict individual differences in creativity, especially real-life creative behavior, with generalization to an independent cohort.
