Introduction

Understanding early brain development is essential for studying neurodevelopmental disorders, yet pediatric neuroimaging remains technically challenging for segmentation tool development. During the first two years, the brain doubles in volume and white matter undergoes significant myelination, producing MRI contrast shifts—including inversion of gray-white matter contrast.1 Cortical regions mature at different rates, creating inhomogeneous contrast even between neighboring areas, while subcortical structures also change dynamically.2 Reliable annotation requires tools that adapt to rapid development, handle motion, and integrate multiple modalities, particularly in longitudinal studies. Despite advances, pediatric segmentation tools are limited by narrow age ranges, few modalities, and nonstandard anatomical labels. Infant-specific tools are restrictive, and adult-trained tools are inaccurate in early childhood MRI. As more imaging data from the first decade of life becomes available, we need tools robust across ages and modalities or understand how current segmentation outputs compare applied together. In this study, we address the latter. We analyzed three publicly available pediatric and developmental MRI datasets spanning ages 0–10 years using three state-of-the-art tools: BabySeg, SynthSeg and SAMSEG. Since none cover the full age range, we focused on the 4–6-year transitional interval, where tool applicability shifts from pediatric to adult. By assessing age, modality, and longitudinal effects, we characterized tool-induced biases, highlighted strengths and limitations, and provided a framework for combining pediatric and adult tools. Our results motivate development of age-adaptive or longitudinally informed algorithms to improve coverage and reliability across development. Improving segmentation accuracy across this range is critical for studying normative and atypical brain development and for enabling robust cross-study comparisons and meta-analyses. 

Methods

We selected brain MRI images from three publicly available control data sets to fully cover the first ten years of life postnatally. The Lifespan Baby Connectome Project (BCP) includes both T1-weighted (T1w) and T2-weighted (T2w) MRI images of 500 participants ranging from birth to 5 years of age. The Calgary Preschool MRI dataset (Calgary) includes T1w MRI of 98 participants aged 2–7 years. The Lifespan Human Connectome Project–Development (HCP-D) includes MRI data from 1,350 participants aged 5–21 years, with T1w and T2w scans. From the HCP-D cohort, only the images acquired at ages 5-10 years were used (Fig. 1a). All data was obtained and used according to their data-use agreements. MRI images with severe artifacts were excluded prior to automated segmentation.  

We compared three automated state-of-the-art brain-segmentation tools: one designed particularly for infants and young children and two that were developed for adults. The pediatric tool, BabySeg integrates domain-randomized synthetic data with highly variable real infant MRIs, enabling generalization to unseen contrasts and resolutions.3 Its group-convolutional framework allows for flexible number of modalities and achieves highly accurate segmentation performance from extremely preterm neonates through five years of age. SynthSeg trains a segmentation network entirely on generative, randomized synthetic MRI scans to enforce domain-independent feature learning.4 As a result, it achieves near–supervised accuracy while generalizing robustly across diverse imaging contrasts and resolutions. Sequence Adaptive Multimodal SEGmentation (SAMSEG), a non deep-learning based tool, integrates intensity clustering with MRI-bias field correction and deformation of a mesh-based probabilistic atlas to achieve a robust whole-brain segmentation.5 This unsupervised clustering approach groups voxels of similar intensity while reliably separating tissue types and generalizing across scanner platforms and pulse sequences.  

The 4–6-year age range is a transitionary period, when the choice of optimal postprocessing tool switches from pediatric-specific to adult ones. Our longitudinal subset included 27 subjects, averaging 2.5 scans per subject: 19 Calgary subjects with T1w MRI data, and 8 BCP subjects with both T1w and T2w scans. 

We ran BabySeg on subjects from birth to 6 years of age, whereas SAMSEG and SynthSeg on subjects between 4 to 10 years of age. BabySeg was applied to images one year older than it is recommended, and SynthSeg and SAMSEG were stretched one year younger. This allowed us to gather and compare segmentation outcomes directly from all these tools. For Babyseg and SAMSEG, we applied T1w, T2w, and – when available – combined pre-registered T1w+T2w inputs. SynthSeg was run only on single-modality images, as the default version does not support combined T1+T2w segmentation. A representative example of segmentation outputs is shown in Fig. 1b.  

From each tool’s whole-brain segmentation output, we calculated the volume (mm³) of all labeled cortical and subcortical structures. We identified common labels shared across all three tools for quantitative comparison, including bilateral white matter, cortex, thalamus, caudate, hippocampus, amygdala, ventral diencephalon, lateral ventricles, cerebellum, and brainstem. We combined SynthSeg and SAMSEG cerebellar white-matter and cortical labels to match BabySeg’s single cerebellar label. Besides the overall morphological comparison, we looked at within-subject percent change in volume rather than absolute volumes within the transition zone of 4-6 years. As estimates differ across segmentation tools, our goal was to isolate tool-induced bias in longitudinal change. Within-subject percent change was computed between the earliest and latest available scans. Percent changes were classified as biologically plausible or implausible, with decreases from younger to older time points deemed implausible based on expected developmental growth. 

Results

Figure 2a displays the right–hemisphere cerebral cortical volume as a function of age-at-scan across the full 0–10-year age range. The right hemisphere was selected as a representative example, as comparable patterns were observed for all anatomical regions of interest. Across all three segmentation tools, cortical volume generally increased with age, consistent with expected developmental trajectories during early childhood. Notably, BabySeg showed a steeper increase in the first 2 years compared to SynthSeg and SAMSEG, which exhibited more gradual increases across the age range. However, despite similar overall trends, systematic differences in volume estimates were observed across tools. BabySeg consistently produced larger cortical volumes overall, whereas SynthSeg and SAMSEG yielded smaller estimates, with the greatest divergence observed between BabySeg and SAMSEG. These differences are reflected in the separation between the linear trend lines, which persist across the age range. A small number of outliers were observed; visual inspection confirmed that these occurred in regions with reduced tissue contrast. Outliers typically affected two or more segmentation methods for a given ROI, but they did not alter the overall trends. 

Figure 2b presents a zoomed-in view of the same cortical volume estimates restricted to the 4–6-year age range, corresponding to the age range where all three segmentation tools were applied. Within this shared age window, the three tools produced clearly separated volume distributions. BabySeg yielded the largest cortical volumes on average, SynthSeg produced intermediate estimates, and SAMSEG consistently generated the smallest values. This ordering was consistent across all evaluated brain regions. Across tools, a modest upward trend in cortical volume with increasing age was observed, indicating that cross-sectional developmental patterns were preserved despite differences in absolute volume estimates. 

Figure 2c summarizes within-subject percent change in right-hemisphere cortical volume for longitudinal scans acquired between 4 and 6 years of age across the BCP and Calgary datasets.  In contrast to the consistent cross-sectional trends observed in Figures 2a and 2b, longitudinal trajectories showed substantial variability across tools. Approximately 50% of within-subject intervals exhibited decreases in cortical volume, which are biologically implausible over this developmental period. This pattern was similar across all evaluated brain regions. Among the three methods, SAMSEG demonstrated the widest range of percent volume change, indicating greater inconsistency in longitudinal estimates relative to BabySeg and SynthSeg. 

Conclusion

Across tools, as expected, age-related increases in the volumes of regions of interest were consistently reproduced in our cross-sectional analyses, demonstrating that state-of-the-art segmentation methods can successfully capture broad developmental trends across early childhood (Fig.2a-b). The ability of these tools to perform well across an extended age range highlights the overall robustness of current segmentation approaches. 

However, our results demonstrate that cross-sectional agreement alone is insufficient for longitudinal developmental studies. Despite preserving expected developmental trajectories at the group level, we found substantial variability in within-subject longitudinal change, particularly during a developmental interval that lies at the boundary between pediatric and adult tool applicability (Fig2c). Approximately 50% of longitudinal intervals exhibited biologically implausible decreases in cortical volume across the 4–6-year age range, a period during which cortical growth is expected. Importantly, this pattern was consistent across brain regions, indicating a systematic limitation rather than isolated segmentation failures. 

These findings underscore a critical gap in current segmentation methods during early childhood, particularly in the transitional period between infancy- and adult-oriented tool operating ranges. While the ability to apply a single tool from birth through early adulthood is highly desirable for longitudinal studies, achieving consistent segmentation performance across this developmental span remains a major challenge.  

Differences in longitudinal behavior across tools likely reflect their underlying design and training strategies. Pediatric-trained methods, such as BabySeg, are optimized for the unique imaging characteristics of early development, including contrast variability, rapid myelination, and evolving tissue boundaries. In contrast, adult-trained tools were not designed to accommodate these developmental changes, which may contribute to their increased longitudinal variability when applied to early childhood data. 

SAMSEG exhibited the widest range of longitudinal percent volume change (Fig.2c). This increased variability is likely related to its atlas-based framework and reliance on intensity clustering, which may be particularly sensitive to contrast changes over time. Notably, this does not indicate poor performance in adult populations—where SAMSEG is well validated—but rather highlights the limitations of unsupervised, adult-trained approaches when applied to pediatric longitudinal MRI. 

Our study has several limitations. To evaluate the 4–6-year age interval, segmentation tools were applied slightly beyond their intended operating ranges, which may have contributed to increased variability in longitudinal estimates. In addition, the longitudinal cohort was relatively small and lacked direct validation against ground-truth manual segmentations. Notwithstanding, our results have important implications for future developmental and clinical neuroimaging research. Tool-induced variability and/or bias may be misinterpreted as true biological change, potentially biasing growth models or obscuring early biomarkers. Our results emphasize the need for careful tool selection based on both age range and study design, particularly for longitudinal analyses in early childhood. A single tool that could reliably segment the brain from birth through adulthood while preserving longitudinal consistency would be transformative, enabling direct cross-study comparisons, large-scale meta-analyses, and more reliable investigations of neurodevelopmental disorders and disease trajectories. Addressing this gap through improved training strategies, age-adaptive models, or longitudinally informed segmentation approaches will be essential for improving accuracy and reliability in pediatric neuroimaging.
