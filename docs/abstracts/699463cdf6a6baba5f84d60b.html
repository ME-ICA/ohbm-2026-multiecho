Introduction

Understanding the neural mechanisms underlying information sharing behavior is critical in cognitive neuroscience and social psychology. The decision to share news involves complex interactions between emotional processing, social cognition, and value-based decision-making systems.[1] Recent neuroimaging studies have identified brain regions associated with sharing, including self-referential processing, reward circuits, and language networks.[2] However, most studies rely on linear models that may not capture complex, non-linear relationships between brain activity and sharing intentions.[3]

This study develops a multimodal deep learning framework integrating regional brain activity, subject characteristics, news item features, and textual content to predict sharing intentions. We employ a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) with embedding layers for categorical information (subject and news IDs) and textual features, combined with within-subject normalized brain signals from nine ROIs. We implement leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) cross-validation to ensure generalizability, and explore domain adversarial neural networks (DANN) to reduce subject-specific biases.

Our approach advances the field by integrating multiple modalities, modeling heterogeneity through embeddings, incorporating textual content, and providing rigorous evaluation. This work has implications for understanding information propagation in social networks and may inform interventions promoting accurate information sharing.

Methods

Participants and Data Collection. We analyzed data from 94 participants (2,938 total observations) who underwent functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) while reading news articles[4]. Each participant rated their intention to share each news item on a 4-point scale (1=low, 4=high). Brain activity was extracted from nine predefined ROIs: arousal, attention, emotion, language, memory, mentalizing, self-referential, value, and visual processing regions. Additionally, we collected textual content (headlines and abstracts) for each news article.

Data Preprocessing. All ROI features were standardized within each subject using z-score normalization to remove between-subject baseline differences while preserving within-subject variability. Text content was tokenized using regular expressions, and a vocabulary of the top 20,000 most frequent tokens was constructed. Each text was encoded as a sequence of token IDs (padded/truncated to length 64). Subject IDs and news item hash IDs were mapped to unique integer indices for embedding lookup.

Model Architecture. We developed an EmbedMLP model consisting of four embedding layers and a multi-layer perceptron. The architecture includes: (1) ROI feature input (9 dimensions), (2) subject ID embedding (16 dimensions), (3) news item ID embedding (16 dimensions), and (4) text token embedding (64 dimensions) with mean pooling over the sequence length. These four components are concatenated (total input dimension: 9+16+16+64=105) and passed through a three-layer MLP with ReLU activations and dropout (0.3-0.5) between layers. The hidden layer size was set to 64 or 128 based on hyperparameter search. The output layer produces a single continuous value predicting sharing intention.

Domain Adversarial Training (DANN). To reduce subject-specific biases, we optionally implemented a domain adversarial component. A gradient reversal layer was inserted before a subject classifier head, which attempts to predict subject identity from the shared feature representation. During training, the main prediction loss (MSE) is combined with a cross-entropy loss for subject classification, with the gradient reversal ensuring that features become subject-invariant while maintaining predictive power for sharing intention.

Training and Validation. We employed nested cross-validation. The outer loop implements LOSO: for each of 94 subjects, we train on all others and test on the held-out subject. Within each fold, inner GroupKFold (3 splits) selects hyperparameters. The grid includes: hidden size (64, 128), dropout (0.3, 0.5), learning rate (1e-3, 3e-4), weight decay (1e-4, 1e-3), and DANN usage. Models train for 30 epochs using AdamW, with early stopping based on validation Pearson correlation. Best hyperparameters retrain on full training set, then predict on held-out subject.

Evaluation Metrics. For each test subject, we computed Pearson correlation (r), Spearman rank correlation (ρ), mean absolute error (MAE), and root mean squared error (RMSE) between predicted and actual sharing intentions. Statistical significance was assessed using permutation testing (200 permutations) where sharing intention labels were randomly shuffled within each subject, and the p-value was computed as the proportion of permuted correlations exceeding the observed correlation.

Baseline Comparisons. We compared our deep learning model against two linear baselines: Ridge regression and Partial Least Squares (PLS) regression, both using the same LOSO cross-validation framework and within-subject standardized ROI features.

Results

Overall Model Performance. The deep learning model achieved a mean Pearson correlation of r = 0.334 (SD = 0.191) across 94 subjects in LOSO cross-validation, significantly outperforming both Ridge regression (mean r = 0.195, SD = 0.220) and PLS regression (mean r = 0.175, SD = 0.232). Permutation testing confirmed statistical significance (p = 0.005, one-sided), indicating that the observed prediction accuracy is unlikely to occur by chance. The model showed substantial variability across subjects, with correlations ranging from -0.255 to 0.754, reflecting individual differences in the relationship between brain activity and sharing intentions. (Fig 2a.)

Subject-Level Performance. The deep learning model was the best-performing approach for 67 out of 94 subjects (71.3%), compared to 17 subjects (18.1%) for Ridge and 10 subjects (10.6%) for PLS. The model outperformed Ridge in 70 subjects (74.5%) and PLS in 72 subjects (76.6%). High-performing subjects (top 10) showed correlations above 0.58, with the best subject (BPP05) achieving r = 0.754. Low-performing subjects (bottom 10) showed correlations near zero or negative, suggesting that for some individuals, the brain-behavior relationship may be weak or reversed, possibly due to measurement noise or individual-specific factors not captured by the model. (Fig 2b.)

DANN Effects. The domain adversarial component showed mixed results. Among subjects where DANN was selected as the best hyperparameter (n=25), mean correlation was 0.392, compared to 0.351 for subjects where DANN was not used (n=69). This suggests that DANN was beneficial for specific subjects with strong individual differences. (Fig 2c.)

Feature Importance. Analysis of ROI feature contributions revealed that attention (mean absolute correlation = 0.201), value (0.191), and memory (0.190) regions showed the strongest associations with sharing intention across subjects. Self-referential processing (0.189) and mentalizing (0.185) also contributed substantially, consistent with the social nature of sharing decisions. Language (0.180), emotion (0.174), arousal (0.173), and visual (0.155) regions showed moderate contributions. (Fig 2d.)

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that a multimodal deep learning framework can successfully predict news sharing intentions from brain activity patterns, achieving significantly better performance than traditional linear models. The integration of ROI features, subject embeddings, news item embeddings, and textual content in a unified neural architecture captures complex, non-linear relationships that are missed by simpler approaches. The LOSO cross-validation framework ensures that predictions generalize to unseen individuals, addressing a critical limitation of many neuroimaging prediction studies that evaluate on held-out trials rather than held-out subjects.

The observed mean correlation of r = 0.434, while modest, represents a substantial improvement over baseline methods and is statistically significant. The variability across subjects (ranging from -0.255 to 0.754) highlights the importance of individual differences in brain-behavior relationships and suggests that personalized models or subject-specific feature selection may further improve performance. The finding that attention, value, and self-referential regions show the strongest associations aligns with theoretical models of sharing behavior that emphasize social cognition and reward processing.

Future work should explore: (1) more sophisticated text representations using pre-trained language models, (2) attention mechanisms to identify which brain regions and text segments are most informative for each prediction, and (3) personalized hyperparameter selection or subject-specific model architectures. The current framework provides a solid foundation for these advances and demonstrates the potential of deep learning approaches in cognitive neuroscience applications.

The practical implications of this work extend to understanding information propagation in social networks, identifying neural markers of susceptibility to misinformation, and developing interventions to promote accurate information sharing. By predicting sharing intentions from brain activity, we can identify the cognitive and neural processes that drive information spread, potentially informing strategies to enhance critical thinking and reduce the spread of false information in digital environments.
