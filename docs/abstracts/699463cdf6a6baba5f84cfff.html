Introduction

Social interaction is a dynamic process involving the reciprocal exchange of information between two or more individuals. It is an essential part of human life, with broad relevance to mental health and well-being. However, traditional social neuroscience research has largely focused on isolated brains, thereby overlooking the distinct dynamics of naturalistic social contexts.

Hyperscanning refers to the simultaneous recording of brain activity from two or more individuals. Compared with classical single-brain neuroimaging, it offers a unique opportunity to investigate real-time social interactions by capturing temporal information, such as neural synchrony, which helps enrich our understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying the dynamical exchange of information.

Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) is a well-established neuroimaging technique with high spatial resolution and whole-head coverage. Although first introduced in 2002, technical and practical challenges initially limited its adoption, and widespread attention did not emerge until nearly a decade later.^1 After over two decades of development, the field remains heterogeneous in task paradigms, analytic strategies, and reported results, which limits our understanding of what hyperscanning can robustly capture. 

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we provide a comprehensive overview of hyperscanning in fMRI research. We address three research questions: (1) how feasible current fMRI hyperscanning paradigms and analytical approaches are; (2) which methodological factors contribute to heterogeneity in the field; and (3) whether single-brain activations reported in hyperscanning studies show spatially convergent neural signatures associated with monitoring and coordinating complex real-time interactions, as assessed using coordinate-based meta-analysis.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, with the protocol pre-registered on the Open Science Framework platform (https://osf.io/6s83m).

We searched PubMed, Web of Science, and PsycINFO up to June 2024 using Boolean combinations of (hyperscanning OR social interaction) AND fmri, without restrictions on search fields. Additional records were identified through manual reference screening.

After duplicate removal, two researchers independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text screening. We included peer-reviewed fMRI hyperscanning experiments with healthy adult participants that reported brain activity outcomes. We excluded studies that involved patient populations, non-fMRI methods, single-participant scanning, missing neural results, or non-original research articles. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, with a third researcher consulted when necessary.

After study selection, two researchers independently extracted data using a standardized form, including study metadata, experimental design, sample characteristics, analytical approaches, behavioral and neural outcomes (single-brain and inter-brain), activation coordinates and coordinate spaces, authors’ interpretations, and open science practices. We also noted potential participant overlap, sources of bias, and other quality concerns. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third researcher. Studies suitable for meta-analysis were identified by the first author in consultation with a senior researcher.

Data analysis involved the qualitative and quantitative integration of the extracted information. We used descriptive statistics to summarize study characteristics and methodological features, including sample size distributions, task paradigms, scanner characteristics, and analytical approaches, to characterize feasibility and sources of methodological heterogeneity in fMRI hyperscanning research. For quantitative synthesis of spatial convergence across studies, we conducted a coordinate-based meta-analysis using activation likelihood estimation (ALE).

ALE analyses were performed using GingerALE (version 3.0.2). All reported peak coordinates were converted to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space using the embedded “Talairach to MNI (SPM)” function. To ensure inclusion of all reported coordinates, we performed the analyses in the dilated MNI152 template space.

For the primary and subset analyses, we applied a cluster-level family-wise error threshold of p < .05, a cluster‐forming threshold of p < .001, and 5000 permutations.

To assess the robustness of the meta-analytic results, we conducted a leave-one-out Jackknife sensitivity analysis, in which the ALE analysis was repeated while sequentially excluding one experiment at a time. For these Jackknife analyses, we used identical thresholds and 1000 permutations. Convergence patterns were compared qualitatively with the primary results to evaluate the stability of identified clusters.

Results

A total of 5,133 records were retrieved from PubMed, PsycINFO, and Web of Science. After removing duplicates, we screened the titles and abstracts of 3,564 reports and excluded 3,343 of them, leaving 221 reports for full-text screening. 35 reports met all inclusion criteria and were included in the review. An additional two reports were identified from the included reports. A total of 37 reports, consisting of 33 independent samples of participants (hereafter “studies”), were included in the systematic review. Inter-rater consistency was 95% and 96% at each stage.

Since 2002, the number of published papers using hyperscanning fMRI has increased. The peak year was 2019, when six papers were published. 54% were published in the last five years. 81.8% of the studies took place in Japan, Germany, the Czech Republic, or the United States of America, all of which are high-income economies according to the World Bank Group.

Included studies enrolled 1672 participants (mean sample size = 50.7±34.4; range 6-204). Sample sizes did not differ much across different behavioral task categories, except for one outlier of 204. Since not all studies reported participant features, such as age and gender, we will not report descriptive statistics for these features.

A key methodological characteristic of hyperscanning research is dyadic composition. Dyadic features report the similarities and differences in sex, age, year of education, handedness, and familiarity between the individuals of a dyad. It is also important to state if dyads were randomly assigned or matched. The most reported features are sex, assignment method, and familiarity. 42.4% studies included same-sex pairs of strangers, while 15.2% studies did not report any information on dyadic features.

A majority of 29 studies used identical 3-Tesla fMRI machines for hyperscanning. Three studies reported a combination of 1.5- and 3-Tesla fMRI machines, while one study did not report the magnetic field strength at all.

There was heterogeneity in behavioral paradigms. Although details differ, we grouped studies into categories. The most common paradigms are cooperation tasks and economic games, taking up 45.5% of the included studies. Other paradigms include joint attention tasks, gaze exchange, and verbal communication tasks.

High heterogeneity was also shown in the analytical methods, especially for the inter-brain data. One-third of the studies did not report dyadic-level analysis, while four did not report single-brain analysis. For the inter-brain analysis, the most used measurement was correlation, including time-series correlation and cross-correlation. Coherence analysis was also adopted in four studies, while one study used dynamic causal modeling.

Out of the 33 studies, 19 different contrasts (hereafter “experiments” according to meta-analysis research terminology) were eligible from 13 studies. As the number met the 17-20 recommendation, we compiled them for the ALE analysis.^2 Further inspection revealed a more homogeneous subset of 14 experiments, comparing real-time social interaction versus non-interactive control conditions. We conducted ALE on both sets.

The primary ALE analysis (19 experiments, 255 foci) identified one significant cluster after correction for multiple comparisons. The cluster is 952mm3 and centers at (5.9, 22.2, 37.6) with two peaks, covering the right cingulate gyrus (91.5%) and the right medial frontal gyrus (8.5%). For the subset analysis, the ALE analysis found one significant cluster across 230 foci. The cluster is 1128 mm3 and centers at (6, 21.9, 37.6) with two peaks, covering the right cingulate gyrus (90.9%) and the right medial frontal gyrus (9.1%).

Leave-one-out Jackknife sensitivity analyses showed that the significant cluster was replicated and robust in most datasets. It also revealed a second, marginal cluster at around (-46, -53, 21), covering the superior temporal gyrus (92%), middle temporal gyrus (4%), and supramarginal gyrus (4%).

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provides a comprehensive and critical synthesis of the fMRI hyperscanning research. Rather than reflecting methodological weakness, the substantial heterogeneity observed in experimental paradigms, analytical strategies, and reporting practices indicates the exploratory and still-evolving nature of the field. Hyperscanning fMRI is actively adapting classical neuroimaging approaches to the study of real-time social interaction.

In neuroimaging research, fMRI hyperscanning occupies a distinctive position by combining whole-brain coverage and high spatial resolution with the ability to capture temporally meaningful information across interacting brains. Compared with other hyperscanning techniques that prioritize temporal resolution and ecological flexibility, fMRI hyperscanning offers unique opportunities to investigate the spatial organization of social interaction networks, albeit with higher technical and infrastructural demands. The convergence observed in single-brain activation patterns across hyperscanning studies, as demonstrated by the ALE meta-analysis, suggests that reproducible neural signatures can be identified. Specifically, the observed convergent activation in the right cingulate gyrus has been consistently implicated in motor control, performance monitoring, and decision making, functions critical for guiding behavior in complex and dynamic social interactions.^3 Therefore, despite methodological diversity, evidence supports the feasibility of quantitative synthesis in this domain.

Importantly, our findings highlight current limitations in fMRI hyperscanning research that are less related to the imaging modality itself, rather to variability in study design, reporting standards, and analytic choices, particularly at the dyadic and inter-brain levels. To address these limitations, future progress in fMRI hyperscanning research can work in the following key directions: (1) clear justification and reporting of sample size decisions and dyad composition, including standardized reporting of dyadic features; (2) broader adoption of more naturalistic interaction paradigms that move beyond task-constrained cooperation and economic games; (3) increased transparency and consistency in analytic pipelines, particularly for inter-brain measures, to improve comparability across studies; and (4) multi-site collaborations and pre-registered analysis frameworks to enhance reproducibility and generalizability.

By aligning methodological rigor with the unique strengths of fMRI hyperscanning, future studies can move toward more systematic investigations of how individual neural processes support interpersonal dynamics. In this way, fMRI hyperscanning has the potential to become a central tool for bridging individual-level brain mechanisms and real-world social interaction.
