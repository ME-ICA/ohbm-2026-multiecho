Introduction

The relationship between cognitive and mental health in older age is complex and not fully understood. Generally, cognition declines with age across domains such as memory and reasoning1, whereas emotional wellbeing tends to improve2, alongside a decrease in the incidence of depression. This apparent disconnect between improved emotional wellbeing and declining cognitive and physical health has been termed the “emotion paradox3.” Clinically, late-life depression increases the risk of dementia, suggesting that poorer mental health is associated with greater cognitive decline, seemingly at odds with the emotion paradox. This study aimed to disentangle this dual relationship between cognitive and mental health in older age.

One explanation for these mixed findings is that people age differently4. The brain reserve framework attributes these divergent trajectories to variation in structural brain resources, with higher brain reserve linked to lower mortality, reduced Alzheimer’s risk, and fewer late-life depressive episodes5. In this study, we derived data-driven aging profiles in an older population cohort based on the brain reserve model, hypothesizing four profiles (Figure 1A): healthy aging (high cognition and positive mood), suboptimal aging (low cognition and negative mood), cognitive reserve (high cognition with negative mood), and affective reserve (low cognition with positive mood). We compared analytic models, characterized the number and nature of the aging profiles, and examined demographic, neuroanatomical, and etiological differences between profiles. Finally, we tested whether aging profiles help explain the emotion paradox.

Methods

We analyzed 22,686 UK Biobank participants (mean age 64 ± 7.5 years) with complete cognitive, negative emotionality, and structural MRI data, using 81 cortical–subcortical DKT gray-matter volumes as input to a data-driven brain reserve model (Figure 1B). The model used canonical correlation analysis (CCA) to identify linear combinations of gray-matter volumes and summary cognition/neuroticism measures that maximized their relationship (Figure 1C).  CCA significance was assessed via 1,000 permutations of the behavioral data, using the first canonical correlation as a max-statistic to obtain family-wise–error–corrected p-values across canonical pairs. Canonical brain (U) and behavioral (V) scores from all significant modes were then entered into fuzzy c-means clustering to derive and further evaluate aging profiles.

The reliability of the brain-reserve model was evaluated using cluster stability (adjusted Rand Index, ARI), cluster separability (silhouette score), and subject-level cluster assignment uncertainty (entropy), each compared against null data across cluster solutions (k=2–5). Neuroanatomical differences between aging profiles were visualized by plotting z-scored mean volumes for each region within each profile. Validity was further assessed using 11 variables spanning demographics, brain size, cardiovascular health, inflammation, and Alzheimer’s disease polygenic risk, analyzed with ANOVA and ANCOVA. Post-hoc Tukey HSD tests were conducted to determine which pairwise group differences contributed to the main effects, with Tukey’s procedure controlling the family-wise error rate within each validation variable.

Results

The brain reserve model yielded a different cluster solution than our hypothesized profiles (Figure 2A), with an ARI of 0.16. Cognitive score dominated group separation, consistent with the first CCA component linking brain structure and behavior (rU1-V1=0.30, p<0.001), whereas the second component was more weakly related to negative emotionality (rU2-V2=0.16, p<0.001). The four-profile brain-reserve solution showed only modest separation in cognitive and neuroticism space and placed a third cluster overlapping the healthy aging and affective reserve groups.

Model reliability was evaluated against null models (Figure 2B). The 4-Profile brain reserve model showed high stability (ARI=0.97, p=0.001), and significant separability (silhouette=0.19, p=0.001), but higher entropy (0.87, p=0.011). No single dimensionality was clearly optimal. Based on our hypotheses, we focused on the 4-profile solution for subsequent analyses.

Age–cognition and age–neuroticism relationships varied across profiles, revealing differential contributions to the emotion paradox (Figure 2C). Profile 1 showed worsening cognition and increasing neuroticism with age, indicating particularly suboptimal aging, whereas Profile 4 showed preserved cognition with age despite low neuroticism, both deviating from the pattern predicted by the emotion paradox. Neuroanatomical characteristics for each profile are shown in Figure 2D.

Figure 2E shows validation results. Profiles differed markedly in age (Ω=0.23, p<0.001), total intracranial volume (Ω = 0.25, p < 0.001), and sex (Ω=0.12, p<0.001). After controlling for age, intracranial volume and sex, there were also small but robust effects of income (Ω=0.04, p<0.001) and cardiovascular risk/burden (Framingham: Ω=0.07, p<0.001; white matter hyperintensities: Ω=0.04, p<0.001). Polygenic risk for Alzheimer’s disease did not differ between profiles (Ω = 0, p = 0.99). 

Conclusion

Using a brain reserve model, we identified four data-driven profiles with different combinations of cognition, neuroticism, and brain volume. Profile 1 reflected suboptimal aging: older individuals with lower cognition, higher neuroticism, smaller brain volume, lower income, and higher risk factors, who cross-sectionally showed both worsening cognition and increasing negative affect with age, directly contradicting the emotion paradox and suggesting a high-risk, potentially pre-dementia profile. Profile 2 showed the classic emotion paradox in younger, higher-income individuals with high cognition, low neuroticism, larger brain volume, and healthier profiles overall, who nonetheless followed the expected pattern of declining cognition but improving emotional wellbeing with age. Profile 3 combined high neuroticism with elevated cardiovascular risk and greater white matter hyperintensity burden, regardless of cognitive level, indicating a group potentially vulnerable to vascular pathways of cognitive decline and dementia. Profile 4 represented a resilient pattern: individuals with average cognition, low neuroticism, balanced demographics, average vascular risk, and notably stable cognition across older ages, suggesting slower cognitive aging and emotional resilience. Together, these four profiles show that the emotion paradox holds for some, and reverses for others. Thus, the paradox is resolved not by a single explanation but by recognizing heterogeneous brain-reserve profiles that differentially couple age, cognition, and emotional wellbeing, highlighting the need for profile-tailored prevention and intervention strategies in later life.
