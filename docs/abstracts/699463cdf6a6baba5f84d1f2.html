Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder in older adults. Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) is recognized as an intermediate stage between normal cognition and mild dementia, characterized by memory deficits. The metabolic syndrome and insulin resistance are related to cortical thinning and other brain alterations1. Moreover, recent studies suggest that there is a link between cognitive impairments and glycemic conditions, including metabolic syndrome/type 2 diabetes2.

To investigate this, we conducted magnetic resonance (MR) surface and volume analysis of healthy control (HC) and MCI patients. Mini Mental State Exam (MMSE), fasting glucose (FG) and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) were measured, and we conducted the general linear model (GLM) to investigate the associations between glycemic state and cognitive function and brain morphometry and the associations difference in groups.

Methods

44 healthy control (38f/6m), age 71±6.88, patients (34f/4m) diagnosed with MCI, age 70±5.5, participated in the study. MMSE, HbA1c and FG were measured. In this pilot analysis  some data points on FG (MCI:6, HC:11) HbA1c (MCI:3, HC:4) and MSME (HC:2) are missing.

 

Study was performed by 3T MR system (PrismaFit) using a 64-channel head coil (Siemens Healthcare, Germany). 

T1-weighted MPRAGE was acquired for the morphometry with the voxel size 0.83mm3. FreeSurfer version 8.0.0 was used for segmentation and parcellation. 

Data were smoothed using a Gaussian kernel of 10-mm full width at half-maximum (FWHM) cortical thickness and volume. Then, we performed whole-brain vertex-wise analyses using generalized linear models (GLMs). We assessed the effect of each predictor variable: MMSE, HbA1c and FG on cortical thickness, and volume. Second, we evaluated how those associations differ between control group and MCI group. All GLMs were adjusted for age. We applied surface-based Monte Carlo simulation-based cluster-wise correction for multiple comparisons with the cluster-forming threshold 3.0. Results were evaluated separately for positive (pos) and negative (neg) contrasts, as well as using absolute statistics (abs), reflecting effects in both directions. Final whole-brain vertex-wise analysis results were displayed on FreeSurfer’s “fsaverage” brain surface using Freeview, a FreeSurfer visualization tool.

Results

Across all participants, vertex-wise analyses revealed widespread associations between MMSE scores and cortical thickness, whereas associations with cortical volume were limited.

Regarding MMSE–cortical thickness associations, positive associations were observed in the left hemisphere in the insula, middle temporal cortex, superior temporal cortex, inferior temporal cortex, banks of the superior temporal sulcus, inferior parietal cortex, superior parietal cortex, fusiform gyrus, entorhinal cortex, and posterior cingulate cortex. A negative association was observed in the left orbitofrontal cortex.

In the right hemisphere, positive MMSE–cortical thickness associations were detected in the rostral anterior cingulate cortex, posterior cingulate cortex, precuneus, parahippocampal cortex, entorhinal cortex, fusiform gyrus, inferior temporal cortex, middle temporal cortex, lateral occipital cortex, supramarginal gyrus, insula, rostral middle frontal cortex, pars opercularis, and pars triangularis.

Associations between cortical morphometry and HbA1c were sparse and restricted to a small number of regions.

We further observed significant group differences in HbA1c–cortical morphometry associations, with stronger HbA1c–cortical volume associations in individuals with MCI compared with healthy controls, indicating altered structure–cognition relationships in MCI.

In the left hemisphere, HbA1c–cortical volume associations in individuals with MCI were observed in the caudal middle frontal cortex, rostral middle frontal cortex, precentral cortex, pars opercularis, insula, supramarginal gyrus, superior temporal cortex, banks of the superior temporal sulcus, inferior parietal cortex, superior parietal cortex, precuneus, isthmus cingulate cortex, superior frontal cortex, rostral anterior cingulate cortex, and medial orbitofrontal cortex.

In the right hemisphere, HbA1c–cortical volume associations in individuals with MCI were detected in the precuneus, posterior cingulate cortex, isthmus cingulate cortex, superior parietal cortex, inferior parietal cortex, supramarginal gyrus, rostral middle frontal cortex, pars triangularis, and superior temporal cortex.

In contrast, MMSE–cortical thickness associations did not differ between healthy controls and individuals with MCI.

Only a few cortical regions showed associations with FG, and these regions did not overlap with those associated with HbA1c, suggesting distinct neuroanatomical correlates of different metabolic measures.

After surface-based cluster-wise correction for multiple comparisons, significant MMSE–cortical thickness associations remained in the inferior temporal cortex of the left hemisphere, as well as in the inferior temporal cortex and insula of the right hemisphere, predominantly showing positive associations.  

Conclusion

Contrary to our initial hypothesis, we did not observe robust negative associations between cortical morphometry and markers of glycemic state across the cortex. Notably, however, associations between HbA1c and cortical volume were detected, partially aligning with our hypothesis and suggesting that volumetric measures may capture subtle metabolic effects that are not yet reflected in cortical thickness.

The absence of more widespread effects likely reflects the modest sample size and limited metabolic heterogeneity of this pilot cohort. In this context, inclusion of individuals with long-standing type 2 diabetes or more advanced metabolic syndrome may be required to reveal clearer structure–metabolism relationships.

Beyond glucose-based markers, future studies incorporating fasting insulin, lipid profiles, and myokines may provide a more comprehensive view of metabolic influences on brain structure. Finally, while a smoothing kernel of 10 mm FWHM was chosen as a conservative approach, exploratory analyses using broader smoothing (e.g., 15 mm FWHM), as adopted in previous work, may help to identify more spatially diffuse effects1,3.
