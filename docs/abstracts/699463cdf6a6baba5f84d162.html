Introduction

The infant brain undergoes rapid neural growth and exhibits significant intrinsic variability, resulting in high trial-to-trial fluctuations in neural activity due to immature regulatory mechanisms. Traditional electrophysiological studies often dismiss this variability as noise, relying instead on grand average Event-Related Potentials (ERPs) to study cognitive processes. However, this averaging approach tends to collapse the temporal dynamics of neural processing and obscures state-dependent variations. Recent research suggests that this "noise" might actually reflect the brain's shifting underlying states1.

Given infants’ significant sleep during early life, sleep states provide a critical context for examining these dynamics. A key question arises: how are sensory representations maintained within this dynamic and fluctuating environment? In a study where syllables were presented at a rate of one per second, it was demonstrated that 3-month-old infants can reliably encode phonetic features such as place and manner of articulation2. However, it remains unclear how different sleep stages or brain states affect this performance. Specifically, can the infant brain consistently encode stimulus features despite varying levels of attention?

To explore these questions, we propose extending classical decoding approaches to a dynamical systems perspective that considers how these patterns evolve. We suggest that early syllable perception is best understood as trajectories within a high-dimensional neural state space. This study investigates (1) the brain’s ability to maintain robust decoding of syllable features across different sleep stages, (2) how the stability of these sensory traces fluctuates with cortical arousal, and (3) whether neural trajectories exhibit stable, attractor-like structures during cortical processing at an early age. We will compare these dynamics in 3-month-old infants, who display more organised sleep architecture, with those of neonates.

Methods

We analysed high-density EEG (128 channels) from 25 three-month-old infants and 17 full-term neonates, who passively listened to syllables generated from a combination of four consonants and four vowels. These syllables were structured into two contrasting phonotactic arrangements: consonant-vowel (CV) and vowel-consonant (VC).

Given the absence of expert-scored hypnograms for this dataset, we utilised the Yet Another Spindle Algorithm (YASA)3, a validated framework for automated sleep scoring. YASA classified 30-second epochs into different sleep stages: Wake, N1, N2, N3, and REM. To delve deeper into latent brain states beyond the conventional sleep staging, we extracted bandpower features and projected them into a low-dimensional manifold using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). Subsequently, we employed unsupervised density-based clustering on this projection to identify data-driven brain states of cortical activity.

To separate representational content from univariate amplitude fluctuations, we utilised time-resolved linear discriminant analysis (LDA). We trained classifiers to differentiate between syllable structures (CV vs. VC) using a stratified cross-validation approach, and we evaluated decoding performance using the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC). Additionally, we created Generalisation Across Time (GAT) matrices to assess the temporal stability of the neural code. We chose this contrast because it should remain decodable even when only a few exemplars are presented within a given sleep stage and at both ages.

To quantify the persistence of the sensory trace, we developed a "Persistence Metric". This involved extracting decoding performance from a 10-sample window centred around the diagonal of the GAT matrix. A high persistence score signifies that the neural code generated at time t remains active and discriminative for adjacent time points, indicating a sustained "reverberation" of the representation rather than a brief sensory spike.

For visualising the state-space trajectory of the evoked responses, we applied t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbour Embedding (t-SNE) to the channel-space data. This enabled us to project both the grand average event-related potentials (ERPs) and single-trial data onto a 2D plane, facilitating an analysis of the neural response's topology. Through this approach, we could observe key features such as recurrence (loops), attractor stability (the tightness of the trajectory), and the geometric relationships between the baseline and processing states.

Results

The UMAP embedding analysis for 3-month-olds revealed a well-structured state space characterised by distinct clusters that aligned with YASA-defined sleep stages, such as Wakefulness, NREM (N1-N3), and REM sleep. However, there was noticeable "smearing" between these clusters, indicating the presence of transitional latent states that were only captured through our density-based clustering approach. In stark contrast, the sleep topology observed in neonates was disorganised, lacking clear dominance of any single sleep stage. This finding underscores the immaturity of spectral differentiation of sleep states at birth and highlights the high variability in neonatal cortical dynamics.

When it came to syllable processing in 3-month-olds, successful decoding of syllable structure (CV vs. VC) using Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) reached a peak accuracy of approximately 0.82. Notably, this decoding was robust across all sleep stages, showing no significant differences in peak accuracy among Wakefulness, NREM, and REM sleep. This suggests that syllable perception in 3-month-olds operates automatically, independent of conscious vigilance. Conversely, LDA decoding in neonates resulted in the peak area under the curve of approximately 0.54. Rather than a mere absence of discriminative information, we interpret this low performance as a limitation of standard temporal decoding methods. While behavioural and standard ERP methods have shown that neonates are capable of differentiating CV from VC structures 4, our analysis suggests that the underlying neural representations are present but temporally unstable. This dissociation served as the primary indication for employing a geometric approach, specifically, trajectory realignment to recover the latent signal. 

While the peak decoding accuracy remained uniform across the different sleep stages for 3-month-olds, the temporal dynamics exhibited notable differences. Our persistence analysis revealed that deep sleep (N2/N3) yielded significantly higher "off-diagonal" decoding scores compared to wakefulness and REM sleep (p < 0.05). This finding suggests that Slow Wave Sleep plays a crucial role in the stable maintenance of sensory traces, allowing neural representations to "linger" in the cortex, likely due to reduced sensory interference.

The manifold projection analysis highlighted a fundamental developmental shift. In 3-month-olds, the neural trajectory formed a smooth, closed-loop "cyclical reset." This trajectory indicates that the brain departs from a baseline attractor, navigates through a specific processing loop that contains discriminative information, and then reliably returns to the baseline state. Crucially, neonatal trajectories exhibited a similar, although ‘broken’ geometric topology. While they failed to form the tight, closed loops seen in older infants, the nascent structure of the trajectory was visible but fragmented. This suggests that the dynamical attractor for syllables exists at birth but is obscured by phase instability, further supporting the hypothesis that trajectory realignment could explain these ‘broken’ loops and enable successful decoding. 

Lastly, when we projected single trials onto the Grand Average t-SNE space, it became evident that the global event-related potential (ERP) functions as a stable neural manifold. Individual trials appeared as noisy realisations that scattered around this idealised path. Notably, the discriminative information (as identified by linear discriminant weights) coincided with specific topographic sub-states within this global loop, reaffirming that successful decoding relies on stable, recurrent neural patterns. 

Conclusion

Our findings indicate a developmental shift in the neural dynamics of speech processing, moving from transient and variable neural responses in neonates to stable neural trajectories by three months of age. While standard linear classifiers failed to detect significant representations in neonates, our trajectory analysis suggests that this is not due to a lack of discrimination, but rather due to ‘broken’ state-space trajectories. The neonatal neural code appears to possess the same fundamental geometry as that of 3-month-olds, but it is highly fragmented by temporal instability. Consequently, we propose that by applying trajectory alignment to correct these deviations, the fragmented neonatal manifold can be stabilised, potentially re-allowing for the decoding of syllable structures that are known to be perceived behaviorally. 

The observation that syllable structure (CV vs VC) remains decodable during deep sleep in three-month-olds indicates that the auditory system can preserve phonetic feature sensitivity across vigilance states. Moreover, the increased persistence of decoding during N2/N3 sleep points to a state-dependent mode of processing, where sensory traces are maintained over longer timescales, potentially aiding in processes such as memory consolidation. 

Finally, our geometric analysis of single trials in relation to the Grand Average manifold provides insight into trial-to-trial variability. We found that the "noise" in individual trials is actually a deviation from the optimal phase of the attractor. We hypothesise that successful decoding is phase-dependent: a trial is effectively encoded only when its neural configuration aligns with the optimal discriminative state of the manifold. Misaligned trials do not indicate a failure in neural response but rather a perturbation off the manifold trajectory. This dynamical perspective reshapes our understanding of infant neural noise, shifting the emphasis from averaging signals to exploring and potentially realigning the geometry of the neural state space.
