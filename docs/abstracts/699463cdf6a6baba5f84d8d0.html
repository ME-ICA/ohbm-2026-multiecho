Introduction

TMS-fMRI has emerged as a powerful approach for probing neural responses of transcranial magnetic stimulation and for establishing causal inferences in fMRI research. Recently, BOLD response of the subgenual anterior cingulate cortex (sgACC) to single pulses of TMS applied to the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) has been proposed as a predictor for the response to repetitive TMS treatments in patients suffering from major depressive disorder (MDD)1. However, using single pulses has been shown to require a high number of trials in order to obtain representative BOLD response patterns2, and thus requires  extensive data acquisition. The application of triplets instead of single pulses has been suggested in several previous TMS-fMRI studies to increase BOLD responses. However, it remains unclear whether the induced BOLD response is identical across these protocols and if the application of triplets also provides benefits in terms of reliability of the BOLD response. Here, we addressed this issue by assessing the test-retest reliability of TMS-induced BOLD response for singlets and triplets in the left DLPFC across different sessions. 

Methods

Eight young healthy volunteers (4 f/4m, mean age 24.6 a) were invited for three separate scanning sessions. MRI scans were performed using a 3T Prisma Fit scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Germany). An overview of the study design can be found in figure 1. During session 1 the manufacturer’s 64ch head coil was used to acquire anatomical (TR=2.1s, TE=3.67ms, flip angle 8°, voxelsize 1x1x1 mm3) and resting state (RS; TR=2s, TE=38ms, flip angle 77°, multiband 4, voxelsize 1.8×1.8×2.0 mm3) scans. Subsequently, neuronavigated active motor thresholding (aMT) was performed, to allow for the calculation of the induced electric field (E-field). Using the RS data, a functional-connectivity-informed left DLPFC target was derived for each participant3, and an optimal coil position was determined using SimNIBS. Based on motor and DLPFC E-field, we calculated a personalized dose adjustment factor4, which was applied to the respective aMT. During session 2 and 3 alternating RS and TMS-fMRI runs (TR=1s, TE=38ms, flip angle 60°, multiband 4, voxelsize 3×3×3 mm3) were performed using three 7-channel surface coil arrays. The TMS coil was positioned on the personalized target using neuronavigation and continuously tracked throughout the whole measurement. In case of between-run subject movement, visual feedback was used to guide the participant back to the target. For session 3, the target was the average coil placement during stimulations of session 2. TMS was precisely interleaved with slice acquisition to ensure artifact free imaging and applied in either 30 single pulses or 30 10-Hz triplets, with minimum inter-pulse intervals of 10 s. Data were processed using fMRIprep v24.0.1 and analyzed using generalized linear models in Python/nilearn. Subject-level effect sizes were further analysed using the Schäfer 400 parcellation as well as Yeo network definitions. Parcelwise means were compared across sessions and individuals using Pearson’s correlation as a metric for reliability.

Results

Although triplets produced stronger responses at the individual stimulation site (E-field hotspot) (paired t-test, p=0.008), no statistically significant difference between BOLD response of singlets and triplets could be identified on group level. For the sgACC, no significant difference between singlets and triplets could be detected and response was highly variable. Whole-brain analysis showed significantly higher within-subject reliability compared to across-subject reliability, with highest correlation values in the case of triplets (figure 2b, mean correlation 0.43±0.19 for singlets and 0.55±0.11 for triplets). Networks showing most reliable activations across sessions included somatomotor and salience regions, followed by default mode and control network. Lowest similarity was found for limbic areas. 

Conclusion

In this pilot study we found increased reliability of TMS-induced BOLD responses by using triplets instead of single pulses. This suggests that triplets improve the stability of individual response patterns, most likely by increasing BOLD amplitudes. Notably, despite personalized targeting, continuous motion tracking and E-field–informed dosing, responses in the sgACC were highly variable, although activation under the coil was consistent across sessions. In conclusion, these findings support the use of triplet stimulation as a practical compromise between physiological specificity and experimental efficiency, particularly for studies aiming to characterize individual TMS effects.
