Introduction

Intoxication with psychedelics has a robust effect on brain activity, an effect that is correlated with the phenomenology of the experience. Since several psychedelics, including psilocybin, have been shown to have long-term positive effects on mood and can treat depression and other disorders, it is likely that during the psychedelic experience our brain switches from one functional state to another, resulting in altered dynamics of brain activity. The phenomenology of the experience and the change in brain state predict its long-term effects on mood and well-being.

Methods

EEG microstate analysis was performed for each participant and condition separately using Python. We extracted the global field power (GFP) peaks in order to account only for temporal points with the highest signal-to-noise ratios. The microstate analysis included two steps: the first was to identify a set of microstate maps, and the second was to project the original multichannel EEG recording into the basis comprised of microstate maps (see Figure for an overview of the process).

The maps at the maxima of GFP time-series were submitted to a modified hierarchical clustering algorithm. Briefly, all maps are initially considered as independent clusters; in each iteration, the worst cluster is identified and split into its constituent maps. Subsequently, the maps of this “worst” cluster are redistributed to the remaining clusters according to the strongest Pearson product-moment correlation. These steps are repeated until the desired number of clusters remains.

We used the microstate algorithm to cluster original maps from each participant and condition into microstate maps specific to each subject and condition. Next, in order to obtain condition-specific maps, we submitted maps from each participant for a given condition to another round of clustering, thus obtaining mean maps for that condition. All maps (both individual and condition-specific) were labeled class A, B, C, and D based on resemblance to normative template maps as per Koenig et al.

After finding canonical microstate maps for each subject and condition, the original multichannel EEG recording was transformed into a temporal sequence of microstate maps by the following procedure: one of the canonical maps was assigned to each GFP peak based on the highest Pearson product-moment correlation. If two subsequent GFP peaks were assigned different maps, we found a midpoint in the temporal sense and created a microstate transition there. From the sequence of microstate maps, we calculated the following features:

  • Average lifespan: Calculated as the time during which all successive original topographies were assigned the same microstate class.
  • Microstate coverage: Calculated by taking the ratio of the total time spent in that particular microstate over the total recording time.
  • Frequency of occurrence: Determined by counting unique appearances of each microstate in one second of the recording.
  • Microstate transition probabilities: The number of transitions from each microstate class into another microstate class was counted and normalized to fractions of all between-class transitions, yielding an N × N matrix with a zero diagonal.

Results

All subsequent analyses rely on two different filtering approaches: since participants in our study were administered psilocybin, which shifts the relative spectral maximum towards faster frequencies, we opted for a wider bandwidth, specifically 1–40 Hz. In order to compare our results with the existing body of literature, we repeated all analyses with the more “traditional” microstate filtering option, namely 2–20 Hz. 

Firstly, we compared the number of peaks in GFP curves between conditions. Our hypothesis was that the number of peaks in the psilocybin condition would be higher, since classical psychedelics tend to shift spectral maxima towards higher frequencies; hence, the signal itself is faster and we expected the number of peaks to be higher. In the 1–40 Hz bandwidth, the number of GFP peaks in the psilocybin condition was indeed significantly higher than in the placebo condition, similar to the 1–20 Hz bandwidth. A higher number of GFP peaks results in a higher number of topographic maps submitted to the microstate clustering algorithm.

Traditionally, microstate studies compute 4 canonical microstates, referring to seminal early works in this area. Here, we also tested the ideal number of microstates as obtained from the cross-validation test proposed by Pascual-Marqui. To our surprise, the ideal number of microstates in each condition and time across subjects (aggregated with median) was 3. Overall, we observed a trend towards a lower number of states in the wider 1–40 Hz bandwidth; however, the finding that the ideal number of states is 3 held true for both bandwidths. In light of these results, we decided to continue our analyses in two branches: the main branch used the 1–40 Hz bandwidth with decomposition into 3 canonical microstates, while the second was the “classical” microstate analysis using the 2–20 Hz bandwidth with 4 canonical states.

Generally, and in line with our initial hypothesis, significant differences between placebo and psilocybin conditions occurred mainly in the T2 and T3 intervals, when the effect of the intoxication was strongest, with the psilocybin condition showing a lower average lifespan, indicating faster state switching. This held true for both filtering paradigms. Similar results were observed for particular microstates: microstates B and C in the 1–40 Hz bandwidth and microstate C in the 2–20 Hz bandwidth. The effect sizes, as measured with Cohen’s d, were rather strong in this interaction setting. Lastly, even the individual microstate factor, while disregarding the time factor, was significantly different in both filtering paradigms (albeit only for microstate B in the 2–20 Hz branch).

Based on our results, we were interested in whether the affected microstate features in the altered state of consciousness induced by psilocybin translate into the questionnaire responses gathered during the experiment. The subjective experience data were gathered in retrospect and include 4 scales of the OAV questionnaire: oceanic boundlessness (OSE), dread of ego dissolution (AIA), visionary restructuralisation (VUS), and altered state of consciousness (VWB). These 4 scales decode the internal experiences of individual participants and were gathered in both placebo and psilocybin conditions. All 4 scales were significantly higher during the psilocybin condition. In general, we found significant correlations between occurrence and lifespan characteristics of microstates A and C with respect to all 4 scales.

Conclusion

Our study confirms that psilocybin intoxication significantly accelerates resting-state brain dynamics, evidenced by a marked increase in Global Field Power (GFP) peaks and a spectral shift toward higher frequencies (1–40 Hz). Methodologically, our data challenges the traditional four-map convention in this context, suggesting that a three-microstate model may better capture these accelerated dynamics. While the spatial topography of canonical maps remained stable across conditions, their temporal structure was profoundly altered: we observed significantly reduced microstate lifespans—specifically in classes B and C—during peak intoxication, indicating a regime of rapid neural switching and reduced state stability. Crucially, these specific temporal alterations (particularly in microstates A and C) showed strong correlations with subjective ratings of altered states of consciousness (OAV scales). These findings imply that EEG microstate dynamics can serve as a sensitive, quantifiable biomarker for the serotonergic model of psychosis, providing a direct neurophysiological link to the phenomenological experience of psychedelic intoxication.
