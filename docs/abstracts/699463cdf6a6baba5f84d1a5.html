Introduction

Technological and data sharing advances have resulted in the proliferation of whole-brain maps of multiple structural and functional features including gene expression, neurotransmitter receptors, synapse and cell types, laminar organization, metabolism and intrinsic dynamics. These brain maps are anchored by global organizational principles constraining and shaping the topographic organization of neuronal populations. Notably, brain regions that are proximal in space have more similar biological properties than regions that are distant in space (Fig. 1a). Other constraints include connectivity (structural or functional) and biological similarity (e.g. genetic, neurochemical or laminar). Using a generative framework, we evaluate how these different constraints shape the topographic organization of biological features across the cortex and probe the dark matter of the brain, identifying unexplained patterns of topographic organization.

Methods

We first quantified the homophily – the tendency for neighboring regions to be similar to each other – of 43 brain maps from the neuromaps repository using Moran’s I.1 This metric is commonly used to quantify spatial autocorrelation (i.e. spatial homophily) in the field of spatial statistics (Fig. 1b), but it can be applied to any arbitrary type of inter-regional relationship (Fig. 1c). We reconstructed matrices of cortico-cortical interactions for six distinct modalities (Fig. 1d): spatial proximity (the inverse of the geodesic distance between brain regions), structural and functional connectivity (reconstructed with diffusion and resting-state functional MRI data from the Human Connectome Project2), transcriptional similarity (reconstructed using transcriptomic data from the Allen Human Brain Atlas), laminar similarity (reconstructed using data from the BigBrain Atlas3) and receptor similarity (reconstructed using data from a positron emission tomography dataset of whole-brain neurotransmitter density4). We next quantified the homophily of each brain map with respect to these six modalities. Then, for each map, we used a simulated annealing framework (Fig. 2a) to generate synthetic brain maps that preserve the homophily of empirical brain maps with respect to these six types of cortico-cortical interactions simultaneously.

Results

Fig. 1e shows the multimodal homophily of each brain maps with respect to the six inter-regional relationships. We generally observe greater homophily of maps with respect to geodesic distance and structural connectivity, consistent with the idea that spatial relationships and synaptic connectivity are the primary physical constraints that shape the patterning of other molecular, cellular and functional features. There are also several noteworthy exceptions. For example, intracortical myelin is highly homophilic with respect to transcriptional similarity, which is consistent with an emerging literature on the role of transcriptomic gradients in the development of white matter5. For each brain maps, we then generated 500 synthetic maps. In Fig. 2b, we embed the synthetic maps in a morphospace spanned by principal components of empirical maps. Synthetic maps tend to cluster near the position occupied by their representative empirical maps, with the mean pairwise distance between surrogate maps in this morphospace recapitulating the pairwise distance between empirical maps (ρ=0.59). This shows that the simulated maps uniquely capture the topographic organizational features of empirical maps. We next compared the spatial topography of empirical brain maps to the topography of simulated maps. Namely, we calculated the mean difference between empirical and simulated values for each brain region, across all brain maps, obtaining a brain map x brain region matrix of unexpected spatial patterns. Using principal component analyses, we identified the four principal axes of variance in this matrix, explaining 53% of the total variance (Fig. 2c). These main axes of variance identify hidden sources of variation in brain maps (i.e. unexplained variation). This “dark matter” potentially reflects underexplored mechanisms and influences shaping brain map organization, such as glial cell distribution, vascular architecture, or microglia activation. 

Conclusion

Altogether, our work offers a novel framework for understanding how large-scale cortical interactions shape the spatial organization of brain maps. Importantly, by modeling these organizing forces and identifying unexplained patterns of variation, our work may guide future research, highlighting gaps in current datasets and pointing toward novel biological processes shaping brain map topographies.
