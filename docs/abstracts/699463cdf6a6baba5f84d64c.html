Introduction

Fear generalization refers to the adaptive process by which conditioned fear responses to a specific threat cue extend to perceptually or conceptually similar stimuli.1 This process forms a generalization gradient in which fear diminishes as similarity to the conditioned stimulus decreases, observable at both behavioral and neural levels. At the neural level, fear generalization is supported by a distributed network encompassing fear excitation regions such as the anterior insula, dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, and thalamus, alongside inhibitory and safety-related regions including the ventromedial prefrontal cortex and middle temporal gyrus.2 The hippocampus is hypothesized to play a modulatory role within this network through pattern separation and pattern completion processes, enabling discrimination between similar stimuli or, conversely, the retrieval of threat representations from similar stimuli.1,2

Excessive fear generalization has been increasingly implicated as a key mechanism contributing to pathological anxiety, as it promotes persistent fear responses to nominally safe or ambiguous cues, amplifies avoidance behavior, and undermines extinction learning. Despite its clear clinical relevance, fear generalization has received limited empirical attention at the neural level in patients with social anxiety disorder (SAD). Behavioral studies using disorder-relevant stimuli have demonstrated that patients with SAD experience difficulties distinguishing between threat and safety cues and exhibiting elevated fear responses to ambiguous and safe stimuli.3 However, the neural substrates supporting this maladaptive pattern remain largely unexplored.

Methods

Participants. Thirty-five patients with SAD and thirty-four healthy controls (CON) were recruited. After exclusions for missing behavioral responses and excessive head motion (framewise displacement > 0.5 mm in ≥ 20% of volumes), final analyses included thirty-one SAD and thirty-one CON.

Experimental Design. A Pavlovian fear conditioning and generalization paradigm was implemented during functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) scanning. The paradigm utilized socially relevant stimuli, with three neutral facial expressions from male actors selected from the validated facial expression database serving as conditioned stimuli (CS+, CS−, bCS−). The unconditioned stimulus (US) consisted of a visual presentation combining an angry facial expression with a contemptuous comment (e.g., "you are good for nothing!"). During the acquisition phase, fifteen trials of each CS were presented. With an 80% reinforcement schedule, twelve of the fifteen CS+ trials were followed by the US. CS− and bCS− were never paired with the US. The generalization phase consisted of two runs. In each run, fourteen CS+ trials, ten CS− trials, and ten bCS− trials were presented. To prevent early extinction, eight of the twenty-eight CS+ trials (four per run) were followed by the US. Eight morphed faces representing generalization stimuli along a continuum between CS+ and CS− were also included, with adjacent morphs grouped into four classes (C1-C4), where C1 was most similar to CS− and C4 was most similar to CS+. The bCS− served as an additional safety cue that was not morphed with CS+ and thus bore no perceptual resemblance to the conditioned threat cue. Throughout all phases, participants rated each facial stimulus on a three-point Likert scale indicating the perceived likelihood that a contemptuous comment would be presented. 

Data acquisition and preprocessing. Brain images were acquired using a 3.0 Tesla MR scanner. Functional images were obtained using an echo planar imaging sequence. High-resolution anatomical images were obtained using a T1-weighted MP-RAGE sequence. Preprocessing included slice-timing correction, motion correction, coregistration, normalization to standard space, and spatial smoothing. 

Statistical analysis. First-level models estimated blood oxygen level-dependent responses to each stimulus type. At the group level, functional regions of interest (fROI) were defined based on contrasts identifying responses to threat versus safety cue in the generalization phase. Specifically, whole-brain analyses of the contrast between responses to the unreinforced CS+ versus the bCS– were conducted using a voxelwise probability threshold of p ≤ .005 and a cluster probability threshold of p ≤ .05 (k > 83). The bCS– rather than CS– was contrasted against unreinforced CS+ to isolate pure conditioning effects independent of fear generalization. Next, beta weights averaged across voxels within each fROI were extracted and plotted across stimulus type to visualize generalization gradients. Exploratory generalized psychophysiological interaction (gPPI) analyses examined fear generalization-related functional connectivity between anterior hippocampus seed regions and other fROIs. Connectivity strength was assessed by contrasting responses to generalization stimuli (C1-4) versus the bCS–. 

Results

Behavioral findings.

In the acquisition phase, risk ratings demonstrated successful and robust fear conditioning. Specifically, participants consistently rated the CS+ as more risky than the CS− (p < .001). 

In the generalization phase, a 2 (Group: SAD, CON) × 6 (Stimulus: CS−, C1, C2, C3, C4, CS+) repeated measures ANOVA demonstrated successful stimulus discrimination, as indicated by a significant main effect of Stimulus (p = .01). A significant main effect of Group (p = .010) revealed that SAD exhibited globally elevated risk ratings compared to CON. Post-hoc analyses showed that SAD displayed significantly higher risk ratings for CS− (p = .005) and C1 (p = .03), with a marginal effect for C2 (p = .060) than CON. The Group × Stimulus interaction effect was not significant (p = .927). Regarding bCS–, SAD showed elevated risk ratings relative to CON (p = .011). Critically, within SAD, CS− received higher risk ratings than bCS− (p = .023), suggesting that threat perception was amplified when safety cues existed on a perceptual continuum with danger cues rather than due to a general increase in threat perception. In contrast, CON showed no difference between CS– and bCS– ratings (p = .077), processing all safety cues as equally safe. 

fMRI findings.

In the generalization phase, whole-brain analyses of the CS+ versus bCS– contrast revealed several brain regions showing differential activation patterns. Regions showing greater activation to CS+ relative to bCS– (positive gradients) included bilateral dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC), bilateral inferior parietal lobule (IPL), dorsomedial prefrontal cortex/supplementary motor area (dmPFC/SMA), right anterior insula, and bilateral thalamus. Conversely, regions showing greater activation to bCS– relative to CS+ (negative gradients) included the precuneus, ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC), bilateral middle temporal gyrus (MTG), bilateral superior temporal sulcus/angular gyrus (STS/AG), and left lingual gyrus. Using these identified regions as fROIs, beta weights were extracted and plotted across stimulus types (Figure 1). Between-group comparisons of beta values for each stimulus type revealed that, among the fear excitation regions, the dmPFC/SMA showed marginally higher activation to bCS– in SAD compared to CON (p = .053), and bilateral thalamus showed significantly higher activation to bCS– in SAD (p = .03). In contrast, among the fear inhibition regions, the precuneus showed significantly lower activation to CS– (p = .045) and C1 (p = .023) in SAD, and the vmPFC showed marginally lower activation to C1 (p =.068) in SAD compared to CON. In addition, patients with SAD showed generally reduced activation across stimulus types in the left lingual gyrus and left STS/AG.

Connectivity analysis revealed that anterior hippocampus-vmPFC connectivity was significantly greater for bCS– compared to C4 in CON (p < .0001), but not in SAD (p = .636).

Conclusion

This study examined the neural correlates of fear generalization in patients with SAD using an ecologically enhanced fear conditioning paradigm using more disorder-specific stimuli. Behaviorally, patients with SAD demonstrated elevated threat perception to safety cues that shared perceptual features with conditioned threat cues, suggesting impaired safety-threat discrimination. At the neural level, patients showed patterns suggestive of altered processing in fear-related brain regions, with some evidence of increased activation in fear excitation regions and reduced activation in fear inhibition regions in response to safety and ambiguous stimuli. Notably, patients also exhibited generally reduced activation in the left lingual gyrus during fear-related face processing, which may reflect altered perceptual or social-affective processing of facial stimuli. Exploratory connectivity analyses revealed that anterior hippocampus-vmPFC coupling during ambiguous stimulus processing differed between groups, suggesting potential group differences in pattern separation and fear inhibition processes. Together, these results suggest that overgeneralized fear in SAD may be associated with altered neural processing of safety signals when encountering perceptually ambiguous social cues.
