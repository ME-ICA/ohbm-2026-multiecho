Introduction

Motor impairment after stroke is one of the leading causes of long-term disability and reduced quality of life.1 Motor symptoms are typically accompanied by a variety of neurophysiological changes, e.g., in corticospinal excitability, intra- and interhemispheric interactions of different motor regions as well as alterations in cortical inhibition. However, how these changes interact and contribute to motor outcome remains poorly understood to date. From a neurophysiological perspective, the successful initiation and execution of goal-directed movement relies on preparatory inhibition, i.e., the transient suppression of corticospinal excitability prior to movement initiation.2 It can be measured by eliciting motor evoked potentials (MEPs) using transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) that are typically reduced during movement preparation in comparison to MEPs elicited at rest. Importantly, preparatory inhibition is thought to depend on the modulation of γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA)-ergic activity.3 While changes in GABAergic signaling have frequently been reported, findings in chronic stroke patients remain inconsistent.4 Hence, investigating stroke-related changes in preparatory inhibition may further elucidate the functional role of changes in GABAergic inhibition in motor recovery after stroke. The present study therefore assessed preparatory inhibition in chronic stroke patients and age-matched control subjects using a choice delayed response task.

Methods

38 chronic stroke patients with prior or persisting unilateral hand motor deficit (time since stroke > 6 months) formerly hospitalized at the University Hospital Cologne as well as healthy age-matched control subjects were recruited for the study.  Electromyography (EMG) was recorded from the paretic hand of the patients and the matched hand for control subjects while TMS pulses were applied to the primary motor cortex of the ipsilesional (or matched) hemisphere using a Magstim Bistim 200² stimulator (The Magstim Co. Ltd, Whitland, United Kingdom) equipped with a 70mm-diameter figure-of-eight coil. A well-established choice delayed-response task was adapted from Greenhouse and colleagues5 to ensure feasibility for stroke patients and elderly participants. Participants responded to lateralized visual cues using their left or right index fingers. After an initial arrow cue indicating whether the next target would require a left- or right-hand response, the arrow turned blue or red, indicating execution or inhibition of the prepared motor response, respectively. Across randomized trials, TMS pulses were delivered either at baseline or during the preparatory delay period, with equal distribution across affected and unaffected hand responses, while non-stimulated trials provided behavioral measures. Preparatory inhibition was quantified as the ratio of MEP amplitudes of trials with TMS administered during the delay period divided by MEP amplitudes of trials with TMS administered during baseline.

Results

Significant preparatory inhibition was observed in age-matched healthy controls. In stroke patients, preparatory inhibition varied according to the degree of motor recovery. Patients who had regained near-normal motor function demonstrated levels of preparatory inhibition comparable to that of healthy controls, whereas non-fully recovered stroke patients showed less preparatory inhibition than control subjects. In the control group, the extent of preparatory inhibition correlated with reaction times of both hands, whereas in fully recovered stroke patients, this relationship was restricted to the affected hand. In contrast, patients with persistent motor impairments showed no association between inhibition levels and reaction times.

Conclusion

We found recovery-dependent changes in preparatory inhibition after stroke, in line with the notion that movement-related modulation of corticospinal excitability plays an important role in post-stroke motor control. In particular, we provide evidence for the fact that the capacity to adjust corticospinal excitability during action preparation is impaired in a recovery-dependent manner. Thus, our findings shed novel light on the role of a dynamic modulation of corticospinal inhibition during movement initiation after stroke, which may help to explain previous inconsistent findings on the functional implications of altered GABAergic inhibition. Of note, the current TMS-based approach offers an advantage over several previous methods that often examined GABAergic signaling at rest rather than during voluntary movements, enabling a more physiologically relevant assessment of inhibitory mechanisms during motor preparation post-stroke. In sum, the present findings advance our understanding of the neurophysiological processes underlying post-stroke motor control and may help guide the development of targeted therapeutic strategies to mitigate motor deficits after stroke.
