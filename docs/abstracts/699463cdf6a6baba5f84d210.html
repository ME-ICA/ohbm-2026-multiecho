Introduction

Recent advances in Brain-Computer Interfaces (BCIs) have led to efficient methods for decoding neural activity recorded with electroencephalography (EEG). Among them, Common Spatial Patterns (CSPs) is a reference approach for optimizing spatial filtering and improving the discrimination of neural activity related to different conditions of motor imagery (MI) ^1. Transposing these methods to neurofeedback could provide individualized feedback adaptation to each participant’s physiological modulations. However, this transposition is not straightforward: while BCIs focus on accurate decoding under supervised conditions, neurofeedback requires physiological interpretability and temporal robustness of extracted features to support learning and modulation of targeted activity.

Typical neurofeedback protocols rely on generic spatial filters, such as Laplacian filters or uniform channel selections, which fail to account for interindividual variability ^2. In contrast, CSPs generate participant-specific spatial filters, grounded in each individual’s neural activity. Nevertheless, their sensitivity to noise, artifacts, and non-stationarities poses a challenge for their use in neurofeedback contexts ^3. Moreover, temporal stability and physiological validity of CSP-derived filters are key conditions for providing meaningful feedback and remain to be fully investigated ^4. 

Building on these premises, the present study aims to bridge the gap between BCI and neurofeedback by evaluating the feasibility, robustness, and physiological relevance of CSPs within a neurofeedback framework. Specifically, we examine whether CSPs capture physiologically plausible MI-related activity, how stable extracted features are over time, and whether they can be reliably used for real-time feedback. We seek to identify the conditions under which CSPs can serve as robust and individualized filters for neurofeedback, enabling more effective self-regulation protocols.

Methods

This study analyzed pre‑existing EEG recordings from 20 healthy adult participants (11 female; mean ± SD = 25.8 ± 5.8 years) ^2. EEG activity was recorded from 28 active electrodes during a MI‑based neurofeedback protocol. Participants performed a right-hand MI task, and neurofeedback relied on a Laplacian filter centered on C3, targeting the 8–30 Hz sensorimotor desynchronization. Recordings also included an initial resting-state and a right-hand MI phase, each consisting of 10 trials of 8 seconds presented in pseudo‑random order. During the MI phase, participants were instructed to repeatedly imagine opening and closing their right hand while observing a virtual hand performing the same movement without any coupling to their neural activity. These data served as the calibration phase for the computation of CSPs.

EEG signals were bandpass filtered between 8 and 30 Hz prior to CSP computation. CSPs were computed to extract spatial filters that discriminate between rest and motor imagery, based on the covariance matrices of both conditions and an eigenvalue decomposition that maximizes variance of one condition while minimizing it in the other. To enable neurophysiological interpretation, the resulting filters were transformed into scalp-level activation patterns by projecting them onto the sensor space, providing topographical representations of the neural activity captured by each CSP. For each participant, the six filters corresponding to the most extreme eigenvalues were selected for subsequent analyses. CSPs were calculated both on uncorrected EEG data and on the ‘clean’ EEG data after ocular artifact correction by independent component analysis (ICA).

Two experts, one with experience in the field of BCIs (Expert 1) and the other with experience in human electrophysiology (Expert 2), independently evaluated the CSP patterns for each participant and each correction condition and selected the pattern that they judged to be the most physiologically plausible (Figure 1). Their selections were compared with two automated criteria: the CSP associated with the largest discriminant eigenvalue and the CSP showing the lowest variance during MI.

To enable the use of CSPs in a neurofeedback context, selected CSPs were converted into personalized spatial filters. Filter weights were normalized to combine the local sensitivity of Laplacian filtering with the participant-specific information captured by CSPs.

As a first step to evaluate the effect of CSP‑based filters on neurofeedback performance, we reproduced offline the entire signal‑processing pipeline used during the real‑time neurofeedback session, but using the selected CSP on the pre‑existing data to compute neurofeedback performance for each participant. The EEG signal was band-pass filtered between 8 and 30Hz, and the CSP spatial filter was applied to the resulting signal. Beta-band power was then computed over sliding time windows, following the same procedure as during the online neurofeedback session. Neurofeedback performance was defined as the logarithm of the ratio between online and reference beta power. The sign of this log-ratio was inverted so that positive values indicated a relative decrease in beta power, reflecting effective cortical modulation. This CSP-based neurofeedback performance was compared to the original performance obtained with Laplacian filter on C3 using the same computation pipeline.  

Results

The average topographic maps of the CSPs selected by the experts, as well as their inter-subject variability (standard deviation), showed that the experts consistently identified spatially and physiologically plausible patterns (Figure 2). The level of agreement between experts was substantial, with a Cohen’s kappa coefficient of 0.63 before artifact correction and 0.81 after correction. Artifact removal using ICA therefore improved the physiological coherence of the components selected by both experts.

For 19 out of 20 participants, the CSP associated with the largest discriminant eigenvalue also corresponded to the component exhibiting the lowest variance during the MI phase. This result is consistent with the expected profile of beta‑band desynchronization, characterized by higher variance at rest and reduced variance during MI execution. Although the average topographies derived from automatic procedures were broadly compatible with motor‑related activity, noticeable inter‑subject variability remained even after artifact correction (Figure 2).

Offline analysis of CSP-based neurofeedback performance, including all CSP selection strategies, and comparison with the original Laplacian-based performance showed no significant differences in performance between conditions. Paired t-tests corrected for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR) confirmed the absence of significant differences (all p > 0.1 after FDR correction). At the descriptive level, individual performances varied across participants. The dispersion of neurofeedback performance appeared lower when using expert-based CSPs compared with automatically derived CSPs and traditional Laplacian. 

Conclusion

Expert based CSP selection yielded spatial patterns that were physiologically plausible and displayed reduced inter subject variability compared with automatically selected CSPs. The improvement observed after artifact correction, reflected in both the increased agreement between experts and the clearer physiological plausibility of the selected components, suggests that ocular artifacts introduce substantial variability in spatial patterns when left uncorrected. These findings underline the importance of implementing real time artifact correction to ensure reliable and interpretable feature extraction ^5.

The correspondance between best discriminative CSPs and CSPs with lowest variance during MI is consistent with the expected beta-band desynchronization during MI, characterized by a decrease in signal variance relative to rest. It further indicates that the calibration phase successfully engaged sensorimotor networks in most participants, allowing CSPs to capture physiologically meaningful patterns.

The offline application of expert selected CSPs to simulate neurofeedback performance did not lead to significant improvement in performance compared with the Laplacian filter used during the original training. A plausible explanation is that participants had been trained exclusively with Laplacian based feedback, and the features exploited during the offline evaluation no longer precisely matched those they learned to control. The use of static CSP calibration performed only once at the beginning of the experiment may have further contributed to this mismatch, as participants were just becoming familiar with the MI task at that stage. Consequently, the initial calibration could have captured suboptimal spatial patterns, especially for participants whose neural representations evolved with task practice.

Additional descriptive analysis revealed three participant profiles: those for whom the change in filtering method had negligible impact, those who showed improved modulation with expert based CSPs, and those whose performance decreased relative to their original online (Laplacian-based) performance. Verifying whether the initial calibration quality differed across these subgroups would be interesting to determine whether inter individual differences primarily reflect calibration issues, learning effects, or actual neural variability ^4. This observation supports the idea that continuous or adaptive recalibration of spatial filters may be necessary for maintaining feature relevance throughout training.

Overall, our results highlight two main methodological considerations for the development of CSP-based neurofeedback. First, artifact correction procedures must be integrated in real time to ensure that the extracted features reflect genuine cortical activity. Second, the temporal stability of CSP filters must be continuously assessed, as static calibration may fail to track the evolving neural dynamics of participants. Addressing these points will be critical for the design of robust and physiologically grounded neurofeedback protocols capable of subtending performance improvements.
