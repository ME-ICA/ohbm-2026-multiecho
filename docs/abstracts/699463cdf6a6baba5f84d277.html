Introduction

Resting-state (RS) functional MRI (fMRI) captures coherent brain spatial patterns called Resting State Networks (RSNs), typically composed of grey matter (GM) regions characterised by synchronous Blood Oxygen Level-Dependent (BOLD) fluctuations. Some studies have extended FC analysis to also include white matter (WM) regions. However, the incorporation of WM analysis with BOLD fMRI is challenging due to the limited vasculature and altered haemodynamic response function (HRF) in WM. 

To overcome BOLD-fMRI limitations, recent studies explored Apparent Diffusion Coefficient (ADC)-fMRI, which is mostly independent of vascular response and equally sensitive to neural activity in both GM and WM via neuromorphological coupling1,2. Earlier proof-of-concept RS ADC-fMRI work2 demonstrated higher sensitivity to WM-GM and WM-WM functional connections than BOLD, but was limited by non-uniform sensitivity across WM due to linear diffusion encoding and by partial brain coverage.

In this work, we acquired BOLD and ADC-fMRI resting-state timecourses using whole brain coverage (excluding the Cerebellum) and isotropic (spherical) diffusion encoding1 for ADC-fMRI, allowing uniform sensitivity to microstructure fluctuations across WM. Our first objective is to identify how the RSNs revealed by ADC-fMRI compare to those typically reported in BOLD-fMRI using Independent Component Analysis (ICA) and seed-based analysis. The second objective is to investigate each modality’s sensitivity to WM-GM functional coupling, and to compare the results with structural connectivity (SC). We hypothesize that ADC-fMRI will reveal stronger functional correlations than BOLD between WM tracts and the GM regions (ROIs) to which they are structurally connected. Revealing WM activity with fMRI could potentially uncover the brain’s circuitry by identifying the specific WM pathways that mediate information flow between activated GM regions

Methods

Acquisition/Preprocessing: This study was approved by the ethics committee of the canton of Vaud, Switzerland (CER-VD). All participants provided written informed consent. Healthy participants (n = 15) underwent two RS runs, one with ADC-fMRI and one with BOLD-fMRI in a single session. To later compute ADC timeseries, we acquired dMRI data with alternating b-values of 200 and 1000 s/mm2 . The acquisition and preprocessing of timeseries was performed as described in ¹ and shown in Table 1. ICA cleaning (FSL MELODIC) and motion correction were applied separately to BOLD, dMRI b1 = 200 and b2 = 1000 s/mm2 timeseries. ADC-fMRI timeseries were computed from the dMRI with alternating b-values as -log(DWI1/DWI2)/(b1-b2), which minimised T2-weighting and direct intravascular signal contributions, and thus BOLD contamination 1-2. 

ICA : Group ICA was used to extract RSNs, with 100 components selected for BOLD based on previous literature recommendations. For ADC-fMRI, we allowed FSL to automatically estimate the dimensionality, resulting in 250 components, since this is the first study to apply ICA to this contrast and no prior guidance on an appropriate model order exists. 

Seed-based analysis:

GM ROIs were defined using the Desikan–Killiany atlas (Freesurfer). WM ROIs were derived using the probconnatlas tool 3, which provides spatial probability maps of WM pathways connecting each pair of cortical ROIs, based on tractography from 66 HCP subjects. This yielded over 2,000 pairwise tract probability maps, which were binarised and then grouped by high spatial Dice overlap, ultimately combining them into 66 WM ROIs. This grouping was performed using hierarchical clustering, where the distance metric was defined as 1−Dice score. The seed-based analysis was run using FSL FEAT, from two seeds : the Forceps Minor and the right precentral gyrus. Voxels with absolute (cluster corrected) z-score values above 2.3 were considered significant.

Graph analysis :

ROI-to-ROI FC matrices were computed using Pearson correlations (later Fisher transformed) between 78 GM regions from the Desikan–Killiany atlas, excluding the Cerebellum because it was outside the acquired field of view and the Accumbens area and Entorhinal cortex because they contained fewer than 10 voxels, and the 66 previously defined WM ROIs. Only positive correlations were considered.Following Fisher transformation, a one-sample t-test with Benjamini–Hochberg correction (α = 0.05) was applied to identify significant group-level connections.

For each FC matrix, we applied a threshold defined as mean + 2.3 × SD and binarised the matrix accordingly. This binarisation enabled us to count the number of significant GM–GM, WM–GM, and WM–WM connections for both ADC- and BOLD-fMRI and directly compare them with a paired t-test. We then computed the group-averaged graph. To construct a binary SC matrix, we identified, for each WM tract, all GM ROIs it structurally connects to by tracing back the set of underlying WM pathways used to generate that tract and the corresponding GM ROI pairs they link. This produced a sparse (#WM × #GM) binary SC matrix.

For each non-binarized FC matrix, we computed the mean connectivity between each WM tract and all GM ROIs, resulting in a (#WM × #GM) matrix. To evaluate its correspondence with the SC matrix, we calculated the precision–recall area under the curve (PR-AUC). Higher values indicate stronger agreement between FC and SC. Finally, we correlated the mean connectivity matrix (this time masked by SC) with the mean vascular density of each WM tract. This was obtained by averaging voxel-wise vascular density from the VENAT atlas 4 across all voxels within each tract. Higher correlations indicated that WM tracts with stronger WM–GM functional connectivity also exhibit higher vascular density. The averaged Pearson correlations (r) and goodness of fit (R²) were computed. All statistical tests for comparisons between ADC and BOLD-fMRI were done with paired t-tests.

Results

ICA (Figure 1A): The main RSNs identified by BOLD ICA were also detectable with ADC-fMRI, but spatially disconnected clusters that appeared as one single component in BOLD were often split across multiple components in ADC. For example, the bilateral sensorimotor clusters appeared in the same component in BOLD and in separate ones in ADC-fMRI. 

Seed-based analysis (Figure 1B): Using a seed in the motor cortex, BOLD-fMRI showed widespread GM connectivity within the entire bilateral sensory motor network and expanded even within the dorsal attention network and visual network. In ADC-fMRI, strong connectivity was confined near the seed, with weaker clusters in frontal, supplementary motor area (SMA) and bilateral precentral regions. With a seed in the Forceps Minor, BOLD-fMRI produced widespread positive connectivity across almost the entire brain. In ADC-fMRI, the strongest connectivity remained near the seed, extending to GM regions structurally connected to the tract (such as the superior frontal and caudal middle frontal cortices) and also to some additional regions such as the somatosensory and motor cortices.

FC graph (Figure 1C):

In FC graphs, ADC-fMRI yielded substantially more significant WM–WM connections (58 vs. 15 on average; p < 0.001) and WM–GM connections (155 vs. 54; p < 0.001) than BOLD-fMRI, whereas BOLD showed more GM–GM connectivity (196 vs. 100; p < 0.001), consistent with previous findings².

Some interhemispheric GM-GM connections (such as those connecting the left and right motor cortex GM ROIs) present in the BOLD FC graph were much weaker or not present in the ADC FC graph. Focusing on WM–GM connections, the averaged BOLD graph revealed that some WM tracts (such as the Forceps Minor investigated previously) are strongly connected to many GM ROIs, while others show no connections at all. In contrast, in the averaged ADC-fMRI graph, every WM tract connects to at least one GM ROI. Visually, the ADC-fMRI graph aligns more closely with the SC graph than the BOLD-fMRI graph.

The AUC analysis confirmed this observation: ADC-fMRI yielded significantly higher PR-AUC values than BOLD-fMRI (0.29 vs 0.12, p < 0.001), indicating a better correspondence between SC and ADC-based FC. Conversely, correlations between averaged WM-GM FC and vascular density were significantly stronger for BOLD-fMRI than for ADC-fMRI (r = 0.36, R² = 0.12 vs. r = 0.01, R² = 0.01; p < 0.001). So, in BOLD, more vascularised WM tracts had higher correlations to GM ROIs. 

Conclusion

The spatial overlap between ADC and BOLD ICA components confirms that ADC-fMRI captures meaningful RSNs. However, the more fragmented ADC ICA clusters likely reflect its lower SNR, which also contributes to the lower z-scores observed in ADC seed-based analyses compared with BOLD-fMRI. Alternatively, the more localised ADC ICA clusters may indicate that long-range BOLD clusters driven by vascular synchrony do not necessarily reflect neural coupling.

When using the right precentral gyrus as a seed, ADC-fMRI produced significant clusters in expected regions (such as the bilateral precentral gyrus and the SMA) indicating that genuine functional connectivity was captured despite the reduced SNR.

When using the Forceps Minor as a WM seed, the brain-wide positive connectivity observed in BOLD-fMRI highlights its limited sensitivity to information flow in WM and thus strong sensitivity to physiological noise in those areas. The BOLD WM timeseries was indeed likely influenced by CSF pulsation and respiratory fluctuations (despite thorough ICA cleaning procedures in the preprocessing), producing artificially high correlations across much of the brain. Regressing the global signal could be considered to help reduce such false positives, but it may also introduce negative correlations, whose interpretation remains debated. In contrast, when seeding the same WM region, ADC-fMRI produced significant clusters primarily in GM areas that are structurally connected to the seed (such as the superior frontal cortex), as well as in GM regions linked to WM tracts that overlap with the seeded tract (such as the somatosensory cortex). This suggests a more selective sensitivity to true WM–GM connectivity.

The subsequent graph analysis allowed us to reproduce previous findings from ², which indicated a higher number of significant WM-GM and WM-WM connections in ADC-fMRI than in BOLD. The stronger correspondence between ADC-fMRI and SC observed in this study represents the first evidence that an alternative fMRI contrast can outperform BOLD in structure–function coupling.

In BOLD-fMRI, certain WM tracts displayed generally higher correlations with GM ROIs, while others showed very little GM connectivity, an imbalance also reported in earlier work ⁵. This WM-GM connectivity appears to depend on the vascularisation of the WM tract, with higher vascular density associated with stronger correlations with connecting GM, while this was not the case for ADC-fMRI. This effect may partly reflect partial‐volume contamination, where some WM tracts include adjacent GM tissue that is more densely vascularised, so the results should be interpreted with caution. It is important to note that this analysis focused specifically on WM–GM connectivity and has not yet been extended to GM–GM connectivity, which is more commonly studied.

This novel ADC-fMRI approach for resting-state analysis is not meant to replace BOLD-fMRI, but rather to serve as a complementary technique as it offers a different view on connectivity. This study demonstrates its potential for probing WM activity (which seemed to exceed the sensitivity of BOLD) while also highlighting its current limitations, particularly the lower SNR that complicates voxel-wise analyses such as ICA or seed-based mapping. Despite this lower SNR, ADC still detected neural activity in expected regions, and perhaps with fewer false positives than BOLD. By averaging timeseries within each ROI, we were able to improve the SNR and obtain very promising results in the graph analysis.
