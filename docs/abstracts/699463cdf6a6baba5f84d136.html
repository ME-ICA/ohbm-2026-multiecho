Introduction

Diffusional kurtosis imaging1,2 (DKI) is a prominent example of a multiple b-shell, multiple diffusion encoding direction method. Developed as an extension of DTI, where the diffusion process is assumed to deviate away from standard Brownian motion, the method provides a means to quantify the extent of such deviation via the kurtosis metric. This elegant mathematical improvement has significantly broadened the applicability of diffusion-MRI in clinical settings. The kurtosis metric estimated from DKI has shown great promise in applications, including grading hepatocellular carcinoma, prognosing chronic kidney disease, differentiating parotid gland tumours, measuring response to radiotherapy treatment in bone tumour and glioblastoma, identifying tissue abnormalities in temporal lobe epilepsy patients with sleep disorders and brain microstructural changes in mild traumatic brain injury, predicting breast cancer metastasis and diagnosing Parkinson’s disease.  

However, the requirement on the maximum b-value not exceeding 3000 s/mm2 for the standard DKI has hindered its full potential in characterising tissue structure. In this study, we develop an elegant new mathematical extension of DKI to enable the estimation of diffusion and kurtosis tensors from diffusion MRI data with high b-values (>3000 s/mm2), such as those collected on the state-of-the-art Connectome scanners. We evaluate the quality of fit and compare estimates between the proposed and traditional DKI methods.  

Methods

Data: In this study, we use the open-source MGH Connectome Diffusion Microstructure Dataset3 (CDMD) for 26 healthy participants. These 2×2×2mm3 resolution diffusion weighted images were collected at two diffusion times (19 and 49 ms) with a pulse duration 8 ms and eight gradient strengths linearly spaced between 30 and 290 mT/m, generating sixteen b-values (eight for each diffusion time) ranging from 50 to 17800 s/mm2 with 32 or 64 uniformly distributed directions per shell. For diffusion time Δ=19ms, b-values are 50, 350, 800, 1500, 2400, 3450, 4750, 6000 s/mm2. For diffusion time Δ=49ms, b-values are 200, 950, 2300, 4250, 6750, 9850, 13500, 17800 s/mm2. Total acquisition time for the diffusion MRI protocol was 55 minutes per participant.

Newly proposed DKI model: Mathematically, the newly proposed DKI model can be considered a generalisation of the approach for computing mean kurtosis derived for directionally-averaged dMRI signals as in Yang et al4. Whereas that approach demonstrated the improvements in tissue contrast that could be achieved for directionally averaged signals, the present work generalises to tensor-based metrics from full directional data. The optimal b-value sampling and two diffusion-time fitting strategy for the new DKI model can be found in Farquhar et al5. 

Diffusion model fitting: Diffusion and kurtosis tensors were estimated from the CDMD dataset in the following three cases: 

(i) the traditional DKI model was fitted to dMRI data with b-value = [0, 950, 2300] s/mm2 at Δ=49ms;

(ii) the newly proposed DKI model was fitted to dMRI data with all the b-values in the CDMD dataset at both diffusion times; 

(iii) the newly proposed DKI model was fitted to dMRI data with optimal b-value sampling, i.e., two b-values from each diffusion time, b-value = [350, 4750] s/mm2 with Δ=19ms and b-value = [950, 6750] s/mm2 with Δ=49ms. 

Mean, axial and radial kurtosis and diffusivity metrics were then computed from the estimated tensors for each case. 

Results

In Figure 1, we present parameter maps of the brain obtained from the three model fitting cases outlined in the Method section, including mean, axial and radial kurtosis and diffusivity, respectively. The diffusion and kurtosis tensor metrics derived from our newly proposed DKI model involving high b-value data from two diffusion times provide remarkably superior tissue contrast compared to the traditional DKI metrics (row A compared to row C). In addition, applying the optimal b-value sampling and fitting strategy proposed in Farquhar et al5, the quality of the kurtosis and diffusivity maps obtained using 2 b-values from each diffusion time (row B) is comparable to those obtained using all the b-values (row A), but with only a quarter of the original acquisition time. 

Conclusion

We extend the traditional DKI to enable the estimation of diffusion and kurtosis tensors from dMRI data involving large b-values (> 3,000 s/mm2). We showcase the performance of our new DKI model using the MGH Connectome diffusion MRI dataset with b-values up to 17800 s/mm2. Kurtosis and diffusion metrics estimated from our new DKI model provide superior contrast compared to the traditional DKI. Metrics from our method have the potential to become more sensitive and more specific imaging markers for characterising healthy and diseased brain tissue microstructure. Our proposed new DKI model and optimal b-value sampling strategy will benefit both researchers and clinicians in the neuroimaging community. Future research will see the application of our new DKI model to characterise various brain diseases and disorders.
