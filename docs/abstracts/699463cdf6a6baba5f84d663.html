Introduction

   Individuals differ widely in how intensely they experience pain, even when nociceptive input is held constant, suggesting contributions from central pain-processing mechanisms. Pain catastrophizing, defined as threat appraisal and persistent negative focus on pain, is a robust predictor of heightened pain [1].

    As a core element of the fear-avoidance model, catastrophizing is theorized to heighten threat perception, increase vigilance, and promote behavioral inhibition [2], yet its neural mechanisms remain unclear. Prior neuroimaging studies report associations between catastrophizing and functional connectivity within large-scale networks supporting salience detection and self-referential processing, including regions of the default mode network (DMN). Such variability may reflect differences in disease state and uncontrolled fluctuations in pain intensity, making it difficult to isolate trait-related contributions of catastrophizing. Moreover, theoretical accounts propose that catastrophizing may alter not only overall pain appraisal but also the way the brain responds as pain intensity increases, motivating examination of a PCS × pain interaction.

    Experimentally controlled sustained-pain paradigms help address this issue. Topical capsaicin induces a slowly developing, stable burning pain, allowing sensory input to be standardized across participants. In this study, we combined this sustained-pain model with seed-based resting-state analyses to examine how catastrophizing shapes intrinsic functional connectivity during ongoing pain. We focused on regions involved in sensory–affective appraisal and self-referential processing, including S1, S2, the insula, and the posterior cingulate cortex—areas consistently linked to sensory encoding, affective evaluation, and internally oriented attention relevant to catastrophizing [3].Our aim was to identify catastrophizing-related connectivity patterns and assess its influence on the pain–connectivity relationship.

Methods

Participants

    Sixty-nine healthy adults were initially recruited for a resting-state fMRI experiment using an experimentally controlled sustained-pain paradigm. Inclusion criteria were: age between 18 and 40 years, right-handedness, and ability to provide written informed consent. Exclusion criteria included current pain due to injury or other causes, known capsaicin allergy, pregnancy or possible pregnancy, use of analgesics or medications affecting the central nervous system, history or current diagnosis of neurological or psychiatric disorders, MRI contraindications, and severe claustrophobia. After data acquisition, nine participants were excluded due to excessive head motion (> 2.0 mm ), resulting in a final sample of 60 participants. All procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Graduate School of Medicine, Tohoku University, and written informed consent was obtained in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki

Sustained-pain induction and behavioral measures

    Sustained pain was induced using a 1% capsaicin cream. A volume of 50 µL was applied to a 9-mm diameter plastic disc and affixed to the left L5 dermatome, located one-third of the distance between the lateral femoral epicondyle and the lateral malleolus. This procedure produces a gradually intensifying burning sensation that reaches a distinct peak at approximately 50–55 minutes after application and then remains stable, as reported in prior capsaicin studies. MRI scanning commenced 50 minutes after application, corresponding to the onset of the plateau phase of ongoing pain. Subjective pain intensity was rated using a 100-mm Visual Analog Scale (VAS; 0 = “no pain,” 100 = “worst imaginable pain”). Ratings obtained before and after scanning were used to compute the area under the curve (AUC) as an index of cumulative pain. Pain catastrophizing was assessed using the Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS), administered approximately 50 minutes after capsaicin application, immediately before scanning.

MRI acquisition and preprocessing

    Resting-state fMRI data from 60 participants were acquired during a 10-minute scan on a Philips Achieva dStream 3.0T scanner (TR = 2 s, 40 axial slices, foot-to-head acquisition).

    Standard preprocessing was conducted using the DPABI toolbox (based on SPM), including slice-timing correction, realignment, normalization to MNI space, and spatial smoothing with a 6-mm FWHM Gaussian kernel. Denoising procedures included nuisance regression (motion and physiological confounds), detrending, and temporal filtering.

Seed definition and connectivity analysis

   To characterize how PCS and pain intensity jointly shape intrinsic connectivity under sustained pain, we conducted seed-to-voxel analyses using a priori regions implicated in sensory–affective and self-referential aspects of pain processing. Seeds included the primary (S1) and secondary (S2) somatosensory cortices, insula and posterior cingulate cortex (PCC), each defined as a 4–6 mm spherical ROI centered on peak MNI coordinates reported in prior neuroimaging studies [4]. For each seed, individual whole-brain rsFC maps were entered into a multiple regression model with PCS scores, pain intensity (AUC), and their interaction term (PCS × pain) as predictors, controlling for age and sex. Group-level results were thresholded at voxel-level p < 0.001 (uncorrected) and cluster-level family-wise error (FWE) correction at p < 0.05; only FWE-corrected clusters were interpreted.

Results

To examine how catastrophizing and pain intensity modulate intrinsic functional connectivity during sustained pain, we conducted seed-to-voxel analyses using four a priori seeds (S1, S2, insula, PCC). Three significant effects emerged across these seeds: (1) a positive main effect of PCS for the S2 seed, (2) a negative main effect of PCS for the PCC seed, and (3) a significant PCS × pain interaction also involving the PCC seed.

1.    S2 seed — Effect of PCS (positive direction).

    A significant cluster was identified in the medial prefrontal/anterior cingulate cortex (ACC/mPFC). The peak voxel was located at MNI = [–8, 23, 21], T = 5.68, k = 50, pFWE = 0.01 (Figure 1A). Higher PCS scores were associated with stronger S2–ACC/mPFC connectivity during sustained pain.

2.    PCC seed — Effect of PCS (negative direction).

    A significant negative association with PCS was observed in the visual association cortex (BA19). The peak coordinate was MNI = [11, –84, 38], T = 4.34, k = 51, pFWE = 0.001 (Figure 1B). Individuals with higher PCS showed reduced PCC–BA19 connectivity.

3.    PCC seed — PCS × pain intensity interaction.

    A significant interaction effect emerged in the supplementary motor area (SMA), extending into the medial superior frontal gyrus. The peak coordinate was MNI = [0, –4, 63], T = 4.34, k = 79, pFWE = 0.001 (Figure 2). The association between PCC connectivity and pain intensity differed as a function of PCS, reflecting reduced PCC–SMA coupling under higher catastrophizing.

    For visualization, interaction patterns were plotted using median-split PCS groups to illustrate differential pain–rsFC slopes (Figure 2).

Non-significant seeds.

No clusters survived FWE correction for the S1 or insula seeds.

Conclusion

  The present study demonstrates that pain catastrophizing is associated with three dissociable patterns of intrinsic functional connectivity during sustained pain. The three contrasts were informed by the distinct functional roles of the seed regions and the theoretical accounts of catastrophizing. Specifically, S2 was selected to test the hypothesis that catastrophizing amplifies sensory–affective appraisal of nociceptive input. The PCC was selected to examine the hypothesis that catastrophizing alters internally oriented and self-referential processing during pain. For the interaction contrast, PCC connectivity was further evaluated across varying pain levels because catastrophizing is theorized to modulate how perceived pain intensity influences downstream behavioral tendencies such as vigilance or avoidance. The whole-brain clusters identified for each contrast were interpreted within this seed-level framework, providing a coherent basis for understanding how catastrophizing may affect multiple layers of pain processing beyond subjective appraisal alone. Together, these hypotheses guided the interpretation of the results, beginning with the effects observed for the S2 seed.

    First, higher PCS scores were associated with increased S2–ACC/mPFC connectivity, indicating amplified sensory–affective appraisal of nociceptive input. Because the ACC/mPFC is central to threat evaluation, emotional salience, and expectancy-related modulation of pain, stronger coupling with S2 may reflect heightened vigilance and affective engagement in high catastrophizers. This pattern is consistent with prior work showing increased connectivity within affective components of the pain matrix, including the ACC and insula [4], potentially biasing individuals toward interpreting pain signals as more threatening.

    Second, PCS was negatively associated with PCC–BA19 connectivity, suggesting reduced integration between self-referential and visual associative systems. As a core hub of the default mode network, the PCC supports internal mentation and self-evaluative processes. Weakened PCC–BA19 coupling indicates a shift from externally oriented perceptual processing toward internally focused attention, consistent with rumination, negative self-focus, and reduced sensory engagement in high catastrophizers [5]. Similar PCC-centered disruptions have been reported in maladaptive pain-related cognition, suggesting attentional resources may be biased toward internal evaluative states at the cost of contextual sensory information.

    Third, the PCS × pain interaction in PCC–SMA connectivity indicates that catastrophizing modulates how perceived pain intensity relates to motor-preparatory engagement. Because the SMA supports action planning and adaptive behavioral responses, reduced PCC–SMA coupling in individuals with higher PCS may reflect diminished readiness for action or behavioral withdrawal, aligning with avoidance tendencies described in the fear-avoidance model.

    Taken together, these three connectivity alterations map onto key components of the fear-avoidance framework: enhanced S2–ACC/mPFC connectivity reflects intensified affective appraisal during early threat processing; reduced PCC–BA19 coupling corresponds to increased internalized and ruminative attention; and attenuated PCC–SMA connectivity aligns with reduced motor-preparatory engagement characteristic of avoidance behavior. By examining sustained pain within a standardized experimental context, this study minimizes confounds from clinical heterogeneity and pain severity, allowing trait-related neural tendencies associated with catastrophizing to be more clearly observed. Although emerging from distinct functional domains, these patterns collectively offer convergent neural evidence for how catastrophizing may increase vulnerability to persistent pain and may guide future investigations into mechanisms of pain maintenance.
