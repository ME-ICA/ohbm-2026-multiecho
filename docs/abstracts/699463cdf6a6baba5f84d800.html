Introduction

Early childhood socioemotional development exhibits marked individual differences, stemming largely from variations in the early caregiving environment.^1 A core mechanism through which the environment exerts its influence is its dynamic interplay with the developing brain. However, current developmental neuroimaging studies aiming to elucidate this interactive mechanism remain methodologically constrained, primarily by a reliance on single imaging modalities (e.g., structural or functional), or in the lack of systematic comparison of differential effects imposed by environmental valence (e.g., positive or negative). These limitations restrict our understanding of how early brain development underlies adaptive responses. 

To address this gap, this study utilized precious multimodal data from the Chinese Baby Connectome Project (CBCP) cohort.^2 Our analysis systematically integrated neuroimaging indices of brain structure (T1w MRI) and functional connectivity (FC, derived from resting-state functional MRI), combined with rigorous latent variable environmental measures, to construct moderation models using Structural Equation Modeling (SEM). The aim was to elucidate how these brain measures conditionally regulate the environment-development link. A differential neural moderation was found: 1) structural metrics dampened environmental influence on socioemotional outcomes across positive and negative valence, while 2) FC potentiated adversity's negative impact. This provides an integrative framework for understanding how early environments shape socioemotional development through differentiated neural pathways.

Methods

This study included 142 participants (aged 36–72 months, M: 76, F: 66) from the CBCP cohort with T1w MRI, of whom 104 also had functional MRI-derived FC data, all acquired from a 3.0T MRI system (uMR890, United Imaging). T1w images were processed using an infant-dedicated pipeline,^3 yielding morphological metrics including cortical thickness (ThickAvg), surface area (SurfArea), and gray matter volume (GrayVol). FC data of 100 parcels (Schaefer’s atlas) were derived based on infant-specific functional MRI preprocessing pipeline,^4 and  both intra- and inter-subnetwork mean FC strength were derived according to Yeo’s seven subnetwork template. 

The early caregiving environment was assessed using a battery of the questionnaires covering dimensions of parent-child interaction quality, parental psychological state, and family environment characteristics,^5 resulting in 15 indicators. Missing data were handled using multiple imputation. Subsequently, these 15 indicators were analyzed using Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) to establish their hierarchical relationship (CFI = 0.951, TLI = 0.937, RMSEA = 0.060). It generated four first-order factors: Proactive Caregiver Support (Supp), Enriching Input Provision (EnrichIn), Internal Emotional Burden (EmoBurd), and Adverse Parenting Tendency (AdvParent); and two second-order latent variables: Positive Environment (Supp + EnrichIn) and Negative Environment (EmoBurd + AdvParent). The first-order factor scores were used for subsequent analyses. Infant socioemotional development was assessed using the Ages & Stages Questionnaires: Social-Emotional, 2nd Edition, with raw scores standardized by age group into Z-scores (ASQz), with higher scores indicating greater socioemotional difficulty. 

A series of SEM moderation models were constructed in R using the lavaan package to examine how brain metrics conditionally moderate the environmental impact on socioemotional development. For significant brain-environment interaction terms (p < 0.05), simple slope analyses were conducted, estimating the predictive effect of environmental factors on ASQz at low (-1 SD), medium (0 SD), and high (+1 SD) levels of the brain metrics (both morphological and FC).

Results

The moderation model analyses revealed a rich pattern of significant brain-environment interactions, identifying 50 significant brain structure-environment and 4 significant FC-environment interactions. These interactions exhibited a systematic crosstalk between environmental valence (Positive or Negative Environment) and the brain measures toward socioemotional development outcomes. Specifically, 1) positive environment moderating effects primarily clustered in brain regions associated with emotion regulation (e.g., anterior cingulate cortex, insula, parahippocampus) and visuomotor areas (Fig. 1a, rows 1/3); and 2) Negative environment moderating effects were significantly enriched in regions and FC associated with self-recognition and cognitive control (e.g., the default mode network and anterior cingulate cortex, ACC) (Fig. 1a, rows 2/4). 

Further analysis of specific brain regions revealed diverse moderating patterns. For instance, ThickAvg of the left ACC consistently dampened the regulatory effects of the environment on socioemotional outcomes across environments. In the positive environment (Supp), its significant positive interaction term (β = 0.1526, p = 0.0489) indicated a significant moderation of Supp's protective effect. Simple slope analysis showed that as ACC’s ThickAvg increased, the strength of this protective effect attenuated (low: β = -0.5153, p < 0.001; medium: β = -0.3626, p < 0.001; high: β = -0.2100, p = 0.0433). Conversely, in the negative environment (EmoBurd), its significant negative interaction term (β = -0.1619, p = 0.0155) demonstrated a resilience effect: the positive predictive effect of environmental risk on ASQz may be significantly blunted with increasing ACC’s ThickAvg (low: β = 0.6315, p < 0.001; medium: β = 0.4696, p < 0.001; high: β = 0.3077, p = 0.0031), suggesting that greater neural structural reserve may buffer the negative impact of internal emotional burden (Fig. 1c). The left parahippocampal cortex demonstrated broad environmental sensitivity, with its SurfArea metric reaching statistical significance across several environments (Supp: β = -0.2290, p = 0.0064; EnrichIn: β = -0.2441, p = 0.0033; EmoBurd: β = 0.2071, p = 0.0068). Notably, its ThickAvg and SurfArea exhibited oppositely directed moderating effects within the same environment (e.g., EmoBurd; ThickAvg interaction β = -0.1721, p = 0.0111; SurfArea interaction β = 0.2071, p = 0.0068), indicating that different structural dimensions within the same brain region assume distinct regulatory functions (Fig. 1d). 

In contrast to this structural complexity, FC demonstrated a consistent risk amplification effect in adverse environments (Fig. 1b). For example, within-default mode network connectivity under the AdvParent environment, with its significant positive interaction term (β = 0.1810, p = 0.0468), showed a gradient enhancement of the environmental risk's negative impact on socioemotional outcomes with increasing connectivity strength (low: β = 0.2960, p = 0.0120; medium: β = 0.4770, p < 0.001; high: β = 0.6580, p < 0.001). Furthermore, connectivity between the Default Mode Network and other functional subnetworks, such as Limbic Network and Frontoparietal Control Network, exhibited a consistent pattern of risk amplification under the AdvParent environment. These findings collectively support the central role of brain functional networks as "vulnerability amplifiers" (Fig. 1e).

Conclusion

By conducting structural and functional moderation analyses, this study systematically elucidated the differential roles that might be played by infant brain structure and functional networks in the process through which early environments shape socioemotional development. Brain structural features appeared to exhibit a dual-mode adaptation based on a neural structural reserve, reflecting relatively high structural resilience. In the negative environment, this reserve may be recruited as a "risk-buffering reserve," partially mitigating environmental harm. Conversely, in the positive environment, it may function as a "gain-regulating reserve," where its higher levels were paradoxically associated with a blunting of the environmental protective effect. This suggests that mature structure may maintain response efficiency by reducing environmental sensitivity to routine supportive input. This capacity to switch functional modes contingent upon environmental context may reflect a highly adaptive, conditional regulatory capacity of brain structure, grounded in fine-grained functional specialization among distinct structural dimensions. By comparison, large-scale FC tended to act as a "negative risk amplifier." This suggests that high FC strength, while signifying processing efficiency, may lead to an excessive or sustained neural response to negative environmental input, thus further potentiating the adverse impact of environmental risk on socioemotional problems. This differential finding of structural dampening versus functional potentiation reveals that in the environment-behavior relationship, the developing brain may rely on multi-level, multi-metric structural features to provide a foundation for plasticity and conditional adaptation, whereas functional networks could reflect an increased susceptibility to environmental risk. This finding advances our multimodal understanding of early brain-environment interaction and implies important potential for differentiated intervention strategies targeting distinct neural substrates.
