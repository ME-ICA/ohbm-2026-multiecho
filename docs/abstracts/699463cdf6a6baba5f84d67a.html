Introduction

Aging is accompanied by declines in cognitive function and motor coordination, particularly during dual-task situations that require simultaneous cognitive and motor processing. Impairments in dual-task performance are also among the earliest behavioral markers of age-related and neurodegenerative processes. Prior neuroimaging work on cognitive-motor dual-tasking has shown that interference arises from limits in processing capacity, constraints on response selection, and the need to coordinate activity across distributed large-scale brain networks, suggesting the involvement of systems-level control mechanisms.1 

A key systems-level mechanism underlying these impairments may involve the degradation of ascending neuromodulatory pathways. The locus coeruleus (LC) and nucleus basalis of Meynert (nBM), as the central noradrenergic and cholinergic nuclei, regulate several cognitive functions via their widespread cortical and subcortical projections, and show early vulnerability to age-related degeneration and neurodegenerative disorders.2 Emerging evidence suggests that structural connectivity between LC and nBM shapes large-scale dynamics of functional brain networks, modulating the flexibility of transitions between integrated and segregated brain states that are essential for cognitive-motor coordination.3

We therefore examined whether LC-nBM structural and functional connectivity differs between younger and older adults, and whether inter-individual variability in connectivity relates to cognitive and motor dual-task costs. We hypothesized that LC-nBM connectivity would decline with age and would be associated with dual-task costs.

Methods

We examined 62 healthy adults, comprising 42 older adults (age > 50 years; mean ± SD = 66.7 ± 7.90; M/F = 20/22) and 20 younger adults (age 20-40 years; 28.0 ± 4.87; M/F = 12/8). All participants were right-handed and had no neurological or psychiatric disorders.

The cognitive-motor dual-task paradigm consisted of three fMRI task runs: a cognitive single-task (Go/NoGo), a motor single-task (alternating pedal presses using an MRI-compatible pedal device), and a combined cognitive-motor dual-task. During the dual-task, participants performed the Go/NoGo task while pressing the pedals in alternating sequence to simulate stepping. Reaction times were recorded for Go and stepping trials. Cognitive and motor dual-task costs were computed as the percent increase in reaction time during dual-task compared to the respective single-task baseline.

To quantify LC-nBM structural and functional connectivity in relation to dual-task costs, we used a diffusion-weighted MRI (dMRI; TR/TE = 3247/79 ms, phase-encoding direction = AP, multiband factor = 3, voxel size = 1.5 mm isotropic, b-values = 300/700/2000 s/mm2 with 8/32/64 directions, interspersed with 13 b0 volumes), and a resting-state fMRI (rsfMRI; TR/TE = 850/30 ms, multiband factor = 4, voxel size = 2.5 mm isotropic, 560 volumes). In addition, a high-resolution T1-weighted anatomical scan was collected (TR/TE = 2000/2.07 ms, voxel size = 0.75 mm isotropic), and reverse phase-encoded b0 and dual-echo GRE fieldmap images (phase and magnitude) were acquired for susceptibility distortion correction in dMRI and rsfMRI.

The LC was defined using the Dahl et al.4 meta-mask (https://osf.io/sf2ky/), and the nBM using the Zaborszky et al.5 cytoarchitectonic atlas (thresholded at 40%). dMRI preprocessing and tractography were performed with QSIprep 0.22.0 (https://qsiprep.readthedocs.io/), and MRtrix (https://www.mrtrix.org/). Whole-brain anatomically constrained tractography was performed with 50 million streamlines. LC and nBM ROIs were transformed into dMRI space and used to generate 15000 streamlines connecting LC and nBM per hemisphere (Figure 1). After filtering by median length (7450 streamlines retained), LC-nBM streamlines were merged with the whole-brain tractogram and refined using SIFT2. The SIFT2-weighted sums of LC-nBM streamlines in each hemisphere were used as the structural connectivity metric (left, right, and their summed value as total). rsfMRI data were processed with fMRIprep 25.0.0 (https://fmriprep.org/) and XCP-D 0.10.7 (http://xcp-d.readthedocs.io/), including regression of six motion parameters and their derivatives, five white matter and five cerebrospinal fluid aCompCor components, no smoothing, 0.008 - 0.09 Hz filtering, and censoring at frame-wise displacement > 0.5 mm. Functional connectivity was defined as the Fisher-z-transformed correlation between LC and nBM time series in each hemisphere; a combined LC and a combined nBM mask were used to yield a total functional connectivity measure.

We assessed age-related changes in LC-nBM structural and functional connectivity by fitting linear, quadratic, and cubic age models to each connectivity measure. Next, to examine the behavioral relevance of LC-nBM connectivity, we used robust linear models in which the cognitive and motor dual-task costs were entered as outcomes, and structural and functional connectivity (left, right, and total) as predictors, adjusting age and sex as covariates. False-discovery-rate (FDR)-corrected p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

After preprocessing and quality control, the final sample included 33 older (66.42 ± 8.28 years; M/F = 13/20) and 17 younger adults (27.41 ± 4.19 years; M/F = 10/7). The two groups did not differ by sex (p = 0.314).

Older adults had significantly lower cognitive dual-task cost (mean % ± SD = 16.9 ± 16.5 vs. 31.7 ± 21.2; p = 0.019) and higher motor dual-task cost (median % [IQR] = 43.5 [31.3-60.7] vs. 31.9 [19.9-42.4], p = 0.026) compared to younger adults. In single-task performance, older adults exhibited slower cognitive reaction times (median [IQR] = 0.52 [0.46-0.58] s vs. 0.45 [0.41-0.48] s, p = 0.006). There was no significant difference in the single motor task between older and younger adults (mean seconds (SD) = 0.32 (0.07) vs. 0.32 (0.07), p = 0.96).

Total structural LC-nBM connectivity exhibited significant nonlinear age effects, with quadratic models outperforming linear models for left (p = 0.014) and total (p = 0.008), while cubic terms provided no additional improvement (Figure 2A). No age-related linear or non-linear effects were observed for functional connectivity.
Higher total structural LC-nBM connectivity was significantly associated with cognitive dual-task cost after adjustment for age and sex (estimate [SE] = 0.0062 [0.0020], FDR-corrected p = 0.032). Associations with right- and left-sided structural connectivity did not survive FDR correction. No significant associations were found between LC-nBM connectivity and motor dual-task cost, nor for any functional connectivity measures (all corrected p > 0.18). In exploratory age-stratified analyses, the association between total structural LC-nBM connectivity and cognitive dual-task cost was present in older adults (estimate [SE] = 0.0046 [0.0018], p = 0.015; Figure 2B), but not in younger adults (estimate [SE] = 0.0022 [0.0050], p = 0.662).

Conclusion

Together, these findings extend prior work on LC-nBM interactions by linking their structural connectivity to cognitive-motor dual-task performance in healthy aging. Jacobs et al. reported curvilinear age-related trajectories in LC-nBM functional connectivity, with higher LC-nBM functional coupling associated with poorer memory performance in old age.2 In contrast, we observed no significant age-related effects in LC-nBM functional connectivity, but detected a quadratic nonlinear age effect in structural LC-nBM connectivity. This discrepancy may partly reflect differences in age-range composition between studies, as Jacobs et al. also included a broader lifespan sample that included middle-aged adults, whereas our cohort did not include subjects aged 40 to 50, potentially reducing sensitivity to midlife functional connectivity changes. This dissociation also further suggests that age-related reorganization of noradrenergic-cholinergic connectivity may be more robustly captured at the anatomical level, whereas resting-state functional coupling may be more state-dependent.

Importantly, higher structural LC-nBM connectivity, specifically in older adults, was associated with greater cognitive dual-task cost, indicating that increased coupling does not necessarily confer behavioral benefit. This finding aligns with those of Jacobs et al., in which elevated LC-nBM coupling in older adults reflects inefficient or dysregulated neuromodulatory control rather than preserved function. Consistent with recent work by Taylor et al.3, strong structural connectivity between the LC and the basal forebrain may stabilize large-scale network dynamics following phasic basal forebrain activity, potentially limiting the flexibility required for rapid cognitive-motor integration and dual-task switching. In a Go/NoGo dual-task context, such stabilization may contribute to increased dual-task interference.

Collectively, our results support a model in which age-related nonlinear alterations in LC-nBM structural connectivity reflect reduced neuromodulatory flexibility of the brain, potentially constraining cognitive-motor integration during dual-task performance.
