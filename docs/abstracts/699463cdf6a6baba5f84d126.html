Introduction

Using resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI), we can profile highly organized intrinsic resting state networks (RSNs), enabling the identification of functional connector hubs, crucial brain regions responsible for long-range and inter-network communication1. In epilepsy, numerous studies have demonstrated that these functional hubs are involved in large-scale functional network alterations in regions associated with the epilepsy focus2. Complementary to this method, diffusion weighted imaging (DWI) provides insights regarding the integrity of the white matter pathways in the brain, which have been shown to be structurally altered within and beyond the epilepsy focus3. Building upon these approaches, the present study aims to map whole-brain functional connector hub reorganization and examine the associated structural changes in these regions in temporal lobe epilepsy.

Methods

We studied 25 patients with temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) and 48 healthy controls (HC) who underwent the same 3T rs-fMRI and DWI acquisitions at the Montreal Neurological Institute and Hospital. All scans were preprocessed using micapipe v0.2.34. We performed connector hub mapping at a subject level using SParsity-Based Analysis of Reliable k-hubness (SPARK), an open-source data-driven sparse modeling approach developed by our research group5. For each voxel, using a sparse decomposition, we counted the number of RSNs found within it, generating a discrete voxel-level hubness map with values ranging between 1 to 6 (where values > 2 are considered connector hubs), while identifying the specific RSNs contributing to each voxel. Volumetric hub maps were mapped to the cortical surface, z-scored relative to controls, and sorted by seizure focus lateralization. Age and sex were regressed out using a fixed-effects model. Group differences between TLE and healthy controls were assessed using a vertex-wise t-test and corrected for multiple comparisons, and were quantified across three domains: (1) along the cortical surface (Random-Field Theory (RFT),  p < 0.05), (2) a voxel-level average of subcortical structures (false discovery rate, p < 0.05), and (3) along the hippocampal surface (RFT, p < 0.05). Using the resulting significant cluster of hub reorganization, we quantified the involvement of individual RSNs by measuring the spatial overlap between each RSN and the cluster to contextualize the widespread cortical disruption. Finally, fractional anisotropy (FA), which measures the degree of anisotropic diffusion within the tissue, and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC), measuring the mean molecular diffusion rate within tissues, maps derived from DWI data were used to examine structural correlates of hub alterations. We first modeled and removed the effects of FA and ADC on hubness at each vertex. We then assessed the functional-structural coupling between hubness and diffusion measures within the significant cluster, ipsilateral hippocampus and their corresponding contralateral homologues (p<0.05, spin testing).

Results

We report widespread decreases in hubness in TLE when compared to healthy controls involving the ipsilateral limbic network, mesial temporal and mesial occipital regions of the neocortex, the bilateral pallidum in the subcortex, as well as the ipsilateral hippocampal head (p < 0.05). We identified several RSNs interacting with the significant cluster exhibiting hub disruption, including the visual medial, occipital, lateral, precuneus, default mode and salience networks. The first three networks demonstrated the greatest spatial overlap with the cluster, whereas the latter three are known to contain high-hub regions. After regressing out ADC and FA measures from the hubness maps, we found no significant changes, suggesting that these structural measures are not the underlying mechanisms responsible for the observed functional hub reorganization. Finally, the functional-structural coupling between ADC and hubness within the ipsilateral hippocampus showed the strongest trends (r = -0.39, p = 0.07), followed by similar coupling within the significant cluster exhibiting significant hubness differences from controls (r = -0.15, p = 0.34). No relationship was observed contralaterally to these regions. Coupling between FA and hubness in ipsilateral regions showed trends of decreased FA and hubness but did not reach statistical significance. 

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that temporal lobe epilepsy is characterized by widespread decreases of functional connector hubs, involving temporo-mesial structures and regions extending beyond the epilepsy focus. The resting state networks contributing to these alterations include both sensory networks and association networks which are known to involve high hubs. Although the functional-structural coupling analyses revealed moderate trends, these findings suggest that microstructural alterations may play a limited role in the widespread functional hub disruptions observed, warranting further investigation into the underlying mechanisms. Together, our results highlight the value of hub mapping for characterizing large-scale network reorganization in epilepsy and point towards subtle interactions between functional and structural network changes in TLE.
