Introduction

During infancy and early childhood, the brain undergoes rapid and nonlinear structural and functional development, driven by neurogenesis, synaptic pruning, and myelination, which together induce substantial temporal variability in brain connectivity. However, existing longitudinal studies commonly suffer from missing data across multiple time points. This problem is particularly prominent in infant cohorts, where limited compliance and severe motion artifacts result in high rates of unavailable neuroimaging observations. These issues pose challenges to reconstructing accurate longitudinal connectivity trajectories and consequently hinder the characterization of continuous developmental patterns in developmental neuroscience. To address the temporal discontinuity caused by high missingness, we propose a progressive multi-stage strategy for longitudinal brain network completion. The strategy integrates low-rank matrix completion, temporal mapping and interpolation mechanisms to achieve coarse-to-fine reconstruction of developmental trajectories, thereby providing high-quality data for subsequent longitudinal analyses.

Methods

We introduce a unified multi-stage completion framework designed for infant longitudinal brain networks with high missingness, enabling coarse-to-fine reconstruction of complete developmental trajectories (Fig. 1).

For the original multi-time-point brain network data, each brain connection is represented as an “individual × time” matrix Xn ∈ R(I × T), where I denotes the number of subjects, T the number of time points, and n indexes brain connections. A binary mask matrix M ∈ R(I × T) is used to distinguish observed values from missing ones.

At the coarsest temporal scale, brain development is summarized into six discrete stages, following age-resolved infant brain templates[1]. For each brain connection, individual–time connectivity patterns are modeled using a low-rank representation to capture shared developmental structure across individuals, consistent with prior findings on low-dimensional longitudinal brain development[2]. Specifically, non-negative matrix factorization[3] decomposes Xn ≈ Wn Hn , where Wn ∈ R(I × K) represents individual-specific weights and Hn ∈ R(K × 6) encodes stage-wise developmental patterns.

A mask-constrained objective is optimized to estimate only missing entries while preserving all observed values, following standard low-rank matrix completion principles[4]. After completion, entries with M(i,t) = 0 are replaced by estimated values, whereas entries with M(i,t)  = 1 retain their original observations.

To recover finer-grained developmental trajectories, the completed six-stage representations are mapped to higher-resolution temporal frameworks (e.g., 12-stage or 18-stage) using physiologically informed temporal mapping. Observed connectivity matrices and coarse-stage completed values are first aligned to the current-stage timeline. Interpolation is then applied exclusively to missing entries to ensure temporal continuity without overwriting original observations.

This multi-stage completion framework provides a principled solution for reconstructing longitudinal infant brain connectivity under high missingness, facilitating biologically plausible modeling of early brain development across multiple temporal scales.

Results

Dataset

This study evaluated the proposed multi-stage longitudinal brain network completion framework using infant neuroimaging data from the BCP and CBCP cohorts (total N = 582). The dataset included 138 infants with multi–time point scans and 444 infants with only a single scan. After parcellation using the AAL atlas, all subjects were represented as longitudinal brain connectivity graphs. Due to variable scan quality in infancy, the raw data contained substantial missingness, with more than 60% missing values in some multi–time point subjects.

Experimental Results

Applying the proposed progressive multi-stage completion strategy markedly improved data completeness, increasing the total number of valid entries by more than sevenfold. To quantitatively assess reconstruction accuracy, we constructed a ground-truth test set and compared the proposed method against temporal linear interpolation and k-nearest-neighbor (kNN) individual-based completion.
 The proposed method achieves the best performance across all evaluated metrics, as shown in Fig. 2(a). Specifically, it attains a mean squared error (MSE) of 0.0168 ± 0.0064, which is substantially lower than that of linear interpolation (0.0802 ± 0.1839) and k-nearest neighbors (0.1033 ± 0.1515). In terms of similarity to the ground-truth connectivity patterns, the proposed approach yields a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.7987 ± 0.0578, outperforming linear interpolation (0.6503 ± 0.1774) and showing a pronounced advantage over kNN (0.3284 ± 0.1497). A consistent trend is observed for cosine similarity, where the proposed method achieves a value of 0.8956 ± 0.0330, compared with 0.7931 ± 0.1256 for linear interpolation and only 0.4676 ± 0.1629 for kNN.

Furthermore, Fig. 2(b) presents a visual comparison of the reconstructed brain connectivity matrices, providing an intuitive illustration of the differences among completion methods. The connectivity matrix generated by the proposed multi-stage completion approach more closely resembles the ground truth in terms of spatial distribution and the preservation of strong connectivity regions. In contrast, the results obtained via linear interpolation exhibit noticeable deviations in connection strengths in certain regions, while the kNN-based completion shows a substantially lower correspondence with the ground truth. These visual observations further corroborate the performance differences indicated by the quantitative metrics.

Conclusion

In summary, this study introduces a progressive multi-stage framework that effectively addresses the severe missing-data problem inherent in infant longitudinal neuroimaging. By integrating low-rank completion, physiologically informed temporal mapping, and multi-scale interpolation, the method reconstructs fine-grained developmental trajectories while preserving original observations and enhancing temporal continuity.
