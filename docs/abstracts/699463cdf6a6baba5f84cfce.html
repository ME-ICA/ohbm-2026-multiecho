Introduction

Heart failure (HF) significantly increases the risk of cognitive impairment and Alzheimer's disease.^1 Emerging evidence suggests this brain vulnerability exists along a continuum of declining cardiac function, potentially beginning in subclinical stages.^2 However, prior neuroimaging research has primarily focused on macrostructural atrophy in clinical HF, likely overlooking underlying microstructural degradation and the critical early window for prevention. Consequently, it remains a pivotal question whether routine cardiac assessments can predict this early damage and if such microstructural degeneration mediates the link between cardiac dysfunction and cognitive decline.

To detect these early pathological processes, we utilize Mean Diffusivity (MD), a sensitive diffusion tensor imaging metric of gray matter (GM) microstructure. Elevated GM MD reflects damage to dendritic processes or cellular membranes and serves as a sensitive biomarker for early neurodegeneration.^3 Despite its utility, the application of GM MD to cardiac-related brain damage remains sparse.

This study bridges these gaps by investigating the associations between baseline cardiac biomarkers (left ventricular ejection fraction [EF] and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide [NT-proBNP]) and subsequent GM MD across a cardiac spectrum, including patients with clinical HF, matched non-HF patients with suspected coronary artery disease (CAD), and healthy controls. Based on our prior work,^4 we test the hypothesis that poorer cardiac function predicts greater future loss of GM integrity. Furthermore, we employ formal mediation analysis to determine whether this specific microstructural degradation mediates the relationship between baseline cardiac dysfunction and subsequent cognitive decline.

Methods

Participants 

We included 73 patients (20 females; mean age 54.8±5.3 years) from the prospective Leipzig Heart Study and 95 healthy controls (17 females; age 52.2±6.3 years) from the Leipzig Research Centre for Civilization Diseases (LIFE) study. All patients presented with clinical symptoms such as chest pain or dyspnea that prompted cardiovascular diagnostic work-up. Based on serum NT-proBNP concentration, 50 patients with CAD were assigned to the HF group, all with NT-proBNP >900 pg/mL, and 50 matched patients without HF referred to as "noHF" (25 patients had been confirmed as CAD in the diagnostic process). Matching was based on age, sex, diabetes mellitus, smoking status, carotid intima-media thickness, and presence of carotid plaques. Participants were excluded due to missed visits, poor imaging quality, ischemic brain lesions, and phase encoding inconsistencies. To ensure rigorous phenotypic contrast, three patients falling into the diagnostic "gray zone" (NT-proBNP 300–900 pg/mL) were excluded from the HF and noHF subgroup analyses but were retained in combined all-patient analyses to preserve the full physiological spectrum of cardiac stress. The final patient sample consisted of 33 HF, 37 noHF, and 3 "gray zone" patients. 

Controls were scanned under identical conditions to ensure comparability. The control group was particularly generated for the availability of EF and NT-proBNP values. All participants were carefully informed about the study and signed a written informed consent form. The study was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Leipzig.

Clinical and Neuropsychological Examination 

All 73 patients underwent comprehensive clinical evaluation and blood sampling at the time of hospitalization, including assessments of NT-proBNP, EF, high-sensitivity troponin T, creatine kinase (CK), and creatine kinase-muscle/brain (CK-MB). To capture the cumulative effects of chronic cardiac compromise, MRI acquisition and cognitive testing were performed 3.5±1.3 years after the initial biomarker measurement. DTI–derived MD served as a measure of GM microstructural integrity. For all 73 patients, cognitive performance was assessed using a comprehensive test battery covering attention (e.g., Trail Making Test A), executive function (e.g., Trail Making Test B, Stroop Test), and learning and memory (e.g., California Verbal Learning Test, Rey-Osterrieth Complex Figure Test) at the time of MRI acquisition. A global cognition score was computed by averaging these four domain scores.

MRI Acquisition and Preprocessing 

DTI data were acquired on a 3T Siemens Verio scanner (56 directions, b=1000 s/mm²). Preprocessing using FSL included denoising, artifact removal, and eddy current correction. Diffusion tensors were fitted to yield voxel-wise maps of MD. MD maps were normalized to standard space, smoothed with an 8 mm Gaussian kernel, and restricted voxel-based analyses to GM regions. For region-based analyses, mean GM MD values were extracted from 54 cortical and subcortical regions using the LPBA40 atlas.

Statistical Analyses 

We analyzed associations between baseline cardiac biomarkers and follow-up GM MD using both voxel-wise and region-based approaches. Models were adjusted for age, sex, and BMI. Analyses were conducted across four groups: HF, noHF, all patients (ALL), and the full sample including controls (ALL+). Mediation analyses tested whether regional GM MD mediated the relationship between cardiac function and cognition. Significance was assessed using bootstrapping with 10,000 simulations; effects were considered significant if the 95% confidence interval (CI) did not cross zero. Sensitivity analyses tested two additional covariate models: (1) a minimal covariate model (adjusted for age and sex); and (2) an extended covariate model (adjusted for age, sex, BMI, and Fazekas score).

Results

Clinical Characteristics

No significant differences were observed between subgroups of HF and noHF in age, sex, BMI, smoking status, diabetes mellitus, or hypertension. 

Lower Left Ventricular Ejection Fraction Predicts Higher Subsequent Mean Diffusivity 

Across all groups, lower baseline EF consistently predicted greater future GM MD, suggesting that reduced cardiac function is associated with greater GM microstructural degradation.

Heart Failure Group (HF, N=33): Widespread negative correlations between EF and MD were found across multiple brain regions. Key regions included right cingulate gyrus, bilateral orbitofrontal and parahippocampal gyri.

Non-Heart Failure Group (noHF, N=37): Significant negative correlations persisted even in patients without clinical HF, primarily in bilateral cingulate and parahippocampal gyri. 

Combined Patient Group (ALL, N=73): Analysis of all patients showed broader spatial extent of significant negative EF-MD correlations, with key findings in bilateral parahippocampal and cingulate gyri, hippocampus, and precuneus. 

Full Sample Including Healthy Controls (ALL+, N=168): Significant negative EF–MD correlations remained detectable in bilateral cingulate and parahippocampal gyri. 

Higher NT-proBNP Predicts Higher Subsequent Mean Diffusivity

Heart Failure Group (HF, N=33): NT-proBNP levels were positively correlated with MD across widespread brain regions, suggesting higher levels of HF severity are associated with increased GM microstructural damage. Key regions included bilateral precuneus and cingulate gyri, right lateral orbitofrontal gyrus, and thalamus. 

Non-Heart Failure Group (noHF, N=37): No significant correlations between NT-proBNP and MD were detected in patients without HF.

Combined Patient Group (ALL, N=73): Voxel-wise analysis detected a spatial pattern similar to the HF subgroup. 

All Participants Including Healthy Controls (ALL+, N=168): Significant positive associations were identified in regions including bilateral parahippocampal and cingulate gyri, hippocampus, and thalamus. 

Reverse-Direction: No significant reverse correlations (positive EF-MD or negative NT-proBNP-MD) were found. Furthermore, additional cardiac biomarkers (high-sensitivity troponin T, CK, and CK-MB) showed no significant associations with MD across any analytical approach or sample group.

Regional Brain Microstructure Mediates the Left Ventricular Ejection Fraction–Memory Relationship

Combined Patient Group (ALL, N=73): Higher MD significantly mediated the association between lower baseline EF and subsequent worse memory performance. Significant mediators included the bilateral cingulate, lingual, and inferior occipital gyri, right parahippocampal gyrus, and left precuneus. The direct effects of EF on memory were not significant, indicating an indirect-only mediation pattern. These findings suggest that lower EF is associated with higher MD (reflecting reduced microstructural integrity) in these regions, which links to worse memory performance. Key mediators, specifically the right cingulate and left lingual gyri, remained significant across both minimal and extended covariate models. No significant mediation effects were identified for attention, executive function, learning, or global performance after FDR correction. Additionally, no significant mediation pathways were identified using NT-proBNP as a predictor for any cognitive domain.

Conclusion

Establishing the Heart-Brain Continuum 

This prospective study provides evidence for a predictable continuum of cardiac-related brain microstructural damage. By tracking patients over 3.5 years, we demonstrate that this cerebral vulnerability is not restricted to overt HF but is instead a progressive trajectory that begins at a subclinical stage. We define this continuum through two complementary biomarker profiles: left ventricular EF acts as the early initiator, predicting microstructural degradation even in patients without HF (the "noHF" group). Conversely, NT-proBNP emerges as the later-stage systemic marker, signaling widespread neural damage only once the threshold of clinical heart failure is crossed. This distinction suggests that the critical window for neuroprotection opens years before traditional cardiac symptoms prompt a neurological workup.

The Mechanistic Pathway to Memory Decline 

We identify that microstructural degradation is the essential mediator driving memory decline in cardiac dysfunction. We observed an "indirect-only" mediation pattern, indicating that the impact of cardiac health on memory is transmitted predominantly through the loss of microstructural integrity in specific brain networks.

Crucially, the topography of this damage is non-random. It targets a convergence of two critical systems: the default mode network (specifically the cingulate gyrus and precuneus), which supports the episodic retrieval required for verbal list learning (California Verbal Learning Test), and the ventral visual stream (lingual and inferior occipital gyri), which supports the visual-spatial processing required for complex figure tasks (Rey-Osterrieth Complex Figure Test). Notably, the localization of cardiac-related brain damage we identify, specifically in the parahippocampus, precuneus, and cingulate, mirrors the trajectory of early Alzheimer’s disease pathology, offering a structural explanation for the memory deficits common to both conditions.

Validation of Gray Matter Mean Diffusivity

Methodologically, this work validates GM MD as a superior, high-sensitivity biomarker for tracking this continuum. In this specific cohort, where prior investigations using standard volumetric MRI failed to detect early associations, DTI-derived MD successfully unmasked widespread vulnerability. This suggests that the cellular environment—likely reflecting dendritic degeneration, neuroinflammation, or extracellular fluid shifts—is compromised long before macrostructural atrophy becomes visible. Consequently, GM MD represents a powerful tool for detecting "silent" brain injury, serving as a subclinical alarm system that conventional imaging modalities miss.

In summary, we have mapped a longitudinal pathway from the heart to the brain, defined by early microstructural degeneration in key memory networks. Utilizing GM MD, our work establishes a novel avenue for neurological risk stratification in patients undergoing routine cardiac assessment, identifying these vulnerable microstructural targets as high-priority objectives for cardiovascular care aimed at preventing cognitive decline.
