Introduction

Despite decades of research documenting electroencephalographic (EEG) alterations in neurodegenerative conditions such as Parkinson’s disease (PD) and Alzheimer’s disease (AD), the translation of EEG findings into clinically actionable tools remains limited. A central limitation of the field is the continued reliance on group-level comparisons between patients and healthy controls. While informative at the population level, this approach obscures substantial inter-individual variability, thereby limiting its relevance for individual-level clinical assessment and stratification.

Normative modeling has emerged as a powerful alternative framework for addressing this limitation. By estimating population-level reference distributions and quantifying each individual’s deviation from these norms, normative models enable subject-specific inference of neurobiological atypicality1. Although normative charts are well established in fields such as pediatric growth monitoring, their application to neuroimaging is relatively recent. MRI-based normative modeling has begun to chart lifespan trajectories of brain structure—including grey and white matter volumes, cortical thickness, and surface area—and has revealed substantial heterogeneity in psychiatric2 and neurodegenerative disorders3. In contrast to MRI, EEG normative modeling remains largely unexplored despite EEG’s scalability, low cost, and direct measurement of neural dynamics. Here, we address this gap through two main objectives: (i) to chart normative trajectories of widely used EEG features in healthy adults across more than 40 years of the lifespan, and (ii) to quantify neurophysiological heterogeneity in AD and PD by evaluating individual deviation scores relative to these normative models.

Methods

Datasets

We analyzed resting-state EEG data aggregated from 14 independent datasets, comprising healthy controls (HC; n = 499), individuals with Parkinson’s disease (PD; n = 237), and individuals with Alzheimer’s disease (AD; n = 197), all aged >40 years. These datasets included publicly available datasets as well as datasets obtained through established research collaborations. All contributing studies received approval from their respective institutional review boards or ethics committees, and the associated ethical documentation is reported in the original publications for each dataset. Additional methodological information is available in4.The goal was to estimate normative age-related trajectories of EEG spectral power and source-level functional connectivity in HC and to quantify electrophysiological deviations and heterogeneity in PD and AD.

EEG preprocessing and feature extraction

EEG data underwent standard preprocessing procedures for artifact rejection, as described in4. Spectral features were computed at the sensor level as relative power within canonical frequency bands. To ensure consistency across datasets, analyses were restricted to the common set of 19 scalp electrodes available in all recordings. For connectivity analyses, EEG signals were source reconstructed using eLORETA. Source-level time series were parcellated into 68 cortical regions (Desikan–Killiany atlas). Functional connectivity was then estimated using amplitude envelope correlation, with zero-lag correction applied.

Normative models

Normative models were trained on 400 HC participants (46% male), while an independent subset of 99 HC participants (42% male) was held out as a comparison group for evaluating clinical deviations. The clinical samples consisted of 237 PD patients (65% male) and 197 AD patients (37% male). Normative modeling was performed using Generalized Additive Models for Location, Scale and Shape (GAMLSS). Separate models were fitted for each EEG feature, covering (i) relative spectral power at each scalp electrode and (ii) source-level functional connectivity values between all cortical pairwise connections. All features were computed for the canonical frequency bands: delta, theta, alpha, beta, and gamma. Using the training HC cohort, we derived age-related normative trajectories for each feature. (Figure 1).

Deriving individual deviations

Participants in the PD, AD, and HC test groups were projected onto the normative models to generate subject-specific deviation scores (z-scores). Following prior work on normative modeling and neurobiological heterogeneity2, we quantified extreme deviations using two complementary metrics:

  1. Burden of extreme deviations:

For each participant, we counted the number of features (scalp electrodes or source-level connections) exhibiting |z| > 2, and we computed the percentage of individuals in each group with at least one extremely deviated feature.

  1. Deviation overlap maps:

To examine spatial consistency within each diagnostic group, we calculated—for every electrode or connection—the percentage of participants showing extreme deviations (|z| > 2). These group-level overlap maps summarize the degree to which deviations cluster spatially within PD, AD, and HC.

Group differences in overlap maps were assessed using permutation-based statistical testing, comparing PD vs. HC and AD vs. HC (FDR corrected; p < 0.05).

Extreme Deviations Index and clinical associations

To evaluate clinical relevance, we derived a patient-level metric—the Extreme Deviations Index (EDI)—defined as the mean functional connectivity value across all connections showing extreme deviations. Spearman correlations were used to assess associations between EDI and clinical scores. For PD, clinical severity was quantified using the Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS), and global cognition using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE). For AD, MMSE was used as the primary cognitive measure.

Results

Spectral deviations:

Overall, relatively few participants showed at least one spectral deviation, with the highest positive deviation rates in the theta band (PD: 31.36%, AD: 27.41%). Negative deviations peaked in the beta band (PD: 12.71%, AD: 23.35%), indicating substantial similarity to the HC(train) group.

AD patients showed significantly more negative deviating channels than HC(test) in the alpha and beta bands (p < 0.01), whereas both PD and AD exhibited significantly more positive deviations in theta, alpha, and beta (p < 0.05). (Figure 2a).

Functional connectivity deviations:

In contrast to spectral findings, the majority of participants exhibited at least one functional connectivity deviation. Negative deviations were particularly elevated in PD, reaching 86.86% at delta. PD showed more extreme negative deviations than HC(test), while AD showed fewer. For positive deviations, PD had fewer deviated connections than HC(test) across all bands except delta, whereas AD showed more only in the alpha band (p < 0.05). 

Spectral overlap maps:

Spatial overlap of deviated channels was higher in HC(test) than in PD and AD. More than 60% of HC(test) individuals shared deviated channels in alpha and beta (negative) and in delta and theta (positive). PD showed >60% overlap in theta (positive), and AD showed similar overlap in beta (negative).

Permutation testing revealed significant differences between clinical groups and HC(test) in delta, theta, and beta. PD showed shared deviations mainly in fronto-central channels (up to 6/19 channels), while AD showed few significant delta-band channels but occipital (theta) and left temporo-parietal (beta) deviations (up to 4/19 channels). (Figure 2a, c).

Functional connectivity overlap maps:

Spatial overlap across individuals was consistently low (<25%) for all groups and frequency bands. Only negative deviations survived permutation testing. Most significant deviations involved the default mode network (DMN), particularly in delta (PD: 39%, AD: 44%), theta (PD: 40%, AD: 35%), and alpha (PD: 42%, AD: 44%) bands. (Figure 2b, d)

Clinical correlations:

In PD, negative deviations correlated with UPDRS in the delta band (ρ = 0.24, p = 0.0254). In AD, delta-band deviations correlated with MMSE (ρ = –0.26, p = 0.0107). PD patients also showed significant correlations between negative deviations and MMSE in delta (ρ = –0.35, p < 0.0001), as well as in alpha and gamma. (Figure 2f, e, g)

Conclusion

In this study, we sought to make three key contributions. First, we characterized age-related trajectories of scalp EEG spectral power and source-level functional connectivity in a healthy cohort aged >40 years—two domains frequently proposed as neurophysiological biomarkers of interest. Second, we demonstrated pronounced electrophysiological heterogeneity within Parkinson’s disease (PD) and Alzheimer’s disease (AD) cohorts, challenging the continued reliance on traditional case–control comparisons and underscoring the importance of capturing individual variability. Third, we showed that patient-specific deviations derived from normative models exhibit meaningful associations with clinical assessments, representing an initial step toward individualized EEG-based biomarkers for neurodegenerative disorders. Overall, this work underscores the necessity of patient-level inference to improve the precision of neurophysiological assessment in PD and AD. Future studies with larger cohorts, longitudinal designs, and multimodal integration will be essential to validate these markers and translate them into clinical practice.
