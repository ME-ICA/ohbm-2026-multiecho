Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a complex neurodevelopmental condition characterized by persistent social affective deficits and repetitive, stereotyped behaviours.1 A central, unresolved challenge in the field is the profound heterogeneity of ASD, which manifests across scales from molecular pathways to macroscopic brain organization. While converging evidence suggests that atypical cortical morphology contributes to the emergence of autistic traits, the spatial patterns of these cortical alterations remain inconsistent across neuroimaging studies.2 This inconsistency may directly stem from the inherent neurobiological heterogeneity of the ASD population, or it could be associated with confounding factors such as limited cohort sizes, narrow age spans, methodological differences, and lack of research focusing on critical early developmental periods such as early childhood.3 Together, these issues present a direct challenge: they hinder the identification of stable and reproducible neuroimaging-based biological subtypes using currently available MRI data.

To address this, we aggregated large-scale multisite MRI datasets to test whether personalized deviations from normative cortical development could resolve heterogeneous neuroanatomy in ASD into stable, biologically meaningful subtypes. We constructed lifespan normative trajectories of cortical thickness (CT) and surface area (SA) using generalized additive models for location, scale, and shape (GAMLSS) and derived centile scores to quantify personalized neuroanatomical deviation for each participant with ASD. We then applied categorical multilayer community detection4 to test the hypothesis that these deviations would cluster into recurrent endophenotypes, each linked to distinct anatomical abnormality, behavioural profiles, and biological processes.

Methods

We aggregated large-scale MRI datasets from 193 scanning sites, comprising 60,234 TD participants (0–100 years; 29,802 males) and 3,117 individuals with ASD (1–62 years; 2,644 males). All scans underwent a rigorous four-step quality control (QC) framework5 integrating automated tools (MRIQC and FSQC), custom QC scripts, and meticulous visual inspection of each image and its reconstructed pial and white matter surfaces. To optimize the accuracy and robustness of MRI data processing across the lifespan, we implemented age-specific pipelines. For infants aged 0–2 years, whose scans present extremely low and rapidly changing tissue contrast due to ongoing myelination, we used iBEAT v2.0, a deep learning–based pipeline that provides robust preprocessing for infant MRI scans. For participants aged over 2 years, T1-weighted data were processed with FreeSurfer’s standard recon-all workflow, and morphological measures were derived based on the Desikan–Killiany atlas for each participant. We constructed lifespan normative models for regional CT and SA using GAMLSS, a robust framework for modelling non-linear growth trajectories, and we modeled batch factors as random effects for harmonization. To ensure optimal model performance, we systematically evaluated alternative fractional polynomial specifications, selecting the model with the lowest Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). For each individual with ASD, we derived personalized centile scores indexing deviation from normative trajectories. We then applied categorical multilayer community detection4 to identify recurring deviation patterns, yielding three CT and three SA endophenotypes. Finally, we integrated these endophenotypes with behavioral assessment and multi-omics data to characterize their underlying molecular profiles (Fig. 1).

Results

We identified three distinct CT-based and three distinct SA-based neuroanatomical endophenotypes within the ASD cohort, each characterized by specific anatomical abnormalities and behavioural profiles. CT1 was characterized by globally increased cortical thickness except within the salience network, primarily including limbic and insular cortex. In contrast, CT2 exhibited the opposite pattern, with reduced thickness in frontoparietal regions but increased thickness in salience regions. CT3 followed a sensorimotor–association gradient, with thinner sensory and visual cortices while thicker association regions (Fig. 2a). Similarly, SA1 exhibited increased surface area in somatosensory and attention networks while reduced surface area in visual and frontal cortices; SA2 presented the reverse pattern; and SA3 showed globally reduced surface area except in visual regions (Fig. 2b). Notably, 59.5% of individuals were assigned to different CT and SA communities, suggesting that CT and SA may reflect distinct aspects of autism heterogeneity (Fig. 2c). We observed significant differences in age distribution across the identified endophenotypes. Individuals in the CT2 subgroup were significantly younger than those in CT1 and CT3 (Welch’s t test, p < 0.001; Fig. 2d).  Similarly, the SA1 subgroup was significantly older than both SA2 and SA3 (Welch’s t test, p < 0.001; Fig. 2e). Sex distribution did not differ across CT endophenotypes (chi-squared test, p = 0.57; Fig. 2d), but varied among SA endophenotypes (chi-squared test, p = 0.02; Fig. 2e). Behavioural and clinical profiles varied across CT endophenotypes but not SA endophenotypes (One-way ANOVA, p-FDR < 0.05). Individuals in CT2 showed more severe core symptoms than those in CT1, particularly in CARS–Listening response, ADOS–Communication, ADOS–Social affect, and ADOS–Play (Welch’s t-tests, p-FDR < 0.0001; Fig. 2f). Conversely, individuals in CT3 had higher full-scale IQ (FIQ) than those in CT1 (Welch’s t = 3.975, p-FDR = 0.0002) and CT2 (Welch’s t = 3.255, p-FDR = 0.002), with similar effects observed for verbal and performance IQ (VIQ, PIQ). Finally, we found microstructural profiles, including gene expression, glial cells, dopamine and serotonin neurotransmitters, and immune-related pathways, are associated with the identified anatomical heterogeneity.

Conclusion

Our findings delineate anatomical endophenotypes within ASD that reflect distinct biological substrates and behavioural profiles. These results provide an integrative framework linking molecular alterations, microstructural profiles, and behavioural variability, thereby advancing a biologically grounded understanding of heterogeneity in autism and informing precision approaches to its diagnosis and treatment.
