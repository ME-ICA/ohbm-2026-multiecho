Introduction

Alcohol is a widely used psychoactive substance with the potential for harmful or maladaptive use. The subjective effects produced by alcohol can contribute to an individual’s alcohol intake behaviours. Individuals who consume more alcohol in their daily lives report liking its effects more than those who engage in lower levels of drinking, and also report higher desire to drink more after consuming alcohol1. This suggests that the rewarding properties of alcohol play an important role in intake behaviours; though it is currently unclear whether alcohol alters reward processing more generally. 

While a number of studies have shown that naturalistic alcohol intake behaviours are related to differences in reward processing, the literature describing how acute alcohol intake affects reward is limited. One functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) study found that activation to facial stimuli in the striatum, an important brain region for reward, was increased following intravenous alcohol administration2. Further, a study employing positron emission tomography revealed that glucose metabolism was increased in the bilateral striatum following alcohol intake compared to placebo3. However, it has not yet been demonstrated whether alcohol affects the neural responses to tasks that are designed to directly assess reward function. Here, we use a reward task to determine whether individuals show alterations in neural reward function under the influence of alcohol compared to a placebo condition.

Methods

We conducted a preliminary single-blind, within-subjects study in healthy individuals. Participants (n = 32; F = 16, M = 16) completed two experimental sessions lasting 4-5 hours each. In order to account for drug expectancy effects, participants were informed at the onset of each session that they could receive alcohol, a sedative, caffeine, or placebo, though they only ever received alcohol or placebo. The order of the sessions was randomized between subjects and counterbalanced. In order to account for differences in body water content between sexes, the concentration of the alcoholic drinks were set at 0.6 g/kg of alcohol for males and 0.4 g/kg for females. The placebo was cranberry juice with a 1% alcohol float to mask for smell and taste.

At approximately 110 minutes following drug or placebo intake, participants completed an MRI scan. Task and resting-state functional MRI (fMRI) data were obtained, though only one of the tasks—the monetary incentive delay (MID) task4—is presented in this abstract. The MID task assesses aspects of reward processing, including reward anticipation. Reward anticipation is measured by presenting participants with a cue that indicates whether the upcoming trial is one where they could gain money (potential reward), lose money (potential loss), or neither gain nor lose money (neutral), depending on their performance. 

All fMRI data preprocessing and analysis was completed using AFNI. Mass univariate linear mixed effects models were applied using the program 3dLMEr5 in order to account for repeated measures within-subjects. For the MID task, models included session (alcohol vs. placebo) as a covariate of interest, and a variable accounting for reward anticipation stimulus condition (potential reward, potential punishment, or neutral). Head motion (number of censored volumes) and sex were included as control covariates. Clusters were defined using a voxelwise threshold of p = 0.001 and a cluster forming threshold of α = 0.05, corresponding to a cluster size of 17 voxels.

Results

For the MID task, there were 21 clusters with a significant main effect of session, and no clusters with a significant session x stimulus interaction. For the session main effect, there was greater activation across all stimulus conditions during the placebo session in areas of the brain related to motor and action planning (bilateral supplementary motor area, putamen, and precentral gyri; left insula and middle cingulate cortex). In sensory areas, such as the bilateral early visual cortex, intraparietal sulcus, and precuneus, the right supramarginal gyrus, parahippocampal gyrus, temporo-parietal-occipital junction, and inferior parietal lobule, and the left superior parietal lobule and lateral occipital cortex, activation was greater during the alcohol session across all reward anticipation conditions.

Conclusion

This is the first study to assess the neural effects of acute alcohol intake on the MID task. Across the entire task, alcohol intake was associated with increased activation in sensory areas of the brain, while areas involved in motor planning and action showed decreased activation compared to the placebo condition. Notably, there was lower activation in the bilateral putamen and the left insula (areas involved in reward processing) following alcohol intake. Future directions include evaluating the relationship between “liking” the effects of alcohol and brain responses observed during the MID task.
