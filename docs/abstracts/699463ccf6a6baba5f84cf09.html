Introduction

Owing to its robustness and readiness to use, resting state has emerged as the de-facto approach for fMRI connectivity investigations in autism. In keeping with the high neural variability observed in the condition, the widespread use of resting state fMRI connectivity mapping on publicly available neuroimaging databases has revealed highly diverse patterns of atypical fMRI connectivity in individuals with autism.1 More recently, the application of classification approaches to resting state fMRI connectivity patterns has shown that autism variability is non-random; rather, when deconstructed, it reveals groups of individuals exhibiting distinct patterns of atypical functional connectivity, also known as neurosubtypes.2

However, the predominant use of resting state paradigms leaves open important questions regarding the significance of these neurosubtypes. Do connectivity patterns of intrinsic brain activity mapped from an unconstrained state fully reflect the context-dependent brain organization in autism? Can naturalistic paradigms - such as movie-watching - provide complementary or even superior insight into the functional architecture of autism? And critically, can these complex audio-visual stimuli be leveraged to identify neurosubtypes grounded in sensory-cognitive meaningful dimensions? 

To address these questions, we applied connectivity mapping to naturalistic fMRI time series acquired from individuals with autism during movie watching. Unlike resting-state, movie watching engages multiple sensory and cognitive systems simultaneously, offering a rich framework to probe the large-scale brain network organization in autism, and its relevance for identifying neurosubtypes with high ecological validity.

Methods

Study sample. To deconstruct autism variability using naturalistic viewing, we analyzed fMRI time series from participants with and without autism, drawn from the HBN Biobank.3 In our study, we included individuals who underwent a 10-minute naturalistic fMRI BOLD scan while viewing the clip Despicable Me. Participants with a median framewise displacement (FD) greater than 0.3 mm were excluded from further analysis. To establish a reference population for normative modeling, we selected typically developing participants matched to the autistic group by demographics using cardinality matching, as implemented in the R package MatchIt. This process resulted in a cohort of 70 participants with autism (8 females, 5.7 - 19.9 years, mean age 12.6 ± 4.1 years) and 70 typically developing participants (9 females, 7.8 - 21.5 years, mean age 12.4 ± 3.0 years), matched for age (t = - 0.31, p = 0.76) and sex (χ² = 0, p = 1).

Preprocessing. We carried out preprocessing and denoising of the fMRI time series by using the AFNI toolkit. Preprocessing included spike removal, slice timing correction, skull stripping, motion correction, and registration to the anatomical T1 image. Denoising included nuisance regression of Friston’s 24 head motion traces, five principal components of white matter with CompCor, and the mean signal of CSF. Time series also underwent percentage normalization, were high-pass filtered at 0.01 Hz, spatially smoothed with a 6 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel, and registered to MNI152 standard space.

Neurosubtyping. Neurosubtyping was performed following the analytical workflow shown in Figure 1. To assess inter-areal functional interactions, we first extracted the average preprocessed naturalistic BOLD time series from 400 cortical regions of the Schaefer Atlas and 14 subcortical regions of the Harvard-Oxford Atlas. Then, we computed the functional connectomes by correlating the fMRI time series of each region with all others using temporal Pearson correlations, resulting in a 414 × 414 correlation matrix (Figure 1a). Next, we harmonized the functional connectomes across the three MRI sites using COMBAT (Figure 1b). To delineate autism neurosubtypes marked by distinct patterns of atypical naturalistic fMRI connectivity, we normalized participants’ functional connectomes using an age-related normative modeling framework. Using local polynomial regression, this method characterizes autistic participants’ inter-areal functional interactions as deviations from a typically developing reference, expressed in units of standard deviation (Figure 1c). Finally, neurosubtyping was conducted by applying Gaussian Mixture Models in Scikit-learn to the mean deviation scores for each region’s interactions with all other regions (Figure 1d). To quantify how frequently autistic participants exhibited atypical naturalistic fMRI connectivity within each autism group, we calculated the percentage of functional connectomes whose deviation scores in each brain region were extreme, defined as values exceeding two standard deviations from the typically developing norm.

Ethical approval. Data acquisition was approved by a local ethical committee

Results

Classification of naturalistic fMRI deviation scores by using a Gaussian Mixture Model revealed two dominant autism subgroups characterized by distinct connectivity profiles (Figure 2a).

Subgroup 1 consists of participants with autism (n = 29, 43% of the cases) who predominantly exhibit positive fMRI deviation scores. Specifically, we found a high proportion of autistic individuals showing extreme positive deviations in the default mode network (mean = 21.1%, max: 79.3%) and language network (mean = 19.2%, max: 48.3%). A set of brain regions of the control network also show extreme positive deviations (mean = 17.7%, max: 69.0%) (Figure 2b). In subgroup 1, we also found foci showing the opposing pattern of extreme deviations, including extreme negative deviations in brain regions belonging to the default mode (mean = 2.37%, max: 13.8%) and dorsal attentional network (mean = 1.56%, max: 27.6%) (Figure 2c).

Subgroup 2 comprises participants with autism (n = 41, 57% of the cases) who primarily show negative fMRI deviation scores. Here, we found extreme negative deviations in brain regions of the default mode (mean = 16.8%, max: 68.3%), language (mean = 15.7%, max: 53.7%) and subcortical (mean = 14.8%, max: 58.5%) network (Figure 2d). A set of brain regions of the ventral attentional network also show extreme positive deviations (mean = 11.1%, max: 68.3%). We also found foci showing extreme positive deviations in regions of the control (mean = 0.3%, max: 17.1%) and default mode network (mean = 0.4%, max: 12.2%) (Figure 2e). 

Participants of Subgroup 1 also showed extreme positive deviations in the visual (mean = 16.2%, max: 55.2%) and auditory network (mean = 17.2%, max: 34.5%). Participants of Subgroup 2 also showed extreme negative deviations in the auditory network (mean = 14.5%, max: 26.8%), and less frequently in the visual network (mean = 9.4%, max: 41.5%).

Conclusion

By classifying naturalistic fMRI connectivity maps of individuals with autism, here we show two autism subgroups characterized by distinct patterns of deviation scores of functional connectivity. In both subgroups, our analysis shows patterns of atypical fMRI connectivity not only in auditory and visual networks but, even more prominently, in networks encompassing integrative areas, such as the default mode, language and subcortical network. These findings indicate that the processing and perception of audiovisual stimuli engage regions that extend beyond the primary auditory and visual cortices, areas traditionally hypothesized to be the origin of atypical sensory processing in autism.4 

Our analysis also shows that the two subgroups of autism are characterized by predominantly positive and negative deviation scores. This set of results is broadly consistent with the patterns of functional hypo- and hyper-connectivity observed in autism neurosubtypes by using resting state fMRI.5 The fact that these broadly opposing patterns can be observed across such different brain states - one unconstrained and internally driven, the other more structured and externally driven - indicates that these two partitions, and their fMRI connectivity profiles, are robust and generalizable. This cross-state stability suggests that atypically increased vs. decreased fMRI connectivity reflect stable, fundamental organizational differences across brain networks of autistic subgroups rather than state-dependent fluctuations.

While the contribution of resting-state fMRI in understanding the functional architecture of the brain in autism is undisputed, the interpretation of patterns of spontaneous brain activity is elusive. In contrast, naturalistic fMRI paradigms may offer a complementary approach by presenting participants with dynamic, ecologically valid stimuli that open the doors for investigations of functional connectivity underlying domains frequently atypical in individuals with the condition, such as perception, sensory processing, and multisensory integration.
