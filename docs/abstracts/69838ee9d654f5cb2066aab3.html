Introduction

Hemispheric asymmetry is a fundamental organizing principle of the human brain, most prominently expressed in the language network. Classical models treat this asymmetry as a static, evolutionarily fixed property, while developmental theories emphasize its experience-dependent emergence during childhood. In contrast, influential aging models such as HAROLD (Hemispheric Asymmetry Reduction in OLDer adults) predict that asymmetry diminishes with aging, often interpreted as compensatory bilateral recruitment. Similarly, network dedifferentiation theory posits that modular organization erodes with aging, which may secondarily reduce hemispheric specialization. 

However, these frameworks share a critical assumption that asymmetry follows a uniform rise and fall trajectory across brain systems. The language network provides an ideal test case for this assumption, because it underpins crystallized intelligence (Gc) – typically assessed through vocabulary and naming tests – which, unlike fluid intelligence, continues to accumulate across the lifespan. If its neural substrate follows a similarly distinct trajectory, this would challenge the prevailing view of asymmetry as uniformly declining with age.

Here, using large lifespan resting-state fMRI datasets from the US and Japan (HCP and Brain/MINDS Beyond; total N > 2,000, ages 8–90), we tested whether functional connectivity (FC) asymmetry of the language network follows a distinct trajectory – continuous reinforcement rather than late-life reversal. We further examined whether individual differences in asymmetry, after removing age-related variance, are selectively associated with Gc versus fluid intelligence (Gf). 

Methods

We analyzed resting-state fMRI data from 2,175 healthy participants aged 8–90 years in the Human Connectome Project (HCP) Lifespan and Young Adult cohorts (n=1488; Van Essen et al., 2013) and the Brain/MINDS Beyond (BMB) dataset (N = 687; Koike et al., 2021). All datasets include high-resolution structural and functional MRI acquired using harmonized or well-documented protocols. From the HCP data, we chose all Lifespan and a subset of YA dataset with complete intelligence test results.

Preprocessing followed the HCP minimal pipeline, including gradient distortion correction, motion correction, ICA-FIX denoising, surface-based alignment to reference sulcal information, and normalization to standard volume space. Before the ICA-FIX, we performed additional very slow physiological noise removal based on perfusion lag mapping (Ref[TH1] ), since it also increases separability of sources in the subsequent ICA. BMB data were processed using a pipeline aligned with HCP conventions. After surface-based multi-modal registration using myelin and functional networks (MSMAll), resting-state functional connectivity (FC) analyses were conducted based on 360 cortical parcels.

The FC was computed using Pearson correlations between parcel-wise time series and converted to Fisher’s Z. Hemispheric FC asymmetry was quantified as 1 minus the Spearman correlation between left- and right-hemisphere connectivity profiles for each parcel, such that higher values indicate greater differentiation of hemispheric FC pattern. Lifespan trajectories of global FC, modularity (Q), and asymmetry were modeled using generalized additive models, controlling for sex.

To harmonize cross-dataset differences between HCP-Lifespan and HCP Young Adult, FC values and behavioral measures were aligned using GAM-ComBat. To assess age-independent individual differences, we performed multiple regression analyses in which parcel-wise FC asymmetry was modeled as a function of fluid (Gf) and crystallized (Gc) intelligence after removing age-related variance using generalized additive models. Sex was included as a covariate. Statistical inference was based on parametric tests derived from the fitted models.

Results

Figure 1 summarizes lifespan trajectories of functional connectivity, hemispheric asymmetry, and their relationships to intelligence.

Global mean functional connectivity (FC) showed a monotonic decline with age, indicating reduced overall synchrony of intrinsic brain activity. Network modularity (Q) increased during development but decreased in adulthood and aging, consistent with progressive dedifferentiation of large-scale functional organization. Critically, these trajectories were replicated across both datasets despite differences in population and acquisition protocols.

In contrast, functional asymmetry exhibited divergent trajectories depending on scale. At the whole-brain level, FC asymmetry decreased during development, reflecting increasing bilateral integration, but reversed direction in adulthood, becoming more asymmetric with aging.

Notably, this pattern was fundamentally different within the language network. Asymmetry of left area 55b (L55b) connectivity increased monotonically from childhood through old age, indicating a continuous reinforcement rather than a late-life reversal. This identifies the language network as a unique exception to the global pattern.

Spatial maps further showed that the FC asymmetry map closely overlapped with the average L55b connectivity pattern, highlighting language-related regions as the dominant contributors to hemispheric differentiation. 

To disentangle age-related effects from interindividual variability, we next examined cognitive associations after removing all age-related variance. As expected, Gf and Gc showed divergent lifespan trajectories.

Finally, the multiple regression analyses of age-independent individual differences revealed clearly dissociable spatial patterns for Gf and Gc: Gf-related effects were distributed across domain-general association cortices, whereas Gc-related effects prominently involved language-network regions, including L55b with significant main effect (P = 0.0006).

Together, these results indicate that while global connectivity and modular organization decline with age, the language network follows a distinct maturational trajectory, characterized by lifelong persistence of hemispheric asymmetry and selective association with crystallized, rather than fluid, intelligence.

Conclusion

Across two independent lifespan datasets from different populations, we demonstrate that the language network follows a distinct maturational trajectory: unlike whole-brain asymmetry, which reverses direction in adulthood, language network asymmetry shows continuous reinforcement from childhood through old age. This challenges the uniform decline predicted by models such as HAROLD.

Furthermore, age-independent individual differences in asymmetry revealed dissociable cognitive associations: fluid intelligence related to distributed domain-general association cortices, whereas crystallized intelligence selectively involved language-network regions, including left area 55b. This suggests that persistent hemispheric specialization provides a neural substrate for the lifelong accumulation of semantic knowledge.

Together, these findings reframe functional hemispheric asymmetry not as a unitary phenomenon destined to decline, but as a system-specific property -- with the language network, a foundation for social communication across the lifespan, demonstrating lifelong maturation rather than senescence.
