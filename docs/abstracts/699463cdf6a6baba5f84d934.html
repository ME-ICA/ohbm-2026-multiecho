Introduction

The challenge of detecting deception has long fascinated philosophers, jurists, and scientists, given its profound implications for interpersonal trust, justice systems, and social regulation. The advent of functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), with its promise of enabling in vivo access to brain function, turned out to inevitably fascinate those working and researching in contact with deceitful behavior, and dozens of studies have been conducted in the last twenty years trying to capture the neural clue that reveals the act of lying. Ten years ago, Farah and colleagues ^1 completed a pivotal coordinate-based meta-analysis (CBMA) revealing a pattern of brain regions coherently involved across fMRI experiments implementing lie-detection tasks. However, how the authors discussed, to rule out the possibility that the pattern was reflecting a combination of different cognitive domains rather than deceptive behavior itself would have required an analysis of selectivity. To address this issue, the present work used Bayes Factor to provide quantitative support to the hypothesis that the observed neural correlates are indeed more likely to be associated with deception than any other cognitive function.

Methods

First, PubMed, PsycInfo, and Google Scholar databases were searched through the keywords: (lie detection OR deception) AND (fMRI OR functional MRI OR functional magnetic resonance imaging) so to obtain an updated set of task-fMRI studies on healthy, young adults. To be included into the analyses, studies were required to meet all technical inclusion criteria for CBMA ^2. Moreover, only studies in which participants actively and intentionally lied in first person were considered, therefore excludeing tasks involving non-intentional or automatic forms of deception (e.g., self-deceptive biases) and paradigms focusing on social judgment or theory of mind, where participants evaluated others’ lies rather than producing deception themselves. Tasks involving logical falsification or abstract reasoning were discarded too, as they did not reflect socially or behaviorally meaningful acts of deception. After screening, 50 task-based fMRI experiments from 46 studies were retained, for a total of 468 activation foci derived from 873 subjects. This set of experiments was analyzed using the Activation Likelihood Estimation (ALE) algorithm, as implemented in GingerALE software package (v.3.0.2) ^3. The statistical significance of the ALE scores was assessed by applying FWE correction at a cluster-level threshold of p < 0.05, with 1000 permutations, in line with current standards.

In order to apply Bayes Factor computation, a second dataset had to be created, encompassing fMRI tasks assessing any cognitive domain but deception. This was obtained by querying the BrainMap database using the Sleuth software (v.3.0.4), explicitly excluding the “deception” paradigm class form the BrainMap taxonomy. This second dataset (consisting of 2.145 experiments, 21,010 foci derived from 34840 subjects) thus provided a comprehensive reference sample representing domain-general brain activations, against which the previously obtained deception-specific 50 experiments could be compared. The selectivity analysis was implemented using the software BACON ^4, that performs Bayes Factor analysis on ALE-derived maps, to estimate the voxel-wise posterior probability that observed brain activations are uniquely associated with a specific condition, in this case, lying. In order to obtain a robust pattern, the posterior probability threshold was set to P ≥ 0.95, which is typically required to establish strong evidence within the Bayesian framework. 

Results

The canonical ALE analysis highlighted nine significant clusters of activation, including right inferior frontal gyrus, left inferior frontal gyrus/insula, left superior frontal gyrus, right supramarginal gyrus, right superior frontal gyrus, left supramarginal gyrus, right lentiform nucleus and right thalamus, left middle frontal gyrus, and left inferior parietal lobule [Figure 1]. However, the selectivity analysis revealed that no activations clusters survived the selected posterior probability threshold of P ≥ 0.95. 

Conclusion

The ALE meta-analysis confirmed the consistent engagement of key frontal and parietal regions, aligning with previous meta-analyses by Farah and colleagues ^1, although a reduction in the number of significant cortical clusters was here observed. This discrepancy may be attributed to the increased number of considered experiments, stricter inclusion criteria and more rigorous statistical thresholds. Moreover, additional subcortical activations, particularly in the thalamus and basal ganglia, were now observed. Although the canonical meta-analysis confirmed the existence of a pattern associated with deception tasks, the BACON analysis revealed that nothing of this seems to be selective (P ≥ 0.95), suggesting that deception does not elicit neural responses that are sufficiently distinct to be considered deception-specific activations. While it would be excessive to state that neural correlates of deceptive behavior do not exist at all, present results support the stance that fMRI is not a suitable tool to perform lie-detection, especially for forensic purposes. Lying remains a complex and multifaceted behavior, with a marked social perspective that makes any in-lab operationalization being too far from naturalistic reality it tries to replicate.
