Introduction

Spontaneous activity in human cortex is organised along large-scale functional gradients that parallel anatomical hierarchies, yet how these macroscale patterns relate to laminar functional architecture remains unclear. The layered architecture of cortical gray matter is a core feature of neocortical organization and function, with individual layers exhibiting distinct patterns of connectivity and functional specialization 1. Canonical microcircuit models distinguish feedforward inputs targeting middle layers from feedback signals targeting superficial and deep agranular layers. Advances in ultra-high-field laminar fMRI now allow non-invasive measurement of layer-specific signals in humans and have revealed dissociable laminar profiles of feedforward and feedback processing in sensory and association cortices during a variety of cognitive tasks 2. By contrast, whole-brain laminar connectivity at rest remains in its infancy, and the layer-dependent connectivity of resting-state networks has not yet been systematically characterized. Parallel work on macroscale gradients has mapped spatial transitions in microstructure, cortical morphology, laminar cytoarchitecture, and resting-state connectivity, and recent multimodal approaches have begun to quantify the heterogeneity of cortical areas across and within modalities 3. Here, we leverage submillimeter 7T resting-state fMRI to construct whole-brain laminar functional gradients using a multilayer embedding that preserves within-layer connectivity while linking the same region across layers. We summarize layer-resolved organization with inter-regional and intra-regional laminar dissimilarity indices that quantify the extent to which each region’s laminar profiles differ from one another and from other regions, and relate these indices to histology-derived microstructural axes and functional hierarchies.

Methods

Twenty-one healthy adults (ages 26–42 years, 11 female) were scanned on a Siemens MAGNETOM Terra.X 7T MRI system using an 8Tx/32Rx head coil. T1-weighted MP2RAGE images (0.75 mm isotropic) were acquired and processed with an automated laminar fMRI pipeline. A bias-corrected T1-weighted image was segmented with CAT12, brainstripped using the gray and white matter surfaces, and passed to FreeSurfer recon-all with the -hires flag. Surfaces were transformed to fsLR space using ciftify recon-all with MSMSulc registration.

High-resolution resting-state fMRI was acquired with single-shot 2D GE-EPI at 0.8 mm isotropic resolution (FOV 192 mm, 135 slices with 10% gap, TR=3290 ms, TE=29 ms, flip angle 80°, 138 volumes; two subjects had 114 and 131 volumes). For 12 subjects, phase was acquired to allow time series denoising with NORDIC PCA prior to motion correction. All runs were motion-corrected with AFNI 3dVolreg and preprocessed in SPM12 with nuisance regression (constant, linear and quadratic trends, mean white matter and CSF, six motion parameters and derivatives) and high-pass filtering at 0.01 Hz; residuals were used for connectivity analyses.

Laminar segmentation was performed in functional space using LAYNII. The anatomical ribbon was upsampled fivefold, three equidistant depth bins (superficial, middle, deep) were computed, and the ribbon was downsampled back to 0.8 mm. Schaefer-400 and HCP MMP 1.0 atlases were transformed from fsLR to native surface with wb_command and ciftify, then projected into each subject’s functional space. For each depth and parcel, we extracted resting-state time series and computed parcel-wise Pearson correlation matrices, which were Fisher z-transformed and averaged across subjects.

To examine laminar functional connectivity, we constructed a multilayer adjacency matrix in which layer-specific connectivity matrices occupied the diagonal blocks and identity matrices occupied the off-diagonal blocks, connecting each parcel to itself across layers (Fig. 1A). Using BrainSpace, we computed an affinity matrix from the multilayer adjacency with a 10% row-wise sparsity threshold and performed diffusion map embedding to extract 15 gradients explaining 85% of the cumulative eigenvalue spectrum. These gradients provide a low-dimensional representation of laminar connectivity patterns across regions and depths.

Following an analysis proposed for multimodal gradients 3, we quantified inter-regional dissimilarity by extracting a laminar gradient feature vector per parcel, L2-normalizing it, and computing pairwise cosine distances between parcels. The inter-regional dissimilarity index of a parcel was defined as the mean cosine distance to all other parcels (Fig. 1B). To quantify laminar heterogeneity within each parcel, we computed an intra-regional dissimilarity index: layer-specific feature vectors were normalized, a mean direction vector across layers was obtained, and cosine distances between each layer and the mean direction were averaged to yield a single intra-regional index per parcel, together with layer-specific values for superficial, middle, and deep layers (Fig. 1C).

To link laminar indices to functional hierarchy, we used send and receive effective connectivity maps estimated with regression dynamic causal modelling (rDCM) in an independent open-source sample 4. Afferent and efferent effective connectivity matrices of the 400 Schaefer parcels were subtracted to form a send–receive asymmetry functional hierarchy. To relate laminar indices to microstructure, we used the histology-derived BigBrain microstructural profile covariance gradient distributed with the ENIGMA Toolbox, which captures a sensory-to-limbic axis of cytoarchitectural differentiation.

Results

We computed resting-state connectivity between cortical regions separately for superficial, middle, and deep layers and assembled these into a multilayer adjacency matrix. Diffusion map embedding yielded fifteen gradients from which we calculated inter-regional and intra-regional laminar dissimilarity indices: the inter-regional index captured how distinct a region’s laminar embedding profile is from all other parcels, whereas the intra-regional index indicated the distinctiveness of each region’s layer-specific embedding when compared to that region’s mean embedding across layers.

The dissimilarity indices showed network differences: visual, limbic, control, and default-mode networks had the highest values, while somatomotor, salience, and dorsal attention networks were most similar. Intra-regional dissimilarity was highest in visual, somatomotor, and dorsal attention networks. The two indices captured complementary aspects of laminar variation (r = -0.10, pspin = 0.28) and both differentiated the seven Yeo resting-state networks (F = 104, pspin < 0.001; Fig. 1D-E).

Layer-specific intra-regional indices revealed distinct laminar motifs (Fig. 2A). Deep-layer embeddings were most prominently different in somatomotor, dorsal attention, and default-mode networks; the middle-layer index did not clearly distinguish networks; whereas, the superficial index showed the largest laminar differences in visual areas. All three layer-specific indices differentiated between networks (F = 23.5, pspin = 0.0026), with high superficial values in the visual network (F = 46.1, pspin < 0.001) and high deep-layer values in the somatomotor network (F = 74.9, pspin < 0.001; Fig. 2B).

We assessed robustness by repeating the analysis using the HCP MMP 1.0 parcellation, yielding similar inter-regional, intra-regional, and layer-specific dissimilarity maps and resting-state network centroids. Layer-specific intra-regional indices were not significantly related to temporal SNR and all indices remained stable when computed on thirteen or more gradients.

To test whether layer-specific intra-regional indices reflect functional hierarchy, we defined two predictors based on feedforward and feedback connectivity based on the canonical laminar model: a receive contrast comparing middle-layer intra-regional dissimilarity to the average of superficial and deep layers, and a send contrast contrasting deep- and superficial-layer intra-regional dissimilarity (Fig. 2C). We related these predictors to the send–receive asymmetry of an rDCM-derived effective connectivity hierarchy (Fig. 2D), which indicates regions that are send-dominant or receive-dominant. In a multivariate regression, send–receive asymmetry was almost entirely negatively driven by the send predictor (partial r = -0.30, pspin = 0.012), with negligible contribution from the receive predictor (partial r = 0.003, pspin = 0.98; Fig. 2E). Parcels with high superficial-layer dissimilarity were more receiver-like, whereas parcels with higher deep-layer dissimilarity were more sender-like.

Next, we examined how laminar dissimilarity indices covary with the BigBrain microstructural profile covariance gradient (Fig. 2F). The inter-regional dissimilarity index showed a positive but non-significant correlation with the gradient (r = 0.49, pspin = 0.055), whereas intra-regional laminar dissimilarity exhibited a significant negative association (r = -0.32, pspin = 0.0071; Fig. 2G). When relating each layer-specific intra-regional index to the gradient while controlling for the other two layers, we observed opposite effects for deep and middle layers: deep showed a negative relationship (partial r = -0.30, pspin < 0.014), middle showed a positive relationship (partial r = 0.15, pspin = 0.033), and superficial did not reach significance (partial r = -0.19, pspin = 0.055; Fig. 2I).

Conclusion

Layer-specific intra-regional indices provide distinct laminar motifs reflective of task-based findings. In somatomotor cortex, deep layers are most distinct, consistent with output-related activity in deep layers of M1. Visual areas show high dissimilarity in both deep and superficial layers, in line with feedback-dominant laminar profiles reported in task-based studies of attention, memory, and prediction. Networks with low intra-regional dissimilarity display more homogeneous laminar signals across depths, which may indicate more stereotyped resting-state processing across layers and echoes tract-tracing work showing that regions with similar laminar differentiation in higher-order cortices are connected by projections spanning all layers.

These patterns are compatible with canonical laminar connectivity models and graded laminar changes across cortex. Along the cortical hierarchy, projections from less differentiated cortex originate predominantly in deep layers and terminate in superficial layers of more differentiated targets, whereas feedforward projections follow the opposite pattern 5. We observe this motif at rest: areas at the top of the functional hierarchy show higher deep-layer intra-regional dissimilarity and are send-dominant, while unimodal granular cortices show stronger superficial-layer dissimilarity and are receive-dominant. Although laminar pathways are more heterogeneous than the simplified model used to construct our predictors and three equidistant depth bins do not map exactly onto cytoarchitectonic layers, laminar dissimilarity indices still align with BigBrain-derived microstructural gradients.

Overall, the present work demonstrates that laminar dissimilarity indices calculated from multilayer functional gradients offer a description of how layer-resolved functional architecture varies across intrinsic networks, aligns with a functional connectivity hierarchy, and follows a histology-derived microstructural axis. In combination, these results suggest that laminar functional gradients provide a useful framework for studying how cortical layers contribute to large-scale communication at rest and for future probing of how hierarchical laminar organization may change across tasks, individuals, or clinical conditions.
