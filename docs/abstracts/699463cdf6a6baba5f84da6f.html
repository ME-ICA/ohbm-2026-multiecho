Introduction

Disease progression models (DPMs) hold distinct advantages over blackbox models in that they are inherently interpretable and require less data to build [1]. Current DPM approaches for neurodegenerative diseases (NDs) face key limitations: Methods such as SuStaIn models as discrete-time events [3] which do not capture spatiotemporal dynamics; Other methods model ND progression continuously without leveraging brain connectivity [2]. Existing network-based models explicitly model disease spread using connectivity but are difficult to scale onto full-brain connectomes without becoming computationally intractable, which makes subtyping infeasible. The approach described here - the Connectome-based Monotonic Inference of Neurodegenerative Dynamics (COMIND) addresses these limitations by providing a scalable, continuous, and connectome-based DPM framework. This is significant because NDs like Parkinson’s Disease exhibit both phenotypic and symptomatic heterogeneity across patients and mechanistic spread through neural pathways. By modeling disease dynamics across complete brain networks, this framework can highlight subtype-specific patterns of neurodegeneration that an approach using fewer brain regions can miss.

Methods

COMIND models neurodegeneration as a system of coupled multivariate logistic trajectories constrained by a structural connectivity matrix. For each cortical region, the rate of change depends on its current level of degeneration. There is a region specific forcing term that represents external sources of propagation which drives the initial spread of accumulation. 

Longitudinal data from 146 subjects in the PMMI dataset were used in the training and validation set split. All T-1 weighted MRI scans were processed with FreeSurfer 7.0, and cortical thickness was extracted from 68 regions of interest defined by the Desikan-Killiany atlas. The model fit maps the progression of the regional cortical thicknesses over time as well as estimating the subject-specific timeshift that denotes the time when their initial scan occurred relative to predicted disease onset. We included clinically derived biomarkers such as the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCa), tremor-dominant (TD) score, and postural instability/gait difficulty (PIGD) as privileged biomarkers to condition subject-specific timeshifts within each subtype.

Results

Two subtypes were found exhibiting divergent progressions patterns. The largest differences were found in the frontal regions of the brain particularly the left frontal pole, right caudal anterior cingulate, and right medial orbitofrontal cortex. One subtype was particularly frontal predominant, and demonstrated greater degeneration in these frontal areas (figure 1). Timeshift estimates found that the frontal-predominant subtype had earlier timeshift estimates suggesting that present symptoms sooner. The frontal-predominant subtype was characterized by MoCA and TD scores, whereas the other subtype was primarily explained by PIGD score. This suggests a relation between faster cognitive decline and TD for the frontal-predominant  subtype versus PIGD dominant subtype.

Conclusion

COMIND identified two distinct PD subtypes from in-vivo cortical thickness measurements, with a major distinction marked by early gray matter depletion in the medial frontal regions of the cortex. Future iterations of this model will incorporate greater number of subtypes as well as larger subject cohorts to find the relationship between known clinical subgroups.
