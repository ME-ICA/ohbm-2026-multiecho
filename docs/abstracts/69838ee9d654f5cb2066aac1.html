Introduction

Successful extinction learning is a key protective factor against the development of anxiety and stress-related disorders following trauma exposure. Conversely, impaired extinction has been consistently observed in patients with anxiety disorders when compared to healthy controls. These findings highlight the importance of extinction mechanisms in the etiology of anxiety disorders and motivate a focus on understanding individual differences in extinction learning even within healthy populations.

Fear extinction occurs when an expected aversive outcome (unconditioned stimulus, US) fails to materialize following presentation of a conditioned stimulus (CS), resulting in a reduction of conditioned fear responses (CRs). Associative learning models describe extinction as being driven by prediction errors (PEs) arising from unexpected US omission and suggest shared mechanisms with appetitive learning, potentially mediated by dopaminergic signaling in the ventral striatum (VS). However, these models assume gradual learning and cannot distinguish whether individual differences reflect PE generation or their translation into CR reduction.

To address these limitations, we used a data-driven approach to characterize heterogeneity in extinction learning. Latent class growth modeling (LCGM) of trial-wise skin conductance responses (SCRs) identified distinct extinction trajectories across multiple datasets from healthy participants. We then tested whether these behavioral subgroups differed in extinction-related neural activity measured with functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), focusing on VS responses to US omission at the offset of the previously reinforced stimulus (CS+). This approach allows assessment of neural contributions to individual differences in extinction learning without relying on specific associative model assumptions.

Methods

Experimental Design. We analyzed fear extinction learning across four experiments conducted in our laboratory (N=234 healthy male participants), all using the same differential fear conditioning (day 1) and extinction (day 2) paradigm. During conditioning, one visual stimulus (CS+) was partially reinforced with a painful electrical stimulus (US), whereas another stimulus (CS−) was never reinforced. On the following day, both stimuli were repeatedly presented without the US (extinction). Conditioned fear responses were measured using skin conductance responses (SCRs). Three of the four experiments were performed during functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) acquisition.

LCGM. To identify individual differences in extinction learning in a model-free manner, we applied latent class growth modeling (LCGM) to trial-wise SCRs from conditioning and extinction. LCGM is a data-driven mixture modeling approach that estimates latent subgroups characterized by distinct response trajectories over time. Linear mixed models with one to four latent classes were fitted, and model selection was based on information criteria, entropy, minimum class size, and interpretability. Participants were assigned to classes based on maximum posterior probability. To test generalizability, the same LCGM procedure was applied to two large publicly available SCR datasets (total N=275, female and male) that met predefined criteria (trial-wise data, differential conditioning design, and sufficient trial numbers), allowing replication of extinction trajectory subgroups across independent samples.

fMRI. In the subset of studies with fMRI data, we examined neural correlates of extinction learning differences between the identified classes. First-level general linear models were specified for extinction learning, including regressors for CS+ and CS− onsets and offsets. Second-level analyses tested class differences in neural responses to US omission, operationalized as CS+ versus CS− offset activity across early, mid, and late extinction. Based on prior hypotheses, analyses focused on ventral striatal regions using both whole-brain and region-of-interest approaches.

Results

Heterogeneity in extinction learning.
Trial-wise SCRs were analyzed with latent class growth modeling (LCGM). Model comparison supported a two-class solution as the most parsimonious and interpretable. The larger class (≈76%) showed rapid and complete extinction of conditioned fear responses, whereas the smaller class (≈24%) showed markedly reduced and slower extinction. These groups are referred to as fast and slow extinguishers, respectively.

The two classes showed comparable differential fear conditioning on day 1 (CS+ > CS−), indicating intact acquisition in both groups. During extinction, however, they diverged strongly: fast extinguishers showed a robust decline of differential SCRs, while slow extinguishers showed little to no differential extinction over the same period. Follow-up analyses in the subset of participants with a day 3 test indicated that slow extinguishers were not unable to extinguish per se, but exhibited delayed extinction relative to fast extinguishers.

Replication in independent datasets.
 To assess generalizability, the same LCGM procedure was applied to two large, publicly available SCR datasets using comparable differential conditioning and extinction paradigms. In both datasets, a two-class solution again emerged, revealing extinction trajectories closely matching the fast and slow extinguisher patterns observed in the laboratory data. Despite differences in reinforcement rates and study designs, fast extinguishers consistently showed pronounced extinction, whereas slow extinguishers showed attenuated extinction. These results indicate that a fast–slow extinction distinction is a robust feature of human fear extinction learning.

Ventral striatal responses to US omission.
 In the subset of participants with fMRI data (n = 122), we tested whether extinction learning types differed in neural responses to unexpected US omission during extinction. Analyses focused on ventral striatal (VS) activity at CS+ offset, the time point at which an extinction prediction error (EPE) is expected. A significant stimulus by time by group interaction was observed in the VS. Fast extinguishers showed a strong differential VS response early in extinction that declined over time, consistent with a canonical EPE signal that diminishes as US omission becomes expected. In contrast, slow extinguishers showed a delayed VS response that emerged later in extinction. Group differences were most pronounced during late extinction. This pattern indicates that individual differences in extinction learning are associated with differences in the timing and evolution of ventral striatal EPE signals.

Conclusion

Using a data-driven approach, we identified two robust extinction learning phenotypes – fast and slow extinguishers – across  four laboratory datasets and two independent public samples. These groups differed not only in the behavioral time course of conditioned fear reduction but also in ventral striatal responses to unexpected US omission during extinction. Fast extinguishers showed an early CS+ > CS− offset signal consistent with an extinction prediction error (EPE), whereas slow extinguishers exhibited a delayed response, suggesting less efficient or slower utilization of safety information. By combining latent class growth modeling with fMRI, our findings provide converging behavioral and neural evidence that individual differences in extinction learning are linked to the timing of ventral striatal EPE-like signaling. This framework advances understanding of extinction heterogeneity and may inform stratification and personalization of exposure-based interventions for anxiety and stress-related disorders.
