Introduction

          Body representation disorders (BRD) may be defined as pathological deficits related to higher cognitive representations of the body, which involves alterations in the sense of body ownership (the judgement that my body belongs to me). These disturbances are present mostly in right-hemisphere stroke patients, and can include illusions of perceptions of its body parts or confusion regarding its owner.1 In recent literature, the term disturbed sensation of limb ownership (DSO1) has been described as a broad concept associated with various manifestations of atypical limb ownership and perception, such as asomatognosia and somatoparaphrenia. The first refers to unilateral disturbances of body ownership, whereas the second one refers to illusional ideas associated with the affected limb or side of the body.1

         Studying these different clinical manifestations is essential for improving patient care. Such disturbances often compromise not only the rehabilitation process but also everyday life. At the same time, they are relatively uncommon and often described in small case series, making it difficult to characterise their neural bases comprehensively. A meta-analytic approach can partially overcome these limitations, increasing statistical power and highlighting robust, large-scale patterns that may not be apparent in individual cohorts. The present study aimed to characterise and compare the neural architecture of BRD using published lesion data. We first scrutinised the literature on the three disorders mentioned above, and derived separate meta-analytic maps from lesion coordinates reported. We then projected these maps onto a normative white-matter prior to identify the main cortical, subcortical and disconnection patterns associated with each disorder. This approach allows us to address whether these clinical manifestations are supported by distinct or overlapping networks, and how cortical and subcortical lesions relate to associated white-matter disconnections.

Methods

           The present study employed PRISMA guidelines and leveraged the Neurosynth Compose software to obtain the meta analytic maps for each disorder (DSO, asomatognosia and somatoparaphrenia).3 Prior to the addition of the papers in neurosynth compose, a specific search query of the title and abstract was conducted in PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science, in July of 2025. The query included different names variations of each disorder with different neuroimaging and lesion methods. For example, the query for DSO included the abbreviation and the full name, among other variations, and neuroimaging methods such as CT or MRI scans. The papers obtained in the search went through the following steps: identification, screening, eligibility and inclusion. During the identification, duplicates were excluded. The primary inclusion criteria for the screening process was the description of the respective body representation disorder. Exclusion criteria for screening were papers related to other disorders, with only healthy participants, studies with animals, children, without the mention of the disorder in the abstract or title, also articles focusing only on the methodological approach and other types without reporting new patients (example: book chapters, studies protocol). The benefit of this approach is that it allows a more replicable and reliable research, ensuring scientific rigour in the methodological steps. The same process was followed during the eligibility phase. However, in this step the whole paper was read and the criteria for the eligibility required that the studies had clear lesion images or coordinates. Furthermore, studies that could not be fully accessed were excluded as well. For each step, the papers were evaluated by two independent researchers. Upon disagreement, a third evaluator decided for the paper inclusion or exclusion. 

         After paper selection, the lesion coordinates provided in the manuscripts were extracted. In case of papers without the explicit coordinates indicated we retrieve the lesion images and trace them onto a standard brain template (MNI152) using neuroanatomical references as markers.4 Following this process we obtained the peak X, Y, Z coordinates of the lesions. The meta analytic maps for each body representation disorder were computed in Neurosynth Compose using coordinate based meta-analysis by applying the Multilevel kernel density analysis (MKDADensity) algorithm with a 10-mm binary kernel. Statistical significance was assessed through 5000 iterations using an approximate null distribution. False Discovery Rate correction (FDR) was applied with an alpha of 0.05. 

         To characterise the white-matter architecture associated with each body-representation disorder, we computed indirect white-matter involvement using the Functionnectome framework with ultra–high-field (7T) structural connectivity priors. Each meta-analytic map was used as input to Functionnectome. The Functionnectome projects scalar values from grey-matter voxels onto white matter by weighting them according to tractography priors derived from 7T diffusion data in healthy participants; in this context, the value at each white-matter voxel reflects a connectivity-weighted projection of the meta-analytic lesion map.

Results

We obtained in total 42 papers (DSO = 8; asomatognosia = 11; somatoparaphrenia = 23). In DSO 7 of the select papers were lesions studies and only one a case study.  The other disorders were more equally distributed, with 6 lesion studies and 5 case studies in asomatognosia. Somatoparaphrenia included 9 lesion studies and 14 case studies. Further analysis indicated that the number of reported foci varied across conditions, with 179 for DSO, 119 for asomatognosia, and 254 for somatoparaphrenia.

 For DSO, 140 recorders were identified, 29 being duplicates. In the screening phase, 5 were excluded because of their type, 5 being other disorders, 2 because they only had healthy participants and 90 because of being irrelevant, which means they were not related to body representation disorders. The high number of papers being excluded because of their irrelevantness could be explained by the abbreviation “DSO” in the search query, which identified several articles from other areas of knowledge. The preliminary eligibility phase excluded one paper due to lack of brain imaging. In asomatognosia, 137 papers were retrieved, 12 being duplicates. Following the screening exclusion criteria, 35 papers were excluded because of their type, 9 were irrelevant, 48 were about other disorders, 2 had focused on the methodological approach, 2 had no brain image and 3 had only healthy participants. The preliminary eligibility phase for asomatognosia excluded 3 papers because they did not present any useful brain imaging, 3 were for other disorders, 3 we could not access it online and 6 did not show a brain image. Lastly, in somatoparaphrenia, 133 were retrieved for the identification, 24 being duplicates. Of the remaining, 109, 60 were excluded during the screening, 8 referred to other disorders, 2 had no brain image, 38 were other types, 1 had focused only on the methods, 7 had only healthy participants, 3 were animal studies and 1 had exclusively children as participants. For the preliminary eligibility phase, 10 had no useful brain imaging, 4 we could not access, 9 did not present a brain image and 3 were referring to other disorders. 

The meta-analytic map for asomatognosia showed a modest convergence peak (z = 2.07) in a cluster of 192mm3 in the right hemisphere at MNI [18, -22, 10]. In contrast, somatoparaphrenia exhibited a robust peak of convergence (z = 4.44) at MNI [28, -30, 18] in a cluster of 9648mm3. This cluster was divided into three robust sub-peaks (z = 3.98, z = 3.44, z = 3.44), at MNI [32, -28, 6], [26, -24, 16], [22, -38, 6]. Four additional clusters were found at MNI [42, -60, 10], [44, -18, -16], [20, -32, 28], [34, -60, 18], showing modest convergence peak (z = 2.71, z = 2.71, z = 1.87, z = 1.87), with a cluster size of 560mm3, 136mm3, 112mm3, and 400mm3 respectively. Finally, the meta-analytic map for DSO did not reach conventional significance, with only a weak peak (z = 1.02). Across the three neuropsychological syndromes our meta-analytic lesion mapping revealed both syndrome-specific patterns and a shared subcortical-white matter architecture (Fig 1). Asomatognosia was associated predominantly with lesions to the basal ganglia, thalamus, and occipital lobe, together with their projection systems. Somatoparaphrenia showed partially overlapping subcortical involvement (thalamus and basal ganglia) but extended more posteriorly, encompassing the posterior corpus callosum (splenium), superior temporal cortex, and long occipito-frontal-parietal pathways. In contrast, DSO was characterised by lesions affecting the basal ganglia, the inferior frontal gyrus, and extensive fronto-occipital tracts, with additional disruption of the body of the corpus callosum. When these meta-analytic maps were projected onto a normative structural-connectivity prior, the involvement of long-range associative systems, particularly fronto-parietal, fronto-occipital, and temporo-parietal pathways, together with key commissural fibres was confirmed.

Conclusion

              Our preliminary results align with and extend current network accounts of body-ownership disorders by showing that the quality of the disturbance in each syndrome maps onto partially distinct, but overlapping, lesion-disconnection profiles. The consistent implication of basal ganglia and thalamus across asomatognosia, somatoparaphrenia and DSO supports the idea that subcortical hubs play a domain-general role in maintaining coherent body representations, by relaying and integrating sensorimotor information to higher-order cortical areas.

              In asomatognosia, patients typically report the contralesional limb or body side as if it does not belong to them. The prominent involvement of basal ganglia, thalamus and occipital cortex fits classical descriptions of personal neglect and body unawareness following posterior cerebral artery strokes, and supports the view that these subcortical–occipital structures provide a basic visuospatial and somatosensory scaffold for representing the body in egocentric space.4 When this scaffold is degraded, the limb tends to drop out of ownership experience rather than being positively reassigned to someone else, which is the phenomenology seen in asomatognosia. Somatoparaphrenia, by contrast, is characterised by a vivid misattribution of the limb to another agent. The additional involvement of superior temporal cortex, the splenium of the corpus callosum and long occipito–frontal connections found in our meta-analysis dovetails with lesion and disconnection studies that emphasise temporo-parietal and interhemispheric disruption in this syndrome. 

             Superior temporal regions and posterior callosal fibres are key for integrating multisensory body signals with perspective-taking and for sharing visual and semantic information between hemispheres. Damage here, and the subcortical hubs, provides a plausible substrate for externally oriented, narrative confabulations about the limb. The pattern observed in DSO occupies an intermediate, more frontally biased position. Clinically, these patients often recognise the limb and its movements but report a persistent feeling that it is not really theirs, possibly accompanied by attribution of feelings toward the limb.1 The involvement of basal ganglia together with the inferior frontal gyrus, occipito-frontal networks and the body of the corpus callosum is consistent with models that ascribe to frontal-striatal and callosal circuits a role in evaluating signals as self-generated and updating internal models of the body.5  

  Taken together, these findings support the view that disorders of body ownership reflect graded disruptions within a common subcortical network, with syndrome-specific patterns determined by the relative involvement of posterior representational vs anterior evaluative hubs. By linking phenomenology to distinct lesion-disconnection profiles, our study refines the taxonomy of these conditions and provides a network-level framework to guide future diagnostic and rehabilitative approaches.
