Introduction

Brain fingerprinting refers to identifying individuals by the unique patterns in their brain activity and this fingerprint can be seen in the functional connectivity profiles derived from functional MRI data. ^1 Brain fingerprinting has been demonstrated in resting-state and task fMRI data ^2, however the patterns that make someone identifiable can change with age ^3 and disease e.g. Alzheimer's ^4.  

 These brain fingerprinting studies have been performed on sample sizes of a few hundred participants, often from a single imaging time point (a typical methodological approach is to compare the functional connectomes from the first half and second half of the same scan).  This research study aimed to replicate these previous findings, with longitudinal data (two imaging time points) and on a much larger sample of participants. Once replicated, this gives us greater confidence in the ability to use functional connectomes as a metric to understand the similarities and differences between people’s brains, and how they change. With these foundations, we can continue to explore how these connections may change with participant characteristics such as chronological age or age-related conditions.

Methods

Our study made use of imaging-derived phenotypes and pre-processed image data generated by an image-processing pipeline developed and run on behalf of UK Biobank ^5 (also explained in https://biobank.ctsu.ox.ac.uk/crystal/crystal/docs/brain_mri.pdf). The participant cohort we analysed consisted of 2,624participants; they were included in the analysis if they had a pre-computed resting-state fMRI time-series on two separate imaging visits from the Glasser HCP-MMP1 parcellation, with no null values. The Glasser atlas defines 360 cortical regions; a BOLD-fMRI signal was averaged across all voxels within each region to generate a region-wise time series. Fig. 1 (panel A) shows the age of these participants, and the time between these two visits. 

All data preparation, pre-processing and analysis of these pre-computed time-series was performed on the UK Biobank’s cloud-based Research Analysis Platform, predominantly using Python. With these time-series, we calculated a Pearson correlation between each pair of regions, creating a functional connectome (correlation matrix) for each of the 2,624 participants, for each of the two imaging visits (Fig. 1, panel B).  

Collating across all 5,248 correlation matrices, we created an identifiability matrix (Fig. 1, panel C) ^2 which allowed us to visualise how similar a participant is to themselves versus to other people. The diagonal values are correlation with self (iself) and the off-diagonal values are correlation with another participant (iother); we are referring here to a correlation between two functional connectomes. Each participant has one iself value and 2,623 possible iother values. Instead of taking a mean iother value for each participant, we structured the analysis question as “How similar is a participant to themselves versus a randomly selected other person?” We ran this 1,000 times and create a bootstrap distribution of mean differences between iself and iother, at the group level.

To investigate the between-subject and within-subject variance contributions more specifically, we quantified test–retest reliability of the functional connectome at the edge level using Intraclass Correlation Coefficients (ICC). Using the PyReliMRI package (https://pyrelimri.readthedocs.io/en/latest/introduction.html) we ran a one-way effects ICC analysis (type 1,1) for each edge in the correlation matrix and visualised these results. 

Results

The summaries of the identifiability matrix show that iself values (correlation with self) are higher than iother values (correlation with other), with a mean difference of 0.19 (Fig. 1 panel C; Fig. 2 panel A). Specifically, looking pairwise for each participant (iself versus a mean iother), only 38 participants out of the 2,624 had iself values that were lower than iother values. These outlier participants were always in the low percentile for their iself value, specifically the 7th percentile or lower.  

The ICC matrix (Fig. 2, panel B) shows regional variation across the Yeo networks. This is further broken down via the violin plots (Fig. 2, panel C) which display the distribution of values for all within and between network pairs. On average, the within-network ICC values are higher than the between-network, however the plot shows further variability within each network pairing. An edge with a high ICC is what we desire for brain fingerprinting; this means high between-subject variance and low within-subject variance. An edge with a low ICC is harder to interpret; it either means the connection is noisy and unstable within individuals or the edge is stable but nearly identical across people. Future inspection of within-subject and between-subject variance matrices, output from the ICC workflow, may be able to help us distinguish between these two cases.  

Future analyses can use this ICC matrix for feature selection; exploring the relationship between functional connectomes (thresholded with this ICC matrix) and participant characteristics such as chronological age or age-related conditions. Taking a rule of thumb for an ICC(1,1) analysis being fair reliability 0.40–0.59, good reliability 0.60–0.74 and excellent reliability over 0.75, we added these thresholds to the plot in Fig 2 and explored which pairs remain. There are no values ≥0.75 but many >0.4 and >0.6. Using the >0.6 threshold (good reliability) we find that 272 pairs remain (see the number annotations on Fig 2 Panel C). 

Conclusion

These results replicate findings in previous literature ^1,2 using an unprecedented sample size and true repeated measures, showing it is possible to use functional connectomes derived from resting-state fMRI data to identify unique patters of brain activity in individuals. Though there is similarity in functional connectomes between people, there are enough differences to act as a brain fingerprint, and some network connections in the brain contribute to this brain fingerprint more than others. These findings show what can be gained when focusing on individual differences, rather than group averages and group differences, in fMRI research. To our knowledge, this is the largest sample to show evidence of brain fingerprinting.  

The ICC values we report are in the fair to good categories for reliability, with none in the excellent category. To potentially improve these, and improve the distinction between iself and iother distributions, we could perform further denoising strategies such as partial correlation for the functional connectomes ^3 or further residualisation of these connectomes via regression, to remove known noise confounds. For any future work that aims to explore how these connections may change with participant characteristics such as chronological age ^3 or age-related conditions ^4, further stratification of participants into groups, alongside further confound removal, will be important to ensure valid research conclusions. Nevertheless, are current results show promising foundations for future work of brain fingerprinting.
