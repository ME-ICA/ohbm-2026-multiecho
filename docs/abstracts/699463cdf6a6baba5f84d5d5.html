Introduction

Current standard fMRI analysis methods use only a small portion of the information that is contained in the data. Methods based on detecting the magnitude of blood oxygenation-level dependent (BOLD) responses, or detecting temporal correlations of BOLD signals between regions, do not provide information about inhibitory or excitatory effects or the direction of neural signaling. Structural and Physiological Modeling (SAPM) is a novel fMRI connectivity analysis method that can provide this missing information 1,2. SAPM combines anatomical information and physiological models to estimate the input and output signaling of each region of an extended network. The method requires a predefined anatomical network model, and anatomical regions are divided into equal-sized subregions to allow for local differences in function. Estimated connectivity values indicate how input signals are scaled to contribute to output signaling, with positive values indicating excitatory signaling effects and negative values indicating inhibitory effects. The total input signaling to each region is equal to the observed BOLD responses. Temporal variations are accounted for by latent (unobserved) inputs to the network, which represent external stimuli, cognitive influences, etc.

The objective of the study was to use existing fMRI data sets to map the neural pathways involved with nociception and pain processing. Comparisons between groups and conditions were done to obtain information about the functions of various components of the pathways that were identified. Analyses involved comparisons across study groups including healthy participants (HC) and a group of people with the chronic pain syndrome, fibromyalgia (FM), who had been studied while undergoing calibrated thermal stimuli to evoke moderate pain. The results were also verified by comparing with results from a second group of healthy participants who had been subjected to noxious stimulation at three different fixed temperatures.

Methods

Participants who had been previously diagnosed with FM were recruited from the local community, as well as a comparable group of pain-free participants (HC). First, participants underwent training and familiarization with the thermal stimulus and the 0-10 pain rating scale. The stimulus consisted of brief (1.5 sec) contacts of a heated aluminum thermode in an MRI-compatible device, with onsets every 2.5 seconds to produce temporal summation of second pain. This method has been used for studies of FM because it demonstrates effects of central sensitization 3. Participants then underwent a series of trials to calibrate the stimulus to evoke a pain rating of approximately 5 on the 0-10 scale.

FMRI sessions were carried out at 3 tesla and consisted of 10 interleaved runs. Participants were able to view a rear-projection screen to provide information during each run. The paradigm lasted 4.5 minutes and included periods when the participants were informed of the impending stimulus and could anticipate the pain, then experience the pain, and then know that the pain had passed. The “fast-contact” condition is described above. A “slow-contact” condition was used for comparison, with contacts of 1.5 second duration presented every 5 seconds. The slow contact condition is not expected to evoke temporal summation of pain in HC participants. Each run involved the fast-contact or the slow-contact condition and five runs with each type of stimulation were carried out in a pseudo-randomized order. Data were acquired from the brainstem and cervical spinal cord using our established T2-weighted BOLD fMRI method which provides optimal data quality 4.

FMRI data from all runs were analyzed using our “Pantheon” analysis package in Python (shared on GitHub). This includes methods for data organization, pre-processing, and analysis at all levels of the central nervous system. It includes references images and region maps spanning the CNS, compiled from numerous sources 1,2.

SAPM was applied to data from each participant, combined across runs of the same type. This method requires a predefined anatomical model of plausible neural signaling between regions. The chosen model was based on descriptions of pain processing pathways 5. The model consisted of 10 regions including the right dorsal horn (DH) of the spinal cord at the level of the 6th cervical spinal cord segment (C6RD) corresponding to stimulation of the thumb side of the right hand. It also included regions known to be involved with descending pain regulation; the thalamus, periaqueductal gray region (PAG), and the nucleus raphe magnus (NRM) and nucleus gigantocellularis (NGc) in the rostral medulla. In addition, regions known to influence descending regulation were included; hypothalamus, locus coeruleus (LC), parabrachial nuclei (PBN), nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS), and the dorsal reticular nucleus of the caudal medulla (DRt). These regions were further subdivided into five equal-size subregions, using k-means clustering, to account for within-region variations in function. An iterative search algorithm was used to identify optimal subregion combinations based on BOLD responses which fit the network model. The C6RD subregion was fixed to include the tip of the dorsal horn (DH, laminae I and II) for some combinations of subregions.

Additional analyses were carried out to test the results, using data from a group of 42 HC (21 female, 21 male) from a previous study. This group of participants was subjected to the same fast-contact stimulation paradigm but rather with 3 fixed temperatures (38, 46,  and 51 C) in order to investigate a range of responses.

For each application of SAPM, significant connectivity values were identified based on group average values being significantly different than zero (2-tailed T-test). Alternatively, analyses of covariance (ANCOVAs) were used to compare connectivity values between FM vs HC, while accounting for variations in pain ratings across participants.

Results

Using data from a group of participants with FM and an age- and sex-matched control group (HC), SAPM revealed several ascending and descending pathways with relatively consistent connectivity values which are summarized here (Fig. 1). When sets of subregions involved the intermediate dorsal horn of the spinal cord, reciprocal pathways were identified between the NRM and NGc which were inhibitory in both directions. Both right and left sides of the NGc were tested and were found to be connected with the NRM. Excitatory signaling pathways of PAGàHypothalamus, HypothalamusàPAG, LCàThalamus, NTSàPAG, and NTSàHypothalamus, were identified. Inhibitory signaling pathways that were identified included LCàNGc and C6RDàPBN. When subregion sets included the tip of the dorsal horn (laminae I, II) signaling pathways were identified between the rostral medulla and the spinal cord as excitatory signaling along C6RDàNRM, and inhibitory signaling NRMàC6RD. However, when subregions included the intermediate dorsal horn, inhibitory signaling was identified along pathways known to be involved with descending pain regulation, including PAGàNGc and NGcàC6RD.

For comparison, signaling pathways investigated using data from healthy participants with stimulation at three different temperatures identified many of the same connections. The consistent connectivity values are included in the summary in Fig 1.

Connections of interest are also those which varied in strength in relation to stimulation temperatures and pain responses. ANCOVAs were carried out to identify group differences (FM vs HC) while accounting for the effects of variations in pain intensity and unpleasantness ratings.  When the C6RD subregion was in the intermediate dorsal horn, the connections with the lowest p-values were primarily along descending pathways and were related to pain intensity ratings. These connections included LCàNGc and NGcàC6RD which had strong interaction effects (p < 0.0055), and LCàNRM which showed a main effect of pain intensity ratings (p < 0.05). However, several ascending pathways were also identified including NRMàHypothalamus which had a strong main effect of pain intensity ratings (p < 0.001).

In comparison, when the C6RD subregion was at the tip of the dorsal horn the neural signaling pathways which were identified were almost exclusively ascending pathways and were more strongly related to pain unpleasantness ratings. These connections included C6RDàPBN and C6RDàThalamus, both with main effects of pain unpleasantness ratings (p < 0.009). Two notable connections which had significant main effects of the study group (FM vs HC) were C6RDàNRM and C6RDàNGc (p < 0.05).

Conclusion

The results demonstrate detailed networks of lower brain, brainstem, and spinal cord regions that are involved with pain and nociceptive signaling. The regions within the rostral medulla, including the NRM and NGc, are well-established as being important components of descending pain regulation 5. However, the present results provide the first-ever demonstration of reciprocal inhibitory feed-back between the NRM and the NGc. Moreover, they show details of the connectivity being with the NGc on both the right and left sides of the body. Notably, the results demonstrate ascending input from the tip of the cord DH only to the left NGc, and the only descending input to the DH that was consistently observed was from the left NGc. The connectivity values for this NGcàC6RD pathway varied across participants in relation to pain ratings.

A key aspect of pain regulation that appears to be demonstrated by the present results is that descending signaling to the spinal cord DH is predominantly to the intermediate regions, with some ascending feed-back from this subregion as well. This subregion likely corresponds with laminae III-V. Conversely, combinations of subregions which best fit the network model and which included the tip of the DH (laminae I, II) involved ascending signaling pathways. Based on known neuroanatomy it is expected that regulation of neurons within the superficial DH occurs via interneurons, and this appears to be relayed from the intermediate DH in our data.

The present results also demonstrate the influence of the PAG and LC on descending pain regulation. While the LC appears to provide inhibitory input to the NGc subregion which also relays signaling to the superficial DH, this same LC region, and a region of the PAG, also provide excitatory signaling to a region of the NRM. The influences of other regions on pain regulation were also detected, such as the thalamus, hypothalamus, NTS and PBN. The information provided includes details of the PAG subregions such as the right dorsolateral PAG receiving ascending input from the DH and the dorsomedial PAG providing input to the NRM.

Advanced network analyses with SAPM can reveal unprecedented, detailed information about neural signaling involved with human pain regulation, including inhibitory and excitatory effects and specific anatomical subregions. This information can reveal details of how human pain processing varies across different stimuli as well as differences between healthy people and people with chronic pain. The present results have provided new information about reciprocal inhibitory signaling between regions of the rostral medulla (NRM and NGc) and how these regions relay information to/from the spinal cord dorsal horn. The results also reveal a pattern of ascending signaling from the tip of the DH, with descending regulation projecting to the intermediate DH. The results obtained with SAPM analysis of fMRI data thus provide new insights into the neural basis of human pain.
