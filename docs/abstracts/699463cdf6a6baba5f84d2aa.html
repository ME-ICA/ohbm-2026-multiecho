Introduction

Early-life exposure to environmental chemicals during vulnerable developmental windows may affect brain maturation.1 Organophosphate flame retardants (OPFR), increasingly used as substitutes for banned Polybrominated Diphenyl Ethers (PBDE), are now detected in most pregnant women and children.2 Experimental studies indicate that OPFRs may be neurotoxic, affecting neurotransmission and key neurodevelopmental pathways, and some compounds have been shown to cross the placental barrier. Epidemiological evidence remains limited, mostly based on prenatal exposure only, with little consideration of infancy—a critical period of rapid brain development. Moreover, no study has investigated whether OPFR exposure relates to MRI-derived markers of brain structure or function, despite the sensitivity of neuroimaging to environmental influences on cortical maturation and white matter microstructure.3

Aims: The overarching goal of this project is to investigate associations between prenatal and infancy exposures to OPFRs and brain morphometry, white-matter integrity, and cognitive performance. In this abstract, we focus specifically on the association between postnatal OPFR exposure (1-year urinary metabolites) and brain morphometry derived from structural MRI.

Methods

The children for this study were selected from an existing mother-child cohort SEPAGES (https://cohorte-sepages.fr) which includes 471 children. For the present study, we focus on the subset of children who completed the 9-year neuroimaging visit (74 MRI datasets currently available, 48 boys / 26 girls). Images were acquired on a Philips Achieva 3T system. Urinary concentrations of 4 (pregnancy) to 9 (infancy) OPFR metabolites were measured in urine samples collected during pregnancy (up to 42 urine samples pooled per participant) and infancy at 1 year (up to 7 urine samples pooled per participant). MRI at age 9 comprises: structural MRI, diffusion MRI using diffusion kurtosis (DKI) and functional MRI during executive function tasks. Structural MRI were analysed with the VolBrain package (https://volbrain.net/home).We selected VolBrain because it has been shown to be robust and reliable. With this package, we extracted volumetric measures for 285 selected brain regions based on the Neuromorphometrics atlas, regional cortical thickness, as well as hippocampal subfield volumes, a structure known to be sensitive to environmental pollutants. To evaluate associations between regional brain morphology and OPFR exposure, we fitted multiple linear regression models with ln-transformed postnatal OPFR concentrations as predictors, adjusting for sex and intracranial volume (ICV) for the present analyses. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to account for multiple comparisons.

Results

Morphometric analyses revealed several significant associations between infancy OPFR exposures at 1 year and several regional brain structures. Increased urinary concentrations of diphenyl phosphate (DPHP), an OPFR metabolite, were associated with reduced cortical thickness in orbitofrontal and occipital regions. Specifically, within these structures significant FDR-corrected associations were observed in: right middle orbital gyrus (β = –0.147 for 1 unit increase in ln-transformed DPHP concentration, p_FDR = 0.026), total thickness of the pars orbitalis (β = –0.107, p_FDR = 0.026), left pars orbitalis (β = –0.106, p_FDR = 0.033), right pars orbitalis (β = –0.106, p_FDR = 0.029) and right calcarine cortex (β = –0.106, p_FDR = 0.047). 

Conclusion

Morphometric analyses revealed significant associations between infancy OPFR exposure—particularly DPHP—and reduced cortical thickness within frontal and occipital cortices. Significant FDR-corrected effects were localized in the middle orbital gyrus and pars orbitalis, regions involved in different processes such as behavioral disorders, olfaction, emotional regulation and in the calcarine cortex involved in early-stage visual processing.

These spatial patterns are consistent with emerging neuroimaging evidence from other classes of environmental chemicals. For example, Peterson et al.4 reported that prenatal exposure to the organophosphate pesticide chlorpyrifos was associated with cortical thinning in orbitofrontal, lateral prefrontal, temporal, and occipital cortices, regions overlapping with the OPFR-sensitive areas identified in our analyses. Similarly, Werder et al.5 found that early-life exposure to phthalates was linked to alterations in gray- and white-matter morphology in frontal and temporal regions, reinforcing the idea that multiple classes of ubiquitous environmental contaminants may converge on similar neurodevelopmental pathways. The convergence between our findings and these independent studies strengthens the interpretation that environmental chemicals may interfere with cortical maturation, particularly within prefrontal–occipital circuits. As MRI data acquisition is still ongoing (total planned sample N ≈ 200), these associations will be refined and validated as the data collection expands.
