Introduction

Tinnitus, the perception of sound without an external stimulus, affects 10-15% of adults and is therefore a significant health concern. It is most often associated with hearing loss, either noise-induced or age-related, which reduces the auditory input and triggers neural changes in (extra)auditory brain networks, such as increased spontaneous activity along the auditory pathway. 

About 25% of patients show changes in tinnitus pitch or loudness due to altered somatosensory afference from the cervical spine or temporomandibular area, known as somatic tinnitus (ST).  Neck or jaw dysfunctions, such as increased muscle tension or restricted mobility, can alter tinnitus perception.  Animal studies provide neurophysiological explanations: somatosensory fibers from the dorsal root ganglia or trigeminal ganglion project to the cochlear nuclei (CN) and inferior colliculus (IC), allowing input from the head and neck region to alter CN firing rate and synchrony, thereby changing tinnitus loudness or pitch.1 Other studies suggest that these projections are primarily excitatory (glutamatergic).2,3 Trigeminal ganglion inputs can exert both excitatory and inhibitory (GABAergic) effects on CN activity,4 while trigeminal nucleus projections to the IC appear mostly inhibitory.4,5 This suggests that increased activity in these pathways elevates CN firing. However, it is still unclear why cervical or temporomandibular dysfunction alters tinnitus in some patients but not in others. This study, therefore, investigates potential differences in γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) and combined Glutamate/Glutamine (Glx) levels in the brainstem and auditory cortex between patients with ST and those with other tinnitus types. As the first human study exploring these neurochemical differences, it aims to provide new insights into the mechanisms underlying this specific tinnitus subgroup.

Methods

For this analysis, thirteen patients with ST and thirteen age- and sex-matched patients with non-somatic tinnitus participated in this study. All subjects were right-handed. ST patients were diagnosed using the diagnostic criteria for ST, while somatic influence was absent in the non-somatic tinnitus group. The severity of tinnitus and neck complaints was assessed using the Tinnitus Functional Index (TFI) questionnaire and the Neck Bournemouth Questionnaire (NBQ), respectively. Pure-tone audiometry was conducted using a two-channel Interacoustics AC-40 audiometer in accordance with current clinical standards (ISO 8253-1, 1989). Air-conduction thresholds were obtained at frequencies of 125 Hz, 250 Hz, 500 Hz, 1 kHz, 2 kHz, 3 kHz, 4 kHz, 6 kHz, 8 kHz, 9 kHz, 10 kHz, 11.2 kHz, 12.5 kHz, 14 kHz, and 16 kHz. When air-conduction thresholds exceeded the normal hearing level of 20 dB HL at one or more frequencies between 250 Hz and 4 kHz, bone-conduction thresholds were additionally measured at 250 Hz, 500 Hz, 1 kHz, 2 kHz, 3 kHz, and 4 kHz. Pure tone averages (PTA1-2-4 kHz) were calculated based on the thresholds at 1, 2 and 4 kHz of the best ear. 

Imaging was performed on a 3T Philips Achieva magnetic resonance (MR) scanner with a 32-channel head coil. First, an anatomical T1-weighted image was acquired using a three-dimensional turbo field echo (3DTFE) sequence (echo time (TE) = 4.6 ms; repetition time (TR) = 9.7 ms; flip angle = 8°; field of view = 256 × 242 × 182 mm; 182 sagittal slices; voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm3). The anatomical T1-weighted image was followed by five consecutive 1H-Magnetic Resonance Spectroscopy (MRS) acquisitions using the J-difference editing MEscher-Garwood Point RESolved Spectroscopy sequence (MEGA-PRESS) (TR = 2000 ms; TE = 68 ms) to measure GABA plus macromolecules (GABA+) and Glx levels. MRS voxels of interest (VOI) were placed in the right and left auditory cortices (28.8 ml; 160 ON/160 OFF spectra), right and left primary sensorimotor cortices (27 ml; 112 ON/112 OFF spectra), and the brainstem (9 ml; 160 ON/160 OFF spectra). Automated second-order pencil-beam shimming and (MOIST) water suppression (bandwidth 140 Hz) were applied. For each MRS acquisition, 16 unsuppressed water averages were collected within the same VOI, adopting the same acquisition parameters and later used as a reference metabolite.

Data were processed using Gannet (v3.5.1), which included eddy-current correction, 3 Hz line broadening, zero-filling to 32k points, transient averaging, and robust spectral registration for frequency and phase correction. GABA+ and Glx were quantified using Gannet’s five-parameter Gaussian-Lorentzian model, which fits GABA+ and Glx peaks (2.79–4.1 ppm) with a curved baseline. Water signals were modeled with a Gaussian-Lorentzian lineshape, and metabolite levels were expressed relative to water (GABA+/H₂O and Glx/H₂O). GannetCoRegister and GannetSegment (SPM12) were used to coregister MRS voxels to T1 images and compute tissue fractions (i.e., gray matter (GM), white matter (WM), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)). Metabolite quantification and tissue correction were performed using GannetQuantify and by applying the Gasparovic method, which accounts for tissue-specific water visibility and metabolite/water T1 and T2 values. Unpaired t-tests (α = 0.05) were applied for normally distributed data; when not normally distributed, group differences were tested using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests (α = 0.05). Statistical analyses were performed in RStudio (R version 4.5.2).

Results

PTA1-2-4 kHz levels (p=0.62) and TFI (p=0.94) scores did not significantly differ between groups. GABA+ levels did not differ between the ST and non-ST group in the brainstem (p=0.72), right (p=0.78) and left (p=0.28) auditory cortices, and right (p=0.31) and left (p=0.90) sensorimotor cortices. In addition, no differences in Glx levels were observed between groups in the brainstem (p=0.76), right (p=0.96) and left (p=0.12) auditory cortices, and right (p=0.60) and left (p=0.17) sensorimotor cortices.

This interim analysis, based on 13 participants per group, is currently underpowered to detect small or nuanced differences in metabolite levels. Recruitment is ongoing, and the final results may differ once the full sample has been acquired. Although the present findings show no significant differences between the ST and non-ST tinnitus groups, this may indicate either a true absence of group differences or that any existing effects are too small to be detected at this stage. In the completed study, the addition of a third group, patients with neck pain but without tinnitus, will allow a more comprehensive evaluation of whether GABA+ and Glx concentrations differentiate between tinnitus subtypes or between tinnitus and non-tinnitus groups. This broader comparison will help determine whether GABA+ and Glx levels specifically explain the presence of ST, whether they are shared across both tinnitus groups and therefore tinnitus-related, or whether the ST group more closely resembles the neck-pain group, which would suggest that any differences may be driven by underlying somatosensory mechanisms rather than tinnitus itself.

Conclusion

Currently, no differences in GABA+ and Glx levels could be identified in the brainstem, auditory cortices, or sensorimotor cortices between patients with ST and non-ST. Future analyses will include a larger sample and will compare (non)somatic tinnitus patients with patients with neck pain without tinnitus.
