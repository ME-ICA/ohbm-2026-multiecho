Introduction

Mild traumatic brain injury (mTBI) or concussion is a common neurological condition defined as a brain disruption resulting from a blow or jolt to the head, neck, or body. External mechanical forces transmitted to the brain lead to neurological symptoms known as post-concussive symptoms (e.g., headaches, concentration difficulties, fatigue).

Pediatric mTBI is a major global public health issue, affecting more than 2.7 million children (0–18 years) worldwide each year.1 Young children (< 6 years) are at the greatest risk, with an estimated incidence of 2% compared to 1% in children aged 6–15. They may also be the most vulnerable to lasting sequelae, as early childhood (0–5 years) is a critical period for brain development. Any disruption could lead to adverse outcomes.

Some magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) studies in children aged 8 to 18 reported macrostructural changes following mTBI, such as reduced regional brain volumes and cortical thickness compared to uninjured peers, while others report thicker cortex or no difference.2–4 These alterations involve the frontal and prefrontal, parietal, temporal, and left occipital cortices2,3 and persist for several some months post-injury, particularly in frontal and occipital regions, appearing more pronounced in girls, younger children, and those with persistent post-concussive symptoms.2,3 Given the high vulnerability of the young child’s brain, early mTBI may be associated with different patterns brain alterations, potentially more severe and persistent than those observed in older children. Despite the high prevalence of mTBI in young children and the characteristics associated with this period of heightened neuroplasticity, most MRI studies have focused on children older than 6 years2, leaving substantial gaps in understanding of brain alterations in early childhood mTBI.

This study aims to use MRI to characterize gray matter structural alterations in young children with mTBI aged 3 to 6 years, one-month post-injury.

Methods

This study was a planned analysis of data from the pilot study (DOLFIN) and the prospective, multicenter, longitudinal cohort study Kids’ Outcomes And Long- term Abilities (KOALA) on the impact of early mTBI on child development.5

Participants

Children aged 6 months to 5.99 years with accidental mTBI or orthopedic injury (OI) were recruited from three pediatric emergency departments (PEDs) in Canada and the USA, and compared to typically developing children (TDC) recruited from the general population. Injured children presented to the PED within 48 hours of a mild non-penetrating head trauma (mTBI; age-appropriate Glasgow score: 13–15, ≥1 mTBI symptom such as headache or any loss of consciousness) or a limb injury (simple fracture or other extremity trauma). Exclusion criteria included insufficient parental mastery of English or French, severe medical or developmental disorders, birth before 37 weeks’ gestation, TBI within the past 3 months or TBI requiring hospitalization, or absence of a legal guardian. Additional criteria for mTBI and OI groups included abusive injury, hypoxia, hypotension, or shock, sedative medicine, neurosurgical intervention. Canadian participants aged 3 to 5.99 years were invited to participate in the MRI sub-study.5

MRI data acquisition and preprocessing

Advanced MRI scans were acquired one month after injury without sedation using a validated age-appropriate behavioral familiarization protocol.5 MRI sequences were performed on 3T GE scanners at CHU Sainte-Justine and Alberta Children's Hospital, including T1-weighted MRI for identifying anatomical abnormalities and performing brain morphometric analyses.

Of 80 participants scanned, 15 were excluded after visual quality control with ITK-SNAP for inadequate imaging quality, yielding a final sample of 65 children divided into two groups (Fig.1): 30 with mTBI (mean age = 59.6 months; 50% girls) and 35 from the OI/TDC group (mean age = 60.0 months; 54.3% girls).

Preprocessing of raw T1w images included N4Field-algorithm (ANTS) for biais correction, segmentation and normalization to MNI space with CAT12 (SPM25-Matlab R2023a) generating derived images such as normalized modulated gray matter maps (mwp1) for voxel-wise morphometric analysis, smoothing of mwp1 images with 8 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel using the SPM25 module with default settings, and estimation of total intracranial volume (TIV) via CAT12, with values used as a covariate to correct for differences in overall brain volume between subjects.

Statistical analyses

Second-level analyses in CAT12 (Basic-Models) compared mTBI and OI/TDC groups using an F-test to assess gray matter density differences. No significant differences were observed between OI and TDC in the identified clusters, supporting their combination into a single control group.

Covariates included age (months), sex, scanner and TIV included via ANCOVA. Orthogonality and homogeneity checks confirmed weak correlations, ensuring TIV did not remove relevant variance.

An explicit gray matter mask was created by averaging mwp1 and mwp2 (white matter) maps for the OI/TDC group. A voxel was included in this mask if its mean gray matter probability exceeded both the white matter probability and a threshold of 0.3. This mask was then applied to restrict the analysis to gray matter voxels, in combination with absolute masking, whereby voxels with a gray matter probability below a threshold of 0.1 were excluded to remove non-brain tissue and low-probability voxels.

The statistical model was estimated and voxel-wise contrasts defined to assess group differences. Only clusters with uncorrected p<0.001 and at least 100 voxels were considered significant (threshold of F=12.02). Effect direction was determined by extracting mean gray matter density values for each cluster and group.

Results

Age and sex distributions did not differ significantly between groups (Age: t-test = 0.15, p = 0.88; Sex: χ² = 0.009, p = 0.92). 

Results revealed six clusters with lower gray matter density in mTBI participants compared to controls, suggesting a relative decrease in modulated gray matter density in these regions (Fig.2a, b):

- A major cluster of 1122 voxels, with a peak at MNI = [-22, 6, 7.5], (F = 31.13, uncorrected p < 0.001)   representing the cluster's maximum intensity and the most robust group effect across the brain, located in   the left putamen. This is the only cluster to survive FWE correction (pFWE=0.019)

- A cluster of 871 voxels, with a peak at MNI = [28, 6, -6] (F = 17.19, uncorrected p < 0.001). The cluster       overall reflects a robust group difference, located in the right putamen.

- A cluster of 306 voxels, peak at MNI = [–15, –37.5, 1.5] (F = 26.3, uncorrected p < 0.001), located in the left agranular retrosplenial cortex (Brodmann area 30)

- A cluster of 259 voxels, with a peak at MNI = [12, 39, 55.5] (F = 15.05, uncorrected p < 0.001), located in the right superior frontal gyrus, corresponding to the right frontal eye field (Brodmann area 8).

- A cluster of 119 voxels, with a peak at MNI = [46.5, -19.5, 57] (F = 18.4, uncorrected p < 0.001), located in the right primary motor cortex (Brodmann Area 4).

- A cluster of 114 voxels, with a peak at MNI = [-27, -96, 4.5] (F = 19.30, uncorrected p < 0.001), located in the left middle occipital gyrus (Brodmann Area 18, secondary visual cortex).

In contrast, two clusters with higher gray matter density in mTBI participants compared to controls (Figure 2a, b):

- A cluster of 185 voxels, with a peak at MNI = [36, –48, –58.5] (F = 15.02, uncorrected p < 0.001), located on the inferior surface of the right posterior cerebellar lobe.

- A cluster of 146 voxels, with a peak at MNI = [–30, 45, 21] (F = 4.04, uncorrected p < 0.001), located in the left anterior prefrontal cortex (Brodmann Area 10).

Conclusion

This study aimed to investigate gray matter alterations in young children who had sustained a mild traumatic brain injury, to better understand the structural consequences of mTBI in early childhood, one month after the injury. These results indicate that the pattern of structural changes following early mTBI is heterogeneous, with both regional decreases and increases in gray matter volumes. Gray matter reductions were observed bilaterally in the putamen in children with mTBI compared to their peers. The putamen is primarily involved in motor control, participating in the planning, execution, and coordination of voluntary movements, as well as motor and procedural learning. It also plays a role in habits, motivation, and certain cognitive functions, including action selection and reward processing. These alterations may have functional implications for motor performance and learning processes in mTBI patients. Reduced gray matter volume following early mTBI was also observed in Brodmann area 30, which plays a key role in episodic memory, spatial navigation, visuo-spatial processing, and internal reflection, in connection with the default mode network and complex cognitive and emotional processes. 

Higher gray matter density in mTBI participants compared to controls was also observed. Brain development is not homogeneous across regions, and recovery trajectories following mTBI-related alterations depend partly on these age-related regional differences as well as the period of heightened plasticity characteristic of early childhood. In this context, the observed increase in gray matter density in children who sustained an mTBI compared to controls could reflect a developmental compensation mechanism. Certain regions may follow an adaptive or accelerated trajectory to support the maintenance or recovery of specific functions, resulting in localized increases in gray matter. It would be interesting to conduct longitudinal follow-up to determine whether these gray matter differences persist over time and how they evolve in relation to the child’s brain development.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Although the final sample size was sufficient to ensure adequate statistical power and robust analyses, it remained relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. A total of 15 out of the initial 80 participants had to be excluded due to excessive motion during MRI acquisition—an inherent challenge when scanning very young children. This highlights the importance of thorough familiarization procedures prior to scanning to reduce movement and improve data quality. Furthermore, CAT12 implemented in SPM12 was not originally optimized for the brain morphology of preschool-aged children. However, the processing steps were carefully adapted to account for age-specific anatomical characteristics and ensure the most reliable segmentation and normalization possible for this population.

Overall, these findings suggest that early mTBI can induce alterations in gray matter morphometry persisting more than one-month post-injury, affecting widespread brain regions without distinct patterns. However, the precise localization of clusters — in particular the significant difference observed in the bilateral putamen — provides a basis for future studies aiming to correlate these structural changes with the clinical and cognitive profiles assessed in these participants.
