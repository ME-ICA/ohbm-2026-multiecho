Introduction

Introduction

Executive function (EF), also known as executive control or cognitive control, refers to a set of top-down mental processes that encompass a group of neurocognitive skills, typically including three main components: inhibitory control, working memory (WM), and cognitive flexibility. Previous twin studies have found that EF components are highly heritable (96%-100%), but with varying heritability across 3 components, indicating genetic heterogeneity among different components of EF1.

Evidence suggested that the effects of distinct genes associated with EF components were modulated by different environmental factors2. However, genome-wide gene-environment interaction studies (GWEIS) on EF remains sparse, and the mechanisms through which genes and the environment jointly influence specific components of EF require further exploration.

Against this background, more and more studies have demonstrated the mediating role of the brain between genetic factors and behavioral outcomes, highlighting the importance of neuroimaging in uncovering these pathways3. It is very likely that gene-environment interaction on EF components may also mediated by related brain regions.

Adolescence is a critical period for the development of EF and is also marked by significant structural and functional brain changes across multiple regions, which are critical for EF development4. The current study addressed 3 questions: (1) which genes interact with which environment factor (developmental adversity, DA; family environment, FE; maternal substance use, MSU; parental psychopathology, PP; school environment, SE; and socioeconomic status, SES) on EF components; (2) which brain regions mediated these gene-environment interaction on EF components; (3) How does the GWEIS mechanism change across adolescence?

Methods

Method

Participants: 11,868 ABCD participants at baseline and 4,754 at the 4th follow-up. 

Measurement: EF was assessed with three NIH Toolbox tasks. Briefly, Inhibition control was assessed via the Flanker Inhibitory Control & Attention Test (referred as Flanker below); WM is measured by the List Sorting WM Test (referred as List below); Cognitive flexible is evaluated using the Dimensional Change Card Sort Test (referred as Card below). Six latent environmental scores were derived from a Confirmatory factor analysis replicating a previously established structure5. Genotyping: ABCD study saliva/blood samples (Wave 1) provided DNA. Saliva and blood SNPs were combined pre-QC. Data were imputed via TOPMed server using Eagle v2.4 (mixed ancestry), yielding 11,099 participants and 8,833,408 SNPs. Post-imputation QC removed SNPs with: missing rate >1%, HWE P<1E-7, MAF <0.01; and participants with genotype missingness >10%. Relatedness cleaning (https://choishingwan.github.io/PRS-Tutorial/target/) resulted in 7,763 participants and 8,546,049 SNPs.

Brain imaging-derived phenotypes (IDPs):  ABCD study brain imaging data were collected using 3T Siemens Prisma, GE 750, and Philips scanners. we used processed ROI data. Structural data: 148 cortical ROIs (Destrieux atlas) and 42 subcortical ROIs (FreeSurfer ASEG). Cortical ROIs had thickness, volume, sulcal depth, surface area; subcortical ROIs had volume. Total: 646 indices. rs-fMRI data: 148 Destrieux ROI temporal variability, 91 FC pairs (13 Gordon networks), and FC between 13 networks & 19 ASEG subcortical regions. Total: 486 indices. DTI data: 42 fiber tracts (DTI AtlasTrack). Each tract had FA, MD, LD, TD, and volume. Total: 210 indices.

Statistical analysis

GWEIS: SNP × environment interaction analyses on EF were conducted using PLINK 2.0, with gene-based tests in MAGMA. 

Gene annotation: Colocalization analysis was conducted using SMR with genome-wide significant GWEIS SNPs (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸) within ±1 Mb windows. Brain eQTL (GTEx v8, PsychENCODE) and meQTL data were used. Significant colocalization was defined as qBH < 0.05 with a non-significant HEIDI test (PHEIDI > 0.05) and ≥10 SNPs; suggestive signals were reported at qBH < 0.10. 

Temporal consistency: To evaluate the consistency and correlation of effect sizes of summary statistics between the baseline and 4th year, we extracted SNPs that showed an association at baseline (P < 1 × 10⁻⁴). Cohen’s κ coefficient was used to assess the concordance in the direction of effect sizes, and Spearman’s ρ was calculated to quantify the correlation between effect magnitudes.

Moderated mediation: We used a moderated mediation model (Figure 1) to investigate relationships among genetic, environment, brain, and EF. Brain IDPs mediated gene effects on EF, while environment moderated gene→brain and gene→EF paths. Analyses used Model 8 in PyProcessMacro 1.0.12, with each EF task as dependent variable, gene scores as independent variables, modality-specific brain IDPs as mediators, and environmental latent scores as moderators. Covariates included sex, age, site, first 10 PCs, plus intracranial volume (structural IDPs) or head motion (rs-fMRI/DTI IDPs). Analyses had two steps: (1) fit model to each IDP, retain significant moderated mediation; (2) PCA on retained IDPs, use PC1 to estimate overall effect. Significant moderated mediation required: (1) model P < 0.05, and (2) environment significantly modulated gene→brain.

Results

Results

GWEIS

At baseline, 39 LD-independent SNPs showed suggestive G×E effects on EF (P<5×10⁻⁸), with 3 surpassing Bonferroni correction (P < 5×10⁻⁸/18 = 2.78×10⁻9), including rs73306185 located in the PDE1C (P = 1.65×10⁻10, interact with DA on Flanker), rs112272734 in LINC01037 (P = 1.55×10⁻9, interact with DA on Card), and rs138019750 in an intergenic region (P = 2.29×10⁻9, interact with DA on List). Gene level analysis by MAGMA revealed 7 genes survived Bonferroni-corrected threshold (P<2.58×10⁻6/18 = 1.43×10⁻7).

At the 4th follow-up, 3 LD-independent SNPs showed significant interactions with MSU or PP (P<5×10⁻⁸), though none remained significant after Bonferroni correction; 13 genes were significant at gene level, including 2 Bonferroni-significant genes.

Gene annotation

Colocalization between GWEISs and expression/methylation signals has been successfully used to provide biological evidence for the causal association between traits. The CpG sites cg01965462, cg26294431, and cg01153613 showed suggestive evidence of shared causality with the WM (qBH < 0.10, PHEIDI > 0.05, N(SNP): 17–20). All three had negative SMR effects (βSMR < 0), indicating that higher methylation is associated with lower List score. The CpG sites cg23435118, cg08523384, and cg13490971 showed positive SMR effects (bSMR > 0) with the WM at the suggestive level. cg06867623 and cg23297230 showed suggestive evidence of shared causality with the inhibition control in SMR/HEIDI (qBH < 0.10, PHEIDI > 0.05, N(SNP):15–19), with βSMR > 0. Additionally, increased TEX40 expression was associated with lower scores on the WM (βSMR =-1.25, qBH < 0.10, PHEIDI > 0.05, N(SNP)=20).

Temporal consistency

The environmental factors FE, MSU, PP, and SES showed moderate to strong positive GWEIS correlations between baseline and 4th year, whereas SE showed a negative correlation. In contrast, G×DA effects displayed task-specific temporal patterns: for Flanker, effect directions were consistent but effect sizes were not (κ=0.15, P=1.7×10⁻²⁷; ρ=-0.01, P=0.79), while for List, both directions and magnitudes were moderately consistent over time (κ=0.26, P=2.6×10⁻²¹⁰; ρ=0.44, P<0.001).

Significant moderated mediation effect

rs-fMRI IDPs

In Flanker, significant mediating effects involved several inter-network connections and temporal-variance in cingulate and insular regions. In Card and List, additional inter-network and network–ROI connections across large-scale networks emerged as mediators (Figure 2a). PCA on significant rs-fMRI IDPs showed that the indirect effect generally increased with higher environmental scores, except for Card under MSU and List under SES, which showed decreasing trends.

Structural IDPs

For Flanker and Card, significant mediators included hippocampal, corpus callosum, ventral diencephalon, and cerebral white-matter volumes, as well as cortical thickness and sulcal depth in occipital, angular, frontal, and insular regions. In the List task, we identified 31 significant mediating brain IDPs (Figure 2b). PCA summaries indicated that indirect effects increased with higher environmental scores, except for List under MSU, which decreased.

Diffusion IDPs

Across tasks, significant mediating effects were observed in deep and fronto-limbic white-matter tracts, as well as broader white-matter networks in List (Figure 2c). PCA on significant diffusion IDPs showed that indirect effects increased with higher environmental scores.

Conclusion

Discussion

In conclusion, we systematically investigated the interactive effects of six environmental factors and genetic factors on three core EF components in an adolescent population. Our findings revealed distinct gene-environment interactions for each EF component, with a unique SNP exhibiting a genome-wide significant interaction with DA in each. Building on this, we further elucidated potential biological mechanisms at the epigenetic, gene expression, and pathway levels. Longitudinal analyses uncovered the dynamic trajectories of these G×E effects from early to late adolescence, identifying divergent developmental trends across different component-environment combinations. Finally, employing moderated mediation models, we discerned dissociable neural mediating pathways underlying the different EF components. In conclusion, this research not only deepens our understanding of the genetic-neural mechanisms of EF but also highlights two critical directions for future research: 1) to disentangle the complex interplay between genetic and environmental factors in EF development, and 2) to elucidate how different neural modalities collaborate to support the development of EFs.
