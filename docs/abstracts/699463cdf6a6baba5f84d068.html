Introduction

The brain is inherently dynamic, characterized by continuously evolving neural activity that interacts across diverse networks to support complex cognition. While traditional neuroimaging has defined a static functional connectome, recent advances highlight that resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) signals are dominated by recurring spatiotemporal patterns, often manifesting as propagating waves. These dynamic motifs may serve as computational primitives enabling rapid cognitive state transitions. Despite these insights in humans, the evolutionary conservation of these dynamics and their biological underpinnings remain unclear. The macaque monkey offers a pivotal model to bridge this gap, linking macroscopic waves to cellular-level mechanisms. This study investigates the evolutionary organization and neurochemical substrates of brain dynamics in macaques. We aim to 1) Validate the existence of evolutionarily analogous spatiotemporal patterns in macaque rs-fMRI and 2) Define the “neurochemical scaffolding” constraints by projecting these functional dynamics onto the underlying architecture of neurotransmitter receptors.

Methods

To robustly characterize spatiotemporal dynamics, we analyzed rs-fMRI datasets from multiple independent macaque cohorts via the PRIMatE Data Exchange1, encompassing a wide developmental range and varying physiological states. The primary dataset was the Oxford cohort (n = 19, isoflurane anesthesia, age 4.01 ± 0.98 years). To establish reproducibility across consciousness states and scanner protocols, we included the Newcastle cohort (n = 10, awake state, age 8.28 ± 2.33 years), and two University of Wisconsin-Madison (UWM) cohorts acquired under ketamine anesthesia (UWM-1: n = 103, age 2.59 ± 1.04 years; UWM-2: n = 346, age 1.56 ± 0.35 years). 

We utilized Complex Principal Component Analysis (CPCA) to decompose BOLD signals into phase-dependent spatiotemporal patterns.  We first applied the Hilbert transform to the BOLD time series of each Region of Interest (Markov Parcellation, 182 regions) to generate an analytic signal, preserving both instantaneous amplitude and phase information. The data were concatenated across subjects and subjected to Singular Value Decomposition (SVD). This process decomposes the complex signal into orthogonal components, capturing traveling wave patterns that propagate across the cortex with specific phase delays. We reconstructed these patterns by phase-resampling the data from 0 to 2π, visualizing the trajectory of neural activation over a full cycle2. To ensure comparability across datasets, pattern order and phase were matched to the Oxford cohort using Orthogonal Procrustes alignment. For cross-species comparison, we aligned macaque data to a human functional space using a cross-species parcellation (Schaefer 200 atlas) derived from functional alignment3. We compared macaque dynamic patterns to established human spatiotemporal patterns4 using Pearson’s correlation.

To investigate the neurochemical basis of the macaque dynamics, we utilized a normative macaque receptor atlas derived from 3D-reconstructed quantitative in vitro receptor autoradiography5. We examined six distinct neurotransmitter receptors, including glutamate (AMPA, Kainate, NMDA) and GABA (GABAA, GABAA/BZ, GABAB). We performed Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on these maps to compute receptor principal gradients (Receptor PCs), defining a low-dimensional molecular landscape. Results

Our analysis revealed that macaque brain dynamics are dominated by three core, recurring spatiotemporal patterns (Fig. 1a). Surface phase maps illustrate the propagation of activation levels (z-scores) across the phase cycle (0 to 2π): Pattern 1 exhibits a trajectory oscillating between Visual and Somatomotor networks; Pattern 2 represents an axis between Insular-opercular/Auditory networks and the Dorsal Attention network; and Pattern 3 shows a complex trajectory originating in Limbic, Visual, and Default networks, propagating toward Somatomotor and Dorsal Attention networks (Fig. 1b). Collectively, these three patterns explain over 50% of the total variance across all independent cohorts (Fig. 1c). Cross-cohort reproducibility analysis confirmed that these dynamic architectures are statistically robust (r > 0.8) regardless of anesthesia (isoflurane, ketamine) or wakefulness (Fig. 1d). Furthermore, intra-subject reliability, measured via discriminability analysis, was high for the first three patterns in both the Oxford and Newcastle cohorts (Fig. 1e).

To assess evolutionary conservation, we benchmarked macaque patterns against three dominant human spatiotemporal patterns (Sensorimotor-Association [S-A], Task-Positive-to-Default [TP-D], and Somatomotor-to-Visual [SM-V]) (Fig. 2a). Using cross-species functional alignment (Fig. 2b), we computed the spatial similarity between species. The correlation heatmap revealed striking homology: Macaque Pattern 1 corresponds strongly (r = 0.64) to the human SM-V pattern, while Macaque Pattern 3 corresponds (r = 0.50) to the human S-A pattern (Fig. 2c).

Crucially, we identified specific neurochemical signatures associated with these dynamic motifs using 3D-reconstructed autoradiography maps for six glutamate and GABA receptors (Fig. 2d). PCA of these maps revealed that the first two principal components (Receptor PCs) explained over 80% of the variance in receptor distribution (Fig. 2e). The spatial distribution of Receptor PC scores (Fig. 2f) and component loadings (Fig. 2g) enabled the biological interpretation of these axes: Receptor PC1 characterizes a "neural timescale" gradient (shorter AMPA/Kainate to longer NMDA/GABAB receptors), while Receptor PC2 reflects an "Excitatory-to-Inhibitory" (E/I) axis (separating excitatory Glutamate receptors from inhibitory GABA receptors).

When projected onto this neurochemical manifold, the functional patterns formed distinct, smooth cyclic trajectories. Pattern 1 dynamics were primarily constrained by the E/I balance axis (Receptor PC2) having high spatial similarity (r = 0.44 at phase 0), oscillating between excitatory- and inhibitory-dominant states (Fig. 2h). In contrast, Pattern 3 dynamics traversed a broader landscape, spanning both the timescale (Receptor PC1, r = 0.33 at phase 0) and E/I (Receptor PC2, r = 0.34 at phase π) gradients (Fig. 2i). These findings suggest that macroscopic functional waves are scaffolded by specific underlying gradients of receptor timescales and excitation-inhibition balance.

Conclusion

This study establishes a comprehensive framework for understanding the evolutionary and neurochemical basis of spatiotemporal brain dynamics in the macaque monkey. We demonstrate that macaque resting-state activity is not random but organized into a compact set of robust, recurring spatiotemporal patterns that persist across diverse physiological states and developmental stages. The striking homology between these macaque patterns and established human functional motifs (e.g., SM-V and S-A trajectories) provides strong evidence for a shared primate functional architecture, suggesting that the fundamental principles governing large-scale information flow have been conserved throughout primate evolution.

Crucially, our findings bridge the gap between macroscopic network dynamics and microscopic molecular substrates, supporting a "neurochemical scaffolding" hypothesis. By projecting functional trajectories onto a receptor-defined state space, we reveal that intrinsic waves are closely associated with the underlying topography of neurotransmitter receptors, suggesting molecular architecture may constrain large-scale dynamics. Specifically, we show that functional propagation tracks gradients of neural timescales (Receptor PC1) and Excitatory-Inhibitory axis (Receptor PC2), suggesting that the brain’s molecular landscape acts as a physical guide for the dynamic routing of neural signals.
