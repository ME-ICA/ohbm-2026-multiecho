Introduction

Multivariate neural representations can track dynamically changing percepts1. Prior work using high-level, ambiguous stimuli (e.g., naturalistic narratives) shows that neural activity differs based on self-generated interpretations2–3. However, little is known about whether and where within-subject multivariate patterns shift when individuals encounter alternative interpretations of the same information, and what consequences such shifts may have4.

Using a novel, ecologically valid task, we tested whether such within-subject multivariate pattern shifts track reinterpretation. Critically, because reinterpretation may alter not only momentary appraisal but also how competing information is stored, we further tested whether these neural shifts shape subsequent memory.

Methods

During fMRI scanning (3T, voxel size=2.7mm3, TR=1.057), subjects (N=56) performed a task where, on each trial, they were presented with ambiguous photographs and interpreted them (SELF). Next, on experimental trials only, they read another possible interpretation sourced from a prior subject (OTHER). They then rated the likelihood of either just SELF (for control trials) or both (for experimental trials; Fig. 1). Alternative interpretations were selected in real-time, ensuring balanced distributions of semantic distances between SELF and OTHER. Reinterpretation was computed as Self1 - Self2 + Other (Fig. 1). One week later, participants completed a surprise memory test for both SELF and OTHER interpretations.

Voxels were parcelled into 100 cortical (Schaefer parcellation5) and then grouped into 17 Yeo Networks. Event-related general linear models (GLMs) were applied to the onset and offset of a given trial phase (e.g., initial or second viewing; Fig. 1), yielding a beta weight for each trial phase per voxel. We computed neural shifts as the cosine distance (1-r) between multivoxel patterns in a given node between the two viewings of the same stimulus. Linear mixed-effects models were used to assess the effects of condition, reinterpretation behavior, and memory outcomes at the parcel and network levels, with FDR correction across networks.

Results

Relative to control trials, exposure to an alternative interpretation in experimental trials elicited larger neural shifts between viewings of the same sensory input (Fig. 2a). Within experimental trials, neural shifts in lateral frontoparietal control, salience, and default-mode network regions (e.g., temporoparietal junction) tracked reinterpretation, particularly when OTHER was more semantically distinct (Fig. 2b).

Behaviorally, both SELF and OTHER interpretations were recalled above chance, though SELF memory was more accurate. Memory representations converged asymmetrically: OTHER interpretations shifted toward SELF more than the reverse (p<0.001). Importantly, greater neural shifts and greater reinterpretation predicted a reduction in this asymmetry in dorsal attention and medial default-mode regions (e.g., posterior medial cortex) that were relatively distinct from those supporting reinterpretation (Fig. 2c).

Conclusion

These findings show that reinterpretation of ambiguous social information is accompanied by reliable within-subject multivariate neural shifts, which also shape how alternative interpretations are consolidated in memory. Together, the results link dynamic neural representational change to both subjective belief updating and longer-term memory formation.
