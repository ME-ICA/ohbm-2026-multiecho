Introduction

Age-related changes in subcortical brain structure are consistently observed across adulthood, with robust volume reductions reported for the caudate, putamen, and nucleus accumbens alongside expansion of ventricular spaces in large-scale lifespan studies1,2. However, volumetric findings are commonly reported using either absolute or normalized measures, and the implications of this choice for interpreting age-related tissue–fluid relationships remain insufficiently examined.

Normalization by total brain volume is frequently applied to account for interindividual differences in brain size, yet it may also alter the apparent strength and structure of age–volume associations, particularly for regions that scale differently with global brain atrophy. This issue is especially relevant for subcortical structures, which show heterogeneous age sensitivity and may differ in their coupling to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and ventricular expansion.

Here, we examined age-related associations between subcortical volumes, CSF, and ventricular space using both absolute and normalized measures in a quality-controlled adult sample. By directly contrasting these representations, we aimed to clarify how normalization influences age-related volumetric patterns and tissue–fluid coupling across individual striatal nuclei.

Methods

Data were obtained from the Nathan Kline Institute (NKI) Rockland Sample3, accessed via the ReproBrainChart (RBC)4

using DataLad. We used the RBC-curated dataset, which provides uniformly preprocessed neuroimaging data and derived measures. Analyses were based on imaging-derived measures and basic demographic variables (age and sex). Participants were selected using the RBC complete-pass quality-control criterion, indicating that structural MRI data met all recommended quality-control procedures implemented by the RBC pipeline.

From this quality-controlled pool, we included participants aged 18 years and older for whom both structural MRI and BOLD fMRI acquisitions were available in the repository. Although the present analyses focus exclusively on structural MRI–derived measures, this availability criterion defined the study cohort, resulting in a final sample of 141 participants.

To illustrate the anatomical localization of the analyzed regions, an example of the putamen segmentation is shown for one older and one younger participant (top and bottom rows, respectively) in sagittal, coronal, and axial views. All regions of interest were defined using automated FreeSurfer segmentation provided by the RBC pipeline.

Statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.2). Pairwise associations between age, subcortical volumes, CSF, and ventricular volume were assessed using Pearson correlation coefficients. Correlation patterns were visualized using pairwise correlation plots, which displayed marginal distributions, bivariate scatterplots, and correlation coefficients for the full cohort and stratified by sex. Analyses were conducted separately for absolute volumes and volumes normalized by total brain volume.

Results

Pairwise associations among age, subcortical volumes, CSF, and ventricular volume are shown in Figure 2, with absolute volumes presented in the upper panel and volumes normalized by total brain volume shown in the lower panel. Pearson correlation coefficients are reported for the full cohort and stratified by sex, with statistical significance indicated as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.

Across absolute volumetric measures, age was strongly negatively correlated with the volumes of the putamen, caudate, accumbens, and striatum in the full cohort and within each sex. In contrast, age was positively correlated with CSF and ventricular volume across all groups, although the age–CSF association was weaker in females, reaching significance only at p < 0.05.

Subcortical structures exhibited strong positive intercorrelations, particularly between the putamen and caudate, putamen and accumbens, and between each structure and the composite striatum, with the largest effect sizes generally observed in males.

Relationships between tissue volumes and fluid compartments differed by structure. In males, CSF volume was negatively associated with putamen, accumbens, and striatal volumes, whereas these associations were not consistently observed in females or in the full cohort. Ventricular volume showed robust negative associations with the putamen and accumbens across sexes, while the caudate exhibited minimal coupling to ventricular or CSF volume. CSF and ventricular volumes were strongly positively correlated across all groups.

Normalization by total brain volume preserved the overall pattern of age-related subcortical decline and fluid expansion but altered the strength of several associations. Age-related correlations with relative subcortical volumes were attenuated compared with absolute measures, whereas associations involving CSF_rel and ventricles_rel were strengthened, particularly in males. Notably, normalization revealed significant negative associations between accumbens_rel and CSF_rel that were not evident in the absolute measures. In contrast, caudate_rel remained weakly associated with both CSF_rel and ventricles_rel across representations.

Together, these results indicate consistent age-related reductions in subcortical tissue alongside expansion of CSF and ventricular spaces, while demonstrating that normalization emphasizes proportional tissue–fluid relationships and reduces effects driven by global brain size.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate robust age-related reductions in striatal substructures accompanied by expansion of CSF and ventricular spaces across adulthood. These patterns closely align with large-scale lifespan imaging evidence from the ENIGMA Lifespan study, which reported linear declines in caudate, putamen, and nucleus accumbens volumes alongside progressive ventricular enlargement across the adult1.

Beyond replicating these global age-related trends, our analyses highlight how volumetric normalization alters the apparent strength of age–structure relationships. Absolute measures captured strong age-related declines in subcortical tissue volumes, whereas normalization by total brain volume preserved the direction of these effects while attenuating their magnitude, suggesting that a substantial proportion of variance in absolute volumes reflects global brain size and generalized atrophy. In contrast, normalized measures emphasized proportional relationships between tissue and fluid compartments, revealing stronger associations between CSF, ventricular volume, and subcortical structures, particularly the nucleus accumbens.

These observations are consistent with conceptual models of healthy brain aging, which describe age-related reductions in brain tissue volume accompanied by compensatory expansion of ventricular and CSF spaces, resulting in coordinated tissue–fluid reorganization rather than isolated regional atrophy2. At a mechanistic level, such volumetric changes are thought to reflect widespread reductions in overall brain tissue volume and associated alterations in neuronal morphology, including changes in dendritic and synaptic structure, rather than extensive neuronal loss5. Within this framework, normalization may preferentially highlight proportional changes linked to global structural reorganization, whereas absolute measures retain sensitivity to combined global and region-specific effects.

Finally, the differential coupling observed across striatal nuclei suggests heterogeneity in age sensitivity within the striatum. The putamen and nucleus accumbens exhibited stronger associations with CSF and ventricular volumes than the caudate, indicating that these regions may be more tightly linked to global tissue–fluid dynamics during aging. Although the cross-sectional design limits inference about within-individual trajectories, the present findings underscore the importance of jointly considering absolute and normalized volumetric measures when characterizing age-related structural brain variation.
