Introduction

Spinal cord injury (SCI) causes sensorimotor deficits, autonomic dysfunction, and secondary complications that lead to reduced independence and affect physical, psychological, and social well-being (1). Rehabilitation is complex and multidisciplinary, addressing motor recovery, secondary health issues, and neuropsychological consequences. Outcomes vary widely (2), making early prediction of recovery a research priority.

Current prediction models rely on demographic and clinical variables collected at admission, such as age, time to treatment, and baseline neurological impairment (3). Recently, interest has grown in neuroimaging biomarkers from spinal cord and brain MRI (4). Spinal cord markers (e.g. tissue bridges) were shown to correlate with recovery and have been integrated into prediction frameworks (5). SCI also induces widespread brain changes, including gray and white matter atrophy beyond sensorimotor pathways, affecting emotional, pain, and cognitive networks (6). Early micro- and macrostructural alterations (e.g., volume loss) predict long-term recovery (7), suggesting brain morphometry as a promising biomarker. However, most studies have small sample sizes, focus on somatosensory regions, and never integrated brain data into validated predictive models. It remains unclear whether brain measures add predictive value beyond clinical data

This study combines clinical and MRI data of N=79 participants with SCI to test whether subacute brain morphometry improves prediction of functional recovery at discharge from the first inpatient rehabilitation. We compared and cross-validated two penalized regression models: Model 1 (clinical variables) and Model 2 (clinical + brain measures), hypothesizing that brain features may enhance prediction and identify regions linked to recovery. Secondary analyses explored associations between clinical characteristics and brain morphometry.

Methods

Participants

We retrospectively analyzed data from 79 individuals with SCI enrolled in the SwiSCI inception cohort study who underwent brain MRI during their first inpatient rehabilitation at the Swiss Paraplegic Centre (SPZ), Nottwill, CH. Inclusion criteria: age ≥18 years; availability of Spinal Cord Independence Measure (SCIM) III assessments at subacute stage and discharge; brain MRI acquired >2 weeks post-injury and within 20 days of clinical assessment; no evidence of brain injury or other neurological disorders. MRI quality was verified by a trained radiologist. Ethical approval was obtained (EKNZ ID 2025-00222), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Clinical Data

Clinical variables were extracted from the SwiSCI inception cohort, which collects standardized data across Swiss SCI centers. The primary outcome was proportional functional recovery at discharge (ΔSCIM), calculated as: ΔSCIM=(SCIM_discharge − SCIM_subacute)/(100 - SCIM_subacute)

Predictors included age, sex, AIS grade (ordinal: A=1 to D=4), neurological level of injury (NLI), baseline SCIM score, time since injury (TSI), and Cumulative Illness Rating Scale (CIRS) to quantify the burden of secondary health conditions.


Neuroimaging Processing

Brain MRI scans (2014–2024) were heterogeneous; sequences included T2-weighted FLAIR and T1-weighted images. All scans were harmonized using SynthSR to generate 1 mm isotropic T1-weighted images. Morphometric analysis was performed with CAT12 (SPM12) under MATLAB. Regional gray matter (GM) volumes were extracted using the Schaefer 100-region cortical parcellation, organized into major functional networks (sensorimotor, attentional, executive, limbic, and default mode).

Statistical Analyses

Analyses were conducted in RStudio.

The primary objective was to predict ΔSCIM using two elastic-net regression models:

  • Model 1: clinical predictors only
  • Model 2: clinical + 100 cortical GM volumes

As a general approach, elastic net (α=0.5) was chosen to handle multicollinearity among brain measures. Lambda was tuned within a repeated 10-fold cross-validation (100 repetitions). Performance was evaluated using mean R² and root mean squared error (RMSE) calculated on unseen folds. Stable predictors were defined as those selected in ≥80% of repetitions.

Secondary Analyses

Exploratory general linear models assessed associations between stable brain predictors and clinical variables (AIS, NLI, baseline SCIM, TSI, CIRS), controlling for age, sex, and TIV.

Results

Participants showed a significant improvement in functional independence during rehabilitation: SCIM III scores increased from 34.7 ± 20.5 at the subacute stage to 65.97 ± 22.65 at discharge (t₇₈ = 15.39, p < 0.001).

Prediction of functional recovery

Two elastic-net regression models were evaluated using repeated 10-fold cross-validation:

Model 1 (clinical predictors only) achieved R² = 0.39 ± 0.02 and RMSE = 0.22 ± 0.004. AIS grade was the strongest predictor (mean coefficient = 0.101), indicating that greater lesion completeness predicted lower recovery. Other predictors contributed modestly: younger age, lower neurological level (NLI), higher baseline SCIM score, fewer comorbidities (CIRS), and longer time since injury (TSI) were associated with better outcomes. Sex was not retained.

Model 2 (clinical + brain morphometry) showed similar overall performance (R² = 0.38 ± 0.04; RMSE = 0.22 ± 0.01) but revealed additional contributions from cortical gray matter volumes. Several brain regions consistently emerged as stable predictors, often with higher coefficients than clinical variables. Positive associations (smaller GM volume → poorer recovery; see blue areas in Figure 1) were observed in: Left parietal operculum (Ventral Attentional Network) (coeff = 0.153); Left intraparietal sulcus (Executive Control Network) (coeff = 0.139); Right medial posterior parietal cortex (Dorsal Attentional network N) (coeff = 0.086); Sensorimotor areas (e.g., bilateral primary somatosensory cortices (coeff = 0.038 - 0.061).

Negative associations (larger GM volume → poorer recovery; see orange areas in Figure 1) were found in regions such as the right precuneus and frontal areas.

Secondary analyses

Exploratory models showed that greater neurological severity (AIS grade) was associated with smaller GM volumes in the left IPS (β = 0.13, p = 0.005) and right sensorimotor cortex (β = 0.07, p = 0.02). Higher NLI was linked to reduced volume in the medial PPC (β = 0.02, p = 0.028). These associations suggest that brain morphometry reflects clinically relevant variation in impairment.

Conclusion

This study examined whether brain morphometry acquired in the subacute stage of SCI adds predictive value to clinical variables for forecasting functional recovery at discharge. Using repeated cross-validated elastic-net models, we compared a clinical-only model with a combined clinical + brain model. Three key findings emerged:

  1. Clinical predictors remain robust and reliable. AIS grade was the strongest predictor, confirming that lesion completeness critically influences recovery potential. Other clinical factors—neurological level, age, baseline SCIM score, comorbidity burden—also contributed consistently, aligning with prior literature.

  2. Brain morphometry provides complementary insights. Although adding brain measures did not significantly improve overall predictive accuracy, the combined model identified distributed cortical regions that systematically contributed to prediction. These included sensorimotor cortices and associative parietal hubs (e.g., parietal operculum, intraparietal sulcus, medial posterior parietal cortex), which exhibited positive associations (smaller volume → poorer recovery). These findings support the notion that structural integrity of sensorimotor and integrative networks influences rehabilitation outcomes. Negative associations (larger volume → poorer recovery) in regions such as the precuneus and frontal areas may reflect compensatory or maladaptive processes, warranting further investigation.

  3. Brain morphology correlates with clinical severity. Exploratory analyses revealed that greater neurological impairment was linked to smaller GM volumes in sensorimotor and parietal regions, reinforcing their relevance as potential biomarkers.


Our results suggest that brain morphometry captures aspects of recovery potential not fully reflected in clinical assessments. While predictive accuracy gains were modest, identifying cortical hubs associated with functional improvement highlights the complexity of recovery mechanisms and the role of supraspinal networks in rehabilitation. These insights could inform future multimodal prognostic models and targeted interventions.

Limitations
The retrospective design and lack of fine-grained ISNCSCI motor/sensory scores may have constrained predictive performance. Imaging heterogeneity required harmonization, and analyses were limited to structural MRI; microstructural and functional measures likely offer additional predictive value. 

Conclusion
The present study demonstrates that while clinical variables remain reliable predictors of functional recovery after SCI, brain morphometry measured in the subacute stage carries complementary information involving a distributed set of cortical regions spanning sensorimotor and associative networks. These findings underscore the complexity of recovery mechanisms and the potential role of brain morphometry as a complementary biomarker of functional recovery. Future research in larger samples, integrating multimodal spinal and brain imaging, will be essential to determine how neuroimaging can most effectively support individualized prediction and rehabilitation planning in SCI.
