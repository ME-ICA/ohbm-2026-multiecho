Introduction

Neuroimaging harnesses complementary modalities to probe distinct facets of cerebral topology and function. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) elucidates the microstructural integrity of white-matter tracts, whereas resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) delineates patterns of temporally coordinated neural activity. Yet, when examined in isolation, particularly in the context of neurodegenerative disorders, these modalities render only a fragmented portrait of brain organization. In Parkinson’s disease (PD), neurodegeneration extends beyond dopaminergic circuits, compromising both anatomical scaffolding and large-scale functional networks1. While prior studies have begun to investigate complementary information of different neuroimaging modalities2, research on the conjoint brain connectomic implications remains limited. Consequently, the integration of structural and functional connectivity into a unified framework, namely "multimodal fusion", offers a vantage from which to apprehend how structural alterations sculpt functional network organization, and conversely, how emergent functional patterns may reflect underlying anatomical substrates; a nuanced interplay that unimodal analyses may be ill-equipped to capture. In this study, we introduce a multimodal fusion pipeline applied to two independent PD cohorts. In the baseline cohort (N = 59), pairwise combinations of structural and functional networks were integrated to generate fused connectomes, which were then used to develop and evaluate the methodology through statistical comparisons between patients and healthy controls (HC). To further validate the robustness of our approach, an independent validation cohort (N = 168) was used to construct fused networks and examine their consistency in statistical significance.

Methods

MRI data were acquired on a 3 T Siemens Prisma scanner using a 64-channel head–neck coil. Rs-fMRI data were collected with a 2D multiband GRE-EPI sequence (2 mm isotropic; TR = 820 ms; TE = 35.2 ms; flip angle = 45°; multi-band acceleration factor = 4; 500 volumes), including two runs with opposite phase-encoding directions (AP/PA) and matched spin-echo AP/PA images for distortion correction. DWI data were acquired at 1.5 mm isotropic resolution (TR = 3.222 ms; TE = 89.2 ms; multi-band acceleration factor = 4), using b-values of 1500 and 3000 s/mm² in 93 directions with additional 7 b0 images, acquired with both AP and PA polarity.

DWI data were preprocessed following the Human Connectome Project (HCP) pipeline3, including denoising, correction for Gibbs ringing, susceptibility distortions, eddy currents, motion, and gradient nonlinearity. Fibre orientation distributions were estimated using multi-shell multi-tissue constrained spherical deconvolution with multi-tissue log-domain intensity normalisation, using the MRTrix3. Ten million streamlines were then generated using anatomically-constrained probabilistic tractography and filtered with SIFT2 to match fibre densities. Functional data were preprocessed using the HCP minimal pre-processing pipeline3 and the HCP rsfMRI pipeline4, including slice-time correction, spatial realignment, coregisteration, segmentation, and normalization to MNI152 space. Volumetric timeseries were projected onto the cortical surface, resampled to a 2 mm mesh, smoothed (2 mm FWHM), filtered (2000 s cutoff), and denoised with independent component analysis to address artefactual components and motion effects.

Rs-fMRI and DWI data were parcellated using the HCP atlas extended by connectivity-based atlases and subcortical segmentations, resulting in a total of 438 regions. Functional connectivity networks were computed using the Pearson correlation of the mean time series between all pairs of nodes. Structural networks were defined as the summation of streamline weights derived from the SIFT2 algorithm, with each edge adjusted by the inverse of the corresponding connected volumes. Negative values were set to zero. 

To explore the integration of two different imaging modalities, we employed the eigenmode approach of 5 to predict one network type from the other, examining all pairwise combinations. Let A ∈ RNxN be the brain connectivity matrix of the structural network and W∈ RNxN be the connectivity matrix of the functional network, where N defines the number of nodes. The hypothesis in the eigenmode approach is that the functional network W can be approximated as5:

                                                                             W ≈ VSVT

where V is the eigenvector matrix of A and S is a diagonal matrix of the weighting coefficients estimated from the eigenvectors of the structural network. Hence, the elements of S should minimize the error ε=||W-VSVT||F, where ||•||F denotes the Frobenius norm. It can be proved that the fitting coefficients that meet this condition can be computed as5:

                                                                 S=diag(v1TWv1,...,vNTWvN)

where vi are the eigenvectors of A. The resulting fused networks were obtained as the averaged output from both predictions. Finally, a second approach of fusion was employed by applying the same methodology to the graph Laplacian of the networks. The network-based statistic (NBS) toolbox was used in MATLAB (v. 9.16) to explore connectivity alterations in subnetworks, while controlling the family-wise error. Both contrasts were investigated using the default settings and primary thresholds of 3.2 and 3.1 for the 59-cohort and 168-cohort, respectively, corresponding to p-value = 0.001. Results

NBS was applied to identify group differences between HC and PD patients across multiple network construction approaches and cohorts. In the baseline cohort, the HC > PD contrast revealed significant network-level differences for all network types; structural, AP and PA functional, fused, and fused Laplacian networks across all combinations (structural vs. functional AP/PA, functional AP vs. functional PA). No significant components were detected for the PD > HC contrast in any of the network configurations. The AP and PA networks presented similar edges mainly within visual-associated and insula regions, while temporal areas appeared more in AP, and opercular and cingulate areas were found more in PA. Interestingly, the fused networks preserved these regions from both AP and PA, while increasing sensitivity to additional edges. In the validation cohort, significant group differences were similarly found for the HC > PD contrast, using the fused Laplacian and the functional AP networks. In contrast, the fused (non-Laplacian) did not show significant components. Figure 2 displays the top 50 most significant edges from the fused Laplacian comparison (HC > PD), which were found significant in both cohorts. The baseline cohort exhibited significant edges predominantly in the temporo-parieto-occipital junction, medial temporal cortex, and posterior cingulate. Additional hypoconnectivity was observed in cerebellar areas, auditory, and visual regions. In the validation cohort, significant edges were mainly found in temporal and insular cortices, with repeated involvement of the anterior and middle area TE1, posterior insula, and limbic regions, including the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex. These cortical alterations were accompanied by subcortical disconnections involving the thalamus (prefrontal, posterior parietal, and occipital), basal ganglia (caudate, putamen, globus pallidus), and the periaqueductal grey matter. Similar effects were observed within the cerebellum, such as cerebellar lobules IV, IX, X, and the fastigial nucleus. The structural networks demonstrated significant differences for both contrasts. Specifically, a hypoconnected network was identified for HC > PD (N≈5000 edges), while a few hyperconnected edges (N=78) were observed for PD > HC. These components primarily involved hypoconnections (HC>PD) in orbitofrontal and anterior prefrontal areas, as well as the basal ganglia, while the opposite contrast illustrated frontal motor and premotor areas.

Conclusion

In summary, our results demonstrate consistent network-level differences between HC and PD patients across multiple imaging modalities and cohorts. Structural networks revealed hypoconnectivity in PD, primarily affecting prefrontal, orbitofrontal, and basal ganglia regions, with a smaller set of hyperconnected edges involving motor and premotor areas. Functional networks derived from AP and PA fMRI acquisitions exhibited largely overlapping patterns of hypoconnectivity in PD, particularly involving visual and insula-related areas; however, AP networks showed stronger involvement of temporal areas, whereas PA networks highlighted connections to opercular and cingulate regions. This subtle difference between phase-encoding directions may influence sensitivity for detecting certain edges, and, thus, AP/PA effects need to be carefully considered in future studies. Notably, fused networks preserved the core regions identified in unimodal analyses while enhancing sensitivity to further connections. Overall, our findings support that integrating structural and functional connectivity captures complementary aspects of PD-related network disruption that are not consistently detectable using single-modality approaches. Hence, these results suggest that the presented fusion pipeline provides an informative representation of large-scale brain network alterations; however, validation on large-scale datasets is required to confirm its generalizability.
