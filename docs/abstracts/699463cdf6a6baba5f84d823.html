Introduction

Stroke often results in focal brain lesions that disrupt not only the local tissue but also the structural connectivity of distributed networks, leading to motor and cognitive deficits. Lesion-symptom mapping approaches focus on the location of damage but may overlook the broader network-level consequences of stroke. Advances in diffusion-weighted imaging and network neuroscience permit mapping of structural connectivity in healthy populations, which can be leveraged to model patient-specific disconnections. By overlaying lesion masks onto normative connectomes, probabilistic structural disconnection maps can be derived to characterize the impact of lesions on network integrity1. This study used structural connectivity matrices and graph-theoretical analyses to identify subnetworks associated with post-stroke motor, executive, and processing speed deficits. Lesion-induced disconnections were quantified by modeling individual structural connectivity graphs to reflect disrupted brain networks. A network-based framework then linked these disconnection patterns to behavioral impairments, enabling a comprehensive characterization of the structural substrates underlying post-stroke functional deficits.

Methods

Structural imaging and clinical data from 340 stroke patients (aged 63.9±10.5 years) included in the multicenter GRECogVASC study cohort, who were experiencing motor, executive function, and/or processing speed deficits, were acquired six months post-stroke at Amiens University Hospital2. For each patient, a lesions mask was manually segmented on 3D T1w images, and normalized into the MNI152 template. To model the effect of lesions on structural connectivity patterns, fiber tracking was performed using diffusion-weighted imaging data from over 400 healthy controls of the same age subgroup from the Cambridge Centre for Ageing and Neuroscience repository (CamCAN, Stage 2)3. A connectivity matrix was initially constructed for each healthy subject based on the number of streamlines connecting parcels using a high-resolution parcellation atlas with 1133 regions3. Each patient’s disconnection profile was modeled as a weighted network, in which edge weights represented the mean number of streamlines connecting each pair of parcels after removing fibers intersecting the lesion from the connectivity matrices of healthy controls matched within five years of the patient’s age and then averaging the resulting matrices. 

For each of the three deficits, 20 bootstrapped bags of lesion-derived connectivity matrices were generated from all stroke patients to balance the proportion of patients with and without deficits across the bags. This number of bags was chosen based on a conservative statistical criterion, ensuring that edges retained through the majority-voting procedure (appearing in at least 50% of bags) have a probability of being selected by chance below α = 0.05, thereby providing a principled threshold for robust and reproducible edge inclusion. Connection strengths derived from lesion-derived structural connectivity matrices were then examined using an edge-wise network-based statistic to quantify the impact of disconnection at the edge level. Significant edges (p<0.05) were grouped into connected components, and a component-level statistic was computed to identify spatially and topologically coherent subnetworks differing between patients with and without deficits. To control for multiple comparisons and improve sensitivity to spatially continuous network effects, a Threshold-Free Cluster Enhancement (TFCE)–inspired procedure4 was applied. TFCE enhanced clusters that were both strong in magnitude and spatially extended by integrating statistical evidence across a continuum of thresholds (t values), thereby eliminating the need for an arbitrary cluster-forming threshold. Statistical significance (p<0.05) was established by comparing each component’s enhanced statistic to a null distribution generated through permutation testing (with 1000 permutations) of patient labels. The final results were aggregated across the bags using a majority-voting aggregation procedure to identify subnetworks whose disconnections were consistently associated with motor, executive, and processing-speed deficits while controlling for false positives. For the stable edges, mean t-values were then calculated across bags.

Results

Lesion-derived structural connectivity analysis revealed that motor deficits were primarily associated with the precentral gyrus contralateral to the affected side (t > 10) and along the corticospinal tract including the pallidum and pons (t < 6), consistent with their established roles in voluntary motor execution (Fig. 1). For left motor deficits, additional associations were observed in the right caudate and thalamus (t < 6), likely reflecting finer motor control, although their relevance for primary movement may be limited.

Executive function deficits were linked to disconnections within a left-lateralized fronto-striato-thalamic network. Core regions included the left inferior orbitofrontal gyrus (ORBinfL; t = 9.82), left triangular inferior frontal gyrus (IFGtriangL; t = 4.94), left putamen (PUTL; t = 9.80), left caudate (CAUL; t = 4.91), left thalamus (THAL; t = 4.84), and left insula (INSL; t = 4.73), supporting executive control, cognitive flexibility, and inhibitory processes. Additional associations were observed in regions indirectly related to executive funstions, including the medial superior frontal gyrus (SFGmedL; t = 4.94), right precentral gyrus (PreCGR; t = 4.89), parietal association regions (PALR; t = 4.83, SOGL; t = 4.94), temporo-parietal junction (TPOsupL; t = 4.91), Rolandic operculum (ROLL; t = 4.77), and left cuneus (CUNL; t = 4.86) (Fig. 1).

Processing speed deficits involved fronto–striato–thalamic networks, with strongest effects in the left thalamus (t ≈ 10.0), left caudate (t ≈ 9.9), and left putamen (t ≈ 9.8). Left frontal regions also contributed, including the inferior frontal gyrus (t ≈ 5.1) and middle frontal gyrus (t ≈ 4.8), reflecting impaired cognitive control. Additional involvement of medial superior frontal and orbitofrontal cortices (t ≈ 10.2), bilateral insula (t ≈ 4.8), and right precentral gyrus (t ≈ 5.0) likely reflects attentional and motor components. Regions in temporo-parietal, occipital, brainstem, and Rolandic areas (t ≈ 4.8–5.0) showed lower t values and limited theoretical relevance, suggesting indirect or nonspecific effects.

Conclusion

In conclusion, lesion-derived structural connectivity mapping revealed distinct and overlapping networks underlying post-stroke motor, executive, and processing speed deficits. Motor impairments were primarily linked to the precentral gyrus and corticospinal tract, reflecting core voluntary movement pathways. Executive function deficits were associated with a left-lateralized fronto-striato-thalamic network, highlighting the critical role of frontal and subcortical structures in cognitive control. Processing speed deficits involved overlapping fronto–striato–thalamic circuits with additional frontal, insular, and precentral contributions, reflecting the integration of cognitive and motor components5. These findings demonstrate that network-level disconnection patterns provide a mechanistic account of post-stroke functional impairments and may guide targeted rehabilitation strategies.
