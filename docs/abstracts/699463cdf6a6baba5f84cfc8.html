Introduction

In a 4 day working week, full-time employees reduce their time at work to 4 days, with no loss of salary. International trials have reported both better employee wellbeing, and performance at work.1 Given that both psychological wellbeing, and work performance, are created by the brain, we aimed to understand the neural mechanisms by which a 4 day week can reshape wellbeing and mental health.

In particular, interoception is the ability of the brain to track internal bodily states, with the aim of responding appropriately in order to achieve homeostasis. Interoception has been associated with cognitive and emotional processes, while dysfunction in the interoceptive system plays a central role in the aetiology of mental health disorders.2

In the current study, we investigated whether a 4 day working week impacted wellbeing, and associated interoceptive function, in a healthy population. We expected that spending less time at work (with no loss of salary) would improve symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress. In addition, we hypothesised that the insular cortex, which processes ascending interoceptive signals from peripheral organs, would show differences in activity after 12 weeks of a shorter working week intervention. We furthermore hypothesised that there might be individual participant differences in strength of response to the occupational intervention, with some showing larger benefits to mental health and insular cortex function than others. 

Methods

34 employees (age 20-50; mean 35; 60% male) in businesses switching from a 5-day to a 4-day working week with no loss of salary were recruited.

Participants completed a pre-intervention baseline magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) session immediately prior to their company switching to a 4 day week. They were then scanned a second time after twelve weeks of the 4 day week intervention in a repeated measures design. At the same time as the two imaging sessions, participants completed a battery of wellbeing and occupational psychology questionnaires, including the Depression, Anxiety & Stress Scale (DASS-213). DASS scores were compared pre- and post-4 day week with Wilcoxon tests (due to normality violations).

During the MRI session, a number of scans and tasks were collected on a 3T Siemens Prisma (32 channel head coil), using sequences from the Human Connectome Project Development & Aging protocol.4 This abstract analyses functional MRI data from the interoceptive focus task that was run as part of a longer imaging protocol.

The interoceptive focus task was chosen in order to test how a shorter working week affects the neural processing of bodily sensations often associated with stress. The task has been used by several research groups and shows robust activations in the insular cortex when comparing interoceptive focus trials with exteroceptive visual control trials.5 In short, participants are presented with interoceptive cues (HEART, STOMACH) and asked to focus on sensations from the instructed organ for 10 seconds (Figure 1). During exteroceptive visual trials (10 seconds), participants are presented with a word (TARGET) that dynamically changes in hues of grey. At the end of each trial, participants rate the intensity of either the bodily sensations or the colour change. Participants completed 16 trials of each condition (HEART, STOMACH, TARGET), giving 48 trials in total.

Data were preprocessed in SPM12 with default settings. First-level models were generated to model activation during interoceptive and exteroceptive trials, with regressors for the three trial types, response periods, inter-trial intervals. Six regressors modelled head movement using realignment parameters. Single-regressor t-contrasts were generated for the three trial types. These were entered to a factorial second-level analysis with timepoint (pre-4 day week, post-4 day week) and task condition as within-subjects factors. 

Furthermore, to investigate individual differences in the magnitude of response to the intervention on both wellbeing and the brain, difference scores from pre- to post-4 day week in depression, anxiety, and stress symptoms from the DASS were calculated. Next, the two fMRI sessions were combined in a single first-level model, and between-sessions contrasts generated to compare pre-post imaging changes. These between-session contrasts were entered to a second-level model in a multiple regression design, with ‘change in DASS score’ as covariates (one each for depression, anxiety, stress). Second-level contrasts identified neural areas where a greater change in brain activity correlated with a greater improvement in mental health. Two second-level regression models were run for each DASS score, to isolate whether changes in mental health were associated with either increases or decreases in neural activity: pre>post, and post>pre.

Further data collection in a larger sample, and a matched control group of employees not switching to a 4 day week (also sampled twelve weeks apart), is ongoing.

Results

The 4 day week improved mental health symptoms, with reduced depression (V = 325.5, p = 0.005, d = -0.55), anxiety (V = 324.5, p = 0.007, d = -0.43), and stress (V = 401.5, p < 0.001, d = -0.81) from pre- to post-4 day week.

When contrasting interoceptive trials (HEART, STOMACH) with exteroceptive visual (TARGET) control trials, there was activity in the mid- and posterior insular cortices (p<0.05 FWE cluster-corrected). However, there were no significant differences in neural activity when comparing pre- to post-4 day week.

There were, however, individual differences in the magnitude of change in mental health, and magnitude of change in neural activity, and these were associated with each other (Figure 2). The multiple regression models testing post>pre showed that greater improvements in depression, anxiety, and stress after a 4 day week were associated with greater increases in neural activity in a range of areas, including the insula, precuneus, and occipital cortices (p<0.05 FWE cluster-corrected). In particular, reduced anxiety was associated with greater activity in the anterior insula after the 4 day week.

In the pre>post analysis, there were no significant regions that showed greater improvements in mental health as associated with lower activity after the 4 day week.

Conclusion

A 4 day working week improves mental health, across depression, anxiety, and stress. At the group level in the current cohort, a 4 day week does not change neural activity in interoceptive networks.

However, there are individual differences in the magnitude of response to the intervention, such that the more an individual’s mental health improved, the greater their neural activity in a number of brain areas. In particular, the more that participants’ anxiety improved, the more activity was seen in the anterior insula, which is a key nexus for processing, perceiving, and reflecting upon bodily sensations associated with anxiety (e.g. fast heartbeat, shallow breathing, stomach pain). This may identify a putative neural mechanism underlying the enhancements to wellbeing that are reported in multiple international trials of shorter working weeks.1

In the present analysis, sample size likely limited statistical power, but recruitment for a larger sample of 4 day week participants is continuing. Furthermore, the ongoing addition of a control group will enhance the possibility to rule out Hawthorne effects in reporting socially desirable outcomes (mental health), or practice effects in repeat imaging (fMRI).
