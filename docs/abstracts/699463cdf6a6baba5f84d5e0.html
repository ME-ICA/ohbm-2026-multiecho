Introduction

Personalized spinal cord models are essential for enhancing the effectiveness of epidural electrical stimulation (EES) therapies aimed at restoring neurological functions in individuals with spinal cord injury (SCI)1,2 and Parkinson’s Disease (PD). Built on detailed, patient-specific medical imaging data, these models play a critical role in both the pre-operative planning and post-operative fine-tuning of multielectrode EES implant placements 

The meticulous processing and annotation of the medical imaging required to create the spinal cord personalized model is labor-intensive and time-consuming. Anatomical tissue segmentation is a bottleneck for the scalability and widespread clinical adoption of personalized spinal cord models.

Artificial neural networks (ANNs) hold promise for automating tissue segmentation from medical imaging, thereby reducing reliance on manual intervention 2. Frameworks such as nnU-Net 3, have proved successful in many segmentation tasks. However, they face significant challenges due to limited labeled medical data, variability between individuals, imaging protocols and anatomical regions.

Self-supervised learning (SSL) has emerged as a powerful method for deriving meaningful data representations without human supervision, leveraging available unlabeled data4. SSL has fueled the success of natural language processing (NLP) models like ChatGPT.

Taking inspiration from NLP, we use SSL to leverage unlabeled MRI volumes (private and public data-sets 5) to develop spinal cord models that can be adapted to tasks with minimal expert intervention. Similar to NLP models, our framework, SpineGPT, learns by reconstructing masked parts from their visible counterparts, extracting representations that capture the spinal cord’s underlying structure and variability. We use these representations to fine-tune SpineGPT to solve clinically relevant problems such as spinal tissue segmentation.

Methods

Self-supervised Pre-training

Our current implementation of SpineGPT builds on a masked auto-encoder (MAE) framework 4, learning self-supervised representations of MRI volumes through a self-reconstruction objective. In this process, a 3D chunk of the volume is largely masked (80%) at random and used as input to an autoencoder neural network, which is trained to reconstruct the missing portions from the visible parts (Figure 1). Through this approach, which we refer to as the pretraining phase, the model’s encoder learns to map the original volume to a latent representation that captures informative and essential structural details.

SpineGPT’s current encoder and decoder consist of standard Vision Transformer layers adapted for 3D volumes. In this stage, we leverage all available unlabeled data, totaling over 1,800 diverse T2 weighted MRI volumes of the spine across different regions (cervical, thoracic, lumbar and sacral) from both publicly available 5 and private datasets 1,2.

Spinal Tissue Segmentation

Segmenting various spinal tissues is vital for developing personalized spinal cord models used in EES therapies. These tissues include spinal roots, white matter (WM), cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), vertebrae, and intervertebral discs (IVD). Current methods, based on supervised learning, allow for segmentations for tissues except instanced spinal roots in the lumbosacral region2.

We have proposed a strategy of freezing the encoder of SpineGPT and finetune the decoder to infer the previously described spinal tissues, leveraging SpineGPT’s latent representations. 

We artificially generate a first larger labeled data-set using a previously trained nnU-Net model for WM, CSF and spinal roots (binary) tissues segmentation 2  using 954 T2 weighted MRI volumes. In this first stage, we finetune the decoder using this larger labeled data-set using 795 MRI volumes for training and 159 for testing.

On a second stage, we continue the finetuning of the decoder using a manually labeled high quality dataset of 18 T2 weighted MRI volumes of the lumbosacral region containing both ventral and dorsal spinal roots 1. In this final stage we use 15 MRI volumes for training and 3 for testing.

Artefact Reconstruction

Magnetic field inhomogeneities often impact MRI volumes in different forms. Ultra-high-resolution MRI volumes inhomogeneities are manifested as bending artifacts, dark thin structures caused by the air around the neck or present in the lungs of the individual. These artifacts can significantly compromise image quality, posing a challenge to obtaining clean and clinically useful images. We fine-tune the decoder of SpineGPT to effectively identify and remove these artifacts, addressing a critical need in high-quality spinal imaging.

One major challenge in this task is the absence of a ground truth dataset for artifact removal, as no MRI volumes are available with both clean and artifact-affected versions for direct comparison. To address this, we manually created a dataset of artifacts in a subset of our dataset (48 T2 weighted MRI volumes) creating a library of real artifacts. We then train an nnU-Net3 to detect and segment artifact-affected regions in our MRI dataset. From this library, we generated synthetic artifacts to apply to clean MRI regions, resulting in a labeled dataset that distinguishes between clean and corrupted areas. We fine-tune SpineGPT to reconstruct these corrupted areas.

Results

Pre-Training

The input is chunks of 33x32x64 with patch sizes of 4x4x8. The current architecture of the encoder of SpineGPT consists of an encoder of 12 standard transformer layers with 16 self-attention heads. The decoder consists of 8 transformer layers. The pre-trained phase minimizes the reconstruction error between the input patches and the original patches using Mean Square Error (MSE). Our model has 64 million parameters.

Latent Space Exploration

We built a framework to visualize and explore the latent embeddings of the autoencoder architecture of SpineGPT. Using dimensionality reduction algorithm T-SNE, we are able to project and analyse in the latent space individual MRI volumes, different regions (cervical, thoracic, lumbar) and different tissues (WM, CSF, spinal roots) decodability.

Spinal Tissue Segmentation

We finetuned the decoder first on the artificially labeled dataset containing CSF, WM and spinal roots consisting of 954 individual T2 weighted MRI volumes (795 training;159 testing). We minimized the Dice loss. We finetuned on a second stage on the manually labeled dataset containing CSF, WM and spinal roots consisting of 18 individual T2 weighted high resolution MRI volumes (15 training;3 testing). Finally, we tested on a public labeled data set of 14 participants on the lumbosacral region only containing dorsal roots. We achieved promising preliminary results, with Dice scores of 0.87±0.21, 0.87±0.31 and 0.615±0.11 for segmentations of CSF, WM and spinal roots respectively (Figure 2) on our first test set and Dice scores of 0.91±0.03, 0.89±0.23 and 0.41±0.17 for segmentations of CSF, WM and spinal roots respectively on the second test set.

Artefact Reconstruction

The trained nnU-Net network for artefact segmentation achieved a dice score of 0.89. Using this network we created a dataset of over 500 segmented artefacts from which we compute shape and characteristics statistics. We generated synthetic artefacts and paired them with artefact-free MRI scans to build a comprehensive dataset for training on artefact removal.

Our preliminary pipeline achieved a Mean Absolute Error (MAE) score of 2.251, showing promising reconstruction of artifact affected regions. We are confident that further training and optimization of SpineGPT will consequently improve SpineGPT’s ability to reconstruct artifact affected regions, ensuring cleaner and more accurate MRI volumes.

Conclusion

We introduce SpineGPT, a foundation framework for spinal cord T2 weighted MRI across different regions of the spinal cord. We finetuned SpineGPT to address, for the moment, two downstream tasks, spinal tissues segmentation, in particular lumbosacral spinal roots and artefact reconstruction. Preliminary results are promising, already achieving state-of-the-art results in the spinal roots tissue segmentation task in the lumbosacral region. 

The segmentation task could be improved by predicting distribution maps instead of binary masks. These maps provide richer information about the model’s uncertainty, which can be leveraged to refine the segmentation process, especially in regions with overlapping spinal roots or artefacts. By focusing on probability distribution maps, the segmentation process can become more accurate and robust.

To improve artefact reconstruction, we could explore generative adversarial networks (GAN) instead of using the MSE loss. Indeed, adversarial training would bring closer to a human level expert the evaluation of the quality of the reconstructed region. Additionally, using percentage-based artefact masking instead of binary detection. This method would assign a pixel-wise percentage to represent the level of artefact present, allowing for a more nuanced blending of the reconstruction with the original image. By combining the reconstructed areas with the original image based on these percentages, the resulting output could achieve smoother transitions and better integration, improving overall reconstruction quality. Introducing additional downstream tasks, such as improving scan quality, is another area of potential improvement.

Preliminary results from SpineGPT demonstrate that its MAE-based framework can serve as a strong foundation model for clinical tasks, including multi-region spinal tissue segmentation and artefact reconstruction. We plan to build upon this foundation for future work to further facilitate EES based therapies.
