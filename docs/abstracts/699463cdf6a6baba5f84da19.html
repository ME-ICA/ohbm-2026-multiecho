Introduction

Irritability, characterized by a heightened sensitivity to frustration, is among the most frequent concerns prompting a clinical referral in childhood and adolescence.1 It also represents a transdiagnostic risk factor linked to the development of both internalizing and externalizing disorders.1 Despite its clinical significance, the neural substrates underlying irritability in youth remain inadequately understood. The present study aimed to clarify these mechanisms by examining associations between irritability and large-scale brain network connectivity in a large sample of children. 

Methods

Participants included 9- to 10-year-old children (N ~ 10,260) from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study.2 Resting state fMRI data (20 minutes of passive viewing) was acquired with multiband echo-planar imaging and processed using standard procedures.3,4 Functional connectivity was quantified using Fisher-transformed correlations within and between large-scale networks and subcortical regions. A sum score for irritability was derived at baseline using items from the Child Behavior Checklist and analyzed in relation to 306 connectivity network variables from the Gordon parcellation (12 within-network, 66 between-network, and 228 network-subcortical regions)5 using mixed effects modeling. Covariates included age, sex, MRI scanner model, and parent’s highest level of education and income as proxies for socioeconomic status. We included a random intercept for family to account for clustering of children within families. Multiple comparisons were accounted for using false discovery rate (FDR) correction.  

Results

Irritability showed significant associations with within-network connectivity of the cingulo-parietal network, dorsal attention network, and somatomotor-mouth network. We also found significant associations with irritability and between-network connectivity involving nine networks focused on dynamic shifting between sensory input, attention, and executive control.

Conclusion

These findings highlight the associations of irritability with distinct large-scale brain networks, particularly those involved with cognitive control, attentional engagement, auditory processing, and motor–emotional expression. Our results utilize a large, community-based sample of children to contribute to a better understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying irritability that may inform future research on targeted interventions for irritable youth.
