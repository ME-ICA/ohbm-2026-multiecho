Introduction

The structural reserve, i.e., residual undamaged white matter, has been shown to play a critical role in motor recovery after stroke by providing circuitous routes that facilitate functional network reorganization.1 Global structural network topology, as well as tract-specific integrity derived from diffusion MRI, have been suggested as estimates of structural reserve.1,2 Importantly, various methodological factors have been discussed to impact these tractography-derived measures. A seminal factor lies in the turning angle, which determines how sharply a streamline is allowed to bend at each step during tractography. Despite their critical impact on tract reconstruction, turning angles are rarely reported or systematically evaluated in studies of stroke patients. Therefore, we here investigated whether the turning angle systematically affects tractography results in a cohort of chronic stroke patients and age-matched controls. Given the established relationship between structural reserve and motor recovery,1 we assessed associations between motor impairment and angle-dependent tractography results as measures of face validity to facilitate comparison of angle-dependent findings.

Methods

Diffusion spectrum imaging (DSI) data were acquired from 72 patients with ischemic stroke (mean age 68 ± 11.2 years; 18 female) and 62 healthy controls (mean age 66.3 ± 8.8 years; 21 female). The MRI-data were preprocessed using an adapted pipeline for DSI scheme based on an established pipeline.3 Whole-brain tractography was performed for each subject at a range of different turning angles (30°, 40°, 50°, 60°, 70°, 80°, 90° degrees) using the deterministic fiber tracking algorithm implemented in DSI Studio.4 A structural cortical parcellation, i.e., Desikan-Killiany atlas in FreeSurfer, was applied for tracts (the number of streamlines) extracted from the tractography of seven turning angles, respectively. Accordingly, the lengths of those tracts were calculated. 

We evaluated the influence of turning angles on whole-brain streamline counts and length using repeated-measures ANOVA and linear mixed-effects models. Besides whole brain analyses, we focused on two representative tracts relevant for motor function: an interhemispheric tract connecting the left and right precentral gyri (M1–M1), and an intrahemispheric tract connecting M1 to dorsal premotor cortex (M1–PMd) within the ipsilesional hemisphere of stroke patients. To control for anatomically implausible tracts, we investigated the connection between the precentral gyrus and the visual cortex (M1–V1). To assess how the turning angle affected estimates of the structural reserve, we first evaluated the relationship between tract-specific white matter integrity and motor impairment by computing Spearman correlations between streamline counts in the single tracts mentioned above and clinical motor scores. Second, we assessed associations between the global topology of structural brain networks and motor function across different turning angles, using graph metrics established in stroke research, such as global efficiency, as a measure of network integration, and modularity, reflecting network segregation.5 

Results

We observed a significant effect of turning angle on whole-brain streamline counts (F(6, 798) = 4132.73, p < 0.001). Linear mixed models confirmed a strong positive effect of turning angles on streamline counts (all p < 0.001), with estimates increasing monotonically from 40° (β = 0.5) to 90° (β = 2.6). Of note, the effect of turning angles far exceeded streamline count differences between patients with ischemic stroke and age-matched controls detected in the ipsilesional hemisphere (p = 0.033).

While some tracts were relatively robust to turning angle variations - for example, the M1–PMd tract - others were highly angle-dependent, such as M1–M1. At low turning angles, estimated lengths of M1–M1 were anatomically implausible, while high turning angles introduced spurious connections, e.g., M1–V1. Spearman correlations revealed positive relationships between streamline counts and motor function for both the M1–PMd tract and the M1–M1-tract only for specific turning angles. Similarly, graph-theoretical measures of network integration and segregation strongly depended on the underlying turning angle. With higher turning angles, network integration increased, while network segregation decreased. Moreover, correlations with motor function were only present for specific turning angles.

Conclusion

Our findings emphasize the critical role of the turning angle in deterministic whole-brain tractography which strongly influenced both whole-brain and tract-specific streamline counts and lengths. Our hypothesis-driven tract analyses align with the notion that low turning angles may underestimate tract length and miss meaningful connections, while high angles likely produce anatomically implausible streamlines at the whole-brain level. In the context of structural reserve estimation in stroke patients, the relationship between tract integrity and motor function is maximized at distinct turning angles for distinct tracts and therefore the optimal turning angle seems tract-specific. Regarding global topology, assessments of the potential positive or negative impact of network properties on motor function after stroke varied depending on the underlying turning angle. In summary, our data highlight that the choice of turning angle substantially impacts both tract reconstruction and clinical interpretations. These results underscore the importance of systematically evaluating turning angles in studies of structural connectivity and post-stroke recovery, and suggest that optimizing this parameter is essential for reliable assessments of structural reserve after stroke.
