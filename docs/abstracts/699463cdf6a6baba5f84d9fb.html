Introduction

According to the dimensional view of psychosis, the psychotic phenotype is expressed along a gradient of severity that ranges from mild psychotic experiences to clinical symptoms. Schizotypy provides a multidimensional framework to capture this continuum, encompassing personality traits that reflect the positive, negative, and disorganized dimensions of schizophrenia. It offers a fine-grained characterization of psychosis-related dimensions, and in contrast to scales which assess clinical high risk, acknowledges subclinical psychotic manifestations which have trait character. 

Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) has become a key tool for investigating biomarkers of psychosis, with dynamic approaches providing access to the brain’s intrinsic activity patterns and transitions between large-scale functional states. Numerous studies have shown widespread reductions in brain connectivity in schizophrenia, and recent work highlights alterations in the temporal properties of functional brain states. Studies of populations who have been identified as at risk of developing psychosis, have reported altered functional dynamics comprising the salience network, mirroring patterns seen in clinical psychosis. However, it remains unclear how such dynamic abnormalities extend along the broader continuum of schizotypy.

In this study, we examined large-scale functional dynamics across the schizotypy continuum using fMRI data from individuals with psychosis and healthy participants with low and high schizotypy. We used the innovation-driven coactivation pattern (iCAP) framework1, which identifies moments of significant change in brain activity to derive large-scale networks and characterize their temporal properties, allowing overlapping brain states to be captured1,2. Our goal was to elucidate whether a continuum of dynamic dysfunction underlies the schizotypy continuum, including its constituent symptom dimensions. 

Methods

Data

The data analyzed belongs to the cross-sectional study “Ventral language stream in schizophrenia with regard to semantic and visuo-spatial processing anomalies” (VELAS)3, conducted at the Psychiatric University Hospital Zurich. We utilized the study’s 3T resting-state functional magnetic imaging (rs-fMRI) scans (TR=1.17s; voxel 3×3×3.75 mm), T1-weighted anatomical imaging, demographic, clinical and behavioral data. After filtering the data to ensure rs-fMRI and T1-weighted anatomical data quality, n=91 participants of the original n=120 from VELAS were included in this study. The final sample comprised a healthy group (n=62 selected through online screening of n=1061), subdivided into a low schizotypy group (n=33) and a high schizotypy group (n=29), and a group of patients diagnosed with early psychosis (n=29). Screening of healthy individuals using the Multidimensional Schizotypy Scale (MSS) and the Oxford-Liverpool Inventory of Feelings and Experiences (O-LIFE) enabled allocation to the low- and high schizotypy group (2SD below or above the mean of the normative data). The gender, educational level and age composition of the three groups were matched. 

Preprocessing

Anatomical and functional data were preprocessed using fMRIprep 23.2.1. The preprocessing of T1-weighted anatomical images included correction for intensity non-uniformity, brain mask extraction and volume-based spatial normalization to the standard MNI152 space (ICBM 2009c Nonlinear Asymmetric template). Functional data preprocessing included: generation of a reference volume, estimation of head motion parameters, co-registration to the T1w reference, and resampling into the standard template. The fMRI scans then underwent a Python-based denoising pipeline, combining AFNI and the Nilearn toolbox. This comprised despiking, demeaning, nuisance regression with 24 motion parameters, temporal scrubbing (FD>0.5) with least squares spectral estimation interpolation, and spatial smoothing with a Gaussian kernel (FWHM = 6×6×7.5 mm).

Total activation and innovation-driven coactivation patterns (iCAPs)

Preexisting MATLAB code was used to derive the iCAPs1,2. We first applied the Total Activation framework4, a spatiotemporal regularization method that deconvolves the fMRI time series from the hemodynamic response and produces an innovation signal, which encodes transient brain activity by spikes (Figure 1). Significant activation changepoints are then extracted from the innovation signal using spatiotemporal constraints, and used to define significant innovation signals, containing positive and negative (sign flipped) significant innovations. These brain signals were then concatenated across all participants and entered temporal k-means clustering with K=17, producing the iCAPs. Finally, in order to measure iCAP recruitment, we extracted each iCAP’s innovation counts, by counting the amount of innovation frames belonging to its cluster. In future work we will derive further temporal measures using transient-informed regression2.

Statistical Analysis

To investigate group differences in iCAP innovation counts, we performed an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) for every spatial pattern including age, gender, and mean framewise displacement as covariates. Global p-values for the group effect were computed and corrected for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR) method. For iCAPs surviving FDR correction, post-hoc pairwise t-tests were performed to identify which groups differed significantly, adjusting for the same covariates. We examined the dependence of the iCAP innovation counts on the schizotypy scales (MSS, O-LIFE) by performing multiple linear regressions, including age, gender, and motion as covariates. The Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) was also tested, as well as the distinct symptom dimensions of MSS, O-LIFE and PANSS. All p-values were corrected for multiple comparisons using FDR. Significant associations were defined at p < 0.05. 

Results

Results 

iCAPs overlap with known resting-state networks

Using rs-fMRI scans from the low schizotypy, high schizotypy and early psychosis groups, we derived 17 iCAPs ordered with respect to their occurrence in innovation frames (Figure 2a). These spatial maps correspond to known resting-state networks (Figure 2a). Importantly, we identified the default mode network (iCAP4:DMN), including a posterior component (iCAP7:postDMN); the salience network (iCAP1:SN); and the frontoparietal network, composed of two principal patterns (iCAP8:FPN1 and iCAP14:FPN2), a left-dominant part (iCAP17:leftFPN) and a right-dominant part (iCAP13:rightFPN). These three networks constitute the well-known triple network, upon which large-scale models of neuropsychiatric disorders such as schizophrenia are built. The sensory networks we identify are the somatosensory (iCAP15:SMN), auditory (iCAP3:AUD) and visual networks, the latter decomposed into two primary visual (iCAP9:PrimVIS and iCAP12:PrimVIS2) and three secondary visual components (iCAP5:SecVIS1, iCAP10:SecVIS2 and iCAP16:SecVIS3). Finally, we identify an amygdala-hippocampus network (iCAP2:AMY/HIP), and the retrosplenial network (iCAP6:RSP1 and iCAP11:RSP2).

Groups show different number of innovations in visual and frontoparietal components

For each participant, we then computed their iCAP-specific total innovation counts, i.e. representing the number of innovation frames that fall within their spatial cluster. We first investigated whether the network innovation count is driven by a group effect (Figure 2B). Using ANCOVA, which corrected for age, gender and motion effects, we found statistical significance within a component of the secondary visual network (SecVIS1, iCAP 5, p<0.05, FDR-corrected) and within two frontoparietal patterns (rightFPN, iCAP13, p<0.05; FPN2, iCAP14, p<0.05, FDR-corrected). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons revealed that, compared with both the low and high schizotypy groups, the psychosis group showed significantly decreased innovation counts in the visual network (SecVIS1, iCAP5) and significantly increased innovation counts in the frontoparietal components.

Frontoparietal innovation counts positively correlate with negative schizotypy dimensions

Regression analyses revealed significant positive correlations between innovation counts in frontoparietal patterns (iCAP13 and iCAP14) and several schizotypy dimensions. Significant associations were observed with the introvertive anhedonia dimension of the O-LIFE (iCAP13: β=0.597, p<0.05; iCAP14: β=0.716, p<0.01), the negative dimension of the MSS (iCAP13: β=0.546; ns; iCAP14: β=0.791, p<0.01), and the negative dimension of the PANSS (iCAP13: β=0.645; p<0.01; iCAP14: β=0.532; p<0.01). 

This also occurred to a lesser extent in the unusual experiences dimension of the O-LIFE (iCAP13: β=-0.038; ns; iCAP14: β=0.433, p<0.05) and the positive dimensions of the MSS (iCAP13: β=0.059; ns; iCAP14: β=0.721, p<0.01) and PANSS (iCAP13: β=0.717, p<0.05; iCAP14: β=0.622, p<0.05). 

For total scores, associations were observed for the O-LIFE (iCAP13: β=0.056; ns; iCAP14: β=0.186, p<0.01), MSS (iCAP13: β=0.122; ns; iCAP14: β=0.273, p<0.01), and PANSS (iCAP13: β=0.236, p<0.01; iCAP14: β=0.201, p<0.01). 

Finally, the disorganization components of the O-LIFE (iCAP13: β=0.170; ns; iCAP14: β=0.392, p<0.05) and MSS (iCAP13: β=0.235; ns; iCAP14: β=0.326; ns) showed weaker positive relationships, while the impulsive nonconformity dimension of the O-LIFE showed no significant associations in either iCAP (iCAP13: β=-0.224; ns; iCAP14: β=0.556; ns).

Conclusion

The derived iCAPs are spatially overlapping and map to well-known resting-state large-scale networks, including the triple network composed of the DMN, SN and FPN (Figure 2A). We note the abundance of visual components and suggest that this might be because participants had their eyes open during the functional recordings.

Our findings on the increased number of transients in frontoparietal components in the psychosis group compared to the healthy groups (Figure 2B) aligns with the salience-network-centered triple-model of schizophrenia, according to which, in healthy individuals, the FPN and DMN are antagonistic networks that get activated or deactivated by the SN. In schizophrenia, periodic surges of dopamine would be ascribing salience to non-salient stimuli, triggering switches from the DMN to the FPN in a maladaptive way. In terms of functional dynamics, this manifests as having less stable functional states within and between these networks. Consistent with this framework, the increased number of innovations observed in frontoparietal components in our study may reflect more frequent activations and deactivations of frontoparietal regions, mapping onto the concept of aberrant network switching.

We further interrogated the data to examine whether a fine-grained mapping exists between these increased numbers of transients and the schizotypy scores, including their constituent dimensions. Regression analysis indicated that the dynamic dysfunction observed in the group comparison correlated with the numerical schizotypy MSS and O-LIFE scores, specifically and more strongly to their negative symptom dimensions (Figure 2C), which include avolition, anhedonia, or asociality. The demonstrated relationship between negative symptom dimensions and the increased transients in frontoparietal regions is consistent with the findings of a recent study which found excessive transitions from a sensorimotor-dominant state to a control-salience network state in individuals with schizophrenia5. We importantly identify a similar relationship within the schizotypy construct, where stable negative dimensions of personality embody also this finding. 

Finally, we will extend the present study to a more complex analysis of iCAPs’ temporal characteristics, for instance by investigating the role of temporal coactivations2 in the different groups and across the schizotypy and PANSS scales. To sum up, the present work constitutes an important step towards the mapping of dynamical functional abnormalities in the schizotypy continuum.
