Introduction

The human brain consumes an exceptionally high amount of energy in comparison to its mass. While representing only 2% of total body weight, it consumes more than 20% of the body’s glucose supply. Glucose is the primary fuel and the consumption varies substantially across brain regions. ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG-PET) enables the quantitative measurement of the cerebral glucose uptake in-vivo and is widely used to investigate brain metabolism in both health and disease.

At the cellular level, glucose metabolism is tightly regulated and differs across brain cell types. Neurons account for the majority of glucose consumption and primarily metabolize  glucose through oxidative phosphorylation (OxPhos) to fuel their energy-costly signaling processes. Glial cells, including astrocytes (Ast), microglia (Mic) and oligodendrocytes (Oli) rely less on the complete oxidation of glucose and may use alternative substrates such as fatty acids. Metabolic differences within the cell types emerge from differential gene expressions and have not have been studied in humans on a whole brain level.

We aim to elucidate the cellular underpinnings of regional glucose metabolism in the human brain by integrating transcriptomic and FDG-PET data. First, we characterize the metabolic profiles of distinct brain cell types using a transcriptomic approach and describe how glucose is utilized at the cellular level. We then relate cell type-specific expression signatures to quantitative measures of glucose uptake to investigate how cellular composition contributes to regional differences in glucose metabolism observed with FDG-PET.

Methods

Spatial microarray data from the Allen Human Brain Atlas was processed through abagen.1,2 Left hemisphere data was assigned to the Extended Human Connectome Project (HCPex) Atlas.3 Microarray data was normalized and aggregated across donors to generate expression maps of 15637 genes across 203 cortical and subcortical ROIs. 

Each cell-type and metabolic pathway was represented by a selected set of genes. Cell-type markers (inhibitory and excitatory neurons (ExNeu), Ast, Oli and Mic) were defined by Seidlitz et al. 2020.4 Metabolic gene sets were extracted from the MSigDB and included glycolysis (Gly), oxidative phosphorylation (OxPhos), Fatty acid (FA) metabolism, reactive oxygen species (ROS) and peroxisome (Per). 

By calculating the median expression of each gene set per ROI, we generated expression maps of cell-types and metabolic pathways. We calculated the spearman correlation between the cell-type and metabolic expression maps to characterize the relationships between regional cellular composition and metabolic processes.

Correlation was calculated for cortical and subcortical ROIs separately, tested for significance through t-tests and p-values were Bonferroni adjusted.

Next, we sought to relate cell type-specific organization to regional differences in glucose metabolism measured in vivo. An average CMRGlc map was created in the standard MNI152 space (MNI152NLin6ASym, 1mm3) from 12 healthy participants (8 female and 4 male, mean age = 32 ± 9 years). The arterial input function and Patlak plot approach was used to quantify subject specific FDG-PET data into CMRGlc images. CMRGlc images were aligned with the standard MNI152 1mm3 space using the ANTs. Average CMRGlc map were computed across all subject’s maps aligned to the MNI space. Pearson correlation between cell-type expression maps and the average CMRglc map were calculated and corrected for spatial autocorrelation using Brainsmash. 5

Lastly, we investigated how cell composition and metabolism contribute to cortical complexity. ROIs were ordered according to the anterior-posterior (AP) axis and genes with similar expression patterns across the axis were clustered hierarchically. For each cluster, we averaged the expression of the corresponding genes per ROI and analyzed the relation between expression and AP axis localization by linear regression.

Results

Cell-type and metabolic pathway expression differs between cortex and subcortex (Fig. 2A). Median gene expression within cortex and subcortex were statistically different for neurons and glial cells. Neuronal markers showed higher median expression in the cortex relative to the subcortex (P ≤ 0.0001), whereas glial marker expression was elevated in the subcortex (P ≤ 0.0001). A similar pattern was observed for metabolic pathways. Neuron-associated pathways related to high energy turnover were significantly higher in cortex (OxPhos, P ≤ 0.01 and ROS, P ≤ 0.0001). Glia-associated pathways, including FA-M, PER, and MYE, were significantly elevated in subcortex (P ≤ 0.0001). Gly showed no difference in median expression across both compartments. 


Regional expression profiles revealed variation in both cell-type composition and metabolic pathway expression across cortical areas and subcortical structures (Fig. 2B).. Neuronal genes were expressed highly and uniformly across cortical regions with marked reductions in subcortical regions. Glia populations showed the opposite trend, with a more heterogenous expression across cortical regions and higher expression in subcortical structures. Metabolic pathways mirrored these distributions: OxPhos and ROS tracked neuronal abundance across cortical regions, while FA-M, PER, and MYE increased in subcortical regions consistent with glial enrichment.


Correlation analyses demonstrated distinct associations between cell types, CMRglc, and metabolic pathways (Fig. 2C). ExN and InN showed positive correlations with CMRglc and with pathways supporting high energetic demand (GLY, OxPhos, ROS). Glia did not correlate with CMRglc and displayed more heterogeneous metabolic profiles. Astrocytes correlated with Gly as well as FA-M and PER; oligodendrocytes and OPCs showed strong associations with FA-M, PER, and MYE; and microglia correlated with GLY, ROS, FA-M, PER, and MYE. Endothelial cells correlated with CMRglc and all metabolic pathways. 

Cell type independent clustering of gene expression reveal evolutionary anterior-to posterior (AP) gradients (Fig. 2D). The cluster characterized by a posterior-to-anterior increase of expression was dominated by glia-related genes. Conversly, the cluster dominated by an opposite (PA) gradient was enriched for neuronal genes. Linear regression confirmed significant spatial organization of cell-types along the AP axis (AP cluster: R2=0.18, P ≤ 0.0001; PA cluster: R2=0.0.38, P ≤ 0.0001). 

Conclusion

Our analysis reveals clear regional differences in both cell-type composition and metabolic prevalence between the human cortex and subcortex. Neurons and glia showed opposing enrichment across these compartments. Neuronal markers and neuron-associated metabolic pathways supporting high energetic turnover (OxPhos and ROS) were significantly elevated in the cortex, aligning with the high synaptic density, sustained firing rates, and computational demands of cortical circuits. In contrast, glial markers and pathways related to lipid metabolism, peroxisomes, and myelination were enriched in subcortical structures. The elevated expression of these genes matches the greater prevalence of long-range axonal tracts and the metabolic requirements for myelin maintenance and lipid processing in these regions. The stability of glycolytic expression across compartments suggests that baseline glucose utilization is preserved throughout the brain, while downstream metabolic fates diverge with cellular composition and functional specialization.

Regional analyses further showed that cortical areas exhibit relatively uniform neuronal expression, whereas glial expression is more heterogeneous and increases markedly in subcortical structures. Metabolic pathway distributions closely mirrored these trends, reinforcing that regional metabolic signatures largely reflect underlying cell-type composition.

Correlation analyses provided additional insight into the metabolic profiles of cell types. In the cortex, neurons correlated positively with CMRglc and with pathways supporting rapid ATP production and redox balance, reinforcing the central role of neurons as primary drivers of cortical energy consumption. Glial populations exhibited more diverse and specialized metabolic associations. Astrocytes were linked to glycolysis and lipid-related pathways, consistent with their role in metabolic support and substrate shuttling. Oligodendrocytes and OPCs were strongly associated with fatty-acid metabolism, peroxisomal pathways, and myelination, reflecting their specialized function in myelin production and maintenance. Microglia showed broad metabolic associations, suggesting a flexible metabolic profile that may support immune surveillance and region-specific functions. Endothelial cells displayed correlations with CMRglc and all metabolic pathways, highlighting their integrative role at the neurovascular interface. Notably, these structured relationships were largely absent in the subcortex, where only isolated correlations were observed between select glial populations and metabolic pathways. This may reflect a more heterogeneous or regionally constrained metabolic organization in subcortical nuclei, or a reduced coupling between transcriptional profiles and bulk metabolic measures.

Clustering of gene expression uncovered prominent anterior–posterior gradients independent of predefined cell types. The cluster with a posterior-to-anterior increase was dominated by glial markers, whereas the opposing gradient was enriched for neuronal genes. The significant linear relationships along the AP axis indicate that large-scale transcriptional gradients reflect systematic shifts in cellular composition and metabolic specialization. These gradients likely correspond to evolutionary and developmental patterning, with posterior regions supporting sensorimotor integration and anterior regions supporting higher-order cognition, each with distinct cellular and metabolic demands.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that regional brain metabolism is fundamentally shaped by cell-type composition and organized along large-scale spatial gradients. By integrating transcriptomic and CMRglc data, this work provides a unified framework for interpreting regional metabolic variation in the human brain and offers insights into how altered cell-type balance or metabolic coupling may contribute to neurological and neurodegenerative disorders.
