Introduction

The widespread availability of several large longitudinal neuroimaging samples has contributed substantially to our understanding of child and adolescent brain development. Efforts like the ABCD Study involve repeated assessments of brain function using tasks designed to stimulate cognitive processes and recording of task-related fMRI. However, recent work has shown that estimates of task-related brain activity using fMRI suffer from poor reliability1, and show relatively weak associations with individual differences in behavior, largely due to task designs that maximize group-level effects2. Indeed, of the more than 1600 publications using ABCD Study data, fewer than 6% clearly involve task fMRI3.

Meaningful improvements in brain-behavior associations have arisen through ‘neural signatures’, a term often associated with multivariate pattern analysis decoders of task-related brain data4,5. These signatures are often derived using machine learning-based methods that differentiate between neural data uniquely associated with different task conditions. The current project demonstrates that (1) neural signatures can be constructed for all fMRI tasks in the ABCD Study, (2) individual differences in scores derived from neural signatures are more reliable between imaging runs than estimates of task-related activation, and (3) these scores appear to relate to individual differences in cognition, behavior, psychopathology, and substance use. This work extends that of Baranger and colleagues5 by applying neural signature-based analyses to all ABCD task data collected at each study follow-up point.

Methods

A neural signature (NS) of task-related activation was created for each ABCD Study fMRI task (N-Back, SST, and MID) using methods like those employed in Baranger et al. (2025)5. Estimates of task-related activation were obtained from the ABCD 6.0 curated release. Activation estimates were averaged across imaging runs for task conditions conditions separately, parcellated into Destrieux cortical and ASEG subcortical regions of interest (ROIs), and residualized for association with participants’ age, sex, and study site. All included participants met task- and image-related inclusion criteria described in the data release notes. Elastic Net logistic regression was used to obtain a multivariate signature of task activation by classifying each participant’s task-related activation map according to its task condition (e.g., 2-back or 0-back; Correct Go or Incorrect Go) using a nested cross-validation scheme. Regression Beta weights averaged across validation folds were Haufe-transformed to ensure feature importance reflected underlying brain activation rather than suppression of highly correlated features. These transformed weights were taken as estimates of the multivariate neural signature. One model was fit per task condition per study follow-up point.

In order to obtain an axis of individual differences related to this NS, the trained classifier was used to make predictions from each participant’s activation maps. To introduce a component of specificity to these values, a prediction obtained using a given participant’s non-target map was subtracted from the prediction obtained using that participant’s target map (for example, 0-back predictions were subtracted from 2-back predictions, as the classifier was trained to predict the 2-back condition). This difference in prediction for each participant was taken as a single, continuous score of individual differences in task-related brain activation.

To evaluate construct validity of these NS scores, we compared group-level estimates of neural signature scores to task-contrast activations using linear regressions. Using a data driven approach, we also evaluated differences in neural signature scores between ABCD cannabis users (>= 20 lifetime uses) and a sample of matched controls.

Results

Neural signature classification models were trained for all ABCD Study fMRI tasks and contrasts. The best-performing models (N-Back task 2-back vs. 0-back and Faces vs. Places) achieved cross-validated ROC AUCs of 0.874 +/- 0.0096 and 0.998 +/- 0.0008 respectively at study Baseline. Similarly strong performance was seen in the SST (e.g., Correct Stop vs. Correct Go: AUC = 0.927 +/- 0.0043) and MID (e.g., Large Loss vs. Neutral Positive Feedback: AUC = 0.876 +/- 0.0057 ). Reliability of derived neural signature scores between imaging runs was also high (e.g., 0.509, 95% C.I. = [0.492, 0.526] for N-Back 2-back vs. 0-back) relative to the most reliable ROI (ICC = 0.156). See Figure 1 for a selection of performance metrics from several ABCD tasks and contrasts.

Figure 2 shows associations between a selection of neural signature classifiers and phenotypes of interest at study baseline. In particular, neural signature scores tended to associate more strongly with indicators of impulsive behaviors, cognition, and dimensions of mental health than most ROI-level estimates of task-related activation. Additionally, differences in neural signature scores between cannabis initiators and matched controls were observed at later timepoints, although we note that the number of initiators was small (n=104).

Conclusion

The present work builds on a growing literature showing that techniques based in multivariate pattern analysis of task-related brain activation can help resolve individual differences by clarifying signal obtained from these images. In addition to providing more reliable estimates of regional activity in brain-wide association studies, neural signatures can distill whole-brain signal into a single value shown to predict phenotypes of interest, including those related to impulsivity and risk-taking behaviors. Ongoing and future work will assess the longitudinal patterns inherent in these neural signature models of brain activation and what those might reveal about child and adolescent cognitive development.
