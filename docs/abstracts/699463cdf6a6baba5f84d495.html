Introduction

We applied deep normative modeling to structural MRI data from two large cohorts (Ger-

man National Cohort, N ≈ 29,000 and UK Biobank, N ≈ 25,000) to characterize individual-

level brain deviations along symptom dimensions of depression, anxiety, and alcohol use. Each

brain was embedded into a 256-dimensional latent space, allowing us to quantify both the

magnitude and direction of deviation from a normative reference trained on the non/low-

symptomatic subpopulation. Deviation magnitude increased with symptom severity, and di-

rectional patterns separated mood-anxiety and alcohol-use tendencies. These deviation axes

generalized across cohorts and supported individual-level classification of symptomatic group

membership, especially at higher symptom levels. Combining deviations with polygenic risk

scores improved classification performance, particularly for depressive and anxiety measures, in-

dicating complementary contributions of imaging and genetics. Our findings demonstrate that

structural brain deviations reflect meaningful, continuous variation in affective and behavioral

symptoms.

Methods

We analyzed structural brain MRI and phenotypic data from two large population-based cohorts: the German National Cohort (NAKO) and the UK Biobank (UKB). NAKO enrolled over 205,000 participants aged 20–69, with MRI data available for 30,861 individuals. After excluding participants with missing questionnaire data (PHQ-9, GAD-7, AUDIT-C), diagnostic information, or key confounders (age, sex, scan site), as well as individuals with stroke or Parkinson’s disease, the final NAKO sample comprised 15,914 healthy controls (HCs) and 13,443 symptomatic participants. UKB was used as a large external validation cohort. From 49,279 individuals with T1-weighted MRI, participants with neurodegenerative diseases or missing questionnaire or confounder data were excluded, yielding 24,838 samples. Comparable HC and symptomatic definitions were applied across cohorts, noting that questionnaire assessments in UKB were temporally more distant from imaging than in NAKO.

Structural T1-weighted MRI scans were processed to derive grey matter volume (GMV) maps. NAKO images were acquired using 3D MPRAGE at 3T and processed with CAT12, including bias correction, segmentation, normalization to MNI space, modulation, and resampling. UKB images were processed using FSL FAST and registered to the same template to ensure comparability. All GMV images were downsampled to an 80×80×80 voxel grid and standardized.

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the PHQ-9, anxiety symptoms using the GAD-7, and alcohol use using the AUDIT-C. Established cutoffs were applied to define symptomatic strata, while retaining continuous scores for dimensional analyses.

To derive compact representations of brain structure, we applied momentum contrast (MoCo) self-supervised learning to GMV images, yielding 256-dimensional embeddings. MoCo leverages contrastive learning with a momentum encoder and a large dynamic memory bank to efficiently learn augmentation-invariant features. Embeddings were linearly deconfounded for age, age², sex, scan site, and total intracranial volume. These embeddings served as input to a normative model implemented as a fully connected autoencoder trained exclusively on HC data. Individual deviations were quantified as reconstruction errors, reflecting departures from normative brain structure.

Because the autoencoder partially reintroduced confounding, deviation vectors underwent a second residualization step, which effectively removed associations with demographic variables. To quantify group-level abnormalities, we computed Mahalanobis distances of individual deviation vectors from the healthy reference distribution and performed a distributional shift analysis comparing symptomatic groups against HCs, emphasizing deviations exceeding normative variability.

Statistical inference relied on regression models adjusted for residual confounders with robust standard errors and false discovery rate control. Beyond magnitude-based deviations, we conducted directional analyses using cosine similarity in the full 256-dimensional deviation space to assess within- and between-group similarity patterns. Group-level geometry was visualized via PCA projections and bootstrapped geometric medians.

To interpret deviations neuroanatomically, we linked embedding dimensions to regional GMV measures using elastic net regression and combined these mappings with symptom–embedding correlations to generate exploratory regional relevance maps. Finally, in UKB, polygenic risk scores for depression, anxiety, and alcohol use were computed and related to brain deviations, and multimodal classification models quantified the added value of brain deviations beyond demographics and genetic risk.

Results

Results Summary

Across both cohorts, normative deviation shift—the proportion of symptomatic deviations not explained by healthy-control variability—increased systematically with symptom severity. Groups defined by higher PHQ-9, GAD-7, and AUDIT-C thresholds exhibited progressively larger shifts, indicating increasing departures from normative brain structure. These effects remained significant after adjustment for age, sex, and nonlinear interactions.

Directional deviation patterns. Analysis of the direction of 256-dimensional deviation vectors revealed a clear dissociation between mood–anxiety symptoms and alcohol-related symptoms. In the NAKO cohort, mood–anxiety groups showed higher within-group cosine similarity than similarity with alcohol-related groups, indicating shared deviation directions distinct from those associated with alcohol use. In contrast, depressive and anxiety symptom groups were not separable: within-group similarity was lower than cross-group similarity, suggesting overlapping rather than distinct structural deviation patterns.

Comorbidity and severity gradients. Alcohol-related symptom groups occupied directions largely orthogonal to mood–anxiety deviations and showed graded displacement with increasing AUDIT-C severity, consistent with an exposure–response relationship. Mood–anxiety groups exhibited severity-dependent displacement from the healthy reference, with low symptom levels close to normative variability and higher severity showing stronger deviations. Individuals with comorbid mood–anxiety and alcohol symptoms clustered in an intermediate region, consistent with additive or mixed deviation profiles.

External validation. These patterns replicated in the UK Biobank. Severity-dependent deviation shifts were preserved, mood–anxiety deviations again showed greater internal similarity than similarity with alcohol-related deviations, and depression and anxiety remained largely overlapping. Absolute deviation magnitudes differed between cohorts, but the relative structure of group differences was consistent.

Neuroanatomical relevance. Symptom-associated deviation dimensions were dominated by cerebellar contributions across all domains. Alcohol-related symptoms showed the strongest cerebellar associations, with additional effects in occipital regions. These patterns differed markedly from direct region–symptom correlations, indicating that normative deviations capture information beyond standard volumetric analyses.

Genetic associations and prediction. Polygenic risk scores for major depression and problematic alcohol use were significantly associated with brain deviation dimensions, whereas anxiety-related genetic risk was not. No interaction effects between genetic risk and brain deviations on symptom severity were observed. In classification analyses, models combining normative brain deviations with genetic risk consistently outperformed models based on demographics or genetics alone, particularly for depressive and alcohol-related symptoms, with performance increasing alongside symptom severity.

Conclusion

We present a deep normative modeling framework that captures both the magnitude and direction of brain-structural deviations relative to a large reference population. Across two independent cohorts, depressive and anxiety symptoms showed directionally similar deviations that increased with symptom severity and were largely distinct from alcohol-related deviations. This replication supports the robustness of the approach and aligns with dimensional models of psychopathology, where symptom severity reflects graded departures from normative brain structure rather than discrete disease categories.

The strong directional overlap between MDD- and GAD-related deviations is consistent with their known clinical and biological overlap, including shared genetic risk, whereas alcohol-related symptoms exhibited a largely distinct deviation profile. Individuals with elevated alcohol use and concurrent affective symptoms showed intermediate deviation patterns, suggesting additive or mixed neurobiological effects. This separation between internalizing (depression/anxiety) and alcohol-related symptom profiles is consistent with transdiagnostic frameworks such as HiTOP, although some alcohol-related deviations may reflect direct neurotoxic effects rather than liability.

Brain-structural deviations were modestly associated with polygenic risk for depression and problematic alcohol use, supporting their biological relevance, but effect sizes were small and distributed. Combining brain deviations with genetic risk improved classification performance over demographics alone, particularly for depression, though predictive accuracy remained limited—especially for mild symptoms—highlighting the difficulty of individual-level prediction in population-based samples.

Mapping deviations back to brain regions revealed strong cerebellar associations for alcohol-related symptoms, consistent with prior work, while depression- and anxiety-related effects were weaker and spatially diffuse. These patterns differed markedly from direct region–symptom correlations, indicating that the model captures multivariate and non-linear effects beyond traditional volumetric analyses.

Methodologically, the framework advances normative modeling by learning representations directly from MRI data and enabling multivariate, directional comparisons of deviations across conditions. Limitations include cohort-specific differences in imaging and assessment timing, selection bias toward milder cases, restriction to structural MRI, and the observational nature of the analyses.

In summary, deep normative modeling reveals reproducible, dimensional, and partially transdiagnostic brain-structural deviation patterns across depression, anxiety, and alcohol-related symptoms, providing a scalable foundation for studying psychiatric heterogeneity and future personalized applications.
