Introduction

Brain function arises from the coordinated reconfiguration of intrinsic activity,1 forming coherent ensembles of neural activity, i.e., functional sources.2 These are typically estimated as intrinsic networks (INs), whose interactions reflect information flow that supports cognition. However, the mechanisms by which such information flow are coordinated, its governing structure or modes of fluctuation, remain insufficiently characterized. Here, we address this gap by introducing a data-driven generative framework, Intrinsic Network Flow (INF), which formalizes common temporal coordination structures of brain activity that are shared across individuals.

Methods

The INF framework centers on group-level temporal coordination structures, termed INF modes, each encoding a distinct information flow across networks. As illustrated in Figure 1, the INF framework operates in four steps. In STEP 1-2, the common structures (i.e., INF modes) are estimated. In STEP 1, coherent networks (i.e., INs) are identified from preprocessed whole-brain fMRI data via group independent component analysis (GICA).3 In STEP 2, we model the temporal transitions of these INs (i.e., ICA components) using a linear dynamic system. By decomposing the system’s transition operator, we isolate INF modes as complex-conjugate eigenvectors. That is, each INF mode is described as weighted complex vectors over INs, where the magnitude of each component reflects IN participation and the phase encodes the relative temporal ordering. Therefore, INF operates as a dynamical extension of ICA, transforming static ICA components into temporally ordered coordination patterns. In STEP 3-4, we capture individual variability in brain dynamics by modeling the subject-specific expression of these common structures. To this end, INF modes are projected onto subject-specific spatial maps to obtain voxel-level expressions (STEP 3), and their temporal evolution (amplitude and phase progression) is optimized to predict individual BOLD dynamics (STEP 4).4 The resulting voxel-level expressions and temporal variables together constitute the spatiotemporal fingerprints of INF modes.

Results

To test the validity, INF modes were extracted from a training sample (n=105, F=55, mean age=28.48, SD=3.78, genetically unrelated, randomly drawn from the HCP S1200 resting-state fMRI dataset; MSMAll registered; ICA-FIX denoised). Predictive performance of future blood oxygenation level dependent (BOLD) signals was then assessed in an independent test sample (n=105, F=59, mean age=29.02, SD=3.49, also genetically unrelated). Across all dimensions (number of extracted INs), INF predictions significantly outperformed first-order autoregressive null model (AR(1); all p<1e−15; Figure 2a). Notably, they also achieved higher accuracy than subject-specific structures (i.e., INF modes extracted separately for each test subject) in most dimensions (specifically, 10 to 80 components; all p<0.001), even though the common structure was inferred independently of the test sample. These findings were replicated in an independent sample (n=210, randomly drawn from the HCP), demonstrating that INF does not arbitrarily mix ICA components, but instead organizes established INs into reproducible, temporally ordered ensembles. Specifically, the principal flow, the mode with the largest amplitude, showed a coordinated progression from sensorimotor (unimodal) to higher-order components (e.g., default mode) (Figure 2b), extending the static sensorimotor-association hierarchy into a dynamic lead–lag structure. Consistent with this, the resulting phase map (Figure 2c) aligned strongly with the primary cortical gradients (r=0.88 and 0.63; Figure 2d, e)5 as well as subcortical and cerebellar gradients (r > 0.7, except striatum: r=0.63; e.g., Figure 2f, g). Remarkably, the phase map topology (Figure 2c) mirrors the joint embedding of two cortical gradients (ref. 5), situating widely used static gradient representations as emergent properties of coordinated network transitions. Beyond this dominant flow, subsequent INF modes recapitulated other canonical functional architectures, including the triple network system and bilaterally lateralized dynamics. Importantly, several modes combined INs in non-trivial ways, linking networks typically treated as independent or weakly coupled in static analyses. Crucially, individual variations in the spatiotemporal fingerprints (including such non-trivial modes) were significantly associated with general cognitive ability (FDR<0.05, n=951, all available subjects in HCP REST1 dataset after quality control). This suggests that INF captures cognitively critical dynamic configurations often overlooked by component spatial maps or zero-lag functional connectivity alone.

Conclusion

The INF framework provides a principled approach to estimating common temporal coordination structures and linking them to observable individual brain dynamics. These coordination structures generalize across individuals, unify multiple established features of large-scale functional organization, and capture meaningful cognitive variability. Together, our findings highlight temporal coordination as a key organizing principle of brain function.
