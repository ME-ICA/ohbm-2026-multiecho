Introduction

The ontogeny of human emotion represents a profound shift in neurobiological organization, moving from the body-bound responses of early childhood to the conceptually driven affective states of adulthood. Emerging research reveals that emotion generation and regulation are not the products of isolated modules but emerge from the dynamic integration of whole-brain distributed networks. In children, emotional generation is heavily anchored in subcortical and limbic circuitry, where neural patterns for distinct emotions exhibit high similarity1. As development proceeds, these "visceral" representations give way to "conceptual" ones. In adults, the burden of emotional representation shifts toward trans-modal cortical regions, particularly the ventromedial prefrontal cortex and the default mode network (DMN).2. 

Just as the generation of emotion becomes more cortically distributed, the systems responsible for its regulation undergo a distinct topographical reorganization. The simplistic model of a linear increase in prefrontal gyrus has been refined to describe a specific Ventral-to-Dorsal Shift in regulatory architecture3. In late childhood, regulation is often reactive and reliant on ventral anterior cingulate and medial prefrontal regions—areas associated with automatic affective valuation and extinction4. By contrast, successful regulation in adolescence and young adulthood increasingly recruits a dorsal executive apparatus, comprising the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, and lateral parietal regions4. 

To address this, we leveraged a large-scale fMRI dataset to delineate the neural modules underlying emotion generation and regulation, and aim to map the trajectories of these modules and quantify their dynamic contributions to affective processing from childhood through late adolescence.

Methods

We analyzed data from Chinese Child Brian Development (CCBD), which includes 6664 participants collected across 22 sites, from 6 to 19 years old (Fig.1.Step1). Participants completed an emotion regulation task comprising three trial types: 'Look Negative' (viewing negative images naturally), 'Look Neutral' (viewing neutral images), and 'Regulate Negative' (down-regulating negative affect via cognitive reappraisal). Each trial included an appraisal cue ('Just watch' or 'Do regulation') followed by a subjective affect rating.

Preprocessing of fMRI data used fMRIPrep, and then we regress regressors and applied bandpass filtering and smoothing. The first and second level analysis of fMRI data used SPM12, the contrast setting is shown in Fig1.Step1 and the contrast maps are binarized as group masks. We do Principal Component Analysis (PCA) of the voxels in the mask of each contrast map and select the optimal number of Principal Components (PCs). Spatial maps were generated by assigning each voxel to the PC with the highest positive loading, and individual PC activation were calculated (Fig.2.Step2).

To quantify the dynamic contribution of these neural components to behavior, we employed a sliding window approach (window size = 2 years, step size = 0.25 years) across the whole age span of sample. Within each sliding window, multiple regression models were constructed to predict task performance (subjective rating scores) by activation z-scores all corresponding selected PCs. The contribution of each PC was quantified by the product of its variance explained (R2) and their respective normalized regression coefficient, revealing age-specific drivers of emotional processing (Fig.2.Step3).

Results

Behavioral analyses confirmed the task's validity, showing that cognitive reappraisal significantly reduced subjective negative affect compared to the 'Look Negative' condition (p = 0.039), albeit with increased reaction times reflecting higher cognitive demand (p < 0.001). For emotion generation, the PCA analysis revealed four distinct functional modules. A Limbic-Salience module (Generation PC1), including bilateral temporal poles, insula and amygdala provided a stable positive contribution to emotional reactivity across all ages. In contrast, higher-order cortical components showed developmental specificity, a Frontal Control module (Generation PC2) exerted negative regulatory effects during mid-childhood and adolescence, while a Left Temporal module (Generation PC3) and a Right FrontoTemporal module (Generation PC4) emerged as positive drivers during distinct adolescent windows, reflecting the growing integration of semantic and executive systems in generating affect (Fig.2 top panel).

The neural architecture of emotion regulation exhibited a more dramatic reorganization. In early childhood (7.5–9 years), successful regulation relied exclusively on a Left Default module (Regulation PC1). However, this early strategy faded with age. Instead, a Visual-Attentional module (Regulation PC3), centered on the right lateral occipital cortex and angular gyrus, maintained a robust, positive contribution to regulation success throughout nearly the entire developmental spectrum (6-19 years). Conversely, a Semantic module (Regulation PC2), anchored in the left temporal pole and extending to the angular gyrus, frequently exerted a significant negative impact on regulation efficacy across multiple developmental phases (Fig.2 bottom panel), suggesting that excessive engagement of self-referential or semantic processing may hinder effective reappraisal in youth.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive spatiotemporal map of the neural architecture supporting emotion processing in youth. We demonstrate that while emotion generation relies on a specific, bilaterally early neural function module, including insula and amygdala, but undergoes a significant age-specific reorganization of prefrontal and semantic inputs. For emotion regulation, visual-attentional and semantic system alternating dominant. These findings challenge linear developmental models, revealing instead that the adolescent brain dynamically reconfigures distinct neural modules to meet affective demands. By delineating when specific brain networks drive behavior, our results offer precise targets for understanding normative emotional development and potential vulnerabilities in youth.
