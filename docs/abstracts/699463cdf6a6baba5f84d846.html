Introduction

Post-stroke cognitive impairment (PSCI) affects more than half of stroke survivors and represents a major determinant of long-term disability, loss of independence, and reduced quality of life.1 Despite its clinical importance, PSCI remains difficult to predict and mechanistically explain from conventional lesion characteristics such as infarct volume or focal lesion location.2 This limitation likely reflects the distributed network organization of cognition, whereby focal injury may cause impairment by disrupting large-scale structural and functional brain circuits rather than isolated cortical modules. Lesion network mapping (LNM) offers a principled framework to test this hypothesis by embedding focal lesions within normative connectomes to quantify their network-level impact.3,4 While prior LNM studies have yielded mechanistically meaningful insights, their added value beyond classical lesion-symptom mapping remains debated, and evidence from large, heterogeneous stroke populations is scarce.

Methods

We conducted a large multi-cohort lesion-symptom mapping study using harmonized imaging and cognitive data from 12 international stroke cohorts across Asia and Europe (n = 2,950), constituting the largest LNM study to date (Figure 1a).5 Neuropsychological assessments performed within one-year post-stroke were normalized using cohort-specific controls and harmonized into six cognitive domains. Lesions were segmented on clinical neuroimaging and registered to standard space (Figure 1c). Domain impairment was defined as <5th percentile of cohort norms; impairment in ≥1 domain indicated PSCI. Each infarct was conceptualized as a network perturbation defined by its structural disconnectivity (structural lesion network mapping, sLNM; Figure 1d), estimated using a high-resolution normative tractography atlas (dTOR985; https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.c.6844890), and its functional connectivity (functional lesion network mapping, fLNM; Figure 1e), derived from resting-state fMRI data from 1,000 healthy adults (GSP1000; https://doi.org/10.7910/DVN/ILXIKS). We tested voxelwise associations between cognitive impairment and (i) lesion presence, (ii) sLNM, and (iii) fLNM, adjusting for infarct volume. Functional effects were further summarized within canonical intrinsic functional networks (Yeo17). LNM procedures were implemented in a dedicated open-source Python package developed for this study, providing end-to-end lesion network mapping functionality (https://github.com/m-petersen/lacuna).

Results

Conventional voxel-based lesion-symptom mapping yielded predominantly focal and partially overlapping association patterns across cognitive domains, implicating a common set of subcortical and perisylvian regions with additional domain-specific clusters (Figure 2, left column). In contrast, sLNM and fLNM revealed spatially distributed, domain-specific network substrates that extended beyond focal lesion sites. Structural disconnection patterns implicated anatomically constrained association, projection, and commissural pathways in a domain-dependent manner, highlighting the role of white-matter network integrity (Figure 2, middle column). Functional lesion networks mapped onto canonical intrinsic functional networks and revealed dissociable circuit-level profiles across domains (Figure 2, right column). Attention and executive dysfunction involved salience and frontoparietal control networks; processing speed deficits linked to visual and attention networks with prominent subcortical contributions; language impairment mapped to left-lateralized frontoparietal and default-mode circuits spanning classical language regions; verbal memory deficits involved visual circuits and medial temporal default-mode components; and visuospatial impairment localized to dorsal visual-attention networks. Global PSCI showed the strongest and most consistent association with disruption of the salience/ventral attention network.

Conclusion

Across a large and diverse stroke population, lesion network mapping identified domain-specific circuit substrates of PSCI that are not captured by focal lesion models alone. Effects of strategic lesion location, structural disconnectivity and functional network involvement dissociate in systematic and neurobiologically interpretable ways, reflecting complementary constraints of vascular anatomy, white-matter architecture and large-scale functional coupling. These findings support a network-based account of PSCI and suggest that the network embedding of a lesion constitutes a mechanistically informative and potentially actionable marker for prediction, stratification, and future network-targeted interventions.
