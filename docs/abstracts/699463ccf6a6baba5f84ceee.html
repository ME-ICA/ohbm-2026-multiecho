Introduction

Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and complex PTSD (CPTSD) share the core symptom clusters of re-experiencing the traumatic event, avoiding situations or reminders associated with it, and increased physiological arousal. CPTSD additionally encompasses disturbances in self-organization, characterized by difficulties in emotion regulation, self-concept, and interpersonal functioning1. Despite their diagnostic differentiation in the International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-111, neurobiological distinctions between the two disorders remain unclear. Functional MRI studies indicate that, compared with PTSD, individuals with CPTSD exhibit heightened activation of the insula and amygdala during threat processing and reduced functional connectivity between the posterior cingulate cortex and subcortical and midline regions2,3. However, structural differences specific to CPTSD have rarely been examined. This pilot study applied multimodal MRI to identify gray- and white-matter signatures distinguishing PTSD from CPTSD.

Methods

Twenty-nine participants who met ICD-11 diagnostic criteria (10 with PTSD and 19 with CPTSD), as diagnosed by a psychiatrist or clinical psychologist, underwent MRI scanning on a 3T Siemens Prisma system. T1-weighted images were acquired using an MP2RAGE sequence (TR = 4000ms, TE = 2.98ms, 0.8mm³ isotropic). Diffusion-weighted images (DWI) were acquired using a multishell protocol (b0 = 8, b = 1000 s/mm², 50 directions, b = 3000 s/mm², 60 directions, reverse phase-encoded b0 = 2, 2 mm isotropic voxels). Surface-based morphometry examined thickness and surface area across the entire cortex and was performed using FreeSurfer v8.1.04 (fsaverage template, 163,842 vertices per hemisphere) using cluster-wise permutation testing (10,000 iterations; cluster-forming threshold p < 0.01, corrected p < 0.05). DWI preprocessing was completed in MRtriX3 and included denoising, Gibbs ringing removal, motion and eddy-current correction, and bias-field correction5. Fiber orientation distributions were estimated using constrained spherical deconvolution. Diffusion metrics were derived from the bi-tensor free-water model6 (extracellular free-water, free-water-corrected fractional anisotropy [FAt], mean diffusivity [MDt], axial diffusivity [ADt], radial diffusivity [RDt]), and from neurite orientation dispersion and density imaging (NODDI - intracellular volume fraction [ICVF], orientation dispersion index [ODI], isotropic volume fraction [FISO])7. TractSeg-based tractometry was conducted on 72 white-matter bundles, with 100 equidistant segments per tract and family-wise error correction (5,000 permutations, p < 0.05)8. Group differences between PTSD and CPTSD were assessed using general linear models controlling for age, age², sex, education, and total intracranial volume.

Results

The total sample ranged in age from 24 to 76 years, comprising 20 females and 9 males. Participants with CPTSD were on average 45 years old (SD = 12.8), whereas those with PTSD were on average 41 years old (SD = 10.8). Surface-based analysis revealed bilateral posterior cingulate cortex thinning in individuals with CPTSD compared to PTSD (left: 44.69 mm², 123 vertices, peak MNI: -13.0, -31.7, 38.0, pcorr = 0.012; right: 47.68 mm², 129 vertices, peak MNI: 8.7, -18.5, 39.0, pcorr = 0.007; see Figure 1). No significant group differences emerged in cortical surface area. Tractometry indicated higher RDt in the left inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (tract segments 76–88) in PTSD relative to CPTSD (see Figure 2), corresponding to the anterior or frontal limb of the tract. PTSD participants also exhibited elevated FISO in the left uncinate fasciculus (tract segments 37–46), localized to the bend region encompassing the anterior temporal stem and limen insulae.

Conclusion

Given the posterior cingulate cortex’s central role in the default mode network, supporting self-referential processing, autobiographical memory, and emotion regulation, CPTSD-related cortical thinning suggests more pronounced structural alterations in self-processing and emotion-regulatory regions. PTSD, on the other hand, was characterized by greater white-matter microstructural abnormalities compared with CPTSD. Increased RDt observed in the inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus may reflect myelin disruption along visual-limbic pathways involved in emotion regulation and response inhibition. Elevated FISO observed in the uncinate fasciculus likely indicates extracellular processes such as inflammation in anterior limbic-frontal connections that underlie affective integration and threat evaluation.

This study provides preliminary evidence that PTSD and CPTSD exhibit distinct cortical and white-matter microstructural profiles. PTSD shows greater anterior diffusion abnormalities, whereas CPTSD shows posterior cortical thinning. These complementary findings support the biological validity of CPTSD and provide a foundation for future longitudinal studies investigating neurobiological trajectories and treatment response in trauma-related disorders. The study is now being extended to include a larger cohort of individuals with CPTSD and PTSD, trauma-exposed controls, and healthy controls without a trauma history.
