Introduction

The human brain undergoes significant structural changes throughout the lifespan, traditionally attributed to chronological age and related neurodevelopmental or degenerative processes1. Structural neuroimaging, particularly T1-weighted MRI, has widely documented age-related changes in Grey Matter Volume (GMV) across cortical and subcortical regions.  However, a critical limitation in many cross-sectional studies is the population bias that confounds age effects with cohort effects, differences driven by the unique environmental, social, and medical conditions experienced by groups born in different eras.

While structural changes have been studied in large datasets, most have been acquired over a short time frame2. Our unique dataset of thousands of scans, acquired across a 20-year period (since 2005) and spanning a wide age range (18-87 years), allows us to study cohorts that have experienced social and technological change in different phases of their lives. This enables us to provide a whole-brain picture of generational differences. 

This study addresses this challenge by analyzing the difference in GMV between cohorts. We divide the sample into narrow 5-year age groups, and use a Multiple Linear Regression (MLR) model to assess the unique contribution of birth year to regional GMV, while controlling for actual age at scan, total intracranial volume (TIV) and sex within a tightly controlled age window.

Our initial analysis focuses on the 30-35 year age group to provide the first evidence of how cohort membership, defined by birth year, shapes regional GMV. These findings have significant implications for interpreting neuroimaging data across the lifespan and understanding the neurobiological impact of social and environmental change.

Methods

Participants

We collected structural scans of 3072 healthy adult subjects (1245 females; 1827 males), that were scanned since the year 2005. The subjects’ age during the scans ranges from 18 to 87 years old (mean = 31.5, SD=10.1). The birth years of the subjects range from 1931 to 2007 (mean=1990.5, SD=10.9). The full distribution of age and birth years is shown in Figure 1A.

The initial analysis was done on the age group of 30-35 years old subjects (N = 604, 206 females; 398 males; mean age= 32, SD = 1.48). This group was chosen due to a wide range of birth years, and a decent amount of scans in each year, so the MLR model is the most likely to be accurate. Their birth years range from 1971 to 1995 (mean = 1987.87, SD = 5.62, see the distribution in Figure 1B). 

Some of the scans were drawn from multiple imaging studies conducted between 2018 and 2025 as part of the Strauss Neuroplasticity Brain Bank (SNBB) (Kepler et al., in prep.). The rest were collected from hardware containing the scans that were acquired during research that took place over the years. 

All scans were preprocessed using Computational Anatomy Toolbox (CAT12).

Validation Of Old Scans 

A primary assumption of this longitudinal cohort analysis is the stability of T1-weighted MRI protocols across the 20-year acquisition period (since the early 2000s). To validate this assumption and mitigate potential scanner or site effects, we performed extensive distribution matching checks across different scanning sources (sites, manufacturers, and hardware generations). We specifically compared the distributions of core anatomical measures (e.g. total GMV, TIV) and technical quality metrics (derived from CAT12 preprocessing). Crucially, scans acquired since 2015 were collected using a standardized MPRAGE protocol on the same scanner at a single institute; this consistent later cohort served as the reference standard against which all other protocols were compared and validated. Scans failing quality control criteria were either corrected or excluded from the final sample.

Grey Matter Segmentation

Grey matter volumes (GMV) and total intracranial volume (TIV) were extracted from 400 cortical regions 3 and 54 subcortical regions 4.

Statistical Analysis

To mitigate the effects of continuous age-related variance and isolate the cohort effect, subjects were initially divided into non-overlapping 5-year age groups (e.g., 30-35, 35-40 years). For this abstract, the analysis is limited to the 30–35 year age group (N=604), allowing for an initial assessment of the cohort effect while controlling for within-bin age and TIV.

Within this age group, a Multiple Linear Regression (MLR) model was constructed for each region, to assess the relationship between grey matter volume (GMV) and birth year. The model was formulated as:

GMV_ROI~ b0 + b1*(Birth Year) +b2*(TIV)+b3 *(Exact Age) +b4*C(Sex)

TIV and exact age were included as continuous covariates, and sex was included as a categorical factor.

T-values were calculated for the birth year coefficient (b1) to quantify the strength and direction of the cohort effect on GMV. Multiple comparison correction was performed across all tested ROIs using the False Discovery Rate (FDR) procedure.

Results

Analysis of the 30–35 year age group revealed significant associations between birth year and GMV in several ROIs following FDR correction (Figure 2).

GMV was significantly larger in later birth years (younger cohort), with t-values ranging from 2.79 to 5.56. The most pronounced effects were observed in the Salience and Limbic systems. A significant bilateral increase was found in the Lateral and Medial Amygdala (t_range: 3.53-5.39) and the Nucleus Accumbens (t_range: 2.79-3.33).

A significant bilateral effect was found in the Salience Network and ventral frontal regions, reflecting increased GMV in the younger cohort, including clusters in Broca's area (part of the inferior frontal gyrus). The strongest effect was seen in the Right Insula/Frontal Operculum (t=5.56), with multiple clusters showing positive effects bilaterally in the Insula (t_range: 2.98-5.56) and the Orbitofrontal Cortex (t_range: 3.13-3.70).

Positive effects were also noted across other cortical regions, including the Dorsal Attention Network (t_max=4.89), Default Mode Network (t_max=4.32), and Somatomotor Network (t_max=3.91).

Conversely, GMV was significantly smaller in later birth years (t-values ranging from -2.85 to -6.69), primarily in areas involved in motor control and visual processing. The strongest finding in the entire analysis was a decrease in GMV in the Left Globus Pallidus (t=-6.69), with a bilateral effect observed (t value in the right hemisphere=-4.83).

Smaller volumes were found in regions of the Visual Network (t_range: -2.89 to -3.23) and the Default Mode Network (dPFC mPFC, t=-2.85; DorsAttn Post, t=-2.90).

Conclusion

Our study reveals profound regional differences in grey matter volume (GMV) across a 20-year span of birth years. The younger cohort showed increased GMV in key regions of the Salience and Limbic systems, while there was a significant decrease in GMV in sub-cortical areas of the Basal Ganglia, specifically the Putamen and the Globus Pallidus. These regions are highly relevant to the established link between these circuits and emotional regulation5. The differential engagement of these systems, particularly the limbic system, may reflect an increased prevalence of stress and anxiety among younger generations.

These findings provide the first evidence of structural neuroanatomical changes between generations that are independent of chronological age at the time of the scan. This demonstrates a measurable cohort effect on brain structure, suggesting that environmental or lifestyle factors tied to the birth era are shaping the volume of systems critical for emotion, reward, and motor control.

Future work will extend this analysis to all planned age groups (35-40, 40-45, etc.) to evaluate the consistency and age-dependency of the observed cohort effects.
