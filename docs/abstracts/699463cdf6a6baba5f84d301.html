Introduction

Language organization is shaped by experience-dependent plasticity, with bilingualism representing an influential experience across the lifespan. Rather than a categorical condition, bilingualism is defined as a continuum in which individuals differ according to multiple dimensions of language acquisition, including proficiency, degree of immersion, and age of acquisition. Increasing evidence indicates that the manner in which a second language (L2) is integrated can modulate the neural architecture supporting the native language (L1), leading to more distributed and control-dependent language networks.

 

In patients with high-grade gliomas (HGG), this experience-driven plasticity interacts with tumor-induced functional reorganization, which is characterized by functional reorganization patterns in ipsilesional and contralesional areas, making interpretation of language organization more complex. Task-based functional MRI (fMRI) is used as a preoperative tool to identify language-related regions in glioma patients; however, it must be complemented by direct electrical stimulation (DES) during awake surgery, which remains the gold standard for intraoperative language mapping to identify language-essential regions.

 

The extent to which L2 immersion influences L1 language mapping with fMRI in HGG patients, and the consistency of these findings with intraoperative DES, remains poorly understood. In this study, we investigate whether different levels of L2 immersion modulate L1 activation patterns during task-based fMRI, illustrating perilesional plasticity mechanisms in both ipsilesional and contralesional hemispheres. Additionally, we assess how L2 immersion mediates the spatial concordance between fMRI activations and intraoperative DES findings. By classifying bilingual patients into high- and low-immersion groups, our aim is to determine how bilingual experience may be integrated into neurosurgical practice to determine the best mapping approach.

Methods

We included 10 Spanish-Catalan bilingual patients diagnosed with a HGG in peri-sylvian language-related areas of the left hemisphere, and because of this, underwent an awake brain surgery including a language electrical stimulation mapping (ESM). Blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) fMRI data were acquired preoperatively on a 1.5T MRI Philips Achieva scanner while subjects performed an object naming task, in a block design scan session. Data were analysed using a first-level whole-brain General Linear Model (GLM) (FEAT, part of FSL). Preprocessing steps included: motion correction, spatial smoothing (Gaussian kernel, full-width half-maximum 5 mm) and high-pass temporal filtering (90s). Activation maps were generated including motion parameters and using cluster statistics performed on all voxels above a threshold of z = 3.1, with a corrected cluster extent threshold of P < 0.05 and cluster sizes stablished in 40 voxels as minimum. 

To evaluate spatial distribution of fMRI and intraoperative language maps, we divided the perisylvian cortical areas into 6 ROIS based on our previous studies on cortical mapping 1. These ROIs included the inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), the middle frontal gyrus (MFG), the anterior temporal lobe (ATL), superior temporal gyrus (STG), middle temporal gyrus (MTG), the supramarginal gyrus (SMG), and angular gyrus (AG). For each patient, we determined which of these regions were involved based on task-based fMRI activation and intraoperative ESM.

Chi-square tests were then performed to assess whether the degree of L2 immersion (high vs. low) was associated with categorical differences in language mapping outcomes. Specifically, we employed Chi-square test to evaluate whether L2 immersion (low vs high) was associated with: (1) the presence of activation in left-hemisphere perisylvian regions on fMRI; (2) the presence of activation in right-hemisphere homologous regions on fMRI; (3) interhemispheric reorganization patterns, defined as differential activation between left and right hemispheric regions; and (4) the anatomical concordance between fMRI activations and intraoperative DES findings.

Results

The analyses revealed a significant association between the level of L2 immersion and the consistency between task-based fMRI activation and intraoperative mapping exclusively in the superior temporal gyrus (STG) (X2 (1) = 6.27, p = .01). Specifically, all patients in the high-immersion group showed no concordance between fMRI and intraoperative mapping in the STG (0/6), whereas all of the patients in the low-immersion group demonstrated such consistency (4/4). No significant associations were observed for the remaining perisylvian regions or for other fMRI-derived language mapping measures.

Conclusion

Our results indicate that the degree of L2 immersion does not substantially modify the identification of language-related regions on task-based fMRI in patients with HGG. Instead, L2 immersion significantly affects the consistency between fMRI findings and intraoperative direct electrical stimulation, particularly within the superior temporal gyrus. In highly immersed bilingual patients, reduced fMRI–DES concordance suggests greater functional dispersion of language-essential sites identified using DES, likely reflecting enhanced experience-dependent plasticity interacting with tumor-related reorganization. This may be interpreted considering that L2 immersion may also influence the functional role of perisylvian regions during language processing by promoting increased reliance on domain-general processes, such as cognitive control and attentional regulation. This functional diversification could contribute to the mismatch between fMRI activations of language-related areas and language-essential sites identified intraoperatively. Accordingly, fMRI should be considered a complementary tool for preoperative language mapping in highly immersed bilingual patients, with intraoperative stimulation remaining critical for accurately identifying indispensable language regions.

Future studies should further explore how immersion-related plasticity shapes the multimodal contributions of language areas (e.g. their integration with cognitive control networks), to improve the interpretation of non-invasive mapping techniques and optimize neurosurgical planning in bilingual patients.
