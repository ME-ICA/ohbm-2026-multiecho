Introduction

The frontoparietal network (FPN) is associated with executive control processes such as working memory and attention. While fMRI studies have repeatedly implicated the FPN in cognitive control, their low temporal resolution limits direct characterization of meso-scale neural dynamics. Here we use electrocorticography (ECoG) to characterize meso-scale electrophysiological connectivity within the FPN and its interactions with other brain regions during rest and cognitive tasks designed to probe executive control. 

Previous research using ECoG has linked broadband high-gamma activity (typically 70–200 Hz) to neural processing1. In our previous work, we showed that high-gamma activity in the frontal cortex spatially overlaps regions associated with cognitive control and is distinguishable from adjacent functional networks2. Coupling between low-frequency oscillations (theta/alpha/beta) and high-gamma activity has also been shown to play a key role in coordinating neural activity related to control processes such as attention3. Building on this evidence, we studied meso-scale connectivity patterns within the FPN and its coupling with adjacent networks. We collected ECoG data from the frontal cortex of 13 patients undergoing awake surgery for tumor resection while performing tasks with increasing cognitive demands and during rest. Functional connectivity was quantified using phase-amplitude coupling (PAC), a measure previously linked to complex cognitive processing including cognitive control3, focusing on coupling between beta-band phase (12–30 Hz) and high-gamma amplitude. Specifically, we addressed three research questions: (1) whether PAC-based connectivity within the FPN show a spatial gradient; (2) whether ECoG connectivity strength is correlated across rest and tasks with increased demand; (3) whether connectivity patterns reveal interactions between healthy and tumor-infiltrated tissue, consistent with evidence that gliomas interact with adjacent healthy regions.

Methods

13 glioma patients undergoing awake craniotomy at the Department of Neurosurgery at Cambridge University Addenbrooke's Hospital (Cambridge, UK)2 were included in the study. During surgery, ECoG electrodes were placed on the exposed cortical surface within the craniotomy, primarily covering frontal regions (Fig. 1a). During surgery while ECoG was recorded, patients completed three task conditions: rest (no task), a simple counting task (Easy), and a more demanding number–letter switch counting task (Hard). The tasks with increased demand were designed to recruit PFN regions. Most of the electrodes were placed on healthy-appearing tissue and some on tumor-infiltrated tissue, based on imaging and intraoperative measures and validated by the neurosurgeon.

For both electrode types, contact-to-contact spacing was 10 mm from the center of contact. Contact disks were 5 mm and 3 mm in diameter for the straight and T-shaped electrodes, respectively. Data were recorded using a 32-channel amplifier (Medtronic Xomed, Jacksonville, FS, USA) sampled at 10 KHz. 

Offline data analysis was conducted using EEGLAB (v13.6.5 b) along with custom MATLAB scripts (pre-processing using 2018a and subsequent analysis using 2024b). The data were downsampled to 2 kHz and grand-average re-referencing. Notch filter at 50 Hz and its harmonics was used to remove line noise; Additional notch filter at 79 Hz and its harmonics was used to remove noise observed in the data, likely due to equipment in the operating room.

To relate electrode positions to frontal-lobe network organization identified by fMRI, we used the canonical 7-network parcellation from Yeo et al. (2011)4. We assigned a network label to every electrode by identifying its nearest voxel (shortest Euclidean distance; pdist2, MATLAB R2018a) and selecting the most frequent network label among all voxels within a 5-mm radius.

To characterize functional connectivity across different brain networks during rest and task states, we used PAC, specifically phase-locking value (PLV) (Fig.1). This measure has been demonstrated as relevant to neural computations in prior studies (e.g., 5).

The PLV measure was proposed by Lachaux et al.5 and others to capture PAC within individual electrode. Here we used PLV to assess PAC between pairs of electrodes. Based on our previous findings2  showing substantial power modulations during task performance using the same dataset, we specifically focused on PAC between beta-phase and high gamma amplitude. Initially, the time-series (X[n], sampled at times tn for n = 1,…,N) was bandpass filtered to isolate the two relevant frequency bands and a Hilbert transform was then used to extract instantaneous amplitude and phase. In our context, the two phases of interest are the phase of the low-frequency (beta), ∅LF[n], and the phase of the high-frequency amplitude (high gamma), ∅A_HF[n] (Fig. 1b). A second Hilbert transform is then applied on the time series of the high-frequency amplitude, from which the complex variable is produced:

(1)                               ZA_HF[n]=AA_HF*exp(i∅A_HF)

that indicates the amplitude (AA_HF) and phase (∅A_HF) of the high frequency amplitude. The PLV between the phase of the low frequency and the phase of the amplitude of the high frequency (HG) is then calculated: 

(2)                              PLV=|∑Nn=1exp(i∗(∅LF[n]-∅A_HF[n]))/N|

When N is the number of samples. For PLV, a value of 1 indicates perfect phase-locking (in this context high PAC), and a value of 0 indicates no locking (no PAC).

To determine the statistical significance of the PLV, we employed a permutation test using a surrogate data approach. We introduced a deliberate random time delay (jitter) between the phase of the low frequency (∅LF[n]) and the phase of the high frequency (∅A_HF[n]) components, allowing the calculation of a surrogate complex variable, PLVs. This was repeated 1000 times. We then determined the p-value of the original PLV by calculating the percentage of surrogate data values that were larger than the original PLV.

Results

The analysis of connectivity in healthy-appearing cortex was conducted on data from 79 electrodes and 241 electrode pairs recorded simultaneously. We observed similar levels of functional connectivity within the FPN across rest and task states, with 32–47% of pairs showing significant PAC across these states. To further characterize functional connectivity within the FPN, we examined how connectivity depends on the two possible configurations of each electrode pair, that is, which electrode was assigned as the low- or high-frequency site for computing PAC. We computed connectivity difference between the configuration in which the low-frequency electrode was anterior to the high-frequency electrode and the reverse configuration. Overall connectivity differences between the two configurations were larger than 0 (one-sample t-test, p<0.002 t>2.84 for rest and task states), indicating that connectivity varies between the configurations. Building on previous theoretical accounts and experimental evidence for activity gradients along the frontal cortex, we further tested whether these configuration-dependent differences reflect a spatial gradient. Correlation analysis revealed an anterior-posterior gradient of connectivity difference (Fig. 2a), with larger connectivity when the low-frequency electrode was positioned anterior to the high-frequency electrode. This effect was statistically significant for the rest and Hard task conditions (Mixed-effects linear regression, p<0.001 and p=0.01, respectively; for the Easy task: p=0.1).

We next examined how connectivity patterns relate across rest and task states, testing whether electrode pairs that show stronger PAC at rest maintain their relative strength during task performance. PAC values were compared pairwise across conditions (Rest vs. Easy, Rest vs. Hard, Easy vs. Hard) (Fig. 2b). For electrode pairs within the FPN, PAC was positively correlated between rest and task states and across the two tasks (Rest vs. Easy: r = 0.50, p < 0.001; Rest vs. Hard: r = 0.43, p < 0.001; Easy vs. Hard: r = 0.32, p = 0.001; mixed linear model; all correlations survived correction for multiple comparisons). In contrast, electrode pairs outside the FPN showed no such significant cross-state correlations (all r < 0.15, p > 0.35). These findings indicate that meso-scale connectivity architecture is preserved across rest and task states, in our data specifically within the FPN, potentially because of the tasks that were used. 

Finally, we examined PAC in electrode pairs involving tumor-infiltrated cortex. PAC strength within tumor-infiltrated regions (18 electrode pairs) was comparable to that in healthy-appearing cortex, with a substantial proportion of the pairs showing significant connectivity (35% and 30% on average across states for healthy and tumor-infiltrated cortex, respectively). PAC strength between mixed (healthy–tumor, 64 electrode pairs) pairs was reduced but still showed substantial connectivity (15% on average across states). These results indicate partial interactions between healthy and tumor-infiltrated regions.

Conclusion

Our findings provide novel evidence that meso-scale functional connectivity within the human FPN can be captured using PAC between beta phase and high-gamma amplitude. Despite differences in task demands, connectivity strength within the FPN remained stable, with correlated strength across states. Several theoretical configurations for how connectivity patterns relate across states are possible (Fig. 2c). These include: similar connections and similar connectivity strength, similar connections and modulated connectivity strength (stronger/weaker), different connections with overall similar connectivity strength, and different connections with modulated connectivity strength. Our findings suggest that the FPN shows similar connections, although the results cannot tease apart the two possibilities of preserved or modulated connectivity strengths. While pairs within the FPN showed similar cross-state patterns, we did not observe this pattern for pairs located outside the FPN. In our data, this stability in meso-scale connectivity was specific to the FPN, potentially because of the tasks with increased demand that were used, tailored to probe the FPN.

We further revealed an anterior–posterior gradient of connectivity within the FPN, such that PAC was stronger when the low-frequency electrode was positioned anterior to the high-frequency electrode. Because PAC is thought to reflect modulation of high-frequency activity by low-frequency signals, these results suggest an organization of connectivity within the FPN, with more anterior regions potentially modulating posterior regions.

Finally, PAC within tumor-infiltrated cortex was comparable to that of healthy regions. Coupling in mixed healthy-tumor pairs, although reduced compared to healthy cortex, suggest partial interactions between healthy and tumor-infiltrated regions, further expanding previous evidence for interactions between the tumor and its surroundings.

Overall, these findings highlight the importance of characterizing meso-scale functional connectivity to better understand the functional organization regions associated with executive control. At the same time, these findings may carry implications not only for addressing fundamental questions in neuroscience but also for advancing surgical mapping tools and the consideration of network organization for clinical decision-making.
