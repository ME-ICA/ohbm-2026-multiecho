Introduction

This study applied the Network Control Theory (NCT) framework to analyze the fMRI data of novice and experienced meditators during the resting state. This is motivated by evidence showing that dynamic reconfiguration of brain networks is crucial for optimizing energy demands [1]. By examining the properties of connectivity patterns, we aimed to explore how meditation shapes resting-state connectivity to efficiently enhance the ability to shift and maintain specific states of attention and awareness. We hypothesized that functional connectivity patterns of long-term meditators exhibit higher controllability properties, particularly in areas associated with attentional control and self-monitoring, reflecting a refined capacity for optimized state modulation. Our findings aim to contribute to a growing understanding of how the brain network dynamics is optimized by meditation practice and, more broadly, the crucial role of advanced engineering techniques, such as network control theory, to study biologically complex systems, such as the human brain.

Methods

Our study included expert meditators (twelve Theravada Buddhist monks, males, mean age 37.9±9.4 yrs, average meditation experience 16.4 years) and novice meditators (ten individuals, males, mean age 35.7±4.0 yrs) who completed a 10-day training program. All participants provided written informed consent approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Chieti-Pescara.

The experiment consisted of nine 3-minute non-meditative resting-state blocks. Images were acquired on a 1.5T Siemens Magnetom Vision Scanner using T2*-weighted EPI sequence (TR=4.087s, 28 slices, voxel size 4×4×4mm³, 860 volumes). A high-resolution T1-weighted image was acquired with 3D-MPRAGE sequence.

Preprocessing used Brain Voyager QX 1.7. The first five scans were discarded. Functional volumes were co-registered with structural images. Temporal filtering included linear and non-linear trend removal. Anatomical images were segmented using FSL FAST. Signals were cleaned by regressing out white matter, CSF, and movement parameters, filtered (0.009–0.08 Hz), and scrubbed (FD>0.5)

We extracted signals from the Schaefer parcellation (300 areas, 7 networks) \cite{Schaefer2018}. Functional connectivity was computed via pairwise correlation, with diagonal set to zero and normalized.

We used NCT to analyze resting-state networks. Brain activity is approximated by: a linear-time invariant model (Fig. 1 eq. 1) 

In the model we defined x(t) as the state of N nodes, A is the N×N connectivity matrix, B_k defines control nodes, and u(t)_k is the control signal.

From these equation, we can obtain the Gramian matrix W (Fig. 1 eq. 2) and we can extract two controllability metrics: average controllability and modal controllability.

Average controllability measures ability to reach easy-to-reach states via the Gramian matrix trace, while modal controllability indicates ability to reach difficult states and can be obtained using  eigendecomposition (Fig. 1 eq. 3). We set the B_k as identity matrix (N×N). Statistical significance was assessed using independent samples t-test with 1000 permutations.

Results

Average controllability was higher in experts versus novices in Visual Network (t=2.63, p=0.008), Control Network (t=3.93, p=0.001), Default Mode Network (t=2.74, p=0.006), and Dorsal Attention Network (t=2.56, p=0.01) as shown in Figure 2A. Visual Network showed positive correlation with expertise in experts (r=0.67, p<0.01)

Figure 2B shows that novices had a higher modal controllability than experts in Visual Network (t=-2.97, p=0.003), Control Network (t=-4.57, p<0.001), Default Mode Network (t=-3.565, p<0.001), and Dorsal Attention Network (t=-3.11, p=0.002)

Conclusion

We applied control theory to brain functional networks, revealing energetic optimization from long-term meditation through controllability metrics.

Average controllability measures network efficiency in reaching nearby states. High values indicate less energy needed to steer networks to target states [2]. Networks trained through meditation—Dorsal Attention, Default Mode, Visual, and Control Networks—showed significantly higher average controllability, confirming theories that long-term practice influences these networks [3] and optimizes sustained attention and emotion regulation.

Modal controllability identifies nodes driving systems to difficult-to-reach states and is inversely proportional to cognitive effort. Experts showed lower modal controllability in meditation-related networks, indicating optimized movement to difficult states with fewer resources. This aligns with theories showing long-term meditation provides effortless automation in reaching high-awareness states [4, 5].

Network control theory effectively identifies brain patterns supporting system optimization, demonstrating how controllability metrics reveal networks that optimize energetic demands after long-term meditation.
