Introduction

For the last decade, the topic of reproducibility has been at the forefront of neuroscience and psychology research.1 One way to address the reproducibility crisis is to conduct power analyses to promote more robust study planning. This requires knowledge about the magnitude of effect sizes expected in the study. For neuroimaging studies, it is particularly difficult to estimate the expected effect sizes due to the uncertainty that arises from examining thousands of brain variables simultaneously. Further, effect size reporting in neuroimaging literature often focuses on the location of maximally significant effects, less frequently includes actual effect size magnitudes, and due to complications involving the high dimensionality of the data, rarely provides estimates of uncertainty that are critical for accurately interpreting effect sizes. In particular, standard “mass univariate” procedures for estimating effects across multiple brain areas simultaneously give an inflated picture of how large effect sizes are. Therefore, relying on point estimates from the literature without accounting for uncertainty can be misleading. This leads to a feedback loop that reinforces inflated expectations and makes it even harder to accurately estimate effect sizes in neuroimaging.

Here, we introduce a method to correct this inflation bias and perform an unprecedented analysis of 63 studies in seven large datasets (n = 100–40,000; 52,979 total participants) to establish effect size benchmarks in functional neuroimaging. To our knowledge, this represents the first comprehensive effort to estimate the “true” distribution of effect sizes across the brain for common fMRI study designs, establishing empirical foundations for more robust evidence-based study planning in the field.

Methods

Datasets

We used seven large, publicly available datasets: the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development study (ABCD), the Human Connectome Project (HCP), the Philadelphia Neurodevelopmental Cohort (PNC), the Healthy Brain Network (HBN), HCP Early Psychosis, the Southwest University Longitudinal Imaging Multimodal (SLIM) dataset, and the UK Biobank (UKB). Sample sizes vary across datasets from 100 to 40,000, with 52,979 total participants.

Preprocessing

Datasets were preprocessed using the Yale Magnetic Resonance Research Center in-house pipeline,2 except HCP activation maps, SLIM connectivity, and UKB connectivity, for which existing processed data were obtained from repositories. The Yale pipeline combined BioImage Suite, AFNI, FSL, and SPM software, including motion correction, spatial smoothing, noise covariate regression (including global signal), temporal filtering, and normalization to MNI space. 

Mass univariate effect size estimation (without correction for cross-brain inflation)

We performed 63 effect size analyses to obtain effect size estimates for ‘typical’ fMRI study designs. Depending on the available data in each dataset, we conducted either brain-behavior correlations, one-sample t-tests, or two-sample t-tests, for both functional connectivity and task-based activation maps. We included network-level and multivariate versions of all analyses, and repeated analyses with five different motion deconfounding strategies (statistical control, full residualization, two FD thresholds, and no correction). We converted the resulting statistical maps to effect size maps with both Cohen’s d and R2. For all effect size maps, we computed simultaneous confidence intervals. Then, we performed meta-analyses across study categories (task connectivity, task activation, psychological, physical). We also created an interactive web application to facilitate the exploration of these results (neuroprismlab.shinyapps.io/braineffex). 

Cross-brain effect size estimation (correcting for cross-brain inflation)

We developed a principled approach to estimate the “true” population effect size distributions across the brain for each category of study (physical, psychological, task activation, task connectivity). To correct for systematic magnitude inflation in smaller samples, we modeled how observed cross-brain effect size variability changes with sample size. We estimated population cross-brain variance using meta-regression with random intercepts for dataset and outcome category. The intercept represents the asymptotic population variance as n approaches infinity. 

Power analyses

To illustrate the practical implications on subsequent power analyses frequently used for study planning in psychology, we developed a method to estimate mass univariate power from a given cross-brain distribution under different correction approaches.

Results

Effect size

Network-level (i.e., pooled within network) and multivariate (i.e., CCA and Hotelling’s T2 test) effects were modestly (network) to substantially (multivariate) stronger than edge- or voxel-level effects. 

While between-subject effects of all types were generally small, physical studies exhibited effect sizes that were approximately seven times larger than those of psychological studies. In contrast, task-based (within-subject) connectivity effect sizes were more than double those found in physical studies. In fact, a quarter of task-based connectivity effects were larger than small (|d| = 0.28). Note that task-based studies rely on within-subject designs, whereas the physical and psychological studies used here represent between-subject designs.

However, even for the same tasks and dataset, task-based activation effects were about half the size of task-based connectivity effects. This makes task-based activation effects potentially on par with some brain-wide association effects.

Power

As expected by the prevalence of very small effect sizes, the vast majority of univariate effects could not be detected with adequate power (β = 80%) using less than a Consortium-level sample size (i.e., using n<100 subjects). In fact, between-subjects studies required a Large Consortium-level sample size (n=1,000–4,999) to detect more than half of the effects in the brain. Yet many more effects could be detected in task-based connectivity; more than half of task-based effects in the connectome could be detected with Consortium+ sample sizes of 500–999 subjects, and a few effects would even be detectable by individual labs. 

Just as for effect size, power increased substantially with broader-scale approaches. For within-subject analyses, a tenth to a quarter of the network-level effects were detectable with sample sizes accessible to individual labs (n < 50). Power also improved when moving to the network-level for between-subject effects, such that more than a quarter of network-level physical effects were detectable at the Consortium sample size (psychological effects showed a more modest improvement). Most importantly, all multivariate effects were detectable at sample sizes either readily available to individual labs (n<25 for physical and task-based effects) or small centers (n<100 for psychological effects).

Conclusion

In the functional neuroimaging community, it is a common refrain that effect sizes are small and sample sizes inadequate.1 Although these concerns have been supported by anecdotal accounts and isolated findings, there has not yet been a comprehensive investigation into these claims. Any such investigation must also contend with the uncertainty that arises when examining thousands of variables simultaneously. In this study, we took a closer look at a variety of commonly used study types in some of the largest datasets currently available to directly assess the magnitude of effect sizes in fMRI, developing a method to also correct for cross-brain effect size magnitude inflation. Our findings align with the prevailing expectation that fMRI effects are often exceedingly small, and that, accordingly, typical labs are often underpowered to detect them.

Even the smallest of the "large" datasets used here included sample sizes out of reach of most labs, and many types of effects are still undetectable at those sample sizes. The fact that between-subject effects in particular require such substantial sample sizes reinforces the suggestion by Marek et al.,1 that consortia-level data may be necessary to estimate replicable univariate brain-behavior correlations. This echoes similar findings in genetics, where studies are known to exhibit similarly small effects across often millions of genes, requiring hundreds of thousands to even millions of subjects for detection.3 In contrast, behavioral psychology effect sizes can be an order of magnitude larger, although reports are often inflated.4

Although revision is warranted, the situation is not as dire as it seems. Brain imaging is a relatively new and impressive field. Best practices for measurement, acquisition, analysis, and more are continuously being refined for neuroimaging in the face of its complex and historically unwieldy data characteristics, and patience is likewise deserved when studying such obscure, individualized phenomena as mental life.5 The scale of neuroimaging discoveries that are replicable remains impressive (e.g., the ability to map critical language cortex during surgery, the discovery of classes of large scale brain networks, etc.), especially keeping in mind that even relatively well-characterized phenomena exhibit “small” effect sizes.

These findings invite researchers to rethink their reliance on traditional study planning methods and embrace empirically informed methods that incorporate uncertainty. In particular, researchers should exercise caution when basing power analyses on point estimates from individual studies, as the potential for both magnitude and sign errors when effects are small relative to sampling error makes such calculations particularly questionable. To facilitate a deeper understanding of empirical estimates and their uncertainty, we provide an interactive Shiny app that enables users and methodology developers to explore effect size estimates based on specific characteristics of interest (neuroprismlab.shinyapps.io/braineffex). It would also be helpful to reflect on findings from the field of statistical genetics, which has spent more than a decade examining how to best estimate effect size distribution parameters in high-dimensional data while accounting for widespread, correlated effects. While it is unclear to what extent genomic assumptions may extend to neuroimaging data, the field would benefit from greater reflection on advances in statistical genetics and how they may be applied to neuroimaging.

In summary, we established data-driven effect size benchmarks for neuroimaging. These findings lay the groundwork for more informed study planning in neuroscience and further contextualize shared challenges facing high-dimensional biomedical research. By grounding study planning in evidence-based benchmarks, neuroimaging can move towards its goal of fostering a culture of robust and replicable study planning to better facilitate discovery.
