Introduction

The anatomical gyri (ridges) and sulci (grooves) are prominent and individualized hallmarks of the human brain. They emerge during embryonic development as the cortical sheet becomes progressively wrinkled, forming highly individualized “fingerprints” that capture individual variability and largely persist across the lifespan1. Indeed, there are two major characteristics of formed folding. (1) Differentiation: Serving as two elementary units of cortical folding, gyri and sulci are structurally, and functionally differentiated2,3. Indeed, distinct regulations in gene expression, cellular mechanisms and mechanical forces4 will result in these differentiations, individually revealing the morphological reorganization and functional specialization at specific ages or growth stages. (2) Pathological relevance: Abnormalities in the gyro-sulcal morphology are frequently associated with neuropsychiatric disorders5,6. 

Yet, two critical questions remain for these characteristics: (1) normative growth charts capturing the lifespan trajectories of gyro-sulcal morphometric differentiations have not been clearly elucidated. (2) whether or not these trajectories of individualized gyro-sulcal patterns can serve as biomarkers for quantifying morphometric deviations in clinical disorders remains unclear.

To alleviate these questions, we propose to use the GAMLSS (generalized additive model for location, scale, and shape) model to derive nonlinear trajectories of gyro-sulcal morphometric differentiations from network, regional, functional coupling, and cognitive relevance perspectives in development, young adult and aging populations. Once gyro-sulcal trajectories are obtained, we will apply them to diseased populations to identify potential biomarkers.

Methods

Dataset

To explore normative trajectories of gyro-sulcal differentiation patterns, we will use publicly available Human Connectome Project (HCP) 3T datasets, including HCP-D (Development, ages 5-21), HCP-YA (Young Adults, ages 22-36+), HCP-A (Aging, ages 36-100+). We exclude a small number of individuals under 8 and over 89 years old and retain only one subject per family, resulting in 1690 subjects (ages 8–88) with both function and diffusion modalities. To assess clinical utility of gyro-sulcal trajectories, we further include two HCP diseased cohorts: BANDA (Connectomes Related to Anxiety & Depression, ages 14-17) and HCP-EP (Early Psychosis, ages 16-35). Previous studies have reported altered morphology in major depressive disorder7 and psychosis8, highlighting the relevance of including these populations.

Methodology

Gyri-sulci delineation

We use the personalized Destrieux atlas9, which automatically divides the cortex into gyral and sulcal substructures based on curvature and convexity information. For each participant, we define 34 gyri and 34 sulci within each hemisphere.

Morphometric networks

Multidimensional vertex-level morphometric metrics are used to construct individual morphometric inverse divergence (MIND) networks, since the MIND method expresses higher developmental sensitivity and strong associations with fiber constraints and genetic factors10. Six  anatomical measures will be incorporated, consistent with prior work10.

Harmonization for different sites

Since out-of-scanner assessment similarities of HCP-D, HCP-A and HCP-YA are maximized11 and scanner sites are related to different age populations, we simply use the neuroHarmonize tool12 to remove site effects, rather than treating site as a parameter in the non-linear modeling.

GAMLSS for modeling non-linear trajectories

 We used the model settings proposed in Liang et al.13: Johnson SU distribution (four parameters: median, variation, skewness, and kurtosis), with B-spline functions (3 degrees of freedom) for location parameters. We controlled for sex and estimated total intracranial volume (eTIV) during model fitting to get trajectories, followed by 1000 Bootstrap resamples across 15 age ranges (5 equal divisions for each cohort) to estimate growth curves and their rate (first derivative).

Results

Globally, gyri show higher MIND similarity mean and standard deviations (SD) than sulci. Their differences decreased rapidly during adolescence and early adulthood (8–30y). From 30–60 years, these differences remained relatively stable, reflecting a plateau in morphometric organization. After 60 years, mean differences increased, whereas SD differences remained largely unchanged.

For the distributions measures, Jensen–Shannon Distance (JSD) was higher in gyri, indicating more heterogeneous and information-richer structural distributions. The gyro–sulcal differences continuously decreased until 54 years, suggesting convergence of their structural organization during adulthood. After this period, the difference rises sharply. The near-linear trajectory of the rate-of-change curve further suggests a stable and gradual reconfiguration process underlying both development and aging.

Similarly, the LMC (where higher value denoted distributions that were neither random nor trivial but instead exhibited “organized complexity”) was greater in gyri than sulci across the lifespan. The gyrus–sulcus differences followed a multi-phase developmental dynamic: 8–15y (increase); 15–30y (rapid decrease with a minimum rate around 22 years), 30–53y (increase with the peak of growth rates near 42y), 53–76y (decrease with the trough around 65y), and 76–88y (rapid increase).

Together, these findings align well with known neurodevelopmental and aging phases: development and reconfiguration (synaptic pruning and myelination) in adolescence14 and early adulthood, consolidation in young-adult, turning point in midlife15, as well as decomplexification and compensatory in old-adults16.

Conclusion

Across all four global morphological network metrics, gyral regions consistently exhibited stronger, more heterogeneous, and more complex structural profiles than sulcal regions, suggesting a fundamental organizational distinction. The lifespan-dependent divergence highlighted distinct developmental pressures and aging vulnerabilities of gyral versus sulcal architecture. These metrics could be used to identify more vulnerable ages and stages of neurodevelopment as targets in developing personalized therapeutic approaches.
