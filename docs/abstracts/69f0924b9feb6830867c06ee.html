Introduction

The relationship between brain organisation and behaviour has traditionally been examined through the functional specialisation of the cerebral cortex. Advances in functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) have enabled refined cortical parcellations by linking regional activity and connectivity patterns to specific cognitive processes (Glasser et al., 2016; Smith et al., 2009). However, task-based fMRI relies on predefined paradigms, limiting the investigation of large-scale integrative systems. In contrast, the brain’s wiring architecture, the connectome, has received less functional characterisation. White matter has mainly been described using anatomical criteria (Catani and Thiebaut de Schotten, 2012), and its functional roles have been inferred indirectly from lesion–symptom associations. These approaches are constrained by lesion distribution biases and coarse cognitive taxonomies, leaving the functional organisation of white matter poorly understood.

Recent methodological advances allow the investigation of white matter contributions to functional brain organisation. The Functionnectome framework (Nozais et al., 2022) projects fMRI-derived activity onto white matter using structural connectivity priors, enabling the study of white matter involvement in functional connectivity. While resting-state approaches remain difficult to interpret functionally, naturalistic stimuli such as movie watching engage multiple cognitive systems dynamically without rigid task constraints (Hasson et al., 2004).

Here, we used a data-driven approach to parcellate white matter circuits according to functional specificity. Applying the Functionnectome to fMRI data acquired during video watching, we identified white matter parcellations and validated them across independent datasets. We further characterised their functional roles by integrating an artificial intelligence framework grounded in a cognitive morphospace, linking white matter activity to cognitive functions. 

Methods

Participants and data acquisition: We analysed data from 110 participants (55 women) from the Human Connectome Project (HCP) 7T release. Each participant completed approximately one hour of fMRI scanning (3655 volumes), comprising alternating resting-state and video-watching runs. Data were preprocessed and registered to MNI152 space as previously described. Participants were randomly split into a discovery group (n = 55) and a replication group (n = 55).

Naturalistic video stimuli: Four short video stimuli were presented during fMRI acquisition. Two clips were sourced from Creative Commons–licensed movies and two were excerpts from Hollywood films previously described in the HCP protocol. Each movie included a repeated clip for validation. A 20-second rest period preceded each clip and followed the full movie, during which a fixation screen displaying “REST” was shown.

Functional MRI data projected into the white matter: To estimate white matter contributions during video watching, fMRI data were projected onto the white matter using the Functionnectome framework. Grey matter BOLD signals were weighted by probabilistic structural connectivity priors derived from normative tractography. Separate anatomical priors were used for association, commissural and projection fibres. For each time point, Functionnectome volumes were extracted across participants and entered into voxel-wise one-sample t-tests using FSL randomise, yielding group-level maps of white matter functional variation. This procedure was applied independently to the discovery and replication groups, generating group-level temporal variation series.

Dimensionality reduction and clustering: To examine large-scale functional organisation, we applied Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) to the group-level temporal variation series. UMAP reduced high-dimensional voxel-wise time courses into a two-dimensional embedding preserving local and global structure. Analyses were performed separately for discovery and replication datasets. Reproducibility was assessed by correlating pairwise Euclidean distance matrices from randomly sampled embedding points and comparing them with a null model generated from voxel-shuffled data. Given the similar embedding structure, data from both groups were combined for clustering. Voxels were clustered in UMAP space using a Hierarchical Density Based Clustering (HDBSCAN), a density-based algorithm robust to variable cluster density and noise. Model parameters were optimised by maximising the Density Based Cluster Validity (DBCV) score. Identified clusters were projected back into brain space, yielding functionally defined white matter parcellations.

Out-of-sample validation: An independent validation group of 20 HCP 7T participants was analysed using the same pipeline. To assess robustness, parcel-wise temporal variation series were correlated between datasets. Functional homogeneity was quantified using within-parcel voxel-wise correlations and compared against a null distribution generated from 1000 size- and number-matched random parcellations. Statistical significance was assessed using Z-scores and permutation-based p-values.

Cognitive morphospace and decoding: To map white matter activity onto cognitive dimensions, we constructed a white matter cognitive morphospace (Pacella et al., 2024) based on 506 Neurosynth meta-analytic association maps. These maps were projected into white matter using Functionnectome and averaged within parcels, yielding parcel-wise cognitive representations. UMAP was applied to generate a three-dimensional morphospace. Group-level white matter temporal variation maps from HCP, Naturalistic Neuroimaging Database, and Natural Scenes datasets were projected into this space using the trained model. Cognitive content at each time point was decoded by identifying the nearest cognitive term, enabling functional interpretation of association and commissural circuits. 

Results

Robust and reproducible functional organisation of white matter

Functional MRI signals acquired during naturalistic video watching were projected onto white matter using the Functionnectome framework, separately for association, commissural, and projection fibres. Group-level voxel-wise temporal variation series were computed for the discovery and replication groups and embedded into a two-dimensional space using UMAP, revealing a structured functional organisation of white matter voxels. The spatial configuration of the embeddings was highly similar across datasets for association (r = .98) and projection fibres (r = .97), and moderately high for commissural fibres (r = .72), while embeddings generated from shuffled data showed minimal similarity (r < .10), confirming that the observed structure was not driven by chance.

Given this reproducibility, discovery and replication datasets were combined for clustering. Using HDBSCAN, we identified 36 functionally distinct clusters for association fibres and 40 clusters for commissural fibres, covering 67% and 90% of voxels, respectively (Figure 1a-d). Projection fibres did not yield stable clusters based on functional activity. Mapping these clusters back to brain space resulted in functionally defined white matter parcellations that aligned with major anatomical tracts, providing anatomical interpretability to the data-driven results (Figure 1e-h).

Out-of-sample validation and functional homogeneity

Parcellations were applied to an independent validation group exposed to the same movie stimuli. Parcel-wise temporal activation profiles were highly correlated between datasets (mean r = .78 for both association and commissural parcellations, all p < .001), demonstrating strong generalisability. Functional homogeneity analyses further showed that voxels within each parcel exhibited highly similar temporal dynamics (association = 0.92; commissural = 0.96). Both values significantly exceeded those obtained from 1000 random parcellations (Z = 8.05 and Z = 21.03, respectively), confirming the functional specificity of the parcellations.

The activation patterns and the concatenated videos for significant activation time points for each parcel for associative and commissural parcellations are provided on the website http://cognipact.bcblab.com

Linking white matter dynamics to cognition

To characterise the cognitive relevance of the parcellations, we constructed a white matter cognitive morphospace by embedding 506 meta-analytic cognitive maps into a low-dimensional space defined by parcel-wise white matter activity. Group-level temporal variation maps from the HCP dataset were projected into this morphospace, allowing each time point to be associated with the closest cognitive term. This approach was validated by projecting independent datasets, including a separate HCP validation sample, a full-length movie dataset (NNDb), and a natural scene-viewing dataset (NSD), which consistently mapped onto similar cognitive dimensions across studies (Figure 1i-k).

Differential cognitive profiles of association and commissural fibres

Analysis of activation frequencies revealed distinct cognitive profiles for association and commissural parcellations. These differences were quantified using a normalised activation index and were consistent across datasets. Together, these results demonstrate that functionally defined white matter circuits exhibit distinct and reproducible cognitive profiles, supporting a principled framework for investigating white matter contributions to cognition.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that white matter circuits exhibit a rich and reproducible functional organisation, challenging the long-standing view of white matter as a passive relay system. By projecting fMRI signals onto white matter using the Functionnectome and leveraging naturalistic video stimuli, we provide the first comprehensive, cognition-driven parcellation of association and commissural fibres. These parcellations reveal that white matter supports distinct and functionally specialised contributions to cognition, extending functional brain mapping beyond the cortex.

Traditional task-based fMRI remains the gold standard for isolating specific cognitive processes but is constrained by predefined paradigms that may not capture the complexity of real-world cognition. In contrast, naturalistic stimuli engage multiple cognitive systems dynamically and allow functional networks to emerge without rigid task boundaries. Our approach integrates these stimuli with structural connectivity priors, bridging a critical gap between functional and anatomical perspectives on brain organisation. Unlike lesion-based, stimulation-based, or purely anatomical classifications, this framework captures white matter function directly and at scale.

The Cognipact parcellation reveals clear functional differentiation between association and commissural fibres. These findings underscore that different white matter systems support distinct cognitive domains and highlight their active role in shaping large-scale brain dynamics. The absence of functional specialisation within projection fibres likely reflects either their more homogeneous role in cortico-subcortical communication or the limited engagement of subcortical systems during passive video viewing. Future studies using targeted tasks may further elucidate their functional organisation.

A key contribution of this work is the development of a white matter cognitive morphospace, which links moment-to-moment white matter activity to cognitive dimensions derived from meta-analytic fMRI data. The successful projection of multiple independent datasets into this morphospace demonstrates its robustness and generalisability across stimuli and acquisition protocols. The identification of consistent cognitive profiles across datasets suggests that the morphospace captures fundamental principles of white matter organisation.

Several limitations should be noted. Naturalistic stimuli may not engage all cognitive systems equally, and group-level analyses do not account for individual variability. Nonetheless, prior work has shown strong inter-subject synchrony during movie watching, supporting the validity of our approach. Importantly, our data-driven framework reduces reliance on predefined cognitive taxonomies, enabling the discovery of underrepresented or integrative cognitive functions. To facilitate interpretation, we provide an online tool linking parcel activations to video content and cognitive terms.

In summary, this study establishes a robust and openly available framework for investigating the functional organisation of white matter. The Cognipact parcellation and accompanying cognitive morphospace provide new avenues for studying cognition, brain connectivity, and their disruption in neurological and psychiatric conditions (http://cognipact.bcblab.com).
