Introduction

The prefrontal cortex (PFC) is the most advanced association region of the human brain, crucial for complex cognitive functioning, including working memory and executive functioning. To facilitate these cognitive tasks, the PFC is densely interconnected by short association fibers (SAFs) that link adjacent gyri and support the local processing necessary for integrating information and guiding flexible behavior.1 However, despite this clear cognitive and behavioral significance, the fine-scale organization of the prefrontal SAFs of the human brain remains largely unknown. Currently, diffusion tractography is the only noninvasive method capable of measuring these connections in-vivo. However, connections derived by tractography are vulnerable to false positives, and suffer from the lack of a definitive ground truth.2 These challenges are amplified for SAFs, where fibers are small, dense, and highly curved. 

Short-range prefrontal connections have been characterized with histological tract-tracing in non-human primates. These tract-tracing studies suggest clear, replicable patterns of connectivity between the divisions of the PFC, specifying both organization and precise terminations for prefrontal SAFs.1 Importantly, these studies provide a biological basis for tractography reconstructions. Here, we aimed to reconstruct the terminations described by the histological literature with tractography, providing the first in-vivo visualization of the PFC SAFs in the human brain.

Here we leverage decades of histological findings to anatomically constrain tractography and find: (i) these small, intricate short-range prefrontal connections can reliably be visualized in humans, (ii) pathways reconstructed with tractography replicate classical anatomical findings, (iii) these connections show both high test-retest reliability and individual variability, and (iv) the microstructural properties of these pathways correlate with cognitive domains traditionally associated with the PFC.

Methods

We analyzed minimally preprocessed, multi-shell dMRI from 1,003 healthy young adults in the Human Connectome Project (HCP; 547F, 456M; age range = 22 - 36) and the 44-subject HCP test-retest cohort (31 F, 13 M).3 Cortical areas were defined per-subject using the HCP-MMP1 atlas4 and grouped into the five canonical PFC partitions: dorsolateral (dlPFC), ventrolateral (vlPFC), orbitofrontal (OFC), frontal pole, and anterior cingulate (ACC). The dlPFC was described as Brodmann Areas 8, 9, 46, and 9/46; the vlPFC as areas 44, 45, and 47; the OFC as areas 11, 13, and 14; frontal pole as area 10; and the ACC as areas 24, 25, and 32. We then performed multi-tissue constrained spherical deconvolution and anatomically constrained probabilistic tractography (iFOD2; MRtrix3)5 between each possible pair of cortical ROI’s, for a total possible 91 connections. Tractography was performed to generate 1,000 streamlines per run between each ROI pair of the dl-PFC, vl-PFC, frontal pole, OFC, and ACC, as well as between ROI pairs within each PFC partition. This resulted in a total of 91 tracts for each subject. For each tract, two runs of tractography were conducted and summed, for a max total of 2,000 streamlines per tract. Firstly, for each tract we masked the subject’s GMWMI volume by the first ROI in the corresponding ROI pair, and seeded from the masked GMWMI image with the second ROI as the target. We then repeated this procedure with ROI’s flipped, and summed the two tracts. Finally, we concatenated the two tracts and performed streamline outlier removal.

To evaluate our tractography results against the primate histological findings, we classified each potential pathway based on its anatomical plausibility, reproducibility, and concurrence with histological literature. Each candidate pathway was classified relative to the histological literature:

  • True positive (TP): Bundles which are present in histology, as well as present and anatomically plausible in tractography. 

  • True negative (TN): Bundles which are absent in histology and absent in tractography. 

  • False negative (FN): Bundles which are absent in histology, but present in tractography. Note, this also includes false positive trajectories where histology is present, but tractography follows a clearly anatomically implausible trajectory.

  • False positive (FP): Bundles which are present in histology and not supported by tractography. 

Using these classifications, we computed accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and precision for the full PFC and by subregion. Tracts were considered present if they were reconstructed in >50% of the subject pool. Based on the histological literature, we expect 72 true positive connections and 19 true negative connections. To analyze test-retest reliability, we repeated the full pipeline in the test-retest cohort and quantified within-subject and between-subject reproducibility using bundle adjacency (BA) and weighted Dice overlap (wDice) for volume correspondence. Statistics were summarized across all TP tracts and within each PFC partition. 

Lastly, to test if microstructural properties of PFC SAF’s are associated with cognitive outcomes typically associated with the PFC, we conducted Pearson correlations to test for the relationship between fractional anisotropy (FA) and working memory, executive functioning, and processing speed scores available with the HCP database. Executive functioning was defined by the Wisconsin Card Sorting and Flanker Tasks scores; working memory was defined by List Sorting Working Memory Task scores; and processing speed was defined by the Pattern Comparison Speed task. Average FA was extracted from each subject’s individualized volumetric SAF mask for each prefrontal region, as well as regional intra-connection masks (e.g., mask of connections between 44, 45, and 47 for the vlPFC). Resulting p-values from the Pearson correlations were corrected for False Discovery Rate (FDR) to control for multiple comparisons. 

Results

Tractography findings closely resembled histological studies. Over 91 possible connections, we identified 49 TP (41 pathways replicable in >80% of the cohort), 17 TN, 12 FN, and 13 FP, yielding 73% overall accuracy, 80% sensitivity, 57% specificity, and 79% precision relative to histological literature. Performance varied by subregion, as the lateral and polar portions of the PFC strongly replicated the histological literature: The dlPFC displayed 74% accuracy, 81% sensitivity, 57% specificity, and 81% precision; vlPFC displayed 75% accuracy, 87% sensitivity, 54% specificity, and 77% precision; and the frontal pole displayed perfect agreement with available histology with 100% across accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and precision. The medial portions of the PFC still showed promising accuracy, but not to the extent of the lateral and polar portions: the OFC displayed 58% accuracy, 57% sensitivity, 60% specificity, and 67% precision; and finally, the ACC showed 58%, 68% sensitivity, 47% specificity, and 59% precision.

Reliability analyses in the HCP test-retest cohort demonstrated that TP bundles showed high test-retest reliability within individuals. Scan-rescan spatial disagreement for TP tracts was exceptionally low (BA range = 1.12 - 1.56mm across PFC regions), meaning the average spatial disagreement between rescans for a given TP tract was usually within the power of one voxel. Spatial overlap of TP tracts was also moderately high (wDice range = .62 - .69 across PFC regions). Tracts also showed high individual variability, as spatial disagreement was high when comparing between subjects (BA range = 2.35 - 2.72mm across PFC regions), and spatial overlap of TP tracts between subjects was low (wDice range = .29 - .32 across PFC regions).

Lastly, microstructure of the dlPFC and frontal pole subregions significantly, though weakly, related to both executive functioning and processing speed scores after FDR correction. The connections of the frontal pole (r = .1, p = .002 with Flanker; r = .1, p = .001 with Card Sorting) and the intraconnections of the vlPFC (r = .1, p = .005 with Card Sorting) both positively correlated with executive functioning scores. Microstructural integrity of the full PFC SAF mask significantly correlated with processing speed (r = .1, p = .001), as well as dlPFC connections (r = .1, p = .001) and intraconnections (r = .1, p = .005), and lastly, frontal pole connections (r = .1, p = .0005). 

Conclusion

Here we provide the first systematic, in-vivo visualization of the human prefrontal short-range connections. These findings highlight the potential of guiding tractography with histological findings, and demonstrate that even small, intricate short-range connections can be robustly reconstructed when tractography is anatomically constrained by the histological literature. Our reconstructions closely replicate classical anatomical findings across the PFC, exceeding 70% accuracy overall. Importantly, our methodology exhibits relative resistance to spurious pathways, with high precision (79%) and sensitivity (80%), overcoming the most significant challenge associated with tractography. Test-retest analyses further show that histology-consistent bundles reproduce within individuals at near-voxel spatial agreement and moderate-to-high overlap, establishing that these short-range pathways are stable and replicable within-subject. At the same time, between-subject comparisons reveal lower overlap and greater spatial dispersion, indicating meaningful individual variability and suggesting that prefrontal SAF architecture constitutes a distinctive wiring fingerprint. Finally, the microstructural properties of these pathways correlate with cognitive domains traditionally attributed to the PFC: higher integrity in dlPFC and frontal-pole SAFs relates to better executive functioning, and integrity in both regional and whole-PFC SAFs relates to faster processing speed. While these relationships were relatively weak, it is important to note that these associations emerge in a healthy cohort, which has been screened for neurological and psychiatric disorders. This points to the possibility that this methodology may be sensitive enough to detect the behavioral relevance of short-range prefrontal wiring and motivating translational studies in clinical populations, where executive control and processing speed are frequently compromised. A definitive claim about the role of PFC SAFs in cognition requires a specialized study with adequately sensitive cognitive tasks designed to test this relationship.

In this study we demonstrate that tractography, when guided by the biological ground truth of NHP tract-tracing, can reconstruct short-range pathways with high fidelity and precision.  By establishing a robust framework for mapping an understudied and previously inaccessible component of the human connectome, we show that these connections are anatomically faithful, robust, highly individualized, and cognitively relevant. Ultimately, this work takes a step towards bridging the gap between invasive animal studies and non-invasive human neuroscience, opening new avenues to investigate the local circuitry underlying complex behavior in health and disease.
