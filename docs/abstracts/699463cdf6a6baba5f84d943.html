Introduction

Mapping whole-brain directed connectivity is fundamental to understanding neural information flow. However, inferring causal interactions from resting-state fMRI remains challenging due to low temporal resolution, sluggish hemodynamic response, and absence of ground truth. Gold-standard methods like Dynamic Causal Modeling (DCM) are biophysically rigorous but computationally prohibitive for whole-brain analysis. Conversely, lagged correlation approaches are computationally efficient but fundamentally flawed: they conflate direct causal paths (A → C) with indirect propagation (A → B → C) and lack anatomical constraints. Critically, while lagged covariances capture temporal precedence, they cannot distinguish genuine directed connections from spurious asymmetries induced by common network sources—regions A and B may exhibit asymmetric lagged correlations not because A influences B, but because both receive inputs from a shared upstream driver C with different delays.

We inverted the Network Diffusion Model (NDM)1 to address these limitations. Rather than predicting functional connectivity from known structural connectivity, we estimate unknown directed connectivity from observed lagged covariances while constraining solutions with structural priors. By embedding a generative diffusion process within multi-lag optimization, the method accounts for signal propagation through network topology, pruning spurious asymmetries from common sources while retaining genuine pathways supported by temporal precedence and anatomical constraints. Applied to resting-state data from 300 HCP subjects, the method revealed hierarchical organization with subcortical, early visual cortex (V1), limbic, salience, and higher-order association networks (control, default mode) as primary sources, contrasting with lagged correlations where spurious asymmetries obscured this architecture—demonstrating the method’s ability to disambiguate directed connectivity from temporal precedence artifacts.

Methods

NDM framework.

The classical Network Diffusion Model (NDM)¹ describes neural signal propagation across structural brain networks. Given a normalized structural connectivity matrix S derived from diffusion tractography, the model posits:

dx(t)/dt = -β(I-S)x(t)

where x(t) ∈ ℝᴺ represents the neural state vector across N brain regions, β > 0 governs signal decay, and (I-S) defines the graph Laplacian. Traditionally, NDM uses known S to predict functional connectivity. We inverted this paradigm: we treat effective connectivity as unknown and estimate it from observed functional dynamics.

Generative model with unknown effective connectivity.

We replaced the known structural matrix S with an unknown directed effective connectivity matrix E, yielding the modified system:

dx(t)/dt = -β(I-E)x(t)

The analytical solution from initial state x(0) is:

x(t) = exp[-βt(I-E)]x(0)

Functional connectivity is defined as the covariance of neural activity. The lagged functional connectivity between time t and t+τ is given by FC(τ) = E[x(t)x(t+τ)ᵀ]. Assuming the initial state has identity covariance, E[x(0)x(0)ᵀ] = I, we derived the model's predicted lagged functional connectivity:

FC_model(τ; E) = exp[-βt(I-E)][exp[-β(t+τ)(I-E)]]ᵀ

While this expression is symmetric at τ=0, it becomes asymmetric for τ > 0, capturing the directional propagation of neural signals through the network. Empirical lagged covariances exhibited clear asymmetric patterns (Figure 1b), providing the directional constraints for estimating effective connectivity.

Multi-lag optimization framework.

Although directed E can be estimated from zero-lag covariance when constrained by structural priors, empirical lagged covariances exhibit intrinsic asymmetries that encode temporal precedence. Including multiple lags provides additional directional constraints that stabilize estimation. We formulated the inverse problem as:

min_E Σ(τ=0 to τ_max) ||FC_emp(τ) - FC_model(τ;E)||²_F + λΣ(i,j) σ(α(E_ij + E_ji - S_ij))

subject to E_ij ≥ 0 for all i,j, where σ(x)=1/(1+exp(-x)) is the sigmoid function. The structural regularization term penalizes deviations from the symmetric structural prior S while permitting asymmetry in E, ensuring anatomical plausibility. We fit all lags from τ=0 to τ_max=9 seconds simultaneously.

Data acquisition and preprocessing.

We utilized the Human Connectome Project (HCP) Young Adult dataset² (N=300 subjects). Structural diffusion imaging and resting-state fMRI (TR=720ms, multiband acceleration) were processed using the Micapipe pipeline³. Structural connectivity matrices were derived from probabilistic tractography with streamline counts normalized by region volume. The cortex was parcellated into 100 regions using the Schaefer functional atlas⁴, and 14 subcortical structures were segmented via FreeSurfer⁵, yielding 114 total regions. Empirical lagged covariances were computed from preprocessed BOLD time series at integer multiples of TR up to 6.48 seconds (9 lag points). Analysis revealed peak asymmetry at τ=4 (Figure 1b), indicating maximal directional information at intermediate temporal scales. For net flow computation and visualization, negative covariance values were thresholded to zero to facilitate interpretation, as they were small in magnitude and complicate directional flow analysis (Figure 1a,c). However, original unthresholded lagged covariances were used in all NDM optimization procedures to preserve complete temporal structure.

Hyperparameter tuning and model validation.

To ensure identifiability, we fixed β·t = 1 (equivalently, β = 1 and t = 1), as the product βt can be absorbed into the scaling of E and cannot be uniquely determined independently. We learned the regularization hyperparameters (λ, α) via grid search on a training subset of N=100 subjects to maximize goodness-of-fit while maintaining sparse, anatomically consistent solutions (α=0.1 and λ=0.0001). The final model was evaluated on the held-out test set of N=300 subjects.

Results

Empirical lagged functional connectivity.

We first characterized the temporal structure of empirical lagged covariances across the HCP resting-state dataset (Figure 1a,b). Analysis of lagged FC revealed maximum asymmetry at τ = 4 seconds (Figure 1b, right), quantified as the Frobenius norm of FC_emp(τ) - FC_emp(τ)ᵀ. While net flow magnitudes varied across different time lags, the spatial distribution of source and sink regions remained remarkably consistent (Figure 1a), indicating stable patterns independent of temporal scale selection. Net flow analysis of empirical lagged covariances identified somatomotor and dorsal attention networks as predominant sources, while visual cortex exhibited strong negative net flow, appearing as a primary sink (Figure 1a,c). However, raw lagged correlations exhibited widespread, dense connectivity patterns (Figure 1b) that likely conflated direct causal influences with indirect propagation and spurious asymmetries from common network sources.

Estimated effective connectivity structure.

The multi-lag NDM optimization identified sparse, directed connectivity patterns constrained by structural anatomy (Figure 2a). Compared to the dense empirical lagged FC matrices (Figure 1b), the estimated E matrices were significantly sparser, retaining only connections supported by both the structural prior S and temporal dynamics across multiple lags. The model achieved high goodness of fit (median Pearson r > 0.7 for τ = 0-6; Figure 2b), with gradual performance decline at longer lags reflecting reduced signal-to-noise ratio. The regularization term effectively pruned spurious indirect connections and ambiguous asymmetries from common sources while preserving anatomically plausible direct pathways.

Dissociation between lagged correlations and effective connectivity.

The estimated effective connectivity revealed markedly different hierarchical organization than empirical lagged correlations (Figure 2c,d). Net flow analysis identified subcortical structures (hippocampus, amygdala, striatum), early visual cortex (V1), limbic regions (temporal pole), and components of salience and association networks (control, default mode) as sources of directed connectivity (Figure 2c). This pattern contrasts sharply with raw lagged correlations, where these sources were less prominent and somatomotor regions appeared as primary drivers. Within visual cortex, early regions (V1) emerged as sources while higher visual areas acted as sinks, establishing a feedforward hierarchy. Somatomotor regions that appeared as sources in lagged FC exhibited net inflow in the estimated E, indicating they are recipients rather than generators of directed connectivity. Cortical surface visualization (Figure 2d) confirmed this organization, with early sensory areas showing variable directional profiles and higher-order regions exhibiting net inflow. These dissociations demonstrate that temporal precedence in lagged correlations does not directly reflect causal architecture, as spurious asymmetries from common inputs can dominate the observed patterns. The NDM framework disambiguates these relationships by explicitly modeling signal propagation through network topology.

Conclusion

We present a computational framework for estimating whole-brain directed effective connectivity from resting-state fMRI that addresses critical limitations of existing approaches. By inverting the Network Diffusion Model and fitting to multi-lag functional covariance matrices, our method bridges the gap between computationally expensive biophysical models like DCM and anatomically unconstrained correlation-based techniques. The integration of structural priors derived from diffusion tractography ensures that estimated directed connections respect underlying white matter architecture, effectively distinguishing direct causal influences from indirect propagation through multi-synaptic pathways. Our multi-lag optimization framework leverages the temporal asymmetries inherent in lagged covariances to stabilize the inverse problem and extract robust directional information. Applied to 300 subjects from the Human Connectome Project, the method revealed hierarchical organization with subcortical structures (hippocampus, amygdala, striatum), early visual cortex (V1), limbic regions, and higher-order association networks as primary sources (Figure 2c,d). Notably, the estimated effective connectivity differed markedly from empirical lagged correlations: early visual cortex (V1) emerged as a source rather than sink, somatomotor regions transitioned from apparent sources to recipients, and subcortical structures became prominent drivers—patterns obscured in raw lagged FC. These dissociations demonstrate that temporal precedence does not directly reflect causal architecture when common inputs induce spurious asymmetries. By explicitly modeling signal propagation through network topology, the method disambiguates genuine directed pathways from artifacts in lagged correlations.

This framework is computationally tractable, scales to whole-brain parcellations of hundreds of regions, and requires only standard resting-state fMRI acquisitions without specialized pulse sequences or experimental manipulations. The method provides a principled, data-driven approach to mapping effective connectivity in large-scale population studies, opening new avenues for investigating directed network disruptions in neurological and psychiatric disorders.
