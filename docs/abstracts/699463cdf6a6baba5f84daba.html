Introduction

Neuroimagers have explored the associations between age-related differences in cognitive functioning and variations in brain structure and function, using brain MRI1,2,3. Yet, most predictive studies remain cross-sectional, leaving unclear the extent to which MRI-based markers predict stable between-person differences versus longitudinal within-person changes over time. Distinguishing these two sources of cognitive variation is crucial, as cognitive functioning varies not only between individuals in a relatively stable manner, but also changes within individuals over time. Markers reflecting stable between-person differences may be valuable for diagnostic purposes, identifying individuals with consistently high or low levels of cognitive functioning. In contrast, markers sensitive to within-person changes would be more suitable for prognostic use, such as forecasting trajectories of cognitive decline. And because MRIs of different modalities reflect different aspects of the brain, the extent to which markers from various modalities can fulfil these complementary roles remains an open question.

Our study pursued three primary objectives. First, we aimed to estimate and compare the accuracy of predicting cognitive functioning from individual neuroimaging phenotypes as well as from their combinations. Second, we sought to quantify the extent to which MRI-based markers derived from various neuroimaging phenotypes accounted for within- vs. between-person variation in cognitive functioning. To achieve this, we employed linear mixed-effects models to separate the within-person from the between-person variance of cognitive functioning and assessed how much each MRI-based marker explained each type of variance of cognitive functioning. Third, acknowledging that cognitive functioning changes with age, we aimed to determine how much of the age-related variance in cognitive functioning could be explained by these MRI-based markers; this was addressed using commonality analyses. 

Methods

The present study used longitudinal data from the Dallas Lifespan Brain Study (DLBS) to benchmark the capacity of MRI-based markers of different modalities in capturing within- vs between-person variations in cognitive functioning. 450 participants, aged 21 to 90 years, 62.7% females, underwent repeated MRI scans at five-year intervals, up to three times. 

We developed machine learning models to predict cognitive functioning using 37 neuroimaging phenotypes, spanning five MRI modalities: (1) task-based functional MRI (task-fMRI), which reflects BOLD activity associated with task events; (2) resting-state and task-based functional connectivity (FC), which captures correlations in BOLD time series across regions; (3) structural MRI (sMRI), which measures brain morphology; (4) diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), which quantifies water diffusion patterns to infer structural connectivity and tractometry; and (5) arterial spin labeling (ASL), which estimates cerebral blood flow.  

Models were first trained on individual neuroimaging phenotypes (phenotype-specific models), then on combined phenotypes within each modality (modality-specific stacking models), and finally across all modalities (multimodal stacking models). The models were based on Elastic Net, Partial Least Squares, Random Forest, Kernel Ridge Regression, and XGBoost. These models aimed to predict out-of-sample cognitive functioning, operationalised as a general factor of cognition (g-score) derived from a higher-order g-factor model using exploratory structural equation modelling (ESEM).

To assess how much within-person and between-person variance in cognitive functioning was explained by MRI-based markers, we applied variance decomposition4 to the neuroimaging-only models. The predicted g-scores were partitioned into within-person (level-1) and between-person (level-2) variance components. This analysis provides separate within- and between-person estimates of explanatory power for each MRI‑based marker.

To examine how much age-related variation in cognitive functioning was explained by MRI-based markers, we performed a commonality analysis5 on the combined age-plus-neuroimaging models. These mixed-effects models included three fixed effects: (1) age, (2) a between-person MRI-based marker, and (3) a within-person MRI-based marker. This approach assessed changes in marginal R², defined as the proportion of variance explained by fixed effects, when each fixed-effect variable was included or excluded. By comparing these changes, we quantified the variance uniquely attributable to age, within-person MRI-based markers, between-person MRI-based markers, and their shared contributions. 

The total amount of age-related cognitive variation was estimated using the marginal R² from a model where age was the sole fixed effect. The overlap in marginal R² between age and the MRI-based markers (i.e., their commonality) indicated the extent to which MRI features accounted for age-related variance in cognitive functioning. Accordingly, we focused on this shared variance to quantify how much age-related variation in cognitive functioning was captured by within-person and between-person MRI-based markers.

Results

Predictive performance of stacked models 

Combining phenotypes via stacking consistently enhanced performance over the best individual phenotype models, increasing the mean Pearson correlation by 0.02-0.12 and reducing the Mean Absolute Error (MAE) by 0.02–0.13. Combining all 37 neuroimaging phenotypes into a multimodal stacking model resulted in the strongest predictive performance overall, achieving a mean Pearson correlation of 0.73 and R² of 0.51 across the five outer cross-validation folds.

Stacking phenotypes within each modality revealed a similar, though slightly altered, performance hierarchy. DWI remained the strongest single modality (r = 0.69, R² = 0.47), but the performance of FC was substantially enhanced, placing it nearly on par with DWI (r = 0.69, R² = 0.46). These were followed by sMRI and task-fMRI, which showed identical predictive performance (r = 0.66, R² = 0.43). Notably, sMRI was the only modality for which stacking provided no performance gain over its best individual phenotype. Finally, ASL again showed the weakest performance (r = 0.39, R² = 0.13).

Variance Decomposition

Neuroimaging phenotypes from most modalities, except ASL, yielded statistically significant β values (p < .05) for both between-person and within-person MRI-based markers. The magnitude of the between-person β was consistently greater than that of the within-person β. For example, the multimodal stacking model, which integrates information across modalities, produced β = 0.77 (SE = 0.04, p < .001) for the between-person MRI-based marker and β = 0.3 (SE = 0.04, p < .001) for the within-person MRI-based marker, indicating a stronger unique contribution from between-person variation.

 For the within-person component, the fixed effect of the within-person MRI-based markers explained up to 17.2% of this variance (global variables from sMRI). Across stacking models, the proportion of within-person variance captured by MRI-based markers ranged from 0.6% (ASL) to 2% (task-fMRI), 2.5% (FC), 6.7% (DWI), 6.3% (multimodal), and 9.7% (sMRI).

For the between-person component, the fixed effect of the between-person MRI-based markers explained up to 60.3% of this component (multimodal stacking). Across modality-specific stacking models, the proportion of between-person variance captured by MRI-based markers ranged from 12% (ASL) to 47.9% (sMRI), 55.1% (task-fMRI), 55.9% (DWI) and 57% (FC). 

Commonality analysis

In the combined age-plus-neuroimaging models for the multimodal stacking markers, age, as a sole predictor, accounted for 48.5% of the variance in cognitive functioning, reflecting age-related cognitive variation. This age-related cognitive variation overlapped with the variance explained by the between-person MRI marker alone (43.4%), the within-person MRI marker alone (0.9%), and the common effects between the two markers (0.9%).

The commonality analysis indicates that all multimodal MRI markers together explained 93.2% of the age-related cognitive variation, calculated as ((43.4+0.9+0.9)÷48.5)×100. And the between-person MRI marker explained 91.3%, i.e., ((43.4+0.9)÷48.5)×100, while the within-person MRI marker explained only 3.7%, i.e., ((0.9+0.9)÷48.5)×100. Notably, although the within-person MRI marker accounted for a minimal amount of age-related cognitive variation, its variance was entirely shared with age, suggesting that the within-person MRI marker was relevant to age-related cognitive effects despite its smaller contribution.

This pattern, where the between-person marker accounted for a large proportion of variance and the within-person marker a much smaller proportion, both largely overlapping with age, was consistent across most modalities except ASL. ASL markers, both between- and within-person, explained only minimal variance in cognitive functioning. Among modalities, those showing the greatest overlap with age were DWI, sMRI, and FC, followed by task-fMRI, with ASL performing worst.

Conclusion

This study benchmarked the ability of MRI-based markers from five modalities to capture within- and between-person variation in cognitive functioning. We pursued three aims. First, we compared predictive performance across neuroimaging phenotypes and modalities. The multimodal stacking, which integrates features from all five modalities, achieved the highest accuracy, followed by DWI, FC, and sMRI. Second, we quantified each MRI marker’s capacity to explain within- versus between-person variation. Variance decomposition revealed an asymmetry: MRI-based markers were highly effective for predicting between-person differences but only modest, though significant, at tracking within-person longitudinal changes. Third, we examined how well these markers captured the cognition–age relationship. Commonality analyses showed that most between-person and within-person markers, except ASL, overlapped substantially with age-related variance. 

Our first objective was to determine the predictive performance of various MRI-based markers for cognitive functioning. Predictive accuracy was highly dependent on the MRI modality, and even within a single modality, performance varied substantially based on the quantification method and the brain atlas used for parcellation. In line with a growing body of research, the multimodal stacking model, which combined information from all 37 neuroimaging phenotypes, yielded the highest predictive accuracy. Stacking phenotypes within each modality also consistently improved performance over individual measures, supporting the view that different imaging phenotypes provide complementary, while overlapping, biological information relevant to cognition. 

Our second objective was to evaluate the capacity of each MRI-based marker to explain within- versus between-person variation in cognitive functioning. Most markers across modalities, except ASL, significantly captured both sources of variation, but the distribution was highly asymmetric. Between-person markers accounted for a much larger share of variance (up to 60.3%) compared to within-person markers (up to 17.2%). The between-person component was largest for models that stacked diffusion and functional connectivity features. At the phenotype level, this signal was driven by structural connectivity metrics, such as streamline count and length, and key morphometric measures, including subcortical volumes and global brain variables. In contrast, the models’ ability to track within-person change was limited, although sMRI-based phenotypes showed the most sensitivity to longitudinal change. ASL-based predictors were negligible at both levels.

Our third objective was to quantify the share of age-related variance in cognitive functioning that the MRI-based models capture. Using commonality analyses, we found that multimodal MRI markers accounted for a large portion of age-related cognitive variation, at 93.2%. This finding demonstrates that MRI-based markers effectively capture age-related cognitive variation. This accords with evidence that cognitive performance declines with age and that MRI features are consistently predictors of age. ASL again diverged from this pattern, showing relatively low overlap with age, likely reflecting its overall poorer predictive performance.

Consistent with our second objective, between-person MRI-based markers captured the majority of age-related cognitive variation, while within-person markers contributed much less. Importantly, although the within-person effects were weaker, they typically overlapped fully with age, suggesting that these markers also indexed age-related cognitive variation despite their smaller effect size. 

Together, these findings suggest that MRI modalities are well-suited for identifying individual differences in age-related cognitive functioning, whereas tracking cognitive change as people age will require markers and study designs with greater within-person sensitivity.
