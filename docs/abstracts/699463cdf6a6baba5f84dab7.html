Introduction

Hemispheric laterality is a fundamental organizational principle of the human brain, supporting critical functions such as language, attention, and motor control.1 Traditionally, laterality has been quantified using laterality indices derived from univariate measures within single regions or modalities. However, such approaches fail to capture the network-level interactions inherent to hemispheric specialization and typically treat structural and functional asymmetries as independent phenomena, thereby limiting mechanistic interpretation.2 Moreover, most conventional indices rely on simple signal differences between the left and right hemispheres, providing limited insight into how functionally lateralized regions are structurally supported by physical white matter pathways.

Diffusion Tensor Imaging (DTI), derived from diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), enables the reconstruction of white matter tracts and offers direct access to the physical substrates underlying interregional communication. DTI-based tractography therefore provides a unique opportunity to investigate hemispheric asymmetries in structural connectivity, network organization, and conduction-related properties that cannot be inferred from functional data alone.3 The primary objective of this study was to develop and validate a network-based framework for hemispheric laterality that integrates diffusion-derived structural connectivity with multi-scale organization, enabling the quantification of how functional lateralization is physically supported by white matter architecture.

Methods

This study analyzed DWI and T1-weighted structural MRI data from 198 healthy adults (117 males; age 35.2 ± years) obtained from the Nathan Kline Institute (NKI) dataset.4 DWI data were preprocessed using a standardized FSL pipeline, including denoising and correction for eddy currents and subject motion. For surface-based analyses, DWI images were registered to individual T1-weighted images using AFNI. Cortical surfaces were reconstructed from T1-weighted images using FreeSurfer, yielding pial and smoothed white matter surfaces. All surfaces were resampled to the standard surface space, consisting of 36,002 nodes and approximately 72,000 triangular faces. DTI modeling was performed using MRtrix3 on the aligned DWI data, followed by whole-brain tractography to extract streamline start and end points.

To localize structural connectivity in a surface-based framework, we employed a hexagonal prism kernel as a local three-dimensional sampling unit linking cortical surface nodes with adjacent subcortical white matter. Tract counts, defined as the number of streamline terminations within each kernel, were assigned to corresponding surface nodes, producing surface-mapped measures of local structural connectivity. Following standard preprocessing and surface reconstruction, we employed a hexagonal prism kernel (Fig. 1A) as a local three-dimensional sampling unit to precisely localize tractography-derived structural connectivity onto cortical surface nodes. Structural connections were classified into four hemispheric interaction categories: left-to-left (LL), left-to-right (LR), right-to-right (RR), and right-to-left (RL) (Fig. 1B-E), to derive two complementary bivariate laterality indices. Specifically, the Segregation Index was defined to capture hemispheric dominance in intra-hemispheric connectivity, while the Integration Index was defined to reflect dominance related to inter-hemispheric connectivity. 5 Group-level statistical analyses were conducted on these surface-mapped indices using one sample t tests with false discovery rate (FDR) correction at p < 0.05.

Results

Fig. 1B-E illustrates the surface mapping information and index derivation for a single subject. By applying this method to the 198 healthy adults for statistical processing, statistically significant and spatially consistent hemispheric asymmetry patterns were identified at the group level, as evidenced by the FDR-corrected surface maps (Fig.2). The analysis of the Segregation index map (Fig. 2A) revealed specific asymmetries in the preference for intra-hemispheric over inter-hemispheric connectivity. Furthermore, the Integration index map (Fig. 2B) demonstrated the overall hemispheric dominance in total structural connectivity strength across the cortex.

Conclusion

Importantly, the resulting surface-mapped laterality patterns showed strong spatial concordance with previously reported BOLD-based functional laterality maps from fMRI studies, indicating an alignment between structural and functional hemispheric specialization.5 This concordance suggests that functionally lateralized regions—such as those supporting language and attention—are underpinned by the asymmetric organization of physical white matter pathways. By extending laterality analysis beyond functional activation to structurally instantiated white matter networks, this study introduces a novel surface-based framework for investigating hemispheric asymmetry from a structural network perspective.
