Introduction

Brain network organization changes across the lifespan, reflecting both neural vulnerability and compensatory processes that support cognition1. Structural brain alterations can be partially offset through functional scaffolding, in which higher-order brain networks are increasingly recruited. According to the Scaffolding Theory of Aging and Cognition, individual differences in these processes are shaped not only by age, but also by biological and lifestyle factors that influence neural resilience.

Cardiometabolic risk factors are increasingly recognized as important modifiers of brain aging. When these risks co-occur as metabolic syndrome (MetS), their combined burden exert additive effects through shared vascular and inflammatory pathways, amplifying neural vulnerability beyond that associated with individual components alone. Midlife is a critical period for identifying these effects, as interventions targeting cardiometabolic health during this stage may substantially reduce later-life cognitive decline. 

Prior work from this sample suggest that midlife MetS is associated with subtle cognitive vulnerability and accelerated structural brain aging, yet structural brain age was not directly related to cognition2. This suggests that functional brain organization may play a key role in maintaining cognitive performance during midlife despite emerging structural vulnerability. Using resting-state fMRI, the present study examines whether MetS is associated with alterations in large-scale functional brain networks and whether these effects vary by age and sex and relate to cognitive profiles.

Methods

Participants were cognitively unimpaired adults aged 40–65 (N = 230) drawn from two prior studies of metabolic and brain health2. Seventy-five participants met criteria for MetS based on established clinical guidelines.

MRI data were acquired on a Siemens 3T Skyra scanner and organized in BIDS format, with acquisition parameters matched across studies. Preprocessing was conducted using fMRIPrep (v25.2.3)3. Anatomical processing included skull stripping, bias-field correction, FreeSurfer surface reconstruction, tissue segmentation of gray matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid, and nonlinear normalization to MNI152 space.

Functional preprocessing included slice-timing and motion correction, co-registration to T1-weighted images, and concatenated transformation to MNI space using ANTs with Lanczos interpolation. Physiological noise regressors were derived using CompCor. Denoising included regression of six motion parameters, mean white matter and CSF signals, global signal regression, high-pass filtering (0.008 Hz), and scrubbing of volumes with framewise displacement greater than 0.5 mm or standardized DVARS greater than 3. Participants were retained only if mean framewise displacement was ≤0.5 mm and at least 50% of volumes remained after scrubbing (≥3 minutes of resting-state data).

Functional connectivity was estimated using the Schaefer 400-parcel cortical atlas mapped to seven large-scale networks4. Pearson correlations between regional time series were Fisher z-transformed to generate 400 × 400 connectivity matrices. Network measures were computed using the Brain Connectivity Toolbox implemented in Python and included within-network connectivity, between-network connectivity, and global efficiency at both network and whole-brain levels5.

Cognitive performance was characterized using previously derived latent cognitive profiles of high memory, low memory, low executive functioning, and global average. Statistical analyses were conducted in R. Group differences in demographics, MetS components, and cognitive measures were tested with t-tests or chi-square tests. ANCOVAs examined differences in functional connectivity metrics by MetS status and components, adjusting for age and sex. Exploratory MetS × age and MetS × sex interactions were tested to identify subgroups with heightened neural vulnerability.  Kruskal-Wallis tests evaluated connectivity differences across cognitive profiles, with Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons. Effect sizes were reported as partial eta-squared or epsilon-squared. This is a preregistered study https://osf.io/jcwt9/overview.

Results

Participants with MetS demonstrated reduced within-network connectivity in the ventral attention (F[3,226] = 6.40, p = 0.012, ηp² = 0.025) and default mode networks (F[3,226] = 7.07, p = 0.008, ηp² = 0.026). Among individual MetS components, waist circumference (p < 0.01, ηp² = 0.030–0.037) and cholesterol (p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.038–0.052) were associated with reduced connectivity within these networks.

There were significant interaction effects of MetS group and age on connectivity metrics, such that at older ages, MetS participants displayed reduced within limbic network connectivity (F[4, 225]=9.16, p=0.003, partial eta = 0.039), reduced global efficiency (F[4, 225]=6.57, p=0.011, partial eta < 0.01), and reduced global between network connectivity (F[4, 225]=6.62, p=0.011, partial eta = 0.029) relative to participants without MetS. 

There were also significant interaction effects of MetS group and sex on connectivity metrics, such that males with MetS displayed reduced between network connectivity (F[4, 225]=5.47, p=0.020, partial eta=0.024) and reduced global efficiency (F[4, 225]=4.59, p=0.033, partial eta=0.020) relative to females with MetS. 

Between network connectivity was significantly associated with LPA-derived cognitive classes, such that connectivity between dorsal attention and ventral attention networks (H[3]=12.48, p=0.006, e2=0.05) was reduced in the High Memory class relative to the Low Memory class (pairwise adjusted p=0.016), and connectivity between ventral attention and limbic networks (H[3]=10.44, p=0.015, e2=0.05) was reduced in the Low Executive Class relative to the High Memory (adjusted p=0.044) and Global Average (adjusted p=0.015) classes. 

Conclusion

Midlife MetS is associated with differences in functional brain network organization, particularly reduced within-network connectivity in higher-order cognitive networks. Functional connectivity patterns were also associated with cognitive profile membership, suggesting that between network-level differences may underlie subtle cognitive risk before overt impairment.

Exploratory sex differences suggest potential variation in compensatory processes. While both males and females with MetS demonstrated reduced within-network connectivity, females exhibited relatively higher between-network connectivity and global efficiency compared to males with MetS, consistent with compensatory network reorganization. These findings raise the possibility that sex-specific biological factors, including hormonal changes during the menopausal transition, may influence functional adaptation to cardiometabolic burden.

Together, these results indicate that functional connectivity metrics may provide sensitive early markers of neural vulnerability linking cardiometabolic health to cognitive outcomes in midlife. Identifying how clustered cardiometabolic risk alters large-scale brain networks may inform targeted interventions during this critical window to support long-term cognitive and brain health.
