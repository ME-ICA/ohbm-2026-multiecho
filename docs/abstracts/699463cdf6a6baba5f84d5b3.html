Introduction

Distinguishing neural signals from physiological noise remains a central challenge in neuroimaging, particularly in the subcortex1. Extracting reliable signals from subcortical regions is inherently difficult, given their small size and low signal-to-noise ratio due to distance from head coils. This problem is exacerbated by physiological noise from nearby cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), increasing subcortical susceptibility to temporal and spatial distortions2. If not accounted for, these artificial signals may either ‘mimic’ or mask neural signals, compromising reliability and validity.

Despite widespread use of noise correction in fMRI, the optimal approach remains uncertain and methods optimized for the cortex may not be as effective within the subcortex. Traditional CSF correction pools signals from spatially distinct noise sources into a regressor that is uniformly applied across the brain. These approaches assume a uniform CSF signal; yet, if the signal is spatially heterogeneous, residual noise may persist. 

To examine this, we computed voxelwise correlations between CSF voxels (80% threshold) and the weighted global CSF time-series, revealing a distinct cortical–subcortical gradient. To better isolate this spatial variability, we developed a local CSF correction procedure that explicitly models CSF adjacent to each individual subcortical ROI. Benchmarking its effects on task and resting-state fMRI data against two global approaches, local CSF regression yields stronger affective task activation (N=40) than mean CSF time-series regression and aCompCor, across most regions and the majority of subject-level estimates. Local CSF also yields stronger subcortical resting-state functional connectivity (N=81) than mean time-series regression. Here, we provide a comprehensive validation across task and rest data, comparing local CSF correction with whole-brain approaches, assessing robustness, and evaluating motion-related contributions to CSF signal. 

Methods

Participants and fMRI Acquisition. Data were acquired on a 7T Siemens scanner at the Martinos Center, MGH, using a gradient-echo EPI BOLD sequence (TR=2.34s, voxel size = 1.1mm). Subjects with a mean framewise displacement >0.5mm were excluded. For the resting-state condition (N=81), each subject completed 3 runs, each lasting 10 minutes. For the affective task condition (N=40), subjects were presented (3s duration) with an equal proportion of aversive and neutrally valenced IAPS images3 for a total of 5 runs, each lasting 9 minutes (24 trials per run).

Regions of Interest. Fourteen subcortical regions of interest (ROIs) were derived from the Harvard-Oxford Subcortical Atlas4. Additionally, group and subject-specific PAG masks were derived from the Brainstem Navigator Toolkit5 and a semi-automated segmentation procedure, respectively.

Constructing Local CSF Masks. To estimate local, region-specific CSF signals, we defined a unique CSF mask for every subject and gray matter (GM) ROI (Fig. 2A). Each GM ROI mask was dilated by 4 voxels (4.4mm), creating a local CSF search region. This search region was then intersected with the subject-specific whole-brain CSF tissue probability map, thresholded at 80%. Voxels within the search region that met the threshold and did not overlap with the original GM ROI were classified as local CSF.

CSF Denoising Methods.

  1. Local CSF Regression. For each subject, condition (task/rest), and ROI, local CSF was estimated by averaging all voxels within the thresholded local CSF mask.

  2. Global CSF Regression. Local CSF was benchmarked against two global denoising approaches—mean CSF time-series regression and an anatomical component method, aCompCor. Here, we define a ‘global’ approach as a denoising technique that combines spatially distinct sources of noise into a regressor that is uniformly applied to all ROIs. 

    1. Mean CSF. For each subject and condition, global CSF signals were estimated by averaging all voxels within the thresholded whole-brain CSF mask.

    2. aCompCor CSF. For each subject and condition, aCompCor CSF signals were estimated using principle component analysis, taking the top 5 components from the thresholded whole-brain CSF mask. Components were constrained to the ventricles using the ALVIN mask.

Nuisance Regression Models. For each subject, condition, and ROI, three nuisance regression models were evaluated. Six head motion parameters (HMP) were removed from all models. For clarity, the corrected ROI signals were estimated as the residuals of the following models, namely:

  1. ROIx  = Local CSFx + 6 HMP + residuallocal,x → ROIcorrected local,x = residuallocal,x

  2. ROIx  = Global CSF + 6 HMP + residualglobal,x → ROIcorrected global,x = residualglobal,x

  3. ROIx  = aCompCor CSF + 6 HMP + residualaCompCor,x → ROIcorrected aCompCor,x = residualaCompCor,x

Voxelwise CSF Analysis. For each subject and condition, voxelwise spatial correlations were computed between each thresholded CSF voxel’s time-series and the weighted global CSF average to assess spatial heterogeneity. 

Resting-state Analysis. For each subject and run, Pearson’s correlation coefficients were computed for all seed regions and Fisher transformed to z-values.

Task Activation Analysis. First-level models were estimated independently for each subject, run, and CSF correction method: (1) local CSF, (2) global (i.e., mean) CSF, and (3) aCompCor CSF. Visual stimulus presentations were modeled using separate regressors for aversive and neutral trials. Effects were assessed on the following contrast: aversive > baseline.

Results

CSF Voxelwise Analysis in Rest. Voxelwise correlation maps exhibited a clear cortical–subcortical gradient, with CSF voxels located closer to cortical surfaces yielding stronger correlations with the weighted global CSF signal (Fig. 1A-B). Unsupervised clustering of the voxelwise map further supported the presence of this anatomically organized gradient (cortex: r̄ = 0.199; subcortex: r̄ = 0.091; Fig. 1C).

As a robustness check, we assessed whether motion was driving the observed gradient by regressing out six HMP from the CSF time-series before recomputing the voxelwise map. The motion-corrected CSF map was highly correlated with the uncorrected map (r̄ = .94), indicating that the observed gradient reflects physiological variability rather than motion artifacts. To evaluate spatial stability, we extracted the top 20% of CSF voxels from both maps and computed a Dice coefficient, which revealed strong spatial agreement (Dice = 0.85), providing further evidence that the observed CSF variance is stable with and without including motion parameters. Last, voxelwise correlations between uncorrected CSF signals and motion parameters clustered near zero (range: –0.002 to 0.008), providing further evidence of negligible CSF–motion coupling.

CSF Voxelwise Analysis in Task. We applied the same CSF voxelwise analysis to task data, revealing CSF maps that strongly replicated the cortical–subcortical gradient observed in rest data (Fig. 1D-F). Further, rest and task maps were highly correlated across time-series variants (non–motion-regressed CSF: r = .84; motion-regressed CSF: r = .87). 

Task-activation Analysis. Among all 16 subcortical ROIs examined, only two (r-amygdala and PAG) showed numerically greater activation following  global (mean) CSF correction compared to local, with differences only significant in the right amygdala (p < .05, FDR-corrected; Fig. 2C). In contrast, local CSF correction did not yield statistically significant differences but produced numerically higher z-scores in the remaining 14 ROIs (mean Δz = +0.40, range = +0.08 to +0.65; mean Cohen’s d = 0.24, range = 0.14 to 0.42), with this advantage observed in the majority of subject-level observations across ROIs (range: 50-70%, median = 59%).

Among all 16 subcortical ROIs examined, only 5 (r-pallidum, r-hippocamous, r-putamen, r-amygdala and PAG) showed numerically greater activation following aCompCor CSF correction compared to local, with differences only significant in the r-amygdala (p < .05, FDR-corrected). In contrast, the l-amygdala showed greater activation following local CSF correction (p < .05, FDR-corrected). Consistent with the directional trend observed previously, although local CSF correction did not yield statistically significant differences, it yielded numerically higher z-scores in the remaining 10 ROIs (mean Δz = +0.31, range = +0.07 to +0.53; mean Cohen's d = 0.17, range = 0.10 to 0.25), with this advantage observed in the majority of subject-level observations across ROIs (range: 45-70%  median = 62%). 

Taken together, the convergence of effect direction, magnitude, and prevalence across ROIs, subjects, and global correction methods suggests that local CSF correction may meaningfully enhance activation sensitivity in subcortical regions. 

Resting-state Functional Connectivity. Mean CSF correction yielded substantially more negative correlations than local CSF. A paired two-tailed t-test with FDR correction (α < 0.05) identified significant differences in edge strength for 80% of all connections. Among these significant edges, 80% exhibited stronger functional connectivity following local CSF correction (Fig. 2B).

In contrast, this directional trend did not replicate when comparing local CSF to aCompCor. A paired two-tailed t-test with FDR correction identified significant differences in edge strength for 85% of all connections. Among these significant edges, 100% exhibited stronger functional connectivity following aCompCor (Fig. 2B). 

Conclusion

The implications of these results can be summarized in a few key conclusions. The most fundamental finding is that CSF signal is not spatially uniform, but is instead spatially heterogeneous. Additionally, this spatial variability is anatomically meaningful, following a distinct anatomically organized cortical-subcortical gradient, which is robust to motion-related effects and is preserved across cross experimental conditions of task and rest. As such, these findings call into question both the underlying assumptions and effectiveness of traditional CSF denoising approaches. Because capturing reliable and robust signals from the subcortex is inherently challenging, selecting a denoising method with the greatest efficacy is critical. To examine the efficacy of traditional CSF denoising models, we developed a local CSF method that explicitly models region-specific CSF signals for each individual ROI. We hypothesized that our local approach would better account for the spatial and temporal variability in CSF signal, thereby improving sensitivity and yielding a more precise representation of subcortical neural signals. 

Using both task and resting-state data, we benchmarked our local CSF approach against two global denoising methods: mean CSF time-series regression and aCompCor. Within task data, although not significant, the local CSF model yielded numerically greater activation in several ROIs compared to the global approaches. Given the inherent difficulty of detecting robust signals within small subcortical structures, even modest improvements in sensitivity are meaningful and underscore the value of approaches capable of capturing larger effect estimates. Within resting-state data, local CSF yielded stronger functional connectivity compared to mean CSF time-series regression, but not when compared to CompCor. While it still remains to be determined whether increased or decreased connectivity is most consistent with the underlying ground-truth, it is a helpful start to know that there are differences according to the extent to which local CSF estimates are taken into consideration.

Our study provides important insight into the meaningful physiological variability of the CSF signal and its influence on fMRI estimates. Although we observed a directional trend in the task data, we are not yet in a position to recommend the local CSF method over the global models examined here. This is because we are limited in our ability to definitively determine whether the observed differences in activation reflect residual noise that was insufficiently removed or neural signal that was recovered. We argue that, even in light of this uncertainty, continually challenging and scrutinizing our current denoising methods is essential for advancing neuroscience toward greater reproducibility, raising methodological standards, and more rigorously evaluating the underlying assumptions of the approaches we use.
