Introduction

Teacher-child interactions provide a crucial social context that can influence children’s learning, yet these social factors are seldom studied in the classroom. Much remains unknown about the neural mechanisms that underpin teachers’ social influences on children’s learning, although Interpersonal neural synchrony (INS) between adults and children has previously been associated with improved learning. The aim of this study was to explore the relationship between teacher-child interpersonal neural synchrony and children’s novel word learning in a dialogic reading (DR) paradigm. 

Methods

Whilst undergoing dual-electroencephalography (EEG), 27 teacher-child dyads (children: N=27, 3-7 years, 12 males & 15 females, Mage=59.93 months & SD=16.05; teachers: N=10, 21-36 years, Mage= 31.96 years & SD=5.48,1 male & 9 females) completed a storybook reading task. The storybook contained four novel object words and teachers were instructed to use their own pedagogical style to help the children learn the names of novel objects and their functions. Teachers’ prompts were scored using a DR coding scheme and children’s performance was assessed via a four alternative forced choice procedure. Children also completed a local version of the Peabody picture vocabulary test and teachers completed an adapted version of the Teacher-Student Relationship inventory after interacting with the child. Teacher-child INS was calculated using the weighted phase lag index, taking  pairwise connections between 5 central EEG channels. 

Results

A multiple regression analysis (R²=0.56, p=.004) revealed that INS (p=0.048), DR score (p=0.024) and vocabulary (p=0.017) significantly predicted novel word learning, whereas age and teacher-rated experience of the interaction did not. 

Conclusion

These findings support previous literature on the association between INS and learning in preschool-aged children, but additionally highlight the importance of teacher scaffolding (as compared to their subjective experience of the interaction). Future research should seek to further clarify the key aspects of teacher-child interactions that support better learning in children.
