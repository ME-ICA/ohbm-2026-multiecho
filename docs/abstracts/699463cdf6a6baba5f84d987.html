Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) represents the most prevalent etiology of dementia worldwide. Current epidemiological data indicate that there are over 55 million individuals living with dementia globally, among whom approximately 60–70% are affected by Alzheimer’s disease.It is recognized as one of the leading causes of mortality, particularly among older populations, and remains a condition without disease-modifying therapies capable of halting or reversing its progression. Available treatments offer only symptomatic relief with limited efficacy. Therefore, elucidating the underlying mechanisms of AD is of critical importance for advancing therapeutic development and improving clinical outcomes. 

Traditionally, while causal mediation analysis serves as a predominant statistical approach for investigating the causal mechanisms of  AD, it often lacks sufficient sensitivity. Batmanghelich et al. (2016) constructed a probabilistic graphical model to analyze the "gene-brain-behavior" network. While their model provides valuable interpretability into biological mechanisms, its high complexity and substantial computational demands hinder its practical application to real-world datasets. Furthermore, Yin et al. (2024) developed a Bayesian network model to analyze the "gene-transcriptome-behavior" pathway. Although utilizing transcriptomic data for dimensionality reduction of genetic information, this model suffers from high computational complexity, limited interpretability of causal effects, and requires further simplification for broader utility. 

To address these limitations, we propose a novel approach for AD causal mechanism analysis. Our method involves constructing a Bayesian network that explicitly integrates "gene-brain-behavior" associations. This framework is designed to localize AD-affected brain regions, thereby enhancing the interpretability of causal pathways in AD and facilitating the identification of potential therapeutic targets.

Methods

We localize AD-relevant brain regions by constructing a Bayesian network that integrates "gene–brain region–behavior" relationships. Within the Bayesian network structure, we model the "gene–brain region" pathway using linear regression, and the "brain region–behavior" pathway using logistic regression. These two processes are combined within a unified Bayesian network framework by conditioning on informative brain regions. The model is implemented and analyzed using the PyMC library with the No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS).

 For validation, we first designed four sets of simulated data corresponding to the following scenarios: (1) genes affect brain regions and brain regions affect behavior; (2) genes affect brain regions but brain regions do not affect behavior; (3) genes do not affect brain regions but brain regions affect behavior; and (4) genes do not affect brain regions and brain regions do not affect behavior. On these simulations, we compared our method with traditional causal mediation analysis. The results demonstrate that our approach achieves higher sensitivity and is able to identify valid pathways that the conventional method fails to detect.

For real-world validation, we employed the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) dataset, which includes 4,394 individuals: 996 with AD, 1,735 with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and 1,663 healthy controls (HC). We evaluated the brain region localization performance of our Bayesian network model across 18 brain regions of fMRI volumes with APOE genetic data.

Results

A comparative analysis between our method and causal mediation analysis revealed distinct advantages in both simulated and real-world data. In simulated datasets where genes influence brain regions and brain regions subsequently affect behavior, causal mediation analysis failed to identify valid pathways, whereas our approach successfully detected them, with results aligning more closely with the ground-truth assumptions of the data.

In validation using the real-world ADNI dataset, among the 18 brain regions examined, our method identified 11 regions as forming valid pathways. Notably, these findings have been biologically validated in prior medical literature.

Conclusion

We localize AD-relevant brain regions by constructing a Bayesian network that integrates "gene–brain region–behavior" associations. Subsequently, we will leverage these brain regions to classify AD-associated genes, thereby enabling disease subtyping. Our approach is designed to be generalizable to a broader range of diseases, facilitating effective medical localization and stratification for various conditions. This framework aims to provide robust support for precision medicine and personalized healthcare across multiple disorders.
