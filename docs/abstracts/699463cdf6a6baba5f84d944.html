Introduction

To ensure stable perception of visual objects the brain requires sensorimotor integration. The internal forward model has been suggested to facilitate behavior, e.g., during sensorimotor integration by providing a neural representation and top-down predictive model of our interaction with the environment. Previously, the neural correlate of the internal forward model was found to operate rhythmically in the theta frequency band. This study investigates the rhythmic nature of the visuo-motor system and how brain activity may be coupled to smooth pursuit eye movements and saccades occurring physiologically during smooth pursuit. 

Methods

We simultaneously recorded eye movements and magnetoencephalography (MEG) data while participants performed a continuous triangular smooth pursuit task. We analyzed the rhythmicity of saccades occurring during smooth pursuit eye movements and correlated it to the mean eye position error. Based on the MEG data, we reconstructed the source activity for the 230 atlas parcels of the reduced Human Connectome Project (HCP) atlas using a Linearly Constrained Minimum Variance beamformer with unit-noise-gain constraint and an individual singleshell headmodel. We then computed the coherence as a measure of connectivity between task-related measures (target position and eye velocity) and cortical brain activity of all parcels (reduced HCP atlas) in 22 healthy participants. Finally, we performed a dependent t-test for directed ramps (only considering eye movements to either left or right) against mixed ramps (considering eye movements to left and right; serving as a baseline). We used a two-sided Monte Carlo t-test with a significance threshold of 0.05 and a frequency-based cluster-permutation to correct for multiple comparisons.

Results

Saccades exhibited a preferred intersaccadic interval (ISI) of 150–300 ms, corresponding to the theta frequency band and showed a negative rank correlation between their rhythmicity and the mean eye position error. Significantly increased coherences between eye movement related measures (stimulus position and eye velocity) and brain activity were found in key areas of the oculomotor brain network, including visual area V5, the somatosensory cortex, premotor cortex, and parietal regions. Most effects showed strong lateralization between leftward and rightward eye movements.

Conclusion

The networks localized by our coherence analysis correspond to those identified in functional MRI studies during pursuit and emphasize the role of rhythmicity for the maintenance of a stable representation of visual motion. Specifically, the periodic changes in stimulus position (0–2 Hz) seem to be encoded in sensorimotor and premotor areas. In addition to this bottom-up effect, we observed rhythmically occurring saccades in the eye velocity signal (2–7 Hz), which we found to be coherent to the brain activity in area V5 and the parietal cortex. This result suggests the involvement of a top-down process, which is also supported by our observation of a significantly better overall eye movement performance for stronger saccadic rhythmicity. Furthermore, the eye velocity is linked to area V5 and further visual areas in the alpha band (9–13 Hz), potentially optimizing the state of neural excitability and therefore visual perception. In sum, these findings indicate a rhythmic top-down regulation of saccade occurrence during pursuit, that is linked with theta activity in posterior hubs of the oculomotor network.
