Introduction

The brain is one of the most metabolically demanding organs, relying on constant nutrient supply to support signalling, housekeeping and repair processes. The brain stores virtually no energy, making it vulnerable to perturbations in blood flow and metabolic supply.1 Energy metabolism pathways use nutrients such as glucose and oxygen to generate ATP as fuel for processes like action potentials and synaptic transmission. Importantly, these pathways also provide key precursors for cellular biosynthesis and antioxidant defense, notably via the pentose phosphate pathway. Metabolism is therefore integral to the brain’s structural and functional integrity. Importantly, metabolic processes are dynamic, adapting to developmental demands and shifts in substrate availability across the lifespan.2

While positron emission tomography imaging has mapped glucose uptake and oxygen consumption in the human brain,3 we lack detailed mapping of specific downstream metabolic pathways such as glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation across the cortex. Understanding regional energy pathway profiles is essential because metabolic vulnerability may underlie the selective patterns of neurodegeneration observed in aging and disease.

Here, we employ neuroimaging transcriptomics to map energy metabolism at pathway-specific resolution. We investigate the cortical distribution of key metabolic pathways and their relationship to multi-scale structural and functional cortical features. Finally, we explore the developmental trajectory of these energy pathways in human and macaque. This cross-species comparison allows us to identify conserved metabolic programs and species-specific adaptations.

Methods

We produced maps of five key energy metabolism processes: glycolysis, pentose phosphate pathway, tricarboxylic acid cycle, oxidative phosphorylation and lactate metabolism using curated gene sets and whole-brain transcriptomics. We retrieved pathway-specific gene sets from functional gene annotation databases including Gene Ontology and Reactome. Genes consistently annotated in both databases were retained to produce pathway gene expression matrices. Whole-brain gene expression data were then retrieved from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (6 post-mortem brains, 1 female, ages 24-57) and parcellated into 400 cortical regions in the Schaefer parcellation using the abagen package. Pathway-specifi­c gene expression matrices were then produced and averaged across genes to create a single mean gene expression map for each energy pathway.

The spatial distribution of pathway gene expression maps was examined across canonical cortical structural classes and functional networks, including von Economo cytoarchitectonic classes, the sensory-fugal hierarchy, and resting-state functional networks.4 We then related pathway maps to a set of multi-scale cortical features from in vivo metabolism, neurophysiological oscillations, cell composition, laminar organization and network topology. Group-averaged PET maps of in vivo metabolism including glucose uptake, oxygen consumption, cerebral blood flow and glycolytic index3 were obtained from the neuromaps package. Similarly, resting state magnetoencephalography data for 6 canonical frequency bands (delta to high gamma) from the Human Connectome Project S1200 were obtained using neuromaps. Cell type gene expression maps, an estimate of cell type composition, were produced for major cell types in the brain (i.e., excitatory and inhibitory neurons, astrocytes, endothelial cells, microglia, oligodendrocytes and oligodendrocyte precursor cells) using data from Wagstyl et al., 2024.5 Cortical layer maps were also produced using layer-specific expression of marker genes as a proxy for laminar organization. Structural and functional connectivity matrices from HCP S900 were used to compute measures of regional importance in the cortical network (e.g., strength and degree) using the bctpy package. To examine the statistical significance of the spatial correlations between maps spatial autocorrelation-preserving null models (10,000 permutations) were employed. To ensure the robustness of the results, pathway gene expression maps were recreated using a lower resolution Schaefer-100 parcellation.

Finally, we investigated the lifespan trajectories of energy pathway expression using the BrainSpan RNA-sequencing dataset, which profiles brain gene expression from 8 weeks post-conception to 40 years of age (42 donors, 26 regions, 16 cortical). Samples were grouped into eight developmental stages: early fetal, mid fetal, late fetal, infancy, early childhood, late childhood, adolescence, and adulthood. Gene expression was log2 transformed and normalized using the upper quartile method and averaged across all genes for each pathway. To identify developmental shifts in metabolism, pathway gene expression within each developmental group was plotted across all regions. Lifespan trajectories were replicated using the BrainSpan microarray dataset. Furthermore, we examined energy pathway expression across the macaque lifespan using the rhesus macaque developmental brain transcriptome from the PsychENCODE dataset, spanning embryonic day 60 to 11 years of age. Samples were grouped into five developmental stages including: fetal, infancy, juvenile, adolescence and adulthood and the trajectories were plotted as above.

Results

Energy metabolism pathways exhibit heterogeneous expression across the cortex, with the most marked difference between pathways mainly involved in ATP production versus biosynthesis (Figure 1b). Glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation show greater expression in the motor cortex and lower expression in the visual cortex, in alignment with the second functional connectivity gradient that distinguishes among these two unimodal cortices (Figure 1c, d). Conversely, the anabolic pentose phosphate pathway shows greater expression in the primary sensory cortices and particularly the visual cortex, aligning with the first functional connectivity gradient that separates the primary cortices from higher order association regions.

Multi-scale analyses revealed distinct correlates for each pathway. PET-derived glucose uptake correlates significantly with the pentose phosphate pathway (rho = 0.42, pspin,FDR = 0.02) and lactate metabolism (rho = 0.53, pspin,FDR = 0.005), while no significant correlation was observed with glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation (Figure 1e). The glycolytic index, a proxy for non-oxidative glucose metabolism, correlates with glycolysis (rho = 0.49, pspin,FDR = 0.047) and lactate (rho = 0.53, pspin,FDR = 0.04). The energy pathways most strongly align with the MEG beta band, reflecting the greater expression seen in the motor cortex. With respect to cell-type distribution, excitatory neurons show significant correlation with the glycolysis (rho = 0.46, pspin,FDR = 0.001) and oxidative phosphorylation (rho=0.42, pspin,FDR = 0.006). Inhibitory neurons show positive correlations across both ATP-producing and anabolic energy pathways. We further show that these relationships are reflected in individual cell markers such as parvalbumin as a marker of fast spiking neurons and Betz cell marker genes. Regarding laminar organization, glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation show significant correlations with cortical layer 5 (both rho ≈ 0.70, pspin,FDR = 0.001), while the pentose phosphate pathway, the tricarboxylic acid cycle and lactate metabolism most positively align with cortical layer 4 (Figure 1e).

Finally, we show that energy pathways exhibit unique expression trajectories from the fetal stage to adulthood, that closely track the developmental milestones. Notably, the main ATP-producing pathways including glycolysis, tricarboxylic acid cycle, oxidative phosphorylation and lactate metabolism show a sharp increase from the fetal stage to infancy and peak in childhood, with oxidative phosphorylation declining by adolescence (Figure 2a). Conversely, the pentose phosphate pathway shows a sharp postnatal decline and further decreases in later life. Given the importance of ketone bodies as an obligatory fuel in the infant brain, we also show that the expression of genes involved in ketone body utilization increases from fetal stage to infancy and decreases in early childhood. Lifespan analysis in the rhesus macaque revealed largely similar developmental trajectories across the energy pathways (Figure 2c).

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that cortical regions possess distinct energy profiles with a consistent dichotomy between primarily energy-producing pathways (glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation) enriched in the motor cortex, and anabolic pathways (pentose phosphate pathway) enriched in sensory cortices. The tricarboxylic acid cycle, contributing to both oxidative metabolism and cellular biosynthesis, shows features of both metabolic functions.

This spatial organization reflects the interplay between cellular composition, laminar architecture, and functional specialization. At the cellular level, excitatory neurons correlate with glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation, consistent with glutamatergic signaling accounting for most cortical energy expenditure. Inhibitory neurons show significant alignment with all energy pathways. Given the fast-spiking activity of these neuronal population, this likely reflects both their high energy demand and cellular defense mechanisms against oxidative damage. At the laminar level, the pentose phosphate pathway, tricarboxylic acid cycle and lactate metabolism align with cortical layer 4, the main sensory input layer, suggesting rapid energy supply and greater anabolic requirements for robust sensory encoding by maintaining synaptic integrity. Conversely, glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation correlate with cortical layer 5, the main output layer, reflecting the substantial energy demands of large pyramidal neurons such as Betz cells and their long-range efferent projections.

Finaly, we show that energy metabolism pathways have diverse expression trajectories across the lifespan that reflect nutrient availability and tissue synthesis needs. Pathways mainly involved in ATP production such as glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation peak in childhood, paralleling synaptic development and elevated glucose/oxygen consumption postnatally. On the other hand, the pentose phosphate pathway shows a sharp postnatal decline followed by a continued decrease through adulthood, in line with reduced neuronal proliferation and tissue generation capacity and likely hinting at decreased antioxidant defense in later life. Finally, ketone body utilization shows a sharp increase in infancy followed by decline in early childhood, in line with elevated circulating ketone bodies during the nursing period, followed by a metabolic shift to glucose during weaning. Notably, lifespan analysis in the rhesus macaque revealed similar developmental trajectories for most energy pathways, suggesting evolutionary conservation of these fundamental metabolic programs across primates.

Metabolism is fundamental to brain development, plasticity, network organization and function. The cortical expression maps of metabolic pathways provide a complementary perspective on metabolic programs that shape brain’s structural and functional characteristics and their dynamics across the lifespan beyond the existing PET data. Collectively, these results highlight the complexity and the heterogeneity of the metabolic pathway in the brain.
