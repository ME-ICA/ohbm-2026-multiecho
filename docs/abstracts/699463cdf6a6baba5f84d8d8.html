Introduction

Epilepsy is traditionally defined as a brain disorder marked by a predisposition to recurrent unprovoked seizures, typically considered as two or more seizures occurring at least 24 hours apart. These seizures are spontaneous and synchronized, often tending to propagate, and their repetition can cause significant brain damage.

It is one of the most prevalent neurological condition, affecting approximately 70 million people worldwide[4], and is frequently associated with Focal Cortical Dysplasia of type 2 (FCD2). Indeed, FCD2 is an anomaly of neurons migration during brain development often responsible for drug-resistant epilepsies [2]. In our work, we focuses on patients having a FCD2 located in the motor regions (precentral gyrus).

The accurate diagnosis of epilepsy remains challenging because seizures can result from various underlying cerebral causes and usually lack visible physical manifestations [4]. As a result, a definitive diagnosis is often made only after extended follow-up. Furthermore, epilepsy surgery poses significant challenges for the medical community, especially in identifying the precise location of epileptic foci, which can be linked to anomalies in cortical folding. In this article, we present a deep learning approach designed to address this challenge.

Methods

Our approach is based on a beta-VAE (Variational Auto-Encoder) optimized for detecting folding anomalies [1]. The model works as follows: an image is input to the VAE, passes through an encoder that maps it to a low-dimensional latent space, and the decoder then reconstructs the original image from this latent representation. During optimization, we performed a grid search to determine the best latent space dimension, among other parameters, and ultimately selected n = 75.

We used three datasets in our study: UKB, containing 42,000 subjects over 50 years old from the general population [3], which was used to train the beta-VAE; HCP, with 1,113 younger subjects from the general population; and epilepsyPBS, comprising three groups: 19 ”controls” (subjects without epilepsy), 13 ”irm neg” (epileptic patients without detectable MRI lesions), and 25 ”irm pos” (epileptic patients with MRI-detectable lesions) [2]. All patients in epilepsyPBS have Focal Cortical Dysplasia type 2 (FCD2). Based on this pathology, we expect prominent anomaly signals in the motor region, particularly the precentral sulcus.

We first applied the beta-VAE to the right central sulcus to evaluate its performance on a single sulcal region. We then visualized the latent space using a 2D UMAP projection to examine how subjects from different datasets are distributed. For each subject, we computed the cross-entropy loss (reconstruction error) between the original image and its reconstruction by the decoder. The histogram of reconstruction errors is shown in Figure 1.

To identify epileptic foci, we repeated this procedure across all sulcal regions. For each region, we defined an epileptic metric such that higher values indicate regions closer to the epileptic foci. In particular, we expect this metric to be highest in the motor region (precentral sulcus) of the brain.

Results

First, Figure 1 shows that, on average, epileptic subjects exhibit higher reconstruction errors than both the ”controls” and the subjects from the general population. Moreover, when plotting the reconstruction error histograms for all 56 sulcal regions, we observed that this pattern holds across all regions. This indicates that our beta-VAE has greater difficulty reconstructing the sulci of epileptic subjects. It is also important to note that anomalies may arise in regions other than the one where the dysplasia is located, due to interactions between brain areas. This may explain why a systematic shift between ”controls” and epileptic subjects is observed in all regions, not only in the motor region.

The Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance provides a statistical measure of distributional shift between the probability distribution of a test dataset and that of the training data [5]. It is therefore an appropriate metric to quantify how far two distributions are from one another. In our case, we defined an anomaly metric based on the average of the Kolmogorov–Smirnov distances between the ”controls” and the ”irm neg” distributions, and between the ”controls” and the ”irm pos” distributions. The results are presented in Figure 2.

We also computed the mean Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance (across all sulcal regions) between ”controls” and ”UKB”, obtaining a value of 0.42, which is among the smallest values in Figure 2. This confirms that, in the reconstruction error space, the ”controls” are, on average, closer to the general population than to epileptic subjects. Additionally, the left hemisphere appears to exhibit more anomalies than the right, and the precentral sulcus stands out as a particularly anomalous region, consistent with our hypothesis that significant anomaly signals should appear in the motor region, in particular in the precentral sulcus.

Conclusion

In this work, we showed that reconstruction errors produced by a beta-VAE trained on healthy cortical folding provide a robust signal for identifying anomalies associated with focal cortical dysplasia and drug-resistant epilepsy. By analysing each sulcal region independently, we observed systematic shifts in the reconstruction error distributions for epileptic subjects relative to controls, findings reinforced by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov–based anomaly metric. These results indicate that epileptic alterations extend beyond the primary lesion and can affect broader cortical regions.

Our approach, therefore, offers an automated, fully data-driven framework for highlighting atypical sulcal patterns and has potential for a group of patients. However, it remains necessary to determine whether the localization of dysplasia can be identified for each patient, which is the focus of the next stage

of this work.

Finally, we are currently preprocessing a database of more than 1,000 epileptic patients, which will allow us to evaluate the potential of our approach to detect localized anomalies anywhere in the brain and help clinicians focus on regions more likely to contain epileptogenic abnormalities.
