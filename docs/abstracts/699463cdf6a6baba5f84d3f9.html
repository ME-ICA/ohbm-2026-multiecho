Introduction

Severe mental disorders are often conceptualized within a neurodevelopmental framework, where early biological processes influence brain development and increase vulnerability to structural alterations persisting in adulthood. Large-scale neuroimaging studies consistently report brain abnormalities in severe mental illness, including changes in subcortical structure. The caudate nucleus has shown inconsistent findings, with studies reporting both volume reductions and increases across patient groups, illness stages, and medication status1-3. Evidence from perinatal research indicates that adverse early-life conditions, can have long-term impacts on caudate nucleus volumes in adulthood2. Structural variation in the caudate may therefore reflect the interplay of early developmental risk, genetic predisposition, and later disease-related processes, making it a critical region for testing neurodevelopmental hypotheses4.

The placenta plays a central role in shaping the intrauterine environment. Placental efficiency, quantified as the birthweight-to-placental-weight ratio (BW:PW), has emerged as a biologically meaningful marker of placental adaptation5. Our previous genome-wide association study demonstrated that placental efficiency is a genetically distinct trait enriched for monoaminergic pathways and placental tissue expression, suggesting relevance for neurodevelopment5. Monoaminergic signaling has also been strongly implicated in the genetic architecture of severe mental disorders and shows pronounced transcriptional enrichment in the caudate nucleus4. However, it remains unclear whether genetic propensity for placental efficiency is associated with adult brain structure, or whether such associations differ by susceptibility to neurodevelopmental perturbations such as severe mental illness. We therefore investigated whether genetic propensity for placental efficiency is associated with caudate nucleus volume in adults with SCZ or BPD and in healthy controls.

Methods

We performed a genome-wide association study (GWAS) of the BW:PW ratio from 63,894 at term infants using the Norwegian Mother Father Child cohort study (MoBa). This GWAS was used to compute placental efficiency genomic risk scores (eGRS) using PRSice v2, in participants of the Thematically Organized Psychosis (TOP) study. The total sample for eGRS analysis was N = 1,317 participants (Male = 694, Female = 623), with 753 healthy controls, 290 patients with BPD, and 274 patients diagnosed with SCZ. 

MRI acquisition and processing:
Participants underwent T1-weighted structural MRI scanning at Oslo University Hospital (Ullevål) between 2005 and 2019 using three scanners (1.5T Siemens Magnetom Sonata; 3T GE Signa HDxt; 3T GE Discovery MR750). Structural images were processed with the Sequence-Adaptive Multimodal SEGmentation (SAMSEG) toolbox included in FreeSurfer v7.2. We performed visual quality control on all segmented images, after which 26 participants were excluded.

Statistical analysis
We first used linear regression to examine differences in eGRS (p-value threshold = 1×10⁻⁵) between patients and healthy controls, including interactions with sex. We further examined associations between eGRS  and subcortical brain structure volumes in the caudate nucleus in addition to interaction effects between eGRS and diagnosis, and second order interaction between eGRS, sex and diagnosis, while controlling for age, sex, and ICV. 

Results

We discovered a significant interaction effect between sex and patient control status, where male controls exhibit a higher eGRS compared to male patients (b = .125, p < .05). Both BPD (b = 0.26 mm3, p < 0.05) and SCZ (b = 0.42 cm2, p < 0.001) were associated with larger caudate volume relative to controls.  We observed a significant interaction between eGRS and SCZ diagnosis on caudate nucleus volume (b = 139 cm3, p < .05). Furthermore, though not statistically significant, we discovered a statistical tendency for the interaction effect between eGRS and BPD on caudate volumes (b = 89.2 cm3, p =.091), indicating a positive trend of eGRS on caudate volumes also in BPD patients. These interactions indicate that higher eGRS was associated with larger caudate nucleus volumes in individuals with SCZ, relative to healthy controls.

Conclusion

We observed significant interaction effects between eGRS and SCZ on caudate nucleus volume, suggesting a potential disease-specific sensitivity of this region to placental influences. Placental efficiency reflects the placenta’s capacity to adapt to fetal demands, and genetic variation in this trait may influence neurodevelopment during sensitive prenatal periods.  Given the central role of monoaminergic signaling in both placental biology5 and striatal development4, the caudate nucleus may represent a convergence point for placental and neurodevelopmental processes. The association between eGRS and caudate volume may reflect genetically mediated mechanisms related to placental adaptation, potentially resulting in differential susceptibility in the context of SCZ. Our findings provide evidence for long term genetic impacts that propagate throughout development and may have downstream consequences in adulthood. Our findings align with prior evidence implicating the caudate as a neurodevelopmentally vulnerable structure in psychotic disorders1, 2. Overall, our results provide support for a link between genetic propensity for placental efficiency and adult caudate nucleus volumes in severe mental illness.
