Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a severe neurological condition associated with multifaceted impairments. 

At the neural level, MS is characterized by white matter lesions that disrupt structural connectivity, thereby modulating functional connectivity. Investigating altered connectivity patterns in MS may provide a better understanding of the disorder. Relapsing-remitting MS may be related to decreased DMN connectivity and increased sensorimotor and visual network connectivity.1 The results of a multimodal study suggest that disconnectivity in MS affects multiple task-positive networks, such as those involved in attention and memory.2

Despite progress in characterizing abnormal network connectivity patterns in MS, the diversity within this patient cohort has received less attention. There is a lack of data linking the clinically relevant parameters in MS to the corresponding network connectivity properties. The current study aims to establish links between the functional connectivity of regions of interest (ROIs) belonging to cognitive, visual, and sensorimotor networks and assessment of cognitive function and quality of life.

Methods

The study included 18 patients with relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis (15 women, 3 men; mean age ± SD: 34.4 ± 10.6 years). Cognitive functions were assessed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) and Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE). No significant cognitive decline was detected in the participants (minimum MoCA score = 27). Somatic and mental well-being were assessed using the SF-36 (The Short Form-36) brief questionnaire on quality of life.

The study was approved by the Local Ethics Committee of Al-Farabi Kazakh National University, approval № IRB-A843 (IRB00010790 al-Farabi Kazakh National University IRB #1).

fMRI data were collected at the National Center for Neurosurgery (Astana, Kazakhstan) using a 3T GE SIGNA Architect scanner. T1-weighted structural images were obtained using a 3D fast spoiled gradient echo (3D FSPGR) sequence (TR/TE = 7.2/2.8 ms; FA = 12°; voxel size 0.5×0.5×0.5 mm³). Resting-state functional data included 200 volumes recorded using gradient recalled echo-planar imaging (GR-EPI) (TR/TE = 3000/30 ms; FA = 90°; voxel size 1.88×1.88×3 mm³).

Data preprocessing and analysis were performed in CONN 22.v2407 (MATLAB). The first 10 volumes were discarded. Functional data were then aligned to the first volume, susceptibility-corrected, slice-timed corrected, and normalized to MNI space with resampling to 2×2×2 mm³ based on tissue segmentation of a co-registered structural image. Ouliers were identified using ART (framewise displacement > 0.5 mm or global BOLD signal changes > 5σ) and regressed out of the data. The denoising model included linear and quadratic trends, 5 CompCor components from white matter and 5 components from cerebrospinal fluid, as well as Friston-12 motion parameters. The data were further filtered with a 0.01–0.08 Hz bandpass filter. No participant exhibited excessive head motion.

The analysis included all ROIs from the HPC-ICA atlas related to the frontoparietal network, dorsal attention network, language network, visual network, and sensorimotor network (19 ROIs in total). At the first level, functional connectivity was assessed pairwise between selected ROIs. Connectivity strength was represented by Fisher-transformed (Z-transformed) pairwise correlation coefficients within a general linear model (GLM) estimated separately for each ROI pair.

At the second level, a separate multiple regression analysis was performed for each psychometric variable. Each GLM included gender and age covariates, as well as one main regressor of interest (MoCA, MMSE, SF-36 somatic component, and SF-36 mental component). Threshold-Free Cluster Enhancement (TFCE) with 1000 iterations of randomization of residuals was used for inference at the cluster level. Results were considered statistically significant at pFWE < 0.01 (TFCE-score).

Results

A higher MMSE score was associated with greater connectivity within four clusters. The most significant findings included connectivity in the following pairs: left-right lateral sensorimotor cortex (t(12)=9.92, p-FDR<0.001), left lateral prefrontal cortex-left posterior parietal cortex (t(12)=5.41, p-FDR=0.007), and left-right lateral sensorimotor cortex (t(12)=5.41, p-FDR<0.001). The cluster of functional connectivity positively related to the MoCA score involved a single connectivity: left-right inferior parietal sulcus (t(12)=6.05, p-FDR=0.003).

An SF-36 mental score was negatively related to a single connectivity cluster including the right frontal eye field-right posterior superior temporal gyrus connection (t(12)=-8.21, p-FDR<0.001).  SF-36 physical score demonstrated a positive association with a visual network connectivity cluster featuring left-right lateral visual area connection (t(12)=7.08, p-FDR=0.003) and a negative association with another cluster including the right frontal eye field-right posterior superior temporal gyrus connection (t(12)=-6.70, p-FDR=0.003).

Conclusion

The current study provides new data on the relationships between resting-state functional connectivity, general cognitive abilities, and quality of life in patients with MS. More specifically, overall cognitive functions, assessed using both MoCA and MMSE, were positively related to the connectivity between the left and right sensorimotor cortex. Some additional findings cover the connectivity values within the frontoparietal and dorsal attention networks. Thus, greater intrinsic connectivities in cognitive and sensorimotor networks may accompany relatively preserved cognitive abilities.

The positive relationship between connectivity within networks subserving high-level cognition and indicators of cognitive performance seems logical. Similarly, the link between interhemispheric sensorimotor cortex connectivity and cognitive functions is noteworthy. This connection may be driven by greater overall integrity of white matter tracts in patients with more prominent interhemispheric connections, particularly those underlying cerebello-thalamic and thalamo-cortical communication, which integrate both cognitive and motor components.³

Regarding quality of life, both the physical and mental components of patients' well-being were positively correlated with visual network connectivity, while they were negatively associated with connectivity between the language and dorsal attention network nodes, specifically the frontal eye field and posterior superior temporal sulcus.

Enhanced or preserved visual network connectivity may more effectively compensate for potential neurological impairments in other domains, particularly motor functions, thereby supporting balance and gait. Normally, the activity in the frontal eye field and posterior superior temporal sulcus is not positively correlated.4 Therefore, one may suggest that an increase in the connection strength in this pair reflects recruiting of an external region to a failing network, possibly due to a lack of normal intra-network connections. Given that, augmented connectivity in this pair might occur in patients with a greater disruption in brain network connectivity. Overall, our results suggest that stronger connectivity within well-known networks is a marker of better quality of life, while inter-network connectivity may indicate poorer well-being.
