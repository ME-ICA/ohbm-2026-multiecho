Introduction

The corpus callosum (CC) is the largest interhemispheric commissure. Histological studies of the midsagittal CC show substantial microstructural heterogeneity: although most of the fibers are myelinated, the diameter and packing density of the fibers vary along the anterior-posterior axis. The genu contains densely packed axons of smaller caliber, with substantial minority of them unmyelinated, whereas the axons of the body are sparsely packed, large-diameter and myelinated, and the splenium containing a mixture of both.1 Using multi-echo T2 magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with multi-T2 fitting and a 10-region CC parcellation scheme,1 we reported that the intermediate T2 relaxation component reflecting intra/extracellular water (the geometric-mean T2, geomT2IEW) reflects the contribution of large-diameter axons.2 As T2 and T1 share relaxation mechanisms related to water mobility (correlation time), T1 in CC regions may also depend on the diameter combined with the spacing between axons, and not myelin content of the axons. In published reports, based on coarse CC parcellation, T1 values are the highest in the CC body and lowest in the CC genu,3, 4 consistent with greater water mobility in regions with larger-diameter, sparsely packed axons.2  Moreover, a recent report of fiber orientation effects on T1 contrasting CC body vs. the genu and splenium in a small sample has not been replicated yet.3 Therefore, our objectives were to replicate these findings in the midsagittal CC regions and extend the inquiry into the lateral extensions of the CC fibers, which unlike the midsagittal section display heterogeneous orientation, all while using the new, more accurate T1 estimated obtained from  an optimized MP2RAGE protocol. We hypothesized that higher T1 values would be associated with greater contribution of large-diameter axons, and that the greatest middle-to-lateral T1 differences would be observed in the CC regions with the highest heterogeneity of fiber orientation. 

Methods

We acquired T1 data from 49 young adults (31F; mean age ± SD: 25.2±3.0 yrs) with a double inversion Magnetization Prepared Rapid Gradient Echo, MP2RAGE, sequence on a Siemens 3T Verio system. Acquisition parameters were: FOV 250×250 mm3, acquisition matrix of 240×240, 160 sagittal slices, 1×1×1 mm3 pixel, TI1/2: 400ms/2350 ms, TR: 4.5 s, FA1/2: 6°/6°, scan time: 7:36 min. The acquisition parameters were selected to optimize the dependence (linearity) between the UNI signal and T1 values between 300-2,000 ms and hence, improved T1 accuracy. Conventional MPRAGE images were also collected with the following parameters: FOV 256×256 mm3, acquisition matrix of 256×256, 160 axial slices, 1×1×1 mm3 pixel, TI: 1100 ms, TR: 2150 ms, FA: 8°, scan time: 4:59 min. 

The CC was first segmented into the genu, body, and posterior portion (isthmus and splenium). The genu and body were further subdivided into three equal segments each (GENU-1,2,3, and BODY-1,2,3) while the posterior portion was split into the isthmus and splenium, with the splenium further divided into three segments (SPL-1,2,3). This parcellation scheme of 10 ROIs mimicked the Aboitiz et al (1992) approach used subsequently by Lynn et al (2021). All 10 CC ROIs were further partitioned into 3 divisions: medial (2 midsagittal slices from the right and left side), and lateral that extended 5 slices right or left from the medial section (Figure 1). All left, medial and right ROIs were pre-defined in the MNI152 standard space and co-registered to the subject T1w space through a non-linear transformation (FSL FNIRT) described in Lynn.2 T1 maps were generated from the lookup table approach,5 thresholded to include only pixels with a white matter probability (via FSL ANAT) of 95% or greater, and co-registered to the T1w space (6 df via FSL FLIRT). 

Lastly, mean T1 relaxation times were extracted from each ROI (i.e., by averaging the 1/T1 value from each pixel) and applied generalized estimating equations (GEE) to assess lateralized and midsagittal differences in CC microstructure repeated measures analysis. In the model,  ROI (10 regions), Side (left, medial, and right), and the ROI×Side interaction were treated as repeated measures. Post-hoc analyses were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Tukey-Kramer approach.

Results

The ROI × Side interaction was significant: χ2 = 42.15, p = .001. Post-hoc comparisons showed that all medial regions differed from immediately neighboring regions along the anterior–posterior axis (e.g., GENU-1 vs GENU-2, GENU-2 vs GENU-3; all p < .046, adjusted), except for the comparisons between GENU-3 and BODY-1 (p = 1.000), BODY-2 and BODY-3 (p = .977), and SPL-1 and SPL-2 (p = .669) (Figure 2a). The mean T1 relaxation times (±SEM) were the lowest in the genu (GENU-1: 642±4ms; GENU-2: 667±4ms; GENU-3: 675±4ms), the highest in the body (BODY-1: 675±4ms; BODY-2: 682±4ms; BODY-3: 687±4ms) and intermediate in the isthmus and splenium combined (ISTH: 675±4ms; SPL-1: 658±4ms; SPL-2: 654±4ms; SPL-3: 677±5ms) (Figure 2a). 

No significant differences were observed between left and right components across all 10 CC regions (all p > .05, adjusted). As there were no significant differences between the left and right sections across the CC ROIs, we collapsed them into one lateral section and compared T1 values therein against those of the medial sections. The lateral T1 values were higher than the medial ones in most CC regions (all p < .0001, adjusted), reflected in a negative percent difference in T1 depicted in Figure 2b. No significant lateral-medial differences observed in GENU-2 and SPL-1,2,3 (all p > .05, adjusted).

Conclusion

After applying an optimized MP2RAGE protocol for accurate T1 estimations alongside a fine-grained CC parcellation scheme, we demonstrate that in vivo T1 values across the CC reflect microstructural heterogeneity noted in histological studies and by other methods of quantitative T1 imaging.1, 2, 4 Specifically, midline, regional T1 differences demonstrated lowest T1 relaxation times in the genu, the highest in the body, and intermediate T1 values in the posterior region, which is in a broad agreement with prior reports based on fewer CC partitions. The profile of the T1 differences observed in this study corresponds to the contribution of large-diameter axons (i.e., the number of fibers >3μm depicted in Figure 1 from Aboitiz et al., 1992) and the geomT2IEW profile from myelin water (i.e., Figure 5 from Lynn et al., 2020). Thus, T1 relaxation times in white matter are linked to the contribution of large-diameter axons rather than myelin content alone.2 This also suggests that T1 and T2 share a similar relaxation mechanism that reflects water mobility in the white matter microstructure. For example, the observed higher T1 and geomT2IEW relaxation times in the CC body reflecting greater water mobility is consistent with increased extracellular water space found in regions dominated by large-diameter, spacer axons such as the body of the CC. 

The lack of systematic left-right differences across CC subregions, and lower lateral vs. medial values in the anterior (genu and body) regions compared to splenium and isthmus are in contrast with the prior small-sample study that reported the opposite pattern and interpreted it as evidence of fiber orientation influence.3 In the present study, T1 values, especially in the body, were consistently higher medially than their lateral counterparts, reflecting a opposite in direction of the effect. Thus, our findings argue against the view of fiber orientation as a primary driver of T1 variability across the CC. That interpretation relied on the assumption that fibers in the body would have a smaller deviation in the fiber direction relative to B0 compared to fibers from the genu and splenium that are relatively perpendicular to B0. Perpendicular fibers would indeed yield a greater T1 anisotropy effect and shorter T1 values. This was not the case in the current study. Importantly, we selected MP2RAGE acquisition parameters based on optimization analyses assessing the linearity of the UNI signal with respect to T1 across the relevant range of T1 values consistent with the biology of the human brain, thereby improving measurement accuracy. Our selection of the parameters relied on comparisons to a gold-standard T1/T2 phantom (CaliberMRI Premium System Phantom Model 130) in the ongoing study at our site. Discrepancies between the present findings and prior reports may reflect several methodological limitations. Besides a small sample (N=6), they include problems associated with estimating T1 regrowth from signal intensities taken at different inversion times from multiple MP2RAGE inversion pairs.3 That is, the regrowth model function does not accurately represent the T1 evolution of the different inversion pairs of MP2RAGE and hence, the approach is not optimal for generating accurate T1 maps.5

Our findings highlight that T1 variability across the CC mirrors the geomT2IEW component, reflecting the contribution of large-diameter axons rather than primarily reflecting myelin content. As such, this underscores the utility of optimized MP2RAGE for T1 mapping aimed at assessing axonal microstructure in vivo.
