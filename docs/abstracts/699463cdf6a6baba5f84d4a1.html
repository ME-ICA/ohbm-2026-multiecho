Introduction

Regular exercise associated with well-established physical and psychological benefits. One significant factor thought to influence such health-promoting behavior is how people are influenced by their group norms1. The in-depth exploration of potential impact factors underlying social influence support the development of interventions that lead to effective behavior change.

An individual’s position within a social network (central vs. peripheral) may moderate susceptibility to norms. Central individuals have more social ties and information-brokering opportunities, potentially refining social-cognitive skills2 (e.g., preference for spreading ideas, etc.). Yet, findings are mixed. According to identity-based motivation theory, how individuals understand their identity in the group may govern their tendency to converge to or diverge from the group’ preferences3. Central individuals focusing on status may inhibit conformity to assert dominance. Conversely, those focusing on group identification may enhance conformity to affirm belonging. Thus, network centrality’s effect may depend on the active social motive.

In the present study, we use EEG to capture the neural dynamics via two event-related potentials: the Feedback-Related Negativity (FRN) and P3. FRN reflects initial conflict detection and negative evaluation (e.g., norm violation), while P3 is linked to subsequent attentional allocation and learning for behavioral adjustment4. Further, we manipulated individuals’ network centrality by assigning them to exercise support groups either with nominated friends (high-centrality) or non-nominated classmates (low-centrality). Social motives were manipulated by framing the groups as collective (group identification) or authoritarian (social status). We hypothesize that under group identification motive, high-centrality individuals will show larger FRN/P3 amplitudes than low-centrality individuals, while under social status motive, the opposite effect will occur.

Methods

Participants

Initially, 184 non-athletic university students (41 males; mean age = 20.24 years; SD = .94 years) completed questionnaires assessing social networks and exercise willingness. Among them, 74 subjects participated in the follow-up EEG experiment voluntarily (19 males; mean age = 20.05 years; SD = .93 years). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Beijing Sport University.

Materials 

Pictorial stimuli 

A total of 60 color images depicting exercise behaviors, downloaded from free internet sources, served as the experimental materials. The pictures portrayed typical fitness center scenarios, such as a person running on a treadmill, as well as outdoor activities, like a person climbing a mountain.

Procedure

The data collection involved two parts. 

The preliminary part. participants completed a series of questionnaires in class. To acquire social network data, they were first asked to nominate classmates to whom they felt “particularly close”. Pictorial stimuli were also presented, and participants rated their willingness to engage in the depicted exercise behaviors on a 10-point scale.

The formal experimental part. A 2 (network centrality: low vs. high) × 2 (social motive: group identification vs. social status) between-subjects design was employed, conducted across two sessions one week after the preliminary phase. 

During the behavioral session (Initial rating), participants were asked to rate their willingness to participate in each of the 60 depicted exercise behaviors using a 10-point scale.

During the ERP session (Final rating), participants again evaluated their willingness to engage in the same set of 60 exercise behaviors (60 trials). The trials included 30 feedback trials (social influence context) and 30 no-feedback (non-social influence context) trials. In the feedback trials, participants viewed group members’ average rating (group rating). Actually, we manipulated the group rating to produce social influence. The group rating was equally likely to be 1, 2 or 3 points above or below the participant’s initial rating in the behavioral session. 

Prior to the task, they were instructed that they would be assigned to a four-person exercise support group. Under the high-centrality condition, the group members were mutual nominated friends of the participants; Under the low-centrality condition, the group members were non-nominated classmates of the participants, while the relationship between these classmates was mutual nomination. Further, to frame the social status/group identification motive, the support group were described as either collective or authoritarian separately. 

ERP recording and analysis

Brain electrical activity was collected from 64 scalp sites using Ag/AgCl electrodes mounted in an elastic cap according to the international 10-20 system (NeuroScan Inc., USA). Standard preprocessing procedures were applied. The FRN and P3 were analyzed within a 200-280 ms and 300-500ms time window, respectively. FRNdiff and P3diff were calculated as the amplitude difference between social and non-social influence contexts, and compared across the four conditions (social status/identification × high/low centrality) using RT-ANOVA. Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied to correct the p-values. Bonferroni post-hoc tests were applied for following pairwise comparisons. 

For statistical analysis of FRNdiff and P3diff amplitudes, nine regions of interest were defined according to hemisphere (left, medial, right) and region (frontal, central, parietal): left frontal (F3, F5, F7, FC3, FC5, FT7); left central (C3, C5, CP3, CP5, TP7); left parietal (P3, P5, P7, PO5, PO7, O1); medial frontal (F1, FZ, F2, FC1, FCZ, FC2); medial central (C1, CZ, C2, CP1, CPZ, CP2); medial parietal (P1, PZ, P2, PO3, POZ, PO4); right frontal (F4, F6, F8, FC4, FC6, FT8), right central (C4, C6, CP4, CP6, TP8) and right parietal (P4, P6, P8, PO6, PO8, O2).

Results

Behavioral results

An influence score was used to calculate how much the participant was influenced by the group rating. Trials in which participants modified their initial ratings during the ERP session in the final rating were defined as update trials. The influence score was calculated by the proportion of update trials under social influence context in the total number of update trials both under social influence context and non-social influence context.

A 2 (network centrality: low vs. high) × 2 (social motive: social status vs. group identification) ANOVA was conducted to compare influence scores across conditions. Results revealed a marginally significant main effect of network centrality, with individuals in the high-centrality group showing a higher influence score (M = 77.73%, SD = 15.27%) than those in the low-centrality group (M = 71.00%, SD = 15.51%; F(1, 70) = 3.68, p = .059, ηp² = .05). Additionally, participants in the group identification condition exhibiting a higher influence score (M = 78.58%, SD = 16.63%) than those in the social status condition (M = 70.37%, SD = 13.54%; F(1, 70) = 5.53, p = .021, ηp² = .07). These differences confirm that the experimental manipulations functioned as intended.

ERP results

Correlations between EEG and behavioral data

Correlation analysis revealed no significant relationship between influence score and FRNdiff amplitude across any of the nine regions of interest (−.13 < rs < .04, ps > .615). In contrast, a significant positive correlation between influence score and P3diff amplitude was observed in the medial frontal area (r = .28, p = .015; see Fig. 3). 

FRN Component 

For the FRNdiff amplitude, the main effect of network centrality was significant (F (1,70) = 10.01, p = .002, ηp² = .13). Individuals in the high-centrality group showed more negative amplitudes than those in the low-centrality group. The results revealed a significant interaction of network centrality × social motives × hemisphere (F (2,140) = 3.76, p = .036, ηp² = .05). Post-hoc tests indicated that, under both social status motives and group identity motives, individuals in the high-centrality group exhibited a more negative FRNdiff amplitude than those in the low-centrality group (Fs (1,36) > 5.84). To further examine the three-way interaction, separate analyses were conducted for social status and group identification motives. In the high-centrality group, no significant main effects or interactions were observed (all Fs < 3.52, ps > .063). In the low-centrality group, individuals primed with social status motives exhibited more negative waveforms (M = -0.12, SE = 0.28) compared to those primed with group identification motives (M = 1.05, SE = 0.29; F (1, 35) = 8.56, p = .006, ηp² = .20). 

Overall, the results indicated that a more negative-going FRNdiff effect was observed for individuals in the high-centrality group when they focused on group identity than on social status. 

P3 Component

For the P3diff amplitude, the main effect of social motives was significant (F (1,70) = 5.02, p = .028, ηp² = .07). Compared to the social status motive, the group identification motive evoked larger P3diff amplitude. The results revealed a significant interaction of network centrality × social motives × hemisphere × region (F (4,272) = 4.41, p = .013, ηp² = .06). Post-hoc tests indicated that in the high-centrality group, individuals focused on the group identification motive (M = 3.10, SE = .28) showed more positive-going waves than those focused on the social status motive (M = 2.23, SE = .27; F (1,35) = 5.10, p = .030, ηP2 = .13). In the low-centrality group, there were no significant main effects or interactions (Fs < 2.25, ps > .101).

Overall, a main effect of social motives over the left frontal and medial frontal areas was observed only for the high network centrality group, not for the low network centrality group.

Conclusion

In the present study, we employed event-related potential (ERP) techniques to examine whether and how normative social influence on exercise behaviors is modulated by network centrality and social motives. Our findings indicate that centrally positioned individuals exhibited greater susceptibility to conflicting decisions with group majorities, particularly when group identification motives were primed. Specifically, a larger FRNdiff effect was observed in individuals with high (versus low) network centrality under the group identification condition; however, this effect was markedly attenuated under the social status condition. Furthermore, relative to the social status motive, the group identification motive was associated with a larger P3diff amplitude in the high network centrality group, but not in the low network centrality group. Together, these results suggest that an individual’s structural position within a social network and their salient social motives interact to shape susceptibility to normative social influence in the context of exercise behaviors.

The present research is one of the first to directly explore the neural representation of how network centrality and social motives interact to modulate the exercise-specific social influence (especially for normative social influence). The results suggest that a central position in a social network can facilitate the detection of social conflicts through group identification motive, rather than though social status motive. Regarding subsequent behavioral changes, group identification motive leads individuals who occupy central positions in their social group adjust their decisions in alignment with group norms. These findings highlight how individuals’ exercise behaviors are shaped by their social environments and motivated by others’ exercise and fitness behaviors. The current findings may have implications for further studies concerning the role of the social network position and social motives in the intervention of exercise behaviors.
