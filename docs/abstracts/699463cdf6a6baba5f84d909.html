Introduction

Alterations in striatal tissue properties and interhemispheric asymmetries have been associated with motor dysfunction across neurodegenerative disorders, including Parkinson’s disease (PD), dystonia, and Huntington’s disease1. In PD, dopamine PET and SPECT, as well as MRI-based estimates of tissue properties (e.g., T1w/T2w) in the posterior putamen, have been shown to correlate with motor asymmetry2. Recently, it has been demonstrated that functionally defined motor regions (e.g., hand, foot, glutes, tongue) can be derived from highly sampled individuals performing motor tasks3,4. Here, we ask whether such functionally defined putamen motor regions can also be used to identify asymmetrical tissue properties in PD, be associated with motor dysfunction and whether these relationships change over the course of the disease.

Methods

We analyzed neuroimaging and clinical data from the Parkinson’s Progression Marker Initiative (PPMI), including two MRI sessions (baseline and last available follow-up) from 91 early-stage PD patients and 42 age- and sex-matched healthy controls. Structural MRI data were used to generate semi-quantitative tissue maps by computing the ratio of T1-weighted to T2-weighted signal (T1w/T2w), an MRI-derived contrast sensitive to tissue microstructural properties and commonly used as a proxy for myelin content. Functionally defined motor regions in the putamen were derived from highly sampled individuals (N = 13) performing motor tasks involving six body parts (right and left hand, right and left foot, glutes, and tongue)3,4. Each individual completed at least eight runs with four repetitions per movement type. Contrast maps were averaged across runs within individuals, and a winner-take-all group map was generated by averaging movement-specific contrasts across individuals (Fig.1A). The resulting regions of interest were transformed from MNI space to the native space of PPMI subjects. Within these predefined regions, we quantified T1w/T2w signal and computed interhemispheric asymmetries. Motor asymmetry was assessed using lateralized items from the MDS Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale Part 3 (UPDRS III).

Results

Interhemispheric asymmetry in T1w/T2w signal within functionally defined motor regions of the putamen correlated with asymmetry in lateralized UPDRS III motor scores, with the strongest association observed between upper-limb motor asymmetry and hand putamen regions (Fig.1B). A combined hand–foot measure showed a similarly strong, though slightly reduced, association. These relationships were not observed in healthy controls. The association was stronger at the baseline session than at the last available follow-up (approximately 1–3 years after baseline; R² = 0.22, p < 0.001; R² = 0.12, p < 0.005). We also tested whether similar associations were present in the cerebellum by quantifying T1w/T2w signal within body-part–specific regions of the anterior and posterior cerebellar body maps; no effects were observed in any region.

Conclusion

These results indicate that MRI-derived micrustractural-related contrast (T1w/T2w) within the putamen body map is informative for PD and for changes over the course of the disease. The stronger association observed for hand-related regions may reflects both the earlier and more lateralized involvement of upper-limb motor symptoms in PD and their greater representation in standard clinical rating scales6. The reduction in association strength at later follow-up may be related to the increasing disease severity, and to motor symptoms become less lateralized. The use of functionally defined, body-part–specific regions  within the putamen and beyond it can provide a generalizable framework, yielding regions of interest across brain structures that may serve as an atlas for studying motor disorders and potentially enable body-part–specific investigations across different conditions.
