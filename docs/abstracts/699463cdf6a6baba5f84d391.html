Introduction

Fluctuations in the power of alpha frequency (8-13Hz) EEG oscillations occur spontaneously but can be strongly modulated by opening/closing one’s eyes. The generating mechanisms of alpha in humans are poorly understood, with opinion divided as to whether: (i) bottom-up, (ii) top-down, or (iii) both processes1,2 are primarily responsible. Simultaneous EEG-fMRI at 7T provides a unique method to study the generating mechanisms of alpha oscillations. Here we investigate the use of three GLM approaches to study layer specific EEG-fMRI correlations during periods of eyes open/closed.

Methods

Data acquisition:

EEG-fMRI data were acquired on 10 healthy volunteers (4F, 28±5yrs). EEG data were acquired using a 64 channel MR-compatible EEG system, with MR-plus amplifier (Brain Products GmbH, Germany). fMRI data were acquired on a 7T Philips Achieva scanner [3D GE-EPI: 0.8mm isotropic, TE/TRvol=32/3800ms] alongside anatomical [PSIR, 0.8mm isotropic] data collected with matched geometry. The paradigm comprised alternating 30s blocks of eyes open/closed, with 4 blocks per run and 4-5 runs per subject. Visual retinotopic mapping data (1.5mm isotropic, 2D GE-EPI) were also collected.

Analysis: 

After gradient and pulse artefact correction. A LCMV beamformer (FieldTrip) was used to calculate a virtual electrode (VE) time-course of alpha power from the site of maximum alpha power change in the occipital cortex for each run. One subject was excluded due to poor EEG quality.

fMRI data were pre-processed for physiological noise, geometric distortion and motion correction3. To identify regions of alpha-BOLD correlation, the alpha power VE time-course was convolved with a HRF and input into a fixed effects GLM (FEAT, FSL) along with 6 motion parameters. Additional GLMs were performed featuring: just a boxcar regressor of eyes open/closed timings; or the alpha regressor orthogonalised with the boxcar PSIR data were used to define a grey matter (GM) mask which was combined with regions of interest (ROIs) of V1, V2&V3 defined from retinotopic mapping. The GM mask was used to define six equivolume layers across the visual cortex and generate 4000 cortical columns (mean diameter 0.8-1mm) (LAYNII). Only those columns which were located entirely within V1, V2 or V3 and contained voxels with a significant BOLD-alpha correlation (z<-2.3) were included. For columns within V1-3, the GLM β-weights were threshold at 5% of the absolute maximum β-weight to remove noise. Draining vein effects were then corrected using spatial deconvolution analysis for each column4. The veinous cerebral blood volume (CBVv) estimate for this deconvolution analysis was calculated from the mean variance of the BOLD signal during the eyes closed periods for each run, which was averaged across all runs. 

The cortical depth profile of β-weights was computed before and after deveining, normalising the range of β-weights within the profile. A weighted average of the normalised cortical depth profiles across V1-V3 ROIs was then computed based on the number of columns present in each ROI for each participant. The participant group mean and standard error in cortical depth profile was then computed. 

The change in the gradient of the β-weight profile due to deveining was computed in each column, and columns were ranked by the magnitude of their gradient change after deveining (bottom 25%, middle 50% and top 25%). Columns with the largest gradient changes (top 25%) were removed to minimise draining vein confounds, and the cortical depth profiles for ‘uncorrected’ and ‘deveined’ data were assessed for three GLMs (1) Alpha EEG regressor, 2) Boxcar regressor, 3) Alpha orthogonalized with boxcar regressor. This allowed testing of whether we could differentiate between the laminar origins of BOLD signal changes driven by the (i) mean effect of the task and (ii) alpha power variability.

Results

Figure 1 compares the ‘uncorrected’ and ‘deveined’ cortical depth profiles for each of the GLM approaches: 1) Alpha power only, 2) Boxcar of task only and 3) Alpha power orthogonalized to boxcar. All profiles were calculated after excluding those columns where the gradient changed the most (top 25%) due to deveining, which reflected large vein contributions (data not shown). In the ‘uncorrected data’ in all cases the typical bias to the cortical surface is seen (Fig.1 column i). After ‘deveining’ a greater variation in cortical depth profiles was seen across participants (Fig.1A.ii). The exclusion of the columns in close proximity to veins ensured the best representation of the underlying neuronal activity. 

Across all GLMs, depth had a significant effect on β-weight (p = 0.004, two-way rm-ANOVA) with a trend toward interaction with GLM type (p = 0.083). To probe this, depth profiles were examined separately (Bonferroni-corrected threshold p < 0.017). For the Alpha only GLM there was a significant “n”-shaped profile (Fig. 1Aii, p = 0.007), driven by β-weights at depth 1 < 4 (p = 0.010) and depth 6 < 5 (p = 0.024) with no other pairwise differences. The Boxcar task GLM showed no significant depth effect (Fig. 1Bii, p = 0.069). The Alpha orthogonalized with boxcar GLM showed a significant “n”-shaped profile (Fig. 1Cii, p = 0.003), driven by depth 1 < 3 (p = 0.004) and 4 (p = 0.003), and depth 6 < 4 (p = 0.001) and 5 (p < 0.001) with no other pairwise differences.

Conclusion

7T EEG-fMRI layer profiles relating to alpha power modulations have been computed non-invasively in humans. Whilst it is impossible to infer alpha-generation from BOLD-alpha power layer correlations1, we suggest that the n-shaped profile of the alpha-BOLD correlations across cortical depths is driven by the spontaneous alpha power modulations (Fig.1Cii) rather than task condition changes (eyes open/closed boxcar, Fig.1Bii). 

Our findings suggest that alpha power primarily originates from superficial and deep layers of the visual cortex, likely through top-down processing, in agreement with recordings in non-human primates5. Future work needs to validate these findings using different task paradigms and with methods that are not confounded by the draining vein effect such as VASO based methods to measure CBV.
