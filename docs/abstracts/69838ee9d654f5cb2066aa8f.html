Introduction

Working memory is a crucial cognitive ability that enables goal-directed behavior and higher cognitive function. It involves the maintenance and manipulation of information in the absence of sensory stimuli.^1 Working memory has been extensively studied using EEG time-frequency analysis, often focusing on predefined frequency bands such as theta (4-8 Hz), alpha (8-12 Hz), and beta (13-30 Hz). The n-back task is a widely used paradigm that integrates key working memory processes, including encoding, maintenance, updating, and recall.

Beyond band-specific spectral modulations, EEG power spectra exhibit broadband aperiodic activity that follows a 1/f scaling pattern. This pattern can obscure changes in periodic (oscillatory) activity unless aperiodic and periodic activity are appropriately separated.^2 A common approach in task-based EEG studies is to apply baseline correction to emphasize task-related changes, assuming stable aperiodic activity across time. However, in continuous paradigms like the n-back task, where no neutral pre-stimulus interval exists, this assumption may not hold.

Recent evidence suggests that aperiodic fluctuations reflect the balance between excitation and inhibition (E:I) in the brain, which is essential for cognitive processing.^3 Ignoring task-related aperiodic fluctuations can distort estimates of periodic activity and mask meaningful physiological and behavioral information.^4 A steeper aperiodic slope implies higher low-frequency power and lower high-frequency power, which may indicate a stronger cognitive engagement.

In this study we examined how periodic and aperiodic EEG components contribute to working memory demands in the n-back task and addressed potential biases introduced by baseline correction. We hypothesized that the aperiodic slope would be modulated by processing demands, varying with load and stimulus type, but not between task modalities, which is consistent with domain-general processing.

Methods

Participants: We analyzed EEG recordings from 57 volunteers (nine males), aged 55–78 years (M=67.4, SD = 6.4), who initially participated in a study on subjective cognitive complaints. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and were free from neurological or psychiatric illness or cognitive decline, as assessed by neuropsychological testing. Twenty-seven participants reported subjective cognitive complaints. The current study focused exclusively on within-subject comparisons and did not investigate subjective cognitive complaints or between-group differences. Subsequent analyses included only subjects and conditions with a minimum of 30 good epochs and accuracy ≥0.6.

Task Design: Participants completed an n-back task in two modalities (visuospatial and verbal) with two load conditions (0-back and 2-back). White capital letters were presented on grey background in one of nine possible positions. Each stimulus appeared for 250 ms followed by a 3000 ms response interval. In verbal conditions, participants memorized the letter; in visuospatial conditions, they memorized the position. For 0-back, targets matched a predefined stimulus shown at block start. For the 2-back condition, targets matched stimuli presented two trials prior. Each modality included 201 stimuli (39 targets) for 0-back and 240 stimuli (39 targets, 30 lures) for 2-back. Modality order was counterbalanced across participants.

EEG Recording and Preprocessing: Data were acquired using a 64-channel ActiCap (Brain Products) with FCz reference, and were sampled at 500-2500 Hz. Preprocessing included FIR high-pass filtering with 0.1 Hz cut-off, CleanLine for line noise removal at 50 Hz, epoching from -1.5 to 2.8 s around stimulus, and baseline correction. Epochs and channels were manually inspected for artifacts. ICA decomposition was performed using the AMICA algorithm to identify and remove components representing blinks, eye movements, muscle noise, and other artifacts. The data were re-referenced to an average reference.

Time-Frequency Analysis: Average ERPs were subtracted from single-trial data before time-frequency decomposition to isolate induced activity. Time-frequency representations were computed using superlets (base wavelet: 3 cycles, order: linearly spaced 1-20, multiplicative combination) across 3-50 Hz in 1 Hz steps.^5

Spectral Parameterization: The FOOOF algorithm (specparam v2.0.0) was applied at each time point (-0.5 to 2 s) to decompose trial-averaged activity into periodic and aperiodic components.^2 Parameters: peak width limits 0.5-12 Hz, peak threshold 2.0, fixed aperiodic mode. Aperiodic activity was characterized by offset and exponent parameters. Periodic activity was reconstructed from parameterized Gaussian peaks.

Validation: We used phase autocorrelation function (pACF) to quantify rhythmicity, independent of amplitude. Rhythmicity is considered to be a property of oscillations, so consistent FOOOF and pACF results would confirm that low-frequency oscillations were truly absent rather than undetectable by FOOOF. We estimated time-resolved pACF using Morlet wavelets (3 cycles), window size of 2.5 cycles, and lags from 1-3 cycles.

Statistical Analysis: We used linear mixed models (y ~ modality × load × stimulus + RT + (1|subject)) to assess differences in periodic power and aperiodic parameters. We fitted models for each channel-frequency-time point with Benjamini-Yekutieli FDR correction. Significance required clusters spanning ≥3 time points or ≥3 channels (α=.05). Similarly, we used linear mixed models with condition as random effect to assess similarities between baseline-corrected power and FOOOF parameters, as well as between pACF and FOOOF parameters.

Replication: The analyses were repeated on two datasets: (1) a verbal n-back dataset (n=21, young adults, 0/1/2-back, 256-channel EEG), and (2) an item-recognition task (n=48, young adults, 32-channel EEG, 2/4-item loads).

Results

Aperiodic Activity Accounts for Low-Frequency Power Changes

Baseline-corrected time-frequency plots revealed a decrease in  alpha and beta power (0-1 s post-stimulus) and an increase of low-frequency power (from 0 s post-stimulus). This pattern was consistent across different baseline correction methods (decibel, relative change, normalized change, absolute change) and baseline periods. However, when decomposing into periodic and aperiodic components, the periodic component showed no low-frequency increases, which appeared only in aperiodic activity.

Linear mixed models revealed strong positive correlations between aperiodic parameters and baseline-corrected theta-range power (marginal R² up to 0.4), with the exponent also showing negative correlations with gamma-range activity (>35 Hz). In contrast, periodic activity correlated strongly with alpha/beta baseline-corrected power but only weakly with low-frequency power. The pACF analysis showed rhythmicity patterns consistent with periodic power in the alpha range, while correlations between aperiodic parameters and pACF were weak, confirming aperiodic estimates were largely independent of oscillatory processes.

Periodic activity changes with working memory load, stimulus type, and modality

Visual inspection of individual power spectra revealed that most participants exhibited alpha peaks, while only two displayed evident theta peaks. Periodic activity concentrated in alpha (8-12 Hz) and beta (13-30 Hz) bands showed significantly decreased power in 2-back versus 0-back conditions across parieto-occipital channels throughout the trial. Greater alpha/beta suppression occurred for targets versus non-targets, particularly 0.3-1 s post-stimulus across the whole scalp. An interaction between modality and stimulus type (0.5-1 s post-stimulus) reflected greater target/non-target differences in the verbal task. Beta power in central channels was positively correlated with reaction times early (0–0.5 s) and negatively correlated with reaction times late (1–2 s).

Aperiodic activity has frontal and parietal components

The aperiodic exponent time course revealed two spatiotemporally distinct components. The first peaked at ~0.3 s post-stimulus with frontal topography and the second peaked at ~0.7 s with parietal distribution. The exponent was significantly larger for targets at ~0.3 s and for non-targets at ~0.7 s (p<.05, FDR-corrected). No significant differences in the exponent emerged between load conditions or modalities, though the offset showed load sensitivity (lower for 2-back) and a load × stimulus type interaction. Associations with reaction times emerged at ~1.4 s in parieto-occipital channels.

Aperiodic activity does not reflect event-related potentials

The correlations between ERPs and aperiodic activity yielded R² values of 0.1-0.2 around stimulus onset but were negligible after 0.5 s. This suggests that the aperiodic modulations and the ERPs reflect largely distinct processes.

Replication confirms main findings

We replicated the following in both additional datasets: (1) task-related alpha/beta periodic modulation, (2) correlation of aperiodic parameters with baseline-corrected low-frequency activity, (3) correlations of rhythmicity with periodic activity, and (4) the presence of frontal and parietal aperiodic components modulated by task demands.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that a substantial portion of the low-frequency power changes commonly observed in working memory tasks, as measured by scalp EEG, reflect modulations in aperiodic activity rather than low-frequency (theta) oscillations. By explicitly separating periodic and aperiodic EEG components and tracking their dynamics over time, we show that both components exhibit distinct task-related spatiotemporal dynamics that are closely related to cognitive demands.

Our findings challenge the conventional understanding of low-frequency oscillations in cognitive processing. Previous n-back studies have consistently reported sustained theta elevations linked to attention, encoding, and cognitive control. While theta-range power can indeed be oscillatory, the sustained low-frequency activity in our data appears largely influenced by aperiodic components, with post-stimulus increases attributable to spectral slope shifts rather than exclusively periodic activity changes. Individual-level analysis revealed that only a minority of participants exhibited clear theta peaks, consistent with recent findings from a visual working memory task showing inconsistent or absent theta peaks in 60% of participants.

Methodologically, these results raise important concerns about the routine use of baseline correction in continuous paradigms. Baseline correction assumes stable aperiodic activity. However, when this assumption is not met, aperiodic slope may be misrepresented as changes in low-frequency power. The qualitative pattern remained consistent whether subtractive or divisive normalization was employed, suggesting that conclusions are not driven by any single normalization strategy.

The identification of two distinct aperiodic components, which are differentially modulated by stimulus type, suggests that aperiodic activity reflects meaningful cognitive operations. We speculate that the second component following non-targets indicates elevated vigilance for potential targets, whereas vigilance diminishes more rapidly after actual targets. The frontal component may be related to decision-making, and the parietal component may reflect anticipatory mechanisms.

The observed aperiodic slope dynamics align with the E:I balance framework, where stimulus-induced slope steepening reflects enhanced inhibitory control commensurate with task demands.^3 This is consistent with the notion that frontal theta indices of cognitive control may partly reflect aperiodic modulations.

These findings also have implications for cross-frequency coupling analyses. If scalp-recorded low-frequency fluctuations primarily reflect aperiodic shifts, assumptions about oscillatory theta modulating gamma amplitude in working memory may require reconsideration. Before interpreting measures of theta-gamma coupling, future studies should verify whether low-frequency signals are genuinely periodic and oscillatory.

We conclude that incorporating aperiodic activity as a fundamental component of EEG analysis is essential for accurate representation of the neural basis of cognition. The modulation of aperiodic activity is a more sensitive index of cognitive state changes than was previously recognized.
