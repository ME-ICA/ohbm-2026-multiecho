Introduction

The development of robust, physiologically meaningful biomarkers for diagnosis, classification, and monitoring of disease progression in neurodegenerative disorders remains challenging due to disease heterogeneity and incomplete understanding of underlying pathophysiological mechanisms. These challenges are particularly pronounced in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), a progressive disorder characterized by degeneration of upper and lower motor neurons in the central and peripheral nervous systems.

Electroencephalography (EEG) and electromyography (EMG) provide promising tools for characterizing functional impairments in ALS owing to their non-invasive nature, affordability, and high temporal resolution. Task-based connectivity analyses quantifying cortico-cortical and cortico-muscular interactions enable the assessment of network-level alterations underlying motor dysfunction.1,2 Such paradigms can capture sensorimotor network changes more directly than resting-state approaches, with previous studies reporting abnormal beta-band frontoparietal connectivity during motor execution in ALS.1

Resting-state effective connectivity analyses have revealed altered directional interactions between cortical regions in ALS.3 However, directional connectivity between central and peripheral neurophysiological signals, that can be assessed during motor tasks, remains underexplored. In this study, we assess directional connectivity across multiple central and peripheral nervous system electrophysiological activity  during a voluntary motor task, to characterize disrupted information flow along CNS–PNS pathways in ALS. By integrating EEG and EMG within a directional connectivity framework, this work aims to advance the understanding of causal network-level dysfunctions, including ascending and descending pathways, and support the development of physiologically grounded biomarkers.

Methods

This study investigated motor network dysfunction in two participant groups: 30 individuals diagnosed with ALS (mean age = 61.9 years) and 30 age-matched healthy control participants. All participants completed a precision force-control pinch visuomotor task designed to engage fine motor control.1 During task performance, high-density EEG (128 channels) and surface bipolar EMG signals were simultaneously recorded from the right hand, enabling assessment of cortico-muscular interactions.

EEG analyses focused on electrodes overlying key sensorimotor and associative cortical regions, including C3 and C4 (left and right primary motor cortices), Cz (central sensorimotor cortex), Fz (frontal midline), and Pz (parietal midline). EMG activity was recorded from three intrinsic hand muscles critically involved in precision grip and force modulation: the first dorsal interosseous (FDI), abductor pollicis brevis (APB), and flexor pollicis brevis (FPB). This selection allowed for targeted evaluation of cortico-cortical and cortico-muscular interactions relevant to fine motor execution using our suggested model.

Directed functional connectivity between cortical electrodes and between cortical and muscle electrodes simultaneously was estimated using a multivariate autoregressive (MVAR) model applied to the combined EEG–EMG signals. This approach provides a frequency-resolved characterization of directional information flow across the PNS/CNS sensorimotor networks while enabling differentiation between direct and indirect interactions within the system. MVAR models were trained using the ARfit toolbox in MATLAB following standard preprocessing procedures, signal normalization and pre-whitening, to ensure model stability and robustness. Model order selection employed several complementary approaches (including Akaike Information Criterion), and the final order (15) was selected to balance goodness-of-fit and prevent overfitting.

To enhance the specificity of inferred directed connections and reduce the influence of common sources and indirect pathways, isolated effective coherence (iCoh) was employed as the primary connectivity metric4, estimated across the 1–80 Hz frequency band. The Band-specific connectivity was estimated in 6 frequency bands: Theta band (4–7 Hz), Alpha band [8–12 Hz], Low Beta band (13–20 Hz), High Beta band (21–30 Hz],Low Gamma band (30–50 Hz], High Gamma band (50–80 Hz]. 

Statistical comparisons of connectivity measures between the ALS and control groups were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test implemented in MATLAB. To account for multiple comparisons across frequencies and channel pairs, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied, with statistical significance defined as a corrected p < 0.05.

Results

In the ALS group, directional connectivity from Cz to the intrinsic hand muscles FDI and FPB was significantly reduced in the high gamma band compared to healthy controls (corrected p = 0.03), indicating impaired descending cortical drive to peripheral motor units at higher frequencies in the designed precision grip task. A similar reduction was observed in the high beta band for the Cz→FDI (corrected p = 0.02) and Cz→FPB (corrected p = 0.03) connections, suggesting that abnormalities in cortico-muscular communications happen in multiple frequency ranges associated with motor control. Furthermore, ascending connectivity from the peripheral muscle APB to cortical regions C3 and Cz was significantly increased in the ALS group relative to controls in the high gamma band (corrected p = 0.03), potentially reflecting compensatory afferent mechanisms in response to impaired descending motor commands.

In addition to altered cortico-muscular interactions, ALS participants exhibited disrupted cortico-cortical connectivity patterns. Specifically, C4 acted as a weaker source for C3, Fz, and Pz in the high gamma band, reflecting reduced information flow from the right motor cortex to both sensorimotor and associative cortical regions. Furthermore, a significant reduction in the C4→Pz connection was also detected in the high beta band (corrected p = 0.01).

Altered bidirectional interactions involving frontal and central regions were also evident. The Fz→Cz connection was significantly stronger in the ALS group compared to controls in the high gamma band (corrected p = 0.01), whereas the reciprocal Cz→Fz connection was significantly reduced in ALS within the same frequency range (corrected p = 0.01). This asymmetric pattern suggests a shift in directional information flow between frontal and sensorimotor areas in ALS. Finally, Cz→C3 connectivity was significantly stronger in the control group across the high beta and both gamma bands, highlighting interhemispheric sensorimotor connections in healthy participants that appears disrupted in ALS (corrected p = 0.01). 

Conclusion

These findings demonstrate marked alterations in directional neural communication in individuals with ALS, involving both cortico-muscular and cortico-cortical pathways. Specifically, we observed a reduction in efferent connectivity, including diminished Cz→muscle interactions. In addition, weakened C4→cortical projections and reduced Cz→C3 and Cz→Fz connectivity indicate impaired descending motor drive and disrupted interactions within core motor networks. These abnormalities are consistent with the degeneration of upper motor neurons in ALS and support the view that disease-related pathology extends beyond localized neuronal loss to encompass large-scale network dysfunction.

The strengthened Fz→Cz connection in ALS may reflect a compensatory mechanism, potentially supporting reduced information flow within motor-area pathways (CZ→ C3 and FZ). This interpretation supports the asymmetric alteration in frontal–central interactions, wherein frontal-to-central connectivity was increased while the reciprocal central-to-frontal pathway was reduced. Such directional imbalance may indicate a shift in information flow toward higher-order control regions to compensate for impaired motor-area communications.

Increased ascending connectivity from peripheral muscles to cortical regions, as observed for the APB→C3 and APB→Cz pathways, further may reflect increased reliance on afferent feedback to maintain motor performance when descending commands are weakened. Alternatively, it may represent maladaptive hyperexcitability which have been implicated in ALS pathophysiology.

Notably, the most prominent group differences were observed in the high beta and gamma frequency bands, with particularly significant effects in the gamma range. Beta-band oscillations are commonly associated with motor planning  and sensorimotor integration. Gamma-band activity has been closely linked to motor execution, cortical excitability, and cortico-muscular coupling, and its disruption has been reported in previous ALS studies.5 The frequency-specific nature of the observed connectivity changes reinforces the relevance of gamma and beta band dynamics as a sensitive marker of ALS-related network dysfunction.

Overall, these results highlight the capacity of combined EEG/EMG directional connectivity measures to capture meaningful abnormalities in both afferent and efferent pathways. This underscores their potential as neurophysiological biomarkers for detecting disease-specific sensorimotor network impairments in ALS and other neurological conditions.
