Introduction

Depression is a major public health issue, and one incidence peak occurs in adolescence1. Maternal distress has been recognized as an environmental risk factor for offspring depression, particularly when children are exposed to stressors. Maternal distress is also associated with alterations in cortico-limbic circuitry throughout development2, 3. Yet it remains unknown how these neural alterations might explain the vulnerability to psychopathology across generations, e.g., from mothers to children.

Methods

Based on data from a prospective Dutch low-risk cohort (Basale Invloeden op de Baby Ontwikkeling, BIBO), the present study examined the role of cortico-limbic connectivity topography in the relationship between middle-childhood maternal distress and adolescent depressive symptoms during a collective stressor (i.e. the COVID-19 pandemic). Maternal distress was averaged across maternal-reported depressive and anxiety symptoms from child ages 8 to 10 years (Fig.1). Children self-reported their depressive symptoms during the COVID-19 pandemic at age 13.5. Connectopic mapping was applied to children’s resting-state fMRI data acquired at age 12.5 to characterize the functional connectivity topography within the amygdala-hippocampus complex across neocortical regions4. 

Results

Results indicated that maternal distress in middle childhood was associated with children’s subsequent depressive symptoms in adolescence during the pandemic. Maternal distress was specifically linked to the third-order mode of connectivity in the bilateral amygdala-hippocampus complex, primarily driven by the difference in connectivity to the rest of the brain within the anterior hippocampus. Mediation models showed that these maternal distress-associated connectivity modes did not mediate the relationship between maternal distress and children’s depressive symptoms in adolescence. Moderation models indicated that the left third-order mode interacted with maternal distress to predict adolescent depressive symptoms (Fig. 2). 

Conclusion

Our results suggested that the cortico-limbic connectivity topography, as one potential neurobiological correlate of maternal distress, may play a key role in the pathway from maternal distress to children’s depressive symptoms. These findings could advance our understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying intergenerational vulnerability and highlight the importance of early prevention and intervention within the mother–child mental health system.
