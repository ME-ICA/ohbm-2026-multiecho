Introduction

Schizophrenia is among the most severe and socially burdensome of psychiatric conditions. Biomarkers derived from neuroimaging could contribute to improve clinical decision-making in schizophrenia spectrum disorders. However, disentangling the typical and atypical brain maturation markers is a challenge that complicates the identification of disorder-specific effects. By enabling subject-level inference, machine learning (ML) brings avenues for diagnostic use of neuroimaging data, but necessitates large and multisite normative datasets.

Methods

Structural MRI data were analyzed from healthy controls (HC) and individuals with five schizophrenia spectrum disorders (SSD). We exploited MRI Freesurfer-based features (cortical thickness, grey matter volume, and subcortical volumes) from 900 SSD (mean age = 32.49 ± 11.25; 43.44% female) and 906 HC participants (mean age = 38.78 ± 16.09; 49.23% female) from 14 sites, gathering the BrainChart consortium1 and the PAFIP cohort2. MRI features were referenced against normative data derived from Dorfschmidt et al., 20253. This yielded individual centile scores per feature, indicating each region’s deviation from age- and sex-typical norms (Fig. 1). Different features were tested according to three possible scopes: global features - average deviation per hemisphere; regional features - region-wise normative deviations; and regional-corrected features - subtracting the hemispheric global value from each region. We then applied ML (linear support vector machines) to these three types of morphometrics deviations maps to differentiate SSD from HC participants, investigating multiple data-handling strategies (stratified k-fold and leave-one-site-out (LOSO) cross validation (CV)). Finally, to assess stability and reproducibility of feature importance for classification, two complementary strategies were employed: ‘average’ and ‘consensus’. ‘Average’ selects top 10% features with highest mean absolute weights while ‘consensus’ selects features in top 10% present in more than 80% models.

Results

With K-fold CV, the regional maps yielded the highest accuracy (66.4 % ±  0.023, ROC-AUC = 72.9%  ± 0.022), closely followed by regional corrected maps (66.2% ± 0.02, ROC-AUC = 72.4%  ± 0.031) and then global maps (63.4% ± 0.032, ROC-AUC = 67.9% ± 0.032). The same tendency is observed for LOSO CV, with, moreover, very minimal loss in accuracy (Figure 2A). With the k-fold CV, there was substantial agreement between the regions that contributed to the identification of SSD participants in the ‘average’ and the ‘consensus’ strategies (respectively 16 and 9 selected features, the latter 9 being shared by both strategies) (Figure 2B and 2C). Moreover, there was substantial agreement between the regions that contributed to the identification of SSD participants in the two CV methods (15/16 regions common in average; 8/9 common in consensus) (not shown). The features common to all data handling strategies primarily pertained to subcortical regions (6/8) and cortical thickness (2/8), in both hemispheres.

Conclusion

While the achieved accuracy does not reach the 80% threshold for clinical applicability, the findings are nonetheless encouraging. They provide a grounded and pragmatic indication of how well classification can perform when applied to a large, and multi-site cohort of individuals with SSD using normative, region-level morphometrics. First, the model shows only a modest decrease in performance in the LOSO setting compared to stratified CV, indicating that the normative approach effectively mitigates site effects. Second, performance remains largely preserved when using regional-corrected maps relative to the original regional maps, suggesting that regional information contributes meaningfully to the classification and is not merely driven by global measures. In addition, the classifiers in the different settings relied on neuroanatomical patterns that were both plausible and highly consistent.
