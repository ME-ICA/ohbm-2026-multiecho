Introduction

Cerebral arteriolosclerosis is a prevalent small vessel brain pathology that is more severe in women1 and black older adults, and contributes to cognitive2 and motor decline. ARTS is a recently developed in-vivo MRI-based marker that provides a quantitative score reflecting the burden of arteriolosclerosis³. Cross-sectional studies have demonstrated that ARTS is associated with various demographic and vascular risk factors (VRFs)4. However, it remains unclear whether these and other clinical factors are linked with the change in ARTS score over time. Therefore, the purpose of this longitudinal study was to test the association of demographics, vascular risk factors, and vascular diseases with the rate of change in ARTS, in a large sample of community-dwelling older adults.

Methods

Participants, MRI, ARTS

Community dwelling older adults (Fig. 1) were drawn from five longitudinal cohort studies of aging, including the Rush Memory and Aging Project, Religious Orders Study, Minority Aging Research Study, Latino Core, and the Clinical Core of the Rush Alzheimer’s Disease Research Center⁵. Participants underwent 3T MRI with acquisition of MPRAGE, FLAIR, and diffusion weighted images. ARTS scores were generated at each imaging visit using the fully automated and publicly available ARTS pipeline (www.nitrc.org/projects/arts). Each participant contributed at least two MRI scans acquired on the same scanner.

Statistical analysis

Separate linear mixed-effects models (hlme, R lcmm package) with ARTS as the dependent variable and with random intercept and slope were fit for systolic and diastolic blood pressure (BP), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), body mass index (BMI), history of hypertension, history of congestive heart failure (CHF), history of claudication, smoking, history of heart disease (including heart attack, or coronary thrombosis, or coronary occlusion, or myocardial infarction), history of stroke, also including the interaction of these predictors with time from baseline. All models were adjusted for age, sex, education, and MRI scanner, as well as interactions of these covariates with time from baseline. Continuous predictors (BMI, systolic and diastolic BP, eGFR), as well as age and education, were mean-centered; binary predictors were categorical. False-discovery rate (Benjamini–Hochberg) was used to account for multiple comparisons (m = 11, p < 0.05).

Results

A total of 684 older adults were included in this study, with longitudinal follow-up spanning up to 11 years (Fig. 2A). ARTS scores increased over time (Fig. 2A). Participants who were older at baseline had higher ARTS score (β = 0.015 ± 0.001, p < 0.001) and a higher rate of increase in ARTS (β = 0.00073 ± 0.0001 year-1, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2B) . Participants who had a history of heart disease also had a higher rate of increase in ARTS (β = 0.0064 ± 0.0023, p = 0.005) (Fig. 2C). No other factors showed a significant association with the slope of ARTS.

Conclusion

In this large community-based sample of older adults predominantly without dementia, older age and a history of heart disease were shown to be associated with a faster increase in ARTS score. These findings suggest that older age and a history of heart disease are associated with a faster progression of brain abnormalities linked to arteriolosclerosis and likely of arteriolosclerosis pathology itself. This longitudinal analysis builds upon prior cross-sectional findings relating ARTS to demographic and vascular risk factors. Furthermore, ARTS is shown to be sensitive to within-person changes in arteriolosclerosis burden over time.
