Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a heterogeneous psychiatric condition affecting over 330 million individuals worldwide, yet lacks reliable neurobiological biomarkers and mechanistic understanding.1 Resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) enables noninvasive characterization of intrinsic brain network dysfunction, revealing abnormalities in the default mode network (DMN), frontoparietal control, and salience networks linked to core MDD symptoms.2 Dynamic functional connectivity (dFC) approaches, including Leading Eigenvector Dynamics Analysis (LEiDA), capture temporal fluctuations in network states at fine timescales beyond static connectivity measures.3 The REST-meta-MDD consortium has demonstrated heterogeneous DMN dysfunction and altered inter-network coupling across thousands of patients; however, technical variability in preprocessing challenges reproducibility.12 This secondary analysis leverages rigorously quality-controlled, harmonized REST-meta-MDD rs-fMRI data and LEiDA to test whether altered temporal dynamics of large-scale resting-state networks constitute a reproducible systems-level marker of MDD pathophysiology.

Methods

Study DesignSecondary analysis focused on between-group differences in dynamic functional connectivity (dFC) between patients with major depressive disorder (MDD) and healthy controls (HC) using resting-state fMRI data from the REST-meta-MDD consortium. From an initial pool of 1,300 MDD and 1,128 HC across 25 cohorts in China, rigorous quality control combining automated metrics and visual inspection excluded cases with spatial normalization errors, incomplete brain coverage, or framewise displacement >0.2 mm, and omitted cohorts with <10 participants per group, yielding a final sample of 848 MDD and 794 HC from 17 sites meeting REST-meta-MDD criteria.¹² 
Participants

Participant demographic data were harmonized across sites, with age inclusion restricted to 18–65 years.1 The final sample included 67% females among the MDD group (mean age 36.2 years, SD = 12.1) and 65% females among controls (mean age 35.7 years, SD = 11.9)1. Education levels were standardized to comparable categories, with the majority completing secondary education or higher.1

Clinical profiles in the MDD group comprised an average illness duration of 4.2 years (SD = 5.1), with 52% in their first episode and 48% classified as recurrent.1 Medication status included 60% medicated and 40% unmedicated participants.1 Symptom severity was primarily quantified using the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS-17), with a mean score of 22.7 (SD = 5.4).1 Other clinical scales were variably collected across cohorts but were not analysed here due to inconsistent availability.1

Image Acquisition and Preprocessing

Rs-fMRI was predominantly acquired on 3T scanners (~10% at 1.5T) using harmonized echo-planar imaging protocols (TR ~2,000 ms, TE ~30 ms, flip angle 90°, field of view 220 × 220 mm, 64 × 64 matrix, 3–4 mm slices, 180–240 volumes).¹² Preprocessing followed the REST-meta-MDD pipeline implemented in DPARSF and SPM12, including slice-timing correction, realignment, co-registration, DARTEL normalization to MNI space, 6 mm FWHM smoothing, regression of Friston-24 motion parameters, white-matter and CSF signals, linear detrending, and 0.01–0.08 Hz band-pass filtering. Global signal regression was not applied to avoid artifactual anti-correlations.¹³ BOLD time series were parcellated into 90 regions using the AAL-90 atlas to align with LEiDA and enhance anatomical interpretability.²

Data Analysis

Dynamic functional connectivity was quantified with Leading Eigenvector Dynamics Analysis (LEiDA).² Band-pass–filtered regional BOLD signals were Hilbert-transformed to obtain instantaneous phase, and at each TR the 90 × 90 phase-coherence matrix was decomposed to its leading eigenvector, representing the dominant whole-brain phase-locking pattern.² Leading eigenvectors from all participants and time points were concatenated and clustered using k-means with cosine distance across K = 2–20 (25 replicates per K), generating a hierarchy of recurrent phase-locking (PL) states that were spatially compared with canonical resting-state networks.²⁴ Primary dFC outcomes were fractional occupancy (FO; proportion of time in each PL state) and secondary outcomes included dwell time and transition probabilities. Between-group differences in FO were tested with two-sided label-permutation tests (5,000 permutations per state), controlling for multiple comparisons with Bonferroni correction across 209 PL states (α = 0.05/209). Effect sizes were summarized as Hedges g with 95% confidence intervals. All analyses were performed in MATLAB R2025a.²

Results

Hierarchical k-means clustering across K = 2–20 revealed a pyramid of recurrent phase-locking states showing continuity and fractionation across resolutions.² At low K, states recapitulated broad intrinsic connectivity patterns, whereas higher K solutions yielded more specialized and lateralized states overlapping visual, somatomotor, dorsal and ventral attention, limbic, frontoparietal, and default mode networks.² Cluster stability was moderate-to-high (median silhouette coefficients 0.31–0.67), with K ≈ 15–20 offering the best compromise between stability and neurobiological interpretability (Figure 1).²

Two states showed robust between-group FO differences surviving Bonferroni correction.² A state overlapping the posterior default mode network (DMN) at K = 18, cluster 4 (K18C4), with dominant loadings in posterior cingulate/precuneus, bilateral angular/inferior parietal cortex, and medial/lateral temporal regions including hippocampal–parahippocampal areas, exhibited significantly reduced FO in MDD compared with HC (MDD: 5.2%; HC: 7.1%; p < .001; Hedges g = −0.51; 95% CI [−0.73, −0.29]).²

Conversely, an occipito–parieto–temporal state at K = 20, cluster 18 (K20C18), engaging occipital visual cortex (calcarine, cuneus, lingual, fusiform), frontoparietal regions, temporal cortex, and hippocampal areas, showed higher FO in MDD than HC (MDD: 2.8%; HC: 2.2%; p < .001; Hedges g = 0.42; 95% CI [0.20, 0.64]).² Across all 209 states, FO differences were generally small and bidirectional, consistent with a redistribution of time across multiple network configurations rather than a uniform global shift in one group.² MDD showed reduced FO in a state overlapping posterior DMN (K18C4) and increased FO in a occipito‑parieto‑temporal state (K20C18). Signed FO differences (MDD − HC) are shown for all states, with these two states remaining significant after Bonferroni correction (p < .001).

Conclusion

This large-scale LEiDA analysis of REST-meta-MDD rs-fMRI data demonstrates that MDD is characterized by selective, bidirectional alterations in the temporal organization of resting-state networks: reduced occupancy of a posterior DMN state and increased occupancy of an occipito–parieto–temporal state relative to healthy controls.¹² These findings extend prior REST-meta-MDD reports of DMN hypoconnectivity by showing that posterior DMN abnormalities in MDD manifest as decreased time spent in a recurrent, anatomically well-defined DMN configuration, reinforcing the view that instability of DMN dynamics constitutes a systems-level hallmark of MDD.¹²

The overlapping posterior DMN state identified here is centered on posterior cingulate/precuneus, angular gyrus, and hippocampal–parahippocampal regions—midline territories implicated in self-referential and autobiographical processing and located near ventricular and perivascular corridors supporting infra-slow vasomotor and cerebrospinal fluid oscillations.²⁵ Reduced occupancy and potentially shorter dwell in this state in MDD align with mechanistic models in which impaired DMN stability contributes to maladaptive rumination, altered internal mentation, and affective dysregulation.¹² Concurrently, increased occupancy of an occipito–parieto–temporal state integrating visual, frontoparietal, temporal, and mnemonic regions suggests compensatory recruitment of perceptual and executive control systems and a dynamic rebalancing toward externally oriented, task-positive configurations in the context of DMN disruption, consistent with dynamic connectivity studies in MDD and broader dFC frameworks.²³⁴

Importantly, these dynamic alterations are detected in data processed with harmonized, physiology-suppressing pipelines, indicating that residual physiology–network coupling persists despite conservative denoising and motivating physiology-aware modeling of BOLD dynamics.¹² Recent work demonstrating network-specific phase coupling between DMN nodes and ventricular signals (BraVe modes) supports an integrated neurofluidic–neurophysiological account in which large-scale network dynamics in midline structures are partially shaped by brain-fluid interactions.⁵ Within this framework, LEiDA-derived PL states may capture joint neural and physiological contributions to DMN instability in MDD, highlighting the need for multimodal designs combining fMRI with direct physiological monitoring and interventions.²⁵

Methodological constraints—including physiological confounds, sensitivity of dFC metrics to analytic choices (parcellation, phase extraction, clustering, filtering), and clinical heterogeneity—limit causal interpretation and generalizability of the present findings.³⁴ Prospective work should triangulate across complementary dFC approaches, incorporate repeated-measures interventions, and adopt longitudinal, ethnically diverse cohorts with detailed symptom profiling and stratification by medication and episode status to test the transportability and clinical utility of LEiDA-derived markers.¹²⁴ Nevertheless, the current results reinforce LEiDA’s value for dissecting the temporal reorganization of resting-state networks in MDD and argue for integrative neurofluidic–neurophysiological frameworks as a path toward mechanistically grounded, personalized biomarkers beyond purely symptom-based nosology.²⁵
