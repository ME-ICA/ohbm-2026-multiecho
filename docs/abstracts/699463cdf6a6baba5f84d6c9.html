Introduction

For patients with drug-resistant epilepsy, the surgical removal of epileptogenic brain areas is their best chance at achieving seizure freedom. An essential component of presurgical evaluation is the delineation of brain areas generating seizures, which can predict postsurgical outcomes.1  In some patients, this delineation relies on intracranial EEG (iEEG), which uses electrodes implanted in the brain to record interictal epileptiform discharges (IEDs). However, iEEG has spatial coverage that is limited to the specific  brain areas in which electrodes are implanted, so it does not provide information about other brain areas not covered by electrodes. In this study, we leveraged simultaneous iEEG and functional MRI (iEEG-fMRI)2 acquired in patients with drug-resistant focal epilepsy to determine whether functional connectivity (FC) derived from fMRI could identify brain regions generating IEDs, as determined from iEEG.

Methods

Data were analyzed from eight patients undergoing simultaneous resting-state iEEG-fMRI acquired on a 3T GE Discovery MRI system (GE Healthcare, Waukesha, WI). Intracranial EEG data were continuously collected at 10 kHz using a SynAmpsRT amplification/digitization system and Scan 4.4 Software (Compumedics NeuroScan). Spoiled gradient recalled echo planar images (EPI) were acquired with the following parameters: TE = 30 ms, TR = 1500 ms, flip angle = 65°, 24-cm field of view, 64 × 64 matrix, 24 5-mm thick slices. Parameters of anatomical 3D T1-weighted images are as follow: TE = 3.8 ms, TR = 9.3 ms, flip angle = 12°, 24-cm field of view, 320 × 256 × 64 matrix, 2-mm thick slices. Three 20-minute scanning runs were acquired for each participant.

EEG data were corrected for gradient artifacts,3 downsampled to 2 kHz, bipolar-referenced between adjacent contacts, and notch-filtered at 60 Hz. fMRI data were quality-controlled for head motion, and the lowest-motion run per subject (framewise displacement < 0.5 mm) was selected. Data underwent standard preprocessing including motion correction, registration to structural images, and regression of white matter, cerebrospinal fluid, and motion-related signals. IEDs were manually marked on a bipolar montage of adjacent contacts on each electrode and averaged. The presence and peak amplitude of the IEDs were then derived for each bipolar channel. BOLD time series corresponding to each bipolar iEEG channel were extracted by combining 3×3×3 voxel neighborhoods centered on each contact of the bipolar channel. Static BOLD FC was computed between all pairs of such regions of interests.

Treating bipolar channels exhibiting IEDs as seeds for the connectivity analysis, we tested whether FC from these seeds to other IED channels was higher than the connectivity to non-IED channels using a distance-adjusted linear regression framework of the form:

〖zFC〗_(s,t)= β_0+ β_IED (〖IED〗_t )+ β_dist 〖Distance〗_(s,t)+ ε_(s,t)

 where zFC denotes Fisher z-transformed BOLD FC between a seed electrode s and a target electrode t; IEDt is a binary indicator (1 = target exhibits IEDs, 0 = no IEDs); and Distances,t is the Euclidean distance between the two electrodes. The regression coefficient βIED estimates the distance-adjusted difference in connectivity between IED-positive and IED-negative targets (for a given IED-generating seed). A positive βIED indicates stronger BOLD connectivity to IED-positive targets, adjusting for effects of spatial proximity.


 

Results

Across subjects, regression analyses revealed that a substantial proportion of IED-generating seed electrodes exhibited significantly stronger resting-state connectivity to IED-positive targets compared with non-IED targets after controlling for inter-electrode distance. The proportion of seeds showing a statistically significant IED-associated connectivity effect (p < 0.05) varied across patients, ranging from approximately 5% to 54% across patients. As illustrated in Figure 1, within each subject the distribution of z-transformed resting-state FC values was consistently shifted toward higher connectivity for IED-positive targets relative to non-IED targets. This pattern was observed across multiple electrode configurations, recording runs, and IED subtypes, although the magnitude of separation between IED and non-IED target distributions varied across patients.

Conclusion

Previous studies have mapped the extent of IED-generating brain areas by detecting BOLD responses to IEDs using simultaneous EEG-fMRI1 or by computing the FC from these BOLD responses to the rest of the brain.4,5 The current study shows that resting-state BOLD FC alone was consistently higher between IED-generating electrode contacts than between IED and non-IED targets, even after accounting for inter-electrode distance. These findings indicate that fMRI connectivity reflects the spatial organization of the irritative network. Given the limited spatial coverage of intracranial EEG, this relationship suggests that resting-state fMRI may help infer network involvement beyond directly sampled regions, although further work is needed to assess its utility for identifying epileptogenic networks without invasive recordings.
