Introduction

The human brain's capacities emerge from an intricate interplay between physical structure and dynamic functional activity. Common modalities for modeling brain networks include Diffusion Weighted Imaging (DWI), which maps white matter tracts to construct a structural connectome (SC), and functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI), used to derive a functional connectome (FC) from blood-oxygen-level-dependent correlations. While DWI provides a static anatomical scaffold, fMRI captures dynamic organization but lacks direct anatomical information. Integrating both modalities offers a holistic view, combining their synergistic information ^1 ^2.

A key application is predicting the age of the participants. We utilize this target as a powerful general benchmark task since age acts as a major confounder for pathologies and cognitive prediction ^3.

While traditional machine learning approaches rely on handcrafted features, deep learning models can learn complex representations deriving from raw data. Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) effectively model the non-Euclidean geometry of brain networks ^4. However, GNN application to multimodal brain age prediction is understudied compared to single-modality clinical classification. Existing research often focuses on disorder classification using small datasets or remains limited to traditional machine learning methods. Consequently, it remains unclear if combining SC and FC enhances performance or which fusion strategies are optimal.                       

To address this, we benchmark GNN backbones and fusion architectures using a large, publicly available dataset. We systematically compare early and late fusion strategies while controlling for backbone architecture. Our contributions include a systematic comparison of fusion methods within a GNN framework and a comprehensive analysis identifying recurrent optimal hyperparameter patterns across structural, functional, and multimodal settings.

Methods

The data was drawn from the publicly available Philadelphia Neurodevelopmental Cohort (PNC) ^5. We included 747 participants with structural diffusion imaging and resting-state functional neuroimaging available. The median age was 16.3 (IQR 13.5-18.5, min 8.2, max 23.1).

Processed neuroimaging data using fMRIPrep and QSIPrep was converted into connectomes with nodes defined by the Schaefer 400 atlas. Edges quantify the relationship between nodes, where SC edges reflect tractography-derived fiber counts and FC edges represent the Pearson correlation of regional activity. This produced two adjacency matrices of size 400x400, denoted AS and AF. We evaluated proportional edge densities of 5%, 10%, and 20% using a hybrid Maximum Spanning Tree backbone based technique.

We evaluated two families of fusion strategies, Early Fusion and Late Fusion, against the single-modality baselines:

the baseline employs a GNN backbone to encode the input graph into node features, followed by global pooling and an MLP for regression; 

Late Fusion utilizes a dual-stream approach where structural and functional connectomes are encoded independently, then aggregated before mapping to the target variable;

Early Fusion Multimodal Architecture treats the brain as a heterogeneous graph where different modalities act as distinct edge relations connecting a common set of nodes, employing relation-specific weight matrices during aggregation. The processed features are then pooled and mapped to the target variable via an MLP.

We evaluated different aggregation methods such as mean, add, max, and soft attention.

Experiments tested five GNN backbones: GCN, GAT, GATv2, ARMA, and Graph Transformer.

To address the lack of intrinsic node features in brain graphs, we implemented two initialization strategies: an identity matrix using one-hot encoding to track node identity, and a node connectivity based encoding ^4.

Performance was quantified using the coefficient of determination (R2) and the Mean Absolute Error (MAE) in years on the test set prediction with a 5-fold cross validation, resulting in 374 test samples, and is presented as R2-MAE.

Hyperparameter search budgets were 10 hours for single-modality and 20 hours for fusion architectures, covering optimizer, thresholding, backbone, and MLP settings.

Crucially, we decoupled hyperparameter tuning in the late fusion model to address distinct data characteristics. Independent optimization ensures that each stream adopts specific architecture settings (e.g., depth, width) best suited to the unique topology of SC vs FC.

Results

Results show that SC consistently served as a superior predictor compared to FC. GCN and ARMA models achieved the highest unimodal performance on structural data (0.583-1.68 and 0.585-1.68, respectively), while functional models exhibited significantly lower explained variance (0.314-2.125 to 0.388-2.037).

The integration of multimodal data provided heterogeneous results contingent on the fusion strategy employed.

• Late Fusion had a median improvement of 3.93% over structural baselines, with the GCN Late Fusion achieving the global maximum performance (0.639-1.565).

• Early Fusion: Conversely, early fusion frequently resulted in performance degradation (median change -0.20%).

Hyperparameter analysis revealed that SC data favored deep, narrow architectures (5-6 layers, 16-32 channels) while FC data necessitated shallow, wide architectures (2-4 layers, 128-256 channels). We argue that these differing architectural requirements, matched by the Late Fusion architecture, explain the performance drop in the Early Fusion model where structure is fixed for both modalities.

Conclusion

We conducted a systematic evaluation of early and late fusion Graph Neural Network architectures for brain age prediction using multimodal structural and functional brain connectivity. Our primary finding is that the choice of fusion architecture is critical for unlocking performance gains. We demonstrate that a late fusion approach, which processes each modality in a separate, optimized stream before integration, can outperform both single-modality baselines and the early fusion strategy pproposed. This study’s top-performing model was a GCN with late fusion that achieved an R2 of 0.639. Furthermore, our hyper-parameter analysis revealed distinct optimal configurations for structural and functional data, suggesting that modality-specific model design is essential for effective feature extraction.
