Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in processing complex human language. Research indicates that LLMs can effectively predict human brain activity during language processing. Notably, Tuckute G. et al. (2024) demonstrate that LLM text embedding can distinguish language network (LN) activations when human reads corresponding sentences. Yet it remains unclear whether such biological alignment extends beyond the linguistic component to complex cognition such as mathematical reasoning. To address this, this study compares fMRI-measured brain responses and an LLM-based encoding model to investigate if LLM representations align with the human multiple demand (MD) network during the processing of word problems requiring mathematical reasoning. 

Methods

Twenty-six healthy volunteers aged 20 to 28 years participated in this functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) study (M = 22.25, SD = 1.90; 14 females and 12 males). The stimuli consisted of 128 problem sentences (Fig. 1A), categorized into two conditions: 64 arithmetic word problems (AWP; requiring one-step math) and 64 nonarithmetic word problems (NWP; requiring reading comprehension). The problem sentences were split into two counterbalanced lists, and each participant performed one of the lists

fMRI data were acquired using a standard echo-planar imaging sequence and preprocessed using SPM12. We utilized the GLMsingle toolbox to extract single-trial beta estimates for each sentence, which served as the ground truth for the subsequent encoding model and RSA. Regions of Interest (ROIs) were defined meta-analytically using the Neurosynth database to ensure functional specificity. The MD network was defined by combining the regions identified from separate searches for “fronto-parietal network” and “salience network”, while the language network was defined using the term "language network", yielding 8 MD and 6 LN ROIs (Fig. 1B).

To investigate human-LLM alignment, we extracted embeddings from the Llama3.1 and FoxBrain and employed the following two approaches (Fig. 1B):

  1. Encoding model: We used PCA-reduced embeddings that retained components explaining 80% of the variance. Then, a ridge regression model (nested cross-validation) was trained to map these LLM features to the averaged BOLD responses across MD and LN. To ensure robust evaluation, we employed a nested cross-validation procedure consisting of an outer Leave-One-Group-Out loop and an inner 5-fold loop for hyperparameter optimization. Model performance was quantified by calculating the Pearson correlation (r) between predicted and actual observed brain activity. We assessed the statistical significance of the correlation, where a significantly positive r indicates successful alignment. Furthermore, to assess if the model captures specific mathematical reasoning, we employed a t-test to compare the predicted activations between AWP and NWP trials (Fig. 1C).
  2. RSA: We compared trial-by-trial neural Representational Dissimilarity Matrices (RDMs) using network-level RDMs constructed using all voxels within the MD and LN network. We then conducted ROI-specific RDM by constructing the top 100 most active voxels within each ROI and comparing with layer-wise LLM embeddings by calculating Spearman’s rank correlation for each layer. Statistical significance was assessed using a permutation test with 10000 iterations to generate a null distribution of correlation values (Fig.1C).

Results

The encoding model demonstrated a robust capacity to predict brain activation in the MD network. The analysis of prediction performance across all model layers and LLMs revealed that the MD network outperformed the LN.   Prior to controlling for multiple comparisons across layers and models, predictions derived from both Llama 3.1 and FoxBrain demonstrated a significant correlation with MD network activation. The best model was achieved by Llama3.1 at layer 10, yielding an overall correlation between the predicted and actual brain activation in the MD network was r = 0.33 (p adjusted = .003), indicating a better fit for arithmetic content. In contrast, the best prediction performance for the LN was notably lower, with an overall correlation of r = 0.12 (p adjusted = .348) (Fig. 2A). This suggests that the LLM embeddings capture features more relevant to the MD network's function than the LN's function in this specific task context. A key finding from the encoding model was its ability to distinguish between AWP and NWP conditions based on predicted activation magnitudes. In the MD network, the model predicted significantly stronger activation for AWP sentences compared to NWP sentences (t = 8.122, p adjusted < 0.001) (Fig. 2B). Conversely, in the LN, the encoding model failed to predict any significant difference in activation between AWP and NWP conditions (t = -0.206, p adjusted = .985).

Network-based RSA demonstrated no significant similarity with LLM representations for either AWP or NWP sentences. Furthermore, region-based analyses suggested that the left anterior insula, a key node of the MD network, showed a significant similarity with LLM representations for AWP sentences. This alignment was layer-specific, peaking in the middle layers of the model (layers 5 to 19). This suggests that the intermediate representations of the LLM map onto the neural computations of the left anterior insula during arithmetic word problem solving (Fig. 2C).

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a functional alignment between LLMs and the human MD network during AWP processing. Our encoding model reveals a similar neural pattern: LLM embeddings successfully predicted MD network activation and distinguished AWPs from NWPs, whereas they failed to predict or differentiate activation within the LN. This suggests that LLMs capture the underlying "cognitive demand" of the task rather than its linguistic surface form. Furthermore, RSA pinpointed this alignment to the left anterior insula—a key hub for human cognition. Collectively, these results indicate that LLMs possibly converge with human neural mechanisms not only in language processing, but in the nonlinguistic cognitive domain given the information is presented in the linguistic domain.
