Introduction

Cardiac pulsation mostly propagates through the cerebral vasculature and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), giving rise to harmonic oscillations in brain signals whose amplitudes reflect underlying vascular and tissue properties. Recent findings suggest a reduced harmonic complexity of brain parenchymal cardiovascular pulse waveforms in Alzheimer's disease, while in a healthy brain, pulse waveforms retain a complex shape (i.e. higher harmonics) 2. Thus, characterizing how these harmonic signatures relate to cardiovascular metrics across different brain regions may therefore provide insight into regional vascular compliance and CSF dynamics. While prior work has focused largely on the magnitude of cardiac pulsations potentially dominated by the fundamental harmonic alone (e.g. RSFA), the relative contribution of higher harmonics may provide complementary information about cerebrovascular stiffening and health. In particular, the ratio between higher-order and fundamental harmonic amplitudes may index alterations in pulse waveform shape associated with increased arterial stiffness and reduced pulsatile damping. In this study, we examined the relationship between cardiovascular metrics and cardiac pulse–induced harmonic oscillations in the brain, focusing on regional associations with the amplitude of the first harmonic (A1) and the ratio of second-to-first harmonic amplitudes (A2/A1).

Methods

1. Data Acquisition

A total of 116 healthy aging subjects (mean age ± SD: 48.25 ± 5.99 years, 64 M/52 F) were studied using a 3T SIEMENS MAGNETOM Prisma scanner (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a 64-channel head coil. Anatomical images were acquired using a 3D T1-weighted magnetization prepared rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE; TR =2.4 s, TE = 2.22 ms, TI = 1000 ms, FA =8°, voxel size = 0.8 ×0.8 ×0.8 mm3 ) and a 3D T2-weighted sampling perfection with application optimized contrast using different flip angle evolutions image (SPACE; TR = 3.2 s, TE = 564 ms, voxel size = 0.8 ×0.8 ×0.8 mm3 ). Resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) data were collected using a T2*-weighted multiband gradient-echo EPI sequence (TR = 800 ms, TE = 30.6 ms, flip angle = 52°, FOV = 220 mm, resolution = 2.0 × 2.0 × 2.0 mm. 72 slices, multiband factor =8), with each run consisting of 600 volumes. Cardiac and respiratory signals were recorded using a photoplethysmography pulse oximeter and a respiratory belt integrated with the scanner and synchronized to image acquisition.

2. Data Processing

Structural MRI data were preprocessed using the Human Connectome Project (HCP) pipeline3. Cardiac and respiratory contributions to the rs-fMRI signal were modeled using RETROICOR1 with second-order Fourier for cardiac and respiratory phases, along with motion regressor from the HCP pipeline output. For each voxel, Fourier coefficients corresponding to the first and the second harmonics (an and bn) were estimated, and harmonic amplitudes were calculated as An = sqrt(an2+bn2) for n = 1, 2. The ratio A2/A1 was subsequently calculated to index relative harmonic amplitude content (Fig.1). To identify voxels exhibiting meaningful cardiac pulsatility, a null distribution was generated using a Monte Carlo approach based on random phase regressors. Voxels exceeding a 3σ threshold (corresponding to 99.73% of the null distribution) were considered to exhibit significant cardiac coupling. Each subject’s A1 and A2/A1 maps were transformed into MNI space and spatially smoothed using a three-dimensional Gaussian filter with a standard deviation of the gaussian distribution = 2. To ensure consistent amplitude scaling across subjects, A1 was rescaled using each subject’s combined L2 norm within a common brain mask, matched to the cohort median.

Voxel-wise permutation testing was performed using FSL randomise to assess associations between harmonic measures and cardiovascular metrics, including systolic and diastolic blood pressure (SBP and DBP), pulse pressure (PP = SBP – DBP), mean heart rate (mean HR) derived from PPG, and consequent calculation of heart-rate variability indices - standard deviation of normal-to-normal intervals (SDNN), and root mean square of successive differences (RMSSD), while controlling for age and sex. Statistical inference was conducted using threshold-free cluster enhancement with 10,000 permutations and a significance threshold of p < 0.05.

Results

No significant associations were observed between either the A1 or the A2/A1 and blood pressure measures or heart-rate variability indices. In contrast, mean HR showed regionally distinct relationships with cardiac harmonic measures. The A1 exhibited a positive association with mean HR near the fourth ventricle and a negative association in the lateral ventricles (Figure 2a). The A2/A1demonstrated a complementary spatial pattern, showing positive associations with HR in cortical regions enriched in small vessels, including the paracingulate and precentral areas, and negative associations within the ventricular system, most prominently in the fourth ventricle (Figure 2b).

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that cardiac harmonic components contribute differentially to resting-state fMRI signal variability across the aging brain. The first harmonic amplitude (A1) was most prominent in large-vessel territories and ventricular CSF regions, consistent with efficient transmission of pulsatile energy along major cerebrovascular and CSF pathways. In contrast, the harmonic ratio (A2/A1) exhibited a broader spatial distribution extending into smaller-vessel territories, suggesting that higher-order cardiac harmonics may be more sensitive to pulsatile dynamics within the microvasculature.

The opposing relationships between HR and A1 across ventricular regions point to region-specific cardiac–CSF coupling. The positive association observed near the fourth ventricle likely reflects enhanced cardiac-driven CSF pulsatility at higher HR, potentially due to its proximity to brainstem vascular inflow and major arterial sources. Conversely, the negative association in the lateral ventricles may indicate attenuation of pulsatile energy as cardiac waves propagate rostrally through the ventricular system.

Associations involving the A2/A1 ratio may be microvascular sensitivity to cardiac dynamics. Positive correlations in small-vessel cortical regions, including the paracingulate and precentral cortices, suggest that increased HR amplifies higher-order vascular responses in the microcirculation, consistent with reduced vascular compliance or increased stiffness. In contrast, negative correlations in ventricular regions—particularly the fourth ventricle—may indicate a relative suppression of higher harmonics in CSF at faster HR, resulting in a waveform increasingly dominated by the fundamental component.

In conclusion, these findings indicate that HR modulates pulsatile forces in a regionally distinct manner across CSF spaces and cortical microvasculature. This functional dissociation between ventricular CSF dynamics and cortical microvascular pulsatility suggests that cardiac harmonic patterns, particularly higher-order components, may serve as sensitive markers of regional vascular compliance and early microvascular alteration.
