Introduction

Synesthesia is a condition in which a sensory experience is automatically and consistently elicited by an object, event, or concept in the absence of direct stimulation in the concurrent sensory domain 1. For example, sounds may evoke visual shapes, letters may be experienced as colored, or odors may be associated with specific perceptual qualities. Epidemiological evidence suggests a privileged role of vision in synesthesia: most synesthetes report visual concurrents, whereas synesthesia involving only non-visual modalities is comparatively rare 2. 

Several predictions can be made regarding the neural correlates of synesthesia. First, inducers such as numbers or lexemes may activate sensory areas associated with the concurrent experience. However, prior univariate fMRI studies have yielded inconsistent results, potentially due to a loss of activation specificity after spatial normalization 3. A second prediction is that neural activity patterns in modality-specific regions are expected to resemble those evoked by the actual sensory experiences. Multivariate pattern analysis (MVPA), including representational similarity analysis (RSA), provides a sensitive method for characterizing the similarity of neural response patterns at the voxel level. Here, we aimed to explore this second prediction applying RSA for the first time to the study of synesthesia, which allowed us to preserve neural specificity without requiring normalization.

We used this approach on a unique case of a congenitally blind individual (CB) with developmental synesthesia 4, who experiences number–texture and lexeme–texture associations. This participant allowed us to explore whether numbers and letters evoke structured texture coding in texture-sensitive regions, in particular in the somatosensory cortex.

Methods

Methods

Participants

A congenitally blind individual (CB) with developmental number–texture and lexeme–texture synesthesia participated in the study, along with seven sighted control participants matched for age and education level. All participants provided informed consent in accordance with the local ethics committee.

fMRI Data Acquisition

Participants completed two fMRI sessions. The main study session employed a block design with three tasks: a letter categorization task (vowel vs. consonant), a numbers categorization task (odd vs. even ), and a musical instruments categorization task (string vs. percussion). A separate texture localizer session included three tasks designed to independently identify texture-selective regions: a texture rating task, a shape rating task, and a movement task. 

fMRI Data Preprocessing

We performed MRI preprocessing using fMRIPrep 25.2.3, based on Nipype. Before RSA, a minimum spatial smoothing was applied to the functional images with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 2 mm using the SPM12 package

Regions of Interest (ROIs)

Analyses focused on both a priori and functionally defined regions of interest. The main ROI was the left secondary somatosensory cortex (S2), defined using the Julich Atlas, as this area is consistently associated with haptic texture perception 4. Exploratory ROIs included the bilateral posterior and medial collateral sulci (pCoS and mCoS), which are involved in visual texture processing 5. We hypothesized that they are recycled for haptic texture in blind individuals, and therefore involved in the synesthetic experience. Functional ROIs were extracted from the independent localizer tasks using the texture-shape and texture-movement univariate contrasts. A functional ROI was defined using six-millimeter spheres around peak activity in the left postcentral gyrus and each collateral sulcus.

First-level analysis

There were 12 experimental conditions related to the 12 numbers and 12 related to the 12 letters . The first-level analysis was computed using the SPM12 package. The brain signal related to the stimuli was modeled as boxcar function (with event duration equivalent to the reaction time) convolved with the canonical HRF in the time window between stimulus presentation and response. We then used the resulting T images (24 volumes, one for each condition) in the following analyses.

Representational Similarity Analysis (RSA)

Model Representational Dissimilarity Matrices (RDMs) were first constructed by asking CB to rate the perceived similarity between textures associated with his synesthetic experiences while processing numbers and letters. These model RDMs were then correlated with the brain RDMs of each participants, allowing us to assess whether patterns of neural activity reflected the structure of CB’s synesthetic texture associations. These analyses were performed using CoSMoMVPA. In the ROI- based RSA, the brain-based RDM was computed using the activity pattern of all voxels inside a given ROI. The second-order correlations between the brain-based RDM of this ROI and the model-based RDMs were then performed with a Pearson correlation method. In the Searchlight RSA, a brain-based RDM was calculated for each voxel using the pattern instantiated in the neighborhood of the voxel of interest within a 100 voxels sphere. We then computed the second-order correlations with each RDM model using a Pearson correlation method. A 6 mm spatial smoothing was then applied to the searchlight images. 

CB’s RSA results, including ROI correlation values and searchlight maps, were statistically compared with those of control participants using Crawford’s t-test, suitable for single-case comparisons. If the t-test was significant, the significance of CB’s and controls’ correlation was assessed through a permutation test in which the rows and columns of one of the model RDMs were randomly permuted and a correlation coefficient was calculated over 10000 iterations.

Results

No significant effects were found with letters, so only results for numbers are shown here. ROI analyses revealed first that, during the processing of numbers, correlation value with our synesthetic texture RDM in S2 was indeed significantly higher in CB than in controls (t(7) = 2.10, p = 0.037). As expected, we found a highly significant numbers-texture coding in CB (p = 0.007 permutation test) but not in our control participants (p = 0.093 permutation test). Results in the somatosensory functional ROI using the texture-shape contrast showed a very consistent pattern (t(7) = 2.93, p = 0.011; CB: p = 0.004 permutation test; controls p = 0.217 permutation test). We observed a similar pattern in the right pCoS, where the correlation value of CB was higher than that of controls (t(7) = 2.24, p = 0.039), with numbers-texture coding being significant in CB (p = 0.007 permutation test) but not in the control group (p = 0.880 permutation test). However, this result was only tangential in the functional ROI (t(7) = 1.76, p = 0.061). No other reliable group differences were observed.

Conclusion

In this study, we investigated the neural representation of synesthetic texture experiences in a congenitally blind individual using representational similarity analysis. Our results provide evidence that synesthetic concurrent experience is represented in modality-specific sensory cortices and preserves the perceptual structure of the corresponding real sensory experience.

First, we found that the left postcentral gyrus, including secondary somatosensory cortex (S2), showed robust texture coding during number processing in CB. Importantly, S2 is the only region that has been consistently found to be involved in haptic texture perception 4, and its engagement here indicates that numbers evoke structured texture representations in this area in CB. This effect was absent in control participants, supporting the interpretation that the observed coding reflects genuine synesthetic experiences rather than task demands or semantic processing. These findings demonstrate for the first time that synesthetic concurrent perception preserves the perceptual similarity structure of the actual sensory percept.

Beyond somatosensory cortex, we also observed texture similarity coding in the posterior collateral sulcus (pCoS), a region associated with visual texture processing in sighted individuals 5. The presence of such coding in a congenitally blind participant supports the idea that visual texture areas can be functionally recycled for haptic texture processing in the absence of visual input. Importantly, the involvement of pCoS in CB’s synesthetic texture experiences suggests that this cross-modal reorganization might extend beyond typical perception to synesthetic experience.

Further analyses will include connectivity analyses to better understand the mechanism underlying CB’s synesthesia.
