Introduction

Social determinants of health (SDoH) are powerful, non-biological drivers of psychiatric risk across development.^1 Growing evidence indicates that adverse SDoH shapes brain development, thereby altering neurodevelopmental trajectories. Whole-brain functional connectivity (FC) patterns have been shown to statistically mediate associations between socioeconomic conditions and cognitive outcomes in youth. However, whether similar FC-mediated mechanisms link SDoH to specific forms of psychopathology remains largely unexplored. Anxiety disorders (ANX), obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) exhibit overlapping disruptions in large-scale brain networks involved in emotion regulation and cognitive control. Psychosocial stressors associated with adverse SDoH may further exacerbate these network-level vulnerabilities, increasing risk for symptom development. 

Using data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study (N = 1,807; ages 9-10), the present study examines whether whole-brain FC mediates the association between SDoH and symptom severity of ADHD, OCD, and ANX in youth. We focus specifically on youth with OCD and/or ANX because these disorders typically emerge in late childhood, are highly sensitive to environmental stressors, and are characterized by early disruptions in large-scale brain networks. However, characterizing these pathways using neuroimaging data is computationally challenging due to the high dimensionality of functional connectomes. To address this, we employ the Directions of Mediation (DM) framework, a high-dimensional multivariate approach that identifies orthogonal linear combinations of FC edges that maximize the likelihood of the joint exposure-mediator-outcome model.^2 This approach enables the identification of distributed brain networks that transmit SDoH effects onto psychiatric symptoms, providing a systems-level account of social-neural-clinical pathways.

Methods

2.1 Datasets 

2.1.1 Participants

The Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study is a large-scale, multi-site longitudinal study tracking brain development and child health in the United States.3 The sample includes children aged 9-10 years at baseline, recruited to reflect the demographic diversity of the US population. Among the 11,878 children ages 9-10 years at baseline, approximately 10% (n=1807; girls: 826) presented with at least one diagnosis of OCD or ANX. Within this clinical subsample, 250 children (91 girls) exhibited comorbid OCD and ANX, with specific phobia being the most frequent co-occurring condition, followed by generalized anxiety disorder (GAD). Additionally, 648 children (262 girls) were identified with OCD only, and 940 children (473 girls) with ANX only.

2.1.2 Measures

Neuroimaging. Resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) data were acquired across 21 sites using harmonized ABCD scanning protocol.^4 We used preprocessed rs-fMRI data from the ABCD pipeline, which included correction for head motion, B0 field inhomogeneity, and gradient nonlinearity distortions. Cortical and subcortical parcellations were defined using the Glasser and Aseg atlases, yielding 360 cortical regions and 19 subcortical and cerebellar regions. For each participant, regional time series were extracted and pairwise FC was computed as Pearson correlations between all region pairs, resulting in a 379 × 379 rsFC matrix. The upper triangular elements of each rsFC matrix were vectorized to form the high-dimensional mediator matrix (M). To examine network-level effects, the 360 cortical regions were grouped into 12 functional networks, together with the 19 subcortical regions.

Social Determinants of Health (SDoH). SDoH measures consisted of neighborhood- and household-level indicators spanning seven domains: (1) bias toward minority groups, (2) education, (3) physical and health infrastructure, (4) natural environment, (5) socioeconomic status, (6) social context, and (7) crime and drugs.^5 To reduce dimensionality, we applied sparse principal component analysis (sPCA). The first sparse principal component (sPC1) was selected for mediation analyses, as it captured the largest proportion of variance, with higher sPC1 scores indicating greater socioeconomic disadvantage.

Psychopathology. Psychopathology for ADHD, OCD and ANX was assessed using parent-reported Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL) DSM-5-oriented scales.^4 T-scores ≥ 70 were considered clinically elevated, indicating a high likelihood of clinically significant symptoms.

2.2 High-dimensional Multivariate Mediation

We performed high-dimensional mediation analysis using the Directions of Mediation (DM) framework, as illustrated in Fig.1. The first sparse principal component of SDoH served as the exposure (X), psychiatric symptom severity (ADHD, OCD, or ANX) as the outcome (Y), and whole-brain rsFC edges as the high-dimensional mediator matrix (M). After standardizing X and Y, we fit a multivariate mediation model linking X, M, and Y. The resulting weight map was decomposed using singular value decomposition to derive orthogonal Principal Directions of Mediation (PDMs), which capture maximally mediating connectivity patterns. For each PDM, we estimated the path-a (SDoH→PDM), path-b (PDM→symptoms, conditional on SDoH), and direct (c′) effects, with mediation quantified as the product ab. Statistical inference was conducted using subject-level bootstrapping (5,000 resamples) to obtain empirical distributions of path coefficients and edge-wise weights, with significance assessed using two-sided bootstrap p-values (p<0.01).

Results

High-dimensional mediation analysis identified multiple principal directions of mediation (PDMs) that captured distinct patterns of FC associated with psychiatric symptom variation. While ADHD exhibited disorder-specific connectivity structures across PDMs 1–3, ANX and OCD shared substantial overlap in their connectivity patterns, particularly in lower-order components. Fig.2 shows, for each PDM, the top 10% of FC edge weights (ranked by absolute value) among statistically significant edges, shown separately for positive and negative components. Together, these results reveal both disorder-specific and shared transdiagnostic connectivity structures that vary systematically across PDMs.

PDM 1

For ADHD, PDM 1 was characterized by widespread positive and negative FC edges spanning multiple large-scale brain networks, including the default mode, frontoparietal, cingulo-opercular, dorsal attention, and visual networks. The extensive and diffuse nature of these connections suggests that PDM 1 reflects a global connectivity signature associated with ADHD-related variation.

In contrast, ANX and OCD shared a similarly dense and distributed pattern of connectivity in PDM 1, with extensive overlap across the same major networks. The presence of both positive and negative shared edges across systems indicates a broad transdiagnostic connectivity pattern common to anxiety and obsessive-compulsive symptoms. While the overall spatial distribution was comparable to ADHD PDM 1, the shared ANX-OCD edges emphasize common network-level alterations rather than disorder-specific effects.

PDM 2

ADHD PDM 2 showed a more focal connectivity pattern than PDM 1, with prominent involvement of control-related networks, including the frontoparietal and cingulo-opercular systems, as well as somatomotor and multimodal regions. Relative to PDM 1, this component exhibited fewer edges and reduced spatial spread, suggesting a shift toward more specific cross-network interactions in ADHD. For ANX and OCD, PDM 2 retained moderate overlap across networks, again involving default mode, control, and sensorimotor systems. However, the shared ANX-OCD connectivity patterns remained more spatially distributed than those observed in ADHD PDM 2. This contrast indicates that, at this intermediate level of variance, ADHD begins to show greater disorder specificity, whereas ANX and OCD symptoms continue to share common connectivity features.

PDM 3

In ADHD, PDM 3 was characterized by sparse connectivity, with relatively few significant edges surviving the top 10% threshold and limited spatial extent. Positive connections were primarily observed among default mode, frontoparietal, and visual networks, and no negative FC weights were identified in this component. This pattern suggests that PDM 3 captures a higher-order, lower-variance connectivity structure specific to ADHD.

Similarly, shared ANX-OCD PDM 3 connectivity was sparse and selective, with limited overlap across networks. The convergence of both ADHD-specific and ANX-OCD shared patterns toward sparsity at PDM 3 indicates that higher-order PDMs reflect subtler and more specialized functional organization, rather than widespread network-level dysconnectivity.

Conclusion

Across three principal directions of mediation, our findings reveal a structured hierarchy of FC patterns linking brain networks to psychiatric symptom dimensions. Lower-order PDMs captured broad, distributed connectivity configurations spanning multiple large-scale networks, whereas higher-order PDMs reflected increasingly focal and selective patterns. This organization is consistent with prior work demonstrating that dominant multivariate components of brain connectivity tend to index global network-level variation, while subsequent components capture more specialized and lower-variance structure. 

ADHD exhibited a clear progression from globally distributed connectivity in PDM 1 to more constrained and disorder-specific configurations in PDMs 2 and 3. This pattern suggests that ADHD-related symptom variation is associated with both widespread alterations in large-scale functional organization and more refined disruptions in higher-order control, sensory, and multimodal systems. Such hierarchical differentiation aligns with evidence that ADHD involves both diffuse network-level dysconnectivity and more specific alterations in executive and attentional systems, particularly at finer levels of variance.

In contrast, ANX and OCD symptoms showed substantial overlap in connectivity patterns within lower-order PDMs, indicating a shared transdiagnostic architecture characterized by broad involvement of default mode, control, sensory, and attentional networks. This convergence is consistent with prior neuroimaging studies reporting overlapping FC alterations across internalizing disorders and with dimensional models emphasizing common neural substrates underlying anxiety-related psychopathology. Notably, the persistence of shared connectivity into PDM 2 suggests that ANX and OCD share not only global but also intermediate-level network interactions, whereas disorder specificity becomes more apparent only in higher-order components.

Importantly, the degree of overlap between disorders systematically decreased across successive PDMs. Lower-order PDMs captured broad, shared network-level variation that may reflect general vulnerability or transdiagnostic processes, while higher-order PDMs isolated subtler, symptom-specific connectivity features. This hierarchical separation supports the conceptualization of psychopathology as arising from multi-level brain organization, in which global network alterations coexist with more selective circuit-level differences.

Together, these findings highlight the value of high-dimensional mediation approaches for disentangling shared versus disorder-specific neural mechanisms in psychiatric symptoms. By decomposing brain-wide FC into ordered mediation components, this framework provides a principled way to bridge transdiagnostic and disorder-specific perspectives, offering a nuanced account of how large-scale brain networks contribute to heterogeneous psychiatric presentations.
