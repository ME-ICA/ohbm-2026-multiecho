Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is an extremely common cardiac arrhythmia and is independently associated with cognitive decline and increased dementia risk, even in the absence of clinical stroke.1 The most common explanation for cognitive decline in patients with AF is subclinical white matter lesions.2 In light of growing knowledge of the close relationship between cognitive function and cardiac phase, as well as the general idea that bodily and neural rhythms and cognition are closely linked 3,4 the question arises as to whether arrhythmic heartbeats in AF are per se associated with alterations in brain function and cognition. We tested this hypothesis based on UK Biobank data by comparing AF patients with control participants who were matched for age, sex, vascular risk factors, and white matter lesion volumes. 

Methods

Participants: UK Biobank data were analyzed under an approved application. Participants with available resting-state fMRI, ECG, and cognitive assessments at the baseline imaging visit were screened; we excluded prior myocardial infarction, stroke/transient ischaemic attack, major neurological disorders (e.g., dementia, Parkinson’s disease, multiple sclerosis, epilepsy), intracranial hemorrhage, brain injury, and substance dependence. Missing covariates were imputed using predictive mean matching (mice, R), and then 1:2 propensity-score matching was performed (MatchIt, R). Covariates included age, sex, educational attainment, BMI, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, total cholesterol, blood glucose, smoking status, and white-matter hyperintensity (WMH) load. The final matched sample consisted of 354 AF patients and 708 controls.

MRI Processing: We analyzed resting-state fMRI volumes from UK Biobank’s standard pipeline, using the ICA-FIX–denoised outputs registered to MNI space. To assess robustness to preprocessing choices, nuisance regression was performed in two branches: (i) WM + CSF + linear trend (No Global Signal Regression (GSR)) and (ii) the same plus global signal regression (With GSR), followed by low-pass filtering (<0.1 Hz).

Connectivity & Hubs: Parcel time series were extracted using the Schaefer 400-parcel 7-network cortical parcellation and the Melbourne subcortical atlas. ROI-to-ROI Pearson correlations were computed and Fisher z-transformed. Network-Based Statistic (NBS)5 tested one-sided contrasts (AF>Control; Control>AF), adjusting for age, sex, diastolic blood pressure, WMH burden, and mean framewise displacement (FD). Primary thresholds t=2.1/2.5/3.0 were evaluated; component-level inference used 5,000 permutations with FWE correction (p<0.05). For each significant component, weighted eigenvector centrality ranked hub nodes.

Local Activity: Voxelwise fALFF maps were computed from template-masked MNI-space BOLD using the same NoGSR/WithGSR branches (fsl_glm, demeaned), with additional linear detrending and spatial smoothing (Gaussian σ=2.04; ~4.8 mm FWHM). fALFF was derived for broadband (0–0.1164 Hz) and main band (0.01–0.08 Hz) using fslpspec, defined as band-limited amplitude normalized by total broadband amplitude. Group differences were tested with permutation GLMs in FSL randomise (5,000 permutations), adjusting for age, sex, diastolic blood pressure, WMH burden, and mean FD (FWE-corrected p<0.05).

Cognition: Four composite domains were derived: Attention/Executive (Trail Making B, Tower Rearranging), Processing Speed (Reaction Time, Symbol Digit, Trail Making A), Memory (Numeric Memory, Paired Associate Learning, Prospective Memory), and Reasoning (Fluid Intelligence, Matrix Pattern Completion). Reaction Time and Trail Making scores were sign-flipped, so higher values indicate better performance. ANCOVA models adjusted for demographics and WMH/TIV, with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR across domains; Cohen’s d quantified effect sizes.

Results

Functional hyperconnectivity (AF>Control). NBS revealed a robust AF-associated hyperconnected subnetwork in the Schaefer cortex. Without GSR, a large component was detected across thresholds (t=2.1/2.5/3.0: 14,515/9,213/4,678 edges; all FWE p<0.0002). With GSR reduced component size but preserved significance (3,080/1,429/497 edges; FWE p≤0.0028). Eigenvector centrality mapped AF-related hubs primarily to peri-Sylvian operculo-insular and temporoparietal regions, dominated by Salience/Ventral Attention and Somatomotor systems (insula; parietal/central/frontal operculum; Heschl’s gyrus; planum temporale), with secondary Default-network involvement (orbitofrontal/anterior cingulate; inferior frontal gyrus). In the Melbourne subcortical atlas (NoGSR), AF>Control components were significant (t=2.1: 218 edges, extent p=0.0010/intensity p=0.0006; t=2.5: 120 edges, extent p=0.0006/intensity p=0.0002), with hubs centered on dorsal/ventral striatum and hippocampal head (putamen/caudate subdivisions; bilateral hippocampal head), and secondary contributions from amygdala, nucleus accumbens, and thalamus.

Control-dominant connectivity (Control>AF). After global signal regression, NBS additionally identified significant Control>AF components (t=2.1/2.5/3.0: 2,553/1,083/298 edges; FWE p≈0.01–0.013). Hubs were dominated by Default and Somatomotor systems, mapping to orbitofrontal/ventromedial prefrontal and cingulate midline regions (frontal orbital cortex; paracingulate/anterior cingulate; posterior cingulate) alongside peri-rolandic sensorimotor cortex (pre/postcentral gyri; superior parietal and parietal opercular regions).

Reduced spontaneous activity (fALFF; Control>AF). No voxelwise FWE-corrected ALFF effects were observed in either preprocessing branch. In contrast, all corrected fALFF effects favored controls over AF, with no significant AF>Control clusters. With GSR, a focal broadband Control>AF cluster was detected (643 voxels; 5,144 mm³; mean t=5.21). Without GSR, Control>AF effects were more extensive in both broadband (4,388 voxels; 35,104 mm³; mean t=5.24) and main band (3,194 voxels; 25,552 mm³; mean t=5.18). Hubs are predominantly located in occipito-parietal (lateral occipital cortex/occipital pole; angular/posterior supramarginal; precuneus/superior parietal), plus anterior prefrontal/orbitofrontal and operculo-insular regions; subcortical contribution was minimal.

Cognitive outcomes. AF participants showed slower Processing Speed compared to controls (p=0.009, FDR q=0.038, d=0.25), while Attention/Executive, Memory, and Reasoning showed no group differences after FDR correction.

Conclusion

Across matched UK Biobank participants, including matching for white matter lesions, AF was associated with a reproducible pattern of functional reorganization across preprocessing branches: large-scale hyperconnectivity centered on operculo-insular/peri-Sylvian cortex and striatal–hippocampal circuitry, alongside reduced spontaneous low-frequency activity (fALFF) predominantly in occipito-parietal association/visual territories. These functional alterations co-occurred with a selective cognitive deficit in processing speed. 

Together, the findings support our hypothesis that the arrhythmia in AF itself is associated with functional neural changes of interoceptive/salience and autonomic control circuits, as well as changes in cognition. Our findings motivate longitudinal and interventional studies to test whether rhythm management can modify these neural signatures and improve cognitive health.
