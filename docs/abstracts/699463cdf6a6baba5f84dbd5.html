Introduction

The anterior–posterior (long-axis) organization is a fundamental organizational principle of the hippocampus, supporting its functional segregation—posterior regions in cognition and memory, anterior regions in emotion and stress regulation1,2. Recent large-scale neuroimaging studies have identified distinct progression subtypes in schizophrenia, notably a “hippocampus-initiating” trajectory characterized by early structural decline in the hippocampus followed by spread to subcortical and cortical regions3. This finding underscores the hippocampus as a critical epicenter in disease pathogenesis. Yet, how schizophrenia disrupts the specific structural–functional architecture of hippocampal organization across these dimensions remains largely unexplored.

Methods

Our study utilized multi-modality MRI from Zhangjiang International Brain Biobank (ZIB) Consortium with 1942 individuals (968 patients with schizophrenia, age=29.2 ±12.0 years). All individuals with schizophrenia were diagnosed according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, fourth edition (DSM-IV). All MRI images underwent HCP minimal preprocess. Schizophrenia progression subtypes and individual disease stages were derived using the Subtype and Stage Inference (SuStaIn) algorithm applied to z-scored gray matter volume deviations across 17 brain ROIs based on T1w images3. We used the HippUnfold tool4 to segment the hippocampus and generate its mid-thickness surfaces. Hippocampal-cortical connectomes were created by correlating rs-fMRI time series between hippocampal vertices and cortical and subcortical regions (defined by the Glasser atlas5 and Tian atlas6). A diffusion embedding method yielded the hippocampal functional gradient (Fig. 1a), and cortical projections were computed by taking the dot product between the gradient and the hippocampal functional connectivity for each cortical region. All progression effects were studied using generalized additive models.

Results

Here, we corroborated the presence and functional significance of a dual long-axis representation—encompassing both linear and quadratic gradients (lLG and qLG)—and a quadratic medial-lateral gradient (qMG) within the hippocampal connectome in schizophrenia patients. As illustrated in Figures 1a and 1b, the linear long-axis gradient of hippocampal connectivity is progressively disrupted and reorganized with disease advancement, particularly in the anterior CA2/3 subfield and the posterior CA1/subiculum regions. These alterations suggest a breakdown in the normal topographic organization of hippocampal–cortical communication. By projecting these hippocampal connectome gradients onto the neocortex and tracking how each cortical region’s connectivity profile with the hippocampus evolves across illness stages, we identified a spatially specific pattern of involvement. Notably, the inferior parietal lobule—predominantly situated within the default mode network (DMN)—exhibited the most pronounced changes in hippocampal coupling during disease progression. This was followed by regions belonging to both ventral and dorsal attention systems, implicating higher-order associative networks in the pathophysiology of hippocampal dysconnectivity in schizophrenia. 

Conclusion

Together, these findings highlight that schizophrenia-related hippocampal disorganization is not uniform but follows a structured, gradient-based disruption that preferentially affects transmodal cortical systems involved in internally directed cognition and attentional control, thereby linking microstructural hippocampal pathology to large-scale network dysfunction.
