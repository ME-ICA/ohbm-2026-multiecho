Introduction

Although there is a substantive literature on a form of childhood maltreatment (CM) called abusive head trauma (AHT, formerly referred to as shaken baby syndrome), there is surprisingly little work on the association between CM and lifetime prevalence of traumatic brain injuries (TBI) in children with a history of CM. AHT is most commonly found in children under five and is frequently catastrophic, resulting in major life-long disability or death. However, throughout the life of a child who experiences CM, there is a greater risk of head injury from discipline practices that result in undiagnosed TBI. A recent review of emergency department (ED) head injuries estimates the abuse-related TBI to be close to 15% of all TBI ED with many more likely left untreated (Kazemi, et al., 2025). It is difficult to get an estimate on the incidence of CM-related TBI in the general population, particularly given parental concerns about the fallout from medical intervention in abuse-related cases where the TBI is milder.

Childhood maltreatment (CM) is considered both more severe than other violence exposure and necessitates referral to CPS. Hence, CM presents an immediate opportunity for intervention where targeted programs could be developed and delivered state-wide or nationwide. At the same time, it is necessary to control for known risks such as poverty and athletic participation. To develop targeted intervention and prevention strategies, it is crucial to understand the longitudinal developmental course of CM and CDC's four-level social-ecological model (Wilkins et al, 2014) risk factors. The aim of this study is to use a large dataset (the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) study) of 12,000 youth from ages 10 through 17 to develop and test a well-controlled multilevel model of the developmental course of CM and later TBI.

Methods

Sample and CM operationalization. The Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development study (ABCD, N = 12,000) will be used to identify CM in ABCD. The ABCD Study aimed to establish a nationally representative baseline by recruiting youth from 21 geographically dispersed sites, primarily through probability sampling of schools, supplemented by camps and volunteers. Rationales for choosing variables are detailed in a manuscript under review; however, in brief, Dr. Perkins has identified six guidelines for variable identification according to WHO, CAPTA, and NIS. The CM baseline cohort (n=1,505) includes subtypes of CM; physical abuse (n = 202), sexual abuse, (n = 73), chemical abuse (n = 102), physical neglect (n = 559); emotional neglect (n = 148), medical neglect (n = 241) and witnessing intimate partner violence (IPV n = 248), with significant subtype overlap.

Specific aims.

To complete the study aim, the study will: 

Specific Aim 1: Model the association of CM to TBI in ABCD. 

H1: Baseline CM will be associated with chronicity later TBI. 

Specific Aim 2: Fit nationally weighted multilevel models of youth (including prematurity, disability status, sports engagement, interpersonal relationships), family (including poverty, interpersonal violence, and parental mental health), and neighborhood (including school, crime, and neighborhood cohesion) risk and protective factors on the development of TBI in ABCD.

H2: Multilevel models of youth, family, and neighborhood risk and protection factors mediate or moderate the development of TBI following CM during adolescence.

Measurement.

Demographics - Income to needs was calculated as a ratio of income to family size (Sripada et al., 2022). The highest parental education was assessed as the years of education completed by the most educated parent. Poverty combined indicators are taken from the ABCD demographics using six questions of food insecurity, utility and phone cut-off, eviction, inability to pay rent, and ten people or more in a household. The ten item race variable was converted to four factors, white, Black, other/mixed, and Asian. Marital status of the primary parent was converted to a binary variable, currently married and not currently married. 

Outcome - TBI is assessed using the Ohio Traumatic Brain Injury Form parent report including worst TBI, whether you have ever been in the hospital or ER for a head or neck injury, number of TBIs with loss of conciseness, earliest age of TBI, and number of periods with multiple or repeated injuries.

Analysis plan.

The CM cohort was compared to the full non-CM ABCD cohort at baseline on the four outcome measures of TBI using t-test. Each CM case was case-matched based on demographic factors such as age, gender, and SES.

Data were imputed using the midastouch: Multiple Imputation by Distance Aided Donor Selection methods in Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) package in R. Matching was conducted using the nonparametric preprocessing for parametric causal inference package MatchIt and the nearest method. 

Data were matched on demographic variables including marital status of the caregiver, sex, age at interview date, income-to-needs, parental highest year of education, race and ethnicity and poverty as measured by home address Area Deprivation Index (ADI) and six items assessing home poverty. Linear models including MICE imputed and matchit subclass to compare CM youth to matched cases on the four TBI outcomes.

Results

The CM cohort identified in the ABCD study included 1338 youth (11.3% of the full ABCD baseline cohort) without WIPV. There were 1505 (12.7%) when WIPV was included. Membership in the CM cohort was defined through evidence of exposure to abuse or neglect within the family and evidence that this exposure caused harm or endangerment. The original operationalization of CM included TBI from shaking prior to age 6 and multiple TBIs prior to age 4 (see Figure 1). For the purpose of these analyses we kept all prior criteria accept those two TBI criteria which reduced the CM with WIPV cohort to 1448.

Out of the nearly 12,000 youth in the ABCD baseline sample (aged 10), 96% have not experienced a TBI and nearly 88% reported that they had never been treated in a hospital or emergency room for a hear or neck injury. Of those who had been treated in the hospital for head or neck injury 94.8% (n = 2427) did not have a suspected TBI. From the full sample 322 had a head injury that was not suspected to have been a TBI while only 135 had a suspected TBI. Most of those were considered “possible mild” (n =128, 1.1%). Ninety-three had one TBI (3.6%), 37 had two (1.4%) and four had three or more (0.1%). Of those who reported a prior TBI, 42.5% were prior to age five.

Compared to the full sample of ABCD, our CM cohort with the TBI questions removed was not more likely to have prior experience with with treatment for a head or neck injury (t = 3.110 (1793,1), p = .002), had worse TBIs (t = 2.016 (1705,1), p < .04) but was not more likely to be a different age or have more periods of repeated TBI. When compared to case match youth the CM cohort was less likely to have experienced a head or neck injury (z = -2.577 (3008,1), p = .01) and have more periods of TBI and more likely to have multiple periods of TBI (z = 3.59 (3008,1), p = .001).

Conclusion

Childhood maltreatment (CM) has a known connection to TBI beyond those that are termed abusive head trauma. However this work is based on studies of emergency department visits. Little is known about the relationship between TBI and CM in a normative sample. Using the rich medical and developmental history data and national and international guidelines for what is abuse and neglect a cohort of probable CM was identified in ABCD. Although this cohort is only 11% of the full ABCD cohort it is closer to expected rates of CM in the population than prior work examining CM using KSADS alone (n = 134, 1.1%).4 Association with TBI in this population was examined both with and without the use of two TBI items that are suspicious for abuse and used in the original operationalization of physical abuse (shaking before age six and multiple TBIs before age four). CM was associated with TBI across the whole study both with and without the inclusion of TBI in the definition of CM. When each case was matched on a series of demographic measures, CM and TBI remained associated. Specifically, CM was associated with more visits to the ED for head and neck injuries and with a greater likelihood of multiple periods with TBIs. This suggests that TBI is an vastly understudied subject in CM research. Future research should examine the longitudinal trajectory of TBI following CM, the role of risk factors such as disability, and conduct multilevel modeling controlling for athletic participation. Future research should examine structural and functional correlates of TBI and CM in ABCD.
