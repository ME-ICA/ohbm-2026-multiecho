Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a multisystem neurodegenerative disorder characterized by marked heterogeneity in clinical symptoms, age of onset, and disease progression. Although diagnosis is driven by cardinal motor signs such as bradykinesia, rigidity, and tremor, many individuals experience prominent neuropsychiatric symptoms—including depression, anxiety, cognitive dysfunction, sleep disturbance, and visual deficits—that often emerge during the prodromal stage and substantially impact quality of life [1,2]. This phenotypic variability complicates mechanistic understanding and limits the development of personalized treatments targeting both motor and non-motor symptoms. Current therapies, including dopamine replacement and neuromodulation, provide limited benefit for neuropsychiatric dysfunction.  

To address this unmet need, we applied machine-learning methods to identify latent brain–behavior dimensions underlying motor and neuropsychiatric symptoms across prodromal and clinical PD. Using multimodal data from the Parkinson’s Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI), including resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) and behavioral measures from 211 individuals with clinical PD and 205 prodromal participants, we sought to determine whether large-scale functional connectivity organizes symptom heterogeneity along interpretable dimensions. Building on prior work demonstrating latent brain–behavior architectures in psychiatric and neurodevelopmental disorders [3,4], we hypothesized that PD symptoms would decompose into dissociable dimensions separating overall symptom burden or affective valence from orthogonal modes of symptom expression.

Methods

Data were obtained from the Parkinson’s Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI), a large, multicenter observational study. Analyses focused on baseline rs-fMRI and behavioral data from 211 individuals with clinically diagnosed PD and 205 prodromal participants identified by genetic or clinical risk factors and striatal dopamine deficits. Clinical measures included the Movement Disorder Society–Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS), Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS), and State–Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI).

Resting-state fMRI preprocessing, quality control, and motion correction followed pipelines previously developed and validated by Buch and colleagues [4], incorporating standard normalization, artifact removal, and control for motion-related confounds known to induce spurious connectivity effects [5]. Whole-brain resting-state functional connectivity (RSFC) features were computed as pairwise Pearson correlations between regional BOLD time series extracted from a validated functional parcellation atlas. Only scans passing strict quality-control thresholds were included.

To identify latent brain–behavior relationships, we applied regularized canonical correlation analysis (RCCA) within a robust cross-validation framework [4]. RSFC features were related to clinical symptom measures separately for each scale (UPDRS, GDS, STAI). To address the high-dimensional, low-sample-size regime, principal component analysis (PCA) was applied independently to RSFC features and clinical symptom matrices prior to RCCA. This PCA→RCCA approach reduced overfitting, stabilized covariance estimation, and emphasized shared structure between brain connectivity and clinical symptoms rather than spurious correlations driven by collinearity. Model performance was assessed using cross-validation on held-out data. Statistical significance of RSFC loadings was evaluated using false discovery rate (FDR) correction.

Results

Across motor, depression, and anxiety domains, RCCA consistently identified two dissociable brain–behavior dimensions. For motor symptoms (UPDRS), Dimension 1 captured global motor–functional disease severity, whereas Dimension 2 isolated motor phenotype, distinguishing tremor-dominant from akinetic–rigid/axial features. Severity-related dimensions were associated with widespread cortico-subcortical reorganization, including prefrontal, limbic, associative, and brainstem networks, while phenotype dimensions emphasized motor, basal ganglia, and visuomotor circuitry. 

For depression (GDS), Dimension 1 reflected a bipolar mood valence axis spanning depressive affect and existential distress versus positive mood and life satisfaction. Dimension 2 captured psychological vitality, indexing energy, engagement, and behavioral activation largely independent of affective valence. Valence dimensions involved distributed default-mode and frontoparietal networks, whereas vitality dimensions preferentially engaged sensorimotor, salience, and motivational circuits.  

For anxiety (STAI), Dimension 1 indexed global anxiety severity, spanning psychological security versus anxious distress. Dimension 2 dissociated anxiety expression style, separating cognitive–somatic tension and worry interference from emotional ease and coping capacity. Anxiety severity was associated with distributed prefrontal, cingulo-opercular, and associative networks, while expression style emphasized salience, sensorimotor-adjacent, and attentional circuitry.  Across domains, these findings reveal a shared organizing principle in which overall symptom burden or affective valence is dissociable from orthogonal modes of symptom expression, each supported by distinct large-scale functional network architectures.

Conclusion

Using machine-learning–based multivariate modeling of resting-state functional connectivity, we demonstrate that motor and neuropsychiatric symptoms in Parkinson’s disease are organized along dissociable latent brain–behavior dimensions. Across motor, depression, and anxiety domains, a dominant severity or valence axis is consistently separable from orthogonal phenotype or expression axes, revealing structured rather than unitary symptom heterogeneity. This dimensional framework extends prior work in psychiatric disorders to neurodegenerative disease and suggests that similar large-scale network principles govern symptom organization across diagnostic boundaries [3,4]. Importantly, these findings move beyond total severity scores to identify circuit-level mechanisms underlying how symptoms are expressed. Such brain–behavior dimensions may provide a foundation for early detection, prognostic stratification, and the development of targeted, circuit-informed therapeutic interventions addressing both motor and neuropsychiatric symptoms in Parkinson’s disease.
