Introduction

When confronted with a stimulus, the brain reacts in a way that is not only specific to the input but also to the individual. Although Magneto- and Electroencephalography (M/EEG) are able to capture the temporal evolution of these reactions, interpreting the acquired high-dimensional data still poses a significant challenge. Recently, multivariate pattern analyses (MVPA) have achieved considerable success in decoding stimuli and capturing representational dynamics but remain limited to being descriptive; that is, high decoding accuracy tells us that information is present, but not how it is generated mechanistically. Consequently, some fundamental questions remain unresolved. For instance, it is still debated whether the brain’s reaction consists of an additive signal imposed on ongoing activity or a phase-resetting of ongoing oscillations, both deemed plausible regimes that classical heuristic methods cannot conclusively distinguish1. Furthermore, growing evidence indicates that the exact composition of these responses differs considerably between subjects, a variability that standard group-level studies largely overlook. Addressing this requires a methodological shift from descriptive to generative modeling.

In order to approach these questions we utilize genephys2, a simulator capable of producing realistic M/EEG data under biologically plausible regimes. However, finding the specific parameters that explain a given subject’s data constitutes an intractable inverse problem due to the inherent stochasticity of the simulator (similar to that of a real brain). To overcome this limitation, we introduce a novel framework combining genephys with Simulation-Based Inference (SBI). By leveraging neural density estimators to approximate posterior distributions directly from simulations, we can estimate parameters of interest underlying individual stimulus processing.

Methods

We developed an end-to-end inference pipeline that integrates the generative model genephys with Simulation-Based Inference (SBI) techniques.

Generative Model: Genephys simulates multichannel electrophysiological time series based on phenomenological mechanisms. Baseline autoregressive oscillations are perturbed upon stimulus onset via two primary regimes, Phase Resetting or Additive Oscillatory Response. Both regimes can be combined with Amplitude Modulation and/or Additive Non-Oscillatory Response representing slow evoked currents. These regimes are controlled by a high-dimensional parameter space, enabling the generation of synthetic data resembling the spectral and temporal complexity of empirical M/EEG.

Temporal Generalisation Matrix: To efficiently capture the high-dimensional time courses, we computed the temporal generalization matrix (TGM) for each dataset. The diagonal of the TGM reflects moment-to-moment decodability, while the off-diagonal reflects temporal generalization, that is, the stability of the neural code over time. TGMs therefore provide a compact, data-driven summary of stimulus representations that is suitable for SBI.

Simulation Based Inference: SBI addresses the intractability caused by stochastic simulators lacking explicit likelihoods3. Which would naturally prevent traditional Bayesian inference on the parameters of interest. Instead, SBI learns the conditional probability distributions from the parameter-simulation pairs. This enables inference without having to evaluate likelihoods directly. Importantly, this approach allows for amortized inference: once trained, inference runs rapidly on new subjects.

Workflow: To invert genephys, we specifically utilized Neural Posterior Estimation. We generated a large training corpus of synthetic datasets (N > 20,000) sampled from a broad plausible prior distribution over regimes and parameters. For each simulation, we computed TGMs. A density estimator was then trained to learn an approximation of the posterior distribution of the generative parameters given the observed data pairs.

Validation Strategy: The pipeline underwent two phases of validation. First, synthetic recovery tests assessed identifiability, by using artificial data with a known ground truth, to quantify how well the true generative parameters could be recovered. Second, we applied the framework to empirical MEG data from Cichy et al.4 a benchmark MEG dataset containing responses to 118 object categories across 15 subjects. This step was critical to demonstrate that the model can recover parameters given real data. We quantified success by the ability of the inferred model to reproduce parameters that can recover the structure of empirical TGMs, specifically of regions of specific interest as the diagonal decoding trajectory and off-diagonal generalization patterns.

Results

Validation on Synthetic Data: The simulation analyses confirmed that the genephys-SBI pipeline is effective at solving the inverse problem. The framework achieved high accuracy in parameter recovery as well as identifying the correct generative regime significantly above chance. Specific parameters of interest such as response latency, oscillatory frequency, and phase difference were recovered with high precision.

Application to Empirical MEG Data: When applied to the Cichy et al. dataset³, the pipeline inferred subject-specific parameter configurations that were able to produce TGMs similiar to real dynamics of human visual processing. Posterior predictive checks demonstrated that the empirical TGMs fell within the high-probability density of the simulated distribution. Quantitatively, the correlation between empirical and simulated TGMs exceeded r = 0.85 (group average), improving over previous fitting attempts (r = .70) ⁴. This confirms that our pipeline is able to capture important aspects of the generative dynamics of the visual evoked response.

Between Subject Variablity: Beyond fitting average performance, the inference revealed inter-individual variability in the underlying mechanisms. The amortized posterior estimates indicated that some subjects responses varied considerably, either in inferred regime but also in the specific parameter values, leaving open the question of a potential fingerprinting. This mapping highlights that variations in "how" a brain processes a stimulus, could be identifiable features of an individuals neural architecture.

Conclusion

By combining empirical but interpretable modeling with Simulation-Based Inference, we begin to bridge the gap between descriptive decoding analyses and mechanistic understanding. We demonstrate that it is possible to invert a complex and stochastic model of electrophysiology to recover estimates of biological parameters from non-invasive data. The application to the Cichy et al.4 dataset serves as a proof-of-concept that individual differences in neural processing are tractable and quantifiable. We show that the temporal generalization matrix can be unpacked into specific, identifiable processes, such as the timing of phase resets or the activation of additive oscillations, providing a view of neural dynamics that heuristic methods cannot access.

Additionally, by quantifying between-subject variability within this framework, we open interesting options for biomarker discovery. The probabilistic nature of the approach ensures uncertainty quantification, distinguishing between genuine individual differences and estimation ambiguity. Importantly, this framework is not limited to specific stimuli or experimental designs but rather allows for inference across a wide array of task paradigms, from passive viewing to attention and memory tasks, opening up a wide array of possible follow up questions.
