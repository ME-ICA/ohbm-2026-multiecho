Introduction

Everyday listening situations often involve competing speakers or background noise, placing demands on cognitive resources even in young normal-hearing listeners. Such effortful listening increases listening effort—the allocation of attentional and cognitive control resources to maintain speech understanding under adverse conditions. When speech intelligibility decreases, listeners recruit additional cortical regions, including the cingulo-opercular network, inferior and middle frontal cortices, and insula, reflecting increased listening-related control demands.1,2 Further, pupillometry provides a sensitive physiological marker of listening effort, with larger pupil dilation reflecting higher processing demands3. The only prior study combining fMRI and pupillometry reported greater pupil dilation and enhanced neural activity in temporal and frontal regions under more challenging speech conditions4, but this relationship remains insufficiently characterized. The current study therefore aimed to examine the link between neural activity and pupil dilation during speech-in-noise perception. We hypothesized (1) greater pupil dilation with worse speech-reception thresholds (SRTs), (2) a negative association between SRT levels and BOLD response amplitudes in the cingulo-opercular network, inferior parietal lobule, inferior and middle frontal cortices and the insula (3) a positive association between pupil dilation and neural activity in superior temporal gyrus, cingulate cortex, superior frontal gyrus, inferior frontal gyrus, precentral gyrus, frontal operculum and putamen, and (4) modulation of this coupling by SRT levels.

Methods

Twenty-five healthy young adults with normal hearing participated. After exclusion for technical issues and head motion (>3 mm), data from 20 participants (14 women; mean age = 23.9 ± 3.4 years) were analyzed. All participants were right-handed, free of tinnitus and neurological or psychiatric disorders, and had hearing thresholds ≤ 20 dB HL from 125–8000 Hz (determined by pure-tone audiometry). The study was approved by the ethics committee of the Carl von Ossietzky Universität Oldenburg. Speech intelligibility levels were determined using the Oldenburg Sentence Test (OLSA)5 to establish 50% and 80% speech-reception thresholds (SRTs) at a fixed 65 dB SPL noise level. During simultaneous fMRI and pupillometry, participants performed an auditory word-recognition task using Oldenburg Linguistically and Audiologically Controlled Sentences (OLACS) based on recorded stimuli from a previous study2. Four conditions were presented: speech in noise at 50% and 80% intelligibility, clear speech, and a rest condition. A sparse-sampling design (TR = 9000 ms) enabled presentation during silent periods and reliable pupil recordings. Participants fixated a fixation cross on the screen and indicated via button press whether a probe word had occurred in the preceding sentence. Each condition included 40 trials (total = 160). MRI data were acquired on a 3 T Siemens Prisma scanner (20-channel head coil): T1-weighted images: MPRAGE (TR = 2000 ms, TE = 2.07 ms, 0.75 mm isotropic); Functional images: gradient-echo EPI (TR = 9000 ms, TE = 35 ms, flip angle = 90°, 38 slices, voxel = 3.4 mm², FOV = 220 mm²). Auditory stimuli were presented through MR-compatible headphones (Opto Active, Optoacoustics Ltd.), and pupil diameter was recorded from the right eye using an EyeLink 1000 system (SR Research Ltd.). Behavioral accuracy across conditions was analyzed via repeated-measures ANOVA using JASP. Pupillometry data were analyzed with R and mean pupil dilations were baseline-corrected relative to the pre-stimulus noise period and averaged per condition. fMRI preprocessing and modeling were performed in SPM12 (Wellcome Centre for Neuroimaging, London, UK). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, family-wise error (FWE) corrected at the cluster level (p < 0.001 cluster-forming threshold).

Results

Mean thresholds in the SRT test were -9.56 for the 50% and -8.66 for the 80% condition. For the experimental task accuracies of 67.1% correct were achieved in the 50% condition, 73.5% correct were achieved in the 80% condition and 88.9% were achieved in the clear speech condition. A repeated-measures ANOVA showed a significant effect of listening condition (F(2,38) = 56.731, p <0.001, η2=0.749). The accuracies for the 50% and 80% intelligibility conditions were significantly different from each other (p=0.005) and both were significantly different from the accuracy obtained in the clear speech version (both p <0.001). In the fMRI data analysis, we first examined general activation patterns across all participants for clear speech, 50% intelligibility, and 80% intelligibility compared with rest. All three contrasts elicited robust bilateral activation in auditory cortices, visual cortex, supplementary motor cortex, inferior and middle frontal gyri, precentral gyrus, angular gyrus, insula, and cerebellum. Comparisons between conditions revealed that 50% intelligibility, relative to clear speech, was associated with higher BOLD response amplitudes in the right frontal operculum. The 80% intelligibility condition, relative to clear speech, showed increased activity in the left cerebellum and right supplementary motor cortex. No significant differences were observed between the two speech-in-noise conditions (50% vs. 80% intelligibility). Further, no significant correlation between BOLD response amplitudes and SRT levels were obtained. Analyses of pupillometry data are currently underway and will be integrated with the fMRI findings to further investigate listening effort.

Conclusion

These findings demonstrate that both clear and degraded speech engage a broad network of auditory, motor, frontal, and cerebellar regions. Increased activation in the right frontal operculum for moderately degraded speech (50% intelligibility) and in the left cerebellum and right supplementary motor cortex for higher intelligibility (80%) suggests that subtle changes in speech clarity selectively modulate motor and frontal contributions to speech processing. Together, these results highlight the dynamic recruitment of sensorimotor and cognitive control networks depending on listening demands, providing a neural basis for adaptive listening effort in challenging acoustic environments. Together, these findings provide initial evidence of neural correlates of listening effort, with complementary pupillometry analyses currently underway to further elucidate the association of neural and pupillary indicators of listening effort.
