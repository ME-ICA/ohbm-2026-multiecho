Introduction

Adolescence is a critical period for brain development during which functional networks undergo major changes and refinements to support emotional and cognitive maturation. We recently published Dev-Atlas, a reference atlas of functional brain networks (FBNs) based on resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) data from 1,391 typically-developing (TD) children and adolescents aged 8 to 17 years.1 We revealed 24 reproducible FBNs, with most of them being sensitive to age in terms of functional network connectivity (FNC) and spatial topography. Our follow-up study aimed to further investigate and refine the age effect by splitting the cohort into smaller age bins (2 years) to map developmental changes in the FBNs more precisely.

Methods

We analyzed rs-fMRI from a cohort of 1,617 TD children and adolescents. We split the sample into five age groups (Table 1). Using the multiscale individual component clustering algorithm (MICCA)2, we identified reliable FBNs for each age group and categorized them into a cognitive domain (i.e., default mode (DM), control, salience, dorsal attention, sensorimotor, visual, language) using the open-access network correspondence toolbox (NCT).3 For each pair of FBNs across age groups, we calculated a cosine similarity score between their respective spatial maps and identified the “best-matched” FBNs across groups (i.e., FBNs with the highest cosine similarity scores). Lastly, we compared them across age groups.

Results

Consistent with literature, we reported that younger children had overall equal or more FBNs than older children and adolescents. Second, we found that all the FBNs were reproducible with an average cosine similarity of 0.85 (sd=0.10) although the FBNs from Age Group 1 were slightly less reproducible (cosine similarity= 0.83 (0.11), range= 0.47-0.96), than the FBNs from Age Group 5 (cosine similarity= 0.86 (0.06), range= 0.62-0.96). Within each cognitive domain, the spatial similarity among the FBNs showed progressive decline as the age group’s differences increased (e.g., FBNs between G1 and G2 were more similar than FBN between G1 and G5). Lastly, we identified the FBNs with the largest spatial changes across the five age groups, which was the posterior subnetwork of the DM Network (DMN, Figure 1). While in the youngest group (G1), the network includes the posterior parietal cortex, posterior cingulate cortex/retrosplenial cortex, medial temporal lobes as well as some orbitofrontal cortex and smaller lateral superior and middle prefrontal clusters, the oldest age group (G5) showed a network with a more integrated pattern in the medial posterior parietal / temporal regions, through the additional recruitment of the precuneus, while the orbitofrontal area was less recruited.

Conclusion

This work investigates the subtle effect of age on major FBNs throughout late childhood and adolescence, using a fully data-driven approach. Even between periods of 2 years, brain networks show progressive changes in their spatial topography, likely reflecting network maturation. In particular, the network capturing the posterior part of the default mode network (namely, medial temporal lobe subsystem4) exhibited the largest changes with an anterior-to-posterior shift, suggesting a progressive refinement of this network. This study provides compelling evidence of spatial changes in major FBNs with age, in TD children and adolescents. This information could further be used to investigate neurodevelopmental disorders and their altered FBNs developmental trajectories. The FBNs for each age group will be made publicly available.
