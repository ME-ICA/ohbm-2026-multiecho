Introduction

The Amygdala-derived EEG-fMRI Pattern (Amyg-EFP) is an fMRI-informed EEG model of amygdala activity1, aimed to target the emotion regulation system2. Developed using a similar approach, the reward-system EEG-fMRI pattern (RS-EFP) is an fMRI-informed EEG model of the ventral striatum, aimed to target reward-processing circuits3. Clinical trials have shown that neurofeedback training down-regulation of the Amyg-EFP or up-regulation of the RS-EFP reduced the clinical symptoms of post-traumatic stress disorder4 or depression5, respectively, supporting the relevance of the two models for their targeted mental processes. However, it is unknown whether the Amyg-EFP and RS-EFP differentially correspond with their targeted neurocognitive systems during naturalistic processing, independent of active regulation. To address this, we mapped the whole-brain correlates of the Amyg-EFP and RS-EFP signal modulation during naturalistic stimulation, examining their associations with large-scale cortical networks.

Methods

Seventy-one healthy participants underwent concurrent EEG-fMRI during naturalistic stimulation with video clips, movie scenes and music excerpts, split into two independent datasets (test group: n=36; replication group: n=35). A linear-regression analysis tested the spatiotemporal coupling between voxel-wise BOLD fMRI activation and EFP signal modulation, which was calculated from the ongoing EEG signal. This was used first to validate the association of each EFP with its corresponding target region, i.e. the amygdala and ventral striatum. We then examined the whole-brain fMRI activations associated with each EFP and tested the differences between them. In addition, a sliding-window correlation analysis characterized the coactivation profile of each EFP in relation to pre-defined cortical networks, including the salience network (SN), default mode network (DMN), dorsal attention network (DAN), cognitive control network (CCN), and sensory networks.

Results

Results from both the test and replication cohorts revealed a clear dissociation between neurocognitive systems associated with the Amyg-EFP as compared with the RS-EFP (Figure 1). Specifically, Amyg-EFP covaried with fMRI activity in the amygdala and cortical regions involved in implicit emotional regulation, including the anterior insula and dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, as well as with sensory processing regions. Conversely, the RS-EFP covaried with fMRI activity in the ventral striatum and cortical regions involved in reward processing, including parts of the prefrontal, orbitofrontal and temporal cortices. Furthermore, each EFP presented with a distinct profile of coactivation with pre-defined cortical networks, primarily mapping the Amyg-EFP to the SN and sensory networks, and the RS-EFP to the DMN, CCN and DAN. Finally, participants’ ratings of the emotional intensity of the movie stimuli, collected outside the scanner, were correlated with the Amyg-EFP, but not RS-EFP.

Conclusion

In the current study we validated the association of the Amyg-EFP and RS-EFP with their targeted fMRI activation regions, and uncovered their corresponding large-scale cortical networks. Importantly, these findings show that the natural fluctuations of the Amyg-EFP and RS-EFP signals covary with fMRI activations in brain regions related to each model’s targeted process. Specifically, the Amyg-EFP was associated with regions and networks involved in emotional regulation, and correlated with a behavioral measure of emotional reactivity, whereas the RS-EFP was associated with regions and networks typically involved in reward processing and prediction. These findings demonstrate the neuroanatomical specificity of the EFP models, highlighting their promise for brain-based interventions targeting neurocognitive systems associated with specific mental disorders.
