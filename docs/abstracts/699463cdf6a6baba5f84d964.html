Introduction

Tinnitus, the perception of a sound in absence of an auditory source, is thought to results from an interaction between cochlear injury, and brain functioning. Depending on the brain organization, sensory deafferentation could lead or not to tinnitus perception. Several models were proposed to explain the brain functioning underlying tinnitus perception, usually involving large cerebral networks 1,2. However, a recent meta-analysis identified only one cluster associated with tinnitus, comprising the posterior cingulate gyrus and precuneus 3. This variability in the results of MRI studies of tinnitus can be attributed to the limited number of patients in each study and the significant variability among participant profiles. This heterogeneity includes differences in tinnitus characteristics, in term of hearing comorbidities and associated suffering (e.g., tinnitus perception, hearing loss, sleep deprivation). This methodological limitation can be addressed by using a very large dataset that includes hearing evaluations and socio-psychological data, alongside neuroimaging testing. The aim of this study is to identify the brain regions associated with tinnitus, accounting for comorbidities. 

Methods

We used the UK Biobank, which stands as one of the most comprehensive and long-term global health research studies. We analyzed the anatomical MRI of the participants with constant tinnitus (reporting perceiving most or all the time noises such as ringing or buzzing) to a control group (reporting that they never perceived such noises for more than 5 minutes) with a 1:3 ratio. Groups were matched in age and sex, but also for the presence of comorbidities: hearing health, sleep disorders, depression and neuroticism4.  Final sample size was  n = 2,907 for the tinnitus group and n = 10,011 for the controls. Cortical regions were analyzed using the Destrieux atlas on the cortical surface, while subcortical structures were analyzed using FIRST segmentations in volumetric space. We analyzed the volume of each brain structure with a linear model, including the factors group, age, sex, and the associated comorbidities to account for their possible effects on the brain. We applied a FDR correction for multiple comparisons. 

Results

Compared to control, participants with tinnitus exhibited decreased volumes in several brain regions, predominantly located within temporal, parietal, cingulate, and sensorimotor areas (p < .05, FDR correction). Specifically, differences in the cortical surface were found in the middle temporal gyrus bilaterally (Cohen’s d left = -0.081, right = -0.074), in the right superior temporal sulcus (Cohen’s d= -0.068), the angular gyrus bilaterally, (Cohen’s d left = -0.064, right = -0.071), the left precentral gyrus (Cohen’s d= -0.068), the right postcentral gyrus (Cohen’s d= -0.061), the posterior dorsal cingulate gyrus, bilaterally (Cohen’s d left = -0.070, right = -0.072), the right marginal branch of the cingulate sulcus (Cohen’s d= -0.089). In the subcortical structures, group differences were observed in the right thalamus (Cohen’s d= -0.073).

Conclusion

This study aligns with the existing models associating tinnitus perception with a large network of cortical regions. More specifically, we replicated the result of a recent meta-analysis identifying differences in the dorsal cingulate gyrus. While the effects in the thalamus could be linked to differences within the auditory pathway, the other results point to the involvement of more cognitive brain regions. The identification of this brain network can be the first step in understanding the brain mechanisms underlying the perception of phantom sounds. Further research should focus on studying the functional connectivity between said regions.
