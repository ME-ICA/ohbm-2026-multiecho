Introduction

Adolescent brain development encompasses a dynamic period of neurodevelopmental maturation that extends through middle childhood into early adulthood. As this period is vital to the development of higher-order cognitive abilities and behavioral regulation, it is particularly susceptible to external influences, such as daily environmental contexts across social, behavioral, and physical surroundings which modulate neurodevelopmental trajectories1. Environmental studies have largely focused on the social and behavioral factors that shape brain development, leaving the effects of the physical environment (i.e. air quality, temperature, noise) comparatively under examined. In filling this gap, a recent study by Bottenhorn et al2 linked the exposure to specific sources of particulate matter (PM) 2.5 with alterations in the cortical microstructure development of adolescents. Despite these findings, their approach focused on specific pollutant factors in isolation, yielding valuable but fragmented insights of their effects on cortical development. Fundamentally, real-world environments require an integrative framework to capture various environmental factors that jointly influence exposure profiles3. As such, the current study implements a holistic approach to link environmental characteristics, such as features of meteorological conditions, land cover & structure, urbanicity & pollution, and ambient exposures, to their effects on youth’s network-level resting-state functional connectivity (rsFC).

Methods

We analyzed baseline data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) study (6.0 release) using partial least squares correlation (PLSC), a multivariate approach for investigating brain-behavior associations4. We used 78 Gordon parcellated rsFC variables and linked environmental data from 9839 youth aged 9-10 years. Measures from external environmental datasets were linked with ABCD using geocoding. Among the available ABCD variables, we chose features capturing meteorological conditions (i.e. temperature, humidity, heat exposure), land cover & structure (i.e. vegetation types), urbanicity & pollution (i.e. built environmental factors, air toxins), and ambient exposures (i.e. noise, nighttime lights), following a structured review to ensure a systematic and relevant representation of the broader comprehensive physical environment in a conceptually coherent manner. PLSC identifies shared components between these datasets by decomposing their cross-covariance structure to derive latent dimensions reflecting the shared structure between exposure-related rsFC changes2,5. After removing participants with incomplete data, we residualized the effects of covariates including demographics, study-acquisition, behavioral, and seasonal factors on both exposure and brain measures using multiple regression models. The resulting residual matrices were then passed to PLSC. Here, singular value decomposition yielded a set of salience weights that quantify how strongly each environmental feature and rsFC connection contributed to the overall pattern for each latent dimension (Fig. 1). Finally, model significance was assessed using permutation testing and the stability of variable loadings within each latent dimension was evaluated using bootstrapping.

Results

PLSC identified three significant latent dimensions (LDs) of brain-environment associations (p < 0.01). LD 1 accounted for about 38.5 % shared variance between brain and environment, followed by LD 3 and 4 accounting for about 8.07% and 5.79% of shared variance, respectively. Within LD 1 there was a broad built-environment and pollution profile that included multiple environmental exposures. Specifically, building density, population density, vehicle density, NO2 concentrations, diesel PM, lead risk, cancer-risk air toxins, respiratory hazard, nighttime lights, access to green space, walkability, and anthropogenic noise all loaded negatively on this dimension (Fig. 2 A). These variables were inversely associated with within-network connectivity for the Cingulo-Opercular Network (CON-CON) and Dorsal Attention Network (DAN–DAN), as well as between-network connectivity for the Default Mode Network and Sensorimotor Hand Network (DMN–SHN) and Salience Network and Ventral Attention Network (SN–VAN). In contrast, vegetation cover (i.e., tree canopy) showed a positive salience, corresponding to a negative loading in SHN–Visual Network (VN) connectivity (Fig. 2 B). Overall, LD 1 captured co-occurring features of densely built land covers and polluted environments mapped onto distributed functional connectivity patterns. Within LD 3, vapor pressure deficit (4–6 days before scan) and maximum temperature were positively associated with CON–DAN connectivity, reflecting a latent ‘meteorological’ dimension. LD 4 captured an environmental pattern characterized by industrial pollutants in air, water, and soil. This exposure showed negative salience loading associations with between-network connectivity for the CON and Cingulo-Parietal Network (CPN).

Conclusion

These findings revealed distinct environmental contexts, including a mixed environmental-burden dimension (LD 1), a short-term meteorological factor (LD 3), and a localized industrial contamination signal (LD 4), each covarying with alterations in rsFC profiles that support sustained attention, salience detection, sensorimotor integration and adaptive control needed for goal-directed behavior. These results underscore the importance of considering broader ecological patterns when  characterizing variability in adolescent brain function. By uncovering latent dimensions that align with the functional architecture of attention, control, and sensory processing networks, this work provides a systems-level perspective on how the physical environment may help explain vulnerability or resilience. Future work will evaluate whether these  environmental-neural dimensions relate to internalizing symptoms, externalizing behaviors, cognitive outcomes, and broader indicators of  physical well being.
