Introduction

Although major depressive disorder (MDD) is the mental disorder with the highest health-related burden, diagnosis and treatment have barely changed over the past decades. Hopes were high that multivariate machine learning (ML) methods can unravel clinically useful biomarkers for mental disorders. However, recent evidence suggests that ML models fail to reliably distinguish patients with major depressive disorder (MDD) from healthy controls using structural neuroimaging data1. One possible reason for the limited progress is that MDD is a highly heterogeneous condition showing only little overlap in regional structural brain changes. Our aim was to address this challenge by using normative modelling. A normative model estimates the variation of brain features around the mean in a healthy population on the basis of age or other relevant demographic and clinical variables2. The resulting normative ranges can be used to quantify deviations from the normative pattern for each individual. Based on previous findings that brain deviations in MDD patients are embedded within common networks3, we constructed normative deviation scores for each network. We then examined whether these deviation scores can improve the classification of healthy controls and MDD patients compared to models using raw gray matter volume features.

Methods

We trained normative models based on T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging data from the Marburg-Münster Affective Disorders Cohort Study (MACS, N = 1,741) to quantify normative deviations in healthy controls and MDD patients. Lifetime MDD diagnosis was assessed using structured clinical interviews. Normative models were computed using the PCNtoolkit4, applying Bayesian linear regression to model gray matter volume of 1000 cortical regions parcellated by the Schaefer atlas, while accounting for age, sex, total intracranial volume and study site. We constructed two normative deviation scores for each of the 17 networks derived by the YEO atlas: (1) The percentage of absolute extreme deviation (z < |2.6|) and (2) the 90% trimmed mean of 10% of the top absolute deviations. Predictive models were implemented using PHOTONAI5, which integrates machine learning pipelines with hyperparameter optimization, model selection and evaluation procedures. Support vector machines and random forests were trained to predict MDD diagnosis from both deviation scores, univariate normative deviations and raw structural brain features. To obtain unbiased estimates of predictive performance, all models were evaluated using nested cross-validation.

Results

The deviation scores did not differ between healthy controls and MDD patients across all 17 networks (percentage of absolute extreme deviation: t(30.52) = -0.42, p = .675; trimmed mean of extreme deviations: t(31.51) = -0.55, p = .590). Using normative deviation scores or univariate normative deviations as input for machine learning-based predictive models did not improve the classification accuracy compared to raw features. Across machine learning models and input features, accuracies for the classification of healthy controls and MDD patients ranged from 44.81% to 59.21%.

Conclusion

In our study, neither normative deviation scores across networks nor univariate normative deviations improved the classification of healthy controls and MDD patients. Our findings highlight current limitations of structural imaging for individual-level prediction in depression and suggest that finding a common brain pattern in MDD patients requires further methodological advances. Sub-analyses on symptom- and network-level are planned.
