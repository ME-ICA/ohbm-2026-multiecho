Introduction

Human whole-brain models typically rely on diffusion-weighted MRI (DWI)–derived structural scaffolds that provide undirected estimates of connection strength but omit axonal directionality. Directed projections—feedforward, feedback and recurrent loops—profoundly shape network stability, information flow and causal propagation; therefore, undirected scaffolds limit a model’s ability to capture direction-sensitive phenomena. Invasive tracing in non-human primates remains the gold standard for mesoscale directionality. Given practical and ethical constraints on analogous human data, cross-species inference from macaque tracer datasets provides a principled route to impute putative directionality onto human connectomes. Therefore, this study employs the blueprint method to provide a human directed connectome based on macaque neural tracing directionality, describing its topological features and potential implications for whole-brain dynamic.

Methods

We constructed a directed human structural scaffold by mapping macaque tracer evidence (CocoMac)1 to a human parcellation (HCP-MMP1.0 + HCPex) via correspondences defined through 42 homologous white-matter tracts2. For each pair of brain regions, we estimated projection probability and retained the top 20% of edges as a putative directional backbone. Group-average diffusion weights from 100 HCP-YA subjects were assigned to these directed edges to yield a weighted, directed human network. 

We evaluated the fundamental properties of this connectome using multiple topological measures, including analyses of symmetry and small-world structure. To characterize large-scale hierarchical organization, we applied the trophic-level framework3 derived from directed connectivity. 

Finally, we used a whole-brain model to assess how changes in topological structure influence large-scale neural dynamics.In the simulations, each brain region in the atlas was treated as an independent node whose dynamics followed the Montbrió model, the analytical limit of an infinitely large, all-to-all coupled population of quadratic integrate-and-fire (QIF) neurons. All nodes shared identical parameters. At the whole-brain scale, we compared three distinct connectomes—(i) a thresholded undirected DTI-based network, (ii) the inferred directed connectome, and (iii) a symmetrized version of the directed connectome—to examine how differences in topology shape whole-brain dynamics under both resting and perturbed conditions.

Results

To investigate putative directionality in the human structural connectome, we combined macaque diffusion-based connectivity (Brainnetome-8; six subjects) with human diffusion-based connectivity (HCP-YA dataset, 100 subjects). We established a cross-species parcellation mapping between the macaque D99 atlas and the human HCPex atlas by leveraging correspondences defined through 42 homologous white-matter fiber tracts shared between the two species. Using these tract correspondences, we computed a connection probability for each ordered pair of brain regions (Fig. 1a). Network topology metrics indicate pronounced small-world organization (ω = −0.16), consistent with a network that balances local clustering (correspond with functional clustering based on resting state fMRI) and short path lengths. Directional projection patterns are congruent with canonical information-flow motifs: for example, primary visual and auditory areas preferentially project toward prefrontal regions and nodes of the default mode network, and classic circuits such as the prefrontal → basal ganglia → motor cortex pathway are recapitulated (Fig. 1b).

To interrogate hierarchical organization, we computed node trophic levels on the directed adjacency matrix and quantified trophic incoherence as a global measure of directional hierarchy3 (Fig. 1d). Trophic levels—here representing the mean downstream distance from putative input sources in the directed graph (Fig. 1c)—correlated strongly with the second cortical gradient (sensory to motor) reported by Margulies et al.4, rather than with the more commonly used principal (gradient-1, sensory to prefrontal) axis (Fig. 1g). Importantly, although the inferred connectome contains many unidirectional edges, the global trophic incoherence indicate that the network does not collapse into a strictly feedforward architecture. Instead, a balance of feedback projections appears to be preserved, suggesting that reciprocal connections and directed loops remain prominent determinants of whole-brain dynamics.

To assess how topological features shape whole-brain dynamics, we conducted large-scale simulations using the directed structural connectome and two control networks: (i) a thresholded undirected DTI-based scaffold (structural-control) and (ii) a symmetrized version of the directed connectome (directionality-control). In all simulations, each brain region was modeled as an independent node governed by the Montbrió mean-field dynamics (Fig. 2a). We examined system behavior in two regimes: (a) an unperturbed resting state and (b) a perturbed state (Fig. 2a, d). During resting-state simulations, we identified the critical operating point for each connectome. However, below the critical point, whole-brain measures including functional connectivity (FC) and functional connectivity dynamics (FCD)—did not differ substantially across the three networks (Fig. 2b, c). To prove the role of network topology under perturbation, we applied a 2-second square-pulse stimulation to V1 and A1, mimicking visual and auditory inputs at each network’s critical point (Fig. 2d). Under these perturbations, directionality exerted a pronounced influence on the system’s dynamical responses. The directed connectome produced more biologically plausible propagation patterns, including activation trajectories (Fig. 2e). Unlike the control networks, the directed connectome did not induce premature convergence of signals in lower-level regions (Fig. 2g); instead, response strength and persistence systematically increased along the trophic-level hierarchy (Fig. 2f). The directed network also exhibited transient amplification, a hallmark of non-normal systems (Fig. 2h). This amplification manifested prominently in prefrontal and motor regions, indicating enhanced signal integration and sustained propagation. 

Conclusion

1. Directed connectome

This study introduces a framework for transferring macaque tracer-derived connectivity to the human brain, enabling inference of directionality that cannot be obtained from diffusion tractography alone. The resulting directed human connectome exhibits an asymmetric, feedforward-biased architecture with clear non-reciprocal edges and marked non-normal network properties. The matrix is intended as a principled approximation of directional structure rather than a definitive ground truth. Accordingly, two limitations are noteworthy. First, although the mapping leverages macaque tract-tracing and human white-matter homologues, human-specific pathways may exist, particularly those supporting uniquely human functions, such as language and the expanded prefrontal cortex. Second, as more comprehensive macaque connectomes become available—potentially at single-neuron resolution—this approach can be updated to refine directional estimates and yield new structural insights.

2. Trophic-level hierarchy as a view on whole-brain organization

Trophic-level analysis reveals a robust hierarchical gradient in which information ascends from low- to high-trophic-level regions. This hierarchy, derived purely from directed connectivity, provides an alternative and complementary perspective on cortical organization. Previous frameworks based on resting-state functional connectivity (RSFC) describe two principal gradients: one spanning sensory-to-association cortex (gradient 1) and one extending from visual cortex to association and then to sensorimotor areas (gradient 2)4. Traditional interpretations treat gradient 1 as reflecting hierarchical control, whereas gradient 2 is often attributed to shifts in sensory modality. In contrast, trophic-level hierarchy strongly aligns with gradient 2 but reveals a clearer feedforward progression from audiovisual regions to association cortex and onward to motor areas, with reduced hierarchical placement of auditory cortex. This pattern also matches dimensionality-reduction analyses of task-evoked activity5 and parallels input–hidden–output structures in artificial neural networks. Thus, gradient 2 may capture a fundamental feedforward organizational principle rather than simply modality transitions, suggesting that the expanded prefrontal cortex functions as an enlarged computational “hidden layer” rather than a highest-tier control center. Overall, trophic-level hierarchy offers a revised lens for interpreting large-scale cortical organization.

3. Functional consequences of directionality for brain dynamics and computation

Directionality exerts its strongest influence on whole-brain dynamics under perturbation. This dynamical sensitivity reflects underlying static topological features of the directed network, including its non-normality. Relative to control connectomes, the directed network supports more biologically grounded propagation pathways, enhanced and prolonged responses along the trophic gradient, and regionally specific transient amplification—particularly in prefrontal and motor regions. These phenomena indicate that directed, non-normal architecture can maintain signal fidelity across long-range pathways while selectively amplifying activity in integrative hubs. Such mechanisms are consistent with efficient feedforward computation and context-dependent processing. From a modeling perspective, incorporating empirically inferred directionality substantially improves the ability of whole-brain models to reproduce perturbation-evoked spatiotemporal dynamics, underscoring its relevance for mechanistic accounts of human brain computation.
