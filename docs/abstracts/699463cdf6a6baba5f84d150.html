Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is traditionally characterized by its main motor symptoms, and late motor symptoms including freezing of gait (FoG), which is one of the most disabling yet poorly understood symptom defined as a brief, episodic absence or marked reduction in the forward progression of the feet despite the intention to walk. Moreover, PD is increasingly recognized as a multisystem disorder with a variety of non-motor symptoms including significant autonomic dysfunctions. Cardiovascular autonomic control is often impaired resulting in the clinically most prominent orthostatic hypotension, but there is as increasing evidence of other cardiovascular symptoms including altered heart rate variability (HRV) compared to healthy controls1. Surprisingly, there is also evidence suggesting more pronounced HRV disruption in patients with FoG2. Studying the brain-heart interactions in PD might provide important new insights into the disease pathophysiological mechanism as the examination of neural and autonomic disorders in isolation provides an incomplete picture. It is well known, that dopaminergic medication does not influence PD cardiovascular symptoms, but the possible impact of other advanced treatment strategies is largely unknown. Deep brain stimulation of the subthalamic nucleus (STN-DBS), an established advanced therapy for motor symptoms, is known to influence cortical and subcortical oscillations as well as the aperiodic spectral component, an index of excitation–inhibition balance. Its effects on autonomic functions, however, are inconsistent across studies3. To address these gaps, this study used simultaneous EEG and ECG recordings to examine the effects of STN-DBS on cortical–autonomic interactions, focusing on 1) associations between short-term autonomic variability and oscillatory EEG power, 2) relationships between long-term autonomic flexibility and the aperiodic spectral exponent, and 3) differences between patients with and without FoG.

Methods

Twenty-four PD patients treated with STN-DBS (13 males, 11 females) were included. Motor symptoms were assessed by Movement Disorders Society Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) and the Freezing of Gait Questionnaire (FOG-Q). High-density EEG and ECG were recorded at rest in two conditions: DBS OFF and DBS ON. For each condition, a 1-minute artifact-free segment was selected for simultaneous EEG and ECG analysis. For ECG, R peaks were detected, and HRV metrics, SD1 (short-term variability) and SD2 (long-term flexibility), were computed from Poincaré plots. EEG preprocessing included exclusion of non-scalp electrodes, band-limiting (0.1–100 Hz), ICA-based removal of ocular and cardiac artifacts, bad channel interpolation, and average referencing. Spectral parameterisation was performed using the FOOOF algorithm to extract oscillatory power (delta, theta, alpha, beta, low/high gamma) and aperiodic features (offset, exponent), averaged across the segment. Regions of interest (ROIs) were defined according to the 10–20 system, with each ROI value representing the mean of the centroid electrode and its six nearest neighbours. For each measure, DBS-induced change was defined as Δ = ON – OFF. Associations between ΔEEG features and ΔHRV indices were assessed using partial Spearman correlations controlling for age, sex, and disease duration. Patients were stratified by FoG severity (FOG-: ≤8; FOG+: ≥9), and group differences were tested with Mann–Whitney U tests. Relationships with clinical scores were also explored.

Results

Analysis revealed that ΔSD1, representing short-term HRV, was positively correlated with Δalpha power across several ROIs, strongest in the frontal region at ROI F4 (r = 0.54, p = 0.011). Overall autonomic flexibility, reflected by ΔSD2, was negatively associated with Δexponent, as shown in Fig.1. Flatter exponents, indicating increased cortical excitability, were linked to higher ΔSD2, whereas steeper exponents, indicating stronger cortical inhibition, were associated with reduced autonomic flexibility. This coupling was most prominent in central and temporoparietal ROIs, with significant effects in ROI C4 (r = -0.59, p = 0.005), T8 (r = -0.46, p = 0.038), and Cz (r = -0.44, p = 0.048). Stratification by FoG severity revealed a consistent subgroup pattern. FoG– patients showed DBS-induced increases in SD2 accompanied by shifts toward cortical excitation, whereas FoG+ patients showed the opposite trend, with decreases in SD2 and shifts toward stronger inhibition. The group difference in ΔSD2 reached statistical significance (p = 0.040). Significant group differences in the Δexponent were also observed in ROIs C4 (p = 0.030) and Cz (p = 0.009).

Conclusion

The results of this study are consistent with previous reports showing heterogeneous autonomic responses to STN-DBS3. Taken alone, this would suggest that DBS exerts no coherent autonomic effect. However, our main findings reveal a structured pattern underlying this heterogeneity. Increases in SD2, reflecting increased autonomic flexibility considered beneficial, were associated with shifts toward cortical excitation, while reductions in SD2 corresponded to increased cortical inhibition. The FoG symptom further clarified this heterogeneity: clinically relevant FoG patients exhibited opposite DBS-induced patterns compared with non-freezers, consistent with more disrupted autonomic regulation2.
