Introduction

Chronic pain and trauma exposure frequently co-occur in older adults. Despite overlapping morphological alterations reported in independent studies of chronic pain and trauma, particularly in stress- and pain-related regions, evidence on their distinct and combined effects in the same cohort remains unknown. The present study leveraged data from the UK Biobank1 (UKB) to determine the effects of trauma exposure at different neurodevelopmental stages (childhood, adulthood), as well as their cumulative effects, on brain morphology in older adults (aged 60 and older) who experience chronic pain. 

Methods

Tabulated 3D T1-weighted structural magnetic resonance imaging data from the UKB (N = 21,996) were analysed. Chronic pain was defined as pain that persisted for three or more months at any body site(s). Childhood maltreatment severity was measured using the Childhood Trauma Screener2 describing childhood physical, sexual and emotional abuse and physical and emotional neglect, and trauma exposure in adulthood was assessed using an equivalent screener adapted from a United Kingdom describing adulthood relationship and financial insecurity, and physical, psychological and sexual intimate partner violence3. Items are summed separately for each questionnaire to derive total childhood maltreatment and adulthood trauma severity scores. Both severity scores were then converted to z-scores and summed to compute an overall cumulative trauma severity score. All neuroimaging data were minimally pre-processed with FreeSurfer using the UKB standardised protocols1, and indices of subcortical volume (14 regions-of-interest [ROIs]), cortical thickness and surface area (66 ROIs using Desikan-Killiany atlas4) were extracted. To examine the isolated impacts of childhood maltreatment, adulthood and cumulative trauma, three independent samples representing participants exposed to childhood maltreatment only, adulthood trauma only and both were derived and analysed separately using a series of linear mixed-effects models. Group (chronic pain versus controls) and trauma/maltreatment severity scores were modelled as fixed effects whereas subject ID and imaging sites were modelled as random effects. Age, squared mean-centred age (age2), sex, socio-economic deprivation and presence of mental health diagnoses were included as covariates. Total intracranial volume was included in analyses of cortical surface area and subcortical volume (but not cortical thickness). False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to account for the number of ROIs studied in each set of analyses and statistical significance was set at pFDR < 0.05. Effect sizes were reported as Cohen’s d for group-level chronic pain comparisons and as standardised beta (β) coefficients for associations with trauma severity.

Results

Among the total sample (N = 21,996), 13,249 individuals (60.2%; 52.6% female) were assigned to the control group and 8,747 individuals (39.8%; 58.6% female) were assigned to the chronic pain group. Within the control group, 2,515 individuals (18.9%) reported being exposed to childhood maltreatment only, 2,553 (19.3%) were exposed to adulthood trauma only, and 4,982 (37.6%) were exposed to both. Within the chronic pain group, 1,609 individuals (18.4%) reported being exposed to childhood maltreatment only, 1,510 (17.3%) were exposed to adulthood trauma only and 3,888 (44.5%) were exposed to both. Chronic pain was associated with widespread reduced surface area and increased thickness across the superior frontal, temporal, superior parietal, somatosensory, occipital, cingulate and insular cortices, independently of trauma severity (see Figure 1). Trauma severity, particularly trauma experienced in adulthood alone or in both childhood and adulthood, was associated with smaller bilateral nucleus accumbens, putamen, thalamus, and left hippocampus, as well as smaller left inferior parietal gyrus surface area, independently of chronic pain. This association was not present in older adults exposed to childhood maltreatment only. No chronic pain-by-trauma severity interaction was associated with any brain morphological alterations, across all three distinct trauma groups. 

Conclusion

This study presents evidence for distinct, but not combined, effects of chronic pain and trauma among older adults from the general population. More pronounced morphological alterations associated with the severity of adulthood and cumulative trauma relative to childhood maltreatment may indicate mechanisms of subsequent resilience to stressful/traumatic experiences following early life maltreatment. These findings highlight the importance of considering and accounting for lifetime trauma exposure when examining brain morphological alterations in this population.
