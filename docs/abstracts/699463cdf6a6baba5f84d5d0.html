Introduction

Anxiety is a pervasive problem in modern society. Excessive levels of anxiety are harmful and disrupt everyday life, causing among others,  difficulties in goal-directed attentional control and executive function [1]. Functional imaging studies in anxiety disorders have reported abnormalities in several cortical networks, with the most consistent being an increased activation of the salience network (including the amygdala) due to excessive vigilance to threat [2], and both reduced or increased recruitment of prefrontal cortex (PFC), part of the frontoparietal networks involved in attention and top-down cognitive control, the latter likely arising from a compensatory attempt to regulate cognition and override emotional arousal [3]. Thus, frontoparietal network functioning appears to play a crucial role in anxiety. 

Attentional bias to threat is also found in healthy people with high levels of dispositional anxiety, and a recent fMRI study found that amygdala activation during an attentional task with emotional distracters (fear faces) was reduced after electrical stimulation of the left PFC with tDCS, suggesting improved top-down control and decreased attentional capture to threat [4].

fNIRS is a useful alternative to fMRI to address brain-behavior relationships in health and disease, with the advantages of being less sensitive to motion artifacts, more ergonomic and easier to employ for multimodal recordings. Aim of the study was to ascertain whether fNIRS can detect task related differences in regional brain activity as a function of trait anxiety during an Affective Flanker Task (AFT) requiring attentional control in the presence of emotion distractor words. No fNIRS  studies to-date on this topic have been published. This report is part of a broader ongoing single blind controlled trial evaluating the efficacy of repetitive tACS to reduce anxiety and its related changes in measures of behavioral performance and cortical brain functioning.

Methods

Subjects. 63 healthy adults took part in the study, after screening with the Spielberger Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI-T). Based on their scores, they were divided in a High Anxiety group (STAI>52; n=22; 23.2 ± 1.68 years; 2 males), a Moderate Anxiety group (STAI>39 and <= 52; n=21; 23.5 ± 3.49 years; 3 males) and a Low Anxiety group (STAI<=39; n=21; 23.0 ± 2.27 years; 3 males). 

Active Task. Each participant performed the AFT during the fNIRS acquisition. On each trial, they were required to indicate with a button press the direction of a central arrow while ignoring the flanking distractor arrows (congruent or incongruent). At the same time, an affective word—neutral, positive, or negative—was displayed above and below the central arrow array, serving as an irrelevant emotional distractor. The task included four blocks: two positive and two anxiety-blocks; Each consisted of 50% Neutral and 50% Anxious/Positive words. Each block lasted approximately 8 minutes and was composed of 12 sub-blocks of 6 consecutively presented words, followed by a 15-second pause for fNIRS recording, for a total duration of about 40 minutes. Accuracy (Acc) and reaction times (RTs) of the AFT task were recorded.

fNIRS recording. fNIRS acquisition was performed using an 88-channel setup, including 8 short channels. Channels covered prefrontal, frontal, temporal, parietal and occipital areas including known frontal and parietal nodes of the DAN and the VAN. 

The fNIRS data were preprocessed in MATLAB using the Homer3 toolbox functions. For each participant, channels considered too noisy were identified using the hmrR_PruneChannels function and discarded from subsequent analyses. The signals considered valid were first converted from optical intensity to optical density. Motion artifacts were first identified and then the correction was performed by combining spline interpolation and wavelet correction (REF). After the correction, a bandpass filter between 0 and 2.5 Hz was applied, and stimuli contaminated with residual artifacts were discarded. The resulting signals were then converted into changes in the concentration of oxyhemoglobin (HbO) and deoxyhemoglobin (HbR) using hmrR_OD2Conc.

The evoked hemodynamic functional responses (HRF) were estimated using a generalized linear model (GLM) with a time window ranging from 2 seconds before the onset of the task to 35 seconds after. This allowed us to obtain the HRF for each subject, channel and experimental condition.

Statistical analysis  

Behavior. Accuracy as a dichotomous variable was analyzed using generalized linear mixed models with binomial distribution and logit link. The RTs of correct responses were log-transformed and analyzed using linear mixed-effects models. Finally, the Inverse Efficiency Score (IES = RTs/1-ACC), was calculated to account for different speed/accuracy tradeoffs, and analyzed using linear mixed-effects models.

fNIRS. For each channel of interest, we calculated the average value of HbO and HbR within the HRF window (5–30 seconds). In this way, it was possible to obtain, for each participant, an average value for each hemoglobin component, channel, and experimental condition. To identify the active channels, we performed 80 one-sample t-tests against zero for each condition and hemoglobin component. The direction of the tail was defined based on the expected trend of the hemodynamic response: for HbO, a right-tailed t-test was used, assuming an increase from zero, while, for HbR, a left-tailed t-test was used, assuming a decrease from zero. The p-values obtained were corrected using the FDR (False Discovery Rate) correction for multiple comparisons. A channel was  considered active when it was significantly different from zero for both HbO and HbR in the same condition. This pattern, increase in HbO and decrease in HbR, reflects the presence of a typical hemodynamic response.

Results

Behavior. There were no significant group differences for Accuracy or RTs between positive or negative blocks. However, a significant difference emerged between groups as a function of arrow congruency. Besides an expected main effect of Congruency for accuracy and RTs (p < .001) –groups performed better in theCongruent than Incongruent trials – , the Group × Congruency interaction was significant for accuracy (p < .001) but no RTs. This was better explained by analysis on the IES, confirming a significant Group x Congruency interaction (p = .002). Specifically, participants in the Low group showed a significantly larger Congruency effect than those in the High group (p = .001) and the Moderate group (p = .005) [see figure 1A].

fNIRS results. Results showed that no active channels were identified in the Low group. In the Moderate group, two active channels emerged in the Positive condition corresponding to the left TPJ 39  and the right visual cortex 19 (ch79 and ch85). In the High group, on the other hand, four channels were active in negative condition, two in the left frontal lobe (FEF 8, DLPFC 9), one in the right frontal lobe (DLPFC 9) and one in the right occipital lobe (visual cortex 19). In the positive condition, sixteen channels were activated in the High group, mostly including the right frontal areas (FEF 6/8, DLPFC 9, VLPFC 44/45)  but also left frontal areas (FEF 6/8, DLPFC 9)  and bilaterally parietal and temporo-parietal areas (LPs/LPi 7/40, TPJ 39), see Figure 1B.

Conclusion

The Affective Flanker task produced a strong Congruency effect in all groups, due to the extra-demands in terms of cognitive control on the incongruent trials, consistent with prior literature. The main behavioral finding was that Anxiety level influenced the size of the Interference effect (difference between Incongruent and Congruent trials), which was significantly greater in the Low Anxiety group relative to both Moderate and High Anxiety groups, even after correcting for possible speed accuracy tradeoffs. In absence of significant anxiety, efficient cognitive control  produced  a benefit in terms of faster but less accurate  performance in case of the easy Congruent trials, to the expense of a cost to pay in terms of speed when shifting to the more taxing Incongruent trials, irrespective of the concomitant emotional distractors. In contrast, the reduced interference effect in the Anxious individuals may be due to inefficient top-down attentional control and inability to ignore irrelevant emotional distractors. It is also well known that anxious people appraise demanding tasks as a threat rather than as a challenge, and are worried about committing errors and receiving negative feedback, which may produce a strategy minimizing the risk of errors.

The fNIRS results indicated that group with high trait Anxiety recruited more intensely and extensively nodes of the frontoparietal networks involved in top-down attentional control (DAN), and bottom-up attentional capture (VAN),  likely as a compensation for a less  efficient goal-directed attentional control [1], suggesting that they found the attentional requirements of the flanker task particularly demanding  in the presence of emotional distracters hard to ignore. We expected a stronger effect in the Negative blocks, containing anxiety-related words, than in the Positive blocks, consistent with literature on attention bias for stimuli related to anxiety. However, studies employing irrelevant emotional faces  have reported larger fMRI activations in anxious individuals for moderate rather than more intense emotional expressions [3]. 

This is to our knowledge the first fNIRS study reporting differences in regional brain activation in participants with high trait anxiety affecting both frontal and parietal nodes of the frontoparietal cortical networks.

These exploratory results will gain more important clinical implications by a future comparison with the fNIRS findings in the AFT task after the randomized trial with repetitive tACS we have recently completed.
