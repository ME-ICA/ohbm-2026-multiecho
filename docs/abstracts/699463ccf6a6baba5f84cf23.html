Introduction

Adolescence is a period of brain plasticity that supports sustained cognitive growth, but also is now understood as a period of vulnerability to diverse risk factors. Longitudinal neuroimaging offers insight into typical and atypical brain development. The Adolescent Brain and Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study is the largest effort to longitudinally track brain, behavior, and cognition across adolescence in the United States. Among its imaging modalities, diffusion MRI (dMRI) is particularly useful for characterizing developmental changes in white matter.

Since ABCD launched, the field has increasingly adopted advanced dMRI processing methods, more detailed quality control procedures, and person-specific bundle tractometry. However, many of these modern methods—and the derivatives they generate—are not included in the official ABCD release. Applying such methods at ABCD scale requires substantial computational resources and technical expertise, limiting accessibility. To support rigorous and reproducible research on adolescent white matter development, we present the ABCD-BIDS Community Collection (ABCC) release 3.1.0, which provides more than 25,000 fully processed multi-shell dMRI datasets longitudinally sampled from over 10,000 participants.

Multi-shell dMRI supports diverse modeling approaches that yield many parametric maps indexing tissue microstructure. In parallel, numerous quantitative indices of dMRI quality capture different aspects of artifact, motion, and signal loss. Faced with this abundance of microstructure and quality measures, investigators often rely on a narrow subset, with little guidance on which dMRI metrics are most sensitive to development or which quality measures are most informative. Here, we compared the developmental sensitivities of dMRI measures and characterized how different indices of dMRI quality relate to these metrics. We also quantified the effects of site-wise harmonization on mitigating scanner effects in these metrics.

Methods

Participants: ABCC includes data from ABCD release 6.0 (baseline and years 2, 4, and 6 follow up). All datasets with prerequisite T1w, dMRI, and field maps were preprocessed with QSIPrep v0.21.4 [1] and postprocessed with QSIRecon v1.0.0rc2. A total of 25,475 datasets (11,970 from females, ages 9-16) from 10,908 participants that completed all processing procedures and had complete demographic data were included in the final analyses.

dMRI Image Quality Metrics: QSIPrep produced 40 image quality metrics (IQMs) capturing key aspects of dMRI quality, including motion, signal-to-noise contrast, gradient table validity, and dMRI–T1w registration. As previously described [2], a subset of 3,101 postprocessed dMRI datasets across vendors were manually rated by trained reviewers. We trained a multivariate XGBoost [3] classifier to predict these manual ratings from the automated QSIPrep IQMs; this classifier was then applied to the entire dataset. We evaluated the relationship of both the resulting quality prediction score and the full set of IQMs with the mean white matter values of dMRI microstructure.

dMRI Postprocessing: QSIRecon was used to execute five postprocessing workflows (Figure 1). These included diffusion kurtosis imaging (DKI) from DIPY, generalized q-sampling imaging (GQI) from DSI Studio, mean apparent propagator MRI (MAP-MRI) from TORTOISE, and neurite orientation dispersion and density imaging (NODDI) from AMICO. The diffusion tensor was additionally calculated. This resulted in a broad set of 35 microstructural measures. We used DSI Studio AutoTrack to segment 62 participant-specific bundles across the brain, and averaged each microstructure measure within each bundle for each participant. We also calculated the average of each metric within an all-white-matter mask for each participant. We used longitudinal ComBat-GAMM, an extension of longComBat [4], as implemented in the R package ComBatFamily, to mitigate site-specific effects in these metrics, protecting the fixed effects of age and sex, as well as a random effect for participants. Age was modeled as a set of non-linear penalized splines.

Modeling Developmental Sensitivity: With the harmonized set of metrics, we used generalized additive mixed models (GAMM) for each metric within each bundle to evaluate the effect of age while controlling for sex and image quality (the classifier prediction score); a random intercept was included for each participant. Age and image quality were modeled as non-linear penalized splines. We also ran reduced models without the age predictor to calculate the age effect size, which was operationalized as the difference in adjusted R2 values between the full and reduced models.

Modeling Image Quality Sensitivity: Using the harmonized white-matter averaged metrics, we used the same GAMM to relate each microstructural value to each IQM. The image quality effect size was operationalized as the difference in adjusted R2 values between the full model and a reduced model without the IQM predictor.

Effectiveness of Metric Harmonization: Next, we evaluated the effect of scanner vendor on each microstructural metric, averaged across all white matter. We ran this model separately in both the raw and harmonized data, using the difference between these to summarize the impact of harmonization in mitigating scanner-driven variance. Additionally, we sought to quantify the degree to which harmonization increased the spatial correspondence of age effects between scanners. For each microstructural metric, separately within each vendor, we estimated the age effect for every bundle both before and after harmonization. We then quantified cross-vendor consistency by computing Spearman rank correlations of bundle-wise age effects for all pairwise vendor comparisons. A high rank correlation indicated that two vendors had similar spatial correspondence of age effects (i.e., the relative order of age effects across bundles was similar between two scanners).

Results

Automated quality prediction corresponded with manual ratings: When applied to held-out labeled data, the classifier accurately predicted manual ratings from the IQMs (Siemens: r = 0.647, GE: r = 0.605, Philips: r = 0.662). The classifier had AUCs of 0.89 (Siemens), 0.82 (GE), and 0.83 (Philips), and accuracies of 0.94 (Siemens), 0.86 (GE), and 0.78 (Philips).

Advanced measures of white matter microstructure are more sensitive to development: Among the 35 metrics tested, the strongest developmental effects were observed in the intracellular volume fraction (ICVF) from NODDI, the mean kurtosis tensor (MKT) from DKI, and the non-Gaussianity (NG) map from MAP-MRI. ICVF age effects explained between 16-52% (range across bundles) of the variance across bundles with an average of 38%. MKT age effects explained between 4-49% of the variance with an average of 33%. NG age effects explained between 5-42% of the variance across bundles with an average of 28%. Tensor metrics, including fractional anisotropy (FA) and mean diffusivity (MD), exhibited demonstrably lower age effects. FA explained between 0.1-23% variance with an average of 11%, and MD age effects explained between 0.1-33% with an average of 13% (Figure 2A).

Susceptibility to image quality varied across microstructure and quality measure: Of the 43 IQMs analyzed, the ones that explained the most variance across all dMRI metrics were the preprocessed dMRI contrast (range across metrics: 0.4-13%, average: 4%), the quality classifier prediction (range: 0.4-7.4%, average: 3.4%), and mean framewise displacement (range: 0.3-9.6%, average: 3.1%). Of the metrics most sensitive to development, ICVF was especially robust to image quality (range: 0-1.9%, average: 0.3%). On the other hand, FA was more susceptible to variation in image quality (range: 0-9.6%, average: 3.1%) (Figure 2B).

Harmonization reduced scanner effects and improved cross-scanner correspondence: Prior to harmonization, some measures (such as kurtosis fractional anisotropy) had as much as 80% variance uniquely explained by scanner vendor. Harmonization largely mitigated these effects. In harmonized data, scanner vendor explained no more than 2% of variance among microstructural indices most sensitive to development (ICVF, MKT, and NG; Figure 2C). Site-wise harmonization also greatly increased the cross-scanner age effect correspondence. For ICVF, Siemens-to-GE correspondence (Spearman ρ) increased from 0.75 to 0.78, Siemens-to-Philips increased from 0.64 to 0.95, and GE-to-Philips increased from 0.36 to 0.90. For MKT, Siemens-to-GE correspondence increased from 0.36 to 0.69, Siemens-to-Philips stayed constant at 0.88, and GE-to-Philips increased from 0.40 to 0.90. For NG, Siemens-to-GE correspondence increased from 0.50 to 0.73, Siemens-to-Philips increased from 0.79 to 0.97, and GE-to-Philips increased 0.32 to 0.82 (Figure 2D).

Conclusion

We defined the sensitivity of dMRI-derived metrics to development and image quality in a large longitudinal study of adolescents. Importantly, metrics derived from advanced multishell models outperformed traditional tensor-based measures such as fractional anisotropy, both in terms of developmental sensitivity and in robustness to image quality. These metrics generally index the amount of non-Gaussian and restricted diffusion, which traditional tensor approaches do not effectively model. These results underscore the value of collecting high-quality multishell dMRI data and fully leveraging the rich modeling it enables.

Metrics derived from advanced models such as NODDI and DKI captured developmental trajectories particularly well and showed strong robustness to variability in image quality, making them especially useful for studies in pediatric populations. MAP-MRI measures also reflected these developmental changes, though some metrics derived from this model may require more explicit control for image quality in statistical analyses. Effects of scanner-vendor were all effectively mitigated by harmonization, making them appropriate to use in large-scale multisite consortia studies such as ABCD. 

Although it is encouraging that the most developmentally sensitive measures were relatively stable across variation in data quality, several quality metrics—including mean framewise displacement, the number of bad slices, and the automated QC classifier score—explained up to 20% of the variance in certain dMRI microstructural indices. Such effects underscore the importance of incorporating quantitative quality measures into developmental analyses. Compared with functional MRI, systematic treatment of quality control in dMRI has been historically less emphasized (but see e.g., Yendiki et al., 2014 [5]). Our findings highlight the need for continued attention to these factors. The ability of the QC classifier to explain variance in dMRI metrics reinforces the value of manual review when feasible, while also illustrating that machine-learning–based QC approaches can effectively scale quality assessment when complete manual review of all scans is impractical.

All QSIRecon-derived dMRI outputs described here are included in the new ABCC release 3.1.0, and are accessible to any researcher with a valid ABCD Data Use Certificate. The complete processing workflow is open-source, transparent, and easily deployable to other multi-shell dMRI datasets. Together, these resources advance the accessibility of modern diffusion imaging methods and help enable rigorous, reproducible research on adolescent white matter development.
