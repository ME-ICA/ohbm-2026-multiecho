Introduction

The human brain shows substantial inter-individual variability. While group-level atlases offer a population reference, individualized parcellations are necessary for capturing subject-specific organization. 1 Yet most existing individualized methods still require mapping each subject onto a common template, creating dependence on template choice, registration algorithms, and alignment accuracy. 2

Qiu et al. addressed this limitation by introducing an intrinsic framework based on Laplace–Beltrami eigenfunctions, showing that eigenfunction nodal lines can partition each cortical surface purely from its geometry, independent of registration. 2 More recently, Pang et al. further demonstrated that geometric eigenfunctions embed key developmental, functional, and microstructural organizations, 3 providing biological justification that geometry is a perfect candidate for cortical subdivision. 4 

However, a major challenge remains: eigenfunctions lack natural correspondence across subjects. Eigenvalue multiplicity and local geometric variation cause nodal patterns to vary in ordering and spatial pattern. Pang et al. avoided this issue by working at the group level, 4 whereas Qiu et al. assumed that the kth eigenfunction corresponds across subjects, a condition that fails in complex cortical regions. 2 The resulting inconsistency in nodal-line identity limits the applicability of intrinsic geometric methods for group-level analysis.

Here, we introduce a parcellation framework that resolves this issue by aligning nodal lines across subjects without registration. By establishing correspondence through intrinsic coordinates and curve-matching in a shared geometric space, our method produces individualized parcellations that remain coherent across subjects while retaining the advantages of a fully geometry-driven approach.

Methods

We first merged the 68-region Desikan–Killiany atlas into 40 major gyri (20 per hemisphere) by combining ROIs that were arbitrary subdivisions of larger gyri or defined by highly variable sulci, following the Human Brainnetome Atlas. 5 We then recursively bipartitioned each region in every subject. At each iteration, a region was divided into two using a single nodal line derived from Laplace–Beltrami (LB) eigenfunctions. To maintain cross-subject correspondence, the appropriate nodal line was identified using the following intrinsic, curve-based procedure, applied separately for each region and iteration.

Extraction of Candidate Nodal Lines. For each subject and cortical region, we computed the first several LB eigenfunctions defined on the surface geometry, using Dirichlet boundary conditions along the region boundary. Each eigenfunction gives rise to a nodal line corresponding to its zero-level set on the surface. We retained only those nodal lines that partition the region into exactly two connected components, as these provide well-defined intrinsic bipartitions. The resulting set of retained nodal lines constitutes the candidate lines for that subject and region.

Intrinsic 2D Template Space. We then constructed a shared intrinsic coordinate system to compare nodal lines across subjects without surface registration. We pooled vertices from all candidate lines and compute principal components. The first two components define a shared 2D coordinate system, onto which all nodal lines are projected for geometry-driven comparison.

Distance metric. Nodal line similarity is quantified via the discrete Fréchet distance in the intrinsic 2D space.

Population Consensus. We computed pairwise nodal line distances for a subset of subjects. For each line, the total distance to all others was calculated. The ten lines with the smallest total distances were selected as global medoids, with the one having the smallest distance serving as the reference.

Parcellation Harmonization. For each subject, the candidate line closest to any medoid was selected and oriented to match the reference. Label ambiguity was resolved by computing the signed cross-product between the aligned nodal line and patch vertices in the intrinsic 2D space; for a region labeled N, the subregion with a negative sign was assigned label 2N−1, and the other subregion was assigned label 2N.

Results

We first demonstrate that the geometry-informed framework produces coherent and reproducible individualized parcellations across scales and subjects. Using T1-weighted structural MRI data from UK Biobank participants without major diseases, subject-specific cortical surfaces were reconstructed using the FreeSurfer longitudinal analysis pipeline. These reconstructed surfaces were then used to generate individualized cortical atlases at multiple spatial resolutions using our geometry-informed parcellation approach. Figure 1a shows 80-, 160-, 320-, 640-region parcellations of a representative subject on white matter and inflated surfaces, and Figure 1b illustrates parcellations of the left paracentral and inferior frontal gyri across four subjects. Corresponding region labels were visually well aligned across individuals and scales, indicating reproducible atlas construction without template registration.

We next assessed test–retest reliability using longitudinal scans from 855 healthy participants. Regional Dice coefficients (Figure 2a) showed high intra-subject consistency across all scales, with maximal values exceeding 0.95. Reliability decreased modestly at finer resolutions, with lower Dice scores in the insula, paracentral gyrus, and banks of the superior temporal sulcus, consistent with increased eigenfunction multiplicity in geometrically simpler regions. Nonetheless, reliability remained high or acceptable for the first three scales.

We then evaluated cross-phenotype generalizability by measuring regional homogeneity across multiple brain phenotypes. Geometry-derived parcels exhibited robust within-region homogeneity across structural, functional, and microstructural measures (Figure 2b), with no single modality dominating the effect, indicating that geometry-aligned boundaries generalize across data types.

Finally, we tested whether the geometry-informed parcellation recovers core cortical structural hierarchy at the individual level. Subject-specific structural similarity matrices computed using Morphometric INverse Divergence yielded principal gradients aligned with the canonical sensory–fugal axis. When benchmarked against functional, diffusion-based, and multimodal atlases, the geometry-informed parcellation consistently outperformed functional atlases and matched the performance of advanced diffusion (Human Brainnetome) and multimodal (Glasser) atlases in differentiating von Economo cortical classes and recovering the sensory–fugal hierarchy (Figure 2c).

Conclusion

This work introduces a fully geometry-informed and registration-free framework for individualized cortical parcellation that achieves high reliability, cross-modal generalizability, and biological interpretability. By resolving eigenfunction multiplicity and enforcing consistent nodal-line correspondence across subjects, the method produces stable multiscale atlases that remain anatomically coherent at the individual level. Longitudinal analyses demonstrated strong structural reproducibility, and cross-phenotype evaluations showed that the resulting parcels capture shared organization across functional, diffusion, and morphological domains. Importantly, the geometry-derived boundaries preserve core cortical structural hierarchies while simultaneously aligning with functional and microstructural variation, indicating that intrinsic geometry provides a unifying scaffold that bridges these modalities. These properties suggest that geometry-informed parcellation is potentially well suited for structure–function integration, multimodal data fusion, and precision mapping of individual brain organization.
