Introduction

Machine learning (ML) models utilizing multi-modal neuroimaging features have achieved promising accuracy in predicting cognitive outcomes, such as Full-Scale IQ (FIQ), in Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD). However, model performance is rarely perfect. While residuals—the divergence between predicted and actual scores—are often dismissed as noise, we hypothesize they represent biologically meaningful heterogeneity. Specifically, individuals whose IQ is "under-predicted" by neuroanatomical models (performing better than their brain structure suggests) may utilize distinct compensatory mechanisms compared to those who are "over-predicted" (failing to reach the cognitive potential suggested by the model). To deconstruct this, we employed a normative modeling framework. By establishing a baseline of brain-IQ relationships in healthy controls, we aimed to map the morphometric landscape of prediction errors in ASD. We specifically contrast two polar subgroups: an "Effective Compensation" group (Under-predicted) and a "Potential Unfulfilled" group (Over-predicted), seeking to identify structural reserves that facilitate cognitive adaptation.

Methods

Participants: The study utilized a large multi-site dataset from the Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange (ABIDE) initiative. We compiled a Typically Developing (TD) cohort (N=412) for model training and testing, and an independent ASD cohort (N=345) for clinical application.

Normative Modeling: We employed Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) with an exponential kernel to establish a normative model of the FreeSurfer-derived morphometric features (surface area, thickness, and volumes) based on 336 features. The model was trained on 247 TD subjects using 5-fold cross-validation and validated on an independent TD test set (N = 165).

Bias Correction and Grouping: To account for the "regression to the mean" bias common in prediction models, a linear bias correction (using polynomial fitting) was applied to the predicted scores. This validated normative model was then applied to the ASD cohort. 

Subtyping via Prediction Residuals: Following the application of the GPR-trained normative model to the ASD cohort, we generated the prediction error, which is the difference between the predicted and actual FIQ values, Predicted FIQ - Actual FIQ. To define biologically extreme subgroups, we first established the null distribution of prediction error by pooling the residuals from the independent TD test set and the entire ASD cohort. We calculated the overall standard deviation of the pooled prediction errors (Overall SD), which was determined to be 0.1944. Two polar ASD subgroups were subsequently defined based on a stringent threshold of ±1 X Overall SD. Potential Unfulfilled Group (Over-predicted): Defined by an error exceeding the positive threshold (FIQ prediction error > +0.1944), indicating the brain structure predicted a higher FIQ than observed. This subgroup consisted of N = 11 individuals. Effective Compensation Group (Under-predicted): Defined by an error below the negative threshold (FIQ prediction error < -0.1944), indicating the brain structure predicted a lower FIQ than observed. This subgroup consisted of N = 34 individuals. The remaining N = 300 individuals, whose prediction errors fell within the range, were categorized as the Typical-predict group.

Statistical Analysis: We performed group comparisons using non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for clinical variables and independent sample t-tests for neuroimaging features (FDR correction applied; trends at p < 0.05 uncorrected reported to generate hypotheses). We focused on identifying regions where the "Effective Compensation" group exhibited greater structural integrity compared to the "Potential Unfulfilled" group.

Results

Demographic and Behavioral Divergence: The subgroups showed a fundamental dissociation in cognitive profiles. The "Potential Unfulfilled" (Over-predicted) group had a significantly higher median FIQ (117.9) compared to the "Effective Compensation" (Under-predicted) group (66.8; p < 0.001, FDR-corrected), validating the grouping strategy. Morphometric Compensatory Signatures: Despite lower absolute IQ scores, the "Effective Compensation" group exhibited paradoxically robust structural integrity in key association cortices compared to the High-IQ "Potential Unfulfilled" group.1. Surface Area Expansion (Under > Over): The Effective Compensation group showed significantly greater surface area in limbic and prefrontal regions (p < 0.05, uncorrected), including the Left Entorhinal Cortex (p=0.036), a hub for memory; the Left Medial Orbitofrontal (p=0.008) and Right Lateral Orbitofrontal Cortex (p=0.036), implicating decision-making circuits; and the Left Frontal Pole (p=0.038). Notable expansion was also observed in the Right Pars Opercularis (p = 0.034) and the Right Pericalcarine cortex (p = 0.036). .2. Cortical Thickness Preservation (Under > Over): The Effective Compensation group displayed greater thickness in executive control regions, including the Left Superior Frontal Gyrus (p=0.004) and Right Superior Frontal Gyrus (p=0.049). Thicker cortex was also found in the Right Parahippocampal Gyrus (p=0.012), reinforcing the memory-circuit finding, and the Right Pars Opercularis (p=0.005) and Pars Triangularis (p=0.038). 3. Subcortical Volume: The Right Pallidum was larger in the Effective Compensation group (p=0.023). 4. Inverse Pattern: Conversely, the "Potential Unfulfilled" group showed greater thickness only in the Right Isthmus Cingulate (p=0.007). These findings suggest the "Effective Compensation" phenotype is characterized by a "structural reserve" in frontotemporal and limbic networks.

Conclusion

This study challenges the view that higher cognitive function in ASD is linearly associated with greater or lesser whole-brain morphometric integrity. By employing a GPR-based normative model trained on healthy controls, we demonstrate that prediction errors reveal distinct neurobiological subtypes. The "Effective Compensation" group, despite having a lower absolute FIQ, exhibits a pattern of "Structural Reserve"—characterized by a greater surface area and thickness in memory (entorhinal/parahippocampal), executive (superior frontal), and socio-emotional (orbitofrontal) regions. This suggests that maintaining cortical real estate in these high-order areas is a critical mechanism for cognitive adaptation in ASD. Conversely, the "Potential Unfulfilled" group, despite having a high IQ, shows relative structural attenuation in these regions, indicating potential neural inefficiency. These results highlight the utility of normative modeling residuals in uncovering latent heterogeneity in ASD.
