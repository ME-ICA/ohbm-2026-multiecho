Introduction

Lesion Network Mapping (LNM) offers a framework for identifying brain circuits causally linked to specific symptoms by analyzing how lesion or brain stimulation seed functional connectivity (FC) maps relate to behavioral outcomes. By comparing these maps across subjects with similar symptoms, researchers identify brain regions associated with specific disorders, which are interpreted as disorder-specific circuits.1 A newer approach, symptom-based LNM (sLNM), improves upon LNM by correlating connectivity patterns with clinical outcomes. Studies using sLNM have successfully predicted treatment responses across diverse conditions. To improve robustness, the method uses permutation testing and cross-dataset replication to ensure findings are not due to chance. Importantly, sLNM studies consistently pass these validation checks, yet published sLNM maps shows cross-disorder similarity, raising questions about the method's validity.

Recent work by van den Heuvel et al. revealed a mathematical flaw in the (s)LNM method: using real and randomized lesion data produces nearly identical brain maps. Using degree-preserving randomized connectomes instead of the real connectome also produced identical outputs, proving (s)LNM output converges toward degree sequence/gradient 1 (PC1) of the connectome rather than disease biology. This represents a fundamental challenge to the validity of (s)LNM. 

Here, we build on this work by replicating the unexpected convergence between sLNM maps of depression and Broca aphasia. To further investigate sLNM, we (1) tested whether sLNM produces converging maps from datasets with various lesion-symptom associations, (2) whether sLNM converge specifically to the degree sequence or to PC1 of the human connectome, and (3) whether treatment effects following TMS were better predicted by sLNM maps compared to connectome PC1.   

Methods

Overview. We conducted four main analyses: (1) applied sLNM to two clinical datasets (Depression and Broca aphasia) to test whether unrelated disorders produce convergent circuits, (2) applied sLNM to simulated datasets with known ground-truth circuits to test how the degree of spurious convergence in sLNM interacts with lesion-symptom pairing at various effect sizes, (3) performed sLNM on simulated datasets in which we dissociated the degree and the PC1 of the connectome to test which of these underlying graph structures drive convergence, (4) compared treatment prediction between sLNM and PC1 to test clinical utility. 

Symptoms-Based Lesion Network Mapping. For a given dataset, each subject's seed was mapped to a voxel-wise mean FC map using the GSP1000 resting-state fMRI dataset.2 Seed FC values were correlated with behavioral outcomes at each voxel across subjects, producing an sLNM circuit map (Fig. 1a). 

Application to Real Clinical Data. We applied sLNM to two unrelated clinical datasets: (1) 25 depression subjects treated with transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS),3 with 4mm radius sphere at TMS sites as seeds and percent reduction in Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) as outcome, and (2) 85 Broca aphasia subjects,4 with stroke lesion seeds and Western Aphasia Battery (WAB) scores as outcome. As controls, we repeated the pipeline using subject age or random integers between 0 and 100 (1000 iterations) instead of behavioral outcomes.

Convergence Test. To test whether two datasets converged, sLNM was applied separately to produce dataset-specific circuit maps. Spatial correlation quantified their similarity. Significance was assessed via 1,000 permutation testing. On each iteration, symptom labels were randomly reassigned to different subjects within each dataset, and the full pipeline was recomputed. Dataset pairs with p<0.05 were classified as convergent (Fig. 1b).

Systematic Evaluation with Synthetic Datasets. To assess whether sLNM produces artifacts under controlled conditions, we generated synthetic datasets with known ground-truth circuits. Synthetic lesions were 4mm spheres at uniformly sampled voxels. Ground-truth circuits were FC maps from randomly selected Schaefer cortical regions. Symptoms were modeled as spatial correlation between subject seed FC map and ground-truth circuit plus noise (Fig. 1c). For each of 7 effect sizes, we generated 1000 dataset pairs with different ground-truths and tested convergence.

Null Connectome Models. To understand the factors influencing sLNM map outputs, we downsampled analyses to 1000-Schaefer’s parcellation to facilitate randomization of the connectome matrix. In this simplified model, lesions were modeled as single Schaefer parcels, with FC maps and ground-truth circuits corresponding to rows in the connectome matrix. sLNM outputs were generated by correlating each parcel's seed connectivity values with behavior, producing 1000-parcel maps. We decomposed the connectome matrix into eigenvectors/eigenvalues and generated randomized connectomes where eigenvalues were swapped to manipulate which component dominated. Additionally, we created a synthetic connectome with PC1 replaced by a cartoon dinosaur. For each connectome model, 500 synthetic datasets with various ground-truth circuits were generated (effect size=0.3), and PCA on the resulting sLNM outputs identified the dominant pattern. We also computed the degree sequence as the row-wise sum of the connectivity matrix to compare against PC1 and sLNM outputs.

Prediction of Behavior. Using a leave-one-subject-out (LOO) analysis on the TMS-depression dataset: the spatial correlation between the left-out subject’s TMS connectivity map and the LOO sLNM map was used to predict improvements in depression. We compared sLNM prediction to using PC1 alone. 

Results

Unrelated Brain Disorders Mapped onto Convergent Circuit. The 'Depression-TMS' and 'Broca aphasia' circuit maps showed significant convergence (r=0.56, p=0.03; 1000 permutations) (Fig. 1a/b). Meanwhile, control maps generated from subject age instead of behavioral outcomes did not converge (r=-0.26, p=0.78; 1000 permutations). Across 1000 simulations using random integers between 0-100 instead of symptoms, 4.3% of runs (n = 43/1000) yielded 'converging' brain circuits, consistent with expected false positive rates. According to LNM methodology, this is evidence of a robust causally linked brain circuit and averaging both maps together would reveal a 'convergent' depression-Broca circuit.

Strength of Lesion-Symptom Association Determines Convergence Rate. Across 1000 synthetic dataset pairs at each effect size, we observed clear trends. At zero effect size (lesions and symptoms independent), convergence rate was 5% with uniform p-values, indicating appropriate null control (Fig. 2a-c). At higher effect sizes, convergence occurred even when ground-truth circuits were unrelated. For example, at spatial r near zero, convergence rates reached 16% at effect size 0.3 and 57% at effect size 0.99, with increasingly bimodal p-values (near zero and one) at higher effect sizes.

Connectome PC1 Accounts for sLNM Convergence. We hypothesized sLNM systematically converges toward the connectome's PC1. We tested this by swapping eigenvalue order to reassign which brain map dominated variance, then performing simplified sLNM on datasets generated by the modified connectome. sLNM-derived circuits systematically converged toward whichever pattern dominated the spectrum: PC1 in unmodified connectomes, and other PCs (2 and 51) when assigned highest variance (Fig. 2d). When we replaced PC1 with a cartoon dinosaur pattern projected onto the brain surface, sLNM circuits converged toward the dinosaur map. Consistent with this, previously analyzed clinical circuits also resembled PC1 (TMS-depression r=0.60; Broca aphasia r=0.75) (Fig. 2e).

PC1 Predicts Treatment Effect More Strongly Than LNM. Using leave-one-subject-out (LOO) analysis to generate LOO sLNM maps as predictors, sLNM predicted TMS response in depression (r=0.40) (Fig. 2f: left). However, PC1 alone outperformed sLNM (TMS-depression: r=0.42) (Fig. 2f: right). 

Conclusion

Recent work by van den Heuvel et al. revealed a fundamental flaw in Lesion Network Mapping: unrelated disorders produce convergent circuit maps because outputs are systematically dominated by the brain's degree sequence/PC1 rather than disease-specific mechanisms. Here, we build on this work by providing an example of unexpected convergence between unrelated disorders using the sLNM variant. Furthermore, we expand on prior work by demonstrating that sLNM specifically converges to the PC1 rather than the degree sequence. However, in practice, they are highly similar in the GSP1000 connectome (spatial r = 0.84).

Two unrelated disorders, TMS-depression and Broca aphasia, produced significantly convergent circuit maps. Controls using subject age or random numbers showed no convergence, which by published standards would confirm robustness. However, synthetic datasets with different ground-truth circuits (spatial r ≈ 0) showed convergence rates of 16-68% depending on effect size, far exceeding the expected 5% false positive rate. Control analyses (using age or random integers) pass permutation testing because they are independent of lesion, but any meaningful lesion-symptom association increases convergence rate regardless of underlying ground truth.

Connectome randomization revealed sLNM outputs are dominated by PC1. Importantly, PC1 is biologically meaningful as the principal gradient contrasting unimodal to transmodal regions, a fundamental organizational axis.4 This creates a paradox: sLNM outputs are mathematical artifacts, yet PC1 itself predicts behavior because lesion position along this axis plausibly affects outcomes. As such, sLNM fails to identify disorder-specific mechanisms, but outputs retain predictive value, which may explain prior work showing the value of sLNM for targeted treatment. 

Our simulation framework modeled symptoms based on seed FC correlation to ground-truth circuits, which may not reflect true disease mechanisms. However, this simulation approach follows the framework assumed by LNM methodology. We demonstrated that even in the ideal scenario where diseases operate exactly as LNM proponents propose, the method still produces systematic artifacts. We analyzed only two real datasets, limiting our ability to conclude whether findings generalize broadly to published sLNM literature. 

Lastly, it is critical to distinguish the LNM framework from the method. The framework—that lesions/brain stimulations affect behavior through distributed networks rather than focal regions—remains valid and is supported by our findings. The convergence we observe would not occur without genuine network-level effects in the datasets. However, the method is flawed: rather than recovering disorder-specific circuits, sLNM systematically produces PC1-dominated outputs. This explains why their clinical utility is retained. Published sLNM findings should be reinterpreted as identifying positions along this shared organizational gradient rather than unique disease circuits. Future work requires improved methods that can isolate disorder-specific mechanisms from this dominant gradient. We provide a dataset simulation framework to benchmark future methods that can isolate disorder-specific mechanisms from this dominant gradient, enabling the field to fulfill the promise of network-based approaches to understanding brain disorders and a step towards precision medicine.
