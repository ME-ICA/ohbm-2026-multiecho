Introduction

Introduction: The rubber hand illusion (RHI) provides a controlled way to perturb body ownership by introducing visuo-tactile conflict between a hidden real hand and a visible artificial one¹. Although the illusion is typically evaluated using binary or summary measures—such as ownership reports, proprioceptive drift, or questionnaire scores—these approaches overlook the fact that the illusion unfolds gradually rather than emerging as an all-or-nothing phenomenon. As a result, the fine-grained temporal progression from early sensory evaluation to partial incorporation and eventual ownership remains poorly understood.

From a neurophenomenological perspective, addressing this limitation requires methods that can characterize temporal transitions in subjective experience and relate them to neural dynamics. To obtain such structured data, we used an elicitation-interview approach and analyzed the transcribed reports using a micro-phenomenological procedure² to identify recurring subjective states and their temporal transitions. This procedure allows first-person descriptions to be systematically organized into stable experiential categories and their sequential structure, providing a data-driven reconstruction of the microgenesis of body-ownership change beyond binary or summary behavioral measures.

To relate these experiential trajectories to neural dynamics, we modeled EEG functional connectivity as time-varying graphs and characterized their evolving topology using a Weighted Graph Attention Network (WeightedGAT)³. Clustering the resulting embeddings identified distinct EEG network states that unfolded alongside experiential phases of the illusion. We then applied Gromov–Wasserstein (GW) optimal transport⁴˒⁵ to align the transition structure of neural states with that of the elicitation-derived subjective states, enabling a principled examination of how experiential transitions correspond to frequency-specific reorganization in large-scale cortical networks.

Methods

Method: Sixteen healthy adults participated in a rubber hand illusion (RHI) experiment designed to modulate body ownership through visuo-tactile conflict. Participants placed their right hand behind a cardboard screen while observing a visible rubber hand positioned 15 cm to the right. The experimenter brushed both hands either synchronously or asynchronously, and each RHI induction lasted approximately 10 minutes.

During the stimulation, participants provided real-time verbal reports of their ongoing sensations, collected using an elicitation interview approach, which were recorded using a digital voice recorder (ICD-UX570FB, SONY, Japan). The audio data were transcribed and analyzed following a micro-phenomenological procedure2 to identify recurring subjective states and their temporal transitions. Each participant’s sequence of states was compared across individuals to construct a subjective state-transition model, representing the typical experiential progression during the illusion. The experimental setup and RHI procedure are illustrated in Figure 1a.

EEG signals were recorded at 512 Hz from 30 scalp electrodes, preprocessed with CleanLine (50/100 Hz noise removal), 1–60 Hz band-pass filtering, and ICA-based artifact rejection. Source reconstruction was performed using standard low-resolution brain electromagnetic tomography (sLORETA), and signals were parcellated into 24 anatomically defined ROIs covering premotor, parietal, insular, and prefrontal regions relevant to body ownership. For each ROI, power spectral density (PSD) was computed across standard frequency bands (delta–gamma). Functional connectivity was estimated using lagged coherence, which isolates phase-delayed interactions and minimizes spurious coupling due to volume conduction. Connectivity matrices were thresholded to retain the top 20 % of edges, yielding weighted, undirected brain graphs for each frequency band. 

An overview of the analysis pipeline, including graph construction, GNN embedding, and GW alignment, is presented in Figure 1b. Each graph was processed using a WeightedGAT composed of two multi-head attention layers and an attention-based pooling module. The network was trained in a self-supervised contrastive learning framework to learn low-dimensional embeddings that captured the topological organization of dynamic functional networks. 

The resulting embeddings were clustered using K-medoids, and each cluster was treated as a distinct EEG network state. To align neural and subjective domains, we applied GW optimal transport, which minimizes geometric distortion between the transition structures of the two state spaces. Alignment significance was tested via 1,000-iteration permutation tests across 20 repeated mappings. Finally, normalized degree centrality was computed for each ROI to quantify frequency- and state-dependent reorganization of cortical hubs.

Results

Results: Eight subjective states (State 1-1 to State 2´) were identified from elicitation interview, describing a gradual evolution from State 1-1 (tactile perception) and State 1-2 (visuo-tactile congruence) through State 1-3 (perceptive dissonance), State 1-4 (cognitive validation), and State 1-5 (beginning of rubber-hand incorporation) to State 2 (illusory ownership) or State 2´ (absence of illusion). These categories formed a consistent subjective state-transition structure, showing smoother progressions toward the illusion in the Sync condition, whereas in the Async condition, the process is more complex, less stable, and often fails to culminate in a sustained illusory state. Significant mappings were observed across all frequency bands except beta3 (p < 0.05, permutation test). Analysis of state-to-state similarity using mean GW distance showed that the theta (4–7 Hz) and beta2 (19–22 Hz) bands exhibited the lowest distances, corresponding to the most pronounced divergence between states.

Frequency-specific network analyses revealed complementary patterns. Linear mixed-effects models identified significant three-way interactions among State, Condition, and Band in L-PMd (F(3,420) = 3.61, p < 0.05), L-Ins (F(3,402.09) = 3.12, p < 0.05), L-dlPFC (F(3,402.47) = 3.33, p < 0.05), L-mPFC (F(3,420) = 3.88, p < 0.01), and R-IPL (F(3,401.72) = 3.96, p < 0.01). Post-hoc analyses within each frequency band revealed distinct patterns.

In the theta band, State 1-5 was characterized by significantly elevated degree centrality under Sync stimulation. L-PMd in the Sync condition showed higher centrality in State 1-5 than State 1-1 (estimate = 0.10, t(422.09) = 3.01, p < 0.01), State 1-2 (estimate=0.159, t(423) = 4.74, p < 0.01), and State 2 (estimate = 0.12, t(422.09) = 3.61, p < 0.01). A similar pattern was observed in L-Ins (State 1-5 > State 1-1; estimate = 0.115, t(422.96) = 4.36, p < 0.01) and L-mPFC (State 1-5 > State 1-2; estimate = 0.113, t(423.12) = 5.08, p < 0.01). These findings indicate that, in the theta band, degree centrality in premotor, insular, and medial prefrontal regions increased during the transition toward rubber-hand incorporation under Sync stimulation.

In the beta2 band, mean degree centrality was significantly reduced during State 1-3. In the Async condition, L-PMd displayed lower centrality in State 1-3 than in State 1-1 (estimate = –0.108, t(431.08) = –2.80, p < 0.05), State 1-5 (estimate = –0.150, t(431.08) = –3.91, p < 0.01), and State 2 (estimate = –0.114, t(431.08) = –2.97, p < 0.01). Consistent reductions were also observed in L-mPFC (Async < Sync in State 1-3; estimate = –0.073, t(19.32) = –2.64, p < 0.05) and L-dlPFC (State 1-3 < State 1-2’ and 2’; t(430.35) = –3.30, p < 0.05). These results indicate that, in the beta2 band, degree centrality in premotor, dorsolateral, and medial prefrontal regions decreased during perceptive dissonance under Async stimulation.

Together, the findings show that, in the theta band, increases in degree centrality indicate the integrative progression toward rubber-hand incorporation (State 1-5), whereas in the beta2 band, decreases in degree centrality indicate breakdown and recalibration during perceptive dissonance (State 1-3). This demonstrates that transitions in subjective states are closely coupled with frequency-specific reorganization of the brain’s hub network structure.

Conclusion

Conclusions: By linking first-person experiential transitions with network-level brain dynamics, this study demonstrates that variability in subjective experience reflects structured neural reorganization rather than random fluctuations. This computational neurophenomenological framework provides a principled way to relate the temporal structure of subjective experience to frequency-specific changes in large-scale brain networks and can be extended to other paradigms in which subjective states evolve dynamically over time.
