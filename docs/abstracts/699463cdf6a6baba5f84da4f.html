Introduction

The brainstem is a vital part of the nervous system, connecting the brain with the rest of the body and containing nuclei essential for brain function. Among them are the nuclei that produce major neurotransmitters in the brain. Thus, the brain stem is a site for production and distribution of dopamine, serotonin and noradrenaline. The functions of these neurotransmitters range from motor control to attentional modulation. 

Despite its importance, the brainstem is a relatively small area that nevertheless has an intricate organization. While studies have extensively mapped the brain stem ex vivo, measuring its organization in vivo had been challenging before the recent advances in MRI technology. In this project, we acquired a high-resolution, precision dataset and identified some of the most important neurotransmitter-releasing nuclei in the brainstem. We utilized multiple contrasts and MRI scanners to leverage the most advanced imaging technology available and found that a multi-contrast and multi-scanner acquisition protocol helps identify small brain stem nuclei. Overall, we demonstrate the feasibility of accurate in vivo localization of small nuclei in the brain stem using high resolution multi-contrast MRI.

Methods

We recruited three volunteers for a scanning protocol that spanned approximately 3 hours, divided into two sessions. In the first session, we acquired high-resolution 3D T2 space (isotropic voxel size 0.4 mm³), MPRAGE (0.5 mm³), MP2RAGE (0.5 mm³), and multi-echo FLASH (0.7 mm³) images. A high-field Siemens Terra.X 7T scanner was used to acquire all images, taking advantage of a parallel-transmission coil setup to improve B1 field homogeneity. The scanner’s high magnetic field enabled us to decrease the voxel size, although at the cost of a longer acquisition time. All images were denoised, bias-corrected, brain-extracted, and coregistered to the T2 SPACE template using rigid-body registration implemented in ANTs package. As high-resolution images are more challenging to align due to the great detail, we optimized the registration to the brain stem using masks. We analyzed the multi-echo FLASH sequence and derived the quantitative Chi values, as well as the susceptibility-weighted contrasts using the SEPIA toolbox1. 

In the second scanning session, we acquired a diffusion-weighted dataset (1.2 mm³) along with conventional T1w MPRAGE (0.7 mm³) and T2 SPACE (0.7 mm³) images. We utilized the Siemens Cima.X 3T MRI machine and leveraged its strong gradient system for diffusion encoding. We corrected the DWI images for distortion and eddy currents using the FSL package, which also computed the DTI tensor image. We then aligned the MPRAGE image from the diffusion session with the average preprocessed b0 image first and with the MP2RAGE image from the anatomical session second. We aligned the DTI image to the same T2 SPACE (0.4 mm³) template to aid with segmentation.

Overall, our protocol provided a multi-contrast dataset that helped identify the brainstem nuclei (Fig. 1).

Results

We segmented the main neurotransmitter-producing nuclei, guided by the Brain Navigator dataset2, the MRI/DTI atlas of the Human Brain3 and the WikiBrainstem resource4. The dopamine-releasing nuclei included the parabrachial nucleus of the ventral tegmental area (PBP, total volume: 223.5 mm³ right, 208.8 mm³ left) and the substantia nigra pars compacta (SN, 118.6 mm³ right, 93.57 left). The serotonin-releasing nuclei included the median and dorsal raphe nuclei (MnR 44.8 mm³ and DR 119.6 mm³), as well as the caudal-rostral linear nuclei (CLi-RLi 109.5 mm³). For the noradrenaline nucleus, we identified the locus coeruleus (LC 5.952 mm³ right, 7.232 mm³ left) (Figure 2). While some nuclei were visible on the images (SN, MnR, and CLi-RLi), others were not detectable (PBP, LC); thus, we relied on the relative position of the nuclei to other surrounding structures. We further computed the Dice overlap coefficients to compare the manual segmentations with the warped nuclei from the Brainstem Navigator. We observed that for most nuclei, the Dice coefficient was low (PBP: 74.14%, SN: 21.44%, MnR: 0.99%, DR:  83.08%, CLi-RLi: 83.18%, LC: 10.55%). These low Dice coefficients indicate discrepancies between atlas-based and subject-specific segmentations and thus, potential advantage of precision high-resolution imaging for individual subject analysis.

Conclusion

Using a combination of advanced imaging sequences and leveraging the advantages of two MRI scanners, we acquired a high-resolution, precision dataset that allowed us to investigate the intricate structure of the brainstem. We identified the major neurotransmitter nuclei responsible for the production of dopamine, serotonin, and noradrenaline in the brain. We further demonstrated that the precision of high-resolution imaging, paired with careful segmentation, is vital in studying intricately organized subcortical brain structures. While limited to a small number of participants, this work serves as a precision imaging proof-of-concept for in vivo brainstem neuroanatomy.
