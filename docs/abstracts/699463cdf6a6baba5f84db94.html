Introduction

Accurate choroid plexus (CP) segmentation in neonatal MRI facilitates quantitative analysis of CP volume and its morphology. The CP contributes to cerebrospinal fluid production and may relate to neurodevelopmental outcomes and glymphatic function1. CP segmentation is particularly challenging in neonates due to rapid morphological changes during the perinatal period, including age-dependent variations in size, shape, and signal intensity of the CP that lead to substantial anatomical variability and complicate automated methods.

Several deep learning methods have been developed for CP segmentation in adults, and perform well in mature brain anatomy2. These models do not translate directly to neonatal MRI, where tissue contrast, ventricular morphology, and CP appearance differ from adults and rapidly change across gestational ages (GA). To address this, previous work has often relied on training separate age-specific models or restricting analyses to narrow GA ranges.

Incorporating age information into segmentation networks offers a promising alternative for handling age-related anatomical variability. Conditional instance normalization (CIN) provides a mechanism for modulating network features based on metadata such as age, enabling a single model to adapt its predictions across different developmental stages. However, existing CIN-based approaches typically require manual, heuristic selection of which network layers should receive an age conditioning that is time-consuming, requires domain expertise, and may result in suboptimal results across different datasets and architectures.

This study introduce an automated framework for age-conditioned CP segmentation in neonatal MRI. We implement a rule-based controller that dynamically identifies age-sensitive layers during training by monitoring their contribution to segmentation performance. This approach reduces manual layer selection and aims to achieve consistent segmentation performance across a wide range of GA.

Methods

Participants and imaging protocol 

We utilized T1-weighted (T1w) MRI from 200 participants in the developing Human Connectome Project spanning GA between 24 and 43 weeks4 (GA at scan ranged from 26 to 45 weeks). The cohort was stratified into five gestational month groups with approximately 15 training subjects per group and 10 validation subjects total. An additional 100 subjects were used to examine developmental trajectories. T1w images were acquired with an inversion recovery turbo spin echo (IR-TSE) sequence (0.8 mm isotropic in-plane resolution, 1.6 mm slice thickness with 0.8 mm overlap, TR/TE = 4800/8.7 ms, and a total scan time of 6 min 24 s). Ground truth CP masks were semi-automatically generated using Gaussian Mixture Model clustering and manual correction within lateral ventricle regions of interest obtained from infant FreeSerfer. This task was performed by a trained researcher and reviewed by an experienced radiologist.

Network 

Our age-conditioned CP segmentation method is described in Figure 1.  A 3D U-Net included CIN layers in all encoder and decoder stages for age-conditioning. GA at scan was encoded using sinusoidal positional encoding to modulate CIN parameters. To automatically determine which layers require age conditioning, we introduced a rule-based controller that monitors each layer’s contribution, defined as the Dice difference with versus without age conditioning. This evaluation was performed every 50 training iterations on the validation set. Layers were categorized based on smoothed contribution scores, and noncontributing layers were frozen to reduce computation.

Model training

The model was trained using a hybrid loss consisting of Dice, focal Tversky, and weighted cross-entropy losses. Data augmentation including random rotation, flipping ,and intensity variations was applied. Training employed randomly sampled 32 x 32 x 32 patches. The network was optimized using AdamW (learning rate = 1e-3, weight decay = 1e-4) with a cosine annealing schedule for 512 epochs. Note that layer-contribution measurements were performed approximately 51 times over 512 epochs.

Evaluation

Layer-contribution measurements were performed approximately 51 times across training. Models were evaluated on a test set of 40 dHCP subjects using Dice similarity coefficient (DSC), 95th percentile Hausdorff distance (HD95), and average symmetric surface distance (ASSD). Comparison methods included baseline U-Net, U-Net trained on a sub-age subset (<34 weeks GA), U-Net with age concatenation, U-Net with squeeze-and-excitation blocks, U-Net with FiLM layers, and nnU-Net. Age-stratified analysis was performed across five gestational month groups. Statistical significance was assessed using paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (p < 0.05). To assess developmental validity, we fitted a linear mixed-effects model to CP volume estimates from the proposed method, with GA and sex as fixed effects (including a GA×sex interaction) and a subject-specific random intercept to account for repeated scans in a subset of infants.

Results

Figure 2 summarizes the quantitative and qualitative segmentation results. 

The proposed age-conditioned method (U-Net + CIN) achieved a mean Dice similarity coefficient of 0.86 ± 0.21 across 40 test subjects, significantly outperforming all comparison methods: baseline U-Net (0.73 ± 0.17), U-Net trained on a sub-age subset of subjects younger than 34 weeks (0.71 ± 0.17), nnU-Net (0.75 ± 0.22), age-concatenation U-Net (0.74 ± 0.20), squeeze-and-excitation block U-Net (0.67 ± 0.25), and FiLM layer U-Net (0.77 ± 0.26) (all p<0.05, paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test; Figure 2A). Boundary delineation metrics also showed substantial improvement, with HD95 decreasing from 13.76 mm (baseline U-Net) to 2.80 mm (proposed method), and ASSD improving from 2.21 mm to 0.59 mm.

Representative segmentation results across the GA spectrum (26, 28, 31, 33, and 39 weeks) demonstrate improved anatomical accuracy and boundary precision compared to baseline methods (Figure 2D). Visual inspection revealed that the proposed method better captured fine anatomical details of the CP, particularly in younger subjects where tissue contrast is lower and ventricular boundaries are less distinct.

To assess performance stability across development, subjects were stratified into five GA groups. Age-stratified analysis showed consistently high performance across all groups, with Dice coefficients for the proposed method ranging from 0.85 to 0.89 (Figure 2B). The proposed method showed improvements over baseline U-Net across all GA groups (ΔDice range: 0.08-0.29), with the largest improvement observed at intermediate GA (30-36 weeks).

To understand which network components benefited most from age conditioning, we analyzed layer-wise contributions across training epochs (32, 256, and 512 epochs). Results showed that decoder layers exhibited consistently higher contributions to age-conditioned performance compared to encoder layers (Figure 2C). Decoder stages 3 and 4 showed mean contribution scores of 0.08 and 0.11, respectively, while encoder stages showed contributions below 0.04. 

A linear mixed-effects model with a subject-specific random intercept and GA as a fixed effect, additionally adjusting for sex and a GA×sex interaction, showed a significant positive association between CP volume and GA (β = 14.44 mm³/week, 95% CI [5.48, 23.40], z = 3.16, p = 0.002; Figure 2E). Sex was not significantly associated with baseline CP volume (p = 0.831) or with the GA–CP volume slope (GA × sex interaction, p = 0.837). The model also indicated substantial between-subject variability in baseline CP volume (random-intercept SD ≈ 151 mm³).

Conclusion

Our results demonstrated that age-conditioning improved neonatal CP segmentation, with gains at younger GA where morphological changes and anatomical variability are pronounced. The improvements in earlier GA may be associated with the more dramatic developmental changes occurring during this window, when CP volume, shape, and tissue contrast rapidly transform. Improvements of performance were observed in both overlap and boundary metrics, including higher Dice and lower HD95 and ASSD.

Previous approaches using conditional instance normalization have relied on domain expertise to determine which network layers should receive age modulation. This process is time-consuming and may lead to suboptimal performance. Our rule-based controller uses layer-wise training signals to identify age-sensitive layers, which can reduce reliance on manual layer selection. Although our experiments used dHCP data, this automated framework may support future application to other datasets and acquisition protocols.

Layer-wise analysis indicated that decoder stages contributed more to age-conditioned performance than encoder layers (Figure 2C). This observation suggests that age modulation has a greater influence on decoder representations in our method. Age conditioning at this stage may help refine boundary delineation by accounting for developmental variations in tissue contrast and CP morphology.

We examined the relationship between CP volume from the proposed method and GA using mixed-effects modeling. CP volume showed an increasing trend with GA across the observed range (Figure 2E). This suggests that the estimated volumes reflect a developmental trajectory during the perinatal period, consistent with prior reports of age-related increases in CP volume5.

This study has several limitations. First, our training data were drawn only from the dHCP dataset, and generalization to other acquisition protocols or scanner types requires further validation. Second, the current model conditions only on GA at scan. Incorporating additional metadata such as birth weight, sex, or clinical risk factors may further improve performance. Future work may explore the application of this approach in pathological populations.

In conclusion, we propose an age-conditioned method for automatic CP quantification across the neonatal period. More consistent CP volume estimates may contribute to developmental studies investigating the role of CP in cerebrospinal fluid dynamics, glymphatic function, and neurodevelopmental outcomes. By enabling single-model segmentation without age-specific retraining, the approach may help support analyses in larger and more heterogeneous datasets, including longitudinal and multi-site studies of early brain development and its potential associations with later cognitive and behavioral outcomes.
