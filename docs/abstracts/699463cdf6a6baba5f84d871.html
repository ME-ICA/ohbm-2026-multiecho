Introduction

Grey matter pathology has been studied extensively in Parkinson’s disease (PD). However, white matter involvement remains poorly understood. Most white matter research relies on diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), which offers limited pathoanatomical specificity. Compartment-specific modelling techniques that separate intra- and extra-axonal diffusion have been underutilised in PD research. Fiber ball white matter (FBWM) modelling uses multi-shell, high b-value diffusion data to separate intra-axonal (restricted diffusion within axons) and extra-axonal (unrestricted diffusion in extracellular space) compartments, potentially providing a more sensitive assessment of alterations within the cerebral microstructural environment.1 This technique has not been used to study PD. We used FBWM to investigate white matter tract alterations in people with de-novo PD.

Methods

We recruited ten patients with newly diagnosed, drug-naïve PD (within 6 months of diagnosis; 3F/7M; age 67.9±7.0 years; H&Y 1.1±0.2; UPDRS-III 15.6±6.5) and ten age-matched controls (5F/5M; age 67.6±6.8 years). All patients were assessed using the Hoehn & Yahr (H&Y) scale and Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale Part III (UPDRS-III) to determine the extent to which motor functions were impaired. For each participant, diffusion MRI was acquired on a 3T Siemens scanner using a multi-shell sequence (bvals = 1000, 2000, 5000 s/mm²; bvecs = 30, 30, 128). Following preprocessing in MRtrix, we used TractSeg to probabilistically reconstruct 72 white matter tracts.2 PyDesigner was used to extract FBWM compartment-specific metrics for each tract (intra-axonal: axonal water fraction, intra-axonal diffusivity, intra-axonal fractional anisotropy (FA); extra-axonal mean diffusivity (MD), extra-axonal radial diffusivity (RD), and extra-axonal axial diffusivity (AD).3,4 We also computed conventional DTI metrics (FA, MD, AD, RD). Within-tract voxelwise comparisons between patients and controls were administered using FSL randomise with threshold-free cluster enhancement and family-wise error correction (p < .050, 5000 permutations, 10-voxel minimum cluster extent), covarying for age and sex. Effect sizes were calculated as Hedges' g with a small-sample correction.

Results

People with PD had increased extra-axonal diffusivity of 23 white matter tracts compared to controls. Subregions of the corpus callosum demonstrated the largest effects, which included significantly increased extra-axonal AD in the genu (cluster size = 883 voxels, t = 4.46, pcorr = .014, g = 1.91) and rostral body (328 voxels, t = 4.02, pcorr = .008, g = 1.72). Additional significant increases were observed in left-hemisphere association tracts (Superior longitudinal fasciculus III: 218 voxels, g = 2.27; inferior longitudinal fasciculus: 151 voxels, g = 1.79), visual pathways (optic radiation, thalamo-occipital), and frontal projection tracts in people with PD. Extra-axonal MD was similarly increased, approaching significance in the genu and rostral body of the corpus callosum (108 voxels, t = 4.56, pcorr = 0.07; 41 voxels, t = 3.66, p = 0.051). There were no significant differences in intra-axonal diffusion metrics for any tract between patients and controls. There were no significant differences in conventional DTI metrics (FA, MD, RD) between patients and controls. Only DTI AD showed weak detection in four tracts, with substantially smaller clusters than FBWM (30 vs 328 voxels in the rostral body of the corpus callosum).

Conclusion

This is the first application of FBWM modelling in PD. Increased extra-axonal diffusivity and preserved intra-axonal diffusivity may reflect reduced axonal density or inflammatory processes, as previously suggested.1 The predominance of alterations in the genu and rostral body of the corpus callosum (prefrontal and premotor circuits, respectively) aligns with established frontostriatal dysfunction in early PD and may underlie executive and motor planning deficits. Previous DTI studies have reported reduced FA and increased MD in the corpus callosum in large cohorts, whereas our findings show extra-axonal diffusion changes in these areas in a small cohort of patients.5 The greater sensitivity of FBWM modelling in detecting WM changes suggests that this method may be a useful biomarker in new-onset PD.
