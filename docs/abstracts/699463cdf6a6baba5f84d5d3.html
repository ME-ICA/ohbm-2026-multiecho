Introduction

In recent years, interest in non-oscillatory, aperiodic dynamics in EEG and MEG recordings has grown exponentially. Several findings now suggest that aperiodic activity, often ignored as background noise, is functionally relevant, and may be related to attentional control processes. In a series of studies,1,2 we demonstrated that aperiodic activity, quantified as the broadband shape of the power spectrum of neural data, shows event-related changes in attentional tasks and could thus provide a novel window into information processing. While these results are corroborated by other recent findings,3 investigations into task-based dynamics in aperiodic activity are complicated by the presence of event-related potentials (ERPs) in response to stimuli. ERPs can confound aperiodic activity estimates as they also have a broadband representation in the neural power spectrum. Despite this, ERPs are usually ignored in event-related aperiodic analyses, and in the cases where they are addressed, the common approach is to subtract the trial-averaged ERP from the single-trial data.1 Here, we demonstrate the importance of accounting for ERPs in aperiodic investigations and show the limitations of a single-trial subtraction approach using simulated data grounded in real EEG recordings.

Methods

We used EEG recordings from 46 healthy participants (mean age =35 ± 22.2, 56.5% female) completing a passive listening task involving the random presentation of short tones (details for original study reported in1). Empirical data was used to extract realistic ERPs from the post-stimulus period and realistic broadband activity from the pre-stimulus period for each individual, uncontaminated by ERPs. These were then used to create 46 simulated single-trial data sets. ERPs were calculated by averaging time-domain activity across trials (mean trial number = 22.76 ± 2.85) in the 512-ms-long post-stimulus window. We then extracted the 512-ms-long window immediately before the auditory stimulus on each trial. This was used as the pre-stimulus window, and assumed to show broadband aperiodic dynamics unrelated to the event.1,2 The simulated post-stimulus window for each trial was then created by taking the pre-stimulus window, and adding the ERP pattern. As such, the two time windows only differed in the presence of the ERPs but showed identical broadband dynamics. Crucially, we also manipulated the trial-to-trial variability of ERPs by shifting the template pattern backwards or forwards in time on each trial (temporal jitter) and by multiplying it by a random weight on each trial (amplitude jitter). We manipulated the magnitude of the temporal and amplitude jitter independently, across 5 levels for each variable. Across levels, temporal jitter increased from 0 time points to 60 time points (30 ms) in either direction, while amplitude jitter increased by 25%. We then extracted "pre-stimulus" and "post-stimulus" aperiodic parameter estimates in all conditions, using a robust extension of the specparam (previously known as fooof) method4 recently introduced by our team.5 This was done before and after single-trial ERP subtraction. ERPs were calculated using the simulated single-trial data, as would be done in an actual study, instead of using the ground-truth ERP template. We then compared pre- and post-stimulus aperiodic dynamics in the different conditions. If ERPs do not confound aperiodic dynamics, no pre-post differences would emerge in any condition. If they do, but ERP removal is sufficient to resolve this confound, we would expect differences before ERP removal, but not after.

Results

Without ERP removal, large illusory differences in aperiodic dynamics emerged in all conditions (Fig. 1, difference between solid lines). Single-trial ERP removal was successful in mitigating these illusory differences only in conditions with minimal ERP jitter (Fig. 1, difference between blue and dashed red lines). In conditions where the ERPs showed larger trial-to-trial variability, illusory pre-post differences persisted in aperiodic dynamics (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p < .05 in all conditions, except the first two subpanels in row 1, Fig. 1). Quantifying the cross-trial standard deviation (SD) of single-trial data, as opposed to its average, helped differentiate the jitter conditions from each other, as the time series of SDs showed larger fluctuations in higher jitter, compared to lower jitter conditions.

Conclusion

Our results clearly demonstrate the importance of considering the confounding effect of ERPs in the investigation of stimulus-induced aperiodic dynamics. ERPs led to apparent stimulus-induced changes in aperiodic activity, even when no such changes were present in the ground truth data. This might lead researchers to conclude that aperiodic activity, assumed to show different generative mechanisms than ERPs, differs across conditions when it is only a trivial difference in ERPs (e.g., in their amplitude or temporal variability) causing such difference. Importantly, illusory differences remained even after controlling for ERPs using the common method of ERP subtraction, as long as the single-trial ERPs showed variability in their timing, which is generally assumed to be the case in real-world data. In fact, real-world ERP variability is substantially more complex than in our approach, given that independent ERP components might show different levels of variation from trial to trial. It is, therefore, not yet evident how best to account for ERPs in single-trial analyses, although stratifying participants based on their cross-trial SDs might help identify low-jitter and high-jitter individuals. Aperiodic changes could then be tested separately in these subgroups, following ERP removal, to examine if aperiodic dynamics are similar.
