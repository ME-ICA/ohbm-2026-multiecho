Introduction

Structural and functional brain aging follow distinct trajectories. Structural aging typically follows well-characterized patterns of cortical thinning and reductions in grey matter and white matter volume1. In contrast, functional aging is more variable, with some brain networks showing early decline while others remain relatively stable or undergo compensatory reorganization2. Although structural alterations have been thought to contribute to modifications in these large-scale functional networks3, their precise relationship remains unclear. 

Existing studies on aging are limited by their reliance on chronological age as the primary index of aging. Chronological age does not fully capture individual variability in neurobiological decline4, particularly in later life, and may obscure meaningful structure-function relationships. Emerging approaches that estimate structural brain age from neuroanatomical features provide a biologically sensitive marker of aging-related change and may better account for variation in functional network integrity than chronological age alone5.

The present study investigated whether regional structural brain age derived from cortical thickness explained additional variance in functional connectivity (FC) beyond chronological age and whether different functional networks differed in their susceptibility to structural aging. By examining both resting-state and multiple task-based fMRI paradigms, this study aims to clarify how biologically driven structural aging shapes FC across different cognitive states.

Methods

Ridge regression models were trained on cortical thickness data from the Cambridge Centre for Ageing and Neuroscience (Cam-CAN) dataset (n = 653, mean age = 54.8 ± 18.6 years) to predict chronological age for each of the 200 parcellated cortical regions. For each ROI, a separate ridge regression model was fitted, and the optimal regularization parameter (λ) was selected using cross-validation. The trained models were then applied to cortical thickness data from Human Connectome Project in Aging (HCP-A) participants (n = 677, mean age = 60.1 ± 15.7 years). This yielded regional predicted brain ages for each participant.

FC matrices were generated for resting-state fMRI and three task fMRI paradigms, namely the inhibitory control CARIT (Go/NoGo) Task, the paired-associates memory Facename Task, and the motor and visual activation VisMotor Task, using the same parcellation scheme applied to the structural data.

For each functional connection (ROI_A–ROI_B), two linear models were fitted: one including chronological age as the sole predictor and a second including both chronological age and the mean structural brain age of the connected ROI pair. Model comparisons were performed using F-tests to evaluate whether regional structural brain age significantly improved prediction of FC beyond chronological age. False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction (q < .05) was applied across all edges within each fMRI condition to identify significant associations.

Results

In resting-state FC, greater structural brain age was significantly associated with weaker FC in regions within the default mode network (DMN) and somatomotor network (SMN), suggesting that advanced structural aging is associated with reduced within-network connectivity in networks typically vulnerable to aging. During the Facename task, negative brain age-FC associations emerged in regions within the visual network (VN), between the VN and dorsal attention network (DAN), and within the DAN, indicating diminished sensory-attentional integration with increasing structural age. For the CARIT task, structural brain age predicted weaker connectivity in  regions within the DAN but stronger connectivity between the DMN and VN, potentially reflecting compensatory engagement of higher-order systems to support perceptual processing under increased cognitive load. In the VisMotor task, mostly negative associations within the VN, within the DAN, and between the ventral attention network (VAN) and DAN suggest reduced efficiency in visual-attentional-motor coordination with more advanced structural aging.

Conclusion

These findings demonstrate that structural brain age captures biologically meaningful variance in FC beyond chronological age and that functional networks differ in their vulnerability or compensatory response to structural aging. Importantly, task context modulates these relationships, indicating that structural aging does not uniformly weaken connectivity but can also promote reorganization across networks to meet task demands. Together, this study provides a more nuanced understanding of how biological aging of brain cortical thickness shapes functional connectivity across different task states.
