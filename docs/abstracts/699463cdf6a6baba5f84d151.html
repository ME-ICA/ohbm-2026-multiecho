Introduction

In Parkinson’s disease (PD), the progressive loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc) projecting to dorsal striatum (DS) leads to the characteristic motor symptoms. However, degeneration also occurs in the ventral tegmental area (VTA) which mainly projects to ventral striatum (VS) and supports reward prediction error processing and reinforcement learning (RL). There is no clear anatomical separation between the two pathways but rather a gradual transition from core VTA-VS reward functions via associative and cognitive functions towards core motor function in the SNc-DS pathway, realized via integrative feedback loops between midbrain, striatum and cortex.1 This allows for an ample information transfer and integration between reward- and motor loops. Thus, both the modulatory effects of the major cell loss in the SNc-DS pathway on the VTA-VS circuit as well as the considerable degeneration of the VTA itself would lead to the prediction of major impairments in reward-based learning in PD. Yet, disease effects on learning are often only subtle and inconsistent across studies.2 

One reason for these inconsistent findings might be that most studies investigating the effects of PD on RL probe learning about the associations of different stimuli (e.g. different images, shapes, color) with reward outcomes, rather than focusing on the core symptoms in PD, i.e. action-outcome contingencies. This study compared learning about action - outcome and non-motor (color) - outcome associations in people with PD ON and OFF dopamine medication and age-matched healthy controls. We hypothesized that an impairment of the “motor” SN-DS pathway might lead to an inadvertent focus away from action-outcome associations and towards stimulus-outcome associations, incorrectly making choices based on the non-informative color-value. Furthermore, we expected that the relatively intact VTA “reward” loops would successfully regulate color – outcome learning.

Methods

Behavioral Assessment

We analyzed data from 24 people with PD and 23 healthy, sex- and age-matched controls. After two preceding experimental days with clinical and neuropsychological assessment (day 1) and ultra-high field (7 tesla) structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (not reported here), participants were invited for two further study sessions: Participants played a novel probabilistic RL task while being scanned with 7 tesla functional MRI (fMRI). Patients in the PD group participated in one session ON medication and the other OFF medication, with the order counterbalanced while HC had no medical intervention. The task had two conditions: one where reward probability was tied to action (push or pull), and another in which it was tied to stimulus color (blue or yellow) (Fig. 1). 

Behavioral Analysis

We used a Bayesian linear mixed model to assess how behavioral performance (accumulated reward) was affected by group (PD vs HC) and session (HC)/medication (PD). We developed a hierarchical Bayesian RL model with a weighting parameter to estimate the relative contribution of action- and color-based learning (exemplified here with pull-blue and push-yellow as available color-action combinations for this trial): 

EVi(pull_blue) = ω × EVi(pull)  + (1 - ω) × EVi(blue)

EVi(push_yellow) = ω × EVi(push) + (1 - ω) × EVi(yellow)

where w is the expected value (expected reward probability x reward magnitude) and where a weighting parameter  close to 1 indicates that the agent primarily considers the expected value of the associated action while "ignoring" the expected value of the color, whereas a  close to 0 suggests the opposite. Expected reward probabilities are updated via a classical reward prediction error, weighted with a learning rate parameter α. Choices are modelled with a softmax-function with an inverse-temperature parameter β. Modeling was performed with Stan in Python. For each model, we ran 8 independent Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) chains to generate 4,000 samples from the joint distribution of parameters, with 1,000 samples used as warm-up, resulting in 24,000 samples for each parameter. We used the Widely Applicable Information Criterion (WAIC) to evaluate the tradeoff between model predictably and complexity.

We used a Bayesian linear regression model (R, brms package) to explore whether the individual change in the weighting parameter ω by medication in the PD group was associated with clinical symptom scores (UPDRS-III OFF medication, disease duration, cognitive impairment (MoCA), apathy (LARS)).

Functional MRI Assessment and Analysis

As participants performed the task, their brain activity was assessed with 7 tesla fMRI. We acquired echo-planar imaging (EPI) with a slab covering the midbrain and striatum (1.7 x 1.7 mm in-plane resolution, slice thickness 2.0 mm, 0.2 mm slice gap, repetition time 1.65 s, echo-time 18 ms). EPI images were corrected for phase-encoding distortions using the top-up tool (FSL), slice-time corrected and realigned using SPM (v. 12.7771). Coregistration of EPI images to a T1-weighted image and subsequently to MNI space was performed using ANTS software. Finally, EPI images were smoothed with a Gaussian 3 mm full-width, half-maximum kernel. Preliminary analysis of the fMRI data was realized with a general linear model including regressors modeling the onset of responses (separate for left and right), onset of reward outcomes and no-reward outcomes as well as regressors of no-interest (inter-trial-interval, error trials, movement and physiological recordings), for each run, condition and session.

We extracted contrast values from pre-defined regions of interest in the ventral tegmental area (VTA), substantia nigra (SN), and striatum (split into ventral (VS) and dorsal striatum (DS)).

Results

Behavior

In terms of performance (accumulated earnings), there was weak evidence (credible interval slightly overlapping with 0) for PD patients performing worse, especially OFF medication (β = -0.80,95%  CI: [-1.62,0.03 ], HC M=4522 (SD 708), PD OFF M=4417 (553), PD ON M=4293 (621)), while session/medication did not have an effect. 

In accordance with our main hypothesis, we found very strong evidence for PD patients OFF medication exhibiting impairments in action-value learning, incorrectly making choices based on the non-informative color-value, compared to healthy controls (Bayes factor (BF) = 42.88, Fig. 2A). There was moderate evidence for an alleviation of this impairment by L-DOPA treatment (BF = 6.85, Fig. 2A) in PD while there was no repetition effect in the HC group (BF = 0.67, Fig. 2A). 

Model comparison revealed that the best performing model included different weighting parameters for the two conditions (ωAV and ωCV) and separate learning rates for positive and negative prediction errors for both conditions (α+AV, α-AV, α+CV, α-CV). In the best performing model, all parameters vary across session (HC)/medication (PD) with the exception of ωAV and ωCV which varied across medication in PD, but not across session in HC. Parameter recovery showed adequate performance.

We explored learning rates and how were affected by disease and medication. Learning rates for positive outcomes (α+) were higher than for negative outcomes (α-) (BF > 100 in both PD and HC, Fig. 2B). In people with PD, both α+ and α- were increased by medication (α+: moderate evidence, BF = 7.2; α-: strong evidence, BF = 18.15) while there was no repetition effect in HC (Fig. 2B). 

Subsequently, we tested whether the degree of medication-induced change in the individually fitted weighting parameters in the PD group was associated with any of the clinical symptom scores (UPDRS-III OFF medication, disease duration, cognitive impairment (MoCA), apathy (LARS)). There was no evidence for any such association with the credible intervals of all regression coefficients spanning across 0. 

Functional MRI

Preliminary analysis of the fMRI data revealed moderate evidence of reduced neural responses in the substantia nigra to reward outcomes in people with PD OFF medication compared to healthy controls (BF = 6.6) and we will report further imaging related results at the conference.

Conclusion

This study provides converging behavioral, computational, and preliminary neuroimaging evidence that Parkinson’s disease (PD) alters reinforcement learning in a manner that is closely tied to its core motor pathology. While previous research has often reported inconsistent effects of PD on reward-based learning, our findings suggest that these inconsistencies may stem from a predominant focus on stimulus-outcome associations rather than action-outcome contingencies, which are more directly linked to the motor circuits affected by PD. By explicitly contrasting these two forms of learning, we demonstrate that patients OFF dopaminergic medication exhibit a shift away from action-value learning toward reliance on non-informative stimulus features. This maladaptive weighting of color-value over action-value was partially normalized by L-DOPA treatment, indicating that dopamine plays a critical role in restoring appropriate action-outcome learning. However, these medication-induced changes in the weighting parameter were not associated with clinical severity, disease duration, cognitive impairment, or apathy, implying that reinforcement learning deficits may represent a distinct cognitive dimension in PD rather than a direct correlate of motor or global cognitive symptoms.

Beyond weighting strategies, our modeling revealed that learning rates for positive outcomes were consistently higher than for negative outcomes across all groups, aligning with prior evidence of asymmetric learning from gains versus losses.3 Importantly, dopaminergic medication increased both positive and negative learning rates in PD patients, suggesting that dopamine enhances sensitivity to feedback regardless of valence. 

Preliminary fMRI analyses further support a mechanistic link between dopaminergic degeneration and altered learning strategies. We observed reduced reward-related responses in the substantia nigra in PD patients OFF medication compared to healthy controls, consistent with the hypothesis that impaired dopaminergic signaling in midbrain-striatal circuits underlies the observed behavioral shift. Future analyses will examine whether reward prediction error signals in motor and reward pathways are differently affected by disease, medication and learning condition, providing a more granular understanding of how motor and reward loops interact in PD.

Taken together, these findings highlight the importance of considering action-based contingencies when assessing cognitive function in PD. Reinforcement learning deficits in PD may not be global but rather context-dependent, emerging most clearly when learning requires integration of motor actions and reward outcomes. Moreover, our results underscore the need for computational approaches that disentangle different sources of value representation, as these can reveal subtle but functionally significant alterations in decision-making strategies. Ultimately, understanding how dopamine shapes the balance between motor and reward learning may inform targeted interventions aimed at preserving adaptive decision-making in PD.
