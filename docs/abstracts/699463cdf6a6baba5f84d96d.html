Introduction

Ketamine is a rapid-acting antidepressant that inhibits the N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor and induces transient dissociative effects, including disembodiment and derealisation. Because these experiences resemble core symptoms observed in schizophrenia, ketamine has been used as a pharmacological model of psychosis. However, their relevance for ketamine’s antidepressant properties remains unclear. Notably, the “oceanic boundlessness”, subscale of the 5-dimension altered consciousness score (5-ASC), which captures dissolution of self-environment boundaries, has been associated with its antidepressant response 1. 

Nevertheless, the neurobiological mechanisms underlying ketamine-induced alterations in bodily and self-perception remain largely unknown. As functional PET (fPET) enables the direct measurement and quantification of cerebral glucose metabolism, we aimed to quantify changes in glucose metabolism associated with subjective alterations in perception during ketamine administration. 

Methods

Twenty-three healthy volunteers (mean age 25.48 ± 4.08 years, nine female) completed two 75-minute [18F]-FDG simultaneous PET/MR scans with a bolus + constant infusion for radioligand administration in a randomized, double-blind, cross-over design receiving either 0.25mg x kg-1 (S)-ketamine or 0.9% saline placebo intravenously. After 20-minutes of fPET baseline acquisition, the study drug was administered over 45 minutes, in a block design, which consisted of three 5-minute infusion blocks each followed by a 10-minute baseline block, during which no medication was infused. Vital parameters, including blood pressure, heart rate and blood oxygenation were obtained before study medication administration and after each infusion block (minute 25, minute 40, minute 55). Subjective drug effects were captured using a visual analogue scale (VAS), on which participants rated perceived alterations every two minutes from the onset of drug administration until the end of fPET acquisition. fPET data were reconstructed at a temporal resolution of 30s and preprocessed following previously established protocols 2, including correction for head movement, normalization to MNI standard space and 8 mm Gaussian smoothing. Changes in cerebral glucose metabolism were assessed with a general linear model as implemented in the fPET toolbox (https://github.com/NeuroimagingLabsMUV/fPET-toolbox) with the group averaged VAS score as used as an additional regressor. For multiple-comparison correction, a family-wise error correction was applied with a threshold of p ⩽0.025 at the cluster level, following p < 0.001 uncorrected voxel level. Average change of the mean arterial pressure (MAP) and the heart rate relative to baseline were calculated for each condition (placebo vs. ketamine) and compared in a paired t-test. 

Results

Ketamine produced a rapid increase in VAS ratings during the first application block and closely mirrored the ketamine infusion protocol block design (see Fig. 1a). The averaged VAS-based regressor identified significant clusters with increased glucose metabolism in the cerebellar vermis, the visual cortex (V1) and the right temporal cortex (pFWE < 0.025) compared to placebo. The significant clusters are illustrated in Figure 1b. Mean arterial pressure (t(22) = 5.57, p = <0.001) and heart rate (t(21) = 3.00, p = 0.007) significantly increased in the ketamine condition compared to the placebo condition.

Conclusion

Ketamine induced rapid changes in perception, which were associated with an increased glucose metabolism in the right temporal cortex, vermis and the visual cortex. Beyond its perceptual alteration, ketamine also increased the heart rate and the blood pressure, which represent key components of interoceptive signalling. Interoception is defined as the integration and interpretation of internal bodily sensation and has recently been conceptualized within predictive coding frameworks, in which mismatches between perceived and predicted bodily states elicit prediction errors. In this context, precision refers to the reliability of these prediction errors3, whereat altered precision has been linked to NMDA hypofunction and psychosis among several psychiatric disorders 4. The vermis, temporal cortex and visual cortex are considered as downstream nodes within interoceptive and multisensory networks 5, such that increased glucose metabolism in these regions might reflect enhanced demands associated with resolving prediction errors. Disruption of interoceptive inference could contribute to explaining ketamine-induced disembodiment. In summary, our results may provide new insight into neurobiological mechanisms underlying ketamine-induced altered bodily- and self-perception.
