Introduction

Previous studies revealed that the cerebral cortex folding scales universally with cortical surface area and thickness across multiple primate species1,2. Specifically, the formula T 0.5 At=kAe1.25 applies, where T is the average cortical thickness, At is the true cortex surface area, Ae is the exposed cortex surface area (ignoring the folded cortex part), and k is a dataset independent linear slope. However, as another highly folded yet important brain structure, whether the cerebellum also exhibits such a similar scaling rule remains unknown.

Compared to cerebrum, the cerebellum has thinner, tighter but deeper sulci, making the cerebellum less studies due to limitations in image resolution and image processing technique. In this abstract, leveraging the high-quality lifespan human brain imaging datasets and the novel cerebellum imaging processing pipeline3, for the first time, we validated that the lifespan cerebellum cortex folding also scales with the cerebellar average cortex thickness and surface areas.

Methods

We adopted 3,079 high-quality human brain (from neonates to 90-years-old) MR images, which are from the Baby Connectome project (638 scans), HCP-Development project (645 scans), HCP project (1094 scans), and HCP-Aging project (702 scans). We first segmented the cerebellum tissue into white matter, grey matter, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)4. And then, we reconstructed the topologically correct and geometrically accurate cerebellar white (boundary between white matter and grey matter) and pial (boundary between grey matter and CSF) surfaces, which were used to compute the cerebellar cortical thickness (T) and true cerebellar surface area (At). After that, we closed the thin sulci of the cerebellar cortex, reconstructed the cerebellar cortical hull surface, and computed the exposed cerebellar surface area (Ae). Fig. 1 illustrates the detailed processing steps. Please note that no data harmonization was done at this stage of data analysis so that all computed features were directly from the individual physical space to ensure their accuracy.

With the computed cerebellar cortical thickness, true surface area and exposed surface area, we drew the scatter plot charted by Ae1.25 and T0.5At . And then, we used ANCOVA to statistically analyze their relationship for the life span cerebellum development.

Results

Fig. 2 (a-c) charts the lifespan developmental trajectories of cerebellum cortical thickness, true surface area and exposed surface area, respectively. The cerebellum cortical thickness true surface area/exposed surface area respectively has a large decrease/increase/increase since birth and then stays stable starting 2000 days in age. The distinct spatial resolution of HCP (0.7mm isotropic) dataset leads to the observed group discrepancies of the thickness and true surface area compared to other (BCP, HCPD, HCPA) datasets (0.8mm isotropic). Of note, exposed surface area is less influenced because unfolded exposed regions are more stable.

Despites the effects of imaging resolution on the estimated thickness and surface areas, Fig. 2 (d) reports the scatter plot charted by Ae1.25 and T0.5At, exhibiting a strong linear relationship with R2 equals to 0.93 and indicating that the cerebellum cortex folding scales linearly with the thickness and surfaces area1.

Conclusion

In this abstract, we have revealed that the cerebellum cortex also scales with the cerebellar surface area and cerebellar cortical thickness, as the cerebral cortex does, for lifespan datasets. This indicates that both the cerebellar and cerebral cortex folding may follow a uniform physical model as the crumpled paper balls 1. In the future, we plan further studies to investigate the potential biological reasons for this uniform scaling rule for both the cerebellum and cerebrum.
