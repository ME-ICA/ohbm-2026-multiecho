Introduction

Resting-state functional connectivity (RSFC) powerfully predicts individual behavioral differences.1 However, most studies rely on group atlas, which obscure substantial inter-individual variability in functional topography,2 and may therefore constrain predictive performance. Furthermore, while dynamic FC (dFC) captures rapid neural fluctuations and provides comprehensive information,3  its predictive advantage—particularly in combination with individualized brain parcellations—remains underexplored. Here, we investigate whether individualized brain parcellations consistently improve behavioral prediction and identify which functional connectivity metric best captures brain-behavior relationships.

Methods

We analyzed resting-state functional MRI (R-fMRI) data from 339 unrelated healthy adults in the Human Connectome Project (HCP S900 release, https://www.humanconnectome.org/), consisting of four runs across two sessions with alternating phase encodings (LR/RL). The data underwent standard HCP minimal preprocessing, ICA-FIX denoising and Multimodal Surface Matching alignment. Additional preprocessing included global signal regression and frame censoring.4 Notably, we applied cubic spline interpolation for the frame censoring to ensure temporal continuity for dFC analysis. Quality control based on head motion resulted in a final sample of 266 subjects (mean age = 28.29 ± 3.70 years; 138 females). 84 behavioral measures spanning six domains from the HCP dataset were used: sensory-motor/alertness, cognition, emotion, personality, psychiatric/life function, and in-scanner task performance. Specifically, we firstly estimated individualized brain parcellations using a multi-session hierarchical Bayesian model4 initialized with a 400-region group atlas. Then, we extracted FC features (strength, stability, and variability) using the Day 1 LR run, based on both group atlas and individualized parcellations, yielding eight feature sets. Static FC (sFC) was computed as the Pearson’s correlation between region-of-interest (ROI) time series. dFC was estimated using a sliding window approach5 (window width = 83 TRs,  approximately 60 s, step size = 1 TR). Three dynamic metrics were calculated5: mean strength (dFC-Str), indicating the average connectivity; stability (dFC-Sta), measuring the smoothness of frame-to-frame transitions; and variability (dFC-Var), capturing the spectral amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (<0.08 Hz). We utilized kernel ridge regression (KRR) with nested 10-fold cross-validation to predict each of the 84 behavioral phenotypes, regressing out age, sex, and framewise displacement based solely on training folds. The regularization parameter searching range for KRR was [0, 0.00001, 0.0001, 0.001, 0.004, 0.007, ..., 1, 1.5, 2, ..., 4, 5, 10, 15, 20]. Prediction accuracy was determined by the correlation between predicted and observed scores, averaged over 100 repetitions of 10-fold cross-validation. We performed a permutation test with 1000 iterations to evaluate statistical significance. To compare performance, we evaluated overall accuracy using a hierarchical permutation test while employing Steiger’s z-tests for phenotype-specific comparisons and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests across feature sets, with false discovery rate (FDR) correction applied to the latter two analyses.

Results

Hierarchical permutation testing revealed that individualized parcellation significantly improved overall behavioral prediction accuracy compared to group-atlas (mean Δr = 0.007, p = 0.013, 10000 permutations). Stratification by domain demonstrated specific significance within the psychiatry (mean Δr = 0.010, p = 0.037, 10000 permutations) and alertness-sensory-motor domains (mean Δr = 0.015, p = 0.041, 10000 permutations). Notably, individualized parcellation models outperformed group-atlas models in 73.2% of psychiatry and 70.4% of alertness-sensory-motor cases. Taking Aggressive Behavior from psychiatry domain as a representative example, prediction analysis showed that while dFC strength calculated with both parcellations significantly predicted scores, the individualized model (mean accuracy = 0.246, p = 0.002) was significantly superior to the group-atlas model (mean accuracy = 0.199, p = 0.008) as confirmed by Steiger’s z-test (z = 29.373, p < 0.001, FDR-corrected). Feature weight analysis indicated that predictive weights for the individualized model were primarily distributed in the Control, Limbic (orbitofrontal), and Salience/Ventral Attention networks, whereas group-atlas model weights were predominantly distributed across the Limbic (temporal pole), Visual, and Temporo-Parietal networks. In terms of feature comparison, individualized dFC strength emerged as the optimal metric globally, significantly surpassing static FC using individualized (mean Δr = 0.011, p = 0.003, FDR-corrected) and group-atlas (mean Δr = 0.011, p = 0.040, FDR-corrected). This superiority was mirrored in the psychiatry domain (individualized parcellation: mean Δr = 0.024, p = 0.004, FDR-corrected; group-atlas: mean Δr = 0.024, p = 0.020, FDR-corrected).

Conclusion

We demonstrate that individualized brain parcellations substantially improve behavioral prediction accuracy relative to group-level models, with the most pronounced predictive benefits observed in the psychiatry and sensory-motor domains. Furthermore, we identify individualized dynamic functional connectivity strength as the most effective predictive metric. These findings highlight the necessity of capturing individual variability in brain functional organization to discover precise neural biomarkers for individual behaviors.
