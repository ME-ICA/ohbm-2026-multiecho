Introduction

The healthcare sector contributes an estimated 4–6% of global greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, with medical imaging alone responsible for up to 1%1. MRI is particularly energy-intensive: over a year of routine operation, a single scanner can consume as much electricity as 25–30 households. Acquiring advanced sequences, such as high b-value diffusion MRI (dMRI), further requires strong gradient hardware rarely available in clinical settings and long, energy-demanding scans. At the same time, the digital infrastructure needed to store and process medical data adds further burden, as data centres account for 1–4% of global GHG emissions1. While computing hardware is becoming more efficient, these gains risk inducing a rebound effect in which cheaper computation drives increased use.

AI-based applications in MRI offer substantial improvements in image quality, reconstruction speed, and diagnostic accuracy. However, developing and deploying large neural networks is energy-intensive, producing significant emissions due to extensive data storage and computation for training and inference1. Reducing this environmental footprint is crucial for ensuring sustainable and equitable access to advanced healthcare technologies, and requires shifting away from ever-larger models toward efficient, lightweight architectures.

We introduce GAIA: Green Artificial Intelligence for Accelerated medical imaging, a paradigm aimed at developing smaller and more efficient AI solutions. Here, we exploit knowledge distillation2,3 to compress large neural networks (“teachers”) into smaller models (“students”) that retain performance while reducing computational and energy costs. As a proof of concept, we apply this approach to dMRI image synthesis, predicting powder-averaged signals at high b-values from lower b-values. Building on an established U-Net architecture4, we show that lightweight, sustainable AI models can mitigate environmental impact and broaden clinical access to advanced dMRI.

Methods

Image synthesis: MRI scanners equipped with ultra-strong gradients provide enhanced sensitivity to tissue microstructure in vivo and allow for the acquisition of high b-value dMRI signals, which remain difficult to obtain on standard clinical scanners. Image synthesis offers a practical alternative to extend these benefits by predicting these high–b-value signals from data acquired on standard scanners. We build on an established U-Net convolutional neural network (CNN) architecture4 that learns the non-linear relationship between powder-average diffusion signal and b-value. It combines a down-sampling path that captures global contextual information with an up-sampling path that preserves spatial detail.

Network architectures: We considered three 3D U-Net networks, with four inputs (four b-shells < 3000 s/mm2) and two outputs (b=4000 and 6000 s/mm2) (Fig. 1):

  • Teacher: the baseline architecture proposed in 4 (depth=5; 4,118,219 parameters in total);
  • Light Model: a reduced-depth network, trained similarly to the teacher (depth=2; 52,848 parameters in total);
  • Student: identical architecture as the light model, trained using knowledge distillation from the teacher (depth=2; 52,848 parameters in total).

Loss functions: Both the teacher and light model were trained using tailored losses LT and LLM, computed between the true and predicted high b-value signals y and ŷ. Each loss is defined as a weighted sum of an L1 norm and the structural similarity index measure (SSIM), supplemented by tissue-specific L1 components computed separately in white matter (WM) and gray matter (GM). This formulation encourages accurate global reconstruction while preserving local structure and accounting for tissue-dependent differences in diffusion signal behavior.

LT(y, ŷ) = LLM(y, ŷ) = λ1 * 1/N * Σn=1N |yn - ŷn| + λ2 * (1 - SSIM(y, ŷ)) + λ3 * 1/NWM * Σn=1NWM |yn - ŷn| + λ4 * 1/NGM * Σn=1NGM |yn - ŷn|

With N the total number of voxels, and NWM and NGM the number of voxels in WM and GM respectively.

Knowledge from the teacher was transferred to the student through a knowledge distillation loss LKD, computed between the last latent representation z and ẑ of the teacher and student networks:

LKD(z, ẑ) = λKD,1 * 1/N * Σn=1N |zn - ẑn| + λKD,2 * (1 - SSIM(z, ẑ)) + λKD,3 * 1/NWM * Σn=1NWM |zn - ẑn| + λ4 * 1/NGM * Σn=1NGM |zn - ẑn|

The total loss used for training the student network is: LS = (1 - λKD) * LLM(y, ŷ) + λKD * LKD(z, ẑ).

We used λ1 = λ2 = λ3 = λ4 = 1, λKD = 0.25, λKD,1 = 1, λKD,2 = 0, λKD,3 = 0.75 and λKD,4 = 1.25.

Dataset: We used the Welsh Advanced Neuroimaging Database (WAND)5 (161 subjects), acquired using a PGSE sequence with b-values = [200, 500, 1200, 2400, 4000, 6000] s/mm2 and respectively [20, 20, 30, 61, 61, 61] uniformly distributed diffusion encoding directions, δ/Δ=7/24 ms, and TE/TR=59/3000 ms. Data were pre-processed by first applying the Marchenko-Pastur PCA denoising approach with MRtrix, followed by corrections for gradient non-linearities using custom scripts, susceptibility-induced and eddy current-related distortions using FSL (topup and eddy methods), and Gibbs ringing artefacts. A brain mask as well as white and grey matter segmentations were derived with MRtrix. Finally, for each b-value, powder-average signals were obtained by computing the mean over all diffusion directions and normalized by the mean non-diffusion-weighted volumes (b=0).

Training: Data were split into training, testing and validation subsets (80%-10%-10%). Each model was trained for 80 epochs using the Adam optimizer (learning rate=10-4). Training took approximately 30 minutes on a GPU (NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090) for each model.

Application to MS: We applied the trained models on one participant with multiple sclerosis (MS) scanned using the same protocol. Data were processed identically. Three MS lesions were manually segmented by a neurologist.

Results

The distilled student network achieved a 78% reduction in model size compared to the teacher, while maintaining comparable prediction accuracy and precision. Qualitatively, synthesized high b-value signals closely resembled the acquired data, showing high structural fidelity and visual similarity (Fig. 2A). Quantitatively, the student outperformed the light model, yielding lower mean squared error (MSE; Fig. 2B) and mean absolute error (MAE; Fig. 2C) between the high b-value signal predictions and the acquired “ground truth” data on the independent test set (17 subjects), with performance approaching that of the teacher.

When applied on MS lesions, the student also produced more accurate reconstructions than the light model (Fig. 2D). Because no MS lesions were included during training, relative errors were slightly higher within lesions than in healthy tissue.

In terms of environmental efficiency, reducing model complexity led to a 35% decrease in total energy consumption and estimated equivalent CO₂ emissions, estimated based on UK National Grid data (Fig. 3E). These savings scale linearly with number of deployments, with the student producing ~20% less CO2 per inference than the teacher (Fig. 3F), corresponding to a reduction of approximately 1.6kg CO2 over one million deployments. This exemplar application demonstrates the sustainability benefits of model distillation in MRI applications.

Overall, our findings indicate that an effective balance between predictive performance and environmental impact can be achieved. Among the tested architectures, the student model offers an optimal trade-off, combining near-teacher accuracy with markedly improved energy and carbon efficiency (Fig. 3G).

Conclusion

A compact U-Net CNN obtained through knowledge distillation accurately predicted high b-value diffusion MRI signals with performance comparable to a larger model. Predicting high b-value signals enables access to microstructural information that cannot typically be acquired on clinical scanners, expanding the reach of advanced diffusion imaging. The smaller network required substantially less computation, reducing both energy consumption and CO₂ emissions. Beyond its technical performance, this approach offers practical advantages: lightweight models are easier to deploy on standard hospital hardware, mobile devices, and in low-resource environments. These findings demonstrate that efficient AI architectures can deliver high-quality results while advancing environmental sustainability in medical imaging.

We advocate for a shift in AI development within medical imaging, moving from prioritizing performance metrics alone toward evaluating efficiency and sustainability. We suggest that, rather than using huge amounts of energy for marginal performance improvements, which are subject to diminishing returns, we should refocus on developing small, efficient, lightweight models that are good enough for specific applications. We present GAIA, a new paradigm exploiting knowledge distillation to achieve this balance, enabling accurate, energy-efficient, and environmentally responsible deep learning solutions. Our results on high b-value prediction exemplify how greener AI can enhance both accessibility and sustainability in clinical imaging.
