Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic autoimmune neurodegenerative disorder that is more prevalent in women and produces heterogeneous motor and cognitive impairment.^1 Although focal white-matter lesions are central to diagnosis, lesion burden often shows a weak correspondence with clinical phenotype, highlighting the need for complementary biomarkers of disease physiology. Ventricular enlargement is common in MS and implicates cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pathways that support metabolic clearance, nutrient transport, and immune surveillance. In conventional fMRI pipelines, CSF is typically treated as nuisance and regressed as a single global signal, potentially obscuring biologically meaningful regional CSF dynamics. Here, we characterize CSF regionalization and quantify CSF–grey matter coupling in MS compared with healthy controls (HC) using participant-specific CSF atlases that account for ventricular expansion.

Methods

Resting-state fMRI was acquired in a Tim Trio 3.0T system (TE=35 ms, TR=2700 ms, voxel size=3.6×3.6×3.0 mm³, 46 slices, 120 volumes). The MS cohort included 41 participants. For group comparisons, a matched subset was analyzed (HC=34, MS=40). Data were preprocessed with fMRIPrep. Denoising regressed the 6 rigid-body motion parameters. Participants were excluded if framewise displacement exceeded 0.5 mm in >25% of volumes; this resulted in the exclusion of 1 HC and 1 MS participant due to excessive motion.

CSF segmentation was individualized by intersecting FreeSurfer aseg-derived CSF/ventricular labels in MNI space with each participant’s CSF tissue segmentation from fMRIPrep^2 (also in MNI space), yielding a personalized CSF atlas (left/right lateral ventricles, 3rd ventricle, 4th ventricle, and outer CSF). Grey matter time series were extracted using the Schaefer parcellation^3 (200 parcels) combined with Tian^4 subcortical regions (scale 2). Time series were band-pass filtered (0.008–0.08 Hz). Functional connectivity and CSF–grey matter coupling were quantified using Pearson correlation and summarized at the level of 7 Yeo resting state networks (RSN)^5 plus subcortex. CSF spectral content was assessed on raw CSF signals using power spectral density (PSD) with Welch’s method, and a band power computed in 0.01–0.10 Hz. Group comparisons used two-sample tests with FDR correction across networks where applicable.

Results

CSF signals were regionalized across compartments: PSD profiles differed by CSF region, with the 3rd and 4th ventricles showing prominent spectral content within 0.01–0.10 Hz, motivating a focus on these regions for coupling analyses (Figure 1). In the band of interest, CSF spectral power showed a robust regional (compartment) effect within each group, and this spatial pattern was mirrored in HC and MS (rmANOVA across CSF ROIs: HC p=3.11×10⁻⁷; MS p=9.24×10⁻¹²). Despite this clear compartment-specific organization, between-group differences were not detected. Notably, this preservation of CSF frequency content across compartments occurred despite ventricular enlargement in MS, suggesting that the compartment-specific frequency structure is a common property of CSF spaces rather than a disease-specific alteration, potentially reflecting differences in compartment size or geometry and associated physiological contributions (e.g., vasomotor components).

Grey-matter network organization was comparable between groups. Within-network functional connectivity across the 7 Yeo RSNs and the subcortical partition did not differ between HC and MS (subcortex: p=0.253, q=0.674; visual: p=0.536, q=0.847; somatomotor: p=0.387, q=0.773; dorsal attention: p=0.995, q=0.995; salience/ventral attention: p=0.166, q=0.674; limbic: p=0.635, q=0.847; control: p=0.872, q=0.995; default: p=0.216, q=0.674; Figure 2A).

In contrast, analyses of CSF-grey matter coupling revealed network-specific effects for the 4th ventricle (Figure 2B). Parallel analyses for the 3rd ventricle did not show differences between HC and MS across any RSN (no nominal or FDR-significant effects). Compared with HC, MS patients exhibited higher coupling with the visual (p=0.0296, q=0.118), somatomotor (p=0.00866, q=0.0693), and control networks (p=0.049, q=0.12). Trends were also observed for the dorsal attention network (p=0.0599, q=0.12), whereas no differences were observed for subcortical, salience/ventral attention, limbic, or default networks (all p≥0.302). Although these effects did not survive FDR correction across networks, they were not spatially homogeneous and preferentially involved networks commonly affected in MS, including visual and somatomotor systems.

Finally, to evaluate clinical relevance, we assessed associations between 4th ventricle-RSN coupling and disability within the MS group using the expanded disability status scales. Nominal positive correlations were observed for somatomotor, visual, and control networks; however, these associations were no longer significant after adjusting for age and sex.

Conclusion

Individualized CSF atlases reveal robust regionalization of CSF fMRI signals, with the 3rd and 4th ventricles contributing substantial power in the canonical resting-state frequency range. While overall CSF band power and grey-matter within-network FC were not detectably altered in MS, ventricular-grey matter coupling showed compartment- and network-specific trends, particularly for the 4th ventricle. These findings support treating CSF as a structured physiological signal rather than a single nuisance regressor and motivate larger, clinically anchored studies to test whether ventricle-specific coupling relates to disability and symptom heterogeneity in MS.
