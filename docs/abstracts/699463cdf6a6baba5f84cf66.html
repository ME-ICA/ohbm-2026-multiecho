Introduction

Achieving reliable and individually tailored modulation of deep brain structures remains a major challenge for non-invasive neuromodulation. Transcranial ultrasound stimulation (TUS) has emerged as a promising approach, as it can reach subcortical structures with millimetre precision. Yet clear demonstrations of consistent and functionally specific effects in humans are still limited1. Here, we test whether TUS can modulate large-scale dynamics.
The lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN), a thalamic hub for visual processing, provides a well-characterised system for evaluating targeted ultrasound modulation2. Each LGN carries signals from the opposite visual hemifield, allowing distinct flicker frequencies presented to the two hemifields to serve as functional tags for each relay. Stimulating one LGN consequently delineates two pathways, one through the stimulated LGN and one through the unstimulated, each indexed by a different flicker frequency. Modulating a thalamic relay such as the LGN is expected to perturb the coordination of distributed cortical dynamics.

In this study, we aim to detect TUS-induced shifts in large-scale network organisation, exploiting the principled and established metrics derived from the Kuramoto order parameter (synchrony) and metastability. We investigated whether low-intensity TUS can produce robust,  site-specific modulation of a deep brain nucleus during sustained visual engagement2,3. We hypothesised that TUS would modulate neural dynamics selectively for the frequency tagged with the stimulated LGN, whereas the contralateral frequency would show no systematic change. We set out to test our hypotheses in the context of steady-state visual evoked potentials (SSVEP), as previously pioneered with TUS by Ash et al.2.

Methods

Twelve participants completed a single EEG–TUS session designed to test site-specific modulation of the LGN. We individualised the LGN targeting based on the structural MRI acquired during an intake session. Participants then completed a single EEG–TUS session comprising four blocks of steady-state visual stimulation3. Blocks 1 (pre-TUS) and 4 (post-TUS) involved visual flicker without the application of TUS, whereas Blocks 2 and 3 applied low-intensity TUS to the LGN during flicker. Flicker frequencies (6 Hz and 7.5 Hz) and the hemisphere selected for stimulation were counterbalanced across participants. A nearby white matter control site was included to test for the spatial specificity of TUS effects within the same session.

TUS was delivered using a 4-element piezoelectric focused ultrasound transducer (CTX-500; fundamental frequency: 500 kHz; aperture diameter: 64 mm), with a pulse duration of 22 ms repeated every 208.2 ms, comprising 63 pulses per 13-second train. Twelve trains were administered per block with a 20-second inter-train interval. EEG was recorded continuously throughout all blocks.

For analysing the neural synchrony, all EEG channels were first segmented into 5-second epochs and decomposed using FastICA. For each participant and SSVEP frequency (6 Hz or 7.5 Hz), independent components exhibiting elevated oscillatory activity within the corresponding SSVEP band (z ≥ 1 in 5–7 Hz or 6.5–8.5 Hz) were retained. Next, components were weighted by their relative band-limited power and used to compute the Kuramoto order parameter from instantaneous phase estimates. Synchrony was defined as the mean Kuramoto magnitude, and metastability as its temporal variability per component4. We used a linear mixed-effects model to test for the effects of Stimulation (LGN, white matter) and Target Frequency (modulated, non-modulated) conditions and their interaction on synchrony and metastability.

Results

Analysis of the EEG-derived network measures showed TUS induced frequency-specific modulation of both synchrony and metastability as hypothesised. We observed a significant interaction for the modulated frequency for both synchrony (F = 9.82, p = 0.00175) and metastability (F = 10.14, p = 0.00148) (Fig. 1). Post-hoc analyses indicated that the modulated frequency exhibited increased synchrony (estimate ≈ +0.0169, p < 0.001) and increased metastability (estimate ≈ +0.0074, p = 0.0122), whereas the non-modulated frequency showed no systematic change. We did not observe a main effect of Frequency, suggesting that the observed differences were driven specifically by the frequency associated with LGN stimulation rather than by intrinsic differences between the two flicker frequencies. Effects were consistent across the counterbalanced flicker frequencies and stimulation hemispheres. Effect sizes were large, with paired Cohen’s dzvalues of 0.92 for synchrony and 0.81 for metastability, supporting robust within-participant modulation by ultrasound. Lastly, to test whether effects were only present in metrics of large-scale network dynamics, or also in oscillatory power measures, we evaluated peak SSVEP power with Rhythmic Entrainment Source Separation (RESS)5 and found no modulation of SSVEP power by ultrasound. Together, these results highlight the specificity of TUS effects on thalamo-cortical network dynamics. 

Conclusion

Our results demonstrate that stimulation of the human LGN with low-intensity ultrasound can induce robust site-specific shifts in large-scale synchrony and metastability, reflecting modulation of a deep thalamic circuit. These findings point to the potential of ultrasound stimulation for modulating thalamo-cortical dynamics in humans.
