Introduction

Investigating the neural mechanisms of speech perception and production with fMRI faces major challenges, particularly scanner noise and head motion artifacts. While sparse sampling1 can mitigate the impact of scanner noise on auditory processing, it requires precise timing that is difficult to achieve for experiments involving sentence-length stimuli. Moreover, sparse sampling cannot capture continuous brain dynamics across regions during naturalistic tasks involving the successive perception of incoming and self-generated speech. Previous studies show that the auditory cortex is involved in processing both incoming2 and self-generated speech,3 which leads to mixed hemodynamic responses in the auditory cortex. To characterize the complete hemodynamic response profiles driven by external and self-generated speech, we scanned the subjects continuously during naturalistic tasks. We used independent component analysis (ICA)4 to identify independent brain processes associated with speech perception, planning, production, and self-monitoring, aiming to capture the temporal dynamics of each process. Furthermore, the precise timings of the rise, peak, and fall of each process’s hemodynamic response may inform optimal designs for future fMRI studies using sparse sampling.

Methods

Participants

This study analyzed fMRI data from 31 bilinguals (22 females, 9 males; mean age = 23.3 ± 4 years) who were recruited by the Brain and Language Project at the University of Macau.5 All subjects were native Chinese (Mandarin) speakers who acquired English as a second language between the ages of 4 and 13 (mean age of acquisition = 7.1 ± 2.1 years). English proficiency was assessed based on their most recent standardized test results (an IELTS score of 6.5 or above, or an equivalent qualification). All subjects gave written informed consent in accordance with the protocols approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the University of Macau. All subjects had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and reported no history of neurological disorders.

Experimental design

In each fMRI session, subjects participated in twelve scans of six sentence-level language perception and production tasks in English.5 Each 256-s scan consisted of sixteen 16-s trials of the same task. Within each trial of the listening task, subjects passively listened to a spoken sentence for 5 s, followed by 11 s of rest. Within each trial of the listening-reciting task, subjects passively listened to and memorized a spoken sentence for 5 s, followed by 5 s of reciting and 6 s of rest. The listening task served as a control condition for contrasting with the listening-reciting task, where successive hemodynamic responses in the auditory cortex were mixed.

Experimental setup and image acquisition 

Subjects were scanned in a Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma 3T MRI scanner at the Centre for Cognitive and Brain Sciences. The subject wore earplugs and heard external and self-generated speech through a noise-canceling system equipped with MR-compatible headphones and a microphone (OptoActive II, OptoAcoustics Ltd.). The inner wall of the scanner bore was lined with acoustic foam to further reduce gradient noise. The subject wore a custom-molded thermoplastic mask to prevent head motion during overt speech production. Together, these measures enabled continuous fMRI sampling during sentence-level speech perception and overt speech production with real-time feedback. Functional images were acquired using a Siemens 32-channel head coil with an echo planar imaging (EPI) sequence (55 axial images, matrix size = 64x64, voxel size = 3x3x3 mm, TE = 30 ms, TR = 1 s, 256 TRs per image).

Behavioral and functional data analysis

The subject’s voice was recorded continuously during functional scans and analyzed offline using Audacity software. For each trial in the listening-reciting task, we manually identified the timings of onset and offset of speech output during the reciting phase, and then averaged the results within and between subjects.

Following motion correction and registration, the 4D dataset of each functional scan of each subject was subjected to spatial ICA, implemented in FSL Melodic v3.15, to obtain spatially independent components (ICs). The resulting IC activation maps were displayed on the cortical surface of each subject. For the listening task, an IC showing activations in the superior temporal cortex (STC) was identified for each subject. For the listening-reciting task, three ICs showing activations in the STC, inferior frontal gyrus (IFG; overlapping Broca’s area), and the orofacial representations in the sensorimotor cortex (SMC) were identified for each subject. Each IC timecourse was upsampled to 100 samples/s using cubic spline interpolation. The upsampled timecourse of each IC was segmented into 16 nonoverlapping 16-s windows and then averaged within each subject. The average timecourses were further averaged across 31 subjects. The timings of peak (maximum), rise (ascending half‑maximum point), fall (descending half-maximum point), and duration (full width at half maximum; FWHM) of the hemodynamic response of each IC were identified in the within-subject and across-subject average timecourses.

Results

For the listening task, the average hemodynamic response of the STC component rose at 5.95 ± 0.55 s, peaked at 8.47 ± 0.57 s, and fell at 10.76 ± 0.63 s. For the listening-reciting task, the group-average onset and offset of speech output were 6.08 ± 0.61 s and 10.94 ± 1.10 s, respectively. The average hemodynamic response of the STC component rose at 6.49 ± 1.15 s, peaked at 8.91 ± 1.62 s, and fell at 11.82  ± 1.73 s. The average hemodynamic response of the IFG component rose at 10.16 ± 3.76 s, peaked at 12.26 ± 3.77 s, and fell at 14.35 ± 3.45 s. The average hemodynamic response of the SMC component rose at 11.16 ± 1.75 s, peaked at 14.26 ± 1.57 s, and fell at 16.48 ± 1.41 s. In the listening-reciting task, the time differences between the onset of speech output (average: 6.08 s, measured from trial onset at 0 s) and the peaks of the hemodynamic responses of the STC, IFG, and SMC components are 2.83 s, 6.18 s and 8.18 s, respectively.

To estimate the hemodynamic response of self-generated speech, the hemodynamic response profile of the STC component in the listening task was subtracted from that of the STC component in the listening-reciting task. The estimated hemodynamic profile rose at 11.91 s, peaked at 13.90 s, and fell at 15.74 s, which aligned closely with the temporal profile of the SMC component, confirming a second activation in the STC during the reciting phase.

Conclusion

We overcame the challenges associated with speech perception and overt production in the MRI scanner and achieved continuous fMRI sampling with real-time auditory feedback and minimal head-motion artifacts. Spatial ICA successfully identified multiple independent components, with their hemodynamic profiles revealing the sequential activations among brain regions during sentence-level language perception and production tasks. The STC component was activated by external speech during the perception phase in both listening and listening-reciting tasks. Although we did not identify separate independent components for processing external speech and self-generated speech,the linear subtraction between the STC timecourses of the listening-reciting task and the listening task revealed a second activation in the STC driven by self-generated speech. The IFG component was activated after the STC component during the planning phase. Finally, the SMC component was activated during the production phase. Together, this study resolved the precise timing of hemodynamic responses in multiple brain regions involved in speech perception and production. The temporal information identified, such as the time difference between speech onset and hemodynamic maximum, may facilitate future language fMRI studies in determining the optimal timings for stimulus presentation, overt production, and image acquisition for sparse-sampling designs.
