Introduction

The habenula (Hb) has emerged as a key node linking aversive learning, monoaminergic regulation, and mood disorders.^1,2 Recent interest in the Hb's role in depression stems largely from animal studies demonstrating that lateral Hb neurons encode negative reward prediction errors and that models of depression consistently show habenular hyperactivity as a primary driver of depressive-like symptoms.^1,2,3 While direct evidence for baseline hyperactivity in human depression remains inconclusive,^1 resting-state functional connectivity studies in healthy individuals consistently confirm coupling between the Hb and monoaminergic nuclei – including the ventral tegmental area, substantia nigra, dorsal raphe – alongside connectivity with limbic and default-mode network regions.^1,4 This suggests the Hb may serve as a gateway linking subcortical reward circuits with cortical networks involved in self-referential processing and affective regulation. 

Recent high-resolution MRI and tractography studies have revealed hemispheric asymmetries and sex differences in habenular connectivity, function, and fibre organization in humans, ^5 underscoring the importance of lateralized and sex-stratified analyses. Despite this progress, how left and right Hb differentially modulate monoaminergic networks in humans – and whether these modulation patterns differ by sex – remains poorly characterized. 

Here, we investigated this regulatory architecture in 188 healthy young adults. We employed multilevel whole-brain moderation analysis followed by spectral Dynamic Causal Modelling (spDCM) to determine how the activity of the Hb gates the connectivity of monoaminergic nuclei with cortical areas by sex. This approach provides a mechanistic map of how the healthy human Hb shapes monoaminergic outflow across dopaminergic, noradrenergic, and serotonergic systems, revealing lateralized and sex-specific motifs that may be relevant for vulnerability to and treatment of mood disorders.

Methods

Participants. 190 healthy young adults (52,6% women) underwent a T1-weighted anatomical and one resting-state fMRI run (8 min, eyes open, 2 mm isotropic) in a 3T scanner; after quality control, 188 participants (51,6% women) were included.

ROI definition. Left and right Hb masks were taken from the Automated Human Habenula Segmentation Program normalized in MNI152NLin2009cAsym space. ^4 Monoaminergic nuclei masks for locus coeruleus (LC), ventral tegmental area (VTA), substantia nigra (SN), substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc) raphe nuclei (dorsal, DRN; median, MnR; and magnus, RMg), were defined using the Brainstem Navigator 7T in-vivo atlas.

Preprocessing. Images were preprocessed with fMRIPrep 25.0.0. T1-weighted images were N4 bias-corrected, skull-stripped, segmented into GM/WM/CSF and non-linearly normalised to MNI152NLin2009cAsym using ANTs. BOLD images were motion-corrected, susceptibility-distortion corrected using a fieldmap-less approach, co-registered to T1 with boundary-based registration, normalised to MNI space and resampled in a single interpolation step. Confound time series included six motion parameters and their derivatives, framewise displacement, DVARS and anatomical/temporal CompCor components, following the default fMRIPrep implementation.

Denoising and smoothing. Further preprocessing used CONN. Functional data were spatially smoothed with a 6-mm FWHM Gaussian kernel. For each run we regressed out 5 WM and 5 CSF CompCor components, 6 motion parameters and their first derivatives (12 regressors), ART-flagged outlier scans (FD > 0.9 mm or global signal change > 5 SD), 5 QC_aCompCor components and linear trends, followed by temporal band-pass filtering (0.008–0.09 Hz). 

Multilevel whole-brain moderation analysis. For each monoaminergic nucleus and hemisphere, we implemented a voxelwise multilevel moderated regression in SPM12, modelling BOLD_Y = β₀ + β₁·X + β₂·W + β₃·(X×W) + covariates, where X and W variables were the denoised but unsmoothed mean monoaminergic nuclei and Hb ROI time series, respectively. The Y variable consisted in the denoised whole-brain BOLD signal images smoothed at 6mm. The β₃ term tested where Hb activity moderated monoaminergic resting-state connectivity with voxel clusters across the brain. Men and women were estimated in separate models; sex effects were assessed with between-group contrasts. Results were thresholded at cluster-level family-wise error correction (pFWE < 0.05).

Spectral DCM. For each significant triad (Hb – monoaminergic nucleus – significant cortical cluster in the moderation analysis), we specified a 3-node spDCM using their unsmoothed denoised ROI time series in SPM12. A-matrices were constrained to include only connections with prior human structural evidence from diffusion/structural MRI (monoaminergic-to-cortex and brainstem-to-brainstem tracts). Hb connection with LC remains unproven structurally in humans, with only sparce rsFC connectivity reports, but we included it as it has been consistently described in animal models. Direct Hb-to-cortex connections were not included, to isolate the gating role of Hb–monoaminergic coupling of our hypothesis. Group-level parameter estimates were obtained using Parametric Empirical Bayes. Leave-one-out cross-validation with posterior probability (Pp) > 0.9 and inclusion frequency metrics were used to identify robust effective connectivity patterns. The strength of effective connectivity is represented by the posterior expectation (Ep; Hz), which quantifies the rate constants of directed neuronal influences, indicating how strongly activity in a source region contributes to the rate of change of neuronal activity in a target region; positive values indicate an excitatory influence, whereas negative values indicate an inhibitory influence.

Results

Across nuclei, we found robust Hb-dependent resting-state connectivity modulation of different monoaminergic nuclei, with marked lateralisation and sex effects. However, no significant moderation effects survived correction for SNc, DRN or MnR.

LC. Right habenula (HbR) significantly moderated right LC connectivity in a sex-specific manner. In women, HbR activity reduced LC-left middle frontal gyrus (MFG) coupling. In men, HbR increased LC-left supramarginal gyrus (SMG) coupling, and HbR-LC effects were stronger in men than women over a left SMG/MFG cluster. However, spDCM showed consistent inhibitory Hb-LC effective connectivity across sexes (women: -0.22; men: -0.16), and positive feedback from LC-Hb (+0.05, both sexes), indicating that sex differences at the rsFC level in the moderation analysis arise from differences in LC-cortex pathways rather than from opposite-sign Hb-LC influences.

RMg. In the whole sample, HbR positively moderated RMg connectivity with the left central operculum. spDCM revealed a strong inhibitory HbR-RMg influence (-0.50), and excitatory feedback RMg-HbR (+0.10).

SN. In men, left habenula (HbL) activity reduced left SN connectivity with left superior frontal and postcentral gyri. spDCM showed excitatory HbL-SN connectivity (+0.05), and negative feedback from SN-HbL (-0.12).

VTA. Moderation analysis identified several Hb-VTA-cortex triads. In men, HbL reduced left VTA-left anterior insula connectivity, an effect stronger in men than women (−34, 24, 0; k=844). HbR reduced right VTA-left frontal operculum. HbR positively moderated right VTA connectivity with clusters in right SPL, left SOG, right cuneus and right IOG. On the other hand, women showed negative moderation of HbR upon left VTA-right postcentral gyrus connectivity. spDCM supported a Hb-driven configuration only for insula and frontal operculum triads in men: HbL-VTA effective connectivity was consistently excitatory (+0.05), with negative feedback from VTA-HbL (-0.08). For the other results involving occipital and parietal clusters, spDCM indicated that dynamics were dominated by self-connections and VTA-cortex links, with no robust Hb influence.

Across validated triads, effective connectivity consistently indicated positive Hb-VTA/SN and negative Hb-LC/RMg influences, with inverse-sign feedback from each monoaminergic nucleus back to the Hb (i.e., negative VTA/SN-Hb and positive LC/RMg-Hb). These results support a neuromodulator-specific gating role of the Hb on monoaminergic circuits at rest, with sex-dependent configurations particularly evident in LC, VTA and SN networks.

Conclusion

In a large sample of healthy adults, we demonstrate that the Hb acts as a neuromodulator-specific, lateralized gate on monoaminergic circuits at rest, with distinct configurations in men and women. Combining whole-brain multilevel moderation with spDCM, we identify specific triads in which Hb activity modulates LC, RMg, SN, and VTA connectivity with cortical regions, and show that these resting-state functional connectivity effects are supported by systematic patterns of directional effective connectivity.

Across validated models, the Hb exerts excitatory influence upon dopaminergic nuclei (VTA, SN) and inhibitory influence upon noradrenergic and serotonergic nuclei (LC, RMg), with inverse-sign feedback from each monoaminergic nucleus back to the Hb. This reciprocal architecture suggests that the Hb does not simply suppress monoaminergic outflow but rather implements a bidirectional control scheme that may dynamically balance reward-related dopamine signalling against arousal- and aversion-related norepinephrine and serotonin release, consistent with recurrent Hb–monoamine feedback loops described in animal tract-tracing and electrophysiology studies.

Critically, these gating motifs are strongly lateralized. Right Hb drives LC and RMg effects; left Hb drives SN effects and VTA connectivity with anterior insula and frontal operculum; while right Hb moderates VTA connections with occipital and parietal regions. This functional asymmetry supports a model in which the left and right Hb differentially tune dopaminergic versus noradrenergic/serotonergic outflow while maintaining tightly coupled reciprocal loops with each monoaminergic system.

Sex differences were particularly evident in LC and VTA networks. Men and women showed distinct Hb-dependent modulation of noradrenergic and dopaminergic projections to frontoparietal and insular cortices, even though spDCM revealed that the sign of Hb-LC effective connectivity was consistently inhibitory in both sexes. This indicates that observed sex differences in functional connectivity arise primarily from differences in downstream monoaminergic-cortical pathways rather than from opposite-direction Hb influences. These findings provide a mechanistic substrate for previously reported asymmetries and sex effects in Hb connectivity and fibre organization, ^4,5 and suggest that vulnerability to mood disorders – as well as differential response to rapid-acting antidepressants such as ketamine – may arise from disruptions in these hemisphere- and sex-specific Hb-monoamine loops rather than from generalized Hb hyperactivity alone.

Methodologically, this work extends human Hb research beyond seed-based connectivity by combining moderation analysis with spDCM, allowing us to separate regions where functional connectivity is merely Hb-sensitive from those where directional causal influences are strongly supported. Limitations include the spatial resolution of 3T fMRI for very small brainstem and diencephalic structures, the use of MNI-normalized group-level Hb masks rather than subject-specific segmentations, and the restriction of spDCM A-matrices to connections with prior structural evidence, which reduces overfitting but prevents testing all observed moderation patterns at the effective-connectivity level.

Overall, our results provide a normative reference map of Hb-monoamine dynamics at rest in healthy adults, and identify specific LC-, VTA-, and SN-centered circuits that can be investigated in future studies of depression pathophysiology, ketamine's rapid antidepressant mechanisms, and the neurobiological basis of sex differences in mood disorder prevalence and treatment response.
