Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic inflammatory neurological condition. It is typically characterised by pronounced white matter (WM) structural changes (lesions), occurring due to processes such as demyelination and axonal loss in the brain.  

Recently, several studies have focused on examining the microstructural differences between lesions and normal appearing white matter (NAWM) in MS patients and have compared these to healthy control (HC) WM through advanced biophysical modelling, such as the soma and neurite density imaging (SANDI)1. One such study2 has applied SANDI in MS and found that there were lower neurite and soma fractions, indicative of structural damage in MS lesions compared to both NAWM in MS and WM in HCs.  

Another biophysical model of interest for WM characterization is neurite exchange imaging (NEXI)3. NEXI offers the advantage of giving an estimate of water exchange time which is thought to reflect cell membrane permeability. While NEXI has not been previously applied in MS, it can be hypothesised that the NEXI exchange time will be reflective of demyelination in MS i.e. the estimated exchange time in lesions will be shorter than in NAWM and HC WM.  

The goal of this work is twofold – firstly, to examine the difference between the SANDI and NEXI metrics in HC WM, MS NAWM, and MS lesions. Secondly, we want to identify the sources of variation in lesions’ microstructure and explore how we can cluster lesions based on SANDI and NEXI. 

One key novelty of this work is to leverage SANDI and NEXI metrics to classify the different MS lesion types on the basis of their microstructural characteristics. This could be relevant not only for MS diagnosis, but also for prognosis and disease progression monitoring. 

Methods

Data Acquisition

23 patients with relapsing-remitting MS with an expanded disability status scale (EDSS) score <4 (mean age [SD]: 40.74[7.5]; F/M=16/7) and 26 healthy controls (mean age [SD]: 34.08[10.47]; F/M=18/8) underwent 3T Siemens Connectom MRI scanning. The scanning protocol included a T1-weighted anatomical scan (voxel size –1x1x1mm, field of view (FOV) = 256x256, matrix size – 256x256x256mm) and 3 multi-shell diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) acquisitions (voxel size –2x2x2mm, FOV = 990x990 mm, matrix size – 110x110x66 mm) with different diffusion times (17, 24, and 55 ms, respectively) and fixed gradient pulse duration of 7 ms. The 17 ms diffusion time acquisition included b values of 0, 2400, 6000 s/mm2, while the other 2 included b values of 0, 200, 500, 1200, 2400, 4000, 6000 and 0, 7750, 15500 s/mm2 respectively. MS patients’ protocol also included a T2-weighted (voxel size - 0.45 x 0.45 x 0.9 mm, FOV = 464 × 512 mm, matrix size 256x464x512) acquisition as a means of lesion identification.  

Data Processing

DWI data preprocessing included denoising, Gibbs ringing removal, topup, eddy and gradient nonlinearity distortion correction. The anatomical (T1-weighted) and T2-weighted data were affinely aligned with the diffusion data. 

To obtain the lesion volumes and a NAWM mask for the MS patients and a WM mask for the healthy controls, we used MindGlide4 – a deep learning segmentation approach on the T2-weighted data for patients and on the T1-weighted anatomical data for the controls respectively. All segmentations were manually checked and edited to control for false positives. Each visible lesion was manually assigned a label such that lesions extending across more than one slice were treated as a single lesion. 

We fit the SANDI model to the 24 ms diffusion time data only (using the code at https://github.com/palombom/SANDI-Matlab-Toolbox-Latest-Release), while the NEXI model was fit to the whole diffusion data at multiple diffusion times (following the same approach as https://github.com/QuentinUhl/nexi). From SANDI, we obtained neurite fraction (SANDI-fneurite), soma fraction (SANDI-fsoma), intracellular diffusivity (SANDI-Din), extracellular diffusivity (SANDI-De), and soma radius (SANDI-rsoma). From NEXI we derived the exchange time (NEXI-tex), neurite fraction (NEXI-fneurite), extracellular diffusivity (NEXI-De), and the neurite axial diffusivity (NEXI-Da) maps. To generally characterise differences between HC and MS, for each patient’s lesions and NAWM masks, we obtained the medians of SANDI-fneurite, SANDI-fsoma, SANDI-rsoma and NEXI-tex metrics. The same was also done for the healthy controls WM masks. Examples of these maps for two sample participants can be seen in Figure 1.  

To investigate whether there are any statistical differences between the HC WM and NAWM and NAWM and MS lesions, we conducted a two-sample t-test (without equal variance assumptions) for each of the metrics. All the statistical tests that were conducted were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni correction. 

Principal Component Analysis & K-means Clustering

We conducted a principal component analysis (PCA) on all the NEXI and SANDI metrics obtained from the MS lesions and picked the top components that described the most variance.  We then used the silhouette evaluation method to determine the optimal number of clusters in order to run k-means clustering on the PCA components. 

Results

Differences between HC WM, MS NAWM, MS lesions 

In Figure 2a we can see the median scores for SANDI-fsoma, SANDI-fneurite, SANDI-rsoma and NEXI-tex plotted for HC WM, MS NAWM, and MS lesions.  

We found that there were no significant differences between the SANDI-fsoma values t(31)=-2.48, p>0.05; the SANDI-fneurite values t(38) = 2.58, p>0.05; the SANDI-rsoma t(38) = -1.80, p>0.05 or the NEXI-tex values t(31) = -0.58, p>0.05, indicating that there are no statistical differences between the HC WM and the MS NAWM metrics.  

Similarly, we then investigated whether there are any differences between the MS lesions and NAWM for the same diffusion metrics. We found significant differences between SANDI-fsoma values t(123) =-5.97, p<0.05; the SANDI-fneurite values t(33) = -23.91, p<0.01 and the SANDI-rsoma values t(414) = -53.34, p<0.05. The NEXI-tex values showed no statistical differences t(31) = -0.09, p>0.05. However, since Figure 2a shows higher variance for the NEXI-tex values for the MS lesions, we conducted an additional two-sample F-test to test for equal variance. We found a significant difference between the variances of the two groups F(402, 22) = 3.37, p<0.05.  

Therefore, we can conclude that there are significant microstructural differences between NAWM and MS lesions only when using the SANDI metrics, while NEXI should be further investigated.  

Principal Component Analysis  

We selected the top three PCA components as they described 75% of the overall variance in the results. Looking at the loadings for the components (Figure 2b) which represent how strongly each variable contributes to each of the components, we can see that for PCA component 1 SANDI-fneurite, SANDI-De, NEXI-De and NEXI-fneurite are the biggest contributors. Therefore, component 1 seems to be driven mostly by the neurite metrics and the extracellular diffusivity, potentially related to neurodegeneration, specifically neurite loss. For PCA component 2, the main contributions are from SANDI-fsoma, SANDI-rsoma, NEXI-Da, and NEXI-tex, reflecting the presence of potential inflammation and demyelination. Finally, for PCA component 3, NEXI-Da and NEXI-fneurite are the biggest contributors, which similarly to PCA component 1’s interpretation, seems to be driven by axonal density and overall indicative of changes in neurite integrity. 

K-means clustering 

Through our silhouette evaluation we identified 2 clusters as the optimal number of clusters which we then used in the k-means clustering. From Figure 2c, we can see that there seems to be one main cluster (cluster 1) with the rest of the datapoints being more scattered (cluster 2). To understand better how the different PCA components might be contributing to the two clusters, we have represented the lesions’ distributions according to the cluster for each component (Figure 2d). The highest differences between cluster 1 and 2 were observed for PCA1, suggesting that neurodegeneration has a dominant effect on the diffusion MRI signal, while demyelination and inflammation seem to have a more secondary effect. 

Conclusion

The present work had two aims. Firstly, to examine the microstructural differences between HC WM, NAWM, and MS lesions using SANDI and NEXI, and secondly, to attempt to cluster MS lesions based on the two models’ diffusion metrics.  

Starting with the findings related to our first aim, we found no significant microstructural differences between HC WM and NAWM. When comparing NAWM and MS lesions, however, we did find significant differences for the three SANDI metrics, but we did not find any differences in the NEXI exchange time, despite the variances being significantly different. Our SANDI results confirm what has been shown previously when comparing NAWM and MS lesions, interestingly with the addition of showing differences in the estimated soma radius, which could be an indicator of axonal loss.   

One key novelty of this work – investigating whether the SANDI and NEXI metrics can be leveraged in an attempt to identify the main sources of variance and cluster different lesions based on those – lead us to identify three main components that capture most of our data’s variance. PCA 1, which could be indicative of neurite loss; PCA 2, potentially indicative of the presence of inflammation and demyelination; and PCA 3, potentially indicative of changes in neurite density. Based on these three components, we identified two clusters, with neurodegeneration having a dominant effect.  

While currently, we cannot speculate as to what each of the clusters represents exactly in terms of lesion microstructure, in the future we will investigate this aspect in more depth using a multimodal approach. This could involve using quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM) data to identify whether the clusters could potentially represent active and inactive WM lesions and/or T1-weighted images where it is thought that active lesions have a dark rim around them5. Furthermore, this analysis could be extended by trying to identify lesions where PCA 2 (inflammation and demyelination) is more dominant than PCA 1 such as in active MS lesions. This would be invaluable to potentially identify chronic active lesions from inactive lesions, not only in prognosis but in stratifying patients in clinical trials. 

In conclusion, there seems to be great promise in using diffusion microstructural metrics (such as these from SANDI and NEXI) in classifying MS lesions. While this work presented some preliminary results, further validation is needed in the future in order to better understand the microstructural basis of lesions’ classification.
