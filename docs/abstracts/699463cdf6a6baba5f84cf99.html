Introduction

Inflammatory processes have established links with brain aging and neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease and Alzheimer’s disease related dementias (AD/ADRD)1. Emerging evidence indicates that an elevated level of peripheral inflammation in older adults as measured by high-sensitivity c-reactive protein (hs-CRP) from blood is associated with brain structural abnormalities in white matter (WM), including lower WM volume and greater white matter hyperintensities (WMH)2. Although peripheral inflammation is often thought to precede neuroinflammation by triggering the activation of astrocytes and microglia within the brain, neuroinflammation can be independently initiated in the presence of neurological diseases3. For instance, glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), a biomarker of astrocyte reactivity that reflects neuroinflammatory processes, is associated with WMH and AD pathology in cognitively impaired patients4. Despite the links between peripheral inflammation and neuroinflammation, no studies have examined these two processes together or evaluated their distinct contributions to white matter abnormalities in community-dwelling, non-clinical populations. Understanding how different inflammatory processes influence brain health is essential for developing prevention and intervention strategies to promote healthy cognitive and brain aging. Leveraging blood-based biomarkers and structural MRI, the present study aims to examine the associations of peripheral inflammation (hs-CRP) and neuroinflammation (GFAP) with WMH separately, and the extent to which these represent unique contributions in community-dwelling older adults.

Methods

Participants were from the Vietnam Era Twin Study of Aging (VETSA), an ongoing multisite longitudinal study of cognitive and brain aging beginning in middle age. In the present study, the sample included 372 community-dwelling men (mean age=67.38 years, SD=2.66; range=61-72) from wave 3 of VETSA, who had blood-based biomarkers and structural MRI data available. Participants are similar to American men in their age cohort with respect to health, education, and lifestyle characteristics.

MRI Acquisition and Processing 

T1-weighted (sagittal 3D fast spoiled gradient echo (FSPGR), TE = 3.164 msec, TR = 8.084 msec), T2-weighted (coronal 2D FRFSE-XL, TE = 94 msec, TR = 4600 msec), and proton-density (PD)-weighted images (coronal, TE = min/full, TR = 3000 msec) were acquired on two General Electric (GE) Discovery MR750 3.0T scanners with an eight-channel phased array head coil.

For global volume of WMH, a multi-channel segmentation approach was used to leverage complementary information from three volumes with different contrasts to increase measurement sensitivity while reducing acquisition-specific noise5. These WMH measures reflect abnormal signal in the white matter, including regions that are hyperintense on T2 and PD and hypointense on T1 images. Processing steps included standard alignment of the T1 (6 degrees-of-freedom, rigid transformation to an anterior/posterior commissure aligned space), registration of T2 and PD to T1 using a mutual information method, and intensity non-uniformity correction using N3. Tissue segmentation was performed using the statistical classification framework of the open source Insight Segmentation and Registration Toolkit (ITK), leveraging Scott's L2E method to determine robust means and covariances, and in combination with anatomical considerations via ITK’s morphological operators. This produced a segmentation of white matter, gray matter, CSF, and AWM; greater detail is provided in Lin et al5. Additionally, since partial voluming of CSF and white matter along the edges of ventricles results in voxels with AWM-like signal, even in healthy individuals, any voxels that touched (i.e., shared a common face, edge, or vertex with) a ventricular fluid voxel were excluded from the estimated volumes. The automated segmentations were visually reviewed and manually edited when necessary to correct misclassifications.

Blood Collection and Processing 

Blood specimens were collected under fasting conditions. Levels of plasma GFAP were quantified using an ultra-sensitive immunoassay on the Simoa HD-X platform (Simoa GFAP Discovery Kit; Quanterix Corporation) by the USC Alzheimer’s Therapeutic Research Institute Biomarker Core. The standard exclusion criteria included hemolysis and a coefficient of variation in plasma concentrations >0.20. hs-CRP was measured from serum samples and processed by Quest Diagnostics using spectrophotometry.

Statistical Analyses 

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.1. Prior to analyses, all biomarkers and WMH volumes were log transformed to improve their distribution properties. For the biomarker data, values exceeding three times the interquartile range outside of the first and third quartiles after transformation were excluded from analyses. Plasma GFAP was adjusted for testing site and sample storage time. To examine the associations of hs-CRP and GFAP with WMH, linear mixed models were performed using the lme4 package. Age, APOE genotype (ε4 positive or negative), health comorbidities (Charlson index), and total WM volume were included as covariates. Because there were twin pairs in our sample, we also included twin pair ID as a random intercept in all models to account for correlated outcomes. 

Results

Before running linear mixed models, we conducted correlational analyses among predictors. Higher hs-CRP was significantly associated with younger age (r=-.14, p=.008), more health comorbidities (r=.15, p<.005) and lower GFAP (r=-.14, p=.009). Higher GFAP was significantly associated with older age (r=.11, p=.036). No other significant correlations were detected among the predictors.

In the main analyses, a higher level of hs-CRP was significantly associated with greater global WMH volume (β=.06, p=.023) after accounting for the effects of age, APOE genotype, health comorbidities, and total WM volume. We did not observe any significant interaction between hs-CRP and APOE genotype (p>.05). Higher GFAP was significantly associated with greater WMH volume (β=.05, p=.045) after accounting for covariates, and there was not a significant GFAP x APOE genotype interaction. We then examined hs-CRP and GFAP together in the same model and found that higher levels of hs-CRP and GFAP were both significantly associated with greater WMH volume (hs-CRP: β=.08, p=.004; GFAP: β=.06, p=.024). No significant interaction effects were observed among hs-CRP, GFAP, and APOE genotype.

We conducted control analyses substituting health comorbidities with body mass index (BMI), as BMI has been shown to influence GFAP level, potentially due to dilution effects associated with greater blood volume. The results remained the same, such that higher level of hs-CRP or GFAP was significantly associated with greater global WMH volume (β=.06, p=.031; β=.06, p=.031, respectively). When examined together, higher levels of hs-CRP and GFAP were both significantly associated with greater WMH volume (β=.07, p=.007; β=.06, p=.023, respectively). Finally, no significant interaction with APOE genotype was detected.

Conclusion

In community-dwelling older adults, we replicated previous findings that greater levels of peripheral inflammation and neuroinflammation are associated with greater WMH burden when examined separately. Most importantly, we found that their links to WMH burden are independent of each other, suggesting that peripheral inflammation and neuroinflammation are distinct biological pathways associated with WMH burden. Collectively, our results suggest that WMH may arise from multiple inflammatory mechanisms in the peripheral and central nervous systems simultaneously. Targeting both inflammatory processes may be beneficial for treating WMH burden and related neurodegenerative diseases.
