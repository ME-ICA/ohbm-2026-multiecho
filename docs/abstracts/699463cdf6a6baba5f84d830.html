Introduction

Recent advances in high-density diffuse optical tomography (HD-DOT) have allowed for mapping the visual cortex, with similar results to previous functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies.1 While HD-DOT has demonstrated resolution comparable to asynchronously acquired fMRI data, no study to date has performed simultaneous measurements with both methods. In this work, we present concurrent HD-DOT and fMRI recordings to enable a direct comparison of population receptive field (pRF) mapping across modalities. pRF mapping quantifies subject-specific retinotopic organization of the visual cortex and enables forward prediction of BOLD responses to visual stimuli using the fitted pRF model.2 

Methods

Ten healthy participants (N = 10) completed a pRF mapping paradigm while both HD-DOT and fMRI data was recorded simultaneously. Each subject performed six runs of pRF mapping (3x 300s per stimulus type), where they were presented with bar, wedge and ring stimuli. Stimuli spanned 9.3 visual degrees and were filled with colorful objects, faces, and scenes overlaid on a pink-noise background, flickering at 3 Hz and moving across the screen in 24 steps of 1s (=1 TR).3 An example of the pRF stimuli is shown in Figure 1a. MRI data was recorded on a 3T Siemens MAGNETOM system, using a 64-channel head coil. Structural MRI included T1-weighted and T2-weighted scans acquired at 1 × 1 × 1 mm resolution. Functional MRI data were collected using a gradient-echo EPI sequence with 2.5 × 2.5 × 2.5 mm spatial resolution, repetition time (TR) of 1 s, and echo time (TE) of 31 ms. MRI and fMRI data were preprocessed using freesurfer 8.1 and fmriprep 24.1.0. 

HD-DOT data was recorded inside the MRI environment using the NIRx Borealis and two cascaded NIRSport2 dual-wavelength (𝜆1 = 760 nm; 𝜆2 = 850 nm) continuous-wave devices with laser coupled diode sources and avalanche photodiode detectors placed in a high-density patch covering the occipital lobe, displayed in Figure 1b. The high-density patch consisted of 16 sources and 23 detectors, yielding 184 measurement channels sampled at ~2.5 Hz. Source-detector separations included up to the third nearest neighbours, with channel lengths of approximately 17, 29.4, and 44.9 mm. Sources were illuminated in three separate illumination steps to allow for optimal stimulation of the three different channel lengths. HD-DOT data was preprocessed using Satori. 

To allow for accurate comparison of the simultaneously acquired fMRI and HD-DOT data, an in-house tool was developed to allow for semi-automatic detection of optode positions from participants’ individual MPRAGE-based head models. Optical density time series were masked to exclude low-quality channels using a coefficient of variation (CV) cutoff of 10%. In order to perform image reconstruction of the HD-DOT time series, light propagation was modelled using subject-specific anatomical segmentation of scalp, skull, cerebrospinal fluid, grey matter and white matter  and Monte Carlo photon simulation (107 photons) using the MCX toolkit. The image reconstruction was performed in the anatomical volume space, and restricted to regions with sufficient optical sensitivity (>10-6) for both wavelengths. The sensitivity profile used for this is displayed in Figure 1c. HD-DOT time series were low-pass filtered (cut-off 0.4 Hz) to reduce physiological noise and downsampled to 1 Hz. The inverse problem was solved using the Tikhonov method with spatially variant regularisation  (a=0.05, b=0.01). Reconstructed absorption changes were converted to oxy- (HbO) and deoxyhemoglobin (HbR) concentration time courses and mapped back into participants’ native voxel space, then sampled on the cortical surface for consecutive pRF fitting. 

Finally, pRF fitting was performed using the vistasoft toolbox for both fMRI and HD-DOT timecourses, where pRFs were represented as circular two-dimensional Gaussian functions. Each vertex was characterized by three parameters: horizontal and vertical position in visual space (x, y) and a spread parameter (σ) defining receptive field size. Model responses were generated by weighting the stimulus contrast aperture with the Gaussian function and subsequently convolving the result with a hemodynamic response function to obtain predicted signal changes. 

Results

Results are shown for one representative subject. Figure 2 shows a close correspondence between HD-DOT (HbO) and fMRI (BOLD) pRF estimates in the visual cortex. Time courses extracted from the vertex with maximal explained variance in the HD-DOT data showed clear stimulus-locked responses in both modalities (Fig. 2a). In line with established population receptive field (pRF) literature showing systematic increases in pRF size with eccentricity,4 pRF size increased as a function of eccentricity for both BOLD and HbO (Fig. 2b). Linear fits revealed a steeper slope for HbO-derived pRF sizes compared to BOLD, indicating systematically larger pRF estimates in HD-DOT, while preserving the expected eccentricity-dependent scaling. The larger pRF size estimates in HD-DOT versus fMRI likely reflect differences in spatial resolution and point spread inherent to each modality.

Spatial maps further illustrated qualitative agreement between modalities (Fig. 2c). While HbO maps appeared spatially broader and with limited coverage compared to BOLD, canonical features of retinotopic organization, such as foveal representations at the occipital pole and polar angle reversals at the V1/V2 boundary, were evident in both modalities. The coarse appearance of the HbO maps is consistent with the lower resolution of HD-DOT compared to fMRI. A likely contributor to restricted coverage in HbO is the modality’s depth sensitivity, which declines with increasing distance from the optodes and limits reliable imaging to the superficial cortex (~13–18 mm).5 This depth-dependent sensitivity may contribute to apparent visual “blind spots” in the reconstructed maps. Potentially, the blind spots also reflect properties of the reconstruction algorithm, which may be further optimized. Together, these results demonstrate that HD-DOT can recover pRF properties that parallel fMRI, supporting its utility as a noninvasive, portable alternative for mapping visual cortical organisation.

Conclusion

This study presents simultaneous HD-DOT and fMRI pRF mapping, enabling a direct, within-subject comparison of retinotopic organization across modalities. Despite lower spatial resolution and depth sensitivity limitations, HD-DOT reproduced key retinotopic features observed with fMRI, including eccentricity gradients and polar angle reversals. Discrepancies between modalities highlight current constraints in optical sensitivity and reconstruction methods, but also underscore the potential of HD-DOT as a complementary, portable technique for functional mapping of visual cortex. Continued methodological refinements may further improve spatial resolution and coverage. These results support the feasibility of concurrent HD-DOT and fMRI for high-fidelity retinotopic mapping, highlight clear targets for improving HD-DOT reconstruction and sensitivity, and pave the way for multimodal studies that integrate optical and hemodynamic signals to deepen our understanding of visual cortex function.
