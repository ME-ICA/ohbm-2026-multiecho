Introduction

 Childhood trauma (CT) has been associated with long-lasting alterations in emotional, cognitive, and stress-regulatory systems, yet the associated brain network alterations have been difficult to identify in adulthood due to their subtle nature. Prior studies have linked CT to alterations in salience, default mode, cerebellar, and fronto-limbic networks, as well as to behavioral and psychiatric outcomes [1]. Retrospective measures such as the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire Short-Form (CTQ-SF) allow the separation of abuse and neglect, but their specific contributions to resting-state functional connectivity (rsFC) in adulthood are still unclear. Existing research has suggested potential connectivity disruptions involving the inferior frontal gyrus [2] and cerebellar–temporal and socio-emotional circuits [3]. 
More recent work using children cohorts and different psychiatric groups has shown that early adversity may affect Theory of Mind, DMN hubs, and temporal networks [4], supporting the idea that CT influences distributed, rather than focal, connectivity systems. However, these effects may be influenced by genetic and shared environmental factors. Twin designs address this gap by enabling the isolation of environmental contributors while controlling for genetic variation.
Here, we examine whether CTQ-total, abuse, and neglect scores predict rsFC patterns in adulthood in a twin sample. Using whole-brain ROI-to-ROI modeling and Multivariate Functional Network Connectivity, we test whether early-life adversity produces network-level alterations or whether effects manifest in distributed connectivity variations across functional systems.

Methods

Participants. Data were drawn from the Mexican Twins Registry (TwinsMX). From an initial sample of 428 twins with resting-state MRI, one twin per family (randomly selected) was included to avoid non-independence, resulting in a sample of 214 adults (ages 32 ± 9 years, X Women). All participants completed the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire–Short Form (CTQ-SF), which provided scores for total trauma within two dimensions: abuse, and neglect.

Image Acquisition. Resting-state fMRI and T1w anatomical images were acquired using a 3T scanner.  Data was preprocessed with fMRIprep and analyzed with CONN, where spatial smoothing (6 mm FWHM), confound regression (36 parameters, including global signal and despiking), and temporal filtering (0.008 - 0.09 Hz) were performed. Functional time series were extracted from 108 brain ROIs, resulting in individual 108×108 Fisher Z-transformed connectivity matrices. 

Analysis. Three GLM models were tested at the second level: (1) CTQ-total; (2) Abuse; (3) Neglect, each controlled for age and sex. to identify trauma-related associations. The exploratory multivariate analysis was performed with functional network connectivity in CONN to identify distributed connectivity patterns associated with CTQ scores. We used a connection-level threshold p < 0.05 (uncorrected), and a cluster threshold of p < 0.05 cluster-level p-FDR corrected (MVPA omnibus test) 

Results

The multivariate functional network connectivity analysis for neglect revealed statistically significant clusters at the group level. . Neglect-related connectivity involved patterns predominantly in inferior Frontal Gyrus (triangular and Opercular portions), Orbital Frontal Cortex, Precuneus, lower Opercular Cortex, Subcortical Cingulate Cortex and ICC. These results showed patterns that span across different positive and negative values, indicating heterogeneous directions of associations. In contrast, no statistical significant clusters were obtained for the abuse dimension after correction for multiple comparison. 

Conclusion

 In this exploratory study, we examined whether childhood trauma was associated with resting-state functional connectivity in an adult sample from the Mexican Twin Registry. While no strong or consistent trauma effects were obtained across all dimensions, our results suggest that in particular childhood neglect may be associated with specific, subtle and distributed connectivity patterns across adulthood. 

Overall, these findings suggest that the neural effects of childhood trauma in adults may be weaker or more heterogeneous than those reported in younger populations, or that they may depend on specific psychological or contextual factors not captured by resting-state connectivity alone. This work highlights the importance of method selection in neuroimaging studies and supports the need for future research using longitudinal designs or more specific behavioral dimensions to better understand how early-life adversity affects the brain across the lifespan.
