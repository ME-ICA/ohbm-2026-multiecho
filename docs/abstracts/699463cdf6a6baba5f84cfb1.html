Introduction

Multimodal neuroimaging exploits complementary biophysical sensitivities to characterize brain activity across spatiotemporal scales1. Neurovascular coupling (NVC)—the physiological link between neural activity and hemodynamic responses—is central to both cognition and neurological disease2. Elucidating NVC requires simultaneous, high-fidelity recording of electrophysiological and hemodynamic signals. Building on our prior proof-of-concept work3, we present a technically integrated, simultaneous whole-brain neuroimaging system combining electroencephalography (EEG), optically pumped magnetometer-based magnetoencephalography (OPM-MEG), and functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS). This system overcomes critical challenges in cross-modal electromagnetic compatibility, crosstalk suppression, and sub-millisecond synchronization—enabling, for the first time, concurrent whole-brain acquisition of magnetic (MEG), electrical (EEG), and hemodynamic (fNIRS) signals. All sensors are rigidly co-registered within a participant-specific 3D-printed helmet, aligned to individual anatomical MRI for precise multimodal source reconstruction. We validated the system in three healthy participants during median nerve stimulation and hand-grip tasks, demonstrating artifact-minimized, temporally aligned recordings that capture fast neural dynamics alongside slow hemodynamic responses. This technical framework establishes a robust system for probing coupled neural–vascular mechanisms in controlled experimental settings, paving the way for mechanistic studies of NVC in health and disease.

Methods

The simultaneous multimodal neuroimaging system integrates three core subsystems: a 32-sensor triaxial OPM-MEG module, a 64-channel custom-built EEG module, and a 32-source/32-detector custom fNIRS module. The OPM-MEG subsystem (Neuro-1, Quspin, USA) yields 96 independent magnetic field channels and operates within a custom-built, horizontally oriented cylindrical magnetic shield. This single-ended shield (70 cm internal diameter), open at one end to accommodate a non-magnetic sliding bed, features a side access port and consists of five layers of 1.5 mm permalloy encased in an 8 mm aluminum alloy outer shell. The EEG subsystem employs non-magnetic electrodes embedded in a participant-specific 3D-printed imaging helmet. Its preamplifier circuit uses components with residual magnetization below 300 nT and is positioned 30–50 cm beneath the sensor array to minimize magnetic interference; subsequent amplification and digitization are performed by hardware housed in an external cabinet outside the shield. The fNIRS subsystem is fiber-free and utilizes dual-wavelength (680/850 nm) vertical-cavity surface-emitting lasers (VCSELs) coupled with silicon photomultipliers (SiPMs) for high-sensitivity, non-magnetic hemodynamic monitoring. All subsystems are synchronized and controlled via a unified PXIe platform (National Instruments, USA), enabling temporally coherent whole-brain data acquisition.

A custom-fitted, rigid helmet was fabricated using three-dimensional (3D) head reconstruction and additive manufacturing techniques. This design permits reconfigurable placement of multimodal sensors to accommodate a wide range of experimental paradigms. The helmet was individually tailored based on a high-resolution 3D model of the participant’s neuroanatomy, reconstructed from magnetic resonance (MR) images acquired via a PETRA sequence.

Three healthy male participants were recruited under a protocol approved by the Institutional Review Board of Peking University, with written informed consent obtained from all individuals. The experimental session began with high-resolution structural MRI acquisition, which informed the fabrication of a participant-specific 3D-printed helmet for precise multimodal sensor co-registration. Neural and hemodynamic responses were recorded during two task paradigms: (1) Somatosensory stimulation via transcutaneous electrical stimulation of the median nerve (DS7A, Digitimer Ltd, UK), calibrated to reliably elicit thumb abduction. A block design was employed (10 stimulation blocks: 20 s duration, 20 pulses at 1 Hz; interleaved with 10 rest periods of 30 s); (2) Volitional grasping, using a block design (10 active blocks of 20 s, 10 rest intervals of 30 s), in which visual “grasp” cues (200 ms duration, presented at 1 Hz) instructed participants to execute standardized hand-grip movements (20 per block, 800 ms inter-stimulus interval). Movement kinematics were monitored via pneumatic pressure transduction.

MEG and EEG signals were bandpass-filtered (1–30 Hz) to isolate neural oscillatory activity within conventional frequency bands. Epochs spanning –100 to 800 ms relative to stimulus onset were extracted from artifact-free trials, averaged across repetitions, and baseline-corrected using the pre-stimulus interval to compute event-related fields (ERFs) and potentials (ERPs). Source reconstruction was performed using Brainstorm: MEG data were analyzed with minimum norm estimation (MNE), while EEG sources were localized via standardized low-resolution brain electromagnetic tomography (sLORETA). fNIRS data were processed using Homer2 and NIRSTORM toolboxes, including bandpass filtering (0.01–0.1 Hz) to retain hemodynamic fluctuations and motion artifact correction. Continuous fNIRS signals were epoched (–10 to +40 s relative to stimulus onset), averaged, and reconstructed into cortical activation maps using the MNE method.

Results

Following integration of EEG and fNIRS components, we systematically assessed their impact on MEG performance, with particular attention to residual magnetic field fluctuations and noise within the region of interest (ROI). Magnetic field characterization was conducted using a QZFM Gen-3 triaxial OPM (Quspin Inc., Louisville, CO, USA) positioned in the ROI, with its sensing axes aligned to the shield’s horizontal, vertical, and axial directions. Continuous one-hour recordings quantified the magnetic stability of the shielded environment. Field drift exhibited anisotropic behavior: 30 pT/hour (horizontal), 10 pT/hour (vertical), and 40 pT/hour (axial), yielding a vector-magnitude drift of 51 pT/hour. Power spectral density analysis of one-minute segments revealed a magnetic noise floor of 20 fT/√Hz. Additionally, the residual static field in the ROI was measured at ~2 nT using a calibrated fluxgate magnetometer (CTM-6W, National Institute of Metrology, Beijing, China). These results confirm that our custom EEG and fNIRS subsystems introduce negligible magnetic interference, fully satisfying the stringent compatibility requirements for OPM-MEG operation in an ultra-low-noise environment.

Figure 1 illustrates multimodal neural and hemodynamic responses to a voluntary grasping movement in a representative participant. ERFs and ERPs display canonical components consistent with established neurophysiological signatures of motor execution. Source localization for both MEG (via MNE) and EEG (via sLORETA) reveals focal activation in the contralateral sensorimotor cortex, aligning with prior literature4. Concurrently, fNIRS captures task-evoked hemodynamic changes: oxyhemoglobin (HbO) increases and deoxyhemoglobin (HbR) decreases exhibit temporal profiles and spatial topography characteristic of the canonical hemodynamic response within the motor network5, further validating system fidelity.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have developed and validated a novel multimodal neuroimaging system that enables simultaneous, whole-brain acquisition of OPM-MEG, EEG, and fNIRS. By resolving critical challenges in hardware co-integration—particularly magnetic compatibility—and achieving sub-millisecond temporal synchronization across modalities, our system achieves the first truly concurrent whole-brain recording of magnetic, electrical, and hemodynamic signals. Experimental validation demonstrates robust capture of canonical neural and hemodynamic responses, fully consistent with established neurophysiological and neurovascular principles. This integrated framework establishes a powerful, scalable platform for probing neurovascular coupling in health and disease, with significant potential to elucidate pathophysiological mechanisms in neurological and psychiatric disorders.
