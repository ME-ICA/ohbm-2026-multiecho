Introduction

The primary motor cortex (M1) plays a pivotal role in human movement control, and alterations in its functionality, such as during aging or neurodegeneration, heavily affect quality-of-live. Large-scale topography in M1 has classically been described as somatotopic in which large body parts such as the hand and the face are represented in a predictable spatial order. However, while this organization is evident at a large scale topographic scale, that is for the representation of body parts, its organization is less clear for the representation of single fingers. For small-scale topography, evidence from invasive electrophysiology and high-resolution neuroimaging has revealed substantial deviations from a simple linear finger map at finer spatial scales. These findings suggest that the functional organization of M1 cannot be fully explained by finger somatotopy alone, but that a combination of topographic- and movement-specific organizational schemes converges.

M1 organization may hence reflect both motor actions and muscle synergies, such that identical digits may be represented multiple times, depending on the movement performed. A recent ultra-high resolution fMRI study has demonstrated action-specific finger representations within M1, providing empirical evidence for the theory of both topographic- and movement-specific organizational schemes representing hand movements in human M1. However, whereas this study only investigated opening and closing movements and only using visual cues for movement tracking, we here extend a similar paradigm by also tracking lateral movements, and using an MR-compatible data glove for precise kinematic sampling. In the present study, we therefore systematically investigated whether distinct fine motor actions involving the same set of fingers evoke differential activation patterns within the human primary motor cortex at submillimeter resolution.

Methods

Participants: We measured and analyzed 7T fMRI data from n = 15 healthy, right-handed adults (age range: 21–33 years; 6 female). For the present abstract, preliminary results from two representative subjects are shown.

MRI protocol:

MRI data were acquired on a Siemens 7T MAGNETOM system (Magdeburg, Germany) using an 8Tx/32Rx head coil. High-resolution anatomical images were obtained using a 3D MP2RAGE sequence at 0.5 mm isotropic resolution. Functional data were acquired using a 2D gradient-echo EPI sequence (TR = 1.5 s, TE = 26 ms, voxel size = 0.9 mm isotropic, multiband factor = 2, GRAPPA factor of 3 , no slice gap), with the slab (36 slices) covering M1 bilaterally.

Experimental design:

Participants performed a set of fine motor tasks in a block design consisting of 12 s of movement followed by 15 s of rest. Tasks included (i) finger tip touching of either D1-D2, D1-D3, D1-D4 or D1-D5 with alternating Open–Close movements (3 s Open, 3 s Close), (ii) all-finger Open-Close movements, and (iii) all-finger lateral spread movements. Movements were visually cued and performed with the dominant (right) hand. Participants wore a 7T-compatible optical fiber glove (5DT) during task performance; these data will be used in subsequent analyses to model movement-related kinematics precisely, taking the individual variability in movement performance into account.

Analyses:

Functional data were analyzed using SPM12. Preprocessing included slice timing correction, rigid-body motion correction, and co-registration using ANTs to each participant’s anatomical image; no spatial normalization was performed. First-level analyses were conducted in native space using a general linear model (GLM). Separate regressors were modeled for each movement condition, including Open and Close phases for individual finger-tip touching conditions (D2 (Index) - D4 (Little)), as well as all-finger movements (All Fist and open, All Lateral spread). Each regressor was contrasted against an implicit rest baseline, yielding condition-specific T-contrasts (e.g., Open > baseline, Close > baseline). These contrasts were used to analyze task-related activation patterns at the single-subject level using an uncorrected voxel-level threshold of p < 0.05, and a cluster threshold of 3. Group-level analyses will be performed after the precise architecture of individual-level motor maps is understood.

Results

Task-related activation was observed in contralateral M1 in both representative subjects. Finger tip touching (Touch, Back) elicited focal activation clusters along the anterior bank of the central sulcus, consistent with engagement of M1. Contrasting Open and Close movement phases revealed partially overlapping but spatially distinct activation clusters within contralateral M1. All-finger movements produced broader activation patterns compared to finger tip touching. Fist - Open movements engaged extended regions of M1, while lateral spread movements recruited additional, spatially distinct cortical patches. Across subjects, different movement types involving the same digits consistently activated non-identical regions within M1. These preliminary single-subject results demonstrate that distinct fine motor actions yield multiple distinct finger-specific maps within M1, sensitive to opening closing, and lateral movement, motivating tracking the activation of these maps using data glove-driven kinematics that we recorded. 

Conclusion

Preliminary single-subject analyses indicate that fine motor actions involving the same set of fingers elicit distinct spatial activation patterns within contralateral M1. Rather than engaging a single unified finger representation, different movement types including finger tip touching, fist–opening, and lateral finger spread recruit partially overlapping but also distinct cortical regions. These findings support the notion that motor cortex organization reflects motor actions in addition to somatotopic finger representations across different movement dimensions, extending a previous study. Future work will extend these observations using group-level and quantitative analyses to further characterize action-specific motor representations.
