Introduction

Insomnia is widely recognized as a disorder of central nervous system "hyperarousal." However, most prior research has focused on unimodal abnormalities, such as elevated EEG beta power or altered fMRI functional connectivity, rarely exploring cross-modal dynamic interactions, particularly the changes in Neurovascular Coupling (NVC) mechanisms during the wake-to-sleep transition. NVC represents the temporal synchronization between neuronal electrical activity and the hemodynamic response, serving as an important mechanism for maintaining cerebral metabolic homeostasis.^1

The core hypothesis of this study is that the healthy brain modulates NVC lag time through specific mechanisms during the transition from wakefulness to sleep; in contrast, patients with insomnia may exhibit impaired or abnormal regulation due to central nervous system hyperarousal or autonomic dysregulation.

By utilizing simultaneous EEG-fMRI acquisition, we aimed to quantify the "Lag Time" between 116 whole-brain regions of interest (ROIs) and 25 EEG channels. This study aims to answer two key questions:

  1. Does the sleep state fundamentally alter neurovascular synchronization?
  2. Do insomnia patients exhibit specific pathological features during this state transition?

Methods

The study included a total of 26 participants, divided into two groups: 15 Healthy Controls (HC) and 11 individuals diagnosed with Insomnia (INS). All participants underwent simultaneous EEG-fMRI scanning at National Yang-Ming University. The experimental protocol consisted of two distinct sessions:

  • Wake: A 10-minute Eyes-Open Awake (OA) resting-state session.
  • Sleep: A 90-minute sleep session (covering NREM and REM stages).

EEG and fMRI data were acquired simultaneously.

  • MRI Acquisition: Data were acquired using a Siemens 3T MRI system. Structural images were obtained using a high-resolution 3D-MPRAGE sequence (1 mm³ isotropic voxel size). Functional images (fMRI) were recorded using an EPI sequence (TR = 2100 ms, TE = 30 ms, slice thickness = 3.4 mm).
  • EEG Acquisition: EEG data were recorded simultaneously using a Brain Products system. A 25-channel electrode cap (10-20 system) was used with a sampling rate of 5000 Hz to facilitate gradient artifact removal. The reference electrode was placed at FCz.

EEG and fMRI data were preprocessed independently.

  • fMRI Preprocessing: Performed using the DPABI toolbox (based on SPM12).^2 The pipeline included: removal of the first 5 time points, slice timing correction, realignment (Friston 24-parameter model), spatial normalization (to MNI space), nuisance regression (CompCor for WM and CSF signals), band-pass filtering (0.01–0.1 Hz), and spatial smoothing (4 mm FWHM). Time series were extracted from 116 ROIs defined by the AAL3 atlas.
  • EEG Preprocessing: Performed using the EEGLAB toolbox.^3 The pipeline included: removal of MR gradient and ballistocardiogram (BCG) artifacts, downsampling to 250 Hz, band-pass filtering (0.3–50 Hz), and artifact removal (eye blinks and muscle noise) using Independent Component Analysis (ICA/ICLabel). After data quality control, 12 Healthy Controls (HC) and 8 Insomnia patients (INS) were retained for the final analysis.

To bridge the temporal disparity between rapid neuronal oscillations and slow hemodynamic responses, we employed Ensemble Empirical Mode Decomposition (EEMD)^4:

  1. Envelope Extraction: The upper amplitude envelope of the EEG signal was extracted.
  2. ISO Reconstruction: The envelope was segmented time-locked to the fMRI TRs and decomposed. The last three Intrinsic Mode Functions (IMFs) were summed to reconstruct the Infra-Slow Oscillation (ISO) component (< 0.1 Hz), representing the primary neural driver of NVC.

We quantified the interaction between the 116 ROIs and 25 EEG channels:

  • Upsampling: The fMRI BOLD signals were upsampled to 250 Hz.
  • Lag-Dependent Correlation: Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between the EEG slow component and the BOLD signal.
  • Lag Definition: The time lag corresponding to the maximum correlation coefficient value closest to the zero point was identified.

Given the high dimensionality and non-Gaussian distribution (high skewness and kurtosis) of the data, we implemented a robust statistical pipeline:

  1. Data Cleaning: We applied 1% Winsorization and established physiologically plausible bounds (+/- 15 seconds) to limit the impact of extreme outliers.
  2. Robust Modeling (RLM): A Robust Linear Model was established as "Lag ~ Group × State + Distance", incorporating physical distance as a covariate.
  3. Effect Estimation: Estimated Marginal Means (EMMs) were calculated to evaluate the interactions.

Results

RLM analysis revealed that physical distance had significant predictive power for Lag Time (P < 0.001), confirming the necessity of incorporating spatial factors into NVC analysis. The cleaned Lag Time data converged between -2.27s and 2.80s, aligning with physiological expectations.

The analysis also revealed a significant "Group × State" interaction (P < 0.05), characterized by the following dynamics:

Wake State: No significant difference was found between groups. Both Controls (EMM = 0.242s) and Insomnia patients (EMM = 0.255s) exhibited a slight positive lag.

Sleep State: A significant divergence occurred.

Healthy Controls: Lag Time significantly decreased and converged near zero (EMM = -0.006s), indicating a state of "near-zero lag alignment."
Insomnia Patients: This group exhibited a drastic downward trend, crossing zero into the negative range (EMM = -0.042s), characterizing an "overshoot" phenomenon.

Conclusion

Our results define the NVC characteristics of healthy sleep as a transition from variable time lags during wakefulness to "near-zero synchronization." This suggests that the healthy brain utilizes a phase-locking mechanism to maximize the efficiency of metabolic waste clearance—potentially via the glymphatic system. This interpretation is supported by recent simultaneous EEG-fMRI evidence demonstrating that the temporal coupling between neuronal slow waves and hemodynamic oscillations acts as a mechanical pump to drive cerebrospinal fluid flow.^5

In contrast, insomnia patients exhibited a unique "hyper-reactivity" pattern during sleep, characterized by a pathological vascular "overshoot" in response to neuronal signals. This finding challenges the traditional view of insomnia as a condition of hypo-function and implies the presence of residual sympathetic tone or hyperactive vasomotor mechanisms. Collectively, these observations propose a novel perspective: insomnia is not merely a disorder of electrophysiological arousal but also involves a dynamic dysregulation of neurovascular coupling. This micro-level dysregulation likely serves as the pathophysiological basis for subjective complaints of "non-restorative sleep," highlighting the critical role of NVC dynamics in sleep quality.
