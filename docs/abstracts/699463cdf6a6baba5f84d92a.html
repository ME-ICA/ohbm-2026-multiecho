Introduction

The critical brain hypothesis suggests that neural dynamics operate in a regime between order and disorder, characterized by rich yet constrained patterns of activity.1 In this regime, the brain does not explore its full state space. Instead, neural activity is organized around a restricted set of recurrent, low dimensional, metastable patterns with weakly attractive properties, sometimes referred to as ghost attractors. 2,3 These configurations allow the system to balance two functional demands. They support flexibility because trajectories can move freely between patterns and reconfigure rapidly, but also provide stability because the weakly attractive structure of these patterns anchors the flow of activity and keeps it coherent. Nearby regions in state space are therefore preferentially visited and traversed, enabling the system to explore diverse functional configurations without losing the stable grounding needed for reliable cognitive function. These metastable states have been linked to resting state networks2,3 and implicated in life-span-related and neurodegenerative changes.3 In the human brain, they are conventionally either extracted from resting state activity such as r-fMRI data2 or alternatively modelled non-linearly.3 We here recover the signatures of these metastable states from linearly modelled network control theoretical trajectories, and use them to characterize and compare brain state specifics in trajectories.

Methods

Network Control Theory (NCT) is a powerful methodological toolbox based on a dynamical systems perspective of how a network (e.g. the human brain) maintains and traverses between various functional states constrained by its structure.4 From a cognitive neuroscience perspective, it is typically used to investigate the energetic cost associated with transitions between pairs of cognitively relevant functional brain states or the propensity of specific regions to control these dynamics.4,5 Inputs will typically be a structural connectome that constrains dynamics, a pair of brain states to transition between, as well as a control matrix constraining which regions are allowed to control dynamics to which extent. Typical outputs will be an energetic cost term associated with these dynamics (per region and/or global), as well as a modelled trajectory of intermediary brain states between start and target state. The precise nature of intermediary brain states will, among other variables, depend on whether the trajectory is optimized for directness between start and target state, or for energetical efficiency.

Here, we constrain dynamics through a group-consensus connectome taken from the genetically unrelated subset of 100 Human Connectome Project participants, parcellated into 400 cortical (Schaefer) and 16 subcortical (Tian) regions. We conduct full network control theoretical analyses4 between pairs of 116 meta-analytically derived and cognitively relevant brain states (Neurosynth database, informed by the cognitive atlas, parcellated accordingly). We optimize either for directness of the trajectory (“direct trajectory”) or for cost-effectiveness (“balanced trajectory). For each trajectory, we extract a characteristic "state of interest” from the energetically balanced trajectory based on the intermediary step t at which it is maximally distant from the direct trajectory. We k-means cluster the activity vectors of the 116*115 states of interests using cosine distance, to reveal 4 cluster centroids corresponding to the patterns of covariation of 4 proposed ghost attractor states (Figure 1a,b). Statistical assessments of spatial similarity (cosine distance) between ghost attractor patterns, as well as similarity of cortical patterns to canonical cortical gradients, are assessed through spatially aware permutations of individual centroid maps (Morans Spectral Randomization as in the BrainSpace toolbox, 1000 permutations). Individual trajectories can then characterized by assigning each intermediary step to the attractor to which it corresponds the most based on the similarity of its spatial co-activation pattern to the cluster centroids. This allows for derivation of Hidden Markov Model (HMM)-style metrics per trajectory (fractional occupancy (FO) per attractor, dwell time (DT) per attractor), and thus direct comparison of modelled trajectories for distinct start and target states (Figure 1c). We derive HMM-style metrics for the trajectories of the subset of 10 most energetically expensive start states, and compare it statistically to a null distribution of 10 randomly selected start states (permutation-based, with a null distribution of 1000 randomly selected sets of 10 start states). Additionally, we briefly explore characteristics of the ghost attractors themselves: Should they be accurate representations of metastability in the brain, they should be weakly attractive, i.e., it should take the brain significantly less energy to transition into them than out of them. We again use network control theoretical analyses to investigate this, calculating energetic cost of transitions from each of the 116 meta-analytically derived brain states into each attractor vs. out of each attractor and to each of the 116 states. We compare energy into vs. out of each ghost attractor statistically via a paired-sample t test. All statistical tests are declared significant at the level of p < 0.05.

Results

We project all 116 functional states and all trajectories between them into a shared principal component (PC) space, and show that the energetically cheaper “balanced trajectories” are associated with a more curved, that is, indirect, trajectory in PC space compared to the more energetically expensive “direct trajectories” (Figure 1b). Clustering of the intermediary “states of interest” reveals 4 distinct patterns of whole-brain covariation (Figure 2a), of which patterns #1 and #3 are significantly anti-correlated (cosine similarity of -0.63, p < 0.001), patterns #1 and #4 are significantly correlated (cosine similarity of 0.11, p < 0.05), and patterns #2 and 3 are significantly anti-correlated (cosine similarity of -0.51, p < 0.01). Cortical regions of pattern #4 are also significantly correlated with the first canonical principal cortical gradient which delineates unimodal from transmodal regions (cosine similarity of 0.43, p < 0.001) and significantly anti-correlated with the second canonical principal cortical gradient which delineates motor areas from visual areas (cosine similarity of -0.42, p < 0.01).

Across all 116 states, we show that transitioning out of the proposed attractor patterns is significantly more energetically expensive than transitioning into them (paired sample one-sided t-tests, t(115) = 7.22, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = .67), satisfying the direction of energetic cost that would be expected from metastable ghost attractors with weak gravitational pull.

Investigating HMM-style characteristics of all trajectories of the top 10 most energetically costly start states, we show that energetic expense is significantly related to a distinct utilization of the attractor landscape: Compared to randomly selected sets of 10 start states, trajectories of the top 10 most energetically costly start states employ co-activation patterns related to ghost attractors #1 and #2 significantly less, and those related to ghost attractor pattern #3 significantly more (Figure 2b). This is reflected both in significantly smaller fractional occupancies in attractor pattern #1 and #2 and greater FOs in attractor pattern #3, as well as lower dwell times in attractor patterns #1 and #2 and greater DTs in attractor pattern #3.

Conclusion

We extract distinct spatial patterns of co-variation with ghost-attractor-like properties from network control theoretical trajectories between various different brain states. Patterns inhabit energetical properties expected from metastable attractors, and can be used to describe brain-state-specific differences in utilization of the state space. Ghost attractor #4 can also be significantly related to the first and second principal cortical gradients, further indicating functional significance. Our results indicate that metastable patterns can be recovered from structurally constrained linearly modelled (and thus simplified and approximated) dynamics

Benefits of this new approach are twofold: From a network control theoretical perspective, the approach allows for closer investigations of the trajectories modelled via NCT, i.e. the path of intermediary states the brain takes between start and target state. While past network control theoretical analyses have been primarily concerned with examinations of energetic cost or controllability, this pipeline allows analyses of the temporal aspect of network control theoretical dynamics. It will thus also enable direct comparisons of state-specific characteristics of trajectories, interindividual variability of the intermediate states, or group-level or pathological differences in trajectories or attractor landscape utilization. From a dynamical systems perspective, the approach allows for extraction of attractor characteristics and dynamics in a computationally relatively in-expensive manner. It also enables analyses of energetic cost without the necessity for nonlinear modelling, thus reducing computational cost. Generally, this approach combines concepts from various domains of dynamical systems theory, allowing for joint investigations into metastability and controlled dynamics in the brain based purely on structural connectivity and linearly modelled dynamics.
