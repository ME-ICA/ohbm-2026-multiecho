Introduction

Fibromyalgia (FM) is a chronic pain disease characterized by persistent pain at multiple locations on the body. Symptoms include widespread pain, cognitive deficits, fatigue, and often comorbidity anxiety and depression1. Despite its prevalence, FM is poorly defined and has no definitive biomarkers, making it difficult to diagnose using laboratory techniques, clinical tests, or imaging methods2,3. Currently FM is identified via questionnaires which assess the location, duration, and severity of a patient’s pain. However, symptoms often overlap with other chronic pain diseases, complicating the accuracy of diagnosis2,3. As it currently stands, there is a great need to identify a clear biomarker for FM to aid in diagnosis, facilitate future research, and help patients get the treatment they need faster. 

            Although FM has no known biomarkers, there is evidence that the anatomical properties of a patient’s central nervous systems are altered. Prior neuroimaging studies in our lab, noted that functional MRI data from FM participants more often required more extensive pre-processing than data from healthy controls (HC) (unpublished observations). This suggests subtle but detectable differences in image in MR images of the brainstem and spinal cord of FM groups, further implying potential differences in anatomical features. 

Recent advances in neuroimaging analysis highlight convolutional neural networks (CNNs) as a promising avenue for identifying such biomarkers. Applying CNNs to fibromyalgia offers a novel way to identify subtle alterations in the spinal cord or brain that may serve as objective biomarkers. This approach can help overcome current diagnostic limitations and improve the specificity of FM identification4. Therefore, we hypothesize that there are subtle differences in the anatomical dimensions and tissue properties in the spinal cord and brainstem in FM patients that can be identified using CNNs.

Methods

This study explored possible changes in spinal cord volume, by analyzing existing MR images from our lab’s database. The dataset used for initial testing consisted of MRI scans from 15 participants diagnosed with FM and 15 HCs. All participants in the study were females between the ages of 25-65, age matched ~3 years. HCs were not diagnosed with any neurological, psychological, or chronic pain disorders and were not taking regular medications while FM participants remained on their regular medications. Images were acquired using Queen’s University’s 3 tesla MRI system (Siemens Prisma), using T2-weighted single-shot fast spin-echo imaging with an initial resolution of 2mm x 1.5mm x 1.5mm and interpolated to 1mm3 voxels during preprocessing for subsequent fMRI studies. MRI data was preprocessed using the Pantheon software which includes motion and slice-timing correction, spatial normalization, and removal of physiological noise.

            The resulting time-averaged MR images were analyzed by a coarse localization CNN which identified coordinates near the spinal cord creating an approximate bounding region of the cord’s location. The model consisted of two convolutional layers which applied small filters that detected spatial features such as edges and textures which were followed by max pooling layers which reduced image size by only including the most important features. The final output of this model was a map that identified approximate regions of the image where spinal cord tissue was located (Fig 1. A). Images were cropped around the identified cord-containing regions, reducing the size of the dataset before segmentation to improve efficiency and reduced training time (Fig 1. B). Cropped images were subsequently processed by a second CNN, trained for voxel-wise segmentation of spinal cord tissue. The network utilized parallel convolutional pathways using various kernel sizes to enable detection of features at different spatial scales. Larger kernels with a 7x7 pixel size detected broad structural contours while smaller ones with a 3x3 pixel size detected finer details. A final 1x1 convolutional layer produced voxel wise probabilities of spinal cord tissue via a sigmoid activation function. This produced a final output map with a probability measure from 0-1 of each image voxel including the spinal cord. Voxels with a probability over the cutoff of 0.7 were recorded as spinal cord. 

Both neural networks were trained using supervised learning where the model evaluated its prediction against a labeled training image and adjusted its prediction to improve accuracy4. Training images were manually labeled on 12 MRI datasets using the software ITK-Snap, yielding 1620 two-dimensional image slices in total for model training. These were separated into 1134 training and 486 testing images to ensure overfitting did not occur when measuring accuracy with the test set. 

            Model accuracy was evaluated using the Dice similarity coefficient which quantifies the overlap between the model’s predicted mask and the manually identified spinal cord mask from testing datasets (Equation 1)4.

            To ensure consistent volume comparisons across participants, spinal cord lengths were equalized prior to analysis. For each image, the model identified the superior starting point of the cord at the base of the medulla oblongata and in cases where this was inaccurate, the starting location was manually corrected. Volumes were then measured over a standardized cord length, determined by the shortest usable cord length in the dataset. Following segmentation, spinal cord volume measures were extracted from each participant and mean volume and standard deviation were calculated for the FM and HC groups. These values were compared using Welch’s t-test to assess whether spinal cord volume differed significantly between FM and HC groups.  

Results

           Spinal cord volumes were compared between FM and HC participants following segmentation and length adjustment. The spinal cords were adjusted to a length of 124mm. Segmentation began at the base of the medulla oblongata and extended to the base of the cervical spinal cord, including the cervical enlargement. The average spinal cord volume was highly similar across groups with HCs showing a mean volume of 8,790 mm3 (SD = 816) while the FM group showed a near identical average volume of 8772 mm3 (SD = 1105.7). A Welch’s t-test was performed to test for differences between the mean spinal cord volume in each group and yielded a p-value of 0.95. This indicates no statistical significance between the volume of FM and HC spinal cords within the dataset.

            After segmentation, the cropped mask was reconstructed to match the shape of the original image, producing the final segmented spinal cord image shown in Figure 2. Model performance was evaluated using Dice similarity coefficient which assesses similarity between the model’s prediction and the labeled testing set. The segmentation network achieved a Dice coefficient of 0.91, indicating a high degree of spatial overlap. This demonstrates that the model produced reliable spinal cord segmentations suitable for volumetric analysis.

Conclusion

            This study developed and applied a two-stage convolutional neural network pipeline to crop and segment the spinal cord to compare cord volumes in individuals with FM and healthy controls. Despite the use of a reliable segmentation model, demonstrated by a Dice coefficient of 0.91, the analysis revealed no measurable differences in the volume of spinal cord between groups which is further supported by a non-significant Welch’s t-test (p = 0.95). While the results of this study suggest that spinal cord morphology captured by MRI analysis does not differ between healthy and FM participants, further analysis is still necessary. To better understand the differences that required additional preprocessing in FM participants, future work will include larger cohorts and analyze the cord shape at specific regions including the cervical enlargement to better understand the anatomy of FM participant’s spinal cords. These expansions have the potential to reveal finer structural variations that were not captured by the overall cord volume. Additionally, future work will aim to improve the segmentation model itself to further enhance the accuracy of its predictions and reduce the risk of incorrect voxel labeling. These refinements can include expanding the training dataset to incorporate more diverse anatomical examples or increasing the model’s complexity to capture more subtle features of the cord. 

            These findings contribute to ongoing efforts to identify biomarkers of fibromyalgia. While spinal cord volume alone may not distinguish the disease from healthy populations, the development of an accurate segmentation tool establishes a foundation for future studies investigating features of the spinal cord.
