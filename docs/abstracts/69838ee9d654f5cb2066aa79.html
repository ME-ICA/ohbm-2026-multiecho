Introduction

Polymicrogyria (PMG) is a malformation of cortical development characterized by excessive cortical folding and abnormal layering1. PMG is frequently observed in patients with drug-resistant epilepsy and is commonly associated with neurodevelopmental delay, motor impairments, and cognitive deficits. Despite its clinical importance, PMG remains difficult to characterise reliably due to its heterogeneous radiological appearance, variable anatomical distribution, and the limited availability of curated neuroimaging datasets.

Previous structural MRI-based studies have predominantly focused on pediatric cohorts, comparing against healthy controls, or too-high-complexity machine learning models trained on limited sample sizes2. As a result, it remains unclear whether routinely acquired clinical MRI scans contain sufficient structural information to differentiate PMG from other epilepsy-related abnormalities.

In this study, we investigated whether regional cortical surface-based features can distinguish PMG cases from non-PMG epilepsy cases in a clinical cohort.

Methods

Population

We included 183 T1-weighted MRI scans from 162 unique patients evaluated for epilepsy (77 females; mean ± standard deviation (SD) age at acquisition: 25.8 ± 17.4 years). Of these, 110 scans (49 females; 24.7 ± 15.9 years) were reported to contain radiological evidence of PMG based on clinical radiology reports. The non-PMG group (37 females; 27.5 ± 19.6 years) consisted of scans in which PMG-related terminology appeared in the radiology report, but a subsequent double-rater confirmed the absence of PMG. Of these, 56 cases had other abnormalities and 17 were reported as having a normal MRI. The overall population distribution is shown in Fig. 1(c). MRI scans were acquired in routine clinical practice and retrospectively collected from the Eastern Region of Denmark, encompassing multiple scanner protocols, two magnetic field strengths (B), and three vendors. While no manual quality ratings were available, signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) was computed in white and grey matter as an objective measure of image quality. The mean ± SD SNR was 11.9 ± 2.7 in white matter and 7.9 ± 1.8 in grey matter, indicating that a variability in scan quality could contribute to errors in cortical surface reconstruction and feature extraction. Fig. 1(a, b) shows example cases from each group.

Method

Two brain MRI cortical features were examined: thickness and gyrification. Gyrification was quantified using the gyrification index (GI), defined as the ratio between the cortical surface area and the area of a smooth outer surface that encloses the cortex. To obtain these features, cortical surface reconstruction was performed using FastSurfer (https://deep-mi.org/research/fastsurfer/), which also enabled the calculation of the local GI (LGI) values at each cortical surface vertex3. For each scan, regional cortical thickness values were obtained from the Desikan–Killiany parcellation. Vertex-wise LGI values were averaged across the same cortical regions, yielding an average regional LGI. These regional metrics were used as the primary features.

A random forest classifier (https://scikit-learn.org/) was trained using a 5-fold cross-validation strategy with 10 randomised initialisations, to distinguish PMG from non-PMG cases. Folds maintained proportional group representation, and scans from the same patient were never included in both training and test sets simultaneously. Three feature sets were evaluated: cortical thickness, LGI, and a combination of thickness and LGI features. Age, sex, and B were included as additional features in all models to account for demographic and acquisition-related variability.

Model performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC), evaluated across the stratified folds and randomised iterations.

Feature importance was computed using the default Gini importance. To further assess the feature importance of the best-performing model, a second data split was performed where 75% of the data (N = 79 PMG / 58 non-PMG) was used for training, and the model was tested on 25 hold out cases with PMG and the remaining non-PMG cases. Ten randomised iterations were performed for the non-PMG distribution. Manual lesion localisation from clinical radiology reports was available for these 25 PMG cases, enabling comparison of model-derived feature importance with known lesion locations.

Statistical analysis

Group differences in demographic variables were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for age and Pearson’s chi-squared tests for sex and B. Associations between patient group and ROI-level cortical thickness and LGI values were examined independently using linear regression models, including age and sex as covariate. Resulting p-values were Bonferroni-corrected for multiple comparisons.

Statistical significance of the top model’s AUC was assessed empirically using a null distribution generated from 1,000 random label permutations to derive a permutation-based p-value.

Results

Group differences

No significant differences in demographic or acquisition-related characteristics were observed between PMG and non-PMG groups (all p-values > 0.05).

ROI-based analyses, adjusted for age, sex, and B, revealed no significant differences in cortical thickness values. For LGI, only three ROIs in the right hemisphere, the caudal middle frontal, middle temporal, and postcentral regions, showed significant differences between PMG and non-PMG groups.

Model Performance

The best-performing model used LGI mean features alone, achieving a mean AUC across folds and iterations of 0.71 ± 0.09. This was followed by the combined LGI and thickness features model (AUC = 0.67 ± 0.10), and the thickness-only model (AUC = 0.58 ± 0.10). Mean ROC-AUCs are shown in Fig. 2(a). For the best scoring model, the LGI-only model, performance was significantly greater than expected by chance, as assessed against a null distribution (p = 0.001).

Assessment of feature importance

Additionally, model feature importance analysis was performed on the LGI-only model using the Gini importance measure. As shown in Fig. 2(b), the right hemisphere exhibited slightly higher importance values, highlighting two of the three ROIs that were significantly different between groups: the postcentral and middle temporal.

To further evaluate the relationship between model feature importance and prevalent lesion locations, we tested the model on a held-out subset of 25 PMG cases. Furthermore, based on the 25 hold-out cases a lesion probability map was computed, shown in Fig. 2(c). The model trained on 80% of the data had similar performance (AUC = 0.69 ± 0.07, p = 0.007) as the model trained via cross-validation on the full dataset. A feature importance analysis of the second model shown in Fig. 2(d) highlights regions that also corresponded to recurrent PMG lesion locations in the probability map, shown in Fig. 2(c). Additionally, the feature importance maps based on the second model, were comparable to those obtained using the full dataset, supporting the robustness of the identified key regions.

Conclusion

In this study, we investigated whether cortical surface–based features, specifically cortical thickness and LGI, could distinguish PMG from epilepsy cases without PMG in a cohort of clinically acquired brain MRI scans. Our results demonstrated that LGI features alone provided the best model performance and could significantly predict PMG status with an AUC = 0.71 ± 0.09. Using standard feature importance measures for random forest models, we could also show that the model identified relevant PMG regions in the right hemisphere, postcentral and around the Sylvian fissure that corresponded with recurrent lesion locations in a manually annotated subset of our cohort. 

A major factor influencing model performance was the wide age range of our cohort. Cortical thickness and gyrification follow distinct developmental trajectories: thickness generally decreases from childhood through adulthood, while gyrification is rather stable from early youth and much less variable with time than cortical thickness4,5. Age-related variability, combined with lesion size, likely contributed to recurrent false negatives, particularly in age outliers, or cases with larger lesions, which may be further affected by less accurate cortical surface reconstructions. 

It is also important to note that cortical surface reconstruction and feature extraction were performed using tools primarily designed for healthy brains. Although FastSurfer provides a faster and more robust implementation than FreeSurfer, reconstructions in pathological brains e.g. exhibiting PMG, and in clinical-quality MRIs, are less accurate, potentially reducing sensitivity in certain regions. The moderate SNR values observed in our dataset further reflect typical clinical imaging conditions, where variability in tissue contrast can introduce additional noise into surface reconstruction and LGI estimation, thereby amplifying reconstruction errors in complex or malformed cortical areas. These limitations also help explain performance in our non-PMG group, which represents a clinically realistic test population, including cases with other abnormalities as well as scans reported as normal. While normal MRIs might be expected to yield lower error rates, the false-positive rate across iterations for these cases remained high (55%), with 75% of misclassified cases acquired at 1.5T, a field strength representing only 16% of the dataset. Although B was included as a predictive feature, its influence was likely secondary to the cortical features extracted from the images, which remained inherently sensitive to variations in image quality.

In summary, LGI-based cortical features from routine MRI can distinguish PMG from other cases suspected of presenting it. But age-related cortical trajectories, lesion characteristics, and limitations of standard reconstruction tools should be considered when interpreting these analyses. Despite these challenges, surface-based morphometry remains valuable, and future work with larger, diverse datasets and advanced modeling approaches may improve detection and characterization of cortical malformations.
