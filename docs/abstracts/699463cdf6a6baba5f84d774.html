Introduction

A key step in numerical development is acquiring arithmetic fact knowledge - learning and retrieving arithmetical facts from memory (e.g., 5x5). This step enables children to solve arithmetical problems more efficiently and progress to more advanced mathematics. During the transition from finger-counting to fact-retrieval, children aged 7 to 9 show increased hippocampal activation. Moreover, hippocampal connectivity with prefrontal and parietal regions was stronger in children who demonstrated higher retrieval fluency.^1 Interestingly, both hippocampal volume and functional connectivity with basal ganglia and prefrontal regions have been found to predict individual performance gains following math tutoring.^2

For some children, however, learning mathematics is particularly challenging. Developmental dyscalculia (DD) is a learning disability attributed to deficient numerical-arithmetical skills and dysfunction of the neural network for number processing. Affected children show pronounced difficulties in acquiring and retrieving arithmetical facts. Previous research has reported abnormal hippocampal activation and connectivity, as well as reduced grey and white matter volumes, in DD children. ^3,4

Recent evidence highlights the involvement of the hippocampus in arithmetical learning and children with DD exhibit atypical functional and structural characteristics of the hippocampus. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the relationship of the hippocampal volume and arithmetical abilities in children with DD and their typically developing peers. First, we examine structural differences between the groups. We then explore the relationship between arithmetic fact knowledge and hippocampal volume. Because of the hippocampus’s role in visuospatial reasoning and the close link between number processing and visuospatial abilities, our final aim is to assess how visuospatial abilities contribute to the relationship between hippocampal structure and arithmetic performance.

Methods

We collected behavioural and structural magnetic resonance imaging (sMRI) data from 3rd- and 4th-grade children with developmental dyscalculia (mean age = 9.9 years). Age-matched typically developing children with no history of neurological or developmental disorders served as the control group. Behavioural measures included assessments of various numerical abilities (ZAREKI-R), math fluency (additions and subtractions, HRT 1-4), and visuospatial abilities (e.g. FEW-3, WISC-V). In addition, IQ, reading, working memory and attention measures were obtained to control for common comorbidities and potential confounds.

Structural MRI data were acquired on a 3T GE scanner using a T1-weighted BRAVO sequence. Preprocessing was performed with the computational anatomy toolbox (CAT) pipeline, adjusted for paediatric data. Hippocampal volumes, including subregional measures, were extracted based on the CoBra Atlas. The subregions included CA1, merged CA2/CA3, CA4 (including the dentate gyrus), and the subiculum.

To address our first research question, investigating hippocampal volumes between children with dyscalculia and controls, we pooled the present dataset with a previously collected cohort of 3rd- and 4th-grade children to increase statistical power. Group comparisons were conducted using independent-samples t-tests on data from 27 dyscalculic and 54 typically developing children. For the second and third research question, partial correlation and multiple regression analyses were performed based on 36 datasets.

Results

For the groupwise comparison of hippocampal volumes between children with and without developmental dyscalculia, independent-samples t-tests revealed significant group differences in hippocampal volume, with typically developing children showing significantly larger volumes in both the right (t(79) = 3.53, p = .001) and the left (t(79) = 3.08, p = .003) hippocampus. 

Regarding the relationship between behavioural arithmetic skills and hippocampal volume, we found a significant positive correlation between arithmetical fact knowledge and hippocampal volume (r = .51, p < .05). In particular, the CA1 (r = .54, p < .05), and CA4 (r = .58, p < .01) subregions of the right hippocampus were significantly associated with higher arithmetic fluency.

Finally, for our third research question addressing whether visuospatial skills influence the relationship between hippocampus and numerical abilities, a multiple regression analysis revealed a significant association between numerical abilities, hippocampal volume and visuo-spatial abilities (F(7,15) = 18.81, p < .001, R2= .85).  There was also a significant effect of visuo-spatial abilities on numerical performance (p > .01). However, the interaction between hippocampus volume and visuospatial abilities was not significant.

Conclusion

Children with developmental dyscalculia exhibit reduced hippocampal volumes compared to typically developing peers. Further analyses of the association between arithmetic fact knowledge and hippocampal volume revealed a positive correlation, particularly with the CA1 and CA4 subregions. Notably, CA1 has been linked to retrieval of verbal information, whereas CA4 has been associated with free recall of information. Moreover, we found that visuospatial abilities predict numerical performance, but did not moderate the relationship between hippocampal volume and numerical abilities. These results hint towards the involvement of the verbal component of the hippocampus in arithmetical fact retrieval. Moreover, the difficulties children with developmental dyscalculia experience in learning and retrieving arithmetical facts may also be connected to neuroanatomical differences within the hippocampus.
