Introduction

Brain functional asymmetry is a fundamental principle of neural organization, underlying hemispheric lateralization and specialization of diverse cognitive processes1,2. Many cognitive functions undergo profound maturation during childhood, but when and how these cognitive functions and lateralization emerge and evolve throughout this period remains poorly understood1. In addition, alterations in asymmetry during the developmental period have been associated with multiple neuropsychiatric and neurodevelopmental disorders, such as autism spectrum disorder and schizophrenia1. This underscores the importance of charting typical asymmetry trajectories to provide a reference for identifying deviations associated with atypical neurodevelopment. Cerebral blood flow (CBF) delivers oxygen and glucose, the principal brain substrates for energy production, to different brain regions. The tight coupling between regional CBF and local cerebral metabolism makes CBF a reasonable surrogate of metabolism in both resting and task-activated brain states. Thus, CBF has been widely used as a sensitive marker of functional development from the perspective of brain physiology3,4. In this study, we aimed to chart developmental trajectories of brain physiological asymmetry across cerebrum and smaller cerebellum during childhood, using high-resolution 2mm3 CBF maps acquired with 3D multi-shot, stack-of-spirals pseudo-continuous arterial spin labeling (pCASL) optimized for the pediatric brain.

Methods

Pediatric subjects and acquisition of perfusion pCASL: A total of 48 typically developing children aged 5-12 years old were recruited in this study, completely for research purposes. Brain MRI scans of children were acquired on a 3T Prisma system with a 32-channel head coil (Siemens Healthineers). High-resolution ASL images were acquired with a 3D multi-shot, stack-of-spirals pCASL sequence. The pCASL MRI parameters were: eight-shot spiral-out acquisition, field of view = 220×220 mm2, matrix = 110×110, in-plane resolution = 2×2 mm2, 64 slices, slice thickness = 2mm, no gap between slices, labeling duration = 2000ms, post labeling delay (PLD) = 1800ms, repetition time = 5s, echo time = 8.3ms, number of controls/labels = 5 pairs, center of labeling slab located between cervical vertebrae C2 and C3. For each pCASL scan, two M0 images were acquired for CBF quantification. In addition, high-resolution T1-weighted (T1w) images with a voxel size of 0.8 mm3 were acquired. Preprocessing: 45 (22 Female/23 Male) out of 48 subjects with good image quality were included in this study. All pCASL images were motion-corrected using the SPM and manually checked. Volumes still affected by movement after realignment have been removed. Regional CBF was calculated following Alsop et al.’s ASL white paper recommendations (CBF maps of representative subjects were shown in Figure 1a). T1w data were preprocessed using FreeSurfer to get individual native space Desikan-Killiany cortical atlases and subcortical segmentations. Cortical atlases and subcortical segmentations were then linearly transformed to CBF image space via M0 image, enabling regional CBF measurements in native CBF image space. Age-related regional CBF and asymmetry changes modeling: For each bilateral cortical or subcortical region, the regional CBF asymmetry index (AI) was calculated as (LH-RH)/(LH+RH), where LH and RH represent the mean CBF values in left and right hemispheric regions, respectively. Age-related regional CBF and AI changes across 5-12 years were estimated using generalized additive models (GAMs), controlling for sex. Brain regions were then grouped into four asymmetry classes: (I) left dominant (AI always >=0); (II) right dominant (AI always <=0); (III) left-to-right shift (AI changing from positive to negative); and (IV) right-to-left shift (AI changing from negative to positive) regions based on the estimated AI trajectories.

Results

Our high-resolution CBF maps at 2mm isotropic enabled tracking of physiological changes not only in the cerebrum but also in structures with much smaller sizes such as the cerebellum, as shown in evident age-related changes in representative subjects aged 5 to 12 years (Figure 1a). The inverted U-shaped trajectory characterized by increasing CBF until around 9 years of age, and gradually declining thereafter (Figure 1b). The childhood regional physiological asymmetry changes across the brain, even for much smaller subcortical gray matter and cerebellar cortex, were also well characterized for the first time, as illustrated in Figure 1c. Figure 2a and b further summarize the developmental asymmetry patterns: most cerebral cortical regions and the cerebellar cortex were found to be either persistently right-dominant or exhibit a right-to-left shift with age, whereas subcortical gray matter structures are largely left-dominant or transition from left to right during development. Finally, hemispheric dominance shift ages (age when the AI trajectory crosses 0, only exists for asymmetry class III and IV) derived from AI trajectories and CBF peak ages (age when the CBF trajectory reaches its maximum value) derived from hemisphere-specific regional age-dependent CBF changes across regions were jointly depicted in Figure 2c, revealing an interesting finding: for regions exhibiting a hemispheric dominance shift during childhood, the previously dominant hemisphere typically reaches its CBF peak earlier than the contralateral side, and the hemispheric dominance shift tends to occur after both hemispheres have reached their perfusion peak. This observation is consistent in all regions with left-to-right asymmetry shift (e.g., many subcortical regions including ventral DC, thalamus, caudate) and in most regions with right-to-left asymmetry shift (e.g., cerebral cortex in general, many regions in the temporal lobe) (Figure 2c).

Conclusion

Our optimized pediatric pCASL protocol achieves 2mm isotropic CBF measurements for pediatric brain, which allows tracking physiological changes and asymmetry patterns not only in detailed cerebral cortical regions but also in the subcortical gray matter and cerebellar cortex with a much smaller size. The CBF peak around 9 years marks the highest metabolic demands of the developing brain. The observed right-to-left hemispheric dominance shift across the cerebral and cerebellar cortex reflects the dynamic reorganization of hemispheric specialization during childhood. Specifically, the shift indicates a transition from early strengthening of function necessary for survival of the species, such as visuospatial ability enhancement, to later maturation of more complex and higher-order functions in language and executive networks2. Right dominance in the cerebellar cortex may play a crucial role in early language processing5, underscoring the cerebellar role in language and cognition beyond motor function. In contrast, subcortical gray matter showing left dominance may reflect modulation by the cortical regions to which they project. The observation that hemispheric dominance shift occurs around or after the previously dominant hemisphere reaches its CBF peak suggests that dominance transitions are closely coupled to the metabolic maturation of that hemisphere. Collectively, these findings suggest that physiological asymmetry dynamics may support underlying shifts in functional specialization during brain maturation.
