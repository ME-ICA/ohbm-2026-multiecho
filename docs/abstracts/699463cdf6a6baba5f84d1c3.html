Introduction

The brain is key to the development and maintenance of chronic pain. However, brain imaging studies have reported a variety of sometimes conflicting findings, largely due to the inclusion of small and heterogeneous datasets. The present study uses the infrastructure of the Enhancing NeuroImaging Genetics through Meta-Analysis (ENIGMA)-Chronic Pain working group (Quidé et al., 2024), to identify common brain alterations existing across different chronic pain conditions. Compared to people who do not experience chronic pain, morphological alterations in regions involved in nociceptive processing and pain modulation (e.g., thalamus, basal ganglia, amygdala, anterior cingulate, insula, prefrontal, and primary and secondary somatosensory cortices) were expected to be common across all pain types included.

Methods

Participants included a maximum of 2,284 individuals reporting chronic pain (experience of pain for over three months; 64% females, mean age (SD) [range]: 51.68 (14.78) [18-94]) and a maximum of 1,941 individuals who did not experience chronic pain (Controls; 52% females, mean age (SD) [range]: 51.05 (16.25) [18-87]) from 36 independent datasets pooled from ENIGMA-Chronic Pain. Chronic pain conditions included back pain (N=400), fibromyalgia (N=344), joint pain/arthritis (N=354), migraines/headaches (N=183), trigeminal neuralgia (N=120), whiplash (N=62), temporomandibular disorder (N=49), traumatic brain injury-related neuropathic pain (N=46), phantom pain (N=44), central neuropathic pain (N=34), abdominal pain (N=33), complex regional pain syndrome (N=25), spinal cord injury-related neuropathic pain (N=23), trigeminal neuropathic pain (N=18), postherpetic neuralgia (N=19), and a mix of unknown/non-specified/miscellaneous conditions (N=524; see Figure 1). All participants underwent 3D T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging. Raw scans were processed using FreeSurfer (recon-all, version 7) and indices of subcortical volumes (16 regions), cortical thickness and surface area (68 regions from Desikan-Killiany atlas) were extracted and quality assessed using standard ENIGMA pipelines. Linear mixed models were used to compare groups (Chronic Pain vs Controls). Age, age squared (to account for non-linear effects of age), sex (males/females), scanning sites (random factor), as well as total intracranial volume for subcortical volume and surface area analyses (not cortical thickness) were included as covariates in all analyses. False-Discovery Rate (FDR) correction was applied to account for the number of regions included in each set of analyses, and indices of effect size (Cohen’s d) were computed. Analyses were repeated after averaging the left and right hemispheres for each region of interest.

Results

Experiencing chronic pain was associated with significantly thinner cortex in 25 regions (-0.16 < Cohen’s d < -0.07), including bilateral inferior/middle/superior temporal, temporal poles, parahippocampal, entorhinal, insular, and fusiform gyri, as well as left lingual and transverse temporal gyri, and right lateral orbitofrontal, caudal and rostral anterior cingulate, pars opercularis and orbitalis, medial orbitofrontal and isthmus cingulate gyri (Figure 2A ). Chronic pain group was also associated with smaller surface area of 18 regions (-0.10 < Cohen’s d < -0.08) including bilateral rostral anterior cingulate, caudal middle frontal, superior frontal, inferior temporal and lateral orbitofrontal gyri as well as the left lateral occipital, middle temporal, cuneus and rostral anterior cingulate, and the right inferior parietal, precentral, lingual and medial orbitofrontal gyri (Figure 2B). No group difference survived FDR correction among measures of subcortical volume (see Figure 2C). 

After averaging left and right hemispheres for each region of interest, experiencing chronic pain was associated with significantly thinner cortex in 19 bilateral regions (-0.17 < Cohen’s d < -0.07), including bilateral inferior/middle/superior temporal, banks of the superior temporal sulci, temporal poles, parahippocampal, entorhinal, fusiform, lingual, transverse temporal, insula, medial/lateral orbitofrontal, caudal/rostral anterior cingulate, pars opercularis/orbitalis/triangularis, posterior and isthmus cingulate gyri. Chronic pain was also associated with smaller surface area of 12 bilateral regions (-0.12 < Cohen’s d < -0.08), including rostral/caudal anterior cingulate gyri, caudal middle frontal, medial/lateral orbito-frontal gyri, superior frontal gyri, precentral gyri, inferior parietal gyri, inferior/middle temporal gyri, lingual gyri and cuneal gyri. Finally, no group difference survived FDR correction among bilateral measures of subcortical volumes.

Conclusion

This ENIGMA-Chronic Pain study represents the first large-scale, multi-site brain imaging analysis combining individuals experiencing chronic pain from diverse clinical populations and community samples across a wide age range. These results indicate alterations in cortical regions critical for the affective and cognitive components of chronic pain and may reflect maladaptive cortical plasticity or reorganization associated with living with chronic pain. Future studies will investigate the effects associated with specific chronic pain types and conditions.
