Introduction

Copper is an essential micronutrient vital for maintaining brain health1. Proper copper homeostasis supports key neurobiological processes, including energy production, iron metabolism, neuropeptide activation, connective tissue formation, neurotransmitter synthesis, intracellular signal transduction, catecholamine regulation, neuronal myelination, and efficient synaptic transmission1. Previous studies have shown that higher brain copper levels are associated with slower cognitive decline and lower global Alzheimer’s disease (AD) pathology2. Histopathological studies have demonstrated the presence of copper deposits within amyloid-laden cerebral vessels, suggesting a potential role of copper in the pathogenesis of cerebral small vessel disease. However, the relationship between brain copper levels and structural MRI measures, such as brain volume and white matter abnormalities, has not been systematically examined in older adults. Therefore, this study aimed to quantify brain copper levels in a large, community-based cohort of older individuals and to investigate their association with gray matter volume white matter hyperintensities (WMH), and enlarged perivascular spaces (EPVS)  independent of age-related neuropathologies.

Methods

Cerebral hemispheres from 439 autopsied participants from community-based cohort of older adults of the Rush Memory and Aging Project3 (Table 1) were involved in this work. Cerebral hemispheres from all participants were obtained at autopsy and imaged ex-vivo with a multi-echo spin-echo (ME-SE) sequence on 3T clinical MRI scanners approximately one month postmortem3. The acquired voxel size was 0.6mm × 0.6mm × 1.5mm, and the echo time ranged from 10-50 ms approximately3. Gray matter was segmented into 34 cortical and 8 subcortical regions using a multi-atlas segmentation approach3. Each gray matter volume was normalized by the hemisphere volume. WMH were segmented based on T2-weighted images. WMH volume was normalized by the total hemisphere volume and then log-transformed to account for skewness4. EPVS were segmented a using deep-learning model on T2-weighted images. EPVS counts higher than median were binned into four groups5. Following ex-vivo MRI, all hemispheres underwent detailed neuropathologic assessment. The assessed pathologies included Aβ plaques, neurofibrillary tangles, limbic-predominant age-related TDP-43 encephalopathy neuropathological change (LATE-NC), hippocampal sclerosis (HS), Lewy bodies, cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA), gross infarcts, microscopic infarcts, atherosclerosis, and arteriolosclerosis (Table 1)3.

Inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry was used on selected autopsied participants to measure copper concentrations (μg of copper per 1gm of tissue) in four brain regions: mid-frontal, anterior cingulate, inferior temporal cortices, and cerebellum2. The log-transformed copper concentrations in the four regions were averaged to generate a global composite score for copper. (Table 1).  

Multiple linear regression analyses were performed separately for each segmented gray matter regional volume and lobar white matter hyperintensity (WMH) burden to examine their associations with brain copper levels. The models controlled for demographic factors (age at death, sex, and years of education), postmortem intervals to fixation and imaging, and scanner effects. Regions showing significant associations were further tested by including all aforementioned neuropathologies as additional covariates. Statistical analyses were conducted using PALM (FMRIB, Oxford, UK) with tail-accelerated 5,000 permutations, and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 after family-wise error rate correction3.

Results

Lower brain copper levels were associated with reduced brain volume in the superior temporal, middle temporal, and temporal pole cortices, as well as in the ventral-diencephalon (DC) (Table 2a). After adjusting for all age-related neuropathologies, significant associations persisted in the superior temporal and temporal pole cortices, and the ventral-DC (Table 2a).

Lower brain copper levels were also linked with greater WMH burden in the total hemisphere, frontal, parieto-occipital and temporo-basal ganglia regions (Table 2b). After controlling for all age-related neuropathologies, the association with increased WMH burden remained significant in the total hemisphere, parieto-occipital and temporo-basal ganglia region (Table 2b).

Lower brain copper levels were also associated with higher EPVS burden in the total hemisphere, and frontal lobe (Table 2c). After controlling for all age-related neuropathologies, the association with increased EPVS burden remained significant (Table 2c).

Conclusion

The present study combined ex-vivo MRI, detailed neuropathologic evaluation, and inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry for trace metals in a large number of autopsied participants from a community-based study of older adults and demonstrated that lower brain copper levels were associated with lower brain volume in gray matter, higher WMH levels, and higher EPVS counts independent of the all age-related neuropathologies, and demographic factors. Previous research in the same cohort reported that higher brain copper levels were linked to slower cognitive decline and lower AD pathology2. The current findings of copper with reduced brain volume and greater WMH and EPVS burden, even after accounting for neuropathological effects, suggest that lower brain copper levels may contribute to additional brain abnormalities above and beyond those linked to age-related neuropathologies.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first investigation that combined ex-vivo MRI, neuropathology and plasma mass spectrometry in a large number of autopsied participants from a community-based study of older adults and showed that lower brain copper levels in older adults are associated with gray matter atrophy as well as increased white matter hyperintensities and enlarged perivascular spaces burden, independent of age-related neuropathologies and demographics, suggesting additional mechanisms of brain abnormalities linked to copper dyshomeostasis.
