Introduction

Functional MRI (fMRI) during naturalistic viewing offers a compelling alternative to traditional resting-state or task-based paradigms. Unlike controlled tasks or unconstrained rest, naturalistic stimuli such as movies resembles our everyday experience: we watch, we listen, and we engage. Movie-watching fMRI, in particular, has become an effective way to derive individual-specific functional connectivity (FC) patterns that are both reliable and behaviorally meaningful [1].

A key challenge, however, is that FC estimated from movie-watching data is usually very high-dimensional, and different connections contribute unevenly to the overall signal and correlation to behaviors. For example, our previous work has shown that connections between and within visual, auditory and higher-order functional networks in movie-watching FC are generally more important in brain-behavior mapping [2]. This highlights the need for brain-behavior mapping methods that can capture the most informative patterns in the data while reducing noise and redundancy.

To do this, we use the Krakencoder [3], a powerful connectome mapping tool that compresses full connectivity matrices into a 128-dimensional embedding vector. This representation is designed to represent resting-state FC and SC in a low-dimensional way while preserving subject-specific features of these networks. In this study, we examine the subject- and clip-identifiability of Krakencoder embeddings derived from movie-watching FC, and evaluate how well these embeddings predict behavioral and demographic measures, including general cognition, sex, and age. Previous work showed that the Krakencoder's resting-state FC embeddings of brain networks outperformed raw FC data when predicting cognitive scores. Together, our results show that Krakencoder embeddings derived from multiple FC flavors largely preserve subject- and clip-specific patterns, and they improve the prediction of age and sex compared to using raw FC.

Methods

To create a compact and meaningful representation of functional connectivity (FC) during naturalistic viewing, we projected FC into the Krakencoder latent space and used these embeddings for subsequent brain–behavior analyses. The Krakencoder is a unified model that encodes and decodes functional and structural connectivity across 15 distinct “flavors,” and was originally trained on Human Connectome Project (HCP) data, including resting-state fMRI FC [3]. Here, we use only the encoder to generate 128-dimensional embeddings from nine FC flavors defined by a combination of three parcellations (fs86, Shen268, CoCo439) and three preprocessing methods (Pearson correlation, Pearson correlation with global signal regression (GSR), and regularized partial correlation). The 9 flavor embeddings are averaged to produce one 128-dimensional embedding vector per subject per movie clip or rest session.

We performed all analyses on the HCP 7T dataset, which includes 178 participants who completed four 15-minute resting-state scans and 13 movie clips (each around 2-4 minutes). In this study, we focused on three clips (Socialnet, Inception, and Dreary) that were previously identified as especially predictive or nonpredictive of behavior [2]. For comparison with movie data, we also used REST1 and REST4, which were acquired on the same day as the movie scans and cropped to the first 3 minutes to match average clip duration.

BOLD time series for each parcellation were extracted from the HCP minimally preprocessed, high–bandwidth filtered, MNI-space data.Note that for the regularized partial correlation flavors, the short duration of individual clips makes the number of time points smaller than the number of regions for some atlases. To stabilize the inverse covariance estimation, we used the concatenated time series from all four movie sessions as the regularization target. We then selected the regularization parameter λ that minimized reconstruction error across clips and applied this λ consistently for all partial-correlation FC matrices.

Each FC flavor was passed into the pretrained Krakencoder encoder and then averaged to produce a single 128-dimensional embedding (“fusionFC”) for every subject–clip pair. In addition to the nine-flavor fusion embedding, we also generated embeddings using only the shen268 GSR flavor to enable direct comparison with prior models trained solely on raw Shen268 GSR FC, whose upper right triangle vector is 35,778-dimensional.

Because the utility of FC for brain–behavior mapping depends on preserving subject-specific patterns [4][5], and because different movie clips vary substantially in behavioral predictiveness [2], we first evaluated both subject- and clip- identifiability in the Krakencoder embeddings. We computed cosine similarity and mean squared error (MSE) across embeddings from different subjects and clips.

For subject identifiability, we assessed whether a participant’s embedding for one clip or rest session was most similar to that participant’s embedding for another clip or rest session. For clip identifiability, we visualized embeddings using the first two principal components and examined whether embeddings clustered by clip content.

Finally, we evaluated the movie-watching FC Krakencoder embeddings in terms of predicting cognitive and demographic information. For each behavior and each movie clip, we trained a separate ridge regression model using either (1) the nine-flavor fusionFC embedding or (2) the shen268 GSR-only embedding. We compared the prediction performance of these models with a previous neural network trained directly on raw shen268 GSR FC.

All models used 10-fold cross-validation. Prediction accuracy for total cognition was quantified using the Spearman correlation between predicted and observed scores. Sex prediction was evaluated using area under the ROC curve (AUC), and age prediction was assessed using Spearman correlation between predicted and true biological age.

Results

We first assessed whether Krakencoder embeddings preserve subject-specific FC patterns across different movie clips and resting-state sessions. For each pair of clips, we computed a cosine similarity matrix and an MSE matrix across all 178 subjects (Fig.1 a,b). Each square block in these clip-by-clip matrices represents the similarity between embeddings of two clips, aggregated across all subject pairs. Because the diagonal of each block corresponds to the same clip, we excluded those values when quantifying identifiability and used the average of other clip-clip combinations.

Subject identifiability was quantified using two complementary metrics applied to the cross-clip matrices:

(1) Top-1 ratio, the proportion of subjects for whom the most similar embedding (highest cosine similarity or lowest MSE) corresponds to the same subject in a different clip;

(2) Average rank percentile, the percentile rank of the true match relative to all possible subjects.

Using cosine similarity, we observed a top-1 ratio of 86.8% and an average rank percentile of 98.9%. Using MSE (inverted for interpretability), the top-1 ratio was 85.5% with an average rank percentile of 98.5%. These values indicate strong subject identifiability, reflected visually by the consistently brighter diagonals in Fig.1 a and darker diagonals in Fig.1 b. Together, these results show that individual FC fingerprints are highly preserved in the Krakencoder space across both movie and resting-state conditions.

We next examined whether Krakencoder embeddings preserve clip-specific patterns. Averaging within each block of Fig.1 a, b revealed that cosine similarity was highest, and MSE lowest, for the diagonal blocks (i.e., when comparing a clip to itself), indicating that embeddings retain information distinguishing clips from one another.

To visualize this structure, we projected all embeddings from the three movie clips into the first two principal components (Fig.1 c). Each point represents one subject’s embedding for one clip (N = 178 per clip). Embeddings from the same clip formed tight, well-separated clusters, suggesting strong clip-level structure in the embedding space. In Fig.1 d, another PCA is performed on concatenated Krakencoder embeddings of three movie clips and two rest sessions, and rest sessions are separated from movie sessions pretty well.

We quantified this by computing the ratio of mean between-clip distance to mean within-clip distance, which was 5.959 / 3.524 = 1.691, indicating substantial separability. To further confirm this result, we trained a linear SVM to classify the three clips; classification accuracy reached 94.6%, demonstrating that clip identity is highly decodable from Krakencoder embeddings. Finally, the ratio of across-subject to within-subject distance was 7.975 / 5.841 = 1.365, also showing subject-specific clustering.   

Finally, we evaluated the brain-behavior mapping performance of Krakencoder embeddings of fusionFC and single flavor FC, as shown in Fig.2. For total cognition, the Krakencoder embeddings achieved accuracy slightly lower (for more predictive clips like Social Network and Inceptions) or slightly higher (for rest sessions and less predictive clips like Dreary) compared to models trained on raw FC. For example, for Social Network, Spearman r = 0.335 for fusionFC; r = 0.346 for Shen268 GSR embedding; r = 0.395 for raw shen268 GSR FC.

For sex and age, Krakencoder embeddings show consistently higher accuracy than raw FC, and fusionFC gives the best accuracy. Sex classification improved from AUC = 0.723 (raw FC) to AUC = 0.774 (Shen268 GSR embedding) and AUC = 0.841 (fusionFC). Age prediction showed similar gains (Spearman r: raw FC = 0.071; Shen268 GSR embedding = 0.127; fusionFC = 0.212).

These results highlight that Krakencoder embeddings not only preserve essential subject- and clip-specific structure but also enhance the extraction of behaviorally relevant information, particularly for age and sex. 

Conclusion

In this study, we used the Krakencoder to derive low-dimensional representations of movie-watching and resting-state functional connectivity, and demonstrated that these embeddings preserve both subject-specific and movie clip-specific structure. Krakencoder embeddings showed high subject identifiability across conditions, indicating that they retain stable individual FC signatures despite substantial variability in stimulus content. At the same time, the embeddings successfully differentiated movie clips and rest sessions, suggesting that they capture meaningful stimulus-driven patterns in naturalistic neural responses.

These representational advantages translated directly into improved behavioral prediction. While cognition prediction performance was largely comparable to models trained on raw FC, Krakencoder embeddings substantially enhanced the prediction of sex and biological age. Notably, the fusionFC embedding showed the strongest results, outperforming raw FC by a considerable margin.

Together, these findings show that Krakencoder's effectiveness in compact, behaviorally meaningful representations extend from resting-state to naturalistic fMRI. By preserving both subject-specific and stimulus-specific features while reducing noise and redundancy, Krakencoder embeddings offer a powerful framework for naturalistic fMRI analysis and open new opportunities for understanding individual differences in brain function and how they map to behavior.
