Introduction

Bipolar disorder (BD) is associated with altered brain connectivity measured using resting-state functional MRI (rsfMRI). As a marker of local brain function, amplitude of low frequency fluctuations (ALFF) measures the absolute amplitude of spontaneous low-frequency fluctuations, and fractional ALFF (fALFF) measures the relative contribution of the fluctuations to the whole frequency range. fALFF may have improved sensitivity and specificity to spontaneous neural activity compared to ALFF, and be less sensitive to physiological noise.1 Prior studies report inconsistent fALFF features in BD compared to controls (CN), but a large meta-analysis (15 studies; 494 BD patients and 593 CN) showed higher ALFF in the bilateral inferior frontal gyrus and insula, right superior frontal and superior temporal gyrus, and decreased ALFF in the bilateral precuneus, left anterior cingulate, and left superior temporal gyrus.2 Sample differences (e.g., illness duration/severity, treatment status), as well as variable processing and analysis methods, likely influence the detection and interpretability of findings across studies, and few studies have evaluated the impact of rsfMRI processing settings when combining multisite data. Here, we test five rsfMRI processing pipelines, two commonly used brain atlases, and two methods to adjust for site effects, to understand their downstream influence on the detection of case versus control differences in fALFF across five independently collected cohorts.

Methods

T1-weighted structural MRI and rsfMRI data were collected from five ENIGMA Bipolar Disorder Working Group sites (296 BD and 261 CN, ages 12-81). All rsfMRI scans were preprocessed using the HALFpipe software package, version 1.3.1 (based on fMRIPrep), which included motion and slice timing correction, spatial normalization, and temporal filtering (frequency-based filter with a low cutoff of 0.01 Hz and a high cutoff of 0.1 Hz) (Figure 1).3 Setting 1 included aCompCor denoising. Setting 2 included motion parameters (+derivatives and squares) and motion scrubbing. Setting 3 included motion parameters (+derivatives and squares), scrubbing, and global signal regression (GSR). Setting 4 included motion parameters (+derivatives and squares) only. Setting 5 was identical to setting 4 plus GSR. fALFF was extracted and averaged for every region of interest (ROI), using the Schaefer 17-network 400 parcellation cortical atlas, and the FreeSurfer Subcortical atlas. Site-specific confounds were modeled using 1) a linear mixed model with site included as a random effect, or 2) linear ComBat trained on all CN data and then applied to the BD data. All results were adjusted for age, sex, and multiple comparisons (FDR q<0.05).

Results

BD showed a widespread pattern of lower fALFF compared to CN (Figure 2). Both site-as-random-effect and ComBat-adjusted analyses resulted in similar patterns. Focusing on the ComBat-adjusted analyses: Pipeline setting 2 (i.e. motion correction and motion scrubbing) showed significant group differences in 371/400 cortical ROIs, with the largest effect in the default mode network including the precuneus/posterior cingulate cortex in the left hemisphere (Cohen’s d = -0.39). Setting 3 (i.e. motion correction, motion scrubbing, and GSR) showed highly overlapping patterns compared to setting 2, with significantly lower fALFF in 371/400 cortical ROIs and the largest effects appearing in the same region (Cohen’s d = -0.43). Setting 5 (i.e. motion correction and GSR) showed significantly lower fALFF in 280 cortical ROIs, with the largest effect in the right temporal parietal network (Cohen’s d = -0.32). BD was associated with lower fALFF across subcortical ROIs. All regions except for the bilateral nucleus accumbens were significant in settings 2 and 3, with the largest effects shown in the right caudate (Cohen’s d = -0.31), and the left thalamus (Cohen’s d = -0.31). The right hippocampus and the left pallidum showed lower fALFF in setting 5. Settings 1 and 4 showed no significant group differences using either site adjustment methods.

Conclusion

We present results from the largest preliminary analysis of its kind, showing BD was associated with a widespread pattern of lower cortical and subcortical fALFF, robust to site adjustment strategies. If confirmed in the larger ENIGMA-BD sample, this may suggest those with BD may have lower and aberrant spontaneous local neural activity compared to CN.1 By reducing motion-related noise, motion regression and scrubbing may help improve neural signals and identify widespread aberrant fALFF values in BD across cortical and subcortical regions, though this may be dependent on specific datasets. The standardized pipeline approach presented here will help identify the impact of different rsfMRI data processing methods on the detection of local functional brain alterations in BD and other psychiatric disorders. Ongoing studies by the ENIGMA psychiatric, neurological, and neurodevelopmental working groups are comparing atlases, adding larger samples, and testing other site adjustment methods such as Block-ComBat for connectomes to identify the most generalizable rsfMRI features across disorders.4
