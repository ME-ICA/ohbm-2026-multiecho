Introduction

The cerebellum supports motor, cognitive and social functions through ongoing interaction with the cerebral cortex.1 Particularly, in early life, cerebellar injury can lead to more severe social and linguistic deficits, highlighting the cerebellum’s critical role in the emergence of social and communicative skills.2 In later life, the cerebellum has been suggested to take on a compensatory role as cortical systems begin to decline.3 Given its involvement in social and cognitive development, understanding how social experiences influence cerebellar structure in development and aging is particularly important. Here, we examine how quality of social relationships affect cerebellar morphology and cerebellar-cerebral covariance in development and aging, the life stages in which cerebellar contributions appear to be especially meaningful.

Methods

We analyzed T1-weighted structural MRI images from participants aged 8-21 years (N = 433, 235 females) and 36-90 years (N = 590, 338 females) using data from the Human Connectome Project (HCP) in Development and Aging (Fig. 1A). The cerebellum was parcellated using a functional atlas comprising socio-linguistic, multi-demand (e.g., working memory), motor, and action domains.4 To measure the quality of social relationships, we derived a latent construct from exploratory factor analysis on five NIH Toolbox surveys measuring loneliness, friendship, perceived hostility, perceived rejection, and emotional support. The associations between the ‘social relationship’ factor (Fig. 1B-D) and cerebellar parcel volumes were modelled using generalized additive models to capture potential age-related nonlinear effects. Each parcel was modeled separately, incorporating age and sex interactions while controlling for intracranial volume. Additionally, we examined structural covariance between cerebellar parcels and cortical Yeo-7 networks using linear models to test whether covariance varied as a function of social relationship.5 We tested significance at α = .05 and applied FDR-correction for multiple comparisons.

Results

In the developmental sample, we found associations between social relationship scores and cerebellar morphology in multi-demand and socio-linguistic regions, with more distributed effects in males (puncorrected < .05). In the aging cohort, no significant associations were found between social relationship and cerebellar morphology even at the uncorrected threshold (Fig. 2A and Fig. 2B). In contrast, analyses of cerebellar-cerebral covariance revealed stronger effects in the aging cohort. In this sample, social relationship scores moderated a negative association between cerebellar and cerebral volumes across all cerebellar functional domains at the uncorrected threshold, with two effects remaining significant after FDR correction (both t = -2.796, pFDR < .05). In other words, individuals with higher social relationship quality showed stronger decoupling between cerebellar and cerebral volumes. This association was weaker and limited to fewer domains in the developmental cohort (Fig. 2C), where no association survived correction for multiple comparisons.

Conclusion

Cerebellar regions supporting social cognition are particularly sensitive to quality of social relationships during development, with males exhibiting broader regional sensitivity than females, highlighting potential sex-specific effects of social experiences on cerebellar morphology in youth. In later adulthood, quality of social relationships appears to play a stronger role in modulating cerebellar-cerebral structural covariance over direct association with cerebellar volumes. This may reflect a potentially compensatory trade-off in age-related and region-specific changes in brain structure relating to quality of social relationships. Overall, our findings suggest a shift across the lifespan from local cerebellar plasticity in youth to distributed structural covariation in aging, highlighting differentiated yet meaningful ways in which social environment relates to brain organization across distinct life stages.
