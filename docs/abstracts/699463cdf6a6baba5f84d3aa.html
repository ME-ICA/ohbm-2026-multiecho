Introduction

Reading is a remarkable, culturally acquired cognitive process supported by distributed cortical networks1. A crucial hub within this system is located in the ventral occipitotemporal cortex (VOTC). Early neuroimaging studies identified a region in the VOTC that responds selectively to visual words compared with other categories2, and more recent work suggests the presence of multiple subregions supporting distinct computations during visual word recognition3. Despite decades of research, both the functional role of these VOTC subregions and their precise anatomical localization remain under debate. Functional localizers of the VOTC reading circuits differ widely across laboratories in stimulus design, contrast definitions, thresholds, and ROI-drawing procedures, resulting in substantial variability in reported VOTC reading region-of-interest (ROI) coordinates and limiting reproducibility in research on the cortical reading circuit.

To address these challenges, we designed a dense-sampled, high-resolution MRI study enabling precise, individual-level characterization of VOTC reading circuits. Using state-of-the-art publicly available neuroimaging pipelines and high-resolution MRI protocols, we processed and analyzed all functional MRI data in individual-subject native space. This high-power, reproducible framework allows us to identify the precise  location of VOTC reading subregions with high reliability, quantifying how this localization varies across sessions and individuals, and establish the minimum number of functional runs within session required to obtain robust and stable VOTC reading ROIs. Together, these analyses provide a high-resolution, reliability-based approach for delineating the VOTC reading circuits at the individual-subject level and for improving methodological consistency in future work on the neural basis of reading.

Methods

Participants and functional MRI design. Ten young adults (6 females; mean age = 29.2 ± 4.5 years) participated in the study. Each participant completed 10 sessions with a minimum intersession interval of 3 days. In every session, participants performed 10 runs (5.5 min each) of a block-design localizer adapted from3 and fLoc4. Each run included 51 randomized blocks, consisting of 42 active and 9 baseline blocks. The activation blocks contained 6 stimulus categories: real words (RW), scrambled words (SC), false fonts (FF), consonant strings (CS), faces, and body/limb images. Two counterbalanced stimulus sets containing 80 examples per category were assigned across participants. Stimuli were presented for 400 ms with a 100 ms ISI, and participants performed a one-back task throughout the experiment.

Data acquisition parameters MRI data were acquired on a 3T Siemens Prisma-Fit scanner. Structural images included a T1-weighted MP2RAGE sequence (TR/TE = 5000/2.98 ms; flip angles = 4°/5°; FOV = 256 mm; 1 mm³ isotropic) and a T2-weighted scan (TR/TE = 3390/389 ms; FOV = 256 mm; 1 mm³ isotropic). Functional images were collected with a gradient-echo EPI sequence (TR/TE = 2000/36 ms; flip angle = 78°; FOV = 138 mm; 1.5 mm³ isotropic; 153 volumes per run). Magnitude and phase images, as well as an additional volume for NORDIC denoising, were acquired, and a single-band reference image was collected to improve registration to the structural scan.

Data Processing DICOMs were converted to BIDS (RRID:SCR_016124) using HeuDiConv 1.3.2 (RRID:SCR_017427). Functional MRI data were denoised with NORDIC5 and preprocessed using fMRIPrep 25.1.1 (RRID:SCR_016216), with T2-weighted images included to improve FreeSurfer’s (RRID:SCR_001847) segmentation. For each subject, a single session’s T1-weighted scan served as the anatomical reference, all sessions fMRI data were registered to this reference. Surface-based time series were generated in each participant’s fsnative space using bbregister. No spatial smoothing was applied. First-level GLMs were estimated with NiLearn 0.10.4 (RRID:SCR_001362) and included confound regressors from fMRIPrep (including 6 motion regressors, framewise displacement, unsteady-state volumes, and a/tCompCor components explaining 50% of variance) (See Figure 1a).

Characterization of VOTC reading ROIs We used a new automated ROI delineation method to obtain three inter-session ground-truth  ROIs. The automated ROI delineation pipeline consisted of a two-stage procedure. First, candidate ROIs were functionally initialized based on the highest T-value contrast RW > PER following3 and iteratively refined using Dice similarity across sessions. Second, the ROIs were validated and refined through cross-session correlations with non-target functional contrasts and structural metrics, yielding consistent posterior occipitotemporal sulcus (pOTS; VWFA1), middle (mOTS; VWFA-2), and anterior (aOTS; VWFA-3) regions. The three ROIs were used for the following analyses.

Estimate scanning time’s influence on ROI reliability To estimate how scanning time influences the localization of the VOTC reading ROI, we quantified both the T-values and spatial localization of the ROI as a function of the scanning time. Within each session, we systematically varied the input to the GLM by using one run, two runs, and so on up to all ten runs. For each run count, runs were randomly sampled from the available ten runs, and this sampling procedure was repeated ten times to ensure that the results were not biased by any particular random draw. For every sampling iteration, we measured the spatial location of the VOTC ROI and the corresponding T-values within the reference ROI. These measurements were then compared with the reference ROI obtained using all ten runs to assess how different amounts of data influence the stability and strength of the localization (see Figure 1b).

Results

Across the 10 sessions collected for each participant, conjunction analysis of the RW > SC contrast revealed two consistent VOTC reading subregions in all subjects, corresponding to the pOTS (VWFA1) and the mOTS (VWFA2). In addition, a third, more anterior subregion aOTS (VWFA3) was identified in 9 out of 10 participants (Figure 2a). The single participant in whom the aOTS was not detected exhibited generally lower activation power across sessions. The across subject average surface area for pOTS was 84.83 ± 24.43 mm² (CV = 0.29, N = 10), for mOTS was 63.96 ± 38.68 mm² (CV = 0.60, N = 10), and for aOTS was 19.91 ± 7.41 mm² (CV = 0.37, N = 9). Notably, the mOTS showed greater inter-individual variability in ROI size than either the pOTS or aOTS, consistent with our previous observations that this region tends to be less spatially stable and more variable across sessions. Together, the reliable identification of pOTS and mOTS in all participants, combined with the presence of a smaller but reproducible aOTS in participants with higher activation power, aligns with prior accounts of functional specialization along the VOTC and highlights the advantage of dense sampling for detecting weaker, anterior reading responses that are typically not observable in single-session designs.

Session-to-session reliability analyses further demonstrated that VOTC reading circuits can be localized with high consistency. Dice coefficients relative to the ground-truth VOTC ROIs showed low variability across sessions for most participants, indicating robust spatial reproducibility. However, several participants exhibited one or two sessions with markedly reduced overlap and weaker activation strength. These “low-quality” sessions were associated with reduced t-values across the VOTC, likely reflecting factors such as reduced participant attention and/or physiological conditions that influenced BOLD contrasts, among others. The presence of such low-quality sessions likely accounts for a substantial portion of the variability reported in single-session studies.

After obtaining the VOTC reading ROIs from the automatic pipeline, for each session, we quantified how statistical power and localization accuracy increased as additional fMRI runs were entered into the GLM. Mean T-values within the ground-truth ROI increased monotonically with scanning time, with the rate of improvement diminishing after approximately six runs (~30 minutes). Beyond this point, gains in statistical power approached a plateau, suggesting that extended acquisition provides limited additional benefit for identifying the core reading-related regions. A similar pattern was observed for spatial convergence: Dice overlap between each session’s ROIs and the ground-truth ROI increased with the number of runs and generally stabilized after six runs (Figure 2b).

Conclusion

This study systematically evaluated the reliability and stability of VOTC reading circuit localization using dense-sampled, high-resolution fMRI. Despite longstanding challenges associated with inter-individual variability, group averaging, heterogeneous task designs, and insufficient acquisition time, our findings demonstrate that reading-related regions in the VOTC can be localized with high precision at the individual-subject level. 

Across all participants, two subregions: pOTS and mOTS, were reliably identified. Moreover, in participants with stronger signal-to-noise ratio, a third anterior subregion (aOTS) was consistently detected. These results align with prior proposals that the VOTC reading circuit contains multiple functional subdivisions and show that dense sampling enables detection of anterior subregions that are typically overlooked due to lower signal strength and greater anatomical variability.

Our quantitative evaluation of session-to-session variability highlights the inherent reproducibility of these regions but also reveals the presence of low-quality sessions within individuals. This observation provides a plausible explanation for inconsistencies reported in previous literature: when studies rely on single-session data, variability in participant performance or data quality can blur the underlying functional organization of the VOTC. 

Lastly, we show that on average 30 minutes of functional acquisition (i.e., 6 functional runs with the current protocol)  is sufficient for stable and accurate localization of the core VOTC reading subregions, providing a practical benchmark for future studies that seek to adopt this localizer protocol. Finally, together, these findings establish a high-resolution, reliability-based framework for localizing VOTC reading circuits in individual subjects and provide methodological guidance for improving reproducibility in research on the neural mechanisms of reading.
