Introduction

Down syndrome (DS) (OMIM 190685) is a complex genetic condition caused by trisomy of chromosome 211. This gene dosage imbalance alters the development and function of multiple systems, including the cardiac, immune, gastrointestinal, skeletal and central nervous systems1. Beyond intellectual disability and premature aging, individuals with DS also present an ultrahigh risk of developing early-onset Alzheimer’s disease (AD)2.

Classic neuroimaging studies, such as Aylward et al.3, first demonstrated that adults with DS showed significantly smaller hippocampal volumes compared to matched euploid controls, indicating a baseline neuroanatomical reduction in DS independent of AD. More recent work has reported progressive medial-temporal lobe volume reduction (including hippocampus) across AD stages in DS, with hippocampal subregions showing early changes, supporting differential vulnerability over disease progression4.

Emerging evidence indicates that trisomy 21 affects males and females differently, particularly in facial morphology5. These sex-differential effects may extend to subcortical structures such as the hippocampus and may help explain sex-related variation in AD manifestations within DS.

Therefore, the aim of this study is to characterize sex-specific patterns of hippocampal atrophy in DS and EU adults across AD stages. By integrating sex, genetic background, and disease status, we seek to clarify how AD-related neurodegeneration manifests in two biologically distinct populations and to identify previously overlooked sex-dependent trajectories.

Methods

Hippocampal volumetric differences were assessed in a sample of 473 adults, including 267 euploid controls (EU) and 206 individuals with DS. All participants were of European origin, with ages ranging from 18 to 62 years in DS, and from 18 to 90 years in EU cohorts. The entire DS group was recruited from the population-based Down-Alzheimer Barcelona Neuroimaging Initiative cohort, while EU asymptomatic and EU with AD participants were selected from the Sant Pau Initiative on Neurodegeneration (SPIN) cohort and the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) database, respectively. Since all individuals were scanned using a 3T scanner with similar scanning protocols, potential experimental bias due to different scan origins was considered negligible.

AD diagnosis was established through neuropsychological evaluations accompanied by molecular biomarkers, including the concentration ratio of Aβ1-42/Aβ1-40 proteins obtained from CSF samples. Participants were first classified as “No Alzheimer” (EU = 215; DS = 126) when no signs of AD dementia were detected, or as “Alzheimer’s disease” (EU = 52; DS = 80) when individuals showed prodromal or advanced signs of AD dementia, following the criteria in Fortea et al.3. For a more detailed AD staging in DS, the group was further divided into “DS no AD” (N = 126), “DS prodromal” (N = 33) and “DS-AD” (N = 47).

Hippocampal volumes (mm³) were extracted from structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans using FSL FIRST. Multivariate statistical analyses were performed in R to compare hippocampal volumes across groups. When age significantly influenced hippocampal volume, analyses were adjusted using the residuals derived from the permutation-based ANOVA. Subsequent analyses were conducted separately for males and females.

Results

Both males (p < 0.0001) and females (p < 0.0001) with DS exhibited significantly reduced hippocampal volumes compared to EU controls. Females with DS showed a slightly larger reduction (27%) compared to males (22%).

When evaluating EU and DS participants together using the binary AD classification (“No Alzheimer” and “Alzheimer’s disease”), AD diagnosis significantly influenced hippocampal volume in both males (p < 0.0001; Figure. 1A) and females (p < 0.0001; Figure. 1B). Pairwise comparisons revealed significant volumetric differences across all AD categories except between EU controls with AD and DS individuals without AD in both sexes (Figure. 1AB). This pattern suggests that the neurodegenerative effect of AD on hippocampal volume in EU participants resembles the baseline hippocampal reduction associated with DS.

Finally, a more detailed analysis of the DS group using the three-level AD staging (DS no AD, DS prodromal, DS-AD) showed a progressive volume reduction across AD stages in both males (p < 0.0001; Figure. 1C) and females (p < 0.0001; Figure. 1D). In males with DS, all pairwise comparisons were significant (DS no AD vs. DS prodromal: p < 0.0001; DS no AD vs. DS-AD: p < 0.0001; DS prodromal vs. DS-AD: p < 0.0001; Figure. 1C). In contrast, in females with DS, no significant difference was observed between the DS prodromal and DS-AD groups (p = 0.5342; Figure. 1D). Overall, these findings highlight sex-dependent patterns of hippocampal atrophy associated with AD in DS.

Conclusion

Our findings reveal sex-specific hippocampal atrophy patterns associated with Alzheimer’s disease (AD) in both Down syndrome (DS) and euploid (EU) adults. EU individuals with AD showed hippocampal volumes comparable to DS individuals without dementia, indicating that baseline DS neuroanatomy resembles AD-level degeneration at the volume level. Within DS, hippocampal volume decreased across AD stages in both sexes, but with distinct trajectories: males showed a clear stepwise decline, whereas females exhibited a weaker distinction between prodromal and AD stages. These results highlight that AD-related neurodegeneration interacts with both genetic background and sex, underscoring the need to integrate sex-specific considerations when interpreting hippocampal atrophy in DS and AD.
