Introduction

Structural or functional connectivity and network gradients have been widely used to study brain functional organization in healthy and diseased states. However, due to the lack of high-resolution whole-brain metabolic imaging capability, metabolic connectivity of the brain has not yet been well studied and understood. Recent advances in magnetic resonance spectroscopic imaging (MRSI) have made it possible to obtain whole-brain high-resolution maps of neurometabolites and neurotransmitters, including N-acetylaspartate (NAA, neuronal integrity marker), glutamate (Glu, excitatory neurotransmitter), and γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA, inhibitory neurotransmitter) This study leverages high-resolution MRSI to investigate the graph-based organization and gradient structure of cortex-wide metabolic connectivity derived from NAA, Glu, and GABA in healthy adults.

Methods

Eighty-eight healthy adults (64 females; mean age = 31.3 ± 8.8 years) were included in this study. MRI data were acquired on 3T Siemens Prisma scanners across three sites, with ten traveling participants scanned at all sites to facilitate cross-site harmonization.

All participants underwent whole-brain proton magnetic resonance spectroscopic imaging (¹H-MRSI) using the SPICE (Spectroscopic Imaging by Exploiting Spatiospectral Correlation, resolution = 2×3×3 mm3, field of view = 240×240×120) sequence1, together with high-resolution 3D T1-weighted MPRAGE imaging. Cortical neurometabolite maps for NAA, Glu, and GABA were parcellated using the Schaefer atlas with 100 cortical regions2. Site-related effects were corrected using TS-ComBat3.

For each metabolite and each subject, metabolic connectivity networks were constructed by defining edges as Jensen–Shannon divergence (JSD)-based similarity4 between regional metabolite distributions (range: 0–1, with higher values indicating greater similarity). Graph-theoretical metrics, including global efficiency (Eg), local efficiency (Eloc), assortativity, characteristic path length (Lp), clustering coefficient (Cp), and small-world properties (λ, γ, σ), were computed using the GRETNA toolbox5. To ensure robustness, the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated for each metric across a sparsity range of 0.05–0.50 (step = 0.01). Age-related associations were assessed using Pearson correlation analyses, and sex differences were evaluated using independent-sample t-tests.

Moreover, metabolic connectivity strength (MCS) was computed for each cortical region. Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to the MCS maps across all subjects to derive macroscale gradients indicating the co-variance patterns of metabolic connectivity. PC1 values of metabolite-specific MCS were compared across seven canonical functional networks. To relate metabolic gradients to cognitive functional architecture, probabilistic meta-analytic maps from Neurosynth were parcellated to the Schaefer-100 atlas and thresholded (z > 0.5). Each cognitive topic was embedded into the two-dimensional metabolic-gradient space by calculating the centroid (mean PC1 and PC2 values) of its activated cortical regions. 

Results

As shown in Figure 1, group-averaged neurometabolite maps and metabolic connectivity metrics revealed distinct cortex-wide organization patterns for NAA, Glu, and GABA. Graph-theoretical analysis demonstrated metabolite-specific age effects on metabolic network topology. Glu networks showed significant age-related decreases in global efficiency (r = −0.251, p = 0.018), alongside increases in characteristic path length (r = 0.227, p = 0.034) and assortativity (r = 0.279, p = 0.008). GABA networks exhibited similar age-related reductions in global efficiency (r = −0.416, p < 0.001), accompanied by increased characteristic path length (r = 0.377, p < 0.001) and normalized path length (λ; r = 0.373, p < 0.001), indicating reduced global integration with age. Sex differences for NAA networks showed trend-level effects for global efficiency (p = 0.054), small-worldness (σ) (p = 0.069), and clustering-related parameter (γ) (p = 0.053). Pronounced sex effects were observed in GABA metabolic networks, with males exhibiting higher global efficiency (p < 0.001) and lower characteristic path length (p = 0.002) and λ (p = 0.001) compared with females. No sex differences were detected for Glu networks. 

Figure 2 illustrates the results of the gradient decomposition of MCS. It revealed robust low-dimensional macroscale organization for all metabolites, with the first principal gradient explaining 88%, 80%, and 77% of variance for NAA, Glu, and GABA, respectively. For NAA, the principal gradient was characterized by lower PC1 values in sensorimotor regions, with the motor network exhibiting the most negative gradient loadings. Glu-based gradients showed a similar large-scale organization, with PC1 loadings prominently distributed in motor and salience/ventral attention networks, and progressively higher values extending toward association cortices. In contrast, GABA gradients exhibited a more distinct spatial pattern, with pronounced negative loadings in salience/ventral attention and motor systems, and comparatively elevated PC1 values in higher-order association networks. Across metabolites, these results indicate convergent but metabolite-specific hierarchical organization of MCS across canonical function networks. Importantly, embedding of Neurosynth meta-analytic maps within the metabolic gradient space suggested metabolite-specific correspondence between metabolic organization and large-scale cognitive domains (Fig. 2d). Across all three metabolites, working memory–related topics consistently aligned along the principal metabolic gradients, indicating a tight coupling between metabolic connectivity architecture and executive control processes. In addition, movement-related topics showed selective correspondence with NAA-based gradients, consistent with the role of NAA-linked metabolic coupling in supporting sensorimotor and action-related functions.

Conclusion

Cortex-wide neurometabolic connectivity based on NAA, Glu and GABA was systematically investigated in this study that revealed metabolite-specific network topology and macroscale gradient organization. Our study showed that metabolic connectivity networks exhibited non-random graph properties, with age-related reductions in global integration that were pronounced for Glu and GABA, suggesting differential sensitivity of excitatory and inhibitory metabolic coupling to adult aging. Gradient analyses further demonstrated robust low-dimensional organization of metabolic connectivity strength, with distinct yet partially overlapping spatial architectures across metabolites. Importantly, these metabolic gradients aligned systematically with large-scale functional systems and cognitive domains, linking neurometabolic organization to perceptual, motor, affective, and internally oriented cognitive processes. Together, these findings bridge molecular metabolism, network topology, and cognition, and may provide a new framework for investigating how metabolic interactions shape brain organization across the adult lifespan.
