Introduction

Isolated REM Sleep Behaviour Disorder (iRBD), is a parasomnia characterised by loss of normal muscle atonia in REM sleep. It is associated with a greatly elevated risk of 𝛂-synuclein disorders such as Parkinson’s Disease (PD), Dementia with Lewy Bodies (DLB) and Multi-System Atrophy (MSA). Animal studies suggest RBD results from damage within a discrete brainstem circuit, but the precise cause in humans is unclear. In recent work using the secondary RBD literature in humans, we used probabilistic tractography to identify a candidate region between the locus coeruleus and ventral medulla, dubbed the Coeruleus-Medullary Tract (CMT), that is potentially responsible for RBD in vivo1. The “Quantitative MRI for Anatomical Phenotyping in Parkinson’s disease” (qMAP-PD) project is an ongoing longitudinal cohort study focused on early stage PD and at-risk disorders including polysomnography diagnosed RBD. Quantitative MRI (qMRI) provides voxel-wise measurements of specific tissue properties2, and can also be used to improve tissue segmentation of structures invisible on conventional T1-weighted structural brain imaging. Critically, this includes brainstem nuclei, which can now be more accurately aligned at a group level, improving statistical sensitivity to detection of changes in these regions. Here, we present the voxel-based morphometry results comparing polysomnography (PSG) defined RBD with healthy controls. Our main, pre-registered hypothesis is that RBD will be associated with focal tissue damage (i.e. volume loss) located within the CMT region of interest (ROI).

Methods

A. Participant recruitment: Participants were recruited from the qMAP-PD study, based at the Functional Imaging Laboratory (UCL), and enrolled between October 2018 - March 2025 and aged between 30 and 80 years. Baseline assessment data was available for 128 subjects (88 HC, 40 RBD) and is reported below. Of these, MRI data was available for 109 subjects (75 HC, 34 RBD). Missing data was mainly due to pipeline automation failure due to minor variations in file-labelling and is currently being processed. All RBD participants had formal video sleep polysomnography via specialist sleep units to confirm diagnosis. All participants completed a standard battery of in-person assessments, including: 

  • Demographic data: Age, sex, ethnicity, years of education, environmental risk factors.

  • Clinical details: Presenting symptoms, time from diagnosis and symptom onset. 

  • Phenotyping measures: Motor (unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS-III), modified bradykinesia rating scale (MBRS)), sleep (PSQI, RBD screening questionnaire, RBDSQ), cognition (MoCA), mood (Becks, Parkinson’s anxiety scale), autonomic (SCOPA-AUS, NNMS) and sense of smell (UPSIT). 

B. MRI Acquisition: All individuals underwent the qMAP-PD MR protocol acquired using a 3T whole-body MRI system (MAGNETOM Prisma-fit, Siemens Healthcare) and 64-channel coil. Multi-Parameter Mapping (MPM) protocol data were acquired at 0.8mm isotropic resolution, total duration 27 mins 14 secs. 

C. Pre-processing: The MPM images were pre-processed using the hMRI toolbox3 to provide an initial estimation of the qMRI maps. Data were optimised by; 1. Replacing default tissue probability maps for brain-spine priors; 2. Changing the proton density bias correction to only be based on grey and white matter segmentations due to the distribution of wrapped non-brain tissue impacting the default approach; 3. Bias correction of weighted images using a 20mm FWHM to better handle 64 Channel data. A two-pass warping step was performed. Initially, we used the geodesic “shoot” toolbox in SPM to generate a basic group average template. The second step used updated, qMRI-specific tissue priors generating eight brain tissue classes (four brainstem, four cortical, Fig1A), previously generated from an independent control MT and PD dataset using a modified mixture of Gaussians approach4. These segmentations were used to estimate the final group average template. All data were warped to this space for further analysis. 

D. Analysis: All data were analysed in MATLAB 2022b. Normally distributed clinical measures were summarised as mean and standard deviations, or otherwise median and intraquartile ranges. Bonferroni correction was applied for each grouped analysis. MRI analysis used SPM12. Prior to any analysis, nine regions of interest where we had a strong prior hypothesis about microstructural differences (Fig.1B) were pre-registered on Zenodo (DOI 10.5281/zenodo.17607747), including the CMT. Because of the differences in the size of structures between the cortex and the brainstem, and the risk of mixing signals between them, two levels of spatial smoothing were used: 6mm for the cortical segmentations, and 2mm for the subcortical. Additionally as the voxel intensities change when pathology is present, meaning they can ‘switch’ between tissue classes, smooth-warped-modulated maps were created for each tissue class, and then the subsequent contrast results merged (retaining the voxel-wise largest T-statistic over the P<0.001 threshold for visualisation and review). In practice, this “multi-channel” approach to analysis produced very similar results to simply combining the grey-matter and white-matter into two masks, but performed better in the brainstem, particularly in regions with strong mechanistic priors. P<0.001 uncorrected was taken as significant if there was greater volume loss in RBD within these specific pre-defined ROIs. Other regions are considered significant if peak family wise error P<0.05.

Results

A. Demographics: Baseline data were available for 128 individuals: 40 polysomnography-proven RBD volunteers of whom 85% were male and median age was 67.23 years (±11.23) at time of assessment. The median symptom duration for RBD was 5.88 years (±4.90), and time from diagnosis was 2.81 years (±1.96). Of the 88 healthy control (HC) volunteers, 43% were male and the average age was 62.67 years (±11.23).

B. Phenotype: Clinical symptoms assessment scales were subdivided into major clinical domains:

 - Motor: Total (UPDRS-III) was significantly higher in RBD, with a median of 3(±4.75) vs 1(±2). Modified bradykinesia rating scale scores were also significantly higher, particularly the “rhythm” sub-score that quantifies motor errors 1.5(±2) vs 0(±1). There was no difference in the timed 10m walk. 

- Autonomic: No significant difference in SCOPA-AUT and NMSQ. 

- Mood: No significant difference in depression (Becks), anxiety (PAS), fatigue (FSS) or apathy (Starkstein). 

- Cognition: No significant difference in MoCA. 

- Sleep: RBDSQ scores were significantly higher in RBD 9(±3.75) vs 1(±2). The MADRE dreaming questionnaire showed significantly more intense dreams, nightmares, emotional dreaming distress and dream recall. The PSQI only showed worse daytime symptoms in RBD. 


- Other: The University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test (UPSIT) and Visual Analog Scales (VAS) for smell and subjective taste also demonstrate large differences between the two groups. Isolated RBD volunteers scored 20 (± 9.25) against 33 (±4) for HC. VAS (smell) scored RBD 50 (±57) for RBD but 80(±43.25) for HC, and VAS (subjective taste) scored 60(±30) for RBD versus 77(±40) for HC. 

C. VBM:  MRI data were available for 109 individuals (75 HC, 34 RBD). The VBM results for these are summarised in Fig 2. In line with our pre-registered hypothesis, focal areas of volume loss were found in RBD compared to controls in the CMT ROI. Within this, there were reasonably focal regions of atrophy within two key anatomical nodes for sleep atonia, namely the Locus Coeruleus (LC) and a region in the ventral medulla consistent with the location of the gigantocellular and magnocellular reticular nuclei. In terms of other regions included in our pre-registration, statistically significant volume loss was also seen in the amygdala (basolateral nuclei), lentiform nucleus, caudate and substantia nigra (SN). There was also atrophy in the cerebellar white matter and the left parietal-occipital junction, which was not within our pre-registered ROIs but survived peak FWE P<0.05. 

Conclusion

Here we present voxel-based morphometry results in isolated RBD. Building on an initial analysis of lesion-based network mapping in secondary RBD, we had an a priori hypothesis that damage to a relatively narrow tegmental region linking the LC and medulla, the Coeruleus-Medullary Tract, was responsible for RBD. Our analysis confirmed this, showing statistically significant volumetric loss in this region, which overlapped the LC and rostroventral medulla, in line with the animal literature. We also found more widespread differences in the striatum, the anterior caudate, and small regions of difference in the SN. In addition to these expected regions, we found FWE significant regions of volume loss in the cerebellar white matter and left parieto-occipital cortex. Whilst cerebellar atrophy aligns with findings in MSA-C, to date none of the cohort have developed this condition and it is a relatively rare outcome (<5%), so is unlikely to be the cause. Recent studies report reduced global cerebellar volume and functional connectivity in isolated RBD, and it is also present in a 2016 study contrasting PD with probable RBD in the Progression with Parkinson’s Markers Initiative. Whilst the cerebellum involvement is known in the production and maintenance of REM sleep, particularly phasic eye movements, removal of the cerebellum in cats does not cause failure of sleep atonia5. Further work is required to better understand this finding. Over the short term we plan a voxel-based quantification analysis of the qMRI parameter maps to complement these initial volumetric results, which may explain these findings, as might future analyses of longitudinal progression. 

This study has several potential limitations. Whilst representing one of the larger voxel-based studies of RBD, it is only based on 34 participants with RBD. However, without PSG-confirmation, a number of common sleep disorders (e.g. obstructive sleep apnea) can be erroneously labelled as RBD, inflating apparent cohort sizes. Inclusion of only PSG confirmed RBD participants was challenging, but more clearly defined our RBD cohort, distinguishing this study from others by increasing the sensitivity of our findings and their specificity. Whilst there is a large male preponderance (85%), this is in keeping with other RBD studies. Analysis of small brainstem structures poses challenges that we attempted to mitigate using a method optimised for this region, coupled with small smoothing kernels to minimise signal mixing, and adopting a “multichannel” approach to analysing multiple tissue classes. Whilst superficially such an approach may seem to risk more false positives due to multiple statistical contrasts, this is minimised by using largely non-overlapping tissue masks in the GLM, meaning the overall number of multiple comparisons remains almost identical to conventional approaches (i.e. a statistical test per brain voxel). Furthermore, the choice has to be balanced against the risk of destroying findings in small regions due to the mechanisms underpinning intensity-based segmentation methods and how these change in pathological processes. We attempted to mitigate this by using a hypothesis led approach with clearly defined a priori pre-registered regions. This would be further supported if concordant findings are found in the planned multimodal qMRI VBQ analysis. However, further optimisation and methodological innovation is required for this challenging region, which is the current focus of COST initiative CA24118 (Advancing human brainstem MRI).

In summary, we show that PSG-proven RBD is associated with widespread volume reductions, most notably within the Coeruleus-Medullary tract involving the Locus Coeruleus and the gigantocellular and magnocellular nuclei in the ventral medulla, in keeping with the animal literature. Marked bilateral cerebellar white-matter atrophy is also found. Further work is required to understand how this fits into the pathophysiology of sporadic RBD.
