Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a severe psychiatric illness characterized by widespread and complex patterns of brain dysfunction. Unlike conventional functional connectivity (FC), which is inherently undirected, effective connectivity (EC) captures the directionality and causal influence between brain regions, enabling more precise characterization of pathological information flow patterns. Previous neuroimaging studies have reported disrupted EC patterns in MDD, indicating altered directional communication across large-scale brain networks.1 However, the structural mechanisms underlying these EC alterations remain poorly understood.

While the associations between brain structure and function is well-established, prior studies have largely overlooked such relationships at the cortical laminar level. Laminar-specific analyses provide a unique opportunity to investigate how structural organization across cortical laminae constrains or supports the directionality of EC.2 Accordingly, this study aims to investigate direction-specific EC alterations in MDD and to explore their potential laminar-specific structural substrates using multimodal neuroimaging.

Methods

An overview of the study workflow is provided in Figure 1, illustrating the processing of both discovery and validation datasets to assess directional EC and laminar structural covariance patterns across MDD subgroups.

Data acquisition: We analyzed data from the large-scale REST-meta-MDD consortium, comprising five subgroup comparisons: total MDD patients vs. Normal Controls (NC) (n = 839 vs. 788), First-Episode Drug-Naïve (FEDN) patients vs. NCs (n = 227 vs. 388), Recurrent MDD (RMDD) patients vs. NCs (n = 189 vs. 423), FEDN patients vs. RMDD patients (n = 117 vs. 72), and FEDN patients vs. First-Episode On-Drug (FEOD) patients (n = 227 vs. 100). Resting-state fMRI and T1-weighted (1-mm isotropic resolution) data were obtained. An independent validation dataset was additionally collected from The First Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine, including 45 First-Episode MDD patients, 95 RMDD patients, and 86 NCs.

Data processing: All resting-state fMRl data were preprocessed consistently at each site using a standardized DPARSF pipeline. The directional EC, reflecting the information outflow and inflow intensity, was estimated using Liang information flow,3 based on the Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL) atlas. Here, the regional EC was defined as the average intensity of a specific brain region to the whole brain of input or output information flow. T1-weighted images of the gray matter cortex were segmented into three equivolumetric laminae (superficial, middle and deep). Laminar structural covariance (SC) was then constructed by calculating the Kullback-Leibler divergence between gray matter volume (GMV) probability density functions across regions and laminae. 

Statistical analysis: Group differences in EC and SC were examined using Linear Mixed-Effect (LME) model, with site as a random factor and age, sex, education, and head motion as covariates. Associations between directional EC and laminar SC were assessed using Pearson's correlations. Multiple comparisons were controlled using false discovery rate (FDR) correction (p < 0.05). The same analysis pipelines were applied to the validation dataset.

Results

Compared with NCs, the RMDD group showed increased EC inputs to the precentral gyrus, postcentral gyrus, angular gyrus and middle temporal gyrus (MTG), as well as increased EC outputs from the cerebellum (Figure 2A). A significant EC difference in the MTG was also observed between the FEDN and FEOD groups, suggesting a potential medication-related effect. No significant EC differences were observed in the remaining group comparisons. Notably, the increased EC input to the MTG and output from the cerebellum were replicated in the independent validation dataset. Regarding the structural basis of these directional information flows, laminar SC analyses revealed specific alterations within the middle lamina. We observed lower SC between the middle cingulum gyrus and fusiform gyrus, and higher SC between the superior parietal lobule and superior temporal gyrus in the RMDD group compared with NCs (Figure 2B). Correlation analyses between EC and laminar SC identified three distinct patterns (Figure 2C): (a) Unidirectional SCs showed significant associations with only one direction of EC but not the other; (b) Shared SCs were commonly correlated with bidirectional ECs, such as connections between occipital and temporal lobes and between the supramarginal and angular gyrus; (c) Distinct SCs showed different associations with bidirectional ECs, such as the temporal-angular connection. These findings demonstrate that laminar SC support for EC varies across brain regions, which were validated by the independent dataset. 

Conclusion

Our findings reveal that RMDD is characterized by a hyper-driven cerebellar-cerebral EC disruptions, which may be associated with laminar SC architectures. The increased ECs input to the sensorimotor regions we observed are consistent with recent evidence,4 suggesting that the sensorimotor integration is a core physiological signature of depression, potentially underlying the embodied symptomatology of the disorder. Furthermore, the reduced SC between the middle lamina of the middle cingulum and fusiform gyrus, aligns with recent reports demonstrating the cingulate-temporal cortices as structurally vulnerable regions in MDD structural networks,5 extending these findings to the laminar level. Crucially, the identification of three distinct association patterns between directional EC and laminar SC supports the framework that functional connectivity directions between regions may be constrained by lamina-dependent structural connection, rather than a simple one-to-one mapping.2 The replication in the independent dataset further supports the generalizability of these findings. Collectively, our results suggest that RMDD pathology involves a functional reorganization of cerebellar-cerebral and sensorimotor circuits, in which bidirectional functional interactions are supported by lamina-specific structural constraints.
