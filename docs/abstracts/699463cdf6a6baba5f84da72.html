Introduction

Filling-in is a perceptual phenomenon in which information is perceptually interpolated across areas where input is missing. In Troxler’s stimulus, a grey figure presented away from the center of gaze becomes filled-in by the surrounding line texture background after prolonged fixation. In a Troxler display, filling-in is thought to involve two stages, involving firstly boundary adaptation as a result of figure stabilization on the retina, followed secondly by a fast interpolation of information from the background representation into the cortical region representing the figure 1. 

Attention has been hypothesized to alter receptive field profiles of neurons through a shift of center receptive field towards attended locations accompanied by receptive field shrinkage around attended stimuli 2. Related psychophysical data have shown that the effect of flanker stimuli on a central target is reduced with focused attention to the target. These data indicate that lateral interactions in retinotopic cortex become reduced with attention. Since horizontal connectivity is likely involved in the interpolation of background information into the to be filled-in figure, we hypothesized that attention to the figure would reduce perceptual filling-in. We tested this hypothesis (1) by relating perceptual filling-in of the figure with (1) alpha lateralization and the balance between forward and backward travelling waves; (2) pupillary constriction/dilation; and (3) performance of an attention-demanding task 3 in the figure. Our data taken together support that low attention precedes perceptual filling-in. This indicates that attention is an important top-down factor that modulates the competition between bottom-up factors related to boundary adaptation and background strength prior to filling-in. 

Methods

Experiment 1: EEG measures of alpha lateralization

Twenty-six healthy adults (19 female, 7 male, age 20-36) with normal or corrected-to-normal vision were recruited for this study. Exclusion criteria included a history of neurological or psychiatric conditions and drug use. Six participants were excluded: two reported no filling-in, two had insufficient sustained episodes (<20 trials) for oscillation analysis, and two showed no pre-filling-in alpha activity. The final sample was N=20.

Participants started with a lateralized attention task to induce alpha lateralization under experimental control. They attended to one of two rotating checkerboards placed at 13.3° eccentricity on either side of the central fixation spot and reported speed changes. They were instructed to report the change in rotation speed of the attended stimulus by pressing the space bar. They then performed a filling-in task, reporting the complete filling-in of an iso-luminant grey figure presented randomly to the left or right of fixation (13.3° eccentricity) against a textured background. Our goal was to compare alpha band EEG during filling-in with the attention task to infer how attentional distribution changes prior to filling-in. 

EEG data were recorded using a 64-channel layout and preprocessed using a standard pipeline. Epochs were locked to cue onset (attention task) or filling-in report (filling-in task). Analyses focused on posterior/occipital channels. We filtered data at individual alpha frequencies (FOOOF toolbox) and applied a Hilbert transform. A modulation index (left- minus right-attending trials, normalized) quantified alpha lateralization. Topographical correlations between tasks over time were tested for significance using cluster-based permutation tests (1000 permutations). We also analyzed traveling alpha waves, which can be seen as proxies of feedforward and feedback influences. All processing used Matlab 2023a and FieldTrip.

 

Experiment 2: Pupillary constriction/dilation and perceptual filling-in

We analyzed data from 55 participants (same exclusion criteria as Exp. 1). Participants reported the filling-in of a grey square at 14.6° eccentricity away from the fixation spot. Pupil size (elliptical area) was measured, downsampled to 100 Hz, z-scored, and smoothed.

Experiment 3: Behavioral attention to the figure and perceptual filling-in

            We analyzed data from a final sample of 29 participants after application of exclusion criteria used also in Experiment 1.

During the filling-in task (identical to Exp. 1), they performed a Continuous Performance Task (CPT) at fixation. The CPT presented a stream of city/nature scenes fading every 800 ms. Participants responded to non-target scenes (cities), which occurred 90% of the time, and withhold to target scenes (nature). Reaction times (RT) to the images were extracted and variability was measured after z-scoring. A median split of the reaction-time variance allowed to divide behavior in either “high attention” (low variability) or “low attention” states (high variability). 

To test whether the CPT indexes global attention or withdraws spatial attention from the periphery, we ran a control block pairing the CPT with a peripheral detection task (using the filling-in stimulus). Using the same median split on CPT RT variability, we assessed detection performance in each state. If misses occur mainly during low-attention states (high variability), the CPT likely reflects a global resource. If misses shift to high-attention states (low variability), it suggests the CPT captures focal, central attention at the expense of the periphery.

Results

Experiment 1: In an initial sample of 20 participants, preliminary EEG analyses used the lateralized alpha signature from the rotating checkerboard task as a reference to interpret alpha dynamics and specifically lateralization of alpha power leading up to filling-in. To track the temporal evolution of spatial attention, we computed a time-resolved correlation (Pearson’s r) between the template alpha lateralization pattern (mean modulation index from 300-800 ms post-cue) and the continuous alpha lateralization pattern (modulation index) preceding the filling-in report.

The correlation time course (Fig. 1A) revealed a structured sequence in the seconds before the response: an initial period of attention oriented toward the target (positive correlation in Fig 1A), followed by a shift of attention away from it (negative correlation in Fig 1A), culminating in a state of non-lateralized attention immediately before the report (correlation near zero in Fig 1A). These results suggest that the figure is not the object of focused spatial attention in the moment just prior to perceptual filling-in.

To dissect the direction of cortical communication during attention and filling-in, we analyzed travelling alpha waves. Here, predominantly forward (FF, blue) propagating waves are thought to reflect feedforward sensory processing, while predominantly backward (FB, red) waves are linked to feedback, top-down control—a key mechanism of spatial attention. This analysis thus tests whether filling-in is mediated by a similar feedback-dominant state. In the attention task, a clear lateralized pattern emerged: FB dominance over ipsilateral cortex and FF dominance over contralateral cortex, consistent with the top-down biasing of a spatial location. Critically, this same lateralized pattern was present in the filling-in task 2s before the report, but this pattern became less distinct as the response approached, collapsing to a non-lateralized state in the final second. This loss of the lateralized bias indicates that the feedback-driven, location-specific cortical dynamics characteristic of spatial attention dissolves just prior to perceptual filling-in.

Together, these alpha lateralization and traveling wave dynamics indicate that perceptual filling-in is preceded by a systematic withdrawal of spatially focused, feedback-dominated cortical attention from the target figure.

Experiment 2: Pupillometry provided a complementary measure of attentional engagement. We observed a consistent constriction of pupil size immediately preceding the filling-in report (Fig. 2A). Given that pupil constriction indexes reduced arousal and attenuated attention, this result independently suggests a state of diminished attention just prior to the perceptual event.

Experiment 3: Behavioral results from the Continuous Performance Task (CPT) showed that the period surrounding filling-in was characterized by elevated behavioral variability. The variance time-course (VTC) of reaction times increased significantly, starting approximately 6 seconds before and persisting for about 5 seconds after the filling-in response (Fig. 2B). This prolonged epoch of high VTC—corresponding to a "low attention" state—closely matched the typical duration of filling-in episodes.

To validate the interpretation of the CPT measure, a control dual-task was analyzed. This analysis confirmed that misses on a concurrent peripheral detection task occurred predominantly during these same low-attention CPT states (i.e., high VTC), and not during high-attention states. This pattern confirms that the CPT VTC primarily tracks global fluctuations in attentional engagement, rather than a local withdrawal of spatial resources from the figure.

Together, these results demonstrate that perceptual filling-in is embedded within a sustained period of reduced global attentional control.

Conclusion

Our EEG analyses revealed a systematic sequence of attentional shifts before filling-in: alpha lateralization first indicated attention directed toward the figure, then away, before collapsing into a non-lateralized state immediately prior to the report. This was corroborated by travelling wave dynamics, which showed a gradual dissolution of the feedback-dominated cortical signature characteristic of spatial attention. Pupillometry independently indicated a reduction in attentional engagement, with a significant pupil constriction preceding filling-in onset. Behaviorally, the gradual onset Continuous Performance Task (CPT) showed that filling-in occurs during a sustained period of elevated reaction-time variability, a marker of low global attentional states.

Collectively, these convergent results demonstrate that perceptual filling-in is predicted by a state of globally diminished and spatially non-focused attention. This is consistent with a model in which reduced attention facilitates horizontal connectivity, allowing the background representation to spread into the figural region. To further validate these EEG dynamics, we are currently collecting additional data to increase statistical power and enable finer-grained time-resolved analysis.
