Introduction

Repetitive negative thinking (RNT), including rumination or worry, is a transdiagnostic cognitive process implicated in mood and anxiety disorders1,2 and is highly related to dysregulated Default Mode Network (DMN) functioning3. Although cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) has been widely applied in the treatment of Generalized Anxiety Disorder and Major Depressive Disorder4, corresponding structural reorganization remains underexplored. Morphometric Inverse Divergence (MIND), a multivariate metric of cortical similarity shown to outperform traditional Morphometric Similarity Networks in reliability and sensitivity to biological changes5, may detect subtle longitudinal structural plasticity. This study examined whether changes in DMN MIND connectivity were associated with symptom improvement, indexed by the percentage reduction in Beck Depression Inventory (BDI-II) scores, in patients suffering from RNT undergoing CBT compared with a waiting list control (WLC) group.

Methods

Participants were drawn from a registered clinical trial (NCT01983033) and included individuals with Generalized Anxiety Disorder and/or Major Depressive Disorder characterized by high RNT. All participants completed questionnaires and underwent MRI scans at baseline. Patients were randomly assigned to either the CBT group, which received eight CBT sessions, or the WLC group, which received treatment as usual over the same period. After the intervention, patients completed follow-up questionnaires and a second MRI scan. Participants with baseline BDI-II scores >13 were included, yielding a final sample of 31 patients in the CBT group and 19 in the WLC group. High-resolution T1-weighted MRI scans were acquired at baseline (T1) and post-intervention/waiting period (T2). The cortex was parcellated with the Schaefer 100 (17-network) atlas, focusing on the core Default Mode Network (DMN-A). MIND networks were computed using five vertex-wise features (cortical thickness, surface area, sulcal depth, mean curvature, and volume). Percentage changes in BDI-II scores and MIND edge weights from baseline to post intervention (Figure1) were calculated. Pearson correlations tested associations between symptom change and MIND change, and group differences in correlation strength were assessed. False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction was applied.

Results

In the CBT group, structural plasticity within the core part of DMN significantly tracked depressive symptom improvement. A strong negative correlation emerged between changes in connectivity between the left dorsal prefrontal cortex (PFCd) and right precuneus/posterior cingulate cortex (pCun/PCC) and BDI change (r = -0.57, p = 0.001, pFDR = 0.021; Figure2A). The correlation in the CBT group significantly differed from that of the WLC group (p = 0.005; Figure2C), where the association was non-significant (r = 0.28, p = 0.277; Figure2B). A positive correlation between the left medial prefrontal cortex (PFCm) and right inferior parietal lobule (IPL) was found in the CBT group (r = 0.55, p = 0.002, pFDR = 0.021; Figure2A), although the group difference was not significant (p = 0.514; Figure2C). A weaker negative correlation between bilateral pCun/PCC edges was observed (r = -0.38, p = 0.042, pFDR = 0.297; Figure2A) in CBT group, without significant group differences between CBT group and WLC group (p = 0.266; Figure2C).

Conclusion

Structural reorganization within the DMN-A, particularly involving connectivity between the dorsal PFC and posterior cingulate/precuneus, was associated with depressive symptom improvement following CBT. The significant interaction with group indicates that MIND captures treatment-specific structural plasticity. These findings highlight MIND as a sensitive biomarker for therapy-induced neuroplasticity and underscore the contribution of DMN structural reorganization to CBT-related changes in self-referential processing.
