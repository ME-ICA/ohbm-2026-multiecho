Introduction

Convolutional neural network (CNN) has been widely used to extract aging-related features from brain MRI for age prediction.1 Difference between the predicted age and the chronological age of an individual, commonly referred to as predicted age difference (PAD) or brain age gap, could reflect the deviations from normal aging.  A positive PAD, indicating a higher predicted age, has been associated with various neurodegenerative or neuropsychiatric diseases.  However, these diseases may not accelerate the aging of brain in a spatially uniform way, so a regional biomarker may offer higher sensitivity for detecting pathological alternations than a global index.  Within the CNN framework, this could be done if age prediction is only based on local structural features extracted from a specified “patch”.2 On the other hand, a fully CNN was introduced to develop a local brain age map corresponding to the structural MRI. 3 Popescu et al split 3D T1-weighted image (T1WI) into overlapping blocks and applied a fully CNN with 11 convolutional layers to each block, with the resulting voxel-wise predictions merged into a whole-brain age map.3 However, block-wise assembly introduced discontinuities that formed mosaic-like patterns unrelated to brain anatomy, and the reported mean absolute error (MAE) across the brain mask exceeded 9 years.  With recent advances in computation power, we propose to develop a 3D U-Net4 with increased network depth to achieve 1-mm isotropic brain age map directly from structural MRI, without cropping into patches or blocks. We hypothesize that brain age mapping can capture region-specific aging-related changes and facilitate visualization of spatially heterogeneous abnormalities associated with diseases. To evaluate its feasibility, two frameworks, a non-deformed and a deformed model, were implemented, and data collected from the OASIS-3 cohort were used to assess the PAD of patients with mild cognitive impairment and dementia.

Methods

To train the brain age mapping model, T1WI of 4,171 healthy subjects (age: 5-100 years) has been obtained from 7 databases, including IXI (n = 561), OASIS-1 (n = 316), FCP (n = 1080), ABIDE-I (n = 547), ABIDE-II (n = 571), NKI-RS (n = 411), and HCP-A (n = 685).   Pre-processing was performed on all images to remove the variations induced by imaging facilities, scanning parameters, and data preparation between different databases.  Non-brain tissues were removed by applying a deep-learning based software (TigerBX, available on https://github.com/htylab/tigerbx).  And CAT12 (available on https://neuro-jena.github.io/cat/) was used for bias correction, global intensity normalization, image registration, and resampling.  Notice that rigid and non-linear registration was performed here, respectively, to obtain non-deformed and deformed T1WI for the development of the corresponding models.   In Fig.1, the original T1WI and the processed images of a healthy subject (89.4 yrs) was demonstrated along with the MNI T1 template for reference.

           In this study, a 3D U-Net with 23 convolutional layers and five max-pooling layers inserted in the contracting path was implemented.  The channel number of the first convolutional layer was 8 and doubled after every max-pooling layer.  At the end of the expanding path, a 1×1×1 3D convolutional layer was used to produce the brain age map in 1-mm resolution.  Data were split into training (n = 3471), validation (n = 350), and test sets (n = 350).  The age distribution of the validation and test sets were nearly uniform.  During each training epoch, samples were drawn equally from all age groups to reduce potential age-related bias.  Age groups with larger sample sizes were nevertheless fully utilized across iterative training epochs.  For each T1WI, its brain mask filled with the individual’s chorological age was taken as ground truth for supervised training.  The model was trained to minimize the MAE loss by using Adam optimizer, learning rate = 10-5, decay = 10-6, and batch size = 2.   The model weights with the minimal validation MAE in 1000 epochs were taken for testing.  The non-deformed and deformed T1 models were trained independently not only to compare the prediction accuracy between different registration methods, but also to develop distinct pipelines for statistical inference.   In the non-deformed model, the predicted brain age map preserved the original tissue structures in the native space without geometric distortion, so regional mean age can be evaluated for any specific parcellation.  On the other hand, the spatial normalization of the deformed pre-processing guaranteed its resulted age map conformed to the template, allowing voxel-wise analysis in the standard MNI space.  To mitigate systematic bias in age prediction, a bias-adjustment scheme based on linear regression5 was performed on regional predictions for the non-deformed model and on voxel-wise results for the deformed model.  To evaluate the PAD map associated with degeneration of cognitive functions, patients with dementia (n = 150) and mild cognitive impairment (MCI, n = 75), and cognitively normal participants (n = 150), all with identical age distribution (range: 59.3-89.4 years, mean: 75.4 years), were selected from OASIS-3 as the external test dataset.

Results

For the non-deformed model, the uncorrected and bias-corrected MAE of validation and test sets were summarized across the whole brain, cerebral cortex, cerebral white matter, ventricles, deep gray matter, hippocampus, and amygdala in Fig.2a.   It can be found that MAE decreased after bias correction for all regions, when the corrected test MAE was ranging from 3.59 (whole brain) to 3.82 (ventricles), all much lower than those reported in the previous study. 3 Besides, the bias-corrected PAD of dementia (4.48-5.18) was obviously higher than that of MCI (2.41-2.99) when mean PAD of CN was around 0.98 (amygdala) to 1.16 (whole brain).

In addition, Fig.2b demonstrated the results of the deformed brain age mapping model in three orthogonal views (MNI x = -26, y = -18, and z = -21).  As in the non-deformed model, MAE was reduced after bias correction although the uncorrected MAE was not shown due to limited space.  The bias-corrected MAE was around 3.1 to 3.4 across most brain tissues.  The mean PAD maps of dementia, MCI, and CN groups exhibited similar trends with those reported by the non-deformed model (Fig.2a) despite that the PAD is slightly negative for CN.

Conclusion

By using the 3D U-Net, we have developed the brain age mapping model capable of capturing voxel-wise aging-related changes from structural MRI.  With different registration strategies during pre-processing, the non-deformed model preserves the native tissue structures, enabling regional analysis across any specific parcellation, whereas the deformed model generates spatially normalized age maps that facilitate voxel-wise comparisons using voxel-based morphometry.  Both models reached a test MAE lower than 4 years.  A significantly higher PAD was found in patients with dementia than that of MCI group, and both groups showed higher PAD than the CN control.  These preliminary results indicate that the proposed brain age mapping framework holds promise for future investigations for aging-related neurological diseases.
