Introduction

Myelination, the progressive ensheathment of axons by lipid-rich myelin, is a fundamental neurodevelopmental process that supports efficient neural signaling during infancy.^1-2 While cortical myelination undergoes rapid changes early in life, the human cerebral cortex is organized into distinct laminae with unique cytoarchitectural and functional properties, and how myelination progresses differentially across cortical layers during infancy remains largely unknown.^3-4

Recent advances in high-resolution MRI enable in vivo investigation of cortical microstructural maturation. The T1-weighted/T2-weighted (T1w/T2w) ratio has been widely used as a proxy for cortical myelination; however, prior studies have primarily focused on whole-cortex or region-averaged measures, overlooking potential layer-specific developmental patterns. Moreover, although early cortical maturation has been linked to emerging behavioral skills, the relationship between laminar cortical myelination and neurodevelopmental outcomes in infancy remains poorly understood.

Neurodevelopment unfolds within an environmental context. Socioeconomic status (SES) and parental perceived stress (PSS) are key contextual factors associated with variability in early developmental outcomes and brain maturation.^5 Yet, whether and how these environmental factors modulate laminar cortical myelination and its coupling with behavior is unclear.

Here, we leverage high-resolution (0.8 mm isotropic) structural MRI in a large infant cohort to characterize laminar cortical myelination trajectories using T1w/T2w ratios. We further examine associations between layer-specific myelination, neurodevelopmental outcomes, and early-life environmental factors. By integrating laminar neuroimaging, behavioral assessment, and environmental context, this study advances understanding of cortical microstructural maturation and its role in functional development and vulnerability during infancy.

Methods

PARTICIPANTS AND BEHAVIORAL ASSESSMENT

Infants aged 1–27 months were recruited as part of an ongoing neurodevelopmental study. Among the recruited cohort, 202 infants completed high-quality structural MRI, standardized behavioral assessments, and parental questionnaires and were included in the analyses.

Neurodevelopmental functioning was assessed by trained psychometrists using the Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development, Third Edition (Bayley-III). Five Bayley raw score domains were examined: cognition, gross motor, fine motor, receptive language, and expressive language. Raw scores were selected to preserve continuous variability in infants’ abilities across age, as the primary focus of this study was on developmental change rather than relative standing with respect to a normative sample. This approach provides a more direct representation of age-related behavioral variation and facilitates examination of brain–behavior coupling during early cortical maturation.

Family environmental factors were assessed via caregiver questionnaires. Socioeconomic status (SES) was derived from parental education, occupation, and annual household income. Parental perceived stress was measured using the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS), reflecting stress experienced during the past month.

MRI ACQUISITION AND LAMINAR MYELINATION ESTIMATION

Structural MRI data were acquired during natural sleep using T1-weighted (T1w) and T2-weighted (T2w) sequences with 0.8-mm isotropic resolution. Standard preprocessing included bias field correction, tissue segmentation, and cortical surface reconstruction. Whole-cortex T1w/T2w ratio maps were computed following established pipelines as a proxy for cortical myelination.

To quantify laminar cortical myelination, the cortical ribbon was subdivided into inner and outer layers using an equi-volume cortical depth modeling approach (Fig. 1a). Skeletonization was applied within each layer to minimize partial volume effects and to extract depth-specific T1w/T2w values. Regional laminar T1w/T2w measures were obtained by averaging values within anatomically defined cortical regions of interest.

STATISTICAL ANALYSES

Developmental trajectories of laminar cortical T1w/T2w ratio were estimated using generalized additive mixed models (GAMs), specified as:

T1w/T2w ~ s(Age, k = 3) + Sex + (1 | SubjectID),

and fitted separately for each cortical region and laminar depth.

Associations between laminar T1w/T2w ratio and behavioral outcomes were examined using linear mixed-effects models. A base model was first fitted to estimate the average association between T1w/T2w ratio and behavior:

Behavior ~ T1w/T2w + Age + Sex + (1 | SubjectID).

To assess the contribution of socioeconomic status (SES) to this association, SES was subsequently added as a main effect. Changes in the t statistic indexing the T1w/T2w main effect between models with and without SES were examined to evaluate the extent to which socioeconomic factors accounted for shared variance between cortical microstructure and behavioral performance.

To further examine environmental moderation, an extended model was fitted including SES, parental perceived stress (PSS), and their interaction terms:

Behavior ~ T1w/T2w + Age + Sex + SES + PSS + T1w/T2w × SES + T1w/T2w × PSS + SES × PSS + (1 | SubjectID).

Likelihood ratio tests were used to compare nested models and to assess improvements in model fit with the inclusion of interaction terms.

All continuous variables (T1w/T2w ratio, Bayley raw scores, SES, and PSS) were z-scored prior to model fitting to enable standardized effect size comparisons. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied within each Bayley domain.

Results

Across the cerebral cortex, T1w/T2w ratios increased with age (Fig. 1 b-d), with pronounced regional heterogeneity. Primary sensory and motor cortices exhibited higher values and steeper age-related increases compared to association cortices, consistent with known gradients of early cortical maturation.

Laminar analyses revealed robust and consistent differences between inner and outer cortical layers. Across all examined regions, the inner cortical layer demonstrated significantly higher T1w/T2w ratios than the outer layer, indicating more advanced myelination in deeper laminae proximal to white matter. These inner–outer differences were observed across sensory, motor, and association cortices and persisted throughout the examined age range, highlighting distinct layer-specific maturation trajectories during infancy.

Layer-specific cortical myelination was significantly associated with neurodevelopmental outcomes (Fig. 2b). Higher T1w/T2w ratios were positively associated with cognitive and motor performance across multiple cortical regions, with stronger and more widespread associations observed for the inner cortical layer. 

Environmental factors further modulated the brain–behavior relationships (Fig. 2c). Adjustment for socioeconomic status (SES) altered the magnitude of the average T1w/T2w main effect. Inclusion of SES as a main effect led to a reduction in the t statistic indexing the T1w/T2w–behavior association, particularly for inner-layer T1w/T2w and gross motor outcomes, indicating that socioeconomic factors account for shared variance between cortical microstructure and behavioral performance.

Linear mixed-effects models revealed significant interaction effects involving laminar myelination, socioeconomic status (SES), and parental perceived stress (PSS). SES-related advantages in cognition and expressive language outcomes were most pronounced under conditions of lower parental stress, whereas higher stress attenuated or reversed SES-associated benefits. Significant interaction terms for Myelination × SES, Myelination × PSS, and SES × PSS indicated that environmental context shapes the strength and direction of brain–behavior coupling (Fig. 2d).

Conclusion

This study provides the first in vivo characterization of laminar cortical microstructural maturation during infancy, leveraging high-resolution MRI–derived T1w/T2w ratios. By distinguishing inner and outer cortical layers, we demonstrate robust layer-specific developmental patterns, with consistently higher T1w/T2w ratios in inner cortical laminae across sensory, motor, and association cortices.

Importantly, laminar cortical T1w/T2w ratios were meaningfully associated with early neurodevelopmental functioning, particularly in cognitive and motor domains. These associations were strongest for inner-layer T1w/T2w, highlighting the relevance of depth-specific cortical microstructure for emerging behavioral abilities during early development.

Beyond establishing brain–behavior associations, our stepwise modeling approach clarifies the role of environmental context. Adjustment for socioeconomic status accounted for a portion of the shared variance between cortical microstructure and behavior, as reflected by changes in the T1w/T2w main-effect t statistic. Furthermore, interactions involving socioeconomic status and parental perceived stress revealed that brain–behavior coupling is context-dependent.

Together, these findings advance understanding of early cortical development by integrating laminar neuroimaging, behavior, and environmental context. They underscore the importance of considering both cortical depth and early-life environments when interpreting brain–behavior relationships in infancy, and provide a framework for future work examining developmental vulnerability and resilience during critical periods of brain maturation.
