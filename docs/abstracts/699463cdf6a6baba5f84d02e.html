Introduction

Language is a core human ability. Aphasia after a stroke causes major incapacity and reduced quality of life. Neuroimaging has refined classical models of language and aphasia, yet effective treatments remain limited, with many patients failing to recover. 

Neurotransmitters shape the neural networks supporting cognition. Post-mortem studies suggest region-specific receptor specialisations in language areas, but a comprehensive model has been lacking. Drug trials targeting neurotransmitter systems in post-stroke aphasia have shown mixed results, likely reflecting patient heterogeneity. Predicting which neurotransmitter circuits are disrupted could guide personalised treatment. However, this progress has been hampered by the difficulty of mapping multiple neurotransmitter systems in vivo.

Recently, we integrated the neurotransmitter density maps published by Hansen and colleagues with tractography to trace white-matter projections for each receptor and transporter, creating a normative atlas of neurotransmitter circuits.1,2 We also developed NeuroT-Map, a tool that estimates how focal lesions disrupt specific circuits and whether the damage is primarily pre- or postsynaptic.1

Here, using this framework, we first identified the neurotransmitter circuits underlying the language network. Cortico-cortical language pathways were mainly serotonergic and glutamatergic, acetylcholine-related projections dominated thalamo-cortical tracts, and GABAergic circuits were prominent in anterior temporal regions. Second, we examined patterns of neurotransmitter disruption in post-stroke aphasia. Most cases showed predominant serotonergic postsynaptic disruption, while subcortical aphasia displayed a distinct pattern of presynaptic damage to acetylcholine or serotonergic systems. Finally, we assessed whether these profiles predicted treatment response across four clinical trials. Patients whose medication targeted the dominant disrupted neurotransmitter were more likely to improve.

Methods

1) Neurotransmitter circuits of the language network

a) Regions of interest

Cortical language regions were defined using fMRI data from 806 healthy participants performing language tasks from the Lipkin and colleagues dataset.3 Individual t-maps were thresholded (p<0.05), and group-level regions were identified using FSL’s cluster tool, retaining voxels present in at least 50% of participants.

For subcortical regions, due to their high inter-individual variability and lower contrast in anatomical MRI, we applied functional alignment using ANTs, projecting all t-maps into a shared functional space, and used a more permissive frequency threshold of 25%.

b) Neurotransmitter projections in the language structural network

PET-based density maps of the large-scale brain circuit modulators—acetylcholine, dopamine, noradrenaline, and serotonin—and of the fast-acting excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmitters—glutamate and GABA— were normalised to a 0–1 range.2 We used the Functionnectome to project these maps onto white-matter pathways connecting language regions.4 Tractography maps were obtained from the HCP 7T dataset.

2)Neurotransmitter circuit disruption in post-stroke aphasia

a) Stroke lesion datasets

We analysed the stroke lesions of 196 patients with chronic aphasia and 31 without aphasia from the Aphasia Recovery Cohort.5 Language was assessed using the Western Aphasia Battery (WAB). Because subcortical aphasia cases were underrepresented in this cohort, we additionally included 12 consecutive patients with aphasia following basal ganglia or thalamic stroke from the Stroke Unit of Hospital de Santa Maria, Lisbon.

b) Neurotransmitter circuit disruption

NeuroT-Map was used to compute the proportion of each receptor and transporter system affected by each lesion. Considering that receptors are mainly postsynaptic and transporters presynaptic, pre- and postsynaptic damage ratios were calculated.1

c) Clustering analysis

To test whether patients formed neurochemical subgroups, clustering tendency was assessed using the Hopkins statistic, followed by k-means clustering. Associations between aphasia types and neurochemical clusters were tested using Fisher’s exact test with Monte-Carlo simulation.

d) Associations with language outcomes

We assessed whether neurotransmitter disruption predicted WAB scores using Elastic Net regression with internal cross-validation (5-fold). One model included continuous neurotransmitter disruption measures; another included cluster membership. Lesion volume was included as a covariate. 

3) Post hoc analysis of clinical trials

We analysed four trials testing modulators of acetylcholine, dopamine, or serotonin systems. Lesion maps and outcomes were obtained from the authors. Drug–circuit “match” was defined by alignment between the trial drug and the highest NeuroT-Map neurotransmitter ratio (e.g., acetylcholine presynaptic ratios for donepezil, serotonergic postsynaptic ratio for sertraline). Improvement was defined as ≥10-point in WAB – aphasia quotient (WAB-AQ) increase or any positive change in Comprehensive Aphasia Test (CAT) comprehension score; a secondary analyses using stricter thresholds was performed. Statistical significance was assessed using Fisher’s exact or chi-square tests.

Results

1) Neurotransmitter circuits of the language network

a) Regions of interest

Analysis of fMRI activation maps revealed six main cortical language clusters: left lateral temporal cortex, left inferior frontal gyrus, left precentral/middle frontal gyri, left superior frontal gyrus, right lateral temporal cortex, and right cerebellum. After functional alignment, two subcortical clusters emerged: one in the anteromedial left thalamus and another involving the left superior lentiform and inferior caudate nuclei.

b) Neurotransmitter projections in the language structural network 

Association fibres linking cortical language regions were predominantly serotonergic, especially rich in postsynaptic 5HT6R and 5HT2aR fibers (figure 1a). Ventral and dorsal pathways showed similar neurochemical compositions. Commissural fibres between the temporal cortices were also mainly serotonergic.

Thalamic projections to the inferior frontal and lateral temporal cortices were primarily cholinergic, dominated by postsynaptic α4β2R fibers (figure 1a). Basal ganglia projections were mostly serotonergic (5HT6R), with additional dopaminergic and cholinergic (M1R) tracts. Regarding the fast-acting excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmitters, glutamatergic fibres predominated over GABAergic overall, except in the left anterior temporal region and parts of the anterior limb of the internal capsule (figure 1b).

2) Neurotransmitter circuit disruption in post-stroke aphasia

a) Patterns of disruption 

Patients with aphasia showed higher injury ratios in presynaptic cholinergic α4β2R, postsynaptic cholinergic VAChT, postsynaptic dopaminergic DAT, and postsynaptic serotonergic 5HTT than patients without aphasia. Conversely, lower injury ratios were observed in presynaptic cholinergic M1R, presynaptic dopaminergic D1R, and presynaptic serotonergic 5HT1aR, 5HT1bR, 5HT2aR, and 5HT4R.

The injury ratios exhibited a pronounced clustering structure, from which five clusters were identified. Cluster 1 exhibited positive serotonergic presynaptic ratios—particularly for 5HT1aR, 5HT2aR, and 5HT6R—while the remaining showed predominantly postsynaptic serotonergic damage. Clusters 2 and 3 both showed positive dopaminergic postsynaptic and cholinergic presynaptic ratios (for α4β2R, or both α4β2R and M1R, respectively). Cluster 4 was characterized by a combination of positive cholinergic postsynaptic and presynaptic α4β2R ratios. In cluster 5, this combination co-occurred with positive dopaminergic presynaptic D1R and D2R ratios. Conduction aphasia was significantly associated with neurochemical cluster 4 (FDR-corrected p = 0.004). No other significant associations were observed.

b) Lobar vs subcortical lesions

Lobar lesions were dominated by postsynaptic serotonergic injury (figure 1c). Subcortical lesions showed maximal presynaptic disruption, notably serotonergic (5HT1a, 5HT2a) and cholinergic (α4β2, M1, figure 1d).

c) Associations with language performance

Elastic Net regression showed that greater cholinergic presynaptic damage predicted poorer fluency and spontaneous speech, while greater postsynaptic 5HT1aR damage predicted worse sentence completion. Cluster 4 was associated with better fluency, comprehension, and naming, whereas cluster 1 predicted worse fluency and comprehension.

3) Post hoc analysis of clinical trials

We analysed 56 patietns from 4 clinical trials. In 36 patients, the outcome measure was the WAB-AQ score: 30 patients received the acetylcholinesterase inhibitor donepezil, and 6 received the selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor sertraline. In the remaining 20 patients, the outcome measure was the CAT comprehension score, and all were treated with donepezil.

Improvement was more likely when the administered drug matched the patient’s predominant neurotransmitter ratio (χ² = 4.129, p = 0.042).

Conclusion

In this study, we characterized the neurotransmitter circuits of the language network, examined the neurochemical patterns of injury in poststroke aphasia, and tested whether circuit-based neurotransmitter mapping could predict responses to neuromodulatory drugs. We found a distinct neurochemical architecture: cortico-cortical language fibers were mainly serotonergic and glutamatergic, thalamo-cortical projections were predominantly cholinergic, and the left anterior temporal lobe showed a high GABAergic load. Patients with lobar lesions exhibited primarily postsynaptic serotonergic injury, whereas subcortical aphasia was driven by presynaptic serotonergic or cholinergic disruption, suggesting that different lesion topographies produce distinct neurochemical vulnerabilities. Importantly, patients receiving a drug that matched the dominant disrupted neurotransmitter circuit—as defined by NeuroT-Map—showed greater clinical improvement, indicating that neurotransmitter circuit profiling may inform individualized pharmacotherapy. Overall, these findings provide a mechanistic framework linking structural disconnection, neurotransmitter imbalance, and treatment response in poststroke aphasia.

These results align with receptor autoradiography studies showing that language areas share similar receptor fingerprints, especially for serotonergic 5HT1aR and cholinergic muscarinic receptors. Our findings extend this work by demonstrating a network-level representation of these circuits.

Neurotransmitter circuit disruption patterns further clarified the neurochemical basis of aphasia. Most patients showed preferential impairment of postsynaptic serotonergic circuits, echoing observational studies reporting improved naming recovery with selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs). Because SSRIs act presynaptically—on structures that were relatively spared—they may counterbalance postsynaptic vulnerability. 

Subcortical lesions showed a markedly different profile dominated by presynaptic serotonergic or cholinergic disruption. This pattern aligns with evidence that acetylcholinesterase inhibitors, which enhance synaptic acetylcholine, may benefit patients with subcortical injury. A randomized trial of galantamine supports this hypothesis, showing greatest responsiveness in patients with subcortical-dominant lesions.

Across four clinical trials, patients whose treatment matched the neurotransmitter system most affected by their lesion (e.g., SSRIs for serotonergic postsynaptic injury; cholinesterase inhibitors for cholinergic presynaptic injury) were more likely to improve. This supports the need to further test a precision-medicine framework for aphasia pharmacotherapy.

Neurochemical clusters showed limited correspondence with classical aphasia syndromes or detailed language scores, consistent with the broad, non-specific functions of neurotransmitter systems. Only one significant association emerged: cluster 4 correlated with conduction aphasia and better fluency and comprehension.

Some limitations shall be considered. First, the clinical trial data have different outcome measures, mainly derived from chronic patients, and some lack a placebo arm. In addtion, aphasia classification depended on the WAB, which may underestimate severity in some cases and lacks fine-grained psycholinguistic distinctions. Finally, language network mapping was based on a sentence–nonword contrast, which does not parse specific linguistic processes.

In conclusion, we provide a comprehensive characterization of the neurotransmitter circuits supporting language and show how their disruption shapes poststroke aphasia profiles. Our findings highlight the promise of neurotransmitter-guided, circuit-level pharmacotherapy and support the need for prospective, mechanistically grounded clinical trials.
