Introduction

Distinct Parkinson’s disease (PD)-related functional connectivity patterns have recently been reported in the literature, but have not been extensively explored. PD is a disease involving widespread motor circuit dysfunction, caused by the loss of dopamine in the substantia nigra. Studies have found distinct oscillatory patterns in PD, particularly prominent beta-band (13-30 Hz) dynamics in the brain. However, it remains unclear how functional connectivity, involving the entire brain, changes in PD.

Lopez et al.^1 found that bootstrapping and fingerprinting 500/4,005 edges from the beta-band phase-linearity-measurement (PLM) functional connectome in the Automated Anatomical Labeling atlas (AAL) accounted for a substantial proportion (44%) of the variance in patients’ Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS-III) severity ratings. Recent preprints have also investigated classification using PD functional connectomes, finding mixed performance relative to multi-class classification of other diseases^2 and healthy controls^3.

In this study, we perform XGBoost classification on the full functional connectome of the brain from the AAL atlas, achieving a mean Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC) of  0.837 ± 0.054SD across folds. We chose AUROC as the metric for evaluating the data, since we are interested in differentiability rather than diagnosis. To reduce feature dimensionality, we then selected 10 of 116 regions to construct MEG functional-connectivity subnetworks for 45 distinct region-pair combinations. We randomly sampled regions from these sub-networks and evaluated performance in age-matched comparisons between 106 healthy controls and 106 patients with PD. We further filtered for the highest-performing sub-networks (AUROC> 0.7) and observed region patterns within these sub-networks that contribute to high differentiation. We suggest that these region patterns may serve as novel biomarkers for Parkinson's disease.

Methods

DATASET ACQUISITION AND SUMMARY

We used imaging data from the Open MEG Archive (OMEGA), which contains control population-derived cohorts. We used 106 healthy subjects aged 50-83 years (mean age: 67.25 ± 5.89 SD years and 106 Parkinson’s disease patients aged 40-88 years (mean age: 64.57 ± 9.37SD).

Whole-head MEG recordings were taken in a magnetically shielded chamber using a 275-channel CTF radial gradiometer system (CTF Systems, Canada) at a sampling rate of 2,400 Hz. Additional 26 reference channels were recorded for noise cancellations, and the primary sensors were analyzed as synthetic third-order gradiometers. An anti-aliasing low-pass filter at 600 Hz was applied online, with CTF third-order gradient noise cancellation. For MEG/MRI co-registration, the head shape with coil positions was digitized and applied to T1-weighted MRI scans.

Participants remained awake with their eyes open on a fixation cross for 5 minutes per session. More details are available in Niso et al.^4.

PREPROCESSING:

Continuous raw MEG data was imported to Fieldtrip, downsampled to 300 Hz, and band-passed between 0.1 and 150 Hz. Subsequently, data was removed at 59-61 Hz and 119-121 Hz to remove line noise. Visual, cardiac, and muscle artifacts were removed using Independent-Component-Analysis (ICA) decomposition between 0-30 Hz of the signal. Between two and twelve ICA components were removed for each subject.

The brain, skull, and scalp boundary surfaces were generated from Fieldtrip using the MRI scans. An automated procedure was used to align these data to the SPM-8 coordinate system. The scalp surface was used to align the structural data with the MEG digitizers. The aligned MEG gradiometers, inner skull surface, and cortical surface were then used to construct a realistic, subject-specific, single-shell forward model. Dipole orientations were fixed normal to the cortical surface. Then, MEG activity in atlas-labeled voxels was reconstructed using the LCMV beam-forming source localization algorithm.

The cortical surface was aligned to the Automated-Anatomical-Labeling (AAL) atlas (116 regions) in Fieldtrip. After applying the LCMV weight vector for each voxel to the MEG sensors, the time series of each region of interest (ROI) was calculated as the average of all voxels within the ROI. All subjects' regions were source-localized with 300 s of signal.

CONNECTIVITY

Functional connectivity was constructed using the volume-conduction-corrected phase-linearity measurement (PLM) described by Baselice et al.^5, applied to 33 epochs of 8-second signals (264 seconds total), to robustly and sufficiently estimate linear phase relationships. The functional networks were constructed within the beta band (13-30 Hz) using a two-way FIR filter with a Hamming window.

MACHINE LEARNING PIPELINE

An XGBoost classifier was trained using 5-fold cross-validation on 84-85 Parkinson’s disease (PD) patients and 84-85 healthy controls (HC), and tested on 21-22 PD and 21-22 HC per fold. This was applied first to the full-brain and then individually to sub-networks randomly sampled from the AAL atlas. Permutation tests for the sub-networks involved, using the same random region sub-networks in the real pipeline, and randomly shuffling labels for each sub-network. This would provide an approximation of a null distribution to save on computational resources.

Results

To begin, we performed LCMV source localization for 300 s on 106 HC and 106 PD, and averaged the results across voxels in the AAL atlas. We then extracted the first 264 seconds of the 300-second recording, epoched it into 8-s epochs, and calculated PLM functional connectivity as the average connectivity across epochs.

We then evaluated the performance of the full-brain MEG functional connectome in distinguishing between HC and PD subjects using a 5-fold cross-validation. We achieved a mean AUROC of 0.837 ± 0.054SD, which exceeds the result reported by Roberts et al.^3 (0.68 ± 0.04SD AUROC). Subsequently, we visualized feature importances from the XGBoost model using the entire dataset. We found that the largest region pair, with a small weight of 0.012, was between the Right Cerebellar Crus I and the Right Temporal Pole, Middle Temporal Gyrus. The broadness of the weight distribution indicated a widespread PD connectivity signature (see Fig. 2 a)).

Moving on, we decided to subsample the full functional connectome, in line with Klepachevskyi et al. ^2, who found that only 34 features from the connectome were needed to classify among multiple diseases (Mild-Cognitive-Impairment, Amyotrophic-Lateral-Sclerosis, PD, Multiple Sclerosis). Reduced feature sizes for classification offer several benefits: a) they are less prone to overfitting, b) explainability for which regions in PD are pathologically related is more obvious and highly weighted, c) when many small features have high differentiation, they can be ensembled to produce stronger classification, capturing finer detail and a larger discoverable search space for PD-specific patterns.

In this study, we began by considering 3 regions, subsampled 1,000 subnetworks, and focused on the number of subnetworks with AUROC> 0.7. We also conducted permutation tests, using the same 1,000 sub-networks, each with a uniquely permuted label set. We compared the means and maximums of the permutation set sub-networks against the means and maximums of the real labels sub-networks.

We finally selected 10 regions because they yielded 161 sub-networks with AUROC > 0.7, and their maximum AUROC was 0.825, comparable to that of the full-brain analysis. Having numerous sub-networks with AUCs above 0.7 allowed us to visualize patterns in the PD regions that contribute to differentiation between healthy controls. Region numbers with sub-networks of AUCs above 0.7: (3 regions, 2 sub-networks), (4 regions, 4 sub-networks), (5 regions, 12 sub-networks), (6 regions, 34 sub-networks), (7 regions, 58 sub-networks), (8 regions, 95 sub-networks), (9 regions, 125 sub-networks), (10 regions, 161 sub-networks).

We then ran 10 regions across 5,000 randomly sampled sub-networks, acquiring 783 sub-networks with AUROC > 0.7. The mean AUROC across sub-networks was 0.651 ± 0.049SD, and the maximum AUROC was 0.825. The mean AUROC for the permutation test was 0.499 ± 0.056SD, and the maximum AUROC was 0.715. We visualized the region frequency of occurrence for these sub-networks, finding that regions are frequently concentrated (Z-score> 2.0) in the Supplementary motor area (Left) (145 sub-networks), the Calcarine cortex/V1 (Left) (138 sub-networks), the Superior frontal gyrus, medial orbital (Right) (117 sub-networks), Left Cerebellar Lobule IX (115 sub-networks) and other regions below Z-score of 2.0 (see Fig. 2 b)).

Lastly, using sub-networks with AUROC > 0.7, we examined how these sub-networks consistently classified subjects. This would indicate that different sub-networks classify different subjects, possibly reflecting variation introduced by disease stage or the medications patients were receiving. Additionally, this suggests that such subnetworks could be ensembled to outperform the full-brain model. Our results show heterogeneity across sub-networks in classification (see Fig. 2 c)) and may present an opportunity for an ensemble model.

Conclusion

Firstly, our results showed that beta band phase-linearity-measurement (PLM) is a stronger classifier of Parkinson’s disease (PD) from healthy control (HC) than the multi-band amplitude envelope correlation used by Roberts et al.^3, showing 0.837 ± 0.054SD AUROC from full-brain connectivity compared to 0.68 ± 0.04SD AUROC.

Secondly, we identify 783 sub-networks across 10 regions or 45 values that differentiate PD from HC, with an AUROC of 0.7, consistent with Klepachevskyi et al.^2, who found that only 34 features from the connectome were needed for multi-disease classification.

In contrast, our study highlights 783 sub-networks that achieve this and provides explainability for the convergence of recurring regions within these sub-networks. In our study, the most salient regions forming sub-networks appeared to be the Supplementary motor area (Left) (145 sub-networks), the Calcarine cortex/V1 (Left) (138 sub-networks), the Superior frontal gyrus, medial orbital (Right) (117 sub-networks), and Left Cerebellar Lobule IX (115 sub-networks).

We additionally find that sub-networks with AUROC > 0.7 contribute heterogeneously to PD patient classification, indicating that different sub-networks classify different subsets of subjects. This indicates a possible ensemble approach that could achieve an AUROC higher than that of the full-brain (mean = 0.837 AUROC).

Previous studies, including those by Lopez et al.^1, using 500/4,005 connectome values from the AAL atlas and beta-band PLM, have demonstrated the importance of the functional connectome for predicting aspects of PD, such as UPDRS-III ratings. In our results, we lacked a measure of PD severity/stage, so we instead used classification relative to healthy controls as a proxy for Parkinsonian region sub-network patterns. We suggest that these sub-networks may serve as biomarkers of PD and may correlate with or predict additional disease characteristics.
