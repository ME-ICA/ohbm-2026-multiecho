Introduction

## Introduction

Down syndrome (DS) is the most common genetic cause of intellectual disability, affecting 1 in 800 births. Individuals with DS exhibit altered brain development, disrupted functional connectivity (FC) within key networks including the Default Mode Network, Frontoparietal Network, and Salience Network, and are at extremely high risk for Alzheimer's disease. Transcranial photobiomodulation (t-PBM) with near-infrared (NIR) light penetrates cortical tissue, modulates neuronal excitability, and improves cerebral perfusion. Prior studies have demonstrated t-PBM's effects on EEG oscillations and cognition, but its effects on FC in DS remain unexplored. We conducted an exploratory analysis examining resting-state FC changes following 18 sessions of t-PBM-NIR versus sham in 7 adults with DS (ages 16-35). Using Schaefer 100 parcellation and Bayesian hierarchical modeling with subject-level random effects, we examined treatment effects on within-network FC across three networks at baseline, short-term follow-up (6 weeks), and long-term follow-up (10 weeks). We hypothesized that t-PBM would enhance FC within these networks, potentially reflecting improved network integration underlying cognitive improvements.

Methods

## Methods

**Participants:** Seven adults with Down syndrome (ages 16-35) were randomized to receive transcranial photobiomodulation with near-infrared light (t-PBM-NIR) or sham control. Participants completed 18 treatment sessions over 6 weeks, with resting-state fMRI acquired at baseline, short-term follow-up (6 weeks), and long-term follow-up (10 weeks).

**Data Acquisition and Preprocessing:** Resting-state fMRI data were preprocessed using fMRIPrep. Timeseries data were extracted for each run and session. Quality control excluded scans with >30% missing timepoints and specific runs based on visual inspection. Functional connectivity (FC) was computed from denoised and scrubbed timeseries data.

**Parcellation and FC Computation:** Brain regions were defined using the Schaefer 100 parcellation (50 regions per hemisphere). Edge-level FC was computed as Pearson correlation between all pairs of parcels, then Fisher Z-transformed. Parcel-level FC was computed as the average of all edges involving each parcel, resulting in 100 parcel-level FC values per subject per session.

**Statistical Analysis:** Bayesian hierarchical models were fitted separately for each network (Default Mode Network, Frontoparietal Network, Salience Network) using `MCMCglmm` in R. Models included treatment (NIR vs. Sham) as a fixed effect and subject-level random intercepts to account for within-subject correlation. MCMC sampling used 13,000 iterations with 3,000 burn-in and thinning of 10. Weakly informative priors were specified for fixed and random effects. Posterior distributions were summarized with means and 95% credible intervals. For parcel-level analysis, t-statistics were computed comparing average FC between NIR and Sham groups for each of the 100 parcels.

**Visualization:** Brain plots were generated using `ggseg` and `ggsegSchaefer` to visualize parcel-level t-statistics on cortical surfaces. Posterior distributions of treatment effects were plotted as density curves with credible intervals.

Results

## Results

Bayesian hierarchical models were fitted separately for each network (Default Mode Network, Frontoparietal Network, Salience Network) to examine treatment effects on within-network FC. Posterior distributions of treatment effects (NIR - Sham) were computed for each network, with subject-level random intercepts accounting for within-subject correlation across sessions.

Treatment effects varied across networks (Figure 1B). For the Default Mode Network, the mean treatment effect was 0.092 [95% CI: -0.053, 0.245], with P(NIR > Sham) = 0.893, indicating a high probability of increased FC in the NIR group. The Frontoparietal Network showed a mean effect of 0.006 [95% CI: -0.116, 0.139], with P(NIR > Sham) = 0.535, suggesting no clear treatment effect. In the Salience Network, the mean effect was 0.05 [95% CI: -0.097, 0.199], with P(NIR > Sham) = 0.776, indicating a moderate probability of increased FC in the NIR group. For all networks, the 95% credible intervals spanned zero, reflecting uncertainty given the small sample size.

Mean within-network FC over time (Figure 1A) showed largely overlapping 95% confidence intervals between Sham and NIR groups across Baseline, Short-term FU, and Long-term FU sessions for all three networks, consistent with the uncertainty observed in the posterior distributions.

Parcel-level t-statistics comparing average FC between NIR and Sham groups (Figure 1C) revealed heterogeneous effects across the 100 parcels. Red hues indicate parcels with higher FC in the NIR group, while blue hues indicate higher FC in the Sham group. Several frontal and parietal regions showed positive t-statistics, suggesting increased FC in the NIR group, while some temporal and medial parietal regions exhibited negative t-statistics, indicating higher FC in the Sham group. Many parcels displayed t-statistics near zero, suggesting minimal differences between groups.

Conclusion

## Conclusion

This exploratory analysis examined the effects of t-PBM-NIR on functional connectivity in adults with Down syndrome using Bayesian hierarchical modeling. Results suggest potential network-specific treatment effects, with the strongest evidence for increased FC in the Default Mode Network (P(NIR > Sham) = 0.893) and moderate evidence in the Salience Network (P(NIR > Sham) = 0.776), while the Frontoparietal Network showed minimal evidence of treatment effects (P(NIR > Sham) = 0.535). However, 95% credible intervals for all networks spanned zero, reflecting substantial uncertainty given the small sample size (n=7).

Parcel-level analysis revealed heterogeneous spatial patterns, with some regions showing increased FC in the NIR group (particularly frontal and parietal areas) and others showing higher FC in the Sham group (temporal and medial parietal regions). This heterogeneity suggests that t-PBM effects may be region-specific rather than global, consistent with the targeted nature of photobiomodulation.

The Bayesian framework provides a principled approach for quantifying uncertainty in small-sample studies, allowing for probabilistic interpretation of treatment effects even when traditional frequentist tests would lack power. The posterior probabilities indicate directional evidence favoring NIR treatment in DMN and Salience networks, though definitive conclusions require larger sample sizes.

These findings contribute to the limited literature on t-PBM effects on FC in DS and suggest that network-specific effects may underlie cognitive improvements observed in prior t-PBM studies. Future work should replicate these findings in larger samples and examine relationships between FC changes and cognitive outcomes. The heterogeneous parcel-level effects warrant further investigation to identify which specific brain regions are most responsive to t-PBM treatment.
