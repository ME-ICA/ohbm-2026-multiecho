Introduction

Threat appraisal refers to the evaluation of potential harm in the environment based on two core components: perceived severity (degree of potential harm) and perceived vulnerability (likelihood of experiencing harm), while excluding any perceived rewards or positive outcomes.1 Learning to identify and respond appropriately to threat signals is essential for survival; however, when this process becomes dysregulated, manifesting as exaggerated or persistent fear responses to harmless cues, it contributes to the emergence and maintenance of anxiety disorders.2 Although univariate fMRI studies highlight roles for the amygdala, insula, and medial prefrontal cortex, there is no validated whole-brain multivariate signature that predicts visually induced threat and generalizes across samples. Here, we aimed to (i) develop a whole-brain fMRI-based multivariate signature of conditioned threat appraisal (VITS), (ii) test its generalization across learning phases, (iii) examine brain-autonomic-subjective experience relationships using mediation analysis, and (iv) assess whether individual differences in VITS expression relate to anxiety and subjective threat experience.

Methods

Participants with varying levels of trait anxiety (N = 172) completed a differential threat-learning task designed to assess threat conditioning and threat reversal while undergoing fMRI and skin conductance responses (SCR) recording. The conditioned stimuli (CS; CS+ = threat cue, CS- = safety cue) consisted of blue and yellow spheres; and the unconditioned stimulus (US) was a mild electric shock delivered on 33% of CS+ trials. fMRI data were preprocessed using fMRIPrep 1.4.1, including distortion, slice-timing, and motion correction, normalization to MNI space, spatial smoothing, ICA-AROMA denoising, and nuisance regression with motion and CompCor components, with motion outliers excluded. Whole-brain GLM contrast images (CS+ and CS-) were used to train a linear support vector machine (SVM) classifier. Data were split into an 80% Training Set (N = 138; stratified by State-Trait Anxiety Inventory-Trait) and a 20% Independent Test Set (N = 34). Leave-one-subject-out cross-validation (LOSO-CV) was performed within the Training Set, and final model performance was evaluated on the held-out Test Set and on reversal-phase data. Reliable anatomical contributors were identified using 5000 bootstrap resamples with FDR correction (q<0.05).

To examine associations between brain threat-related activity, autonomic nervous system (ANS) responses and subjective emotional experience during the fMRI session (valence and arousal ratings), we conducted a mediation analysis using 10000 bootstrapped samples.

Finally, to assess the relationship between VITS responses and individual differences in anxiety and threat sensitivity, we first conducted a principal component analysis (PCA) on the following measures: the DASS-21 subscales (Depression, Anxiety, and Stress), the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory – Trait (STAI-T), and the Sensitivity to Punishment scale from the Sensitivity to Punishment and Sensitivity to Reward questionnaire (SPSR-P). Participants were divided into upper and lower tertiles (N = 114) based on PCA factor 1 (PC1) scores and each individual trait. VITS pattern expression values were then computed for eight contrast images reflecting early (first five trials) and late (last five trials) phases of conditioning and reversal (CS+ early, CS+ late, CS− early, CS- late, CS+ reversal early, CS+ reversal late, CS− reversal early, CS- reversal late). Pattern expression values were derived by taking the dot product between the VITS signature and each contrast image. Logistic regression with bootstrapped feature selection (500 resamples), 10-fold cross-validation, and 5000-permutation testing was used to classify high vs. low anxiety groups based on VITS pattern expression values. 

Results

Model identification & validation

The VITS showed high LOSO-CV accuracy in the Training Set (91% ± 2.5% SE, p < .001). In the Independent Test Set, accuracy reached 97% ± 2.9% SE (p < .001) and generalized well to the reversal phase (91% ± 4.9% SE, p < .001).

Functional anatomy

Significant positive weights were observed in bilateral supramarginal gyri, left anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) superior medial prefrontal gyrus, inferior frontal gyrus, left amygdala, periaqueductal gray (PAG), and right superior cerebellar peduncle. Significant negative weights were identified in two system: first in left postcentral extending to precentral gyrus, parietal operculum extending into the posterior insula and the right precuneus, regions involved in the somatosensory representation. Neural responses – Autonomic Nervous System – Subjective emotional experience

Skin Conductance Responses (SCRs), used as an index of autonomic nervous system activity, significantly mediated the associations between VITS expression and both subjective arousal and valence. For arousal, VITS expression showed a significant total effect (path c; t=2.89, p=0.007), and higher VITS responses predicted greater SCR amplitudes (path a; t=2.95, p=0.005), which in turn predicted higher perceived arousal (path b; t=4.33, p=0.001). The indirect effect (a × b) was significant (t=2.55, p=0.004), while the direct effect (c’) was not (t=1.42, p=0.144), indicating full mediation. For valence, VITS expression again predicted subjective ratings (path c; t=3.20, p=0.001), and SCRs significantly mediated this association (significant a, b, and a × b paths; t=2.97, p=0.004; t=4.15, p<0.001; t=2.51, p=0.002, respectively). However, the direct effect (c’) remained significant (t=2.28, p=0.009), indicating partial mediation.

Associations with anxiety and threat sensitivity

Cross-validated classification of upper vs. lower PCA-derived anxiety tertiles reached 64% accuracy (p = .005). Stable predictors included negative CS+early and positive CS+late and CS–late VITS expression. Trait-specific classification models were significant for STAI-T (66%, p = .001) and SPSR-Punishment (68%, p < .001), with smaller but significant effects for DASS-Anxiety and Stress. Across analyses, late-phase VITS responses (CS+late and CS–late) were the most consistent predictors of higher anxiety.

Conclusion

We identified a robust and generalizable whole-brain multivariate signature of visually induced threat appraisal (VITS) that reliably discriminates conditioned threat from safety with high cross-validated accuracy and generalizes across learning phases, independent samples, and large multi-site datasets. The functional anatomy of the VITS provides important insights into the neural architecture that supports threat appraisal. Positive weights were located in regions that form part of the core fear-learning circuitry (i.e. amygdala, ACC, and medial prefrontal cortex) which coordinate threat detection, associative learning, and defensive responding.3,4 Together with the PAG pathway, these regions reflect the integration of autonomic control processes.5 In contrast, negative weights emerged in two large-scale systems linked to self-related and sensorimotor representation. First, regions in the postcentral gyrus, parietal operculum, and posterior insula—which are involved in somatosensory representation, sensory integration, and motor preparation—showed substantially reduced expression. Second, additional negative clusters were observed in regions typically included in the DMN, including vmPFC, OFC, precuneus, and superior/middle temporal cortex, suggesting a down-regulation of self-referential and contextual integration processes.5 Notably, a similar deactivation pattern in these DMN and somatosensory regions has been reported in previous work using fMRI data from fear conditioning-tasks.4 Taken together, this pattern suggests a coordinated and relative upregulation of salience and defensive-control networks, accompanied by reduced engagement of somatosensory and self-related representational systems.

Beyond its anatomical characterization, the mediation analyses showed that autonomic arousal (SCRs) significantly mediated the relationship between VITS expression and subjective valence and arousal. This finding suggests that physiological arousal serves as a key mechanism through which large-scale neural threat processing influences the perceived emotional experience of threat cues.

Finally, VITS expression showed robust associations with individual differences in anxiety and threat sensitivity. Across analyses, individuals with higher anxiety-threat sensitibity exhibited proportionally greater VITS responses during late phases of learning for both CS+ (threat) and CS- (safety) cues, alongside with proportionally reduced early responses to CS+. 

Together, these findings establish the VITS as a robust, generalizable, and mechanistically interpretable whole-brain signature of visually induced threat-appraisal. They highlight the relevance of learning-phases dynamics in explaining anxiety variability and underscore autonomic arousal as a central component linking brain activity to subjective threat experience.
