Introduction

Diffusion MRI enables quantitative investigation of white matter organization and microstructure in vivo¹ ². Despite its widespread use, most reconstruction approaches rely on inverse modeling techniques that estimate microstructural parameters by fitting specific models to the measured signal. These approaches require strong model based assumptions, depend on optimization procedures that may converge to local minima, and often become unstable in regions that contain shallow fiber crossings where multiple configurations can produce similar signals. As a result, separate pipelines are typically needed to obtain diffusion tensor metrics, kurtosis based measures, and multicompartment features such as neurite density, dispersion, or free water, which can introduce inconsistencies across models and across datasets.

FORCE addresses these limitations by adopting a forward modeling strategy that generates a large ensemble of biophysically plausible diffusion signals and identifies the closest match to each voxel using cosine similarity. This shift reframes diffusion MRI analysis around the generative relationship between microstructure and signal rather than an inversion process that attempts to estimate parameters directly from the measured data. By evaluating many possible microstructural configurations in signal space, FORCE simultaneously estimates fiber orientations, tissue fractions, and a broad suite of diffusion and multicompartment indices from a single procedure. This approach avoids the instability of nonlinear fitting routines and allows the recovery of configurations that are difficult to resolve using inverse models. The goal of this framework is to offer a unified, robust, and scalable alternative for diffusion MRI modeling across acquisition types and biological conditions.

Methods

A total of 500000 biophysically grounded diffusion signals were generated to span a wide range of plausible microstructural configurations. Each simulated voxel was modeled as a mixture of white matter, gray matter, and free water. These partial volumes were jointly sampled from a Dirichlet distribution, which ensured realistic combinations that sum to one and allowed smooth variation across the full range of possible tissue compositions. White matter could contain zero, one, two, or three fiber populations. Fiber orientations were sampled from a uniformly distributed spherical grid, and dispersion was modeled using a Bingham distribution with parameters drawn from biologically plausible ranges. Intra axonal diffusion was modeled using a stick representation with zero radial diffusivity, while extra axonal diffusion followed an anisotropic Gaussian distribution. Axial and radial diffusivities were sampled from literature supported intervals ⁵. Gray matter and free water compartments were modeled as isotropic Gaussians with fixed diffusivities.

The biophysical parameters used in the simulations were selected to reflect realistic ranges observed in healthy and pathological brain tissue. Axial diffusivities for intra and extra axonal compartments were sampled between 2.0 and 3.0 × 10⁻³ mm²/s in vivo, consistent with reported values for diffusion along axons in human white matter. Radial extra axonal diffusivities were sampled between 0.1 and 1.5 × 10⁻³ mm²/s to capture variation in extracellular spacing and myelination. For ex vivo conditions, axial diffusivities were sampled between 0.6 and 1.0 × 10⁻³ mm²/s and radial diffusivities between 0.01 and 0.35 × 10⁻³ mm²/s to reflect reduced diffusivity following fixation. Intra axonal diffusion followed a stick model with zero radial diffusivity, consistent with strong radial restriction within axons. Orientation dispersion indices for white matter ranged from 0.01 to 0.30, and gray matter and free water diffusivities were fixed at 1.0 × 10⁻³ mm²/s and 3.0 × 10⁻³ mm²/s, respectively. Fiber weights and fiber count probabilities were chosen to represent the diversity of single fiber, fanning, and crossing configurations.

For each voxel of the measured diffusion MRI dataset, the diffusion weighted signal was compared against all simulated signals using cosine similarity. This metric captures the directional structure of the diffusion profile while remaining invariant to global signal scaling. A small penalty term discouraged matches that contained more fiber populations than necessary. The simulation that achieved the highest penalized similarity score provided the fiber orientations, tissue fractions, and microstructural properties for that voxel. To characterize degeneracy in the signal space, uncertainty and ambiguity metrics were derived from the similarity distribution of the matched candidates. Uncertainty was defined as the interquartile range, indicating how broadly plausible configurations were distributed. Ambiguity was defined as the proportion of candidates whose similarity exceeded half of the maximum similarity, providing a normalized measure of how many configurations were nearly tied with the best match. The framework was evaluated on synthetic two fiber configurations spanning 10 to 90 degree crossings across multiple SNR levels. It was further validated on the DiSCo phantom, where tractography and connectivity analyses were compared with ground truth. In vivo experiments included the HCP multi shell dataset and the single shell Stanford HARDI dataset. Ex vivo tests included high resolution mouse brain and human post mortem datasets with reduced diffusivity. FORCE was also applied to clinical cohorts involving glioma and Parkinson disease to evaluate biological specificity. Comparisons included CSA, CSD 4, GQI, ODFFP, AMICO NODDI³, and standard diffusion tensor¹ and kurtosis² fitting.

Results

In synthetic experiments, FORCE resolved shallow fiber crossings with high accuracy. For angles between 10 and 20 degrees, FORCE exceeded 80 percent accuracy at SNR 50, while CSA, CSD, GQI, and ODFFP often fell below 40 to 60 percent in the same regime. This advantage remained consistent as noise increased. FORCE also demonstrated reliable fiber number estimation, achieving greater than 90 percent accuracy at high SNR and showing reduced degradation relative to other methods as SNR decreased. These tests demonstrated that the forward modeling strategy supports precise recovery of low angle crossings that typically challenge inverse methods.

In the DiSCo phantom, FORCE produced tractography solutions that closely matched the ground truth geometry. Connectivity matrices achieved correlations of 0.894 at SNR 50 and 0.868 at SNR 10. These values matched or surpassed MSMT CSD under comparable conditions, including scenarios where high b value shells were excluded. The results confirmed that the orientations estimated by FORCE support robust downstream tractography.

In vivo HCP data showed that FORCE reproduced tensor based metrics with minimal voxelwise residuals, reflecting strong agreement with conventional fitting. DKI metrics such as radial kurtosis were more anatomically stable than those obtained from standard fitting, particularly in regions where complex fiber configurations challenge inverse models. Multicompartment measures also showed improved specificity. ODI displayed a stronger correlation with inverted T1 contrast for FORCE (r = 0.93) than for AMICO (r = 0.82), and neurite density showed a similar improvement, with FORCE achieving r = 0.90 compared with r = 0.88 for AMICO. FORCE further avoided free water overestimation near the ventricles, a known artifact in AMICO that arises from fixed diffusivity assumptions. Figure 2 shows FORCE results on an axial slice from an HCP 3T dataset, comparing FORCE with DTI, DKI, and AMICO. FORCE produces tensor maps with negligible residuals relative to DTI, yields more anatomically stable DKI metrics, and provides clearer ODI, NDI, and free water contrasts than AMICO, illustrating both quantitative and visual improvements across model families.

Ex vivo datasets confirmed the robustness of the approach in environments with altered diffusivity. FORCE produced smooth and anatomically coherent NDI, ODI, and free water maps, with lower residuals at tissue boundaries compared with AMICO. High resolution mouse data demonstrated stable DTI and NODDI like indices despite single shell acquisition, indicating that the forward modeling approach can extract meaningful microstructural information even from constrained acquisitions.

Clinical analyses reproduced expected pathological patterns. In glioma, FORCE detected markedly increased free water and reduced neurite density in and around tumor tissue, consistent with extracellular fluid accumulation and disrupted cellular architecture. In Parkinson disease, tractometry using FORCE derived bundles and scalar profiles revealed segment specific alterations that matched previously reported BUAN findings. Conclusion

FORCE introduces a unified forward modeling strategy for diffusion MRI that estimates fiber orientations, tissue composition, and microstructural parameters by matching measured signals to biophysically plausible simulations. The approach resolves shallow crossings with high accuracy, generalizes across synthetic, phantom, in vivo, ex vivo, and clinical datasets, and provides DTI, DKI, and NODDI like maps without the instability of inverse fitting. FORCE supports tractography and tractometry pipelines, avoids common artifacts such as ventricular free water inflation, and produces coherent tissue segmentations from a single reconstruction. By combining physical modeling with scale invariant matching, FORCE offers a robust and biologically grounded alternative for diffusion MRI analysis.
