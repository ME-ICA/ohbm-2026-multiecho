Introduction

Recent neuroimaging studies using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) have shown that functional connectivity based on correlation reveals functional hierarchical organization of the brain (Margulies et al., 2016). Although correlation-based connectivity is a major methodological approach to illuminate the brain functional connectivity in fMRI, recent studies have also used phase synchronization between blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signals of two brain regions as alternative functional connectivity measurements. However, it remains unknown how phase-synchronization-based functional connectivity and hierarchical organization in the brain are related to each other. Moreover, from phase synchronization, we can estimate the mean phase difference of manifest phase synchronization. However, it is also unknown how this mean phase difference of manifest phase synchronization is related to brain hierarchical organization. In this study, therefore, we investigate how phase synchronization, as well as mean phase difference, is related to brain hierarchical organization. Furthermore, we investigate whether this relationship varies with diverse brain states representing arousal levels, behavioral tasks, and pathological states.

Methods

We analyzed a publicly available fMRI dataset (N = 27) (Lee et al., 2022a; 2022b) scanned during resting-states with pupillometry to measure changes of arousal revels. Preprocessed data were partitioned into 200 parcels based on the Schaefer’s atlas indexed with 7 functional networks: Visual network (Vis), Somatomotor network (SM), Dorsal attention network (DA), Ventral attention network (VA), Limbic network (Lim), Frontoparietal network (FP), and Default mode network (DM). BOLD signals within a parcel were averaged, then band-pass filtered at slow-4 frequency band (0.027–0.073 Hz). Using the Hilbert transform, we extracted oscillatory phases of BOLD signals per parcel. The phase difference between every pair of parcels was calculated at every time instant. Then, the phase difference was located on the complex plain as a vector with unit length. The circular mean of the phase differences during a given condition was calculated. Its magnitude represented phase synchronization, while its angle represented the mean phase difference. For each participant in a given condition, there were 200 X 200 = 40,000 parcel-pairs in which be calculated phase synchronization. Among these, upper 30 % of phase synchronization values were defined as manifest phase synchronization. Furthermore, we calculated mean phase differences in the parcel-pairs which represented manifest phase synchronization. Among these mean phase differences, lower 33.33 % were defined as lag mean phase differences, while upper 33.33 % were defined as lead mean phase differences. 

In the analysis, we calculated the ratio of manifest phase synchronization in the whole parcels or in the set of parcels belonging to a certain network. Furthermore, we calculated the ratio of lead-mean phase differences minus lag-mean phase differences, i.e., the lead-lag phase difference, in the whole parcels or in the set of parcels belonging to a certain network. To analyze the relationship between brain hierarchy over networks and ratios of manifest phase synchronization, or that and lead-lag phase difference, we used linear regression (p < 0.05). 

In a subsequent analysis, to investigate the relationship between phase synchronization and brain hierarchical organization in other brain states, including behavioral tasks and pathological states, we analyzed another publicly available fMRI dataset scanned during working memory tasks (Repovš and Barch, 2018; 2012). This dataset consists of two groups: healthy controls (HC, N = 39) and schizophrenic patients (SZ, N = 20). Methods of fMRI processing were similar to above methods, but ratios of manifest phase synchronization and ratios of lead-lag phase difference were analyzed network-wise. Comparisons between groups were performed using Wilcoxon rank sum test with significant level of false discovery rate (FDR)-corrected p < 0.05.

Results

We first analyzed the relationship between ratios of manifest phase synchronization or ratios of lead-lag phase differences and brain hierarchical organization in resting-states. Brain hierarchical organization we analyzed had two axes: 1) From unimodal to transmodal and limbic axis (Vis and SM – DA and VA – DM, FP, Lim) and 2) cross-modals axis (Vis – DA - DM, FP, and Lim – VA – SM). Notably, ratios of manifest phase synchronization and the 1st axis had linear relationships with a negative slope (N = 25 X 7 = 175, R-square = 0.55, p = 3.99e-32). Moreover, ratios of lead-lag phase difference and the 2nd axis had linear relationship with a negative slope (N = 25 X 7 = 175, R-square = 0.32, p = 2.11e-16). The ratios of manifest phase synchronization between DM and Vis, Lim differed during high-arousal states from those during low-arousal states (Wilcoxon rank sum test, N = 25, FDR-corrected p < 0.05). Furthermore, the ratios of lead-lag phase difference between DM and all other networks differed during high-arousal states from those during low-arousal states (Wilcoxon rank sun test, N = 25, FDR-corrected p < 0.05).

In the subsequent analyses, we first compared healthy groups during resting-states and during working memory tasks. Ratios of manifest phase synchronization between Lim and all networks were changed between the states (Wilcoxon rank sun test, N = 25 and 39, FDR-corrected p < 0.05). Then, we compared HC and SZ groups during working memory tasks. Ratios of manifest phase synchronization between FP and SM were significantly different between the groups (Wilcoxon rank sun test, N = 39 and 20, FDR-corrected p < 0.05).

Conclusion

Our results revealed that the ratios of manifest phase synchronization and the 1st axis had linear relationships with a negative slope. This indicates that brain regions in lower hierarchy have manifest phase synchronization with broad brain regions, while brain regions in upper hierarchy have manifest phase synchronization with limited brain regions. This may suggest how the brain functional connectivity accords with brain hierarchy. Furthermore, the ratios of lead-lag phase difference and the 2nd axis had linear relationship with a negative slope. That means that Vis leads other networks and SM lags other networks, suggesting the order of BOLD signals propagation. However, it is uncertain whether this order represents the order of neural information processing due to the very slow time scale. Furthermore, hierarchical representation differed between high and low arousal states, between rest and task, and between HC and SZ. Such results suggested that hierarchical representation varied with diverse brain states, including arousal levels, behavioral tasks, and pathological states, exhibiting state-dependent hierarchical organization.
