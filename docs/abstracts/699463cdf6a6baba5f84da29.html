Introduction

Most fMRI studies typically perform static functional connectivity (SFC) that estimates relationships between brain regions across the entire time series. However, this approach is inherently undirected and fails to model temporal order or directional signal flow. To address this gap, we applied the Graph-based Diffusion Autoregressive (GDAR) model that quantifies directed information propagation.1 Additionally, traditional fMRI scans take 2-3 seconds to acquire a whole-brain image, a sampling rate too slow to understand how information flows through the brain and to capture rapid neural dynamics. We overcome this limitation using simultaneous multislice (SMS) acquisition, a parallel imaging approach that achieves sub-second repetition times (TR).2 By combining GDAR with high-temporal-resolution SMS fMRI, we can investigate the directional flow of neural communication in ADHD for the first time. We refer to directional flow as "brain traffic" in this study. ADHD is a highly heterogeneous disorder in which males and females often present distinct symptom profiles. However, it remains unknown whether these sex differences can be attributed to patterns of directional neural flow, particularly within key networks such as the salience network (SAL). The SAL network, anchored in the anterior insula and dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, is crucial for detecting salient events and initiating cognitive control.3 Here, we propose using GDAR-based directed modeling to analyze fast SMS fMRI data and identify potential ADHD signatures through brain traffic patterns, with a specific focus on comparing the left and right hemispheric differences in the SAL network between males and females.

Methods

This study received ethical approval from the New Zealand Health and Disability Ethics Committee (21/CEN/230) and was conducted at the Matai Medical Research Institute. We recruited 20 participants diagnosed with ADHD and 20 neurotypical (NT) control participants. Among the ADHD group, 8 participants completed the Flanker task, while among the NT group, 5 participants completed the task. All participants provided written informed consent before scanning. Participants with ADHD abstained from medication for 24 hours prior to the session. Each participant underwent two fMRI paradigms: a resting-state scan and a continuous Flanker task. Data were acquired on a 3T GE SIGNA Premier MRI scanner using a simultaneous multislice (SMS) sequence with the following parameters: TR = 0.43 s, TE = 0.02 s, flip angle = 80°, slice thickness = 3 mm, number of slices = 50, field of view = 240 × 240 × 150 mm³, voxel size = 1.88 × 1.88 mm², GRAPPA acceleration factor = 2, and multiband factor = 8. We preprocessed functional data using the CONN toolbox (Whitfield-Gabrieli & Nieto-Castanon, 2012).4 Steps included realignment and unwarping for motion correction, spatial normalization to MNI space, outlier scan detection, spatial smoothing (5 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel), and bandpass filtering (0.01–0.25 Hz) to retain physiologically relevant frequencies.5 We performed linear regression to remove signals from cerebrospinal fluid, white matter, and motion parameters. We extracted time series data from 32 regions of interest, derived from an independent component analysis of the Human Connectome Project dataset (N = 497).4 These regions are parcellated into eight functional networks: default mode (DMN), sensorimotor (SMN), visual (vis), salience (SAL), dorsal attention (DAN), frontoparietal (FPN), language (LAN), and cerebellar (CER) networks. Directed functional flow was estimated using a Graph Diffusion Autoregressive (GDAR) model (Figure 1) that was adapted from a diffusion-constrained autoregressive framework.1 This model integrates the temporal flow of BOLD activity with a structural prior to constrain physiologically plausible signal propagation. The structural prior was simulated using a Network-Enhanced Distance Calculation (NEDC) based on pairwise Euclidean distances between the 32 regions. The resulting connection matrix was thresholded at 15% sparsity to create a biologically plausible network. The incidence matrix of the structural prior and functional time series was integrated into a constrained vector autoregressive (VAR) model to estimate autoregressive and diffusivity weights. Instantaneous GDAR flow along each directed edge was computed using a model order of p = 15. We then calculated the average GDAR flow by taking the temporal mean of absolute instantaneous flow over the entire observation period. This produced an undirected flow magnitude for each connection, representing the mean strength of information propagation between two regions. Finally, group-level statistical analysis compared brain traffic between resting-state and task conditions. For each edge, we used Welch's t-test with significance set at an uncorrected threshold of p < 0.05.

Results

Our analysis revealed that biological sex modulates brain traffic in ADHD differently between rest and task conditions. Overall, our findings revealed that ADHD females showed an increase (mostly RED) in directed signal flow when performing a cognitive task compared to resting-state, as shown in Figure 2A. In contrast, ADHD males exhibited an increase (RED) and a decrease (BLUE) in brain traffic when performing a task. Figures 2B and 2C expand this by showing the significant rest–task modulation in ADHD males and females, respectively.

In Figure 2B, ADHD males during task performance showed increased brain traffic within specific networks, such as the SMN (U = 29.00, p = 0.0081, |r| = 0.638) and the right hemisphere of the SAL network (U = 33.00, p = 0.016, |r| = 0.588). Moreover, brain traffic was also intensified between networks SAL to FPN (U = 34.00, p = 0.018, |r| = 0.575). On the other hand, more brain traffic emerged during resting-state than during task performance in the left hemisphere of the SAL network (U = 122.00, p = 0.033, |r| = 0.525) and between SAL and DAN networks (U = 136.00, p = 0.003, |r| = 0.700). Overall, this suggests that ADHD males exhibit right hemispheric task engagement and left hemispheric resting activity.

Figure 2C revealed that ADHD females demonstrated a more selective modulation pattern. Task performance increased brain traffic activity within the right hemisphere of the SAL network (U = 19.00, p = 0.035, |r| = 0.620), a trend that was also observed in males. Additionally, ADHD females have stronger LAN network (U = 20.00, p = 0.042, |r| = 0.600) brain traffic during task performance compared to resting-state. The elevated right SAL hemisphere brain traffic activity supports previous work demonstrating that the right anterior insula regulates DMN and CEN networks for less complex cognitive task processes.3 Despite these trends being strong, the significance did not remain following Bonferroni correction for multiple statistical comparisons across all tested connections. This means our findings are exploratory and require replication in larger, independent samples before firm conclusions can be drawn. Nevertheless, the consistency of the sex-specific patterns in the distribution of brain traffic between rest and task provides compelling preliminary evidence.

This study is the first to characterize the directed neural propagation in ADHD using high-temporal-resolution fMRI (TR=0.43s). Previous research has documented alterations in static functional connectivity (FC), but reports remain inconclusive, and findings often contradict each other. Our application of the GDAR model advances beyond basic FC correlation to reveal how information flows within and between networks. The integration of ultra-fast SMS fMRI with directed modeling uncovered sex-divergent modulation patterns that have revealed new potential signatures for ADHD between resting-state and task conditions.

Conclusion

This study combined high temporal resolution SMS fMRI with directed GDAR modeling to characterize brain traffic in ADHD. We demonstrated that ADHD females exhibit greater overall brain traffic during tasks than at resting-state, relative to males, while ADHD males show elevated brain traffic in resting activity that decreases during task engagement, relative to females. Furthermore, we observed hemispheric localization between resting-state and task conditions. Task performance drives brain traffic in the right hemisphere of the SAL, whereas resting state drives the brain traffic of the left hemisphere of the SAL. While the exploratory nature of this study suggests replication in larger samples, the overall framework establishes directed brain traffic as a powerful new metric for understanding ADHD. By moving beyond static FC and undirected correlation to directed flow modelling of brain networks, we were able to gain an improved understanding of ADHD. This work highlights the importance of considering biological sex and fMRI paradigm (resting-state or task) for investigating neural signatures of ADHD. Future research should investigate whether these sex-specific traffic patterns relate to symptom profiles, treatment responses, or developmental trajectories in ADHD.
