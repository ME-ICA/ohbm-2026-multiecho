Introduction

Temporal lobe seizures in epilepsy frequently co-occur with transient shifts in autonomic balance toward sympathetic dominance, manifesting as tachycardia, elevated blood pressure, and excessive catecholamine release.1 Ictal sympathetic activation is more severe in seizures that generalize to tonic-clonic seizures (TCS),2 and strong evidence has implicated prolonged cardiorespiratory deficits during TCS as a major cause of sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP).3 However, the neural mechanisms underlying autonomic dysfunction in TCS remain largely unknown. Therefore, we aimed to identify electrophysiological biomarkers of cardiac autonomic instability during temporal lobe seizures with secondary generalization (focal-to-bilateral TCS [FBTCS]). We focused our investigation on the insula and orbitofrontal cortex, which are key areas involved in central autonomic regulation and interoception.1

Methods

This study included simultaneous EKG and stereo-EEG (SEEG) data from 65 temporal lobe seizures recorded during long-term monitoring in 17 epilepsy patients. The seizures were categorized into three types according to clinical awareness testing: focal aware seizures (FAS; n = 16), focal impaired awareness seizures (FIAS; n = 34), and FBTCS (n = 15). From the EKG data, we computed the peak increase in heart rate (HR) during the early ictal and postictal periods relative to the preictal period as a measure of cardiac sympathetic activation (Fig. 1). The early ictal and postictal periods were defined as the first 70 seconds of each seizure and as the first 70 seconds following seizure termination, respectively. This time-period was chosen to exclude movement artifacts and the secondary generalization phase occurring during FBTCS.

SEEG contacts were grouped according to three brain areas ipsilateral to the side of seizure onset: the seizure onset zone (SOZ) in the temporal lobe, the insula, and the inferior frontal gyrus/orbitofrontal cortex (IFG/OFC). We then estimated the spectral power of these brain areas in six frequency bands during the early ictal and postictal periods relative to the preictal baseline. Linear mixed-effects models were employed to compare the HR and SEEG power metrics between the three seizure types and to relate the SEEG power to the HR during the ictal and postictal periods. The side of seizure onset was included as a covariate in the models (44 left-onset and 21 right-onset seizures). Statistical significance was assessed at p < 0.05 with false discovery rate (FDR) adjustment.

Results

The peak increase in HR was significantly larger for FBTCS than FIAS and FAS during both the early ictal and postictal periods (Fig. 1). During the early ictal period, FBTCS exhibited significantly greater alpha, beta, and gamma power in the IFG/OFC compared to FIAS and in both the IFG/OFC and insula compared to FAS (beta band shown in Fig. 2a). However, the delta, theta, alpha, beta, and gamma power during the early postictal period was significantly lower for FBTCS than FIAS and FAS in the SOZ, IFG/OFC, and insula (beta band shown in Fig. 2b). Additionally, a larger peak increase in HR was significantly associated with greater theta, alpha, beta, and gamma power in the insula and IFG/OFC during the ictal period and with reduced power in the SOZ, IFG/OFC, and insula during the postictal period (beta band shown in Fig. 2c,d).

Conclusion

Our results agree with prior observations of greater sympathetic activity during FBTCS compared to non-convulsive seizures.2 In addition, we found that the early ictal period of FBTCS is characterized by greater high-frequency power in the IFG/OFC and insula, which may correspond to greater brain activation in extratemporal regions even before secondary generalization.4 Postictally, FBTCS exhibited prominent power reductions in the SOZ, IFG/OFC, and insula, consistent with previous reports of neural suppression following FBTCS.5 Furthermore, our findings suggest that more severe cardiac autonomic dysfunction is accompanied by stronger ictal activation and postictal deactivation in central autonomic regions, such as the IFG/OFC and insula. Future studies with a larger sample size will be performed to investigate how the relationship between SEEG power and HR changes across seizure types. Overall, mapping these electrophysiological power dynamics may aid in identifying biomarkers predictive of seizure severity and in uncovering therapeutic brain targets to reduce the risk of SUDEP.
