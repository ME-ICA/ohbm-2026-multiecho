Introduction

Naturalistic viewing, or movie watching, provides a powerful tool to study high-order brain functions. Specifically, ecologically valid, multimodal and dynamic naturalistic stimuli allow for capturing complex emotional processing (Sonkusare et al, 2019). Previous research shows that cognitive functions are not static, but rather exhibit fluctuating whole-brain configurations. Hidden Markov Models (HMM) introduce a framework capturing these discrete recurring cortical patterns, or brain states (Meer et al., 2020). In this study, we aimed to identify the recurring brain states modulated by highly emotional movie clips. We applied HMM framework to fMRI data of subjects watching 7 clips, associated resulting brain states with time-resolved emotional annotations and characterised them according to Neurosynth-derived functional classification.

Methods

For this analysis we used fMRI data of 60 subjects obtained with 3T Siemens Prisma MRI. Participants were presented with 7 clips from Hollywood movies ranging from 7 to 10 minutes. Additionally, an independent sample of 39 participants rated the emotional intensity of each movie clip according to 6 emotions: fear, disgust, joy, surprise, sadness, and anger. The ratings were made at each second, shuffled across participants, resulting in the value from 0 to 100 at each 100 ms. We calculated the BOLD signals according to the Schaefer atlas. Next, 400 parcels were assigned to 17 networks (Yeo et al., 2011), averaged across the nodes within each network and z-scored across subjects. HMM was constructed with the OSL Dynamics toolbox (Gohil et al., 2023). After testing the model parameters, we selected a learning rate of 0.1, 40 epochs and 50 iterations per state numbers from 2 to 20. According to the lowest free energy and the lowest correlation between the states, we selected the best fitting model with 8 brain states. Next, we calculated multiple linear regression between the probability of each brain state and emotional annotations. Additionally, In order to understand functional significance of the brain states we applied functional decoding using neurosynth within the Nimare toolbox (Salo et al., 2023).

Results

We identified 8 brain states across participants and movie clips that showed unique whole-brain activation profiles and connections to emotional annotations (Fig 1). State 1 showed dominant activations in premotor, primary motor and somatosensory cortices (M1, S1) and suppression of dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (dmPFC) and social-cognitive systems of default mode network (DMN). This state is positively associated with disgust (p = 0.001) and negatively with joy (p = 0.035) and anger (p = 0.008). On the contrary, we observed the opposite associations with the emotional annotations and brain state 6: positive with joy (p = 0.002) and anger (p < 0.001) and negative with disgust (p = 0.002). State 6 consisted of increased activations in early, ventral occipital and fusiform areas, while parietal-premotor network showed decreased activation. Additionally, we discovered increased integration in state 2 with early visual and anterior temporal semantic pathways and decreased activity in the frontoparietal network (FPN). This state was expressed during fear (p < 0.001) and surprise (p < 0.001) and negatively correlated with joy (p = 0.001) and sadness (p < 0.001), suggesting decoding of salient events. Two states exhibited strong semantic-linguistic structure: state 3 with increased dmPFC activity and midtemporal gyrus (MTG) and decreased somatomotor areas, while state 5 anterior temporal lobe, MTG and language specific frontotemporal areas, accompanied with suppression of visual areas. State 3 positively associated with sadness (p = 0.011) and negatively with fear (p < 0.001) and joy (p < 0.001), state 5 - positively with anger (p = 0.005). Lastly, three other states exhibited distinct DMN-based activations. State 4 consists primarily of strong activations in posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) and ventromedial areas, related to reflective cognitive states, positively associating with sadness (p = 0.004). State 7 involves PCC precuneus and retrosplenial cortex (RSC) and suppression of ventral visual cortex expressed during sadness (p < 0.001) and decreased during joy (p = 0.004) and anger (p = 0.001). State 8 was expressed as a conceptual and autobiographical state, positively associated with fear (p < 0.001) and joy (p = 0.039), while suppressing surprise (p < 0.001) and anger (p = 0.02).

Conclusion

These findings reveal dynamic and functionally distinct brain configurations in response to the valence of the naturalistic stimuli. Processing of narratively complex and emotional stimuli, such as naturalistic viewing, requires rapid transitions between functionally relevant cortical states, ranging from perceptual, sensorimotor, semantic and memory related modes. Namely, the motor and ventral visual states respond to action-related and salient content, visual-semantic and linguistic-semantic highlight the necessary transitions between perceptual integration and conceptual inference and DMN related states show internally oriented processing. Importantly, emotional valence did not simply modulate the amplitude of a specific network, but rather adjusted functional requirements according to the cognitive demand.
