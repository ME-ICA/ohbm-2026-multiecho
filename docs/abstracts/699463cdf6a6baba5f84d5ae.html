Introduction

In children with drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE), brain surgery can be an effective treatment; yet up to 40% of patients continue to have seizures after resection. Intracranial EEG directly records from the brain tissue and provides essential biomarkers of the epileptogenic tissue, but it is invasive and cannot be used routinely. This creates a need for noninvasive scalp EEG biomarkers that can identify epileptogenic tissue prior to invasive and guide surgical decisions. Among interictal EEG tools (that do not depend on seizure capturing), spike localization is the current clinical standard for epileptogenic zone localization; however, spikes can arise from non-epileptogenic regions as well, thus lacking specificity, which limits their reliability for planning surgery. More robust EEG-derived measures are needed.

As epilepsy is increasingly viewed as a brain network disorder, we hypothesize that the presurgical value of interictal EEG can be augmented by capturing the behavior of the underlying EEG networks rather solely localizing independent spike foci. Our recent work has combined electrical source imaging with functional connectivity analysis of interictal EEG to characterize whole-brain networks, extract connectivity biomarkers of the epileptogenic zone, and localize the resected area, outperforming spike localization1. This study highlighted the value of connectome-based approaches in the presurgical evaluation of children with DRE, encouraging further validation of EEG-connectome, AI-driven approaches for postsurgical outcome prediction.

This study aims to develop a noninvasive, connectome-based EEG framework to predict the postsurgical seizure outcome of children undergoing epilepsy surgery based on a virtual resection approach. We trained machine learning (ML) classifiers on source-imaged connectivity features extracted separately from brief EEG epochs with or without visible epileptiform activity.

Methods

We studied children with DRE who underwent resective surgery and had a known 1-year postsurgical outcome (Engel class). All patients had presurgical scalp EEG and structural MRI acquired as part of their clinical work-up. Scalp EEG recordings were reviewed and artifact-free sleep segments were labelled as either epileptiform (containing interictal spikes) or non-epileptiform (background activity without visible spikes). 

For each child, we selected multiple 3-s epochs of both types (epileptiform or not) and analyzed them separately, as outlined in Fig. 1. EEG source activity was reconstructed with electrical source imaging on an individual head model derived from each child’s MRI (Fig. 1A–B). We solved the inverse problem using a Linearly Constrained Minimum Variance (LCMV) beamformer on a cortical grid of ~15,000 points and then downsampled to ~1,000 representative sources per subject. 

For each subject, we computed whole-brain functional connectivity using the amplitude envelope correlation (AEC) method across six frequency bands (delta: 1-4 Hz, theta: 4-8 Hz, alpha: 8-12 Hz, beta: 12-30 Hz, low-gamma: 30-70 Hz, broadband: 1-70 Hz) on the reconstructed source-level time series, separately for epileptiform and non-epileptiform epochs. Using postoperative MRI, we derived an individual resection mask and defined two subnetworks: a “resected” (targeted) network, containing nodes within the resection, and a “residual” network, containing nodes spared by surgery (Fig. 1B). We thus calculated functional connectivity matrices separately for the resected and residual areas. From these matrices, we derived graph features, including measures of network node distribution (i.e. degree, closeness, betweenness centrality, eigenvector centrality, clustering coefficient) and of global topology (characteristic path length, efficiency, modularity, assortativity), to characterize each patient’s network organization. Each feature was compared between good and poor surgical outcomes using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, and their discriminative value was also quantified with the area under the ROC curve (AUC; Fig. 1C).

To predict individual outcomes, we trained five ML classifiers (listed in Fig. 1D). For each condition, we applied feature selection based on either the AUC of single features or recursive feature elimination to identify the most informative subset and reduce risk of overfitting. The selected features were then used to train the classifiers with 5-fold cross-validation. Classification performances (e.g., balanced accuracy, AUC, F1-Score) were evaluated on the held-out fold and then averaged across folds (Fig. 1D).

To assess the added value of connectome-based analysis, we also performed spike localization using three standard methods (dipole fitting, beamforming, and cMEM). For each methodology, we computed features of spike localization distribution (similarly to the network features) and used them as inputs for ML training. This allowed us to compare outcome prediction based on spike localization versus network-aware features.

Results

We included 66 children with DRE (mean age = 10.6 ± 5.6 years; 65% Engel class I: good outcome). Looking at individual EEG network features (Fig. 2A), we observed that several properties of the targeted network differed between good and poor outcome patients (p<0.05). The targeted networks of patients who had good outcome were characterized by higher eigenvector centrality and degree, but lower clustering and variability: specifically, beta-band eigenvector centrality and alpha-band degree were higher in good than poor outcomes, whereas the skewness and variability of alpha/beta eigenvector centrality and alpha clustering coefficient were lower. Among these, beta-band median eigenvector centrality within the targeted network showed the strongest discriminative value (p=0.0066, AUC=0.70), followed by beta-band skewness of eigenvector centrality (p=0.0246, AUC=0.67), with the remaining features showing consistent effects (AUC ≈ 0.63–0.66).

For patient-level outcome prediction (Fig. 2B), the best performing model was a k-nearest neighbors (kNN) classifier, when features were selected by a recursive feature elimination procedure. Using spike-free EEG, the classifier reached 83% balanced accuracy (84% sensitivity, 83% specificity, p<0.00001). Using epileptiform EEG epochs, the same network-based approach achieved a slightly lower but still significant balanced accuracy of 75% (p=0.00004).

In contrast, features derived from conventional spike localization did not provide reliable predictions of postsurgical outcome. When the classifier was trained using only spike localization measures, balanced accuracy dropped to 62% and did not reach statistical significance (p=0.051). Overall, these results indicate that source-level network properties of the targeted brain subnetwork provide more informative and robust prognostic biomarkers than traditional spike localization alone.

Conclusion

Noninvasive EEG network analysis of interictal data, both with and without spikes, enables the accurate prediction of surgical outcomes in children with DRE. By focusing on the virtually resected area, our results show that beta-band eigenvector centrality within this targeted subnetwork is a strong predictor of postsurgical outcome and that network-based features yield substantially higher classification accuracy than spike-derived measures alone.

Our findings extend prior noninvasive source imaging studies, showing that interictal EEG connectivity, combined with ML, can delineate epileptogenic networks and candidate resection targets beyond conventional spike localization1. Additionally, resection of pathological hubs identified from M/EEG is more strongly associated with seizure freedom than dipole-based spike mapping2. Our results are also in line with invasive iEEG studies demonstrating that the degree of resection–hub alignment based on eigenvector centrality is a powerful predictor of postoperative seizure control3 and with graph-theoretical analyses of HFO networks where local centrality measures combined with ML better identify epileptogenic tissue than HFO counts alone4. Framed within the broader context of epilepsy as a network disorder, in which connectome fingerprints can inform surgical planning and outcome prediction5, these converging results support the use of scalp EEG based connectomics of the virtually resected network, derived from brief interictal EEG data, as a practical and scalable strategy for presurgical evaluation.

In conclusion, integrating this approach into clinical workflows could refine target selection, complement invasive recordings and multimodal imaging, and ultimately support more personalized, network-informed surgical procedures for children with DRE.
