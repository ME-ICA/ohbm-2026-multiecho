Introduction

Cortical structural alterations are widely reported across substance use disorders (SUDs) in neuroimaging studies1. These alterations show shared structural patterns across different SUDs2, and they also overlap with structural abnormalities observed in major psychiatric disorders3, partly due to co-morbidities. Several mechanisms may contribute to these structural alterations. Early life adversity can lead to developmental brain changes and increase the vulnerability to later substance use4 and contribute to structural deviations observed in adolescence and adulthood. In the meantime, the neurotoxic effects of different substances can also induce cortical alterations, and it is also associated with cumulative substance exposure and addiction severity. However, it remains unclear how different morphological alterations in SUDs are embedded within large-scale brain networks, and how these patterns relate to early substance exposure and underlying neurodevelopmental mechanisms. In this study, we aim to investigate the joint embedding of substance use alteration networks to identify the substance-related effects across the cortex. We further examined how these structural alterations in SUDs relate to the shared co-alteration networks observed in other neuropsychiatric and early substance exposure.

Methods

To characterize substance-related alterations across adolescence and adulthood, we analyzed two large cohorts. The ENIGMA-SUD dataset, which included 4,332 individuals with SUD (age: 35.12 ± 11.93 years, 1360 females) and 2,756 healthy controls (age: 33.45 ± 12.41, 935 females), covered six substance categories: alcohol, methamphetamines, cannabis, cocaine, opioids, and nicotine. ABCD participants (enrolled in age 9 and 10 years) were divided into two groups as in a previous study: substance-naive participants (N = 7089, 3479 females), defined as individuals with no substance use from baseline to 4 years follow-up, and substance-use initiation participants (N = 3,029, 1369 females), defined by any reported substance use (alcohol, nicotine, cannabis, and others) during this period. Cortical thickness from both datasets was derived from T1-weighted FreeSurfer outputs and parcellated by the Desikan-Killiany atlas. Multisite cortical thickness data were harmonized using ComBat to remove the scanner- and site-related effects. We first analyzed the case-control differences in cortical thickness for each substance by estimating effect sizes across cortical regions (Fig. 1A), and constructed co-alteration matrices based on the covariance of these effect-size maps across substances and major psychiatric disorders (from a previous study5). We then applied dimensionality reduction to these matrices to identify latent dimensions that capture shared structural alteration patterns across SUDs and psychiatric disorders. In parallel, we derived low-dimensional embeddings of cortical-thickness structural covariance networks for both healthy and SUD participants. We then assessed the correspondence among these gradient maps using spatial correlation analyses. To assess whether early substance use affects cortical thickness development during adolescence (Fig. 1B), we used two complementary approaches. First, we applied linear mixed-effects models across three time points (baseline, 2-year, and 4-year follow-up) to test whether substance initiation was associated with baseline cortical thickness differences and with altered age-related thinning trajectories. Second, we quantified the cortical thickness change from baseline to the 4-year follow-up among participants with completed longitudinal data to directly compare the magnitude of cortical atrophy between substance-initiation and substance-naive adolescents. Together, these approaches allowed us to characterize both developmental trajectories and the overall magnitude of cortical-thickness change associated with adolescent substance use. Finally, we quantified the correspondence between these ABCD effect-size maps (from the mixed models and ΔCT analyses) and ENIGMA SUD-derived cortical gradients using spatial correlations.

Results

We revealed two principal gradients in SUD structural covariance networks (Fig. 2A). The primary gradient differentiated the temporal-parietal lobes from the rest of the cortex, while the second gradient extended from the bilateral insula, inferior temporal lobes, to cingulate, frontal lobe and the rest of the cortical regions. Both gradients showed strong and significant correlations with their corresponding components in healthy controls (Fig. 2B, G1: r = 0.91, Pspin < 0.001; G2: r = 0.58, Pspin = 0.022), indicating that SUD-related structural covariance networks largely preserve the macroscale cortical organization observed in non-SUD individuals. Participants with different SUDs also showed widespread cortical thickness reductions, with the strongest effects in alcohol and cocaine users (Fig. 1A), and we observed a significant correlation between G1 in SUD co-alteration and gradient 2 SUD structural covariance (Fig. 2B, r = 0.43, Pspin = 0.045). However, no significant correlations were detected between substance-use co-alteration and neuropsychiatric co-alteration gradients (Fig. 2B). Also, we found that substance-specific cortical alterations exhibited different correspondence with substance coalteration or substance structural covariance gradients (Fig. 2C), indicating that each substance contributes distinctively to the overall organization of SUD-related gradients. 

We then examined whether early substance use alters cortical developmental trajectories among adolescents. Using linear mixed models, we examined whether substance initiation was associated with baseline cortical-thickness differences and altered developmental trajectories (Fig. 2D). Group effects indicated that adolescents who initiated substance use showed thicker cortex at baseline relative to substance-naive peers. In addition, we observed widespread negative age × group interaction effects in most of the cortical areas, indicating that adolescents who initiated substance use showed accelerated cortical thinning relative to substance-naive peers. To complement the findings from the linear mixed model, we also compared 4-year cortical-thickness changes between groups among participants with complete longitudinal data (Fig. 2E). This analysis revealed weak, widespread negative effects, indicating slightly greater cortical thinning in the substance-initiation group during adolescence. Finally, we investigated how these early cortical changing patterns relate to ENIGMA SUD structural alterations. Spatial correlation analyses were performed between ABCD effect-size maps (ΔCT, group, and age × group from the linear mixed models) and the structural covariance and co-alteration gradients derived from the ENIGMA SUD cohort. These analyses revealed different correlation patterns between these effects with ENIGMA SUD gradient profiles(Fig. 2F), indicating that the extent of cortical thickness change, baseline differences, and the changing rate differences between substance initiation and substance-naive adolescents are related to ENIGMA SUD structural alteration patterns in different ways. Taken together, these findings suggest that early adolescent substance exposure is associated with the macroscale cortical organization that characterizes structural alterations in adult SUDs.

Conclusion

In summary, these findings indicate that SUDs are characterized by cortical co-alteration patterns that are distinct from those observed across major neuropsychiatric disorders. We further identified that adolescent substance exposure alters the cortical thinning trajectory, and this altered developmental pattern relates to structural alteration patterns that are observed in adults with SUDs. These results suggest a developmental continuity in how early substance exposure relates to cortical maturation trajectory, highlighting the need for future work to delineate substance-specific influences and their interactions with neurodevelopmental processes.
