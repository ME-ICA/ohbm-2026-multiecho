Introduction

The urge to use alcohol has been associated with various alcohol-related outcomes, such as binge drinking and relapse, whereas decreased craving correlates with improved long-term outcomes in individuals with alcohol use disorder (AUD). Hence, treatments targeting craving may lower the severity of AUD. A potential mechanism underlying craving for alcohol is co-occurring dysfunction of regions involved in cognitive control and reward processing, two intercorrelated but separate systems that drive addictive behaviors. The current study aimed to examine the effects of methylphenidate – a pharmacological treatment for attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) that modulates reward and top-down control circuits – on alcohol craving. To test the potential of methylphenidate to modulate neural connections implicated in craving, we assessed the resting-state brain functional connectivity (rsFC) between regions of cognitive control and reward processing in young adults with AUD and ADHD.

Methods

Thirty-three young adults (17 males) with comorbid AUD and ADHD were recruited in a double-blind, placebo-controlled, within-subjects study, where they received either a 20-mg oral methylphenidate or a placebo in separate visits. An eight-minute resting-state fMRI scan was implemented one hour after the pills were taken, and self-reported craving was measured before pill intake, and before and after the scan by the Alcohol Craving Questionnaire. FMRI preprocessing, time-series data extraction for rsFCs, and group-level data were modeled using AFNI, with nine bilateral ROIs relating to cognitive control and reward pathways being examined (ventral striatum [VS], amygdala, anterior insula, anterior cingulate cortex [ACC], posterior cingulate cortex, frontal eye field, intraparietal sulcus, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, and inferior frontal gyrus). A set of general linear models and FDR correction with nlme and stats packages in R was used to identify methylphenidate effects on rsFC when covariates were included (pill order, lab visit, sex, age, rsFC in the placebo session, mean whole-brain connectivity for each ROI, and mean head motion). A mediation analysis was computed using the mediation package in R to determine if the change in craving by methylphenidate could be explained by rsFC. Specifically, we tested: a) the association between pill intake and rsFC; b) the association between rsFC and alcohol craving; c) the direct effect of pill on craving without considering rsFC; and c’) the effect of pill on craving when rsFC was considered as a mediator in the model. In addition to the aforementioned covariates, we also included the craving reported before pill intake as a covariate to account for the variation of craving at baseline.

Results

We found that only the rsFC between bilateral VS (MNI coordinates: -13, 8, -13 and 16, 7, -12) and ACC (a cluster surrounding MNI coordinates: 4, 41, 18) was significantly regulated by methylphenidate. Specifically, methylphenidate reduced the connectivity between these regions (FDR-adjusted p < .001). There was a main effect of the pill on alcohol craving when rsFC was not considered, with a decreased craving in the methylphenidate compared to the placebo session (p = .01). Mediation with bootstrapping revealed that the effect of methylphenidate was fully mediated by the VS-ACC rsFC as the direct effect of methylphenidate became non-significant in the mediation model (total pill effect without considering rsFC: β = -4.23, p < .001; direct pill effect on craving when rsFC was included: β = -2.29, p = .14; rsFC mediation effect: β = -1.94, p = .02).

Conclusion

Our preliminary data showed a promising effect of methylphenidate on mitigating craving in young adults with comorbid AUD and ADHD, and this result may be explained by methylphenidate-associated decreases in VS-ACC connectivity. These ROIs have been proposed as the underpinning of dysfunctions in AUD and are also targeted for improving clinical outcomes. The present results confirm the importance of VS and ACC to alcohol problems and suggest that their functional interaction may be an underlying mechanism through which methylphenidate may alleviate craving.
