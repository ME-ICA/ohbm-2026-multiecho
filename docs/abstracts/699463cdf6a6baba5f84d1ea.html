Introduction

Replay, the sequential reactivation of neural firing sequences that represent past experiences, has been suggested to construct internal models of experienced environments1. This hypothesis is mainly based on two proposed functions for replay during rodent navigation: retrieving recent experiences (backward replay) and planning upcoming routes (forward replay), within the same brain areas1.

Human neuroimaging studies suggest that replay is not limited to the spatial domain. For example, it has been shown that replay can reorganize experiences2. Reactivation of past events has also been identified at moments of transition between narrative events (event boundaries3), suggesting that replay links relevant events to form an overarching understanding of unfolding narratives4. Importantly, this reactivation of contextually-relevant past information is mainly detected in areas of the default mode network (DMN), which are also associated with rodent replay, including the precuneus/retrosplenial cortex, angular gyrus, and hippocampus.

It is therefore possible that navigating episodic experiences is subserved by similar neural mechanisms to those used to navigate physical space. By extension, similar to its role in building internal spatial models of the environments, replay may also build internal models of episodic experiences. Yet, current findings on narrative understanding support a role for replay in integrating past with present events, but not in planning, as attending to narratives lacks explicit planning components that could be detected neurally.

To investigate whether replay also supports planning, we analyzed a dataset in which participants not only watched a story but also recalled it afterward. Retelling a story requires both remembering where one is in the plot and thinking ahead about how to continue the narrative. Drawing a parallel with spatial cognition, we hypothesized that both types of reactivations would appear during narrative recall.

Methods

We used a dataset of participants (n = 17) who watched the first episode of BBC’s Sherlock and later freely recalled it while their neural activity was recorded with fMRI5. Most participants recalled the events in chronological order. In previous work, we developed a method for detecting replay in fMRI data and applied it to the movie-watching sessions from this dataset4. We quantified reactivation as the similarity between fMRI representations of event boundaries and scenes, computed using inter-subject correlations in a whole-brain searchlight analysis. To control for fMRI autocorrelation and for the possibility that reactivations reflect inter-scene similarity, we contrasted similarity between event boundaries and past scenes with similarity between boundaries and future scenes4 (i.e., lower vs. upper triangles of the similarity matrix; Fig. 1b). 

We applied this approach to test for fMRI reactivation during free recall. However, we reasoned that reactivating each specific scene, as done during the movie watching, would be an inefficient strategy for reconstructing an entire narrative. Instead, if scenes were bound into a broader narrative structure during movie watching4, then movie event boundaries may have served as moments at which compressed narrative models (i.e., abstract representations of scenes and their interrelations) were constructed and encoded. These models could then be reactivated at recall event boundaries to support efficient narrative reconstruction. 

This hypothesis predicts that event-boundary representations contain information that is distinct from the information in scene representations. To test this, we used a whole-brain searchlight analysis, in which we correlated the representations of each pair of corresponding movie and recall scenes and each pair of movie and recall event boundaries across subjects (see Fig. 1a). To isolate unique information held in boundary representations, we removed any variance shared between movie-to–recall boundary similarities and movie-to–recall scene similarities. We then identified brain regions in which the residual movie-to–recall boundary similarities had a mean significantly different from zero. The resulting map, and all other maps reported here, were cluster-corrected for multiple comparisons across the entire brain using nonparametric permutation tests (FSL randomise, cluster-forming threshold of p < 0.001, cluster-level significance threshold of p<0.05).

We next tested whether these unique representations of movie event-boundaries contribute to narrative reconstruction during recall. To do so, we applied the whole-brain searchlight reactivation analysis from previous work4, but here correlated each subject’s movie event-boundary representations with the group’s recall event boundaries using a leave-one-out approach (see Fig. 1b). Because recall durations varied across subjects, recalled narratives were not temporally aligned, and autocorrelation could not bias this analysis. This enabled us to separately examine reactivation of past and future movie event boundaries, rather than contrasting them as in the within-movie analysis4. To control for inter-scene similarity, we removed from the boundary-by-boundary matrices any variance that was shared with the scene-by-scene similarity matrices (see Fig. 1b). Thus, positive values in the residual boundary similarity matrix reflect reactivation that cannot be explained by scene similarity. Finally, we directly contrasted past and future reactivation across the brain using a searchlight analysis. 

To test the specificity of reported effects to regions of the DMN, we repeated all analyses using independent ROIs (bilateral precuneus, angular gyrus and hippocampus), defined based on reported reactivation of past events in an independent dataset of participants listening to a story4. Effects were Bonferroni corrected for multiple comparisons across the three ROIs.

Results

We hypothesized that at recall event boundaries, the brain reactivates the representations of movie event boundaries. However, boundary representations are inherently correlated with scene representations simply because scenes and boundaries are temporally adjacent. Hence, before testing for reactivation, we must verify that there is information present in boundary representations that is not present in scene representations. To this end, we correlated the recall-to-movie boundary representations while removing any variance shared with the corresponding recall-to-movie scene representations. If boundaries still correlate positively after removing scene-driven variance, this means that event boundary representations contain computational content above and beyond scene representations. Indeed, we found that residual movie–recall boundary similarity was significantly greater than zero in a set of DMN areas (see Fig. 2a). Analyses of ROIs taken from an independent dataset4 (precuneus, angular gyrus, and hippocampus) confirmed that the same regions that reactivate scenes during movie watching also hold unique event boundary representations (all p-values ≤0.001, Bonferroni corrected). 

We next tested whether representations of movie event boundaries are reactivated at recall event boundaries. A whole brain searchlight analysis revealed a set of brain areas that reactivate both past and future representations of movie-event boundaries. Independent ROI analyses confirmed that these areas correspond to DMN regions that reactivate past scene representations during movie watching (see Fig. 2b). 

Finally, we contrasted the reactivation of past and future movie boundaries across the brain. We found a set of areas that show stronger reactivation of future compared to past movie boundaries (see Fig. 2b). An ROI analysis revealed that reactivation in precuneus and angular gyrus is stronger for future compared to past movie boundary representations (see Fig. 2b). These results support the hypothesis that DMN regions reactivate both past and future movie boundary representations during narrative recall, with stronger future reactivation in precuneus and angular gyrus.

Conclusion

We report the reactivation of both past and future representations of movie boundaries at the event boundaries of the recalled narrative. These reactivated representations hold unique information above and beyond the information contained in scenes. These results hint that, during event boundaries of recall, reactivation of the representations at past movie boundaries support tracking of the narrative, while reactivation of representations at future movie boundaries aid in recalling and planning the narrative’s progression. These processes resemble backward and forward replay in rodents, which are thought to support cognitive map construction. We therefore postulate that human reactivations construct a cognitive model of evolving experiences, enabling narrative regeneration from memory.
