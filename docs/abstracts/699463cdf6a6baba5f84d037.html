Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a leading cause of disability that often causes persistent symptoms and deficits in processing speed, memory, and executive function, even after injuries classified as “mild.” Most structural MRI studies have focused on regional atrophy of gray and white matter or on lesion burden and have typically summarized morphometry within single regions or lobes. These univariate approaches capture focal effects but are unable to evaluate disrupted brain networks or the distributed cortical reorganization that accompanies recovery.

Recent work has introduced multivariate inverse divergence (MIND) networks, which compute similarity between gray matter regions via symmetrized Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence of their distributions of morphometric features1. MIND networks are more robust, more consistent with cortical cytoarchitecture, better correlated with tract tracing measures of axonal connectivity, more sensitive to age-related changes, and more heritable over edge weight and degree centrality statistics compared to other approaches that map brain structural similarity.

Leveraging the Transforming Research and Clinical Knowledge in TBI (TRACK-TBI) cohort, we construct MIND networks at two weeks and six months after injury in patients with acute TBI and demographically matched friends/family controls (FC). We use MIND networks to capture the cross-sectional and longitudinal effects of TBI, show these effects to be stratified across cortical microarchitecture, and evaluate their ability to track and predict disability, symptom burden, and cognitive outcome following TBI.

Methods

Participants and outcomes

We analyzed TRACK-TBI participants from 18 US Level-1 trauma centers2. Inclusion criteria were age ≥17 years, evaluation for TBI within 24 hours of injury, a head computed tomography (CT) scan, adequate pre-injury hearing & vision, and fluency in English or Spanish. Exclusion criteria included penetrating TBI, MRI contraindications, severe pre-existing neurological or psychiatric disease, incarceration or custody, pregnancy, involuntary psychiatric hold, and enrollment in competing interventional trials. FC participants were demographically matched friends or family of TBI patients and free of recent injury.

All participants provided written informed consent in accordance with local institutional review boards. We included individuals with volumetric MRI at both two weeks (2W) and six months (6M) post-injury and usable cortical segmentations. The final sample comprised 745 TBI patients and 166 FC participants. Of the TBI patients, 63% were male, 95% had admission Glasgow Coma Scale 13-15 and 68% had no findings of acute TBI on head CT scan.

Global outcome at 6M was assessed with the Glasgow Outcome Scale–Extended (GOSE) scored both as GOSE-All (overall disability) and GOSE-TBI (disability attributable to brain injury). Symptom burden was measured using the Rivermead Post-Concussion Questionnaire (RPQ16), processing speed using the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale Processing Speed Index (WAIS-PSI), executive function with the Trail Making Test parts A and B (TMT-A/B), and verbal memory with the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT) Sum and Delay scores. Outcome analyses in TBI used n=689 for GOSE-TBI, n=674 for GOSE-All, n=721 for RPQ-16, and n=670 for the cognitive battery.

MRI acquisition and cortical features

All sites implemented a standardized 3 Tesla (3T) protocol on Siemens, General Electric (GE), or Philips scanners with multi-channel head coils. The MRI protocol included a sagittal 3D MPRAGE (Siemens/Philips) or FSPGR IR (GE) T1-weighted sequence at 1.0×1.0×1.2 mm resolution with 3D T2 FLAIR, T2*, and T2 sequences for clinical evaluation.

T1-weighted scans were processed with FreeSurfer 7.4.0, incorporating SynthSeg-based lesion handling and rigorous visual quality control. For each vertex we extracted cortical thickness, surface area, mean curvature, sulcal depth, and gray matter volume. We excluded scans with major segmentation errors or motion artifacts.

MIND structural similarity networks

For each subject and time point we estimated MIND similarity between every pair of Desikan-Killiany (DK) parcels and coarser parcellations: lobes (frontal, parietal, occipital, temporal, insula, cingulate), Yeo functional MRI (fMRI) networks3 (visual, somatomotor, dorsal attention, ventral attention, limbic, frontoparietal, default mode), Mesulam’s hierarchy of laminar differentiation (idiotypic, unimodal, heteromodal, paralimbic) and cerebral hemispheres (left, right). We assessed cross-sectional and longitudinal differences via subject-wise permutation tests with false discovery rate correction across edges and a significance level of 5%. We averaged FC imaging across the 2W and 6M timepoints for cross-sectional comparisons to TBI.

Outcome Analysis

All imaging versus outcome analyses were conducted by the TRACK-TBI Biostatistics Core masked to imaging preprocessing/analysis; the Imaging Core was masked to clinical and outcome data.

We used multivariable logistic regression to relate 2W MIND measures to the odds of GOSE<8 at 6M. For RPQ16 we fit zero-inflated negative binomial models to account for excess zeros. For WAIS-PSI, TMT-A/B, and RAVLT Sum and Delay we used linear regression; for change-change analyses we modeled the change in outcome as a function of the change in MIND similarity. For all multivariable models, we adjusted for age, sex, years of education, and pre-injury psychiatric history. Odds ratios and regression coefficients were reported per 1-SD increase in the similarity measure.

Results

Sensitivity to TBI Cross-Sectional and Longitudinal Differences

At 2W, 128 DK network edges had statistically significant differences (51 positive, 77 negative) between TBI and FC groups, which decreased to 43 significant edges (16 positive, 27 negative) by 6M. TBI patients exhibited higher similarity of frontal/orbitofrontal to sensorimotor/parietal edges but lower similarity of medial/lateral orbitofrontal and rostral middle frontal to temporal/insular edges as well as occipital to parietal and supramarginal edges (Fig. 1A,B). We identified 11 significant longitudinal DK edges, all reflecting higher similarity at six months with Cohen’s d ≈ 0.14–0.20 (Fig. 1C). Participation coefficients, counting the number of significant incident edges, highlighted hubs of altered structural similarity in dorsal attention, cingulo-parietal, and orbitofrontal areas, with a more restricted, attenuated pattern at 6M (Fig. 1D-F).

Analysis of networks from the Yeo fMRI atlas (Fig. 1G) revealed increased coupling of somatosensory to limbic (2W: d=0.2, p=0.045) and dorsal attention network (DAN) to ventral attention network (VAN) (2W: d=0.21, p=0.0339), limbic (2W: d=0.29, p=0.0023; 6M: d=0.22, p=0.0432), frontoparietal network (FPN) (2W: d=0.31, p=0.001; 6M: d=0.26, p=0.0272), and default mode network (DMN) (2W: d=0.24, p=0.0187). Negative coupling was observed for visual to DAN (2W: d=-0.22, p=0.0339; 6M: d=-0.24, p=0.0309) and for FPN to VAN (2W: d=-0.35, p=0.0008; d=-0.27, p=0.0272), limbic (2W: d=-0.23, p=0.0321), and DMN (2W: d=-0.35, p=0.0008; 6M: d=-0.25, p=0.0272). DAN–limbic coupling decreased (d=−0.12, p=0.0109), while VAN–FPN (d=0.10, p=0.0447) and FPN–DMN (d=0.14, p=0.00252) couplings increased longitudinally.

We found significantly lower cross-sectional effect sizes between cortical regions connected via white matter, ascertained by dMRI tractography (2W: d=−0.21, p=0.0295; 6M: d=−0.20, p=0.03574) and negative correlations with gene expression similarity (2W: r=−0.27, p=0.0504; 6M: r=−0.29, p=0.0369). Assortativity analysis showed significant negative associations between cross-sectional TBI effect sizes at 2W and cortical gradients and annotations spanning molecular, microstructural, electrophysiological, and functional ontologies (Fig. 1H). Disassortative mixing predominated in TBI, with weaker effects at 6M.

Association with Outcomes

DAN to limbic (GOSE-TBI: OR=0.823, p=0.016; GOSE-ALL: OR=0.794, p=0.006) and DAN to DMN (GOSE-TBI: OR=0.819, p=0.017; GOSE-ALL: OR=0.789, p=0.006) similarities at 2W were significant predictors for lower odds of disability at 6M (Fig. 2). In zero-inflated negative-binomial (ZINB) models, 2W heteromodal–unimodal (OR=0.718, 95% CI 0.592–0.870, p<0.001) and DAN–limbic (OR=0.820, 95% CI 0.674–0.999, p=0.048) similarities were associated with absence of 6M RPQ16 symptoms, but not symptom severity. Cross-hemispheric (β=−4.622, p<0.0005) and DAN—FPN (β=−4.848, p<0.0005) similarities were significant predictors of TMT-B scores. Longitudinal changes in paralimbic–idiotypic (β=2.198, p=0.024) and DAN–limbic (β=2.616, p=0.007) similarities were significantly associated with changes in TMT-B scores.

RAVLT-Sum memory scores had several significant predictors: visual–DAN (β=−1.275, p=0.001), DAN–limbic (β=−1.457, p<0.0005), DAN–DMN (β=−1.236, p=0.002), frontal–temporal (β=−1.282, p=0.001), frontal–insula (β=−1.050, p=0.005), and occipital–cingulate (β=−0.795, p=0.039). Similarly, paralimbic–idiotypic (β=−0.413, p=0.001), heteromodal–unimodal (β=−0.302, p=0.014), visual–DAN (β=−0.288, p=0.019), DAN–limbic (β=−0.468, p<0.0005), DAN–DMN (β=−0.412, p=0.001), frontal–insula (β=−0.483, p<0.0005), and occipital–cingulate (β=−0.332, p=0.008) similarities were significant predictors of RAVLT-Delay memory. WAIS-PSI processing speed had the following significant predictors: heteromodal–unimodal (β=−1.456, p=0.009), visual—DAN (β=−1.464, p=0.008), DAN–limbic (β=−1.983, p<0.0005), and DAN—DMN (β=−1.516, p=0.009) similarities.

Conclusion

We utilized Morphometric Inverse Divergence (MIND) to map the longitudinal trajectory of cortical gray matter structural similarity following traumatic brain injury in the TRACK-TBI MRI dataset. Group-level findings reveal a distinct pattern of cortical reconfiguration at 2 weeks post-injury, with the DAN as the hub for increases in structural similarity and the FPN as the hub for decreases in structural similarity. This is followed by partial recovery by six months post-TBI, with DAN coupling to other networks increasing and FPN coupling to other networks decreasing. We demonstrate that TBI leads to hyper-coupling between external and internal/limbic systems, which is predictive of disability, symptom burden, and cognitive deficits.

We found structural evidence supporting the "Three Network Hypothesis" of TBI4. Efficient cognitive control typically requires a balance between the DMN (introspection), FPN (executive control), and VAN (salience/task switching), with the DAN sustaining top-down, goal-directed focus. DAN is often anti-correlated with the DMN and limbic network to prevent internal distractions from interfering with external tasks. However, our cross-sectional analysis reveals a maladaptive collapse of this relationship. Specifically, higher 2W DAN–limbic similarity was a significant predictor for worse long-term disability (GOSE-All/TBI), symptom burden (RPQ-16), verbal memory (RAVLT) and processing speed (WAIS-PSI) at six months. Progressive increases in DAN–limbic similarity from 2W to 6M were also associated with worsening executive function (TMT-B). Similarly, increased DAN–DMN similarity early after TBI was a significant predictor for worse disability, symptom burden, processing speed, and verbal memory. Previous fMRI studies of chronic TBI have reported reduced segregation and abnormal coupling among DMN, DAN, and FPN, with links to executive dysfunction and attentional lapses5. Our results suggest that these well-described functional network disturbances are mirrored by a persistent reshaping of structural similarity within the same systems.

MIND abnormalities were strongly constrained by underlying microarchitecture. Cross-sectional TBI effect sizes had greater magnitude for edges connected by white-matter tracts and were negatively correlated with regional gene expression similarity. Moreover, assortativity analyses showed that edges linking regions with similar cortical gradients and properties: the SA axis, functional connectivity principal components, BigBrain histological gradients, electrophysiological power, and neurotransmitter receptor densities, had more negative cross-sectional TBI effect sizes at two weeks post-injury. In TBI, these normally cohesive cortical gradients became disassortative, implying that injury preferentially alters similarity among microarchitecturally matched regions.

We demonstrate the clinical utility of structural similarity networks, which can be computed using a standard 3D T1-weighted volume, requiring only a few minutes to acquire on any clinical MRI scanner even without advanced hardware or software, and with no need for inter-site or inter-scanner data harmonization. MIND-derived similarity features provide a compact, biologically informed set of imaging biomarkers that capture network-specific vulnerabilities. Such measures could be used to stratify patients in clinical trials, identify individuals at risk for persistent deficits despite few or no conventional imaging findings, and monitor cortical reorganization in response to pharmacotherapy or rehabilitation. Future work can extend these observations by also integrating diffusion and functional connectivity biomarkers for improved TBI patient care.
