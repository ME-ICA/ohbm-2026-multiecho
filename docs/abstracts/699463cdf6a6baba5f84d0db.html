Introduction

Mismatch negativity (MMN) is an auditory event-related potential that indexes pre-attentive change detection and predictive-processing mechanisms. MMN amplitude is consistently diminished in schizophrenia and is linked to cognitive deficits, functional impairment, and disease progression.1 Although antipsychotic medication alleviates positive symptoms for many patients, reports on its influence on MMN has been inconsistent.2 Meanwhile, electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) is a well-established augmentation option for individuals who do not sufficiently improve with antipsychotic treatment. While its precise therapeutic mechanism is still not fully understood, accumulating evidence suggests that ECT promotes neuroplasticity.3 Given that MMN generation relies on N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor-mediated synaptic plasticity,4 MMN may provide a sensitive indicator of neurophysiological changes induced by ECT. The present study therefore examined whether ECT restores MMN amplitude and whether such changes correspond with improvement in clinical symptom.

Methods

The study included patients treated with both ECT and medication, patients who received medication alone, and healthy controls. For the ECT group, assessments and data acquisition were conducted before and after ECT, whereas the medication-only group was evaluated at comparable time points. Data from healthy controls were obtained at baseline. For each participant, electroencephalography (EEG) was recorded during a passive auditory oddball task, and underwent preprocessing. MMN was calculated by subtracting standard from deviant waveform, with measurements taken at the FCz electrode. Baseline differences in MMN amplitude and latency among groups were tested using analyses of covariance (ANCOVA) with age and sex as covariates. For patient samples, group-by-time interaction effects in MMN amplitude and latency were analyzed using mixed-effects models for repeated measures, while adjusting for age, sex, and antipsychotic dosage in olanzapine equivalents. Within the ECT group, repeated-measures correlations were conducted to evaluate associations between MMN amplitude and PANSS subscale scores. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied for multiple testing.

Results

The analyses included 26 participants who received ECT with medication, 23 who received medication alone, and 50 healthy controls. At baseline, patients exhibited diminished MMN amplitude and shorter latency compared with controls, while the two patient groups did not differ from each other. Mixed-effects modeling demonstrated a significant group-by-time interaction for MMN amplitude, characterized by an increase in the ECT group and a decline in the medication-only group. No significant interaction emerged for MMN latency. In the ECT group, repeated-measures correlations indicated that greater MMN amplitude was significantly related to improvements in PANSS positive symptoms.

Conclusion

By directly comparing patients who received ECT alongside medication with those treated with medication only, our study demonstrates that ECT enhances MMN amplitude, and that this change is linked to improvements in positive symptoms. These findings indicate that ECT modulates neural mechanisms central to MMN generation, such as NMDA receptor-mediated plasticity and excitation-inhibition balance. They further imply that ECT may correct disruptions in predictive coding, indexed by MMN changes, and thereby play a role in reducing positive symptoms. Because sensor-level waveform measures cannot fully capture predictive-coding alterations at the systems level, future research employing more refined approaches is needed to clarify how ECT affects predictive coding dysfunction.
