Introduction

While reading a text, we can find ourselves dissociating from it and thinking about something else. This tendency to mind-wander has been linked to perceptual decoupling and is related to poor narrative comprehension.1 Neurally, medial default mode network (DMN) regions are recruited during autobiographical memory (AM) recall while ignoring text distractors, whereas left temporal and lateral prefrontal DMN regions activate when reading while ignoring AM distractors.2 Crucially, regions of the DMN linked to reading are more functionally connected to the ventral visual stream than DMN regions implicated in AM, which are more connected to the medial aspects of the visual cortex.2 These results suggest that engaging with either reading or engaging with AM implicates DMN coupling and decoupling patterns with ventral and medial visual regions.

Counterintuitively, while challenging tasks typically suppress mind-wandering, more difficult texts actually elicit greater mind-wandering during reading.3 We hypothesize this occurs because difficult texts fail to efficiently engage the semantic system with the input, allowing semantic regions to couple with other DMN regions and internally-generated thought patterns rather than the text itself. Language proficiency may therefore critically modulate the neural basis of reading focus: when comprehension is poor, the brain may recruit controlled semantic retrieval processes that paradoxically increase coupling with mind-wandering networks. The current study investigates how language proficiency modulates brain activation during reading while ignoring AM and during AM retrieval while ignoring text.

Methods

Participants

A group of 35 healthy participants aged between 18 and 30 years (mean age = 20.85 ± 2.25; 26 females) was recruited from the University of York. They were all right-handed, native English speakers with normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no history of psychiatric or neurological illness.

Task procedure

First, participants were presented with cue words on a computer and asked to generate personal thoughts or associations related to them. In the MRI scanner, for each trial, participants were presented with one of the cue words. Immediately after that, they were presented with a word in capital letters (either “comprehend” or “recall”). This word indicated the instructions they had to follow: either to try to comprehend text that was about to be presented on screen, or to recall the thoughts related to the cue word while ignoring the text. After that, a sentence in English or Spanish was presented. After each sentence, participants had to rate how focused they were on the task they were asked to do (indicated by the question “Comprehend?” or “Recall?” on screen). Finally, they were asked to rate their comprehension of all sentences they had seen, both in English and Spanish (see Figure 1 for procedure and task structure). The main experimental task lasted for approximately 12 minutes, preceded by a brief practice.

Analysis of focus and comprehension ratings

We compared comprehension ratings of Spanish and English sentences across participants by using a two-sample t-test. We also carried out a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) across participants to compare focus ratings between our four conditions, followed by post-hoc pairwise comparisons using two-sample t-tests. Statistical significance was determined by a p = 0.05 threshold, and Bonferroni correction was used in the post-hoc pairwise comparisons.   

Preprocessing and analysis of fMRI data

Functional MRI obtained during the task was preprocessed using a standard pipeline with the tools of FMRIB Software Library’s (FSL). The preprocessed time-series data were modelled following a general linear model, including four explanatory variables (EV) of interest comprising the periods when participants were: reading sentences (1) in English or (2) in Spanish, and recalling in relation to the cue word while being shown sentences (3) in English or (4) in Spanish. We included another EV of interest associated with the effect of (5) Spanish comprehension (introduced as demeaned ratings in the model) for each of the sentences presented in the reading in Spanish condition.

We identified whole-brain voxels responding differently to each language in each condition by establishing the following contrasts in each participant: reading sentences in English > reading in Spanish while ignoring AM; recalling while ignoring sentences in English > in Spanish; and the reverse of both of them. We also explored the interaction of the four conditions by establishing two contrasts with opposite weights (1 and -1) for conditions in the same language. We investigated the effect of degree of comprehension of Spanish sentences while reading by adding a contrast of this EV against baseline. We used a cluster-forming threshold of z = 3.1 and a familywise-error-corrected significance level of p = 0.05.

Finally, we wanted to investigate if our results overlapped with focus effects we had previously found using a similar task with the same conditions, with only one language and comparing reading and listening (independent sample of 26 participants, mean age = 20 years). In that study, we found positive effects of focus during comprehension in superior temporal lobe, and negative focus effects during comprehension in medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) bilaterally. For the condition of recalling while ignoring sentences, we found positive focus effects in medial temporal regions, and negative focus effects on right angular gyrus, dorsolateral and mPFC.

Results

Behavioural results

Comprehension of English was significantly higher than Spanish (t = 21.42, p < .001). As for focus ratings, there were statistically significant differences between the conditions (F = 18.74, p < .001). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons revealed that participants were more focused on the task when reading sentences in English than when recalling while trying to ignore sentences in English (p < .001). They were also more focused while reading English than while reading Spanish (t = -2.92, p = 0.016), and more focused when recalling while ignoring Spanish sentences than while ignoring English (t = 2.73, p = 0.032). This confirms that our participants found English sentences easier to comprehend and to focus on, suggesting that comparing languages is an appropriate test of how reading may engage the semantic system differently in easier and harder contexts.

Brain activation results

All results reported as statistically significant in this section fulfilled the thresholds of z > 3.1, p < .05. Participants showed more brain activation in the left anterior temporal lobe (ATL) when reading sentences in English as compared to when reading Spanish, indicating that when reading is easier, task-relevant semantic regions are more strongly engaged with the text, supporting efficient comprehension and sustained attention. They also showed more activation when reading Spanish than English in lateral visual and intraparietal sulcus (IPS) bilaterally, suggesting Spanish is more reliant on visual and control processes, but not semantic processes.

We found significant interactions between our four conditions in clusters showing different interaction patterns: (1) mPFC, right supramarginal gyrus and IPS, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dPFC) and inferior frontal gyrus (orbital) were most deactivated for reading English; (2) left dPFC and IPS activated the least for reading English; (3) left insula was more active when reading Spanish compared to recalling while ignoring Spanish, as well as more active when recalling while ignoring English than when reading English; and (4) lateral visual regions were more active bilaterally when reading Spanish. This suggests that suppressive control mechanisms facilitate ignoring AM distractions when reading the language participants are proficient in; while control mechanisms which are active when retrieving AM while ignoring text in one’s native language are also at play when trying to read a language one is not proficient in, which could be related to the lack of automatization of both processes.

We also found a significant effect of comprehension of Spanish sentences while reading them. This effect was present in bilateral ATL, insula, mPFC and supramarginal gyrus, and right angular gyrus, indicating that these control regions are not exclusively associated with poorer reading and suppressed during efficient/easy reading, but can also be engaged to understand text in challenging circumstances. 

Finally, we also explored the overlap between our significant results and the focus effects found in our previous investigation using similar tasks in only one language (Figure 2). As a summary, reading English deactivated regions negatively related to focus during reading (mPFC) and regions negatively related with focus during recall (right angular gyrus, mPFC and dPFC), while activating regions positively related to focus during reading (ATL), further supporting the idea that that suppressive control mechanisms facilitate ignoring AM distractions when reading the language participants are proficient in. Reading Spanish activated medial regions positively related with focus during recall, and the effect of comprehension of Spanish was found in regions negatively related to focus during both reading and recall (right angular gyrus, mPFC and dPFC). This further reinforces the idea that the same control mechanisms are suppressed or activated depending on the demands that degree of comprehension imposes.

Conclusion

Our study aimed to investigate how degree of comprehension (in the form of language proficiency) modulates brain activation when reading and mind-wandering. Our findings reveal two fundamentally different modes of engaging with text, each with distinct neural architectures and attentional consequences. Fluent focussed reading in one's native language appears to rely on activation of ATL semantic representations, while dorsomedial control regions are suppressed. This configuration enables direct semantic engagement with the text and maintains attentional focus by preventing coupling between semantic and AM networks within the DMN. In contrast, effortful comprehension in a less proficient language recruits a control network that, paradoxically, may facilitate semantic disengagement from the text itself by reducing the reliance of reading on long-term semantic memory. This suggests that cognitive control during reading is a double-edged sword: it can support meaning extraction from difficult material while inadvertently creating the neural conditions for attentional drift.

In conclusion, our results show that subjective experiences of focus and brain activation change depending on the degree of comprehension of the text, both when trying to retrieve AM while ignoring it and when trying to read it while ignoring AM. Our results indicate that the same control mechanisms can be suppressed or activated depending on the demands imposed by the task based on language proficiency. More broadly, this work suggests that attentional focus during reading depends not simply on recruiting control resources, but on achieving a specific neural architecture that balances semantic access with isolation from other DMN regions. This provides novel evidence related to mind-wandering mechanisms, contributing to the growing literature on this topic.
