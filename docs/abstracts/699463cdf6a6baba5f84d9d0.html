Introduction

Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD) is a common and often highly impairing neuropsychiatric disorder characterized by unwanted, intrusive, disturbing thoughts, impulses, images, and urges (obsessions) accompanied by difficult to resist stereotyped repetitive behaviors (compulsions) thought to serve the function of momentarily reducing distress from obsessions. Two-thirds of adults with OCD report symptom onset before age 18. However, approximately 50% of youth with OCD show limited responses to first-line cognitive behavioral or pharmacological therapies.1 Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) is a non-invasive neuromodulation method that has demonstrated efficacy for adult OCD. The clinical use of TMS in youth is limited due to a lack of studies investigating possible benefit of TMS in youth with OCD. However, many TMS studies have been conducted in other child populations besides OCD that have demonstrated safety of TMS in adolescents.2 Accelerated theta-burst stimulation (TBS) is a high-frequency rTMS pattern shown to offer faster clinical improvement.3 

Our study examines safety, tolerability, and efficacy of treating adolescent OCD with accelerated theta burst TMS. We investigate neural and behavioral change in adolescents ages 13-17 with treatment-resistant OCD following accelerated TBS to either the right frontopolar/orbitofrontal cortex (R FP/OFC) or dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (dmPFC). We examine treatment-related network modification to evaluate how targeted stimulation of the R FP/OFC modifies functional connectivity using functional MRI (fMRI) with the hypothesis that inhibitory TMS will decrease R FP/OFC connectivity with the default mode network (DMN).

Methods

Participant and Protocol: Eleven participants (age: 15.1±0.9 yr) were randomized to receive TMS to either the R FP/OFC (n=8) or the dmPFC (n=3). The intervention consisted of three TMS treatments hourly over the course of 15 days (45 total treatments). Prior to TMS treatment, participants underwent a resting motor threshold assessment to identify the minimum stimulation intensity needed to elicit neuronal activation, defined as a reflexive thumb movement in at least five of ten single-pulse trials to the motor cortex. Both groups received 1800 pulses of inhibitory TBS at up to 120% of the established resting motor threshold (RMT) baseline each treatment. In the R FP/OFC condition, daily treatment sessions involved 1800 continuous TBS (cTBS) pulses applied to the R FP/OFC (approximately 5 min duration; Figure 1, A) using a MagVenture Cool-B65 butterfly coil. In the dmPFC condition, participants received 1800 intermittent TBS (iTBS) pulses applied on the dmPFC (approximately 9 min duration) using a Cool D-B80 bent butterfly coil. A 50-minute break between each stimulation session allowed participants a resting period with the next treatment starting on the hour from the preceding treatment. Imaging was acquired pre- and post- completion of TMS treatment period. Functional connectivity for R FP/OFC regions and intra-network DMN connectivity using resting-state fMRI (rsfMRI) were used to evaluate the TBS-induced network change. The clinician-administered semi-structured interview Children’s Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale II (CY-BOCS II) was performed at baseline and one-month following the last treatment session. The primary outcome was change in the CY-BOCS-II score, as assessed by clinician. 

Imaging Analysis: Imaging data include structural MRI (T1-Weighted) and resting state functional MRI with following parameters: EPI BOLD signal, time of repeat: 800ms, number of volumes: 391. The MR images was acquired before (pre) and post treatment to examine potential brain network changes using ROI to ROI and seed based functional connectivity for R FP/OFC seed. Structural and rsfMRI data were preprocessed using fMRIPrep-23.4.1.4 The preprocessing includes bias field correction using ANT’s, then averaged to generate a subject‐specific anatomical reference image using FreeSurfer. Followed by skull‐stripping using FMRIB's automated segmentation tool. Prior to any spatiotemporal filtering, head-motion parameters (transformation matrices and six related rotation and translation parameters) with respect to the fMRI reference were determined using mcflirt. Bold runs were slice-time corrected to 0.35s using AFNI and resampled to native space by applying transform for motion correction. Boundary-based registration was used to align the BOLD images to the T1-weighted anatomy of each participant. Framewise displacement and DVARS were used to quantify motion, and frames with high motion were identified as outliers. A single combined interpolation step that integrated all motion and anatomical alignment transformations was then used to convert the BOLD timeseries into MNI152NLin6Asym space. 

For post-processing, we extracted the BOLD timeseries for 456 cortical and subcortical brain regions by parcellating each regions using 4s456Parcels atlas and taking the mean intensity of each voxel within the region of interest (ROI). Then using Pearson Correlation ROI-ROI functional connectivity matrices were constructed and Fisher r-to-z transform was applied for normalizing the distribution. Followed by the brain connectivity modulation were assessed in two dimensions: i) Intra-network DMN connectivity ii) R FP/OFC to whole brain connectivity changes. The intra-network DMN connectivity was computed by taking the mean connectivity values of the ROIs corresponding to the DMN. Paired t-test was used to find TBS modulated neural and behavioral changes.

Results

The accelerated TBS protocol was well-tolerated, with only one severe adverse event reported. Across participants, TMS produced measurable clinical improvement with CY-BOCS-II scores significantly decreased one month after treatment (baseline: 31.55 ± 6.92; follow-up: 24.09 ± 6.19; p<.001, one-tailed paired t-test; 24% reduction; Figure 1, B). Significant reductions in OCD symptoms were seen in the R FP/OFC treatment group (baseline: 29.75±5.50; follow-up: 22.63±5.24; p<.05, one-tailed paired t-test) and the dmPFC group (baseline: 36.33±9.29; follow-up: 28.0±8.0; p<.05, one-tailed paired t-test). Because only three participants have received stimulation at the dmPFC site this far, further analyses focus on the R FP/OFC stimulation cohort. 

Our fMRI analysis demonstrated that the R FP/OFC stimulation site was positively functional coupled with bilateral frontal cortex and core DMN regions, including the posterior cingulate cortex (Figure 1, C-D). Right FP/OFC connectivity was reduced with the left middle prefrontal cortex, middle anterior cingulate, middle temporal and parietal cortices, and thalamus, alongside increases in the right inferior frontal and parietal regions (Figure 1, E). ROI-to-ROI analyses revealed a significant reduction in intra-DMN connectivity following treatment within the right hemisphere (pre: 0.26 ± 0.09; post: 0.24 ± 0.07; p = 0.023), whereas no significant changes were detected in the left hemisphere (Figure 1, A). Reductions in right intra-network DMN connectivity showed a positive association with symptom improvement (r = –0.45, p = 0.262).

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that the accelerated TBS protocol is feasible for adolescents and is associated with meaningful reductions in OCD symptoms. Recruitment of additional subjects will allow us to determine whether accelerated TBS can potentially be an effective treatment for adolescents with OCD and to further characterize the impact of TMS on brain functional connectivity.
