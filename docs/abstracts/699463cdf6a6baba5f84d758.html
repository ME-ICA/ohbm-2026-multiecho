Introduction

The relative contribution to which brain function is bound by the differential structural constraint, remains poorly understood. Multimodal functional brain recordings provide complementary information, however, how much this is mediated through the underlying structure remains unexplored. Our study, leveraging multimodal open datasets, explored the constraints of brain’s multiscale structural networks–geometric, microstructural and structural connectome on different functional networks (EEG and fMRI), and attempted to uncover the potential contribution of structure-function coupling on the observed EEG-fMRI coupling.

Methods

We analysed two openly available datasets:  concurrent resting state EEG-fMRI recordings from a group of 35 healthy participants (Wirsich et al., 2024), and multiscale structural datasets (including T1 weighted, diffusion weighted images and qT1 maps under 3T scanner) based on a group of 50 healthy controls (Royer et al., 2022). Geodesic distance (GD) matrix was computed along the cortical midsurface and distances between the regions was computed using Dijkstra’s algorithm. Structural connectome was derived by applying spherical deconvolution informed filtering to the tractograms (40 million streamlines) to reconstruct the whole brain streamlines weighted by cross-sectional multipliers, and the connection weights between the nodes were defined as the weighted streamline count. The MPC (Microstructural Profile Covariance) was constructed by deriving the 14 equivolumetric intracortical surfaces from qT1 maps across the cortical depth (representing the myelin proxy intensity profile) and cross correlating the intensity profiles between the regions. High-density EEG data were source reconstructed based on their individual head models and connectivity was measured by lagged coherence. Pearson based correlation was used to assess the functional connectivity between regions for fMRI data. All the connectomes were parcelled according to the DK (Desikan-Killiany) atlas. For more details in data processing and preprocessing, please refer to the studies Wirsich et al. (2024) and Royer et al. (2022). 

A group-level connectome was derived for each modality, and multiple linear regression model was employed to build prediction models from the structural networks on each functional network (for EEG, the beta frequency band was taken as an exemplary band). The functional gradient was computed from the fMRI connectome using diffusion mapping approach implemented in the toolbox BrainSpace (https://brainspace.readthedocs.io/en/latest/pages/matlab_doc/index.html). 

Results

Multiple linear regression modelling demonstrated that functional networks (for both modality) are well explainable by the brain geometry (indexed by the inter-regional GD), and the similarity of local microstructural profile (indexed by the MPC), with fMRI additionally explained by the structural connectome (adjusted R2 of 0.334 for fMRI, and 0.499 for EEG). While the structure-functional correspondence for fMRI diverges in parallel to the sensory-association axis (primarily motivated by the geometry and the microstructural similarity) (evidenced by a close correlation between the regional R2 and principal functional gradient: Rho=0.4845, p=0.0105), the structural tethering of EEG network seems more uniformly distributed across the cortex (Rho=0.2434, p=0.3165). Furthermore, the structure-function relationship exhibits a variable degree of interhemispheric asymmetry across the cortex: more leftwards in parietal region for fMRI, and rightwards in parietal region for EEG networks. Finally, our results demonstrated an important decrease in the EEG-fMRI correlation after regressing out all the queried structural covariates (correlation between the EEG and fMRI networks: Rho=0.3786 and Rho=0.1019, before and after the regression, respectively), suggesting that the observed EEG-fMRI coupling could largely be explained by the shared underlying structure. Additionally, we observed that by increasing the number of structural modalities considered in modelling, there is less remaining functional correlation between EEG and fMRI networks. 

Conclusion

Altogether, our findings first provide a comprehensive quantification of multiscale structural architecture’s constraint on multifunctional networks. Further, a large part of EEG-fMRI coupling could be explained by the underlying structure, which reveals a critical role of structure-function coupling in observed EEG-fMRI relationship. Our study highlights the benefit of multimodal approaches (particularly the geometry and microstructure) in understanding the link between structure and function, and further the interpretation of EEG-fMRI coupling.
