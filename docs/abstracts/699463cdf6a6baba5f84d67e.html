Introduction

Language is often processed in perceptually challenging conditions, i.e., conditions in which the linguistic signals are masked or degraded. Brain imaging studies suggest that listening in challenging situations involves the recruitment of fronto-parietal working memory-dependent regions1,2. If language processing in perceptually challenging conditions is supra-modal, similar resources should be involved when encountering these situations in sign language.  In the present study, we examined the neural resources that hearing bimodal bilingual individuals employ when faced with degraded language stimuli in both speech and sign across perceptually challenging conditions. Among different types of perceptually challenging conditions, background noise and spectral degradation stand out as the most studied in speech research2. 

Background noise refers to perceptual conditions where competing stimuli are added to the language signal, such as distracting sounds for speech or a busy visual environment for sign language. In speech, background noise, as implemented in speech-in-noise tasks, has been found to have larger deteriorating effects on language perception than spectral degradation2. Spectral degradation, on the other hand, implies the removal of the spectral information of the signal. This commonly occurs when analogue signals are digitalised, for instance, auditory signals in cochlear implants or visual signals in retinal implants. Spectral degradation can be simulated, in speech, with the noise vocoding technique and in sign by pixelating the videos.

Given the modality-independent nature of language, we hypothesize that the regions outlined as sensitive to spectral degradation and background noise in the study by Alain et al2, will be recruited for both speech and sign.

Methods

Twenty-two adult hearing signers (aged 18-65) fluent in Swedish and Swedish Sign language (STS) participated. The study had a within-subject design; all participants performed the task in two language modalities (speech and sign) with three conditions (spectrally degraded, background noise, clear). In the auditory modality, spectral degradation was obtained by applying noise vocoding at 8 bands and background noise by superposing white noise at an SNR –6.02 dB (70% intelligibility). The stimuli in the visual modality involved pixelating each frame, for spectral degradation, and randomly replacing pixels for salt and pepper noise, for the background noise. An outline on how the degradations were created can be observed in Fig.1 – panels 1 & 2. 

At the beginning of the session, participants were instructed to try to understand the spoken or signed words. Between stimuli blocks, the participants were asked to rate how difficult it was to understand the stimuli on a scale from 1 (very easy) to 4 (very difficult) on a response pad with four horizontally placed buttons.  Fig. 1 – panel 3 depicts an example run and block from the sign language modality. Ratings were compared between conditions separately for each modality with a Chi-Squared test.

Participants were scanned in a single session at the Stockholm University Brain Imaging Center (SUBIC), using a 3.0 T scanner (Siemens Magnetom Prisma, Siemens Healthcare GmbH) with a 64-channel head coil. We acquired functional images with an echo-planar T2*-weighted imaging sequence with whole-brain coverage while participants perform the fMRI task (TE/TR = 30/2000 ms, FA = 70°, oblique axial slice orientation, FOV = 192 × 192 mm, slice thickness = 2, in-plane resolution = 2 × 2 mm, number of slices = 58, GRAPPA acc. = 2, SMS acc. = 2). We also collected T1-weighted structural images (3D MPRAGE) with TI/TE/TR = 900/2.98/2300 ms, FA = 9°, oblique axial slice orientation, FOV = 256 × 256 mm, 256 × 256 × 208 acquisition matrix, 1 × 1 × 1 mm3 voxels, GRAPPA acc. factor = 2. The standard SPM procedure was employed for preprocessing, encompassing realignment to correct for subject motion, coregistration of structural and functional images, segmentation, and spatial normalization, aligning subject brain geometry with standard space. In the normalization process, a segmented deformation field from the previous step was applied to all functional and structural images, and the spatial smoothing was implemented using an 8 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel. Movement detection was monitored by  a realignment performing a 6-parameter rigid-body transformation from the mean of images, and framewise displacement was estimated from trealignment parameters. Runs with more than 3 mm movement in x, y, z, or 3° in pitch, yaw, and roll; and timepoints with a framewise displacement higher than 1 mm were excluded.

Analyses of whole-brain activation were performed by fitting two general linear models with three regressors each relating to the six conditions (clear, spectral degradation, and background noise compared to rest in each of the two modalities separately) as well as the six motion parameters derived from the realignment. At the first level, the statistical parametric map images pertaining to the spectral degradation and background noise conditions were contrasted with the clear condition within each modality, rendering four contrasts per participant. At the second-level, we compared perceptually challenging conditions within modality, i.e. spectral-sign > background-sign, spectral-speech > background-speech, background-sign > spectral-sign and background-speech > spectral-speech. No statistical comparisons were performed across modalities.

Results

In both modalities, ratings were significantly different across conditions (pcorr < .001), suggesting that participants did perceive that the condition impcted their ability to understand the stimuli. Background noise condition was rated as more difficult to the rest while the clear condition was considered the easiest for both modalities (see Fig.2 -panels 1 & 2, for the response profile of the ratings in each difficulty). This aligns with previous research that suggests background noise has a more detrimental effect in linguistic perception than spectral degradation2. Despite the observed results at behavioural level, the significantly activated regions do not align with our original hypotheses.

The auditory modality yielded no significant results across comparisons at peak level. However, at cluster level STG  bilaterally was significantly more activated for spectral speech compared to background speech (pfwe< .05 at cluster level, cluster shown in Fig.2 - panel 3).  

In the visual modality, all contrasts yielded significant results. The contrasts background-sign > clear-sign and spectral-sign > clear-sign presented increased activation on the lingual gyrus bilaterally, though to different extents (see Fig 2- panel 4, turquoise and yellow).  Background-sign > spectral-sign showed significant results in the right lingual gyrus and the left occipital pole (pfwe < .01 at peak and cluster level for all results). Spectral-sign > background-sign revealed significant activations in the inferior occipital gyrus bilaterally

Conclusion

The aim of this study was to assess the neural resources required for language processing in perceptually challenging conditions (i.e., background noise and spectral degradation). The results show that the neural resources recruited for speech processing in perceptually challenging conditions did not differ significantly from those used for listening to isolated words in the clear condition, despite participants reporting varying levels of perceived difficulty across conditions. For sign language, all conditions elicited activation on the occipital cortex, with the lingual gyrus being a repeated cluster throughout different comparisons. 

Although the design did not allow for comparisons, the patterns of our results indicate that speech and sign require different neural resources to overcome perceptually challenging conditions. These results are in agreement with a previous study3  looking into a working memory task with pixelated signs in isolation. Nonetheless, our results seem to be in discord with previous research in the auditory field2, which suggested that fronto-parietal working memory-dependent regions were involved in speech processing during effortful listening, which indicates that results obtained in both modalities require further research. These conflicting results might be to the conditions being moderately challenging (intelligibility level set to 70%) and not enough to elicit frontal activation. Nonetheless, the lack of contextual and semantic information in the stimuli which added difficulty to the task. Contrary to our study, research focusing on words in effortful listening conditions usually includes contextual information (see meta-analysis2). If the difference in results is due to the lack of contextual information, it would imply that perceptually challenging conditions are not sufficient to elicit working memory activation in either modality. 

The significantly activated regions identified in this study are all related to primary sensory areas, suggesting that the increased neural activity reflects higher demands on auditory and visual identification, rather than on linguistic processing. For example, increased activation on the lingual gyrus has been linked to global shape processing4 under increased visual demands, and increased STG activity has been associated with auditory noise5. 

In conclusion, we suggest that: 

  • Perceptually moderately challenging conditions are not sufficient to elicit working memory resources in either speech or sign.
  • The linguistic resources needed to identify isolated words and signs in perceptually challenging conditions do not differ from clear conditions.
  • Isolated words/signs in perceptually challenging conditions increase bottom-up processes in the absence of the necessary resources to engage in top-down processes.

Further research with more complex linguistic material and/or with more difficulty is needed to confirm whether the resources employed for language processing in perceptually challenging conditions differ across modalities.
