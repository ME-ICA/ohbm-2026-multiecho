Introduction

Primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG) is a progressive optic neuropathy characterized by the irreversible loss of retinal ganglion cells. This loss might lead to peripheral visual field defects (VFDs) that progress toward the center of the visual field, potentially resulting in complete blindness. Consequently, POAG has become a target for restorative approaches such as stem cell-based therapies that aim to restore vision. However, it remains unclear whether the visual cortex, after years of reduced retinal input, will still be able to effectively process restored signals. Neuroimaging studies have demonstrated that POAG's impact extends beyond the eye, causing structural damage throughout the visual pathways 1. Determining whether cortical visual processing is preserved requires assessing not only structural properties, but also functional ones. Resting-state fMRI is particularly well suited to address this question in POAG, because it enables assessment of the intrinsic functional organization of the visual cortex without relying on visual stimulation, stable fixation, or task performance, all of which can be compromised in patients with VFDs. This stimulus-independent approach allows evaluation of whether visual cortical areas retain the functional capacity to process restored retinal input. In this study, we investigated how POAG affects spatial integration of visual information along the visual hierarchy, from the primary visual cortex (V1) to higher-order visual areas (V2 and V3). To do so, we used Connective Field (CF) modeling combined with resting-state fMRI 3. CF modeling estimates how the activity in a higher-order visual area can be predicted from a spatially weighted region in a lower-order area providing a measure of cortico-cortical spatial integration, the CF size 4. Our results indicate that CF properties in POAG do not differ from those of healthy controls, suggesting that the intrinsic spatial organization in the visual cortex is largely preserved.

Methods

Data were collected at two sites: the University Medical Center Groningen (UMCG) in The Netherlands and Otto-von-Guericke University Magdeburg (OVGU) in Germany. At UMCG, we recruited early-to-moderate stage 18 POAG patients (mean age ± SD: 68.5 ± 9.0 years) and 21 healthy controls (mean age ± SD: 67.0 ± 8.0 years) with normal or corrected-to-normal vision. At OVGU, the cohort consisted of 12 early-stage POAG (mean age ± SD: 70.0 ± 4.5 years) and 20 healthy controls (mean age ± SD: 72.0 ± 5.5 years). All participants underwent a full ophthalmological examination where visual field mean deviation (VFMD) was measured with HFA and the peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer thickness (pRNFL) was measured with OCT. MRI data were collected on a 3-T Siemens Prisma scanner. We acquired T1-weighted anatomical scans (UMCG and OVGU voxel size: 1 mm3), three to four runs of retinotopic mapping, and two runs of resting-state fMRI (UMCG voxel size: 3 mm3; OVGU voxel size: 2.5 mm3). We applied a preprocessing and analysis pipeline, which included motion correction with SPM, co-registration of the anatomical to the functional images with ANTs, and gray matter segmentation for data resampling with FreeSurfer. The visual areas were defined using the Benson atlas, and later on confirmed for the central visual field using population receptive field (pRF) mapping 2,5. CF models were computed through coarse-to-fine fitting implemented in Python to estimate the spatial integration from V2 and V3 to V1. To test for group differences, CF size was regressed against eccentricity for each participant using bootstrapped linear fits based on 1,000 resamplings with replacement and weighted by variance explained of the CF model fit. These individually derived CF slopes were compared between groups using non-parametric tests, where a p-value of 0.05 or less was considered statistically significant.

Results

The results showed comparable CF properties between healthy controls and POAG in both V2 and V3. In the UMCG cohort, CF sizes did not show significant group differences after Bonferroni correction (V2: p = 0.41; V3: p = 0.21). These results were consistent in the OVGU cohort, with no significant group effects after correction (V2: p = 1.00; V3: p = 1.00). Analysis of the CF slope showed no significant group differences in either the UMCG (V2: p = 0.13; V3: p = 0.33) or OVGU datasets (V2: p = 0.24; V3: p = 0.31).

Conclusion

These findings support the assumption that the cortico-cortical spatial integration of visual information is stable in early-stage POAG. This stability may indicate that the visual cortex remains functionally preserved and potentially capable of processing the restored visual input. It is also true that the VFDs in early stages are more common in the periphery, and progress toward the center of the visual field only later in the disease. However, a substantial loss of retinal ganglion cells already occurs in the central visual field in early-stage POAG. For this reason, further analysis relating CF alterations to disease severity measures, such as pRNFL thickness and VFMD, are warranted. The next step is to extend the analysis to the peripheral cortical representations using the Benson atlas, as these correspond to the visual cortex’ areas most affected by the VFDs. Assessing the presence of functional alterations in these locations is essential to determine whether the visual cortex retains the capacity to process restored input, as these are the areas where the stem cell-based therapies will be expected to restore visual input.
