Introduction

The folded cortex in the human brain is composed of hundreds of cortical areas1 which in turn are composed of 5/6 layers. Folding results in gyral crowns having thick deep layers and thin upper layers, and conversely sulcal fundi having thin deep layers and thick upper layers. This observation came from the classic histological studies in the 1890s and 1900s, and was formalised as an equivolume principle by Bok2. The past decade has seen the volumetric conservation across layers between gyri and sulci used in fMRI studies of cortical areas2. In particular, the principle has been implemented in a few fMRI analysis software. However, not only are these approaches image-based but also cortical depths are uniformly sampled therein. So, surface-based approaches3 that provide a geometric realization of the cortical ribbon were developed. Now the availability of ground truth surfaces of cortical layers in cortical areas from a post-mortem human brain called BigBrain1 is an opportunity to estimate the parametric depths of layers from different cortical areas. Estimating the depths is done in two steps2. First, for a cortical area, the triangulated inner grey/white inner surface is deformed towards the gray/csf outer surface with the normal lines between the two surfaces constrained to be orthogonal to the evolving surface. Second, the depths are reparametrized for equivolumetric preservation during the evolution. Estimation is based on a novel metric called varifold3 which is also used to compute the evolving surface. By comparing the evolving surface and cortical layer interfaces, the parametric depths for each cortical area are obtained. The goals of this study are: 1) use PyKeOps4 developed for kernel operations to speed up the intensive calculations of the evolving surfaces, and 2) assess the variation in the parametric depths across cortical areas.

Methods

Cortical layer interfaces with six-layer architecture (VI/V, V/IV, IV/III, III/II and II/I from WM-GM to pial surfaces) from 14 regions of interest along the sensorimotor-association axis (SA-axis) (7Am, 7PL, MIP, 2, 8Ad, 46, 9-46d, PHA1, STSda, TGd, TE1a, IP0, PFm, PGs) were extracted from the BigBrain Glasser 360 v1.0 surface atlas1,5. Approximately, cortical thickness is patterned according to the position along the SA-axis5 (SA rank): SA are thinner and association areas are thicker. For each region, local cortical thickness and a parametric depth map were derived from a surface matching process. Emulating the direction of cortical columnar migration, the WM-GM surface was deformed to match the pial surface using surface LDDMM, with the flow constrained to be normal to the evolving surface3. Local cortical thickness was calculated as the length of a normal line between corresponding vertices on the WM-GM and pial surfaces. The parametric depth corresponds to the reparametrized evolution time tϵ[0,1], in which the mapping satisfies an equivolume constraint, t=0 and t=1 correspond to the GM-WM (inner) and pial (outer) surfaces, respectively, and other values represent the depth of a cortical layer interface relative to the GM-WM surface. The maps were calculated using two metrics: (i) the median time of intersections of trajectories of the evolving surface with ground truth cortical layer interface and (ii) the time of minimal varifold distance between ground truth cortical layer interfaces and evolution surfaces. Parametric, relative cortical layer thicknesses were calculated using first forward differences on the parametric depth map. Infragranular dominance was calculated in terms of relative thicknesses as (V + VI) / (II + III). Spearman’s rho was used to capture correlations between relative layer thicknesses and SA rank.

Results

Aligning with Bok’s equivolume principle, within the same region, gyri tend to be thicker than sulci, deep layers in gyri are thicker than those in sulci, and vice versa for superficial layers, and the total laminar volume remains constant even as the cortex folds. The parametric depth map for layer interfaces VI/V, V/IV, IV/III, III/II and II/I  is estimated as t= [0.12, 0.23, 0.55, 0.64, 0.83] respectively, with layer IV having the biggest relative thickness (0.32). Layer II relative thickness in the right hemisphere positively correlated with SA rank (R: ρ = 0.43, p=0.08), suggesting layer II may occupy a larger proportion of the cortical ribbon toward the association pole. Layers III (L: ρ = -0.40, p=0.16; R: ρ = -0.38, p=0.18) and IV (L: ρ = -0.28, p=0.32; R: ρ = -0.26, p=0.36) relative thicknesses negatively correlated with SA rank, indicating layers III and IV may take a smaller proportion of the cortical ribbon when moving toward the association pole. Infragranular dominance was positively correlated with SA rank in the left hemisphere (L: ρ = 0.34, p=0.23; R: ρ = 0.002, p=0.99), aligning with previous laminar work in BigBrain on the link between infragranular dominance and hierarchical positioning/connectivity. Finally, using PyKeOps for kernel operations on GPU on a smooth unedited surface versus numpy+jit on CPU on the decimated version (necessary to avoid memory errors) for constrained LDDMM and varifold calculations were respectively accelerated by 50x and 973x. Results were dependent on the quality of the reconstructed surfaces obtained from BigBrain. Also, results may not generalize to younger or developmental groups since BigBrain was from a single and older adult. Furthermore, there were difficulties segmenting the layer II/III interface and low variation in layer II thickness between areas1. Similar difficulties occurred for the other interfaces.

Conclusion

This study is the first to demonstrate how BigBrain can be used to validate Bok’s equivolume principle in 3D. Cortical layer thickness -  and by extension, cortical thickness -  is a local measure strongly correlated with local shape and should not be averaged across regions. Future work will test more regions and subjects in health and disease, as well as test whether spatial transcriptomics in cortical layers adheres to the parametric depth maps developed here.
