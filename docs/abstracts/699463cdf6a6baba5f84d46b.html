Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) typically shows widespread cortical atrophy on neuroimaging1, yet its spatial pattern varies markedly across individuals, even at similar clinical stages and biomarker profiles. Whether this heterogeneity reflects discrete anatomical subtypes or a common process progressing at region-specific rates remains unresolved, partly because links between group-level alterations and interindividual variability have not been quantified.

Growing evidence supports the view that AD is a disorder of network disconnection, in which pathology spreads along vulnerable connections and disrupts distributed brain systems2,3. Thus, heterogeneous atrophy may be constrained by connectome architecture, but how strongly network structure explains anatomical variability remains uncertain.

In this study, we comprehensively characterized neuroanatomical heterogeneity across the AD continuum using large-scale, multicenter, harmonized structural MRI (sMRI). We tested the coupling between regional mean alterations and variability and its strengthening with disease progression. We then applied a connectome-constrained diffusion model to assess whether locally initiated network propagation can recapitulate structural abnormalities. Finally, we linked derived neuroanatomical subtypes to clinical features and cognitive outcomes, delineating subtypes with distinct pathophysiological signatures.

Methods

Participants and image processing

After rigorous quality control, we ultimately included 47,572 participants (ages 45–85) from 10 cohorts, spanning the AD continuum from cognitively normal (CN) to mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and AD dementia. All T1-weighted images were processed using FreeSurfer to derive regional gray matter volume (GMV), cortical thickness (CT), and surface area (SA). We parcellated the cortex into 62 regions based on the Desikan–Killiany–Tourville (DKT) atlas, and additionally included 15 subcortical structures: the bilateral thalamus, caudate, putamen, globus pallidus, hippocampus, amygdala, nucleus accumbens, and the brainstem.

Individual brain atrophy maps based on normative modeling

First, we estimated normative models to predict each structural feature by hierarchical Bayesian regression (HBR) in a large healthy reference cohort from the UK Biobank (N = 38,833). Covariates included age, sex, site, and estimated total intracranial volume (eTIV). Then, we transferred the normative models to external cohorts by site-wise posterior adaptation, using 20% of local controls to harmonize reference scales. For each participant, we computed regional standardized deviation scores (z-scores) for each structural measure, yielding an individualized deviation profile.

Statistical analysis

Based on the regional z-scores of each structural measure, we used two-sample t-tests to compare mean differences between patients and controls, and quantified relative variability in patients compared with controls using the log variability ratio. These analyses were performed in both MCI and AD.

Individualized epicenter mapping with connectome-constrained diffusion

We next employed a connectome-constrained diffusion model (CDM) to simulate putative spatial propagation patterns of GMV alterations across brain regions. Multi-modal normative connectome architectures were constructed in an independent sample of 150 healthy young adults using diffusion MRI, resting-state fMRI, and T1-weighted MRI. Using a seed-search procedure, we identified the optimal epicenter that best recapitulated the observed pattern of gray-matter atrophy and assessed its spatial concordance with tau-PET-derived propagation patterns. At the individual level, we estimated personalized epicenters to test whether variability in initiation sites contributes to anatomical heterogeneity. Finally, we examined associations between the resulting neuroanatomical phenotypes and cognitive performance, clinical features, and longitudinal progression.

Results

We found significant between-group differences in GMV across 71 brain regions, CT across 60 regions, and SA across 50 regions. In most regions, patients showed significantly lower values than normal controls, with the largest effect sizes in the bilateral hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, and amygdala. Notably, only GMV in the left pallidum and SA in the pericalcarine cortex were significantly larger in patients.

Additionally, variability increased significantly for GMV in 54 regions, CT in 58 regions, and SA in 17 regions, whereas greater homogeneity was observed only in the left isthmus cingulate cortex and rostral anterior cingulate cortex. In a direct comparison, mean regional variability across the brain was higher in AD than in MCI. Furthermore, the most variable regions differed by disease stage: in MCI, variability was mainly localized to the bilateral entorhinal cortex and hippocampus, whereas in AD it was concentrated in the inferior temporal and inferior parietal regions. At the whole-brain level, regional variability was significantly negatively correlated with the case–control effect-size map of structural differences (MCI: r=−0.55, P=2.17×10⁻17; AD: r=−0.66, P=6.24×10⁻25).

The CDM reproduced the observed group-level pattern of GMV differences in both the MCI and AD groups. Across the three network-constrained models, the medial temporal–limbic system was consistently identified as the optimal seed (epicenter) for diffusion, particularly the entorhinal cortex. Tau-PET–based propagation analyses yielded convergent results. In addition, diffusion patterns seeded in the inferior temporal and inferior parietal regions were likely to account for the group-level GMV heterogeneity. At the individual level, the NDM achieved significant fits in 85.47% of patients (N=2,247) and revealed separable spatial subtypes, with the medial temporal–limbic subtype (33%) and the posterior subtype (17%) being the most prevalent.

Conclusion

This study provides further evidence for heterogeneous neuroanatomical changes across the AD continuum and suggests that AD-related structural heterogeneity evolves with disease progression. Across stages, we observed both widespread group-mean atrophy and increased interindividual variability. Importantly, these features showed a systematic spatial correspondence: regions with more pronounced atrophy tended to exhibit greater between-subject differences. This coupling also varied by clinical stage, consistent with an intensification of structural heterogeneity as the disease advances.

At the group level, a connectome-constrained diffusion model captured the principal spatial organization of the observed atrophy pattern. At the individual level, we identified multiple heterogeneous patterns that could be grouped into distinct atrophy subtypes, suggesting that variability in neuroanatomical initiation sites is an important source of anatomical diversity. Together, these findings indicate that interindividual differences in the loci of gray-matter loss may reflect etiological diversity at the molecular level, ultimately manifesting as increased macroscale structural variability in patients.

Overall, we propose an analytical framework to infer, at the individual level, putative origins of pathological change and their propagation pathways. This framework offers a promising avenue for understanding AD-related structural heterogeneity and for enabling individualized stratification.
