Introduction

Neurosurgical resection remains the most effective treatment for diffuse brain tumors, yet its success depends on preserving critical cognitive functions, particularly language.1 Diffusion MRI tractography has reshaped neurosurgical planning by enabling patient-specific visualization of major white-matter pathways, which are increasingly used as “no-go” borders around presumed language-related bundles such as the arcuate fasciculus (AF), inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (IFOF), frontal aslant tract (FAT), and both inferior and superior longitudinal fasciculus (ILF/SLF, respectively).1 This bundle-centric approach, however, assumes a direct mapping between single tracts and single functions and may fail to capture the distributed organization of language within large-scale brain networks.

Contemporary network neuroscience models instead conceptualize language as an emergent property of interactions across multiple pathways embedded within broader structural and functional networks.2 Dysconnectomic approaches build on normative connectomes and lesion network mapping to relate focal lesions to the large-scale networks they engage, providing a complementary, network-level estimator of functional risk.3

In this study, we compared bundle-based and network-based models of surgical risk in a cohort of glioma patients undergoing tumort resection. Using deterministic and probabilistic tractography, we derived bundle-specific measures of integrity and resection distance for key language tracts, and contrasted these with dysconnectomic indices from semantic and phonological networks obtained via functional white-matter lesion network mapping. We then related these measures to phonemic and semantic fluency one week and one month after surgery. Network-level dysconnection consistently outperformed single-bundle measures in characterizing short and long-term surgery-induced language deficits, supporting the integration of network-informed metrics into neurosurgical planning.

Methods

We retrospectively analyzed a cohort of glioma patients undergoing surgery to address three questions: (i) the specificity and sensitivity of deterministic and probabilistic tractography in capturing surgery-induced language impairments; (ii) the relationship between bundle-resection distance and postoperative language preservation; and (iii) whether subject-specific tractography or population-based dysconnectomics better predicts postoperative language deficits.

Patients and Neuropsychological Assessment

The cohort included 83 patients (56 men, mean age 52.7±14.3), with 66 high-grade and 17 low-grade gliomas. Tumors were located in the left (n=43) or right hemisphere (n=40). Forty-two patients underwent awake surgery; 29 had IDH mutations and 34 exhibited MGMT promoter methylation. Language was assessed preoperatively and at one week and one month postoperatively using standardized phonemic and semantic fluency tests.

MRI Acquisition and Preprocessing

Pre- and postoperative MRI were acquired on a clinical 1.5T scanner, including T1-weighted structural imaging and a 60-direction diffusion scheme (b=1000 s/mm²). Postoperative gadolinium-enhanced T1 images (24 h after surgery) were used to segment resection cavities. T1 images were skull-stripped, aligned to preoperative anatomy, and finally registered to MNI space. Diffusion data underwent standard preprocessing (eddy current and head motion correction) and diffusivity reconstruction. Probabilistic tractography was performed using constrained spherical deconvolution; deterministic tractography used a tensor model and EuDX.

Tractography and Bundle-Based Measures

For each patient, we reconstructed the AF, SLF, FAT, IFOF, and ILF in the tumor hemisphere using ROI-based segmentation.4 Patients were stratified according to bundle integrity (intact vs. partially resected) based on overlap between each volumetric bundle representation and the resection cavity. Group differences in phonemic (AF, FAT, SLF) and semantic (ILF, IFOF) fluency were tested at both postoperative time points using one-sided Mann-Whitney U tests, with effect sizes quantified using Cliff’s δ. In patients with intact bundles, we correlated minimum resection distance with absolute fluency scores and with change relative to presurgical levels (Pearson’s r). Bonferroni correction at the bundle level was applied to control for multiple comparisons.

We also derived bundle-specific safety distance thresholds. Distances from the surgical cavity to each bundle were sampled across percentiles of the empirical distribution, and for each threshold we computed Cliff’s δ. The bundle-specific safety distance threshold was defined as the distance that maximized the between-group effect size. Intuitively, this threshold represents the closest distance neurosurgeons can approach to the bundle before functional deficits become clinically meaningful.

Network-Based Dysconnectomics

To compare tract-based and network-based models, we repeated the threshold and correlational analyses using normative semantic and phonological networks derived from the integration of white-matter direct electrical stimulation and functional lesion network mapping.3 Dysconnection severity was defined as the 90th percentile of the overlap between each resection cavity and the corresponding unthresholded frequency map.

Control Analyses

We examined whether clinical factors – including type of surgery, tumor grade, IDH status, and MGMT methylation – were associated with language outcomes using one-sided Mann–Whitney U tests. We also correlated resection volume with fluency performance.

Results

Bundle-specific dysconnection

Across bundles, the number of patients with partial resections varied remarkably (N between 8 and 36).

IFOF

Deterministic (N=72) and probabilistic (N=79) tracking showed higher semantic fluency in patients with an intact IFOF one week but not one month after surgery (p<0.01; δ=0.38 and 0.31, respectively), surviving Bonferroni correction. Resection distance correlated positively with semantic scores but did not survive multiple comparison correction. No significant associations with resection distance were observed.

ILF

Neither deterministic (N=75) nor probabilistic (N=78) tracking revealed differences in semantic fluency between intact vs. resected ILF at either time point (δ ≤ 0.23, all p-values > 0.05). No correlations with resection distance or score change were observed.

AF

With deterministic (N=74) and probabilistic (N=82) tracking, phonemic fluency did not differ between groups at one week or one month after surgery (δ ≤ 0.19). Resection distance did not correlate with absolute scores. A moderate correlation with recovery at one month (r≈−0.33) did not survive Bonferroni correction.

FAT

The FAT was reconstructed only with probabilistic tracking (N=79). We observed higher phonemic fluency for patients with an intact FAT one week postoperatively (p-value <0.05; δ=0.32), but not after Bonferroni correction. No associations with resection distance or recovery were detected.

SLF

Deterministic tractography (N = 77) showed higher phonemic fluency for patients with an intact bundle at one month after surgery (p < 0.01; δ = 0.52), surviving Bonferroni correction, whereas the one-week effect did not. No correlations with resection distance or score change were observed. Probabilistic tractography (N = 81) showed no between-group differences surviving Bonferroni correction. In patients with an intact SLF, greater resection distance was associated with better phonemic recovery at one month (Δ pre–post: r = −0.38; p < 0.01), surviving the adjustment for multiple testing.

Hemisphere-specific analyses

Effects generally followed the whole-cohort trends, with somewhat larger effect sizes for left-hemisphere tumors in AF, FAT, and SLF. Interpretation was limited by small sample sizes for patients with partial resections (often N<10).

Bundle-specific safety distance zones

Optimal safety distances varied substantially across bundles, algorithms, and time points (Fig.1). One week after surgery, the largest effects were observed for the IFOF (semantic, δ > 0.6) and SLF (phonemic, δ > 0.6). Deterministic tracking identified maximal effects at near-zero distances, whereas probabilistic tracking showed maximal effects when resections remained ≥10 mm away from the bundles. At one month after surgery, IFOF and SLF (both δ > 0.5) again showed the strongest effects. The discrepancy between tracking approaches was largest for the SLF (0 mm vs. >15 mm), and less pronounced for the IFOF (10-15 mm).

Normative network-based dysconnectomics

Normative dysconnectomic measures were available for 82 patients. Greater semantic or phonological network disconnection was consistently associated with poorer fluency scores at both time points (semantic one week: r=−0.39, p<0.001; semantic one month r=−0.23, p<0.05; phonological one week r=−0.28, p<0.05; phonological one month r=−0.33, p<0.01). Score changes were not significantly correlated.

Network disconnectivity thresholds outperformed bundle-based distance thresholds. A semantic dysconnectomic value of 0.35 yielded a δ=0.54, 42-260% larger than bundle effects. Phonological dysconnection thresholds also produced large effects (δ up to 0.54), exceeding bundle-based performance by 3-767%.

Control analyses

Asleep surgery, IDH wild-type status, and resection volume were not associated with fluency outcomes. Unexpectedly, MGMT methylation was linked to poorer phonemic fluency at one week (δ=0.30), and low-grade gliomas to poorer semantic fluency at one month (δ=0.35) after surgery.

Conclusion

The present study aimed to clarify whether postoperative language outcomes after glioma surgery are better captured by tract-specific measures or by network-level dysconnectomics. By systematically comparing deterministic and probabilistic tractography, bundle-resection distance metrics, and normative network-based indices, we demonstrate that network-level disconnection provides a more sensitive and robust predictor of surgery-induced language deficits than single-bundle approaches.

Our findings confirm that major white-matter bundles remain critical structural substrates for language. Integrity of the inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus and superior longitudinal fasciculus was consistently associated with semantic and phonemic fluency, respectively, reinforcing the concept of bundles as anatomical pillars supporting higher cognitive functions. Importantly, we extend this framework by providing, for the first time, quantitative bundle-specific safety distance thresholds, offering a principled estimate of how close surgical resections can approach key tracts before functional impairment becomes likely.

However, bundle-centric models only partially explain postoperative outcomes. Normative dysconnectomic measures derived from semantic and phonological networks yielded substantially larger effect sizes than any single-bundle metric, even under conservative correction. Network-level disconnection consistently predicted language deficits at both early and later postoperative stages, capturing functional vulnerability that tract-specific integrity and distance metrics failed to detect. These findings support hodological models of brain organization, in which language arises from distributed, dynamically interacting networks rather than from isolated pathways.5

From a clinical–theoretical perspective, our results argue for a shift from a strictly tract-based “eloquent fiber” paradigm toward an integrative framework combining local tract anatomy with global network vulnerability. While respecting tract-specific safety margins remains essential, network-level metrics provide a complementary dimension that better reflects the distributed nature of language processing. Integrating dysconnectomics into neurosurgical planning may therefore improve functional risk estimation and refine decision-making in brain tumor surgery.
