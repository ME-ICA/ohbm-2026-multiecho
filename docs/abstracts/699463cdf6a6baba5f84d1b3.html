Introduction

Panic disorder (PD) is a common and disabling anxiety disorder characterized by sudden, recurrent panic attacks accompanied by anticipatory anxiety and avoidance behaviors. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is a first-line intervention for PD and has demonstrated robust efficacy in symptom reduction. However, the neurobiological mechanisms underlying CBT's therapeutic effects remain insufficiently understood.^1 Prior neuroimaging studies have suggested that CBT may exert its effects by modulating activity in brain regions responsible for emotional regulation, cognitive control, and self-monitoring, particularly the inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), and superior temporal gyrus (STG).^2

Near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) offers a non-invasive and practical method for assessing task-related cortical hemodynamic responses in clinical populations. Due to its portability and resistance to motion artifacts, NIRS is well suited for repeated use in psychiatric research settings. Although prior studies have examined brain activation patterns in PD, few have applied longitudinal NIRS to investigate neurofunctional changes resulting from psychotherapy, particularly CBT.

This study aimed to examine whether CBT induces measurable changes in prefrontal and temporal activation among patients with PD using NIRS during a verbal fluency task (VFT). We hypothesized that CBT would increase [oxy-Hb] in frontotemporal regions, reflecting enhanced cognitive-emotional integration. We also explored whether these neural changes were associated with improvements in panic and anxiety symptoms, providing insight into mechanisms of recovery and potential biomarkers of treatment response.

Methods

Seven female patients (mean age 42.4 ± 3.2 years) meeting DSM-IV criteria for panic disorder (PD) were recruited from the outpatient psychiatry clinic of Gunma University Hospital. All participants were right-handed native Japanese speakers as confirmed by the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory. Exclusion criteria included age over 60, neurological disorders, psychosis, intellectual disability, or substance abuse. Three patients were on stable doses of psychotropic medications, which were not changed during the study.

Participants received a standardized cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) program comprising twelve weekly sessions of approximately 50 minutes, delivered by licensed clinical psychologists with over five years of CBT experience. The program consisted of five core modules: psychoeducation about panic mechanisms, cognitive restructuring, interoceptive exposure, situational exposure, and relapse prevention. Weekly homework assignments were provided and reviewed to promote engagement and consolidation of therapeutic skills.

Clinical symptoms were evaluated before and after CBT using the Panic Disorder Severity Scale (PDSS), Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAM-A), State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI), 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17), and Zung Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS). Verbal fluency was assessed with a phonemic verbal fluency task (VFT), wherein patients generated as many words as possible beginning with assigned Japanese syllables (/a/, /ka/, /sa/) during a 60-second period.

Cortical hemodynamic responses during the VFT were recorded using a 52-channel near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) system (ETG-4000, Hitachi Medical Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), which employs dual-wavelength light (695 and 830 nm) to measure changes in oxygenated ([oxy-Hb]) and deoxygenated hemoglobin ([deoxy-Hb]). The optode array followed a 3×11 configuration placed over the bilateral frontotemporal cortex in accordance with the international 10–20 system. Channels covered the inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), and superior temporal gyrus (STG). The sampling rate was 10 Hz, and spatial resolution was approximately 3 cm.

The task protocol comprised three phases: a pre-task baseline (10 seconds), an activation block (60 seconds), and a post-task baseline (55.1 seconds). During activation, participants generated words under syllabic constraints. During baseline periods, they repeated vowels aloud to control for articulatory activity.

NIRS data were preprocessed using a custom MATLAB pipeline. Artifacts were removed via moving average filtering (5-second window), and signals were linearly detrended. All data were normalized to the pre-task baseline. Only [oxy-Hb] data were analyzed due to its higher signal-to-noise ratio and better sensitivity to cortical activation.

Statistical analysis was conducted using cluster-based non-parametric permutation testing to identify significant pre- vs. post-CBT differences in [oxy-Hb] time courses. One-way repeated-measures ANOVA was applied at each time point and channel, and clusters were formed from spatially and temporally adjacent suprathreshold F-values. A Monte Carlo simulation with 10,000 permutations was used to generate a null distribution, and cluster-level p-values were corrected for multiple comparisons.

Mean [oxy-Hb] values for each significant cluster were extracted. Correlation analyses were conducted between the mean cluster signals and changes in clinical scale scores (e.g., PDSS, STAI-state) using Spearman’s rank correlation. Significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Following the completion of cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), patients exhibited significant reductions in panic-related symptoms. The mean Panic Disorder Severity Scale (PDSS) score decreased from 10.4 (±5.9) at baseline to 3.1 (±2.8) post-treatment (p = 0.0117). Although other clinical indicators such as the Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAM-A), State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) for both state and trait anxiety, Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17), and Zung Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) showed numerical improvements, these changes did not reach statistical significance. Additionally, verbal fluency performance, as measured by the number of words generated during the task, increased modestly from a mean of 32.4 (±11.4) to 38.3 (±13.2) words (p = 0.35), suggesting that task familiarity alone was unlikely to account for the neural changes observed.

Near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) analysis revealed significant changes in cortical activation following CBT. Cluster-based permutation testing identified five spatiotemporally distinct clusters showing increased oxygenated hemoglobin ([oxy-Hb]) concentrations during task performance. These clusters were distributed across bilateral frontotemporal regions, specifically the right inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), right superior temporal gyrus (STG), left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), left middle temporal gyrus (MTG), and left STG. Temporal analysis indicated that clusters in the right hemisphere tended to be more active during the early phase of the verbal fluency task (VFT), whereas left hemisphere clusters demonstrated peak activity in the latter half or even post-task, suggesting dynamic reorganization of cognitive control and semantic processing pathways as a result of CBT.

Time-course plots of the [oxy-Hb] signal further confirmed these observations. In all five clusters, post-CBT measurements revealed elevated and sustained increases in [oxy-Hb] during task engagement compared to pre-CBT measurements. Importantly, neither [deoxy-Hb] nor total hemoglobin showed similar significant changes, reducing the likelihood that the effects were attributable to systemic physiological noise such as blood pressure or respiration. Topographical representations of [oxy-Hb] revealed a clear increase in signal strength localized to the bilateral IFG and STG, supporting the hypothesis that CBT facilitates functional enhancement in regions implicated in language production and emotional regulation.

Among the five significant clusters, activity in the left STG (corresponding to channel 31) demonstrated a significant negative correlation with clinical symptom improvements. Specifically, higher increases in [oxy-Hb] in this region were associated with greater reductions in PDSS scores (ρ = −0.793, p = 0.0334) and STAI-state scores (ρ = −0.775, p = 0.0408), suggesting that neural changes in the left temporal cortex may serve as a potential biomarker for therapeutic response. In contrast, no significant correlations were observed between [oxy-Hb] changes in other clusters and clinical outcomes or VFT performance metrics.

Conclusion

This study provides converging evidence that cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) produces measurable functional changes in cortical activation patterns in patients diagnosed with panic disorder (PD), as captured by near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS). We observed significant task-related increases in oxygenated hemoglobin ([oxy-Hb]) concentrations in bilateral frontotemporal regions following CBT, most prominently in the right inferior frontal gyrus (IFG) and the left superior temporal gyrus (STG). These regions are functionally associated with emotion regulation, attentional control, internal verbalization, and semantic processing—core domains known to be disrupted in PD. ^3^4^5 Our findings suggest that CBT supports neural recovery in distributed cognitive-affective networks and that NIRS is capable of detecting such changes in ecologically valid clinical contexts.

Notably, these increases in [oxy-Hb] were not accompanied by significant changes in verbal fluency task (VFT) performance, indicating that the observed neural activation was unlikely due to task learning or repetition effects. Instead, the enhanced hemodynamic responses are likely attributable to therapy-induced neuroplasticity. Among the five significant clusters, activation in the left STG showed a robust negative correlation with symptom improvement on the Panic Disorder Severity Scale (PDSS) and the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI-state), suggesting that increased engagement of this region may serve as a potential biomarker of therapeutic response. The STG is known to play a critical role in self-referential language and processing of internal dialogue, processes that are targeted in CBT through techniques such as cognitive restructuring and self-monitoring.

Temporal dynamics of activation revealed a lateralized pattern: right frontotemporal regions, including the IFG, peaked during early task engagement, likely reflecting attentional and inhibitory control processes, while left hemisphere activation in regions such as the STG emerged later, possibly representing semantic elaboration and integration. This temporal shift may correspond to a CBT-facilitated transition from reactive, fear-driven processing to more deliberate, language-mediated regulation. Such findings offer a plausible neurobiological model of CBT’s clinical effects and align with broader theories of hemispheric asymmetry in affective disorders.

These findings support the hypothesis that psychotherapy can normalize dysfunctional brain circuits and that NIRS can detect such functional shifts. Given the non-invasive, low-burden nature of NIRS, its clinical utility as a monitoring or predictive tool for treatment response is particularly promising. The ability to observe cortical changes over time in real-world therapeutic contexts offers opportunities for personalized interventions and early identification of non-responders.

However, the study has several limitations. The small sample size restricts the generalizability of results, and the lack of a control condition prevents causal attribution of changes solely to CBT. Additionally, three participants were taking psychotropic medications throughout the study, which, although stable, may have influenced neural responses. Future studies should include medication-free samples and appropriate control groups, and ideally incorporate follow-up assessments to examine the durability of observed effects.

In summary, this study demonstrates that CBT induces distinct neurofunctional changes detectable by NIRS in patients with PD. Enhanced activation in prefrontal and temporal regions—particularly the left STG—correlated with clinical improvement and supports the utility of NIRS as a translational tool in psychiatric care. These results contribute to the growing body of evidence for psychotherapy-induced neural plasticity and highlight the importance of integrating neuroimaging methods into the evaluation of therapeutic outcomes in anxiety disorders.
