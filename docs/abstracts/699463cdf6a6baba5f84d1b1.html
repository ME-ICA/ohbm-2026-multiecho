Introduction

Foundation models for fMRI have rapidly expanded in size, often adopting scaling strategies inspired by natural language processing and computer vision. Yet it is increasingly unclear whether very large architectures are necessary—or even optimal—for neuroimaging, where domain constraints, data limitations, and neuroscientific interpretability all differ substantially from general-purpose machine learning. BrainSymphony takes a contrasting approach: instead of increasing parameters, it embeds domain-specific priors directly into the architecture to create a lightweight, multimodal model capable of capturing spatial, temporal, and structural regularities using fMRI and diffusion MRI.  Recent large-scale models have emphasized scale and data volume [1-2], but BrainSymphony demonstrates that efficiency and interpretability can coexist without sacrificing generalization.

Here we evaluate whether a pretrained BrainSymphony model—trained independently of any psychedelic data—captures drug-related and context-specific reorganizations of whole-brain dynamics in the external PsiConnect dataset (62 participants scanned before/after psilocybin across rest, meditation, music, movie). Furthermore, PsiConnect data includes subjective ratings using the MEQ-30, a validated questionnaire capturing mystical-type experiences including unity, transcendence of time and space, ineffability, insightfulness, and positive affect. Linking the MEQ to neural mechanisms remains an open challenge.

This dataset provides a stringent test of out-of-distribution generalization, mechanistic interpretability, and sensitivity to subjective experience intensity. We show that BrainSymphony reconstructs unseen fMRI time series with high fidelity and reveals fine-grained psychedelic-related network reorganization that classical functional connectivity fails to capture.

Methods

BrainSymphony's architecture consists of three parallel fMRI encoding streams: (i) a Spatial Transformer operating over ROIs augmented with mixed positional encodings (including neurobiologically informed gradient maps), (ii) a Temporal Transformer that treats each time point as a token and uses sinusoidal positional embeddings to encode temporal order in the fMRI sequence, and (iii) a 1D convolutional context extractor that captures short-range, transient temporal dynamics. These create a set of multimodal tokens summarizing the time series from complementary perspectives (Fig. 1a). A Perceiver module then performs cross-attention between a set of learned latent queries and all encoder outputs, followed by deep self-attention within the latent space. This results in a compact, information-rich latent representation from which ROI-level signals can be reconstructed (Fig. 1b).

The attention mechanism provides a principled way to estimate directed influence between ROIs. We use two complementary metrics:

• Incoming attention to A: degree to which other regions attend to A (A influences others).

• Outgoing attention from A: degree to which A attends to others (A is influenced by the rest).

This distinction treats attention as a structured, interpretable signature of information flow—not ground-truth causality but a biologically meaningful directed influence.

We applied the frozen BrainSymphony model (pretrained on HCP and HCP-Aging) directly to the external PsiConnect dataset (baseline vs psilocybin across rest, meditation, music, movie). Reconstruction was quantified using R², Pearson ρ, and MAE, with ROI-shuffle controls to test significance (Fig. 2a). We then tested condition discriminability by applying a simple linear classifier to the ROI×ROI directed-influence matrices to decode the four imaging states at baseline and under psilocybin.

For mechanistic interpretation, we derived directed attention–based influence maps from BrainSymphony’s Perceiver latent space. For each participant, condition, and session (Baseline vs Psilocybin), we computed two forms of directed influence: incoming (receptive) attention, which quantifies how strongly other regions attend to a given ROI (i.e., how much that ROI influences the rest of the system), and outgoing (expressive) attention, which quantifies how strongly that ROI attends to other regions (i.e., how much it is influenced by the rest of the brain). Psilocybin-induced changes were then isolated by computing psilocybin-minus-baseline difference matrices for both measures. These difference maps were summarized at two complementary levels:

(i) global patterns of expressive influence, visualized using circos plots of the top 500 psilocybin-related changes in outgoing attention (Fig. 2b). In these plots, edges reflect how strongly each network’s ROIs attend to other networks, and inner bar tracks summarize the total outgoing attention for each network, highlighting networks that act as major integrators of input from the rest of the brain under psilocybin;
(ii) canonical network-level averages of receptive influence, obtained by pooling across ROIs within each functional network, yielding network-wise fingerprints of how much each network influences the rest of the system (Fig. 2c).
 

Finally, to examine subjective modulation, participants were ranked by MEQ-30 total score, and the top/bottom 10% were classified as High-MEQ and Low-MEQ. We then recomputed psilocybin-related reorganization separately for these groups (Fig. 2d), focusing especially on whether distinct canonical brain networks displayed differential changes depending on subjective intensity when comparing baseline to psilocybin. For comparison, we repeated analogous analyses using functional connectivity matrices.

Results

BrainSymphony reconstructed unseen psychedelic fMRI time series with high fidelity across all conditions (Fig. 2a). R² and Pearson ρ values substantially exceeded shuffled controls, confirming that the model captures structured psilocybin-state dynamics despite being trained entirely on non-psychedelic data. The quality of reconstruction was consistent across subjects and conditions, demonstrating robustness to both pharmacological state and behavioral context.

Directed-influence matrices allowed accurate decoding of rest, meditation, music, and movie conditions at baseline (accuracy ≈0.64). Under psilocybin, decoding dropped to ≈0.46, closely echoing prior findings that psychedelics disrupt modular boundaries and increase integration between large-scale systems (while still notably above chance level of 0.25) [3-4]. The fact that a pretrained model reproduces these effects is strong evidence for the biological validity of the influence patterns it extracts.

Outgoing influence (how much a region depends on the rest of the brain) showed strong psilocybin-related increases in the DMN, Control, Somato-motor, and Visual networks (Fig. 2b).

. The DMN became more influenced by others in all coniditons, consistent with reduced DMN autonomy under psychedelics.

. The Control network showed increased influenced status during rest and meditation, suggesting executive circuits integrate more external information.

. Somato-motor regions showed increased outgoing influence during meditation and movie—contexts emphasizing bodily state and sensorimotor coupling.

. The Visual network was especially influenced during all conditions, aligning with enhanced multimodal processing and vivid imagery.

Incoming influence (how much a region influences the rest of the brain) revealed a striking pattern (Fig. 2c): visual cortex consistently demonstrated heightened influence across all eyes-closed conditions (rest, meditation, and music). This suggests that visual territories become dominant drivers of whole-brain dynamics even in the absence of external visual stimulation—a hallmark of psilocybin phenomenology including closed-eye visuals, geometric imagery, and internally generated visual scenes.

Limbic regions showed strong increases in incoming influence during emotionally rich conditions (particularly music). This aligns with well-documented affective amplification and increased emotional sensitivity under psychedelics.

The salience/ventral attention network also exhibited notable increases in influence during movie viewing, suggesting enhanced detection of behaviourally or emotionally significant stimuli.

Across all subjects, receptive influence increased globally under psilocybin—with no negative values for psilocybin minus baseline—indicating a widespread reduction in segregation and heightened cross-network permeability.

When subjects were grouped by MEQ-30 score, a clear divergence emerged: the depth of the psychedelic experience strongly shaped how psilocybin reorganized large-scale influence patterns (Fig. 2d).

High-MEQ subjects showed stronger and more spatially coherent increases in visual incoming influence during rest, meditation, and music, pronounced limbic influence increases during meditation, music, and movie,  and more substantial cross-network redistribution overall.

Low-MEQ subjects showed weaker, noisier patterns, suggesting that large-scale influence reallocation tracks the depth of mystical-type experience.

In contrast, repeating the same analysis using functional connectivity matrices showed broad decreases (High-MEQ) or increases (Low-MEQ) but without spatial specificity or alignment to subjective intensity.

Conclusion

BrainSymphony provides a compelling demonstration that compact, domain-aware foundation models can not only generalize to radically altered brain states but also yield mechanistic insights into how pharmacological interventions reshape large-scale communication. By distinguishing between influencer regions (high incoming attention) and influenced regions (high outgoing attention), the model extracts structure that maps meaningfully onto psychedelic phenomenology, neural integration, and behavioral context.

Psilocybin broadly increases network integration while redistributing influence toward visual and limbic systems, with these changes scaling according to subjective experience intensity. Visual territories emerge as dominant influencers even with closed eyes; limbic circuits become major drivers during affectively charged contexts; and canonical networks (DMN, Control, Somato-motor) exhibit condition-specific increases in how strongly they are influenced by other regions, reflecting reduced DMN autonomy, greater executive integration during internally focused states, and enhanced somato-motor coupling during embodied or perceptually rich contexts. These results align with, extend, and mechanistically explain prior evidence of reduced modularity and increased global integration under psychedelics [3-4].

More broadly, BrainSymphony illustrates that interpretable, efficient, multimodal foundation models can rival or surpass far larger architectures [1-2], democratize access to high-performing neuroimaging tools, and serve as transparent hypothesis-generating engines for systems neuroscience with proven implications in psychopharmacology. Its modular design, directed influence maps, and robust cross-domain generalization suggest a path forward for foundation models that are not only predictive but also mechanistically insightful, clinically applicable, and aligned with neuroscientific theory.
