Introduction

The plasticity-like after-effect has been reported to be induced by various excitatory repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) protocols, with a focus on alterations in neurochemistry and synaptic function. It has been shown that the balance between excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmitters is highly correlated with cortical plasticity. Our previous research, as demonstrated by Lin et al. (2023), has shown that 10-Hz high-frequency rTMS can alter the excitatory-to-inhibitory (E/I) ratio in neural networks, increasing excitation by weakening inhibitory processes as assessed by MRS. Specifically, the MRS response becomes evident only after a few hours following rTMS stimulation, rather than immediately. Meanwhile, intermittent theta burst stimulation (iTBS) is recognized as another form of excitatory neuromodulation. Although previous studies had indicated that iTBS applied to the visual cortex does not alter excitatory and inhibitory neurometabolic concentrations 1. However, its specific neurometabolic effects remain incompletely understood during an extended observation period. To address this question, we conducted multiple MRS measurements after iTBS stimulation to understand the excitatory and inhibitory cortical states by evaluating the underlying neurometabolic mechanisms.

Methods

We applied iTBS (n=12) and sham (n=12) stimulation over the visual cortex on healthy volunteers, and observed magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS)-derived glutamate+glutamine (Glx) and γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA+) levels using MEGA-PRESS sequence (TR/TE = 1500/68 ms, edit on/off pulse frequencies = 1.9 ppm/7.5ppm, number of average = 256 for each edit-on and edit-off, voxel size = 2 × 2 × 2 cm3) before and after stimulation (Before, 0.5 h, 3.5 h, and 24 h post-stimulation) 2. GABA+ and Glx levels were quantified by Gannet, standardized to tCr, and used to calculate the E/I ratio (Glx/GABA+). Phosphene Threshold (PT), indexing visual cortex excitability, was measured across two separate TMS sessions (TMS #1 and TMS #2). The protocol included seven total measurements. Two initial Baseline PTs were conducted. For TMS #1, PT was assessed pre-TMS, then post-TMS at +4.5 h and +24 h. Following a one-week washout, TMS #2 involved PT measurement pre-TMS and immediately post-TMS to capture the immediate effect of the second session. All measurements utilized a standardized staircase procedure to determine the minimum stimulus intensity required to elicit a phosphene sensation. A two-way linear mixed-model was conducted on neurometabolic change with factors of time and group.

Results

The results showed no significant Group × Time interaction for any neurometabolite.

GABA (F3,66 = 0.14, p = 0.93, η2g = 0.006) (Fig. 1a), Glx (F3,66 = 1.21, p = 0.31, η2g = 0.05) (Fig. 1b), or the E/I ratio (F3,66 = 0.94, p = 0.43, η2g = 0.04) (Fig. 1c) remained unaffected by the iTBS intervention.

The effect of the intervention on PT also revealed no statistically significant effect for the primary comparison of interest (Fig. 1d). Specifically, neither the main effect of Group (iTBS vs. sham; F1, 22 = 0.53, p = 0.47) nor the critical Group × Session interaction (F6, 105 = 0.58, p = 0.75) reached significance. Although the main effect of Session showed a numerical trend (F6, 105 = 1.97, p = 0.08), this suggests a non-specific change over time, independent of the active intervention.

Pearson correlation analysis across all subjects indicated that the inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA+ showed a positive trend toward correlation with PT values (r = 0.300, p = 0.076) (Fig. 1e), suggesting that higher GABA+ level is associated with increased cortical excitability (lower PT). Conversely, Glx level (r = 0.047, p = 0.785) (Fig. 1f) and the E/I ratio (r = -0.100, p = 0.562) (Fig. 1g) showed no significant association with PT.

Conclusion

We propose that, unlike high-frequency rTMS, iTBS applied over the visual cortex does not induce alterations in cortical states, even over an extended observation window. The advantages of TBS in modulating cortical excitability within the corticospinal system are not strongly advocated here 3,4. This finding is consistent with previous MRS study 1 and resting-state functional connectivity studies 5.

The varying efficacy of TBS protocols on motor and visual cortices may stem from discrepancies in receptor distribution. For instance, NMDA and GABAA receptors exhibit high densities in the primary visual cortex, whereas these receptor types demonstrate markedly lower densities across all layers in primary motor and premotor areas.

Another possible reason may be that applying 10-Hz rTMS to the visual cortex could potentially synchronize intrinsic brain rhythms by entraining occipital alpha oscillations. In the meantime, TBS protocols were considered to target theta oscillations, which are primarily recorded in the motor regions. Consequently, such oscillatory characteristics could elucidate the effectiveness of TBS protocols in the motor cortex, while their impact on the visual cortex remains less pronounced.

Therefore, through a comprehensive examination of these stimulation protocols and their impact on brain function, we aim to provide further insights for the development of relevant clinical applications.
