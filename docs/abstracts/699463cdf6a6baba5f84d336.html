Introduction

How does the human brain’s electrophysiological activity evolve across the lifespan? Age is one of the most fundamental factors shaping brain function, influencing neural dynamics from early development through older adulthood. Electroencephalography (EEG) offers a unique window into these age-related processes, providing a direct, non-invasive measure of large-scale neural activity with high temporal resolution and scalability across populations.1 Across the lifespan, EEG activity undergoes continuous and systematic changes reflecting maturation, stabilization, and decline of neural systems.2 However the nature and extent of these changes remain incompletely characterized. Prior EEG studies of aging have been constrained by limited sample sizes, narrow age ranges, or a focus on a small number of canonical biomarkers.1 These limitations have contributed to fragmented and sometimes inconsistent findings, hindering a comprehensive understanding of how electrophysiological properties of the brain evolve across the full lifespan.

Here, we address this gap by leveraging a large multisite resting-state EEG dataset comprising more than 1,800 healthy individuals aged 5 to 85 years, spanning nearly the entire human lifespan. We extracted an unprecedented set of quantitative EEG features yielding more than 2,000 feature–channel combinations per participant. This combination of sample size, lifespan coverage, and feature richness enables a comprehensive and unbiased characterization of age-related electrophysiological patterns. By systematically quantifying feature-specific and region-specific associations with age, we establish a large-scale reference framework for age-related EEG changes.

Methods

This study aims to examine how age shapes and modulates the brain’s electrophysiological activity by examining EEG-derived features across the human lifespan in a large cohort of healthy subjects.  The dataset comprises multi-site, cross-sectional resting-state EEG recordings acquired under eyes-closed conditions from more than 1,800 healthy subjects (54.6% female), aged between 5 and 85 years. The EEG data were aggregated from multiple sources, including publicly available datasets and datasets shared through established research collaborations. All contributing studies obtained approval from their respective local ethics committees or institutional review boards which are reported in the original publications associated with each dataset. To our knowledge, this represents one of the largest EEG datasets to characterize age-related patterns of brain electrical activity across the lifespan. For each participant, an extensive feature set of 103 quantitative measures was extracted, covering spectral, complexity and time domain signal characteristics. Features were computed for each of 19 scalp channels, along with an additional averaged-channel measure.  Associations between age and each feature-channel combination were quantified using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient ρ (rho). Statistical significance was assessed at a threshold of p <0.01. Features revealing substantial effect sizes were then examined for their spatial distribution across the scalp to identify the brain regions exhibiting the strongest age-related electrophysiological changes. 

Results

Analysis of EEG features in this large cohort revealed widespread significant associations between electrophysiological markers and age. Complexity-related features exhibited the strongest sensitivity to aging, with the highest number of  features displaying significant age-related correlations (|rho|>0.3, p<0.01). This was followed by the spectral category where 11 features were highly associated with age, whereas time-domain features  showed the lowest sensitivity to age-related effects (Fig. 1a). Spatially, age-sensitive features were broadly distributed across the frontal, temporal and central regions, rather than being confined to a specific focal zone or region (Fig. 1b). To evaluate spatial consistency, Fig. 1c maps the Spearman correlation values for all significant base features against electrode location. The heatmap demonstrates heterogeneous patterns across feature domains. While spectral and time-domain features display variable sensitivity across scalp locations and low cluster density, complexity measures show widespread stability and clear dominance, maintaining high correlation magnitudes across the majority of electrodes (Fig. 1c). Looking further at the spectral feature category, the relative beta power in the central region specifically at the central electrode C3, demonstrated a significant increase with age (rho=0.469, p<0.001) (Fig. 1d). Whereas for complexity category, Detrended Fluctuation Analysis (DFA) coefficient at temporal electrode T5, generally declined with age reflecting the negative correlation (rho=-0.359, p<0.001) (Fig. 1e). Furthermore, topographic representations demonstrated that age-related effects were most pronounced in temporal and central regions, where both relative beta power and DFA coefficient showed consistently elevated correlations with age across the scalp (|rho|>0.3, p<0.01) (Fig. 1f). Moreover, analysis of biological sex revealed significant group differences across the features listed below (Mann-Whitney U, p<0.001), indicating that during aging males and females exhibit distinct baseline characteristics despite similar aging trends (Fig. 1 d, e).

Conclusion

Our analysis demonstrates that the aging brain undergoes a pervasive electrophysiological reorganization that is most robustly characterized by changes in signal complexity. While traditional spectral markers—specifically the age-related increase in central beta power—provided valuable regional insights, complexity features such as Detrended Fluctuation Analysis (DFA) emerged as a sensitive indicator of age-related changes, exhibiting widespread spatial stability and a systematic decline across the lifespan. This dominance of complexity metrics suggests that healthy aging is accompanied by a reduction in the irregularity and information content of neural signals. 

Finally, by combining large sample size, wide age coverage, and high-dimensional feature extraction, this work provides a robust large-scale characterization of age-related variation in human brain electrophysiology. While rank-based association analyses offer a useful first-order description of age–feature relationships, an important next step will be to adopt more flexible modeling approaches, such as non-linear and mixed-effects models, to better capture age-dependent dynamics, inter-individual variability, and site-related effects. Such approaches will be essential for disentangling the complex relationship between age and brain electrophysiological features and for advancing our understanding of lifespan brain dynamics.
