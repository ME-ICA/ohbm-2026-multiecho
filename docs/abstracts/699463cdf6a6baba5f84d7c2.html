Introduction

The ability to imitate others’ actions relies on the capacity to perceive and understand observed behavior, including its motor goal and kinematic properties. This ability is supported by the Mirror Neuron System (MNS), a fronto-parietal network which maps the visual description of an observed motor act onto one’s own motor representation. The MNS is also known to be involved in imitation learning and  the formation of new motor memories, such as after Action Observation Training (AOT).^1 Neuroimaging research demonstrated that this mechanism is robustly engaged during the observation of biological motion, regardless of the level of visual detail provided by the stimulus. In particular, recent fMRI studies showed that observing actions presented by means of Point-Light Displays (PLDs) technique evoke activation of the MNS comparable to that recruited during the observation of actions in Full Vision version (FV).^2 PLDs facilitate the investigation of kinematic properties of biological motion in the absence of pictorial or contextual information, allowing the extraction of dynamic features of observed actions.^3 Behavioral evidence further indicates that PLDs motion cues are sufficient to recognize relevant attributes of the agent (e.g., her/his emotional state). On this ground, the present 3T fMRI study aims to evaluate the efficacy of a long-term imitation-based AOT, using either FV or PLD version, on the improvement of motor performance and on the neural modulation over time.  

Methods

Thirty-two healthy right-handed participants (21 females; mean age= 24.4 ± 4.1 years) were randomly assigned to two sex-and-age matched groups based on the type of hand model they observed during the training phase (N=16 in each group). All participants underwent two fMRI sessions (T0 and T1) separated by one week. During this interval, they completed at home an AOT protocol based on observation and imitation of manipulative hand actions presented either in FV or in PLD format (Fig. 1).  

fMRI sessions. During each fMRI session, participants were instructed to reach and screw a wooden MR-compatible manipulandum with the right hand, performing clockwise or counterclockwise rotations. As control condition, participants performed grasping actions in separated trials. In these trials (Grasp) they were required only to grasp the manipulandum, without rotating it. The device included a white marker, which rotated in real time following the participant’s movements. Behavioral data were recorded using a digital MR-compatible in-bore camera. Videos acquired during the scanner tasks were analysed using Tracker software (version 6.3.3) ^4 to extract behavioral variables of interest, allowing for subsequent analyses of movement parameters. This behavioral measure will be entered as regressor in subsequent fMRI analyses, with the aim of identifying brain regions whose activation covaries with movement performance and to better understand the neural correlates of training-related motor improvements. The entire data analysis was performed with SPM25. Data were analysed using standard GLM univariate analyses to investigate specific effects in brain activation. Preliminary fMRI analyses were conducted using a flexible factorial design separately for each group, modeling the conditions of interest (Clockwise, Counterclockwise, Grasp) at T0 and T1. Long-term Action Observation Training. At the end of the T0 session, each participant received the materials needed to perform the at-home AOT, including the videos used for the observation phase and the apparatus for the manipulation task. FV and PLD videos were created with male and female actors, from a first-person perspective, and assigned to participants based on their gender. The AOT protocol began on the day of the T0 session and lasted seven consecutive days (approximately one hour per day). The apparatus consisted of a board with 12 pegs arranged in a 3x4 matrix. Participants were required to screw three different types of caps as fast as possible using their right hand, following a predefined daily sequence. Each day, both before and after the AOT session, participants were instructed to screw all 12 caps once without watching the action videos (as baseline and follow-up). These trials were video-recorded each day by the same participant using a camera. From these videos, daily pre- and post-training execution times were calculated. The FV group performed an observation/imitation training based on FV version of the manipulative sequence, while the PLD group performed the same training, but observing the PLDs actions. A 2x2x7 repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted with Time (pre- vs post-training), Group (FV vs PLD), and Day (7 training days) as factors, in order to examine group differences in behavioral performance across pre- and post-training phases, over the 7 days of AOT. 

Results

Overall, fronto-parietal and motor cerebellar lobules activation was observed in both the FV and PLD groups in the experimental conditions compared with Rest at T0 and T1. In the FV group, the Clockwise condition showed greater activation at T1 compared with T0 in the right prefrontal cortex, primary motor cortex, and hippocampus. In the Counterclockwise condition, increased activation was observed only in the right hippocampus. In the PLD group, both experimental conditions showed increased activation at T1 in the right hippocampus, caudate nucleus, and cerebellar lobule VI. In the control condition, no significant activation differences over time were observed in either group. Regarding the behavioral analysis of the AOT videos over the week, the results revealed a significant main effect of Time (p<0.001), indicating that participants were faster in the post-training condition compared to pre-training across all days. A significant main effect of Day was also observed (p<0.001), showing a progressive reduction in execution time over the course of the week. 

Conclusion

The fMRI findings suggest that the FV group shows a time-dependent change in brain activation primarily involving cortical regions in the right hemisphere, only for the Clockwise condition. This pattern likely indicates cognitive involvement in the learning process, specifically related to the trained manipulative actions. In contrast, the PLD group shows time-related effects in both conditions, but restricted to subcortical and cerebellar structures, likely reflecting motor-related mechanisms of the learning process, that generalize to both the trained and the untrained action, i.e., counterclockwise rotations. Among the subcortical structures, the right hippocampus shows increased activation after training in the whole sample, across both experimental conditions. This suggests that the training, regardless of the version, modulates motor memory and learning processes typically associated with long-term practice. Finally, the control condition does not show activation differences over time, indicating that learning effects emerged specifically for manipulative actions and did not extend to other behaviors such as grasping actions. Regarding the kinematic analysis of the AOT videos, results indicate that both groups showed a clear improvement in motor performance over the course of the week, as expected. Participants became faster in completing the task both within each day (pre- vs. post-training) and across days, reflecting a progressive enhancement of execution speed over the 7-day training period. These findings indicate that long-term AOT effectively promotes motor learning regardless of stimulus type, suggesting that MNS activation facilitates sensorimotor learning even when actions are conveyed through purely kinematic information (i.e., PLD). Although functional outcomes were comparable between FV and PLD stimuli, these effects may rely on partially distinct neural mechanisms. AOT-based on FV actions may preferentially engage cortical and cerebellar motor regions, whereas PLD-based training may involve a greater recruitment of subcortical structures, such as the basal ganglia and thalamus, supporting action automatization and generalization. These results highlight the potential of kinematics-based training approaches, particularly in neurorehabilitation, where simplified stimuli may facilitate motor learning in patients with neurological deficits.^2
