Introduction

Misinformation increasingly circulates via long-form video on social media and news platforms, yet experimental research on misinformation often relies on corrections for text-based or simplified stimuli, limiting ecological validity. Behavioural studies show that pre-emptive measures, prebunking, inoculation, warnings or corrective framings, presented before misleading content, can significantly reduce susceptibility to misinformation1-2. However, these studies rarely examine how such warnings influence neural processing when people consume realistic video content.

Naturalistic neuroimaging methods offer a promising route to bridge this gap. In particular, Inter-Subject Correlation (ISC) allows assessing how similarly individuals’ brains respond over time when watching the same dynamic stimulus. Prior EEG and MEG work has shown that ISC reliably indexes attentional engagement, narrative processing, and shared sensory-cognitive processing across viewers during naturalistic content3-4. Importantly, ISC strength varies with stimulus content: engaging narrative videos evoke higher ISC than non-narrative or low-engagement stimuli.

At the same time, despite a vast literature on misinformation, no known study has combined ISC with a misinformation or pre-exposure warning paradigm using real-world video content. In the present study, we aim to fill this gap by combining pre-exposure discrediting text (warning) vs. neutral text, a real political news video, and MEG-based ISC. We hypothesise that participants receiving a warning will show reduced ISC during the news video, reflecting diminished shared neural engagement relative to control participants. We also expected converging behavioural differences, such as reduced engagement-related judgements, consistent with the idea that warnings shape both neural and evaluative responses.

Methods

Participants
MEG data from 56 participants (34 women; Mage = 21.73, SDage = 2.67), all right-handed, with normal vision and no neurological disorders, were included in the analysis. All participants provided written informal consent. Before the experiment, each participant was randomly assigned to Group 1 (18 women; mean age = 21.85, SD = 2.85) or Group 2 (16 women; Mage = 21.62, SDage = 2.08).

Stimuli and procedure
The experiment was programmed in NBS Presentation. Four videos (duration = 356 ± 44 s) were presented in random order with 120-s breaks (Methods Figure). To interpret ISC results meaningfully and avoid confounding narrative-driven synchronisation, the design included (a) a target BBC news video; (b) a baseline sea video (SEA) providing a low-engagement reference; and (c) two control videos (“Bang, You’re Dead!” (BYD) and “The Age of Pioneers” (AP)) that differed in content and emotional tone but contained no manipulative narrative elements. These controls helped determine whether ISC differences in the BBC condition reflected the manipulation rather than genre or emotional intensity.

Before each video, participants read a printed text, except for BYD, to control text effects. Texts were identical across groups except for the text before the BBC video: Group 1 received a warning from an external media source stating that the upcoming video was fabricated, whereas Group 2 read a neutral Wikipedia-based description. Participants were tested in an electromagnetically shielded MEG room. After the session, they completed a questionnaire assessing interest, motivation, perceived influence of each text, and whether the BBC video seemed fabricated (1–10 scales).

Data collection and preprocessing
MEG recordings were obtained with a 306-sensor Elekta Neuromag system (204 planar gradiometers, 102 magnetometers). EOG channels were used to remove eye-movement artefacts during preprocessing. The sampling rate was 1000 Hz. Videos were presented via a projector (1.5 m distance) outside the MEG cabin. Anatomical landmarks were digitised using a Polhemus tracker. Preprocessing was performed in MaxFilter with default parameters; a 50/100 Hz notch filter and a 0.3 Hz high-pass filter were applied. The data were then downsampled to 250 Hz. All 204 gradiometers were used for ISC computation.

Data analysis
ISC of neural responses during the videos was computed using correlated components analysis3-4 (CorrCA). Following previous work, ISC was quantified as the summed correlation of the three strongest components to capture global neural synchrony regardless of anatomical origin4. Forward models were computed to visualise component topographies. ISC was calculated using a leave-one-out procedure (LOO), yielding one ISC value per participant4. To capture temporal dynamics, ISC was computed in sliding 5-s windows with 4-s overlap. CorrCA spatial filters were also applied to each participant’s preprocessed signal to obtain subject-wise component time series. For every video, pairwise correlations were computed within sliding windows and averaged across subject pairs to derive group-level ISC time courses. Group differences over time were assessed using a cluster-based permutation test (Nperm = 1000, pthresh < 0.05).

We additionally computed time-resolved mean correlation coefficients across participant pairs and applied the Wilcoxon signed-rank test to assess whether group differences for each video could arise from a zero-median distribution3. After deriving LOO ISC values, we examined their correlation with Interest, Motiavtion, and Influence. Given the mixed design and non-normality, we used a Welch-James ADF test with LOO ISC as the dependent variable, Group and Video as interacting fixed factors, and participant-specific random effects. The same test was applied to Interest, Motivation, and Influence. For the BBC condition specifically, these parameters and Fabrication were analysed using the Wilcoxon test.

Results

ISC
We estimated the three most correlated CorrCA components. For the BBC video (Results Figure, panel B) and narrative control videos, the first and the second components were spatially located at the temporal sites, while the third was located at the temporal site due to participants watching and listening to the narrative.

For each video, group differences in ISC were first evaluated using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests applied to the top three CorrCA components. For the BBC video, all three components showed significant group differences, with the first component having higher ISC in the warned group and the other components having higher ISC in the non-warned group (Component 1: W = 16674, p = .006; Component 2: W = 12654, p < .001; Component 3: W = 16036, p = .001), indicating a robust effect of the warning manipulation on neural synchrony (Results Figure, panel D). Narrative control videos showed slightly different results. For AP, only one component differed significantly between groups (Component 1: W = 11253, p < .001). For BYD, at most two components reached significance (Component 1: W = 29658, p = .038; Component 2: W = 16189, p < .001). No differences were found for the baseline Sea video (p > .05).

Time-resolved ISC traces were compared between groups using cluster-based permutation testing. For the BBC video, six significant clusters were identified across two of the three components (all ps < .05), indicating temporally sustained synchronisation differences induced by the warning manipulation (Results figure, panel A). Across narrative videos, four significant clusters in each video were found spanning all three components (all ps < .05), but these clusters were temporally shorter compared to the BBC clusters. No clusters were observed for the Sea video (cluster-level p > .05).

LOO-ISC visually differed between videos, with the Sea video having the lowest ISC values in both groups and the narrative videos having higher ISC (Results Figure, panel C). Specifically, the AP video showed the highest ISC in both groups, while the BBC video showed lower values. The Welch-James ADF mixed model for the LOO-ISC showed no Group effect (WJ = 3.167, p = .081), indicating no overall shift in ISC magnitude between groups when aggregating components. However, the effect of the Video was found (WJ = 264.668, p < .001), with all videos differing from each other in ISC strength (post-hoc tests: all ps < .001). The interaction term was also found to be insignificant (WJ = 1.752, p = .170, post-hoc tests: all ps > .05). Thus, while ISC varied strongly across videos, the overall magnitude did not differ between groups.

Behavioural measures
Self-reported Interest, Motivation, and Influence were analysed with the same Welch ADF model. Interest and Motivation showed significant main effects of Video (Interest: WJ = 75.219, p < .01; Motivation: WJ = 78.010, p < .01; post-hoc tests: all p < .05), with both being higher for narrative control videos (Results Figure, plane E), but no Group or interaction effects (all ps > .05). Significant effects of Group, Video, and their interaction were observed for the Influence (Group: WJ = 14.00, p < .01; Video: WJ = 15.32, p < .01; Interaction: WJ = 16.59, p < .01). Post-hoc tests indicated that the warning significantly influenced the perception of the BBC video compared to non-warning texts. Fabrication levels, in turn, were higher in the warned Group 1, though the difference did not reach significance (W = 475.5, p = .15).

Spearman’s correlations between the LOO-ISC and behavioural measures were first computed across all videos, then per video. Across all videos, higher LOO-ISC was associated with greater Interest (r = .525, p < .001) and Motivation (r = .457, p < .001), while Influence showed no significant correlation with ISC (r = -.057, p = .395). Within individual videos, no correlations survived statistical significance (all ps > .05), likely reflecting reduced power per video.

Conclusion

Across complementary ISC analyses, we found converging evidence that warnings about misleading content selectively modulate neural synchronisation during real-world narrative processing, though the strength and direction of these effects depend on the analytic approach and the stimulus. As expected, the baseline video produced the weakest ISC across all analyses, validating that neural synchrony was driven by narrative engagement and not by general group differences. Statistically significant group differences also emerged in the narrative control videos; however, these were limited to one or two components and involved shorter temporal clusters, suggesting subtle pre-existing variability rather than effects of the warning manipulation.

In contrast, the BBC video showed the most coherent and temporally extensive ISC differences, with significant effects across all three components and temporal clusters. These effects indicate that the warning specifically altered the neural processing of the targeted narrative. Leave-one-out ISC (aggregated across components) showed no overall group effect, suggesting that warnings modulate synchrony in a component- and time-specific manner, rather than inducing a uniform decrease in global neural alignment. Behavioural outcomes mirrored this pattern: Interest and Motivation varied by video but not by group, while Influence results showed that the warning shifted subjective appraisal of the BBC content. Correlations between LOO-ISC and engagement measures further confirmed that stronger synchrony reflects higher engagement across stimuli.

Our hypothesis that warning would reduce ISC during the BBC video was partially supported. While component-wise ISC differences were robust, they were not uniformly lower in the warned group. Instead, the warning shaped the pattern and temporal profile of neural synchronisation rather than producing a simple downregulation of engagement. Nevertheless, the BBC video showed the clearest divergence between groups, indicating that preemptive warnings can selectively modulate collective neural processing of potentially misleading media.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, statistical power was limited, particularly for per-video behavioural correlations and higher-order ISC interactions; this likely contributed to mixed results across analytic levels. Second, we did not measure participants’ attitudes toward the BBC or the warning media source, which can modulate both behavioural and ISC outcomes. Third, while our control stimuli offered essential comparison points, the design remains constrained: differences in emotional tone, pacing, and narrative complexity across videos could differentially influence ISC and may interact with the warning manipulation in ways not fully captured by our current approach. Finally, ISC was analysed primarily at the sensor level; this approach is highly sensitive to temporal alignment but remains spatially coarse.

Our future work on this research will leverage the spatial resolution of MEG to examine source-level ISC and oscillatory synchronisation, allowing us to identify which brain regions and frequency bands drive shared processing of contested or misleading information. Oscillatory ISC (e.g., theta synchrony for narrative integration, alpha suppression for attention, beta synchrony for top-down control) may reveal cognitive mechanisms underlying warning-induced differences that are not apparent in broadband ISC. Combining source-level ISC with measures of individual media attitudes and trustworthiness assessments will clarify how pre-existing beliefs shape collective neural processing.
