Introduction

It is crucial to understand the dynamic development of white matter fiber bundles in infancy. However, comprehensive longitudinal tractography atlases remain lacking. Based on high-quality diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) and T1-weighted imaging (T1WI) data from the Chinese Brain Child Project (CBCP)[1,2], we established an infant-specific white matter atlas construction framework that integrates population-level streamline clustering, probabilistic White Matter Query Language (WMQL) annotation, and manual refinement. The resulting atlas provides anatomically coherent white matter bundles across nine developmental time points, offering a foundational resource for both developmental neuroscience and clinical studies of early white matter maturation.

Methods

For each target age A in {0, 3, 6, 9, 12, 24, 36, 48, 60} months, data from NA subjects (dozens to hundreds) are processed through parallel anatomical and diffusion processing streams.

For each subject i, T1WI data DiT1 undergo FreeSurfer[3] reconstruction to generate detailed white matter parcellation Piorig in native space, which is then normalized to the UNC template space through symmetric diffeomorphic registration:

Pi = ΦiT1(Piorig)

where ΦiT1 represents the deformation field to the age-matched UNC T1WI template[4].

Concurrently, DWI data DiDWI are used for whole-brain tractography, yielding streamlines Fiorig in native space. The b = 0 image is registered to the UNC T2WI template, producing deformation field ΦiDWI that transforms streamlines to template space:

Fi = ΦiDWI(Fiorig).

For each age A, normalized streamlines from all NA subjects are downsampled, aggregated and clustered via spectral clustering, yielding KA population-level clusters {Ck}k=1KA, which form the template cluster atlas[5].

This template is then used to cluster each individual subject's streamlines. For each resulting individual-level cluster, WMQL is applied incorporating Pi to obtain a candidate tract label.

Finally, each population-level cluster Ck in the template is annotated via majority voting over the labels assigned to its counterpart clusters across all subjects:

Label(Ck) = argmaxt∈TA Count({i | tk(i) = t }),

where TA is the set of identifiable tracts at age A.

Finally, clusters sharing the same tract label are aggregated. After careful manual refinement, the final atlas TA for age A is obtained. The whole pipeline is shown in Figure 1.

Results

Datasets:  DWI and T1WI data from the CBCP were collected at 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, 24, 36, 48, and 60 months, including 385 subjects in total (91, 23, 24, 23, 34, 20, 25, 59, and 86 at each age, respectively). The UNC-BCP atlas provided age-specific T1WI and T2WI templates for spatial normalization.

Experimental Results: The rigorous atlas construction pipeline, integrating registration, clustering, and labeling techniques, successfully generated a longitudinal atlas encompassing the major white matter fiber bundles across the nine defined developmental time points. Visual inspection of the resulting age-specific atlases confirmed high anatomical plausibility and remarkable coherence in tract morphology and spatial organization throughout the entire 0-to-60-month span. Using the Automated Fiber Quantification (AFQ) framework, we were able to model the developmental trajectories of fractional anisotropy (FA), as illustrated in Figure 2. The results demonstrate a systematic increase in FA values, accurately reflecting the ongoing myelination and maturation process of the white matter microstructure during early infancy.

Conclusion

We have successfully pioneered the construction of the first comprehensive longitudinal atlas detailing the intricate architecture of infant white matter fiber bundles, spanning the critical developmental window from birth up to five years of age (60 months). Our methodological framework is highly robust, integrating advanced techniques including multimodal template registration to ensure precise age-matched spatial normalization, spectral clustering for unbiased, population-level definition of fiber tracts, and a probabilistic White Matter Query Language (WMQL) labeling and refinement approach to achieve high anatomical accuracy. This rigorous process yields a series of anatomically precise and age-specific atlases that capture the rapid and dynamic changes occurring in the developing brain. Crucially, this unique resource now serves two primary functions: first, it enables detailed, quantitative studies of the underlying mechanisms and trajectories of early white matter development; and second, it establishes an essential, normative anatomical reference critical for the early identification and assessment of developmental anomalies in clinical and research applications.
