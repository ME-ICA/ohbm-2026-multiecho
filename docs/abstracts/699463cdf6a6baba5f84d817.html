Introduction

　Attentional blink (AB) refers to the failure to detect a second stimulus (probe) presented shortly after a detected first stimulus (target) in a rapid sequence. Recent research suggests that AB reflects not only limitations in stimulus processing but also attentional shifts, highlighting the importance of capturing attentional states. ^1 Research on auditory AB has primarily employed rapid serial auditory presentation tasks using simple, non-semantic stimuli. Neurophysiological studies of AB have traditionally relied on event-related potentials (ERPs), particularly the P3b component, which is associated with stimulus evaluation and post-perceptual processing. P3b, a positive component occurring approximately 300–600 ms after stimulus onset, has been shown to decrease in amplitude during AB. ^2 

 Everyday listening requires processing meaningful speech, making it important to study auditory AB with meaningful words to understand attentional states. Previous studies focused on simple stimuli, leaving it unclear if auditory AB occurs with more complex, meaningful words that require greater attention. When all stimuli, including distractors, are meaningful words, ongoing cognitive processing makes it difficult to isolate responses to specific time periods using ERPs. Therefore, this study focused on the alpha suppression elicited by target stimuli. A decrease in electroencephalogram power around 10 Hz (i.e., alpha suppression) is considered to reflect active cognitive processing. ^3 

 Thus, it was assumed that alpha power would be decreased by trials including a target (Target) and trials including a target and a probe (Lag1) compared with trials consisting of only distractor (Non). If alpha power can serve as an index of AB, we hypothesized that 1) alpha power would not differ between the Lag1 condition in which AB occurred and the Target condition, and 2) alpha power would be reduced in the Lag1 condition in which AB did not occur relative to the Target condition.

Methods

 In this experiment, 31 adult participants completed an RSAP task that required them to indicate the presence or absence of both target and probe stimuli using a keyboard. The mean age was 21.81 years (SD =1.96). All participants had no hearing impairments. 

 The auditory stimuli consisted of 15 three-mora words, which were presented at a fixed stimulus onset asynchronicity (SOA) of 170 ms. The experiment consisted of five conditions: target-only (Target), probe-only (Probe), distractor-only (Non), and two lag conditions (Lag1 and Lag5). The lag conditions differed in the number of intervening stimuli between the target and the probe (Fig. 1). The stimulus words were all Japanese nouns—selection controlled for audio word familiarity, imageability, and accent type. The Target stimulus used Japanese chain store names, while the Probe stimulus employed the word of food. Each condition was tested over 128 trials. Each block comprised 80 trials, with eight blocks completed. Breaks were taken between blocks. 

 EEG and electrooculogram (EOG) signals were recorded with the Biosemi Active2 system (2048 Hz) from 32 electrodes according to the 10–20 system, with CMS/DRL placed at AF1/AF2 and earlobes serving as references. All EEG preprocessing was performed using MATLAB R2025a (MathWorks Inc.) and EEGLAB v2025.0.0. The down-sampling rate was 500 Hz, and the bandpass filter was set to 0.05‒30 Hz. Re-referencing was the average of all channels. Target-locked epochs between -170 ms and 1700 ms were divided from each behavioral outcome. Five conditions were defined: Non-condition (both correct responses), Target condition (both correct responses), Probe condition (both correct responses), Lag1 condition where the Target stimulus was detected but the Probe stimulus was not (AB), and Lag1 condition where both Target and Probe stimuli were detected (Not AB). Epochs exceeding 50 μV were excluded. The baseline was set 200 ms prior to the presentation of the target stimulus. 

 For behavioral analysis, the detection rate of the probe stimulus was calculated for each individual in the Probe, Lag1, and Lag5 conditions, and the mean was determined. This represented a conditional correct response rate, considering only trials where the presence or absence of the target stimulus was correctly identified. The three conditions were compared using ANOVA.

 To examine alpha-band activity, single-trial EEG data were decomposed using Morlet wavelets (1-Hz steps), and log-transformed power values were obtained. Alpha power (8–12 Hz) was averaged across 8–12 Hz for each trial and normalized to the mean power during the 170-ms pre-stimulus period. A non-parametric cluster-based permutation test was performed for each stimulus type combination (Target vs. Non / AB vs. Non / Not AB vs. Non / Not AB vs. Target / AB vs. Target) using "ft_timelockstatistics" function of Field Trip ^4 to identify spatiotemporal differences between conditions. Paired t-tests were computed at each time point and electrode, and clusters were defined as samples containing at least two adjacent electrodes and/or two consecutive time points. Cluster-level statistics were calculated as the sum of t -values within each cluster. Permutation testing used 5000 random label exchanges, with paired t -tests and cluster formation repeated for each permutation. The resulting minimum and maximum cluster-level t-value sums were used to generate Monte-Carlo reference distributions. Cluster significance was assessed at α = .025 (two-tailed α = .05), and p-values were doubled to obtain two-tailed estimates. Clusters with doubled p < .05 were considered significant. Significant clustering indicates statistically significant differences in alpha power between the comparison conditions. Positive clustering indicates that one condition has stronger alpha frequencies than the other, while negative clustering indicates that one condition has weaker alpha frequencies than the other.

Results

　The mean detection rate of the probe stimulus was 0.81 (SD = 0.19) in the Probe condition, 0.71 (SD = 0.23) in the Lag1 condition, and 0.84 (SD = 0.12) in the Lag5 condition. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed a significant main effect of condition on probe detection rate (F (2,30) = 12.16, p < .001, η² = 0.08). Post hoc comparisons indicated that the detection rate in the Lag1 condition was significantly lower than in the Probe(t (30) = 3.02, p < .05) and Lag5 conditions(t (30) = 5.08, p < .001).

 Alpha power was compared between conditions using cluster-based permutation tests (Fig. 2). First, alpha power was compared between the Target and Non conditions (Fig. 2A). This comparison revealed a significant negative cluster (cluster-level tsum = −1976.85, p < .01) spanning 682–834 ms after target onset. Comparisons between the Not AB and Non conditions also revealed a significant negative cluster (cluster-level tsum = −10051.69, p < .01, 574–1012 ms). Similarly, comparison between the AB and Non conditions revealed a significant negative cluster (cluster-level tsum = −6279.00, p < .01, 462–866 ms).

　Next, alpha power was compared among conditions in which the probe stimulus was absent (Target), present but not detected (AB), and present and detected (Not AB) (Fig. 2B). Comparison between the Not AB and Target conditions revealed a significant negative cluster (cluster-level tsum = −3342.86, p < .05, 912–1118 ms). In contrast, comparison between the AB and Target conditions yielded no significant clusters, either positive or negative.

Conclusion

 The present study investigated whether auditory attentional blink (AB) occurs when meaningful spoken words are used as stimuli in a rapid serial auditory presentation task and examined neural correlates of attentional state using alpha-band activity. Behavioral results showed that probe detection accuracy was significantly reduced in the Lag-1 condition compared to the Probe and Lag-5 conditions, demonstrating that auditory AB occurs even when all stimuli are semantically meaningful. This finding indicates that the processing of meaningful stimuli places substantial demands on attentional resources, similar to those reported in studies using simple auditory stimuli. To characterize attentional processes associated with probe detection, alpha-band activity was examined as an index of ongoing attentional state. When both target and probe stimuli were presented, alpha modulation differed depending on whether the probe was successfully detected or missed, despite identical physical stimulation. Specifically, successful probe detection was accompanied by a distinct pattern of alpha suppression, whereas missed probes occurred under neural states that closely resembled those observed in target-only trials. This suggests that probe detection during AB was conditioned by task-focused attentional states.

 Notably, alpha-band activity associated with successful detection emerged within a similar latency range for target and probe detection. This temporal consistency suggests that alpha-band activity is not strictly synchronized with stimulus onset, but rather reflects a functional attentional phase associated with conscious report. These findings suggest that attentional shifts during AB unfold over time and are characterized as fluctuations at the level of conscious state rather than discrete events triggered by stimuli.

 Finally, while this study initially aimed to examine the P3b component, the use of meaningful spoken words likely introduced temporal variability in higher-order cognitive processing, such as lexical access and semantic integration. Because the P3b reflects post-perceptual evaluation and working memory updating, variability in the timing of these processes across trials can result in latency jitter, thereby complicating the interpretation of phase-locked ERP components.

 In contrast, induced alpha-band power does not require strict time-locking to stimulus onset and is considered to reflect ongoing attentional state. By focusing on alpha power as a state-level index, the present study was able to capture fluctuations in conscious attentional engagement that were robust to temporal variability in stimulus processing. These findings suggest that time–frequency analysis of alpha activity provides a particularly practical approach for investigating attentional states during auditory AB tasks involving complex, continuously processed stimuli, for which traditional ERP measures may be limited.
