Introduction

Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) drug cue-reactivity (FDCR) paradigms have emerged as a putative biomarker for addiction neuroscience, with more than 480 FDCR studies published based on our systematic review1. However, inconsistent methodological implementation and reporting limit the replicability and reliability of FDCR findings2. While the human neuroimaging domain benefits from a range of well-established standards and checklists, such as the brain imaging data structure (BIDS), they do not capture many FDCR-specific features, limiting their applicability to this domain. To address these challenges, we developed a consensus-based expert-driven methodological checklist specially designed to meet the needs for FDCR3. Yet,  the adherence to these protocols has been suboptimal, in part since these are not integrated with widely endorsed data standards. 

Methods

To propose a feasible framework in which the FDCR checklist appends to the BIDS, enabling researchers to implement the minimum FDCR-specific standards more effectively to increase replicability and large-data harmonization, we systematically mapped the 38 consensus-derived FDCR checklist items onto existing Brain Imaging Data Structure (BIDS) entities and metadata fields, introducing FDCR-specific extensions only when no suitable BIDS element existed. Standardized templates and documentation were developed following BIDS design principles and iteratively refined through expert review. Feasibility was evaluated by organizing and releasing a publicly available FDCR fMRI dataset (n = 60; methamphetamine use disorder) fully compliant with the proposed BIDS-appended framework.

Results

All 38 FDCR checklist items were successfully operationalized within the BIDS framework, requiring only a limited number of FDCR-specific extensions while preserving full BIDS compatibility. The resulting templates enabled complete and standardized reporting of FDCR paradigms across task design, stimulus characteristics, cue presentation, and analysis parameters. The released dataset (n = 60) fully validated the framework’s feasibility, demonstrating seamless integration with existing BIDS-compliant preprocessing and analysis pipelines and supporting scalable data sharing and cross-study harmonization.

Conclusion

Here, we present a practical manual along with the required templates, encompassing all 38 consensus-derived FDCR items. These documents maintain the same structure and logic of BIDS and are intended to be appended to this standard when researchers are conducting and reporting FDCR studies. Furthermore, we provide the first publicly available FDCR dataset, including 60 subjects with methamphetamine use disorder, organized according to this framework. Together, these contributions aim to establish a unified, scalable, and standards-aligned foundation not only for FDCR studies but also as an example for other neuroimaging fields, ultimately enhancing data harmonization and replicability of results.
