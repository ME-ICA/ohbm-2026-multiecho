Introduction

Quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM) provides in-vivo estimates of tissue magnetic susceptibility and reflects biophysical contributions from iron, myelin, and related microstructural sources, making it a valuable marker of aging-related brain changes.¹ While cross-sectional studies have reported susceptibility differences across age groups, the longitudinal behavior of magnetic susceptibility in community-dwelling older adults remains insufficiently understood.² Characterizing both trajectory-level changes and distributional shifts in whole-brain susceptibility is essential for understanding aging-related homeostasis processes of relevance to cognitive decline and neurodegeneration.

This study investigates longitudinal changes in whole-brain magnetic susceptibility in older adults using repeated MRI acquisitions over multiple years. We examined individual trajectories of whole-brain susceptibility as a function of age, and quantified changes in voxel-wise susceptibility distributions across visits, including metrics such as peak amplitude, dispersion, FWHM, kurtosis, entropy, and central AUC. Together, these analyses provide a detailed characterization of how whole-brain magnetic susceptibility evolves during aging.

Methods

A total of 255 community-dwelling older adults (764 total scans; 2–4 visits per subject; mean inter-scan interval ≈ 1.5 years) were included from the Rush Memory and Aging Project, Minority Aging Research Study,³ Religious Orders Study,⁴ and Latino/Clinical Core cohorts. All participants underwent 3T multi-echo 3D GRE (0.7×0.7×1.3 mm³; 5 echoes; TE₁ = 4.6 ms; ΔTE = 4.5 ms) and T1-weighted MPRAGE imaging (1 mm isotropic). Magnetic susceptibility maps for each of the visits were reconstructed using Morphology Enabled Dipole Inversion (MEDI).⁵

Whole-brain masks were applied on the magnetic susceptibility maps to find the median susceptibility values for each scan, then adjusted for sex and scanner information. For each participant, all scan-level observations—each depicting the adjusted whole-brain susceptibility at the corresponding age at scan—were plotted, and points belonging to the same individual were connected to form longitudinal susceptibility trajectories. A global linear regression model was performed on all observations. For distributional analyses, voxel-wise susceptibility values from all participants were pooled separately for the first, second, and subsequent scans, and histograms were generated for each visit. From these pooled distributions, summary metrics such as mean, standard deviation, FWHM, entropy, kurtosis, peak amplitude, and central AUC (–0.05 to 0.05 ppm) were computed to quantify changes across visits. Changes in these metrics across visits were used to assess progressive flattening and dispersion of susceptibility distributions.

Results

Regression of whole-brain susceptibility against age at scan showed a positive slope of 0.000157 (p < 0.05), adjusted for sex and scanner. Visit-wise susceptibility distribution of all the voxels showed progressive lowered peak amplitude, higher dispersion (including increased standard deviation and FWHM), higher entropy, lower kurtosis, and reduced area under the curve for susceptibility between –0.05 and 0.05, compared to previous visits.

Conclusion

This longitudinal study demonstrates that whole-brain magnetic susceptibility in community-dwelling older adults follows a systematic temporal pattern, with regression analyses revealing a positive age-related slope and histogram metrics indicating progressively broader, flatter susceptibility distributions across visits. The combination of increasing magnetic susceptibility and reduced central peak with greater dispersion is consistent with aging-related processes such as gradual iron accumulation and/or myelin loss in brain tissue. These longitudinal patterns suggest that QSM may serve as a useful predictive tool for identifying and tracking diffuse microstructural changes that emerge during late-life aging.
