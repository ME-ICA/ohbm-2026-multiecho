Introduction

Alpha-synucleinopathies, including Parkinson’s disease and dementia with Lewy bodies, are among the most prevalent neurodegenerative disorders worldwide, with incidence expected to rise substantially due to population aging.1 These conditions are characterized by the misfolding, aggregation, and progressive propagation of alpha-synuclein, leading to widespread network dysfunction and prominent cognitive deficits that often precede overt motor symptoms. Understanding how pathological alpha-synuclein spreading impacts large-scale brain networks and cognition remains a major challenge for translational neuroscience.

Animal models have provided key insights into alpha-synuclein biology, yet most studies rely on rodents, whose brain organization and cognitive repertoire differ markedly from those of humans. Non-human primate models offer a closer approximation of human brain structure, functional networks, and executive functions, but their widespread use is limited by cost, ethical considerations, and long disease timelines.

The common marmoset (Callithrix jacchus) represents a valuable compromise. As a small-bodied primate with a lissencephalic cortex and human-like large-scale network organization, the marmoset is well suited for advanced neuroimaging and cognitive testing.2 Importantly, its smaller brain size relative to macaques or baboons is expected to facilitate faster brain-wide propagation of pathological proteins, enabling shorter longitudinal investigations and earlier emergence of measurable symptoms.

Here, we aimed to develop and longitudinally characterize a marmoset model of alpha-synucleinopathy induced by intracerebral injection of pre-formed fibrils of marmoset alpha-synuclein. By combining multimodal MRI, cognitive assessment, actimetry, and histological analyses over one year, we sought to capture the functional, behavioral, and pathological consequences of alpha-synuclein spreading across time.

Methods

Twelve adult common marmosets were included in the study. Six animals (1 female) received a unilateral injection of pre-formed fibrils (PFF) of marmoset alpha-synuclein (50 μL) into the left striatum, while six control animals (1 female) received an equivalent injection of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Animals were assessed longitudinally at multiple timepoints over a period of up to one-year post-injection. All procedures followed Canadian Council on Animal Care guidelines and were approved by the University of Western Ontario Animal Care Committee, and we have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.

Multimodal MRI was used to test the hypothesis that pathological alpha-synuclein spreading would lead to progressive alterations in large-scale brain organization. Functional MRI was acquired to assess changes in functional connectivity and network integrity using independent component analysis, seed-based connectivity, fractional amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (fALFF), and regional homogeneity. Structural MRI was performed to investigate whether neurodegeneration or regional volume loss could be detected in PFF-injected animals as pathology developed.

Cognitive function was evaluated using a touchscreen-based pairwise visual discrimination task. Animals were required to learn an association between one of two visual stimuli and a reward; following successful acquisition, reward contingencies were reversed to assess cognitive flexibility and the ability to update established stimulus–reward associations.

Sleep patterns and diurnal motor activity were continuously monitored using wearable actimeters (Camntech Nanowatches), motivated by the early emergence of sleep disturbances and the later development of motor symptoms in alpha-synucleinopathies such as Parkinson’s disease.

At the end of the study, brains were processed using immunofluorescence and light-sheet microscopy to assess the presence and spatial distribution of alpha-synuclein aggregates and to characterize their spread relative to the injection site.

Given the limited sample size, the study was not powered for reliable sex-based statistical analyses; however, results were systematically examined separately for males and females to assess potential sex-related trends.

Results

Histological analyses provided clear evidence of alpha-synuclein propagation in PFF-injected marmosets. Classical immunofluorescence revealed bilateral spreading of pathological alpha-synuclein, extending both rostrally and caudally relative to the striatal injection site. Accumulation of aggregates was observed in multiple cortical and subcortical regions, including frontal areas. These findings are consistent with previous observations of alpha-synuclein propagation in the marmoset brain,3 while extending the observation window up to 15 months post-injection.

In addition, for the first time to our knowledge, an entire marmoset brain was optically cleared and imaged using light-sheet microscopy following PFF injection. This methodological advance enables whole-brain, three-dimensional visualization of alpha-synuclein pathology at cellular resolution. Importantly, light-sheet data can be registered to the marmoset MRI template, allowing direct spatial correspondence between ex vivo histological findings and in vivo neuroimaging measures. This approach opens new avenues for integrative analyses of pathological spreading and network-level functional alterations.

Cognitive assessment revealed a selective and progressive impairment in PFF-injected animals. Beginning approximately four months post-injection, PFF-treated marmosets showed deficits in the pairwise visual discrimination task. While acquisition of the initial stimulus–reward association did not differ between PFF and PBS groups, PFF-injected animals were significantly impaired in reversing the learned contingency. This deficit was reflected in increased trials and time required to reach criterion and persisted throughout the one-year follow-up period.

Functional MRI analyses indicated that this cognitive impairment was associated with altered functional connectivity within frontal brain regions. Reduced connectivity within frontal networks, particularly in the injected hemisphere, emerged around four months post-injection in the PFF group and persisted throughout the following timepoints. These regions are classically implicated in executive functions and cognitive flexibility, supporting a link between alpha-synuclein pathology, frontal network disruption, and impaired reversal learning. Ongoing independent component analyses aim to further characterize potential alterations in large-scale functional networks, including default mode and somatomotor networks.

Structural MRI and volumetric analyses are currently underway to assess whether neurodegeneration or regional volume loss accompanies the observed functional and cognitive changes.

In contrast, analyses of sleep architecture and diurnal motor activity did not reveal significant differences between PFF- and PBS-injected animals across the observation period. PFF-injected marmosets did not exhibit measurable motor impairments or sleep disturbances relative to controls. While sleep and motor symptoms are prominent features of human alpha-synucleinopathies, their absence in the present study may reflect the relatively early disease stage or the need for longer longitudinal follow-up, as commonly reported in non-human primate models of neurodegenerative disease.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that intracerebral PFF injection in the common marmoset induces progressive alpha-synuclein spreading, with bilateral propagation to regions both rostral and caudal to the injection site, as confirmed by immunofluorescence and, for the first time, whole-brain light-sheet imaging. This pathological spread is associated with selective frontal network dysfunction and impaired cognitive flexibility, as revealed by deficits in reversal learning, while acquisition of stimulus–reward associations, motor performance, and sleep architecture remain largely preserved. The multimodal phenotype observed mirrors early non-motor features of human alpha-synucleinopathies, such as Parkinson’s disease and dementia with Lewy bodies.

Taken together, these results support the common marmoset as a valid and translationally relevant model for studying the functional and cognitive consequences of pathological alpha-synuclein propagation. Its small size, primate-like brain structure, and compatibility with advanced imaging and behavioral assessments allow for accelerated and detailed longitudinal investigations. This model provides a unique platform for integrating in vivo imaging, behavioral testing, and ex vivo pathology, and may serve as a robust tool for evaluating network-targeted therapeutic interventions in alpha-synucleinopathies.
