Introduction

Roughly one-third of autistic individuals are minimally speaking (MS-AUT) – they do not primarily communicate using spoken language. [1] For some, this may not reflect limited intellect or language comprehension, but rather a severe disconnect between cognitive and motor abilities. Some MS-AUT individuals who employ alternative forms of communication have described the experience as being “locked in” - trapped in bodies that do not obey their intentions. Yet because language comprehension assessments for adults require either limb-motor (gestured) or oromotor (spoken) responses, the abilities of MS-AUT individuals can be underestimated. Furthermore, neuroimaging data from MS-AUT individuals remains extremely limited, as they struggle to tolerate MRI due to its confined space, loud noise, and requirement for stillness. Recent advances with high-density diffuse optical tomography (HD-DOT), including the development of wearable systems that allow for a wider range of movement during data acquisition, make it possible to address this crucial gap. HD-DOT combines spatial resolution approaching that of fMRI [2] with the comfortable, quiet, and naturalistic setting more suitable for evaluating MS-AUT individuals. Herein, we used HD-DOT to investigate brain function underlying limb-motor and spoken language processing in MS-AUT and neurotypical (NT) adults.

Methods

Gowerlabs wearable LUMO HD-DOT systems measured brain activity in MS-AUT and NT participants. To better accommodate variability in head shape and size, we worked with Gowerlabs to enhance their standard static neoprene cap and designed systems that incorporate a series of Velcro straps between each set of tile arrays allowing for improved degrees of freedom during cap fit. We designed two systems: each accommodating a range of head sizes. A 51-tile system (Fig. 1A) with 153 dual wavelength sources and 204 detectors was used for individuals that had a head circumference (HC) between 50-55 cm, and a 54-tile system (Fig. 1B) with 162 dual wavelength sources and 216 detectors for HC between 55-60 cm. Both systems recorded data at a framerate of 6.67 Hz and shined light at 735 and 835 nm. Data were collected across two sites: Washington University in St. Louis (WUSTL) and the Kennedy Krieger Institute (KKI) in Baltimore, Maryland. Brain responses associated with limb-motor praxis tasks, including performance of repeated finger sequencing and full hand tapping, and semantic language processing tasks, including listening to intact and degraded audio clips of short interviews were collected. We worked closely with our Stakeholder partners to develop a desensitization protocol designed to reduce anxiety, increase comfort, encourage cooperation and improve feasibility while enabling reliable mapping of individual variability in brain function underlying the motor and language tasks. Data were preprocessed and reconstructed using standard NeuroDOT processing pipelines and quantitatively assessed for coupling quality and movement content. General linear modeling (GLM) was used to generate first-level statistics of individual brain maps and Cohen’s-D estimated within- and across- group effects. 

Results

Participants included 15 MS-AUT (Fig. 1C, D) and 24 NT individuals. All participants wore the LUMO system for over an hour including cap placement, optimization, and at least six runs of tasks with breaks in between. Metrics of data quality show that both NT and MS-AUT groups exhibit strong coupling with the cap and do not exhibit movement beyond our previously determined acceptable thresholds for data quality (red lines; Fig. 2A). While NT clearly replicated expected patterns of brain activity in the fMRI literature, the MS-AUT group maps show distinct variability in prefrontal, temporal, and motor regions (Fig. 2B). Notably, while in the MS-AUT group hand tapping motor responses are largely similar to those observed in the NT group, the more complex finger sequencing and semantic language processing tasks exhibit unique patterns relative to the NT group. Strong brain responses from the MS-AUT during motor tasks are distributed across the brain compared to the focal activation in the motor cortex seen in the NT group. In the language task, MS-AUT brain responses are localized to prefrontal regions as opposed to temporal areas more associated with language processing.

Conclusion

This feasibility study for wearable brain imaging in MS-AUT demonstrates that high-quality and reliable functional brain responses can be captured in MS-AUT participants. Additionally, the individual variability observed provides immense opportunity to investigate dimensional brain-behavioral associations of motor and language dysfunction in MS-AUT, providing a foundation for refining interventions addressing the limb and oromotor dyspraxia that severely limits communicative (as well as non-communicative) functioning in some individuals with MS-AUT
