Introduction

A major challenge in psychiatry is the absence of objective, physiology-based measures for disorders that are typically assessed through self-report questionnaires and clinical interviews. EEG offers a practical and accessible avenue for addressing this gap, yet its clinical utility remains limited despite decades of research. Findings across EEG studies are often inconsistent or contradictory, with reproducibility remaining a persistent concern. A key driver of this problem is the widespread use of underpowered study designs, characterized by small sample sizes and unstable effect estimates, which diminishes the ability to detect true effects, increases the risk of misleading results, and ultimately undermines reproducibility1,2.

Methods

This study aims to systematically quantify how sample size influences the stability of EEG-derived group differences and brain–behavior relationships across four common psychiatric conditions, namely Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD), Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD), anxiety, and learning disorders. To this aim, we leveraged a large multisite resting-state EEG dataset comprising ~2,900 participants aged 5–18 years. The EEG data were compiled from multiple sources, including publicly available datasets and datasets obtained through established research collaborations. Ethical approval for all contributing studies was obtained from the respective local ethics committees or institutional review boards, as documented in the original publications. Further details about the dataset are provided in 3,4.

For each subject, we extracted a total of 2060 features (103 feature metrics computed across 19 channels, along with metrics averaged across all channels4). Within each diagnostic group, we performed extensive iterative subsampling across a broad range of sample sizes (n = 10 to several hundred). For each sample size, we repeated the analysis 1,000 times. At every iteration, we performed two complementary analyses: (1) ANCOVAs comparing each clinical group to healthy controls while adjusting for age and sex, and (2) Spearman correlations quantifying associations between EEG features and clinical symptom scores. We then evaluated how effect sizes, statistical significance, and reproducibility evolved as sample size increased.

Results

Small sample sizes produced highly unstable results, with large fluctuations in effect magnitude, direction, and spatial localization. For instance, across subsamples, alpha-power differences shifted unpredictably; some iterations suggested higher alpha in ASD, others lower, and many showed no difference. In contrast, analyses of the full dataset showed significantly higher alpha power in healthy controls. (Fig. 1a,b). Likewise, the electrodes identified as significant varied widely across iterations, producing spatial patterns that bore little resemblance to those derived from the full sample (Fig. 1a). At n = 30, the mean statistical power across features was approximately 10%, and more than 90% of detected effects were inflated by at least a factor of two relative to full–sample estimates (Fig. 1d). Applying a stricter p-value threshold further increased effect-size inflation while reducing statistical power. Across all diagnostic groups and EEG features, the minimum sample size required for 95% of iterations to reproduce significant group differences or correlations (referred to as the sample size threshold; Fig. 1c) far exceeded the typical EEG study sizes of ~20–30 participants per group5. In the ADHD cohort, for instance, the mean sample size threshold reached 808 ± 290 for correlation analyses and 490 ± 46 for case–control comparisons. Adjustment for age and sex further reduced the number of detectable effects, highlighting the sensitivity of EEG analyses to demographic variability. Importantly, these patterns were consistent across both ANCOVA-based group comparisons and within-group EEG–behavior correlations, pointing to a generalizable impact of sample size on the reliability of EEG-derived inferences.

Conclusion

Our results demonstrate that sample size is a primary limiting factor in the reproducibility of psychiatric EEG research. Conventional sample sizes are insufficient, leading to unstable results, inflated effect estimates, and frequent false negatives. Advancing EEG-based biomarker discovery will require substantially larger cohorts and a reassessment of the analytical strategies used in psychiatric EEG studies.
