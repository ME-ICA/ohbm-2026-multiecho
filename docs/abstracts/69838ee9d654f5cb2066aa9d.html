Introduction

Memory relies on two fundamental operations: encoding, which transforms experience into stored representations, and retrieval, which accesses those representations to guide behavior. While converging research highlights functional distinctions between these two processes, a central question on their neural organization remains a matter of debate.

One theoretical account proposes that successful retrieval reinstates the neural representations present during encoding1. Supporting evidence shows reactivation of neural patterns with reinstatement scaling according to retrieval fidelity. A complementary body of work, however, characterizes encoding and retrieval as opposing processing states, supported by distinct hippocampal rhythms, connectivity profiles and neuromodulatory systems that enable reciprocal gating2,3. Consistent with this view, functional MRI studies have reported opposing activity during encoding and retrieval in regions such as the posteromedial cortex1, further indicating that the two processes rely on dissociable brain activity patterns.

Together, these perspectives raise a critical question on the shared and distinct neural representations of encoding and retrieval in the human brain. To address this under ecologically valid conditions, we investigated the neural representations of naturalistic retrieval and encoding using a virtual-reality Object Location Memory task with ultra-high resolution 7T fMRI scanning, and employed multivariate pattern analysis to develop a whole-brain decoder that accurately classified the two memory processes. By linking decoder features to meta-analytic cognitive terms and neurotransmitter distributions, we further characterized the functional distinctions underlying encoding and retrieval in the human brain. Together, these findings offer a comprehensive framework for understanding how the human brain differentiates memory states under naturalistic conditions.

Methods

A total of 56 healthy right-handed participants (age = 24 ± 2.8 years old; 35/21 female/male ratio) completed all behavioral and neuroimaging assessments. During 7T fMRI scanning, participants performed an Object Location Memory (OLM) task, in which they freely navigated a virtual arena to locate six hidden objects. Each trial required participants to retrieve an object’s location while navigating to the estimated position (i.e. retrieval phase), and re-encode the correct location upon feedback (i.e. encoding phase). Control trials involving simple movements were included to account for motion demands. In each of the two task runs, six objects were presented three times in a pre-randomized order, yielding 18 trials per run (Fig. 1a).

An HCP-compliant high-quality neuroimaging data acquisition procedure was set up and optimized. While anatomical measures (T1w/T2w) and resting-state fMRI data were collected using a 3T Prisma MRI scanner, task fMRI data were acquired using 7T Terra MRI scanner (1.6 mm isotropic resolution, TR = 1,000 ms, TE = 22.2 ms). Preprocessing of neuroimaging data was performed via containerized version of the HCP pipelines (Qunex), including ICA+FIX denoising and MSMAll areal-feature-based cortical surface registration4. 

With the aim of identifying a neural signature distinguishing retrieval from encoding, we implemented whole-brain multivariate pattern analysis5 (Fig. 1c). A linear support vector machine was trained on beta estimates for retrieval > control and encoding > control contrasts derived from a general linear model. Prediction performance was evaluated using a rigorous 10x10-fold cross-validation. To enhance interpretability and reliability, the final decoder weights were Haufe-transformed with statistical significance assessed via 10,000 bootstrap iterations (FDRq<0.05). 

For the interpretation of decoder features, we examined (i) meta-analytic associations with cognitive terms using Neurosynth and (ii) spatial correlations with neurotransmitter receptor distributions using Neuromaps. Decoder weights were further projected onto a retrieval-encoding representational space defined by group-level contrast maps, allowing identification of regions showing shared versus process-specific representations.

Results

Behavioral analyses confirmed the engagement of both memory processes during pre-defined phases of naturalistic virtual navigation. Placement accuracy was significantly above chance from the first repetition, indicating successful retrieval even at task onset, while placement error decreased across trials and runs, reflecting ongoing encoding (Fig. 1b). Though no significant differences were observed in the translation of participants during the engagement of both memory processes, there were also distinct movement patterns. Encoding was characterized by more focused trajectories and less stationary behavior, while retrieval showed greater rotational behavior.

At the neural level, despite the strong spatial similarity between beta maps (r = 0.77 ± 0.01), the SVM-based whole-brain decoder robustly distinguished retrieval from encoding (prediction accuracy = 94%, AUC = 0.96), demonstrating separable neural representations (Fig. 2b). Biased towards retrieval, positive decoder weights were primarily located in the lateral prefrontal cortex, superior parietal regions, somatomotor cortex, and anterior hippocampus (across all subfields), largely overlapping with dorsal attention and frontoparietal control networks. Biased towards encoding, the negative weights were concentrated in key default mode regions, including lateral parietal cortex, posterior cingulate cortex, occipital regions, and hippocampal CA1 (Fig. 2a). Projection of these group level results onto the retrieval-encoding representational space revealed that retrieval reinstated only a subset of encoding-related representations, while encoding engaged additional regions not recruit during retrieval.

Meta-analytic decoding linked shared features to memory process (Fig. 2d).  Retrieval-related features were further associated to attention and goal-directed control, whereas encoding-related features were aligned with perceptual functions and memory-related terms like belief and intention. Neurotransmitter mapping further revealed preferential alignment of encoding-related regions with glutamatergic, dopaminergic, and μ-opioid receptor distributions, consistent with their neuromodulatory roles in learning and memory formation. Together, these cognitive and neurotransmitter profiles provide converging evidence for dissociable mechanisms underlying the two memory processes.

Conclusion

Collectively, the results illustrate that retrieval and encoding during naturalistic navigation share substantial representational overlap, yet remain robustly dissociable at the level of whole-brain patterns. Retrieval reinstates only part of the broader encoding-related representations, indicating partial overlap rather than full representational equivalence between memory processes.

While retrieval preferentially engages attention and control-related networks to align memory with behavioral goals, encoding relies more strongly on default mode and perceptual systems to update internal representations of the external environment. Together, these results provide a comprehensive account of how the human brain differentiates encoding and retrieval under ecologically valid, naturalistic conditions, reconciling reinstatement-based and process-based views of memory within a unified representational framework.
