Introduction

 The velocity of neural signal transmission fundamentally constrains brain function, with axonal conduction velocity determined primarily by axon diameter and degree of myelination.1,2 Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) enables non-invasive microstructural measures sensitive to these properties, yet the genetic architecture underlying white matter organization remains incompletely characterized.3–6 

Classical twin studies leverage the contrast between monozygotic (MZ) twins, who share virtually all genetic variation, and dizygotic (DZ) twins, who share approximately 50% of segregating alleles, to decompose phenotypic variance into additive genetic (A), common environmental (C), and unique environmental (E) components.3 Previous twin neuroimaging studies have demonstrated moderate-to-high heritability of fractional anisotropy (FA) and mean diffusivity (MD) in major white matter tracts, with estimates ranging from 0.3 to 0.9 depending on region and methodology.4–8  

Advanced multi-compartment diffusion models and quantitative imaging approaches now permit more specific inference about axon density, fiber dispersion, and myelin content.9,10 Such characteristics of white matter anatomy are more likely to be under genetic selection pressure than functional activity, per se.3,11 Examining the heritability of these biophysically-grounded parameters may reveal distinct genetic influences on the separable components governing conduction velocity—information critical for understanding neurodevelopmental disorders and identifying imaging endophenotypes for genetic association studies.4,12

We applied voxel-wise ACE structural equation modeling to DWI-derived microstructural indices and myelin-sensitive measures in MZ and DZ twin pairs, aiming to characterize the genetic and environmental contributions to the biophysical determinants of axonal conduction velocity throughout the brain.13,14 

Methods

Participants: Multimodal imaging data were obtained from 550 participants collected as part of the baseline MRI scan of the Adolescent Brain and Cognitive Development (ABCD) study (MZ = 165, pairs; DZ = 60 pairs; 110 twin pairs were both boys, 115 were both girls, mean age 10.20 years old ± 0.58 S.D.).15 

Image Acquisition: Multimodal MRI data was obtained on 3T scanners from Siemens, GE, and Philips across 21 sites, including T1-weighted MPRAGE and multi-shell DWI collected at 1.7 mm isotropic resolution, multiband EPI (acceleration factor 3), with 96 gradient directions across four b-values (b = 500, 1000, 2000, and 3000 s/mm²), with 60 gradient directions at the b=3000 s/mm². 

Image Preprocessing: T1-weighted images were processed using FreeSurfer for cortical reconstruction and parcellation.16 DWI data underwent distortion correction, eddy current compensation, and motion correction using FSL's eddy with outlier replacement.17 The white matter fiber orientation distribution (WM-FOD) was calculated via single-shell constrained spherical deconvolution on the outermost b=3000 s/mm2 shell and a cohort specific template constructed from a sex-balanced randomly selected subset (Fig. 1).  

Microstructural Modeling: Fixel-based morphometry fiber density and cross-section (FDC) was used to estimate apparent axonal volume fraction (AVF) by combining fiber density—derived from the integral of white matter fiber orientation distribution (WM-FOD) spherical harmonic lobes—with cross-sectional area modulation computed from the Jacobian determinant during template normalization.18 These metrics were calculated in reference to the cohort specific FOD template (Fig. 1), which demonstrates qualities, such as reduced frontal WM fiber development, known to be representative of early adolescence. The template normalization process thus preserved individual subject variation relative to the template baseline.  

Statistical Analysis: Voxel-wise ACE structural equation models were implemented in OpenMx.13,14 For each imaging measure at each white matter voxel, the phenotypic variance was decomposed into additive genetic (A), common environmental (C), and unique environmental (E) components by modeling MZ and DZ twin covariance structures. Heritability (h² = A/(A+C+E)) was computed voxel-wise. Likewise, the influence of environment (E/((A+C+E)) was also computed.   

Results

Three full voxel-wise maps of genetic heritability, common environmental, and unique environmental are displayed (Fig. 2). Deep WM tracts in the posterior portion of the brain typically had the highest genetic heritability, with h2 values between 0.45-0.6 throughout the occipital and parietal cortices. The inferior longitudinal fasciculus, forceps major, and corticospinal tracts all displayed high h2 values compared to shared or unique environmental components. In contrast, the common environmental components c2 were highest (0.5-0.65) in the frontal lobe and surrounding much of the subcortical gray matter (Fig. 2). The values for unique environmental components e2 were highest in the WM/GM boundary and around the peripheral WM, with comparatively very high values especially in the WM/GM boundary (0.65-1.0). There was also a distribution throughout the cerebellum, with moderate e2 throughout the cerebellum, but higher h2 in the superior and inferior cerebellum related to motor activity, and higher c2 in the cognitive posterior part of the cerebellum.  

Conclusion

We have demonstrated voxel-wise heritability and environmental influences on axonal microstructure, specifically fiber density and cross-section, a well-established measure of intra-axonal volume. FDC is proportional to the diameter and density of axonal fibers, a contributor to axonal function and action potential conduction velocity. These results further support a division between genetic influences on more early developmental, motor, and perceptual processing areas, and environmental influences on the WM/GM boundary, frontal lobes, and subcortex. These results highlight the dual contributions of genetics and environment on brain development and function, particularly how environmental influences are important for maturation of frontal, cognitive, and executive function circuits.
