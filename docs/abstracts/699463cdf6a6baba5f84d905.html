Introduction

The Iowa Gambling Task (IGT) is a widely used paradigm to assess decision-making under uncertainty, requiring reinforcement learning to optimize choices by balancing immediate rewards against potential long-term punishments, which can be disrupted by poor impulse control leading to cognitive biases such as loss-chasing or gambler's fallacy. The ventromedial prefrontal cortex (VMPFC) plays a crucial role in encoding outcome values and integrating negative feedback to guide adaptive behavior,1 but how individual differences in impulse control moderate its impact on computational parameters of reward learning, such as learning rates, remains underexplored. This study employed a modified IGT combined with functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) to investigate VMPFC activation during negative feedback as a predictor of reward learning rate Arew derived from the Outcome-Representation Learning (ORL) model2, moderated by impulse control. We hypothesized that low impulse control would amplify the effects of VMPFC responses on impulsive reward updating, leading to suboptimal performance.

Methods

Thirty-seven healthy adults (16 females, mean age 22.64 ± 2.90 years) completed an online Self-Control Scale (revised Chinese version), focusing on the Impulse Control subscale (SCS-IC).3 In the modified IGT, which differed from the classic version by presenting pseudo-randomly highlighted decks for participants to decide "play" or "pass" (rather than free selection) and showing only net outcome feedback,4 participants aimed to maximize earnings starting with an initial endowment of 2000 units across 120 trials divided into 6 blocks of 20 trials each. The four decks were: B and D as disadvantageous (high immediate rewards of 100 units but frequent or large punishments leading to negative expected values of -25 each); A and C as advantageous (low immediate rewards of 50 units but infrequent or small punishments yielding positive expected values of 17.5 and 25, respectively) (Figure 1). Behavioral data from "play" trials were fitted using the ORL model,2 which separately tracks expected values and outcome frequencies for superior fit in IGT data compared to other reinforcement learning models, implemented via the hBayesDM package for hierarchical Bayesian estimation of Arew. Net scores, reflecting learning, were calculated as [(number of advantageous deck selections / number of advantageous deck presentations) - (number of disadvantageous deck selections / number of disadvantageous deck presentations)].

Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) data were acquired on a 3T Siemens Prisma scanner using a T2*-weighted echo-planar imaging sequence (TR=1000 ms, voxel size=2.5×2.5×2.5 mm³). General linear models (GLMs) were fitted with regressors for the decision phase (advantageous/disadvantageous decks, duration=reaction time) and feedback phase (positive/negative/zero outcomes, duration=1s). Motion parameters and temporal derivatives were included as nuisance regressors. Additionally, separate GLMs were fitted for the full task, block 1, and block 6 to examine temporal dynamics in VMPFC activation during the decision phase for advantageous decks. Behavioral-fMRI integration included Pearson correlations and moderated regression analyses (VMPFC parameter estimates during negative feedback × SCS-IC predicting Arew), with simple slopes probed at ±1SD.

Results

Net scores showed a clear increasing trend across blocks, indicating progressive learning toward advantageous decks. Arew was negatively correlated with final earnings (r=-0.439, p=0.007), suggesting that higher reward sensitivity impairs overall task performance by promoting short-sighted choices. SCS-IC negatively correlated with Arew (r=-0.492, p=0.002), implying better impulse control stabilizes reward updating. 

Complementing these behavioral findings, fMRI analyses validated the paradigm’s efficacy through bilateral nucleus accumbens (NAcc) activation, which exhibited significantly greater parameter estimates (β values) for positive compared to negative feedback (NAcc_L: t=4.657, p<0.001; NAcc_R: t=3.872, p<0.001;), consistent with its established role in reward anticipation and processing of gains over losses.5 Moreover, VMPFC parameter estimates during negative feedback (VMPFC_neg; mean β negative, indicating overall deactivation relative to baseline) predicted Arew, moderated by SCS-IC (interaction t=-3.160, p=0.003). Simple slope analyses revealed a significant positive association at low SCS-IC levels (-1SD: β=0.001, t=2.634, p=0.013), but not at high levels (+1SD: β=-0.00048, t=-1.508, p=0.141; Figure 2). Thus, among participants with low impulse control, less pronounced VMPFC deactivation to negative feedback was linked to elevated Arew, exacerbating reward sensitivity and susceptibility to loss-chasing behaviors; in contrast, high impulse control mitigated this neural influence, fostering more adaptive learning. To further probe VMPFC's temporal involvement in value-guided decision-making, separate analyses showed positive activation (higher β) for advantageous decks during the decision phase in early trials (block 1: β=0.265, p=0.003) but not in later trials (block 6: β=-0.011, p=0.805), consistent with its role in initial value encoding and exploration during goal-directed learning before habituation.1

Conclusion

Impulse control moderates the influence of VMPFC responses to negative feedback on reward learning rates, such that individuals with low impulse control are more vulnerable to the detrimental effects of insufficient VMPFC deactivation to losses, resulting in elevated reward learning rates and impaired decision-making, whereas high impulse control buffers this impact. These findings elucidate the neural mechanisms linking self-control to reinforcement learning under uncertainty, with potential implications for targeted interventions in impulsivity-related disorders such as addiction or pathological gambling.
