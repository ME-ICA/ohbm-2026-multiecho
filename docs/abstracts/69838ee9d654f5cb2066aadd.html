Introduction

Listening comprehension in a second language (L2) involves multiple cognitive processes that play a central role in language acquisition. Decoding spoken input requires the integration of perceived phonetic information with prior linguistic knowledge. Depending on the learner’s proficiency and the automatization of certain processes [1], L2 listening may involve higher or lower cognitive load. From a neurophysiological perspective, listening comprehension engages a distributed cerebral network. First language (L1) listening primarily activates the left temporal-frontal core network, as well as supportive regions in the right hemisphere, the cerebellum and subcortical structures [2]. L2 listening similarly recruits the left perisylvian speech network but additionally relies on a broader set of support system, forming a more widespread language network [3]. Beyond cerebral activations, listening comprehension also elicits measurable responses in autonomic nervous system (ANS) activity, as reflected in electrodermal activity (EDA) and heart rate variability (HRV).

Objectives of the study: This study aims to investigate the neurophysiological mechanisms underlying different processing stages involved in French L1 and Italian L2 listening comprehension in French-speaking learners of two proficiency levels (A2 and B2) according to the Common European Framework of Reference for Languages (CEFR). Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) was used to identify neural networks involved in linguistic processing in both L1 and L2. In particular, the analysis focused, on the one hand, on L1 listening compared to fluent L2 listening and, on the other hand, on obstacles-induced L2 listening compared with fluent L2 listening in both proficiency groups.

Methods

Subjects and ethics: Twenty-one healthy right-handed subjects were recruited at University Grenoble Alpes: twelve had a B2 level and nine a A2 level. All were studying Italian as second language. They were included in a cognitive study about investigation of neurophysiological functioning during listening comprehension task (NCT06178276), approved by the local Ethics Committee and gave informed written consent. 

Listening tasks: To investigate the neural correlates of L2 speech processing, we designed a dedicated protocol that consists of 3 distinct tasks. Task 1 is designed to produce the neural correlates of oral fluent comprehension in native language (L1, French). Each audio is displayed as a waveform, while a yellow cursor progresses from left to right in synchrony with the unfolding sound. Task 2 is designed to produce the neural correlates of oral fluent comprehension in L2 (Italian). The stimuli were presented in the same way as in L1 condition and consisted of A1-level audios materials drawn from language manuals or certification exams, which were below the participant’s proficiency level. Task 3 is the main oral comprehension task. It involves listening to audio sequences (drawn from radio programs or podcasts episodes) presented slightly above the participants’ proficiency level (C1 for B2 subjects and B1 for A2 subjects). For delimitation of the obstacles, an additional red cursor appears on the waveform when subjects click to indicate them. 

Data acquisition: Functional MRI experiments were carried out at the “IRMaGe” facility of Grenoble. All MR images were acquired on a 3T MRI scanner with a 32-channel head coil (Experimental setup in Fig. 1). To hear the audio stimuli and not the scanner noise, subjects wore earplugs and MR-Confon headphones that actively removes scanner noise while transmitting the audios to the subject. Simultaneously, a physiological monitoring was performed, using the PPU of the scanner and the electro-dermal activity (EDA). Finally, for recording the delimitation of obstacles, a 4-buttons response box was used. The MRI experiment begins with a high-resolution 3D structural image. A 1-min duration functional sequence with a single L2 audio permit to verify if the subject hears properly the audios. Then, five T2*-weighted functional runs were acquired with a brain coverage that includes the cerebellum. The 1st one presents in blocks, 5 alternations of L1 audio vs. rest. The 2nd one is like the 1st one but with fluent audios in L2. The 3 last functional runs are for task 3 and are presented in the following way. It is a block presentation with three different audios, each presented with 3 phases: a phase of hearing of audio (‘E1’), a second phase of hearing while delimitating the obstacles (‘E2’) and a rest phase (‘+’).

Data processing and statistical analysis: fMRI data were analysed using SPM25 software. Individual analyses were performed in the referential of each subject. Pre-processing included motion correction, slice-timing, coregistration of the functional images on the structural image and spatial smoothing with a 5x5x5mm3 full width at half maximum Gaussian 3D-kernel. Individual statistical analyses were conducted within a block design using the generalized linear model containing the images of the 5 functional runs. The following contrasts of interest were computed: L1 vs. fluent-L2, and obstacle-L2 vs fluent-L2 that represents the L2 with obstacles vs. L2 without obstacles. After applying the transformation in the MNI referential to the contrasts and anatomical images, statistical group analyses were performed. The contrasts L1 vs. fluent-L2 and the obstacle-L2 vs. fluent-L2 were entered in a two-sample t-test for the A2 and the B2 groups. Conjunction analyses were performed across the two groups, and differences between them were also examined for both conditions. Statistical significance was considered for pFDR corrected < 0.05 and p-value on extent < 0.05.

Results

L1 vs fluent-L2: There is a higher cerebral activity in comprehension in fluent-L2 as compared to L1 in the conjunction of the 2 groups, located in the mTG, in the mFG and in the precentral gyrus (face area) in the left hemisphere. In addition, in the B2 sub-group, there is a higher activity in the left posterior Wernicke’s area as compared to A2 sub-group in fluent-L2 > L1 comprehension.

Fluent-L2-fluent vs obstacle-L2: Listening in L2 in the presence of obstacles to comprehension, compared with fluent L2 comprehension, elicited a large pattern of activity mainly located in the occipital lobe and predominantly in the right hemisphere. This activity involved visual areas V1, V2, V3 and V5, as well as bilateral activation of the fusiform gyrus and the right-hemisphere activation of the lingual gyrus (Fig. 2A). In the A2 subgroup, additional activity was observed in the left precentral cortex (face representation) and in the right anterior prefrontal cortex (Fig. 2B). In the B2 subgroup, additional activity was found in the ventral premotor cortex (Fig. 2C).

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this study is among the first to investigate oral comprehension of natural language in a second language across two proficiency levels, comparing fluent L2 listening with obstacle-induced L2 listening. The results reveal large common activation patterns as well as specific activities according to the L2 level (advanced vs. intermediate). Behavioural differences between these two groups include variations in attentional demands, translation in the L1, degree of processing automaticity, and linguistic knowledge, among other factors. The comparison between L1 and fluent L2, show higher cerebral activity, probably due to a higher cognitive demand. In addition, in the B2 subgroup, activity was detected in the posterior part of Wernicke’s area, which may reflect a process supporting enhanced understanding. The findings of this study on L2 speech comprehension with obstacles vs. fluent L2 comprehension may seem surprising. Indeed, in both subgroups, a large common pattern was observed in the occipital lobe. Although unexpected, this finding is consistent with previous work showing that spoken language processing can also engage the primary visual cortex [4]. The additional activities in the A2 subgroup in the face area corresponds to orofacial articulatory muscles. Its involvement in this study might be paralleled with the translation strategy of the subjects from L2 to L1 language. Additional activity in the anterior part of the prefrontal cortex was found in previous work related to a multisensory network involved in understanding speech in noise [5]. In this sense, the difficulty in comprehension may resemble the challenge of processing L2 language at an intermediate proficiency level.
