Introduction

Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS) surgery implants electrodes to modulate neural circuits for treating movement disorders such as Parkinson's disease and shows promise for psychiatric and cognitive conditions. Postoperative verification of electrode placement is critical for clinical outcomes. MRI provides superior soft-tissue contrast without radiation exposure but suffers from severe metallic artifacts, including signal voids, pile-ups, and geometric distortions that obscure anatomy and complicate electrode localization. This is particularly problematic given that DBS efficacy is sensitive to millimeter-scale targeting errors. Segmenting the electrode artifact is therefore essential for subsequent postoperative analysis.

While deep learning, particularly U-Net-based architectures, has advanced medical image segmentation, its application to DBS MRI remains constrained by the need for costly, scarce expert annotations to handle severe metallic artifacts. To overcome this annotation bottleneck, we developed a hybrid framework combining classical image processing with deep learning. A traditional algorithm first generates large-scale training labels, which are then used to train a 3D U-Net (DBS-ElecNet) that subsequently surpasses the traditional method in speed and robustness. The resulting segmentations can be further refined for precise electrode reconstruction and contact detection, enabling a scalable and automated analysis pipeline that does not require manual annotation.

Methods

Dataset and Preprocessing

This study utilised 280 post-operative 3D-MPRAGE T1w MRI scans (265 bilateral, 15 unilateral) from patients at Toronto Western Hospital, scanned at 1.5 tesla (T), with identical four-contact electrodes. Data standardisation was performed using FreeSurfer^1 and Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs)^2 with resampling to 1 mm isotropic resolution (Sinc interpolation), skull-stripping with SynthStrip, intensity inhomogeneity correction with N4 algorithm, and preliminary segmentation with SynthSeg.

Segmentation Algorithm using LINe-fitting for Electrodes (SALINE)

Automatic electrode region segmentation was done first using a novel image processing workflow, shown in Figure 1a, which is hereafter referred to as SALINE (Segmentation Algorithm using LINe-fitting for Electrodes). First, Electrode artifact candidate regions were identified by applying Laplacian and Frangi filters^3 to the image. Both filter outputs were normalized and thresholded (0.21 and 0.25, respectively) to highlight the electrode's intensity variations and tubular structure. Thresholded outputs were combined, retaining only overlapping voxels. The SynthSeg brain segmentation masked anatomically improbable regions and identified target endpoints. Filtered voxels served as points for line-fitting to approximate electrode trajectory using either SVD-based least squares (few outliers) or RANSAC^4 (if many outliers). The fitted line was dilated by a 6-voxel radius to create the final electrode mask, which encompasses electrode artifacts and accounts for any anatomical variations or minor trajectory deviations.

DBS-ElecNet

We used a standard 3D U-Net with encoder-decoder architecture, shown in Figure 1b, employing skip connections, with each layer performing two sequential 3×3×3 convolutions followed by BatchNorm and LeakyReLU. The encoder uses max-pooling for downsampling, while the decoder uses transposed convolutions for upsampling, culminating in a final convolutional layer that produces voxel-wise logits. 

The model, DBS-ElecNet, was trained using 258 post-operative MRI volumes (211 training, 7 validation, 40 test), excluding 22 inadequate SALINE segmentations. Data was augmented using TorchIO, and all volumes were resized to 128³ and z-score normalized. Training used a Dice-BCE loss over 150 epochs (batch size 16, Adam optimizer with β₁=0.9 and β₂=0.999, lr=1e⁻⁴) on an NVIDIA H100 GPU.

Precise Electrode Segmentation

The DBS-ElecNet output is intentionally inclusive for tasks like inpainting. For precise geometric localization, the mask is refined via dynamic thresholding to isolate the core electrode. The dynamic threshold, T, is computed as max(μ - 1.5σ, I_min + 0.65(μ - I_min)) from the mask's intensity statistics (mean intensity μ, standard deviation σ, and minimum intensity I_min). An anatomical mask excludes ventricular cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) to prevent false positives.

The refined segmentation enabled trajectory reconstruction by adapting the PaCER^5 algorithm (originally designed for CT images) to T1-weighted MRI (referred to as T1-PaCER). A parameterized polynomial is fitted through the segmented points, and the curve is refined by re-fitting to the inverse intensity weighted centroid, leveraging the electrode's hypointense signature. Centroids within the ventricles are masked out during this stage.

Finally, contact localization is performed by further adapting PaCER's intensity integration. Instead of using a full disk orthogonal to the trajectory, intensity is computed within a ring-shaped region (a larger disk minus a smaller concentric disk). This design specifically captures the intensity bulge of the physical contacts, amplifying their signal variation and producing a pronounced oscillatory intensity profile along the trajectory that simplifies the identification of individual contact locations.

Results

Figure 2a compares segmentation results from expert manual labeling, SALINE, and DBS-ElecNet. SALINE fails to capture the full electrode extent (yellow arrow) because its rigid trajectory model does not account for anatomical deviations during implantation. Being purely image-driven, SALINE is vulnerable to contrast variations and, in rare cases, struggles to separate bilateral electrodes due to head orientation, leading to suboptimal or incorrect electrode masks.

In contrast, DBS-ElecNet learned to identify electrodes directly from image context, demonstrating robust performance despite these variations. It achieves superior segmentation compared to SALINE, especially for non-straight electrodes. DBS-ElecNet masks are comparable to expert manual segmentations, consistently producing more anatomically coherent and complete representations of electrode trajectories.

DBS-ElecNet was evaluated against expert-annotated ground truth on a portion of the test set, achieving an average Dice score of 0.805 and F2-score of 0.869. The higher F2-score reflects the intentional expansion design to favor slight over-segmentation for complete artifact coverage, beneficial for downstream tasks like inpainting. For comparison, SALINE achieved a similar Dice score (0.805) but a lower F2-score (0.859). The distributions of Dice and F2-scores across the test set are summarized in Figure 2b. We further evaluated DBS-ElecNet on a subset where SALINE struggled to capture full electrodes. On this challenging subset, SALINE achieved a Dice coefficient of 0.608 and an F2-score of 0.647, while DBS-ElecNet achieved 0.765 and 0.820, respectively, demonstrating substantially improved overlap with ground truth and higher recall of relevant regions.

Regarding computational performance, DBS-ElecNet was substantially faster than alternative methods. Manual segmentation required 3-5 minutes per image, and SALINE took 1.5 minutes, whereas DBS-ElecNet performed inference in ~3 seconds per image on a standard CPU. This represents a 30× speedup over SALINE and 60-100× speedup over manual segmentation, enabling rapid, scalable analysis.

The precise segmentation method performed reliably only on images with minimal artifacts (Figure 2c). Global thresholding often erroneously includes hypointense shading artifacts and non-electrode regions such as CSF. Unilateral cases, having fewer artifacts, yielded better results; in bilateral cases, the less-affected electrode was typically segmented more successfully.

When applied to T1-weighted MRI, PaCER provided satisfactory contact detection given successful segmentation, producing clear trajectories and integrated intensity profiles with four local minima (Figure 2d). However, one limitation was sensitivity to nearby hypointense structures (e.g., ventricles) and shading artifacts, which biased trajectories via inverse intensity weighted centroids. In severe artifact cases with unsatisfactory precise segmentation, PaCER could produce incorrect trajectories, miss contacts entirely, or falsely detect three or five contacts, likely due to residual artifacts or suboptimal image quality. This is currently being investigated further.

In summary, both methods perform well on artifact-free images but degrade with typical clinical artifact levels. This necessitates MRI-specific modifications, particularly for T1-PaCER, to robustly handle anatomical structures and artifacts, a challenge absent in its original CT-based design.

Conclusion

This study introduces a hybrid framework for segmenting DBS electrodes in postoperative MRI by combining traditional image processing with deep learning, eliminating the need for manual annotation. The resulting DBS-ElecNet provides robust, efficient segmentation that can be further refined via dynamic thresholding to generate precise masks suitable for geometric analysis. This precise segmentation enabled the successful adaptation of the PaCER algorithm from CT to T1-weighted MRI (T1-PaCER) for electrode trajectory reconstruction and contact localization.

While the current approach simplifies certain geometric assumptions, it establishes a foundational pipeline for generating large-scale labeled data and extracting clinically relevant features. Future work will focus on refining segmentation accuracy, improving contact localization robustness to MRI artifacts, and applying the model to clinical tasks such as artifact inpainting and image registration to enhance electrode localization and postoperative outcome analysis.
