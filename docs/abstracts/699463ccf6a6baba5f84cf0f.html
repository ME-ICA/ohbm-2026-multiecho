Introduction

Cortical areas vary widely across individuals in their surface area, areal scaling, and shape. For example, visual parcels can differ in surface area by approximately threefold among individuals, whereas total cortical surface area varies less than twofold. Differential areal scaling (scaling of cortical surface area of different regions to the total hemispheric cortical surface area) reveals that frontoparietal networks are disproportionately expanded in larger brains, whereas sensory, limbic, and motor networks scale more modestly.1 These patterns also align with evolutionary gradients of cortical expansion. Beyond size and scaling, cortical areas also exhibit substantial shape variability. Early sensory areal boundaries often align with cortical folds, such as the calcarine and central sulci, but higher-order cognitive areas do not align with folds.2 Shape variations may provide distinct insights into cortical organization and may also serve as sensitive markers of developmental or pathological changes. Such variability can be objectively quantified through registration-induced distortions; which capture local shape differences more accurately than conventional shape metrics. Leveraging recent advances in multimodal cortical areal classification3 and deep learning-based inter-subject registration, we quantified individual variability in cortical area size, scaling, shape, and assessed areal bilateral symmetry across 1,071 HCP Young Adult (HCP-YA) participants. We further examined whether areas within each of 12 functional networks covary in their scaling and compared these patterns with previously generated interspecies expansion maps. Together, these analyses present a novel characterization of the complexity of human cortical variability, offering new insights into the organizational principles of the human cerebral cortex, which can serve as a benchmark for future studies involving diseased populations. 

Methods

For this study, we utilized the full HCP-YA dataset (N = 1071; age range 22–35 years; 485 males, 586 females) to analyze individual variability in cortical organization across three metrics: (1) cortical area size, (2) areal scaling with total hemispheric surface area, and (3) areal shape variation. Inter-individual variability in area size was quantified as the ratio between the 95th and 5th percentiles of surface area across individuals for each cortical area. Areal scaling was estimated using log–log linear regression between each area’s surface area and the total hemispheric cortical surface area; the regression slope represents the scaling coefficient, where a slope of 1 indicates isometric scaling and deviations from 1 indicate allometric scaling. Statistical significance for each cortical area was assessed using p-values corrected for multiple comparisons with a false discovery rate of q = 0.05. Sex, age, and the interaction between those two terms were included as covariates in the regression model. In addition, areas within each of 12 functional networks previously mapped to the HCP multimodal parcellation using resting-state fMRI4 were examined for covariance in their scaling patterns. The resulting areal scaling maps were compared with evolutionary cortical expansion maps derived from macaque, chimpanzee, and human MRI processed using HCP pipelines using an interspecies registration framework; macaque-chimpanzee and chimpanzee-human using 15 putatively homologous regions. Finally, to quantify areal shape variation, we computed registration-induced distortions of the individual to the template spherical surface. Surface registration produces local deformations that can be characterized by changes in the size and aspect ratio of tessellation triangles. Vertex-wise shape distortions were computed as the ratio of principal in-plane stretches5, and the standard deviation of mean distortion (weighted by vertex area) was calculated for each cortical area to represent variability in shape. Bilateral symmetry was assessed by computing (1) the coefficient of interhemispheric determination and (2) an asymmetry score, defined as the absolute value of (L-R)/((L+R)/2) for each cortical area.

Results

Individual cortical areas exhibit substantial variability in size, ranging from approximately 2-fold in early sensory regions such as V1 and area 4 to nearly 10-fold in higher-order regions including 9p, VMV3, and a10p (Fig. 1A). Size variability is strongly correlated across hemispheres, with corresponding areas in the left and right hemispheres showing high bilateral consistency (Fig. 1B; r2 = 0.74). Our analysis of differential areal scaling indicates that frontoparietal regions expand disproportionately in individuals with larger brains (red), whereas limbic, sensorimotor, and visual regions show more modest expansion (blue) (Fig. 1C). All areas exhibit statistically significant positive scaling across participants after controlling for age, sex, and their interactions. Statistical significance was assessed using residual permutation testing (10,000 permutations), and multiple comparisons were controlled using the false discovery rate (q = 0.05). Areal scaling shows strong bilateral symmetry (Fig. 1D, r2 = 0.52). To compare our findings with Reardon et al. (2018), we computed a second set of scaling coefficients using folding-based registration (MSMSulc). Many regions show close correspondence across studies (e.g., p10p, LO3, 7PC, V1; yellow arrows), whereas others show notable deviations (e.g., PFop, VVC; blue arrows), likely reflecting methodological differences, as Reardon et al. used a vertex-wise approach and applied extensive spatial smoothing (Fig. 1E). When examining mean areal scaling across the 12 functional networks defined using the HCP multimodal parcellation, we find that the ventral-multimodal, dorsal-attention, and frontoparietal networks scale supralinearly; the default-mode network scales approximately linearly; the visual1 and orbito-affective networks scale sublinearly; and the language network scales supralinearly on the left and sublinearly on the right (Fig. 1F). The covariance matrix reveals strong positive covariance between the ventral-multimodal and orbito-affective networks and strong negative covariance between the auditory and cingulo-opercular networks (Fig. 1G). Comparison of human areal scaling with evolutionary areal scaling (macaque-chimpanzee-human) shows qualitatively similar patterns (Fig. 1H). Evolutionary scaling also shows strong hemispheric symmetry (Fig. 1I). Finally, our analysis of cortical area shape (ratio of principal in-plane stretches) shows that visual and sensorimotor areas have low shape variation, while higher-order cognitive regions exhibit considerably greater variability (Fig. 1J). Shape variability also demonstrates strong bilateral symmetry (Fig. 1K, r2 = 0.82).

Conclusion

Our analyses demonstrate that individual human cortical areas vary widely in their size, scaling, and shape, with higher-order association regions showing the greatest variability and early sensory regions exhibiting relatively low variability. Differential areal scaling patterns highlight that frontoparietal, dorsal-attention, and ventral-multimodal networks expand disproportionately in larger brains, consistent with evolutionary expansion gradients, whereas sensory, limbic, and visual networks scale more modestly. These scaling patterns are strongly bilateral and covary across functional networks, revealing coordinated principles of cortical organization. Shape variability analyses further indicate that higher-order regions exhibit more variation than primary sensory and motor regions, while maintaining robust interhemispheric symmetry. Together, these findings provide a detailed characterization of human cortical variability, establish a benchmark for expected normative variation, and offer a framework for future studies investigating cortical organization in development and neurological disorders. Such investigations will also be essential for understanding how normative anatomical variability relates to behavioral differences and neuropsychological conditions across individuals.
