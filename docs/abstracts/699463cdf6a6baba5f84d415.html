Introduction

Schizophrenia is amongst the top 15 causes of disability worldwide. The strongest predictors of poor functioning are negative symptoms and memory deficits, which are further associated with hippocampal dysconnectivity. To investigate the illness progression from hippocampus to functioning across psychosis subtypes, we previously implemented the machine-learning algorithm Subtype and Stage Inference (SuStaIn) which integrates clustering and disease progression modeling.^1 We identified two progression patterns developing from reduced verbal memory to social cognition, higher negative symptoms and poorer functioning.^2 In one subtype, this development was preceded by morphometric hippocampal dysconnectivity. In an independent sample of enduring schizophrenia, we used SuStaIn to show that deficits in social cognition were preceded by reductions in hippocampal volume in one subtype,^3 while we further replicated our initial work with resting-state fMRI.^4 These findings provided evidence that reductions across hippocampal metrics (i.e., volume, morphometric and functional connectivity) have an additive effect in explaining the progression from the hippocampus to functioning in psychosis. Between hippocampal metrics, we further found that deviations in volume precede reductions in connectivity.^5 We now aim to investigate the neurocognitive model of psychosis in an independent sample of first- and multi-episode psychosis while considering multiple hippocampal metrics. We a) hypothesize to identify one subtype progressing from reduced hippocampal metrics towards memory, negatives symptoms and functioning, while b) reductions in hippocampal volume precede reductions in morphometric connectivity. Given the central role of working memory and global episodic memory in psychosis, we further explore the role of distinct memory domains in our model.

Methods

We sampled neuroimaging data of 161 individuals with first-episode psychosis and 391 individuals with schizophrenia. Cortical thickness values were parcellated in accordance with the Desikan-Killiany-Tourville (DKT) atlas through Free Surfer 7.4.0, and the hippocampal regions in accordance with the Free Surfer hippocampal module. Upon parcellation, we selected the 62 cortical regions of the DKT atlas which overlapped with our previous work^2 and grouped together the hippocampal regions to result in 12 regions per hemisphere which were based on subfields rather than anterior-posterior segmentations. We then used NeuroCombat to harmonize the data across MRI field strengths (1.5T vs 3T), and derived bilateral hippocampal volume by summing up the hippocampal volumes of the left and right hemispheres respectively. To calculate morphometric connectivity, we computed a structural covariance matrix of the cortical and hippocampal regions for all patients, and then calculated subject-specific covariance matrices using the jackknife bias estimation procedure. Subject-specific matrices were used to compute the participation coefficient, a graph-theoretical measure evaluating the diversity of intra- vs. inter-modular connectivity for each brain region. Cortical regions were grouped into the 7 Yeo modules while the hippocampal regions were grouped into a hippocampal module. Participation coefficients were interpreted to be high when regions of one module were connected to a variety of regions across other modules, and low when regions showed more connections within their own module. The participation coefficients of the left and right hippocampal regions per hemisphere were then averaged to derive bilateral morphometric connectivity. Global episodic memory was measured through the Wechsler Memory Scale (WMS) while working memory was taken from the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS-III). Negative symptoms were measured through the negative subscale score of the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS), and functional outcome through the Global Assessment of Functioning (GAF) Scale. To consider potential differences across subdomains of memory, we ran distinct analyses for both domains of memory. For the analysis of global episodic memory, missing data was handled by excluding individuals with missing memory scores followed by univariate imputation based on means for the other variables, resulting in a sample size of n = 233. For the analysis of working memory, we handled missing data by univariate imputation based on means for all variables, resulting in a sample size of n = 552. Variables were then z-scored in relation to control means and standard deviations as reported in the literature or through our own work. We then applied z-score SuStaIn to the variables of bilateral hippocampal volume and connectivity, memory (one analysis per domain), negative symptoms and functioning.

Results

The optimal number of subtypes was identified based on log-likelihood model comparisons and the cross-validation information criterion, a SuStaIn-specific measure of model fit. The analysis focusing on global episodic memory (WMS Model, Figure 1) identified two progression subtypes, and notably no subtype of individuals with normal-range performance. In the first subtype (n = 116), SuStaIn identified a progression from reduced global episodic memory towards reduced bilateral hippocampal volume, higher negative symptoms, reduced bilateral hippocampal connectivity and ultimately poorer functioning. In the second subtype (n = 117), SuStaIn showed a progression from reduced memory towards higher negative symptoms, reduced bilateral volume and then connectivity, followed by poorer functioning. For the analysis focusing on working memory (WAIS Model, Figure 1), SuStaIn identified three subtypes of which one included individuals with normal-range performance on all variables (n = 13). In the first progression subtype (n = 332), SuStaIn identified a progression from reduced bilateral hippocampal volume toward reduced working memory, higher negative symptoms, reduced bilateral connectivity and ultimately poorer functioning. The second progression subtype (n = 207) showed a progression from increased negative symptoms towards reduced bilateral volume, working memory, bilateral connectivity and functioning. 

Conclusion

We provide distinct progression subtypes of psychosis in an independent dataset, and individually consider the role of global episodic memory and working memory across psychosis subtypes. First, we identify two subtypes which are led by reduced global episodic memory. These findings indicate that global episodic memory is the marker which is consistently and most severely impaired in our sample of first- and enduring psychosis. In previous work, we assessed the role of verbal memory and found hippocampal volume and connectivity to precede these deficits in a subsample of psychosis. Considering that the WMS provides an index of global episodic memory that goes beyond verbal memory, the relationship with hippocampal volume and connectivity might be less specific while global episodic memory might be more central prior to illness onset. We do however identify progressions from impaired memory towards higher negative symptoms and poorer functioning, in line with prior meta-analyses and our own work.^2,4 We also consistently identify reductions in hippocampal volume as preceding morphometric connectivity and thus replicate our previous results.^5 Second, we do identify one subtype in which hippocampal volume precedes working memory, symptoms and functioning, thus supporting our model. For both working memory subtypes, our findings indicate that hippocampal volume might be central for predicting working memory decline, suggesting that global episodic memory, hippocampal volume and working memory might have distinct temporal relationships. Third, our previous work showed that deviations in connectivity across the whole sample were much smaller than deviations in volume,^5 and did not necessarily differ between patients and controls. A mediation analyses from our group showing an effect of reduced connectivity on increased negative symptoms as mediated by verbal memory was performed in first-episode psychosis, yet research in functional MRI has shown that reductions in connectivity can be re-established through antipsychotic medication, suggesting that connectivity might be malleable over the course of the illness. While similar effects have been shown for hippocampal volume, the baseline differences between patients and controls appear larger and might thus place volume earlier in the progression and connectivity later. To conclude, these findings partially replicate and extend our work in an independent sample of first-episode psychosis and schizophrenia. Hippocampal volume appears to be impaired more severely than connectivity, while both might be malleable to medication. Future research should consider our model across further subdomains of memory, bearing the potential of patient stratification into subtypes and a prediction of their progression through stages.
