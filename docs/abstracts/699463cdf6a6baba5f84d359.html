Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a major cause of long-term neurological disability. Severe TBI often leads to coma and brain damage, and clinical evaluation in the acute phase is difficult as patients are non-communicative and cannot perform functional tests. Thus, the Glasgow Outcome Scale Extended (GOSE) cannot provide precise prognostic information. Imaging biomarkers of brain integrity are therefore crucial for predicting recovery potential and guiding clinical decisions ^1, as damage to specific neural pathways directly impacts functional brain areas and convalescence duration. 

Diffusion MRI (dMRI) non-invasively assesses white matter microstructure through water diffusion properties ^2. Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) metrics, such as fractional anisotropy (FA) and mean diffusivity (MD), are widely used to evaluate global or regional white matter damage in TBI ^3. MD quantifies the average rate of water diffusion through tissue, while FA indexes the degree of anisotropy in white matter, serving as a marker of white matter integrity. Whole-brain FA and MD have been linked to long-term outcomes such as GOSE ^4.

 However, global measures ignore the heterogeneous distribution of injury and its impact on specific pathways, which may have a stronger relationship with functional deficits than overall averages. The different parts of the lesion may differently affect surrounding bundles. We suppose the necrotic core to contain fully disrupted fibers, and surrounding oedema reflects fluid accumulation due to inflammation. We hypothesize that the perilesional zone shows altered but potentially functioning fibers. 

These variations modify water diffusion and are reflected in microstructural metrics, helping to better characterize the lesion. 

Here, we propose a framework to quantify the impact of TBI on microstructure measures of nearby white matter bundles by analyzing FA and MD across different lesions layers, and then in intersection with bundles.

Methods

Data analysed in this study come from the ComaScore cohort, a multicenter study coordinated by Pr. Louis Puybasset (Hôpital Pitié-Salpêtrière, Paris) (NCT 03874208). Patients in coma after a TBI, cardiac arrest, and aneurysmal Subarachnoid haemorrhage were included in this study. Inclusion criteria were age > 18 years, non-penetrating head trauma, and persistent unresponsiveness to simple orders for at least seven days after injury. A MRI acquisition was performed between day 7 and day 45 post-injury. The protocol was done on a 3T MRI machine, included a T1-weighted structural image (MPRAGE), a T2 Fluid Attenuated Inversion Recovery (FLAIR) image, a single-shell diffusion-weighted sequence (b = 1000 s/mm2, 64 directions, 2 mm isotropic voxels, plus 8 b0 volumes) and a Susceptibility Weighted Image (SWI). The GOSE score at one year was used as the clinical outcome measure. The controls, also from the Comascore study, are 20 healthy volunteers scanned with the same MRI protocol as the TBI patients. Controls were selected to match the age distribution of the patient cohort. In this preliminary study, we used 3 adult patients with moderate to severe TBI, recruited from the Rennes center.

Neuroimaging data were preprocessed using the Anima toolbox. Processing steps included denoising, motion and eddy-current correction, tensor fitting, and FA and MD maps computation. Each subject’s maps (FA and MD) and T1, T2 FLAIR images were nonlinearly registered to MNI template using ANTs library registration function. Lesions were manually segmented on T2-FLAIR images prior to registration to MNI space. Each lesion was segmented into three layers: 1/ necrotic core, 2/ perilesional area, and 3/ oedema, whose boundaries were refined using the SWI image. The necrotic core was defined as the central hyper-intense region on FLAIR, corresponding to a hypo-intense blood-related signal on SWI. The perilesional area was delineated as the darker grey region surrounding the necrotic core. Finally, oedema represents the lighter grey outer region, reflecting fluid accumulation secondary to inflammation. Figure 1 shows a lesion with its 3 manually segmented layers, and a bundle whose segmentation intersects the lesion. Automatic white matter bundle segmentations of 71 major tracts were generated using TractSeg ^5. 

First, we analyzed microstructural metrics at the lesion level. To do so, for each patient, z-scores of FA and MD were computed in each layer by comparing patient values to the mean values of a control group. Regions with |z|>1.96 were considered abnormal, corresponding to values outside the 95 % confidence interval of the control population.
To test whether the three lesion layers showed different levels of damage, a one-way ANOVA was performed with those z-scores as dependent variables and layers as the independent factor. 

Secondly, to evaluate the direct impact of lesions on white matter bundles, we computed the average FA and MD within the intersection zone between each lesion layer and each bundle segmentation. The z-scores for each microstructure metrics, were calculated on those new areas for patients and controls. To measure the zone effect, we ran a mixed effect model, where lesion layers were included as a fixed effect, while bundles, patients, and lesions were treated as random effects. After that, for both analyses, post-hoc comparisons were performed between the mean microstructural z-scores of each layer. Z-scores were weighted by number of voxels in each zone to account for lesion size.

Results

Concerning the lesion analysis, results showed different conclusion between FA and MD.
For FA values, the zone-based ANOVA did not reveal a significant effect of zone on FA z-score values (F(2, 31.1) = 0.64, p= 0.53). Mean FA z-scores were consistently negative across all zones, indicating reduced anisotropy relative to controls, but no systematic variation was observed between the necrotic core (mean z-score of -6.55), the perilesional zone (mean z-score of -5.26), and the oedema zone (mean z-score of -6.33). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons confirmed the absence of significant differences between zones (all p > 0.59). For MD, however, the ANOVA showed a strong effect of the zone on z-score values (F(2,27.3)=9.51,p=0.00073). Indeed, mean z-score in necrotic core was at -3.19, which means that, generally, MD value in necrotic core was highly abnormal and below controls mean MD values in same area. 

Post-hoc comparisons revealed significantly higher MD z-scores in the oedema zone compared to both the necrotic core (p = 0.0006) and the perilesional zone (p = 0.028), while the difference between the necrotic core and perilesional zone did not reach significance. MD also presented a mean z-score of 3.8 in oedema zone, showing abnormal diffusivity around the lesion compared to controls, which makes sens as oedema is described as accumulation of liquid due to inflammation, causing higher isotropic diffusivity thus higher MD. 

The absence of a zone effect on FA suggests that its alterations may be driven by factors not captured by simple lesion zoning, motivating a tract-specific (bundle-based) analysis to better account for white matter organization. 

For the second analysis, concerning the bundle effect, the mixed effect model of FA and MD showed a significant effect of zone (both presented a F-test result F(3,1590)>60, with p<10^-16) on resulting z-score. For FA, post-hoc comparisons revealed that z-scores in the rest of the bundle were significantly higher than in lesion zones: vs necrotic core (p = 0.0056), vs oedema (p < 0.0001), and vs perilesional zone ( p < 0.0001). Random effect estimates showed that variability was mainly driven by patients (SD = 7.05) and lesions (SD = 16.60). 

For MD, post-hoc comparisons showed a graded increase in z-scores from necrotic core to perilesional and oedema zones, with necrotic core vs oedema (p < 0.0001) and necrotic core vs perilesional zone (p = 0.0005) being significant. The main source of variability was the patient (SD = 20.74). 

Overall, these results confirm that lesion layer strongly affects both FA and MD, while patient variability is the main contributor to differences, and bundle differences are minor. Lesion consistently exhibited decreased FA and increased MD, as expected in TBI context as displayed in Figure 2. We also saw that the injury has an impact on the bundles, as shown by abnormal values of microstructure metrics inside the several layers of injury intersected with lesion, but the impact was not generally extended to the rest of the bundle.

Conclusion

We proposed a framework aiming to estimate the impact of lesion on TBI patients, and analysing the effect of distance to the lesion. This preliminary study demonstrates that lesion layers have different impact on white matter microstructure. FA and MD were the most abnormal in lesion necrotic core, as fibers originally passing through the lesioned zone were most likely destroyed by the injury. The metrics gradually normalize towards outer layers, while the effect on the entire bundle outside the lesion appears limited. While these preliminary results were obtained from a small set of patients, the goal would be to extend this analysis to more patients with more control each, along with the addition of tract specific metrics such as Apparent Fiber Density (AFD), since this metric could inform about bundles fiber loss and bundles deformation. With more data, the objective would be to use this approach to investigate the relationship between white matter microstructure changes with patient clinic measures such as Glasgow Outcome Scale Extended (GOSE) or Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA)).
