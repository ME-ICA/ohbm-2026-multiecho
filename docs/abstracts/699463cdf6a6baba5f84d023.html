Introduction

Trigeminal Neuralgia (TN), also referred to as the "suicide disease", is a chronic condition marked by debilitating attacks of stabbing facial pain. While TN can be controlled with pharmacotherapy, there is no cure and a high rate of refractory pain.1 Classical TN involves compression by an offending blood vessel resulting morphological change of the trigeminal nerve root on the affected side, which may be treated by microvascular decompression (MVD). MVD can result in long-term pain relief, though many surgery recipients experience pain recurrence over time despite no evidence of recurrent vascular compression.2 This points to other factors influencing long-term pain relief in TN. 

Most prior work aimed at understanding TN and its response to treatment has been focused on the structure of the trigeminal nerve. However, recent literature suggests that TN is associated with widespread structural brain changes as in other chronic pain conditions3. Additionally, the literature suggests that TN patients show volumetric changes on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans in somatosensory (e.g., thalamus) and limbic/emotional (e.g. hippocampus) brain structures that may relate to surgical outcome2. This project aims to more comprehensively describe structural brain changes in medically-refractory classical TN to elucidate how these relate to the chronic pain state and durable post-MVD pain relief. To achieve this, advanced quantitative approaches were used to analyze prospectively collected brain imaging data from one of the largest ongoing studies in TN.

Objectives

1. Identify how pain-relevant gray matter structures (insula, cingulum and post central gyrus in the cortex, and hippocampus, amygdala and thalamus subcortically) differ structurally between TN patients undergoing MVD and controls, and between MVD responders and non-responders.

2. Examine correlations between subcortical volumes and cortical thickness measurements across different brain regions in classical TN.

Methods

This study involves the retrospective analysis of prospectively collected MRI scans from 20 healthy controls (HC), 19 classical TN (CTN) patients who were responders to MVD surgery, and 7 CTN patients who were non-responders to MVD surgery. Scans were obtained as part of a longitudinal TN study at a single centre between 2017 and 2024. Any history/evidence of multiple sclerosis, other lesional causes of TN, psychiatric diagnoses, or previous TN or non-TN neurosurgical treatments were exclusion criteria. HCs had no identifiable mental illness or chronic pain conditions. One year following MVD surgery, any patient reporting no pain was considered a responder to MVD, while any pain at all was considered to be non-response.

CTN and HC patients underwent MRI scanning (CTN was one month before surgery). A 3T Siemens (Erlangen, Germany) Prisma Magnetom MRI scanner with a 64-channel head radiofrequency coil was used to scan all participants. At every MRI acquisition, participants underwent the following sequence to be used for structural  analysis: 3D T1-weighted structural scan (magnetization-prepared rapid acquisition gradient echo (MPRAGE), field-of-view (FOV)= 250 × 250mm2, 208 sagittal slices, voxel size 1 × 1 × 1 mm , repetition time (TR)=1800 ms, echo time (TE)=2.37 ms, inversion time (TI)=900 ms, 8° flip angle, 3:41 min.

Freesurfer image analysis suite (v.7.4.1 Martinos Center for Biomedical Imaging, Harvard-MIT, Boston, MA) was used to produce fully automated subcortical volumetric segmentation and an evaluation of cortical thickness. This process includes motion correction, removal of non-brain tissue, Talairach transformation, segmentation, intensity normalization and cortical reconstruction4. 

Statistical tests were performed on patient demographic variables to ensure that variance in baseline characteristics were accounted for in further analyses. Sex and side-of-pain were compared between CTN, HC, and responder/non-responder groups using Fisher’s Exact Test. Age was compared using a t-test. Age was significantly different between TN responders and non-responders and as such was used as a covariate in related analyses. Inter-side comparisons of subcortical structure volume in CTN patients were done using a paired t-test. For this analysis a statistical significance threshold of p < 0.05 was set without a correction for multiple comparisons. This is primarily because the structures were not independent of one another.

In order to assess volumetric differences between structures ipsilateral and contralateral to the side of pain in CTN patients compared to HC, each subcortical structure from the HC was treated as separate such that 40 volumes for each subcortical structure (left and right each from 20 controls) were used in each statistical analysis. An unpaired t-test was used to do 3 comparisons for each structure (ipsilateral, contralateral and total) (GraphPad Prism version 10 for Windows, GraphPad Software, La Jolla, California, USA). The threshold of statistical significance was p < 0.05. 

Subcortical structure volumes were compared between CTN responders and non-responders to MVD using one-way analysis of covariance (ANCOVA, IBM SPSS Statistics for Mac OS, version 24.0., IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). The outcome variables were volumes/thickness of selected brain structures. The independent variable was response to surgery. Two ANCOVAs were carried out per structure (ipsilateral and contralateral). Age was used as a covariate. Multiple comparison correction was auto-performed by least significant difference. 

To determine the optimal predictive value of preoperative subcortical structure volumes and cortical structure thickness to classify response versus non-response to MVD in CTN, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was computed using 3 classification models: Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and XGBooster.

Results

Twenty-six CTN patients and 20 controls (HC) were included in all subcortical volumetric analyses (Table 1). Out of the TN patients 19 were responders and 7 were non-responders to MVD surgery.  MVD Responder and non-responder groups and TN and HC groups did not differ in sex distribution or side of pain (where applicable) found through a Fisher’s Exact Test. Mean age at surgery was found to be significantly greater in MVD responders (56.76 ± 10.21 years) as compared to non-responders (44.80 ± 6.48 years) (p = 0.0020). Accordingly age was added as a covariate within further analyses comparing the two groups. The mean age of the TN cohort and HC cohort were not significantly different. 

Further correlational analysis to determine if age was linearly correlated with structure size/thickness in the TN Group was done with Spearman’s rank correlation. We found the ipsilateral thalamus, ipsilateral cingulate, contralateral cingulate and ipsilateral insula correlated negatively with age (i.e., as age increases structure volume/thickness decreases) at an alpha of 0.05; Spearman r: -0.4767, -0.4935, - 0.4213, - 0.4062 respectively; p = 0.0104, 0.0321, 0.0395, 0.0138 respectively. For the sake of uniformity age was used as a covariate in all ANCOVAs and marginal means (following correction for age) were graphed. 

We also developed correlational matrices with all studied structures for each group (TN Responder, Non-Responder, HC) to determine any inter-structure correlations. Excluding from analysis ipsilateral/contralateral correlation for the same structure, we found notably strong (r > 0.7, r < -0.7) and statistically significant correlations between the contralateral thalamus/contralateral hippocampus and ipsilateral thalamus/contralateral hippocampus in TN responders (r: 0.733, 0.728 respectively), (p = 0.0003, 0.0004 respectively). In non-responders we found strong correlation between the contralateral amygdala/ipsilateral thalamus, contralateral amygdala/contralateral thalamus and contralateral cingulate/contralateral amygdala (r: 0.857, 0.821, -0.786 respectively), (p = 0.024, 0.034, 0.048 respectively). In HC there were no strong correlations. In all groups there were several significant weak correlations ( 0 < r < 0.05, 0 > r > - 0.05). 

Paired t-tests performed for ipsilateral/contralateral to side of pain analysis found that there was a significant difference in the thickness of the primary somatosensory cortex (S1) (2.018 ± 0.110 mm ipsilateral vs 2.129 ± 0.275 mm contralateral, p = 0.0474). Unpaired t-tests between TN patients and controls found a significant difference in the ipsilateral thalamus (7941 ± 951.49 mm³ in TN and 7506 ± 742.71 mm³ in HC, p = 0.0380). Differences were also found in the ipsilateral S1 with TN patients having a thinner S1 than HC (2.018 ± 0.110 mm vs 2.102 ± 0.135 mm respectively, p = 0.0095). When comparing Responder and Non-Responder groups, after correcting for age we found that the contralateral cingulate was significantly larger in responders compared to non-responders (2.477 ± 0.215 mm vs 2.272 ± 0.142 mm respectively, p = 0.0285). 

In the 3 different classification models we tested we found the Logistic + LASSO used ipsilateral insula as the optimal predictor variable for response to surgery and it had an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.816 when using the entire data set and 0.625 when using 80% of the data as a training set and 20% as a test set. The Random forest model used ipsilateral amygdala and ipsilateral insula and had an AUC of 1 for 100% of the data and 0.75 for 80/20 test. The XG booster used ipsilateral amygdala, ipsilateral cingulate, ipsilateral insula, ipsilateral S1, contralateral thalamus and contralateral cingulate. It had an AUC of 1 for 100% of the data and 0.875 for the 80/20 test.

Conclusion

This is a relatively large, single-centre, retrospective assessment of prospectively collected structural brain imaging data in 26 CTN patients undergoing virgin MVD surgery (19 responders and 7 non-responders) and 20 HC. We focused on determining structural volumetric characteristics of the amygdala, hippocampus, and thalamus and thickness measurements of the cingulate, insula and S1—pain-relevant structures—in these groups. 

We found that the ipsilateral S1 was on average thinner in CTN compared to HC, largely driven by a significantly thinner S1 thickness ipsilateral to the side of pain in CTN patients. This may suggest atrophy in the S1 cortex contributing to the chronification of TN pain. We found additional differences between the ipsilateral thalamus with TN patients having larger ipsilateral thalami as compared to controls. This may suggest an impaired relay system for pain signals to the appropriate cortices. TN MVD responders also had a mean thicker contralateral cingulate as compared to TN MVD non-responders. This suggests that perhaps a thicker cingulate is a positive adaptation that can contribute to overall surgical response.

We also looked at inter-structure correlation to establish whether any of the areas studied are related via coordinated structural change with another area. While the resulting correlations were different between participant subgroups (TN responder, TN non-responder and HC) an interesting thing to note is that there were fewer correlations in TN responders and HC compared to non responders. This suggests that there are correlated structural changes that occur in the brains of non-responders perhaps associated in some way with non-response.

Using advanced classification modeling techniques we were able to predict response outcome based on pre-operative scans using a variety of predictors with moderate to excellent certainty. A prominent risk of robust models is a tendency to overfit to the data, thereby presenting exaggerated predicting power. While there are mechanisms implicit to each of these algorithms aiming to prevent overfitting, we further minimized the risk of overfitting by splitting the data into a training set and a test set. We also found some interesting correlations between structure volume/thickness and age, most notably in the ipsilateral thalamus, ipsilateral and contralateral cingulate and ipsilateral insula, while these results remain underexplored, this presents a potential area for further research. 

There are many other limitations to this study and one must be careful to make any firm conclusions from this study alone. Due to the observational retrospective study design all of the data comes from patient records which makes it difficult to generalize to different patient groups with potentially different baseline characteristics and can only reliably demonstrate correlation (rather than causation). It is worth mentioning that we had a sample size of 26 TN patients (19 responders and 7 non-responders) which while above average for TN studies remains fairly small and an obvious limitation to the generalizability of these findings. Future research may benefit from a larger patient cohort, specifically with a larger surgically non-responsive group of patients. 

The presence of chronic pain in TN may suggest that there are differences in brain subcortical and cortical areas, important for somatosensation and nociception that can contribute to the etiology. We studied the differences in volume/thickness of 6 gray matter structures in TN patients (MVD responders and non-responders) and HC. We showed that there are differences in subcortical structure volume and cortical thickness in between these 3 cohorts and that we are able to predict response to surgery moderately well using a variety of classification models. These findings suggest that response to surgery in medically refractory TN may partially depend on limbic structures involved in chronic pain.
