Introduction

Freezing of Gait (FOG) represents one of the most enigmatic and debilitating features of Parkinson’s disease (PD), affecting over 50% of advanced patients. FOG is characterized by an episodic inability to generate effective forward stepping, devastatingly impacts mobility and quality of life in PD. Despite its profound clinical burden, the neural mechanisms underlying FOG remain unclear. The prevailing "dopamine depletion" model fails to account for the phenomenon's resistance to levodopa and its paradoxical occurrence during the "ON" medication state. Although traditional neuroimaging approaches have revealed that complimentary yet competing neural network communications might underlie the pathophysiology of FOG, the proposed mechanism failed to capture momentary failures of the impaired brain to transition between specific functional states1. 

Recent advances in statistical physics and network neuroscience offer a paradigm shift by viewing the brain as a complex dynamical system that navigates a high-dimensional "Energy Landscape." 2, 3 In this framework, brain dynamics are modeled as a trajectory moving across an energy surface, where "valleys" (local minima) represent stable attractor states (e.g., motor execution or cognitive processing). Specifically, we posit that this dynamic rigidity is not a global failure, but is mechanistically driven by the topological breakdown of specific basal ganglia nodes. We propose that functional failure in some nodes decouples the basal ganglia from global network efficiency, trapping the brain in a fragmented "conflict state" that manifests clinically as freezing. This study aimed to decode symptom-specific spatiotemporal features of FOG and identify topological targets for potential treatment using neuromodulation.

Methods

2.1. Participants

The study enrolled 208 participants, comprising 127 patients with idiopathic PD and 81 age- and sex-matched healthy controls (HC). PD diagnoses were confirmed via UK Brain Bank criteria. Patients were stratified into PD with Freezing of Gait (PD-FOG, n=46) and without FOG (PD-noFOG, n=81) based on the FOG Questionnaire (FOG-Q) and standardized video assessment. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Review Board of Anhui Medical University (No. 5101006; PJ 2022-13-55), with written informed consent obtained from all participants.

2.2. MRI acquisition and preprocessing

Structural and functional MRI data were acquired on a 3.0 T GE Discovery 750 scanner. High-resolution T1-weighted images were obtained via a 3D BRAVO sequence (voxel size = 1x 1 x 1 mm3). Resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) was acquired in two runs (TR/TE = 2400/30 ms; voxel size = 3 x 3 x 3 mm3; 434 volumes for 2 runs) to maximize state sampling. Preprocessing utilizing DPABI and SPM12 included slice timing, realignment, MNI normalization, and spatial smoothing (6-mm FWHM). Nuisance regression (Friston-24 parameters, white matter, CSF signals), linear trend removal, and band-pass filtering (0.01–0.08 Hz) were applied to minimize artifacts.

2.3. Network definition and signal extraction

We employed a hybrid parcellation strategy to define eight functionally distinct brain networks (Figure 1A). Cortical regions were defined using 214 regions of interest (ROIs) from the Power 264 atlas, excluding 50 uncertain/subcortical nodes. These ROIs were assigned to seven canonical networks: Attention (ATN), Salience (SAN), Fronto-parietal (FPN), Default Mode (DMN), Visual (VN), Auditory (AUN), and Somato-motor (SMN) networks2. An eighth Basal Ganglia (BG) network was defined according to previous studies4. BOLD signals were averaged across ROIs within each network to generate representative time series for 8 networks.

2.4. Energy landscape analysis (ELA)

To characterize non-linear brain dynamics, we performed ELA using the following pipeline2:

2.4.1 Pairwise Maximum Entropy Model (MEM): For each participant, network time series were binarized based    on the mean signal threshold (active +1, inactive -1) (Figure 1B). We fitted a pairwise MEM to the concatenated binary data from both runs to estimate the probability distribution P(Vk) of all possible brain states (28 = 256 states). The energy E(Vk) of each state was calculated, where lower energy indicates a higher probability of occurrence.

2.4.2 Local Minima and Basins: We constructed a disconnectivity graph to identify "Local Minima" (attractor states)—energy valleys lower than all adjacent states (Hamming distance = 1). The "Basin" of each local minimum was defined as the set of states that converge to it (Figure 1C).

2.4.3 Dynamic Indices: To quantify dynamics, we computed three indices based on empirical data: Basin size (the frequency of occurrence of all states belonging to a specific basin), Duration time (the continuous duration the brain remains within a specific state) and Transition Frequency (the probability of switching between specific major states, direct or indirect).

2.5. Topological and statistical analysis

State-specific weighted networks were constructed for identified pathological states (e.g., Minor State #2). Graph theoretical metrics (Global/Local Efficiency, Modularity, Nodal Degree and Efficiency) were calculated using DPABINet. Statistical analyses were performed in JASP. Group differences in dynamic and topological metrics were assessed using ANCOVA, adjusting for age, sex, education, framewise displacement (FD), and levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD). Post-hoc comparisons utilized Bonferroni correction. Spearman’s rank correlation assessed associations with FOG-Q scores (Figure 1D). Mediation analysis tested whether structural topology (e.g., global efficiency) mediated the relationship between nodal degree and state dynamics (p < 0.05, two-tailed) (Figure 1E, F).

Results

3.1 Energy landscape architecture and identification of dominant brain states

To characterize the dynamic repertoire of brain network activity, we fitted a pairwise maximum entropy model (MEM) to the resting-state fMRI data. The model demonstrated high fidelity, achieving a fitting accuracy of > 99% and a correlation coefficient (r) > 0.99 between empirical and model energy values across all participants (HC, PD-noFOG, and PD-FOG). This robust fitting allowed for the construction of reliable energy landscapes. We identified four stable brain states (local energy minima) consistent across groups: Major State #1, characterized by co-activation of the FPN and DMN; Major State #2, dominated by ATN, VN, AUN, and SMN activations; Minor State #1, VN-DMN co-activation; and Minor State #2, representing a complex and high energy-demanding intermediate state involving the BG, ATN, SAN, FPN, AUN and SMN (Figure 2A, B).

3.2 Aberrant brain dynamics characterized by "state trapping" in PD-FOG

Quantitative analysis of dynamic metrics revealed a symptom-specific "trapping" phenomenon in the PD-FOG cohort. The PD-FOG group exhibited a distinct dynamic signature characterized by significantly increased basin size and duration in the intermediate Minor State #2 (motor planning and execution state) compared to both HC and PD-noFOG groups (both p < 0.001, Bonferroni corrected) (Figure 2D). Concurrently, the size and duration of the Major State #2 were significantly reduced in PD-FOG patients (Figure 2C). Transition analysis further elucidated this rigidity: PD-FOG patients displayed a significantly reduced frequency of direct transitions between the two major states (Major State #1 - Major State #2). 

3.3 Correlation analysis between brain dynamic metrics and clinical symptoms of PD-FOG

We investigated whether these altered dynamics were behaviorally relevant. In the PD-FOG cohort, the duration of Minor State #2 was positively correlated with FOG-Q scores (r = 0.38, p = 0.013), 5-meter Timed Up and Go (5m TUG) time (r = 0.43, p = 0.004), and turning time (SS180) (r = 0.35, p = 0.023), controlling for sex, age, education and LEDD (Figure 2E). These findings indicate that prolonged retention in this specific intermediate state is closely linked to all clinical measurements of FOG symptoms.

 

3.4 Associations between network topology and brain state dynamics

To decipher the neural mechanisms underlying the pathological trap in Minor State #2, we analyzed network topology specifically within this state’s time windows. The PD-FOG group exhibited a segregated and inefficient topology during Minor State #2, characterized by significantly lower global efficiency (Eglob) and higher modularity compared to controls (p < 0.001, Bonferroni corrected) (Figure 2F, G). Partial correlation analysis revealed that prolonged Minor State #2 duration was associated with reduced global integration (Eglob AUC), nodal degree and efficiency of the right pallidum (Figure 2H-J). Finally, mediation analysis uncovered a complete mediation pathway: the structural-functional disruption of the right pallidum (degree) influences the pathological trapping of Minor Sate #2 via the effect on global network efficiency reductions (Figure 2K). This may suggest that pallidal dysfunction precipitates a breakdown in global integration, causing the brain to become "stuck" in the intermediate state, manifesting clinically as freezing behavior.

Conclusion

This study presents the first whole-brain energy landscape characterization of Freezing of Gait (FOG), fundamentally redefining FOG as a dynamical state-transition disorder. We demonstrate that FOG is not merely a static disconnection or functional decoupling, but a dynamic failure where the brain becomes sequestered in a specific "high-energy trap" (Minor State #2). This pathological state represents a deadlock between motion-initiating (BN and SMN) and conflict-monitoring (Salience/FPN) networks where sensory inputs are possibly further overwhelming the system. 

Our findings provide a mechanistic framework explaining how static network deficits translate into dynamic dysfunction in PD. Although we identified widespread topological alterations, the mediation analysis reveals a specific hierarchical pathway: the structural integrity of the right pallidum is critical for maintaining efficient whole-brain communication (Eglob AUC). When the pallidum loses its role as a functional hub (decreased degree), it precipitates a decline in global integration. This loss of integration, in turn, seemingly raises the energy transition barrier, causing the brain to become "trapped" in the pathological Minor State #2. 

In FOG, the functional erosion of the pallidum leads to network fragmentation (low efficiency, high modularity), leaving the brain unable to overcome the energy barrier required to exit the conflict state and initiate movement. These findings have direct translational implications for neuromodulation. Current therapies often target general excitability; our results suggest that effective treatment might be benefited by targeting the stabilization of network dynamics. Specifically, the right globus pallidum emerges as a critical, spatially-specific target. Future closed-loop Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS) or other non-invasive protocols for deep brain regions (e.g. focused ultrasound or temporal interference techniques) should aim to detect the onset of the "trap" state and deliver stimulation specifically designed to restore right pallidal connectivity or lower the energy barrier to the action state, thereby releasing the PD-FOG’s brain from its frozen dynamics.
