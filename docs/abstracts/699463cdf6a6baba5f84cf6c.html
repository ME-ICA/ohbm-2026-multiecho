Introduction

Analytical diffusion models are limited in their ability to capture the full complexity of brain tissue microstructure, as they are describing simple geometries such as spheres, sticks or cylinders. This motivates the use of numerical phantoms that can be flexibly tuned to varying levels of realism. Existing phantoms predominantly mimic white matter and typically rely on triangular meshes, which are computationally demanding and difficult to construct while preserving realistic geometries. To overcome these limitations, we developed a software tool that generates realistic neuronal or glial cells using overlapping spheres as building blocks, as implemented in,1,2  significantly reducing computational cost. 

However, growing biologically plausible structures introduces its own challenges, particularly when attempting to preserve key histological features such as undulation, beading and branching order. This raises a critical question: which microstructural features are truly detectable with preclinical or clinical MRI scanners? Identifying these features enables simplifications in substrate generation while preserving relevant geometry. 

Through Monte Carlo simulations in generated realistic cells, we examined the effects of essential morphological characteristics, namely branching, undulation and beading, on the diffusion MRI (dMRI) signal. We quantified the temporal variation of mean diffusivity (MD) and mean kurtosis (MK) across cells with different geometric complexities at clinically realistic signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). Our findings provide promising insights for non-invasive characterisation of grey matter microstructure, as we find that branching strongly impacts the dMRI signal. Hence, our results inform the design of more complete dMRI models with potential applications in assessing neuronal and glial morphologies in vivo.

Methods

Numerical substrates of astrocyte-like cells were grown as ensembles of overlapping spheres, with consecutive sphere centers spaced at one-quarter of their radius, using in-house C++ software (Fig. 1A). Monte Carlo simulations3 of water diffusion within these cells were performed using a modified MCDC simulator to accommodate overlapping spheres.2 

Each digital cell consisted of a soma with a 4 μm radius and seven primary processes, connected to the soma via a funnel of continuously decreasing process radii. The decrease was exponential along the funnel length following R=R0e-kx, with k=L-1log(R1R0-1), R1=2, R0=0.8 and L=4 μm.1,2 Beyond this funnel, the mean process radius was 0.8 μm. We grew two types of cells (non-branched and branched) at four levels of process complexity: straight, beaded, undulated and undulated+beaded. The processes of the non-branched cells were 70 μm long. In contrast, the primary processes of branched cells split into two subprocesses twice in a row, yielding seven segments of 10 μm length each. The branching angle was 45°. By keeping the soma size, number of primary processes, and funnel parameters identical across all configurations, we ensured that the soma-related effects and the exchange between soma and processes contributed equally to each condition. We also maintained the total process length constant between branched and non-branched configurations, resulting in a similar overall process volume for all generated cells. In each configuration, five cell skeletons were grown to capture variability arising from random growth, including beading, undulation, and the directions of the subbranches (Fig. 1A). For undulation, we randomly varied the growth direction to achieve an overall tortuosity of about 1.25, as expected from histology.5 Beading of cell processes was modeled as an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process varying the branch radii, parametrised by a coefficient of variation of 0.2 and a correlation length of 1 μm. 

Simulation parameters were: simulation time (t) of 127 ms, free water diffusion coefficient (D) of 2 µm2 s-1, and N=15500 time steps, leading to a step size (2DtN-1)-0.5 of 0.18 μm. From each simulation run, signals corresponding to diffusion times (td = Δ - 𝛿/3) of 20 to 100 ms in steps of 10 ms were obtained by truncating the full walker trajectories at the corresponding lengths. Each cell configuration was evaluated over five repetitions for a Pulse-Gradient Spin Echo sequence with δ=16.5ms, TE=127ms, b=0, 0.2, and 1 to 10 ms μm-2 in steps of 1 ms μm-2, and 128 directions isotropically sampled on the sphere. 

The convergence of the Monte Carlo simulation was benchmarked on one branched substrate for different intra-cellular walker densities ranging from 0.71 to 36.8 walkers μm-3. Each density was evaluated over 5 repetitions and the optimal density (22.1 walkers μm-3) for signal convergence and computational efficiency was retained (data not shown). 

Numerical simulations were then run 5 times on all cell substrates with these parameters. In parallel, we calculated theoretical estimates of the diffusion signal in an impermeable sphere compartment and a set of randomly-oriented sticks (ROS) based on our substrate parametrisation, and compared it to the powder-averaged numerical dMRI signal. To study the signature of non-branched versus branched cell structure, we fitted the DTI model to b = 0, 0.2 and 1 ms μm-2 measurements across 128 directions to get the mean diffusivity (MD), and the DKI model to b = 0, 0.2, 1, 2 and 3 ms μm-2 measurements across 128 directions to get the mean kurtosis (MK). Gaussian noise with σ = 1/SNR was added on the real and imaginary parts of the signals, using a SNR of 30. All computations were performed on a computer cluster using 1 node (2x24 Cores, 1.5 GHz).

Results

Fig. 2A shows the signal decay for branched cell structures with the four complexity levels (straight, beaded, undulated and undulated+beaded) for a short and a long Δ (Δ1 = 25.5 and Δ2 = 100.5 ms). The analytical solution of diffusion in sphere+sticks is also depicted as a reference. For both Δ, the signal magnitude followed the order undulated+beaded > beaded ~ undulated > straight. This ordering reflects increased diffusion restriction in the undulated and beaded configurations relative to the straight geometry. At intermediate b-values (3-4 ms μm-2), the analytical solution in sphere+sticks underestimated the numerical signal decay, while it overestimated it for higher b-values. At short Δ, all simulated signals converge towards smaller signal values than at long Δ. The higher signal amplitude at longer Δ was consistent with sensitivity to restriction inside a bounded impermeable cell.

Figure 2B highlights that differences between the non-branched and branched configurations are most pronounced at low b-values (1–3 ms μm⁻²) for both Δ. At Δ1, all complexity levels exhibited similar trends, with the undulated case displaying the largest difference between branched and non-branched configurations. At Δ2, the differences due to branching were larger (peaking at 7.5% variation), and their attenuation with higher b-values was split into two plateaus at b > 3 ms μm-2. Without undulation, the signature of branching remained marked throughout, while with undulation, it was more rapidly attenuated. Using Einstein’s equation (Fig. 1B), the free mean squared displacement of water molecules in 1D was 9 and 20 μm at Δ1 and Δ2, respectively, while the time to cross one segment (10 μm) was 25 ms. Consequently, at longer Δ, where water displacements exceed individual segment lengths, the dMRI signal is expected to be more sensitive to branching, consistent with the trends observed at Δ₂. A similar trend is, to some extent, also anticipated at the shorter Δ, as the diffusion length is already on the order of the segment length. 

We then fitted DTI and DKI models to the simulated dMRI data at an SNR of 30 (Fig. 2C). Because the primary processes were arranged isotropically around the soma, fractional anisotropy remained low (around 0.1), and we therefore focused on MD and MK. MD decreased with increasing Δ in both branched and non-branched configurations. It also decreased with increasing geometric complexities, following the trend MDstraight > MDundulated ~ MDbeaded > MDundulated+beaded. The MD values were lower in the branched versus non-branched cases. These results confirmed that branching and all process morphologies hindered diffusion. In the non-branched case, MK displayed a non-linear behavior: it initially increased with Δ before plateauing and slightly decreasing after an inflection point around Δ = 80 ms. In the branched case, MK displayed a much more marked non-monotonic trend: it similarly increased with Δ up to 50 ms, after which it sharply decreased to values similar to or lower than those observed at short Δ. At long Δ, the undulated and undulated+beaded configurations exhibited substantially lower MK than the straight or branched cases.  

Conclusion

In this work, we examined how cell arborisation and process morphologies influence the dMRI signal at short and long Δ. Our results highlighted the limitations of analytical solutions in capturing signal decay in realistic cells, underscoring the value of numerical substrates. Future work will explore shorter Δ and longer cellular processes to determine at what point exchange between branches needs to be included into a suitable analytical diffusion model.

Interestingly, at long Δ, undulation overshadows the effects of beading and branching. Specifically, the signal differences between the branched and non-branched cells were smaller in the undulated and undulated+beaded configurations compared to the straight or beaded cases. This suggests that undulation acts similarly to branching: local deviations in process orientation alter the direction of water diffusion, mimicking directional changes induced at branching points, but on a shorter length scale.

The decrease in MD with both increasing process irregularities and branching can be interpreted as enhanced geometric hindrance, which reduces diffusivity, as previously described.4 At longer Δ, water has more time to probe its surroundings and encounter cell boundaries, leading to further reductions in MD. Similarly, increasing process irregularities first elevate MK by introducing greater diffusivity heterogeneity, but decrease MK after significant exchange between different compartments occurs.

While MK of water diffusion in brain tissue is experimentally reported to increase up to Δ of ~20 ms before decreasing, simulations and measurements of metabolite intracellular diffusion4 report only an MK increase followed by a plateau. In our simulations, MK monotonically increased with Δ up to ~50 ms in non-branched cells, then remained relatively stable, consistent with metabolite diffusion measurements. In branched cells, MK increased with Δ up to ~50 ms, then dropped sharply, consistent with water diffusion measurements. The decrease was even more marked in branched cells with undulated or undulated+beaded processes. This pattern suggests a cumulative effect of branching and undulation that reduces MK as a function of Δ, likely reflecting a more homogenised microenvironment. Experimentally, this homogeneity is achieved faster for water than metabolites which diffuse much more slowly.

The discrepancy between our findings and the simulations in ref4 can likely be attributed to the impact of undulation and process radius on the dMRI signal. In these earlier simulations, the cell span and process volume fraction were kept constant by altering the process radii. This emphasises the importance of generating digital substrates with realistic geometric features. Nonetheless, any simulation results depend on the substrate parametrisation. Here, we chose these parameters to reflect histologically reported astrocyte morphologies,5 and therefore the conclusions may not generalise to other cell types or geometries, which will be the subject of future work.

Our framework provides a powerful platform to investigate how microstructural features shape the dMRI signal. It is particularly relevant for metabolite diffusion MRS (dMRS), which only probes the intracellular space. By extending the present approach to incorporate slower metabolites diffusivities and longer Δ, the methodology becomes directly applicable to dMRS. This simulation framework can be used to optimise acquisition protocols to enhance microstructural sensitivity. 

For water dMRI, it will be important to extend the framework to pack cells into realistic voxels, thereby also creating a realistic extracellular environment. This will allow to model diffusion in both intra- and extracellular compartments and investigate permeative exchange effects.

Overall, our findings highlight the potential of this framework to improve the interpretation of dMRI signals and guide the development of more sensitive microstructural imaging strategies.
