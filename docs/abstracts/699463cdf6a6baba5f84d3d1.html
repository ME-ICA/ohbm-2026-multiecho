Introduction

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) techniques assess perfusion parameters, which can serve as markers of brain vascular health. Among these, dynamic susceptibility contrast (DSC) uses a contrast injection to estimate cerebral blood flow and volume, while cerebrovascular reactivity (CVR) measures vascular responses to stimuli such as CO₂ or breath-hold. Both rely on T2* MRI time series that require substantial post-processing.
 Although software suites such as AFNI and FSL offer tools for building analysis pipelines, they require expertise to use effectively and may not be suitable for perfusion analysis without customization. Integrated open-source solutions for DSC—including automated arterial input function (AIF) extraction—remain limited (Quantiphyse, PerfusionNobel). CVR analysis faces similar challenges, with few complete and user-friendly toolsets available (SeeVR, CVRmap). In most cases, these tools lack interactive time-series and image navigation with instantaneous signal updates.
 We present PerfMRI, an interactive software that guides users through all processing stages with built-in visualization. Developed in Python 3.10, PerfMRI supports DSC and CVR analysis from NIfTI or DICOM input to final maps, without external tools. For DSC, it provides relative and quantitative perfusion metrics and a fully automated AIF module. For CVR, it handles breath-hold or CO₂ paradigms, synchronizes stimulus waveforms, and produces maps of CVR magnitude, lag, correlation, and contrast-to-noise. The software accommodates fast clinical workflows and customizable research analyses via an advanced-options text file. It also exports intermediate and final results, including time courses.

This abstract presents the workflow and main components of PerfMRI and validates its functionality using perfusion maps generated from DSC and CVR data in healthy subjects.

Methods

Software description (see Figure 1)

Preprocessing

In PerfMRI, the preprocessing pipeline includes the following steps: slice-timing correction, motion correction, signal detrending and scaling, spatial and temporal smoothing.  The MR signal is converted to concentration time curve for DSC (C(t) = ΔR2* = -(1/TE)∗log(S(t)/S0)), and BOLD time series for CVR (BOLD(t) = 100∗(S(t)-S0)/S0), S0 being the mean baseline signal.

DSC Analysis 

Relative perfusion metrics

Relative perfusion metrics include area under the curve (AUC), maximum concentration (CMAX), smoothness SMS=100∗[(1/CMAC)*ΣC''(t)]-1, signal recovery (SR), first moment (FM) and several time parameters measured along the concentration curve using a vectorized function to avoid voxel loop and improve processing speed up.  For a chosen percentage (p) of the maximum bolus, it generates the associated sub-TR onset (TON,p) and offset time (TOFF,p).  In this way, several parameters are calculated: blood arrival time: BAT = TON,15, full-width-half-max: FWHM = TOFF,50 – TON,50, and time-to-peak: TTP = (TON,95+TOFF,95)/2.

Relative cerebral blood volume (rCBV)

CBV is calculated by scaling the AUC map with its 99th percentile.   However, since the relationship between concentration and ΔR2* in vascular voxels might not be linear, we have to consider CBV as a relative measure.

DSC Arterial input function (AIF)1

The AIF is then generated using a series of maps thresholding where the remaining voxels from the previous thresholding, are fed to the next as follows:

  1. |SR| < 20% 
  2. SMS > 25th percentile
  3. CMAX > 10th percentile
  4. TON,95 < pth percentile
  5. TTP < pth percentile
  6.  TOFF,95 < pth percentile

p = 100*(Nf/Nint)1/3

Nf : Final #voxels (user input), Nint : #voxels after CMAX thresholding  

The average AIF (mAIF) of the remaining Nf voxels is then scaled AIFs = mAIF∗(AUC99/AUCmAIF)

rCBF, MTT and Tmax

Using standard or block-circulant single value decomposition, C(t) is deconvolved by sAIF to obtain the residue function (R(t)).  rCBF and Tmax are respectively the peak, and time-to-peak of R(t).  MTT is the AUC of R(t) divided by its peak.

CVR Analysis:

Construct and import stimulus.

For the CVR analysis, a one-dimensional time series can either be imported from a physiological recording such as the end-tidal CO2 or constructed as an ON/OFF timing in the case of a breath-hold (BH) experiment.

Stimulus position and Regression

CVR magnitude (CVRm), correlation (Corr) and contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) maps are calculated using a linear regression of the BOLD signal by the stimulus (stim(t)).  The stimulus can be shifted in time with instantaneous maps calculation and display.

Lag and response time (RT)

Lag is calculated using best correlation with multiple shifted versions of the stimulus: stim(t-LAGi) , whereas RT uses multiple convolved versions of the stimulus1: stim(t) ⊗ exp(-t/RTi) with LAGi and RTi  going from 0 to T every dt (defaults: T=8s, dt=0.2s).

CVR AIF and Deconvolution

For CVR, the thresholding cascade is:

  1. Corr < 0
  2. LAG < 0.4s (default)
  3. |Corr| > pth percentile
  4. |CVRm| > pth percentile  

p =  100*(Nf/Nint)1/2 

Nf : Final #voxels (user input), Nint : #voxels after LAG thresholding 

The BOLD signal is then averaged across boluses using an automated tool, transformed to absolute values to flip the negative voxels and converted to concentration.  The AIF is extracted by averaging across the AIF mask.  rCBV, rCBF, MTT and Tmax are calculated using the same deconvolution procedure as for DSC3.

Experimental data

The whole analysis workflow was tested on 9 subjects with DSC experiment (5mL of 1M gadobutrol injection at 5 mL/s ) on a 3T MRI (50 volumes, TR=1.5s, TE=30ms, voxel size=3mm iso), and 5 subjects with BH experiment (8 cycles of 16s BH, 44s rest) on a 7T (250 volumes, TR=2s,TE=20ms,FA=50, voxel size=2mm iso).

Results

PerfMRI successfully processed all subjects using the complete DSC and CVR pipelines and all generated NIFTI images were automatically saved and organized in separates directories without error. In each technique, we selected one example subject for illustration (Figure 2). 

For DSC (green panel): preprocessing included brain masking, and concentration conversion. All relative (AUC, CMAX, BAT, T50,ON, T50,OFF, TTP, FWHM, FM) and quantitative (rCBV, MTT, rCBF) perfusion maps were generated successfully, with the expected range of values and contrast.  Due to space constraints, only TTP, rCBV, MTT and rCBF are displayed.  Panel A shows the time series of the brain average (green, thick line), 50 AIF voxels (multi colors, thin lines) and its average (magenta, think line) along with their locations (B and C) generated from the automated AIF extraction tool.  B and C demonstrate that the spatial distribution of AIF voxels aligned with major feeding arteries.  In addition, the tool was used to compare arterial clusters of 20 and 50 voxels, confirming the expected sharper 20-voxel AIF (D).  For the deconvolution, both the standard and block circulant SVD were tested yielding very small difference in MTT and rCBF.  

For CVR (blue panel): preprocessing included brain masking, detrending, baseline scaling, and spatial/temporal smoothing. Panel E shows one voxel’s response (orange), the brain average (green), and the breath-hold stimulus (red). Results of the linear regression of the BOLD signal against the stimulus yields CVRm, CNR, and LAG maps with the expected range of values. All boluses were averaged (F; orange matches the voxel in E) effectively supressing temporal noise. The use of the CVRm, Corr, and LAG maps in the selection algorithm correctly identify 50 AIF voxels with sharp characteristics.  As expected, negatively correlated AIF voxels lie near the ventricles1.

From NIFTI files import to final maps, analysis took less than 2min for DSC and less than 5 min for CVR.

Conclusion

In this study, we used a set of healthy subjects to demonstrate the capability of PerfMRI to calculate accurate CVR and DSC parametric maps, identify AIF voxels within major arterial vessels, and generate the corresponding quantitative maps.

PerfMRI was designed to analyze MRI DSC and CVR data from standard preprocessing through to the final relative and quantitative maps. In developing the tool, we emphasized real-time visual feedback, an intuitive interface, and advanced options suitable for research applications.
