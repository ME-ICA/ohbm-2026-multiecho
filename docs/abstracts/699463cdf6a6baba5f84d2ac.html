Introduction

The neocortex is traditionally considered a slow-learning system, with memory consolidation occurring gradually through repeated reactivation driven by the hippocampus 1. However, recent evidence suggests that neocortical engrams can form rapidly. Brodt et al. (2018) demonstrated that repeatedly encoding and retrieving novel associations can rapidly induce lasting and behaviorally relevant functional and microstructural changes, particularly in higher-order association cortex. However, episodic memory engages a distributed network that also includes sensory areas. While memory engrams have been found inprimary auditory cortex2, a role of early visual cortex (EVC) in long-term memory has remained elusive/highly debated/is less well known.

The EVC is increasingly implicated in memory processing beyond its established role in sensory encoding. Human neuroimaging shows that the EVC can sustain high-resolution working memory representations while processing new inputs 3. Furthermore, studies investigating perceptual learning observe experience-induced plasticity changes in the EVC after extended time periods and repetitions 4. Emerging evidence from our own research suggests that the EVC may contribute to long-term memory formation as well. Klinkowski et al. (in revision) demonstrated that memory engrams for novel visual concepts can rapidly form within the EVC.

In the present study, we directly investigate the possibility of rapid EVC engram formation by examining memory formation for basic visual features using a novel experimental paradigm designed to induce memory traces specific to basic visual features (color vs. motion direction) in combination with functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and diffusion-weighted MRI (dwMRI)

Methods

Healthy young adult participants (planned N = 80) completed an object-feature association task designed to investigate memory formation specific to features. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two groups: the color group or the motion group. During the encoding phase, both groups were shown 40 line drawings of inanimate objects filled with uniformly colored and moving dots. Depending on their group, participants learned either associations between objects and specific colors (color group) or associations between objects and specific motion directions (motion group).  Colors were isoluminant, with a difference of ΔE*=8.239 between individual colors. Individual motion directions were separated by 12°. Each group learned only one type of feature association, which allowed for the isolation of neural correlates related specifically to color- versus motion-based memory.

During the recall phase, objects were presented with stationary gray dots, and participants were asked to retrieve the previously learned feature (color or motion). Participants could then select their recalled feature and receive feedback. Learning was reinforced through five encoding-recall repetitions per item. Delayed recall sessions were conducted 24 hours later to assess memory retention; these sessions included no feedback to avoid interfering with initial memory formation. Encoding and recall blocks included baseline trails, displaying visually similar line drawings and participants were asked to observe visually and judge how round or edge these looked. See Figure 1 for an overview of the object-feature association task.

Memory formation and retention were tracked using multimodal MRI at 3T. fMRI was acquired during the initial learning and delayed recall sessions. dwMRI was used to assess microstructural plasticity changes before learning, one hour after learning, and 24 hours after learning. This design allows us to examine the neural correlates of feature-specific memory formation during immediate and delayed retrieval, as well as the relationship between behavioral performance and neural activation patterns. Anatomical T1w and T2w images were acquired in the second day 24h after initial learning.

MRI data were acquired on a 3T Siemens Magnetom Prisma scanner with a 64-channel head coil at the MPI for Biological Cybernetics, Tübingen. Functional images were obtained using a multiband EPI sequence (TR = 2000 ms, TE = 30 ms, FA = 72°, 2 mm isotropic, 60 slices, FOV = 208×208 mm², GRAPPA 2, MB 2), along with a B0 field map for distortion correction. A T1-weighted MPRAGE (TR = 2300 ms, TE = 4.18 ms, TI = 900 ms, FA = 9°, 1 mm isotropic, FOV = 256×256×176 mm³, GRAPPA 2×1) was acquired for anatomical reference.

Preprocessing was performed using a custom MATLAB-based pipeline. The T1w image was ACPC-aligned, bias-field corrected, brain-extracted, and nonlinearly registered to MNI space (ANTs). Functional data were motion corrected (MCFLIRT), co-registered to the single-band reference and then to the T1w image with B0 distortion correction (epi_reg), and resampled to MNI152NLin2009cAsym space at 2 mm resolution in a single transform step. Images were then intensity-normalized, smoothed with a 4-mm FWHM Gaussian kernel, and high-pass filtered at 0.01 Hz.

Whole-brain first- and second-level GLM analyses were implemented in Nilearn (version 0.12.1). First-level models included task regressors convolved with the canonical hemodynamic response function, alongside motion parameters as nuisance regressors. Resulting subject-level maps were entered into second-level random-effects analyses to assess group differences. Statistical maps were corrected for multiple comparisons using FDR cluster correction, and significant clusters were localized using the HCPex parcellation 5.
 

Results

Behavioral Results. Preliminary analyses (n = 39) showed clear learning across repetitions, indicated by reduced numerical distance of the selected feature to the correct feature (F(4,148) = 30.13, p < 0.001), with no performance differences between groups (F(1,37) = 0.016, p = 0.899). 24 h recall was significantly better than after the first, but slightly lower than after the last repetition of the encoding task (T(38) = 6.26, p < 0.001; T(38) = –2.40, p = 0.021). Again, there were no group differences (T(37) = –0.88, p = 0.38). See Figure 2A.

fMRI Results. First, we assessed functional activity during recall, where both groups were presented with similar visual stimuli, not perceiving any color or motion information. When comparing recall-related activity between groups, the motion group showed higher activation in large bilateral clusters along the visual processing streams, including the inferior parietal cortex, lateral occipital cortex, superior parietal lobule, precuneus, and supramarginal gyrus (Z_peak = 7.06, cluster size = 112024 mm³; p_FDR < 0.05). Notably, activation also extended into sensory motion processing area MST bilaterally (Right: Z_peak = 3.55; Left: Z_peak = 4.48; p_FDR < 0.05). The color group showed no significant clusters with higher activity in color-selective regions.

To assess rapid changes in functional activity occurring with learning, we computed the contrast Recall Repetition 5 > Recall Repetition 1. Comparing learning specific activity between groups revealed higher activity in a significant cluster with a peak in left MST (Z_peak = 3.59, cluster size = 64 mm³; p_uncorr < 0.001; Figure 2B). Recall activity within this cluster increased linearly with repetitions specifically in the motion group (r_mean = 0.72, T(19) = 8.92, p < 0.001) but not the color group (r_mean = 0.19, T(18) = 1.72, p = 0.102), with a significant group difference (T(30.12) = –3.20, p = 0.003; Figure 2C). 

Indicating that learning-induced changes remained stable across the 24-hour offline interval, recall activity after 24h mirrored the initial recall pattern: here the motion group again showed higher activity in a large cluster including bilateral MST (Right: Z_peak = 4.94; Left: Z_peak = 5.18; p_FDR < 0.05). No significant Color > Motion clusters were found.

Activity levels within the learning-specific cluster (Recall 5 > 1, Motion group > Color) were behaviorally relevant, as evidenced by a significant correlation with recall performance in the motion group (r_mean = –0.46, T(19) = –6.52, p < 0.001), but not in the color group (r_mean = –0.16, T(18) = –1.35, p = 0.194). This suggests that activity in motion-sensitive cortex specifically supports memory recall of motion direction. However, it is important to note that with this preliminary sample, group differences did not reach significance (T(24.14) = 1.27, p = 0.218; Figure 2D).

Conclusion

These preliminary findings suggest that rapid memory formation for basic visual features may critically depend on feature-sensitive (early) visual cortex. Behaviorally, participants showed clear evidence of learning and stable retention over 24 hours, demonstrating that the novel paradigm effectively induces feature-specific memory traces. Neurally, motion-feature learning consistently engaged parietal and occipital regions, including the bilateral motion-processing area MST, with these changes remaining stable even after 24 hours. Additionally, repetition-related increases in functional activity within MST, coupled with their association to recall performance, highlight the behavioral relevance of these neural changes in this sensory region.

These findings extend prior work on rapid neocortical engrams in higher-order association cortex to the domain of simple visual features. They also align well with emerging evidence suggesting a genuine role for the visual cortex in long-term memory.

As these analyses reflect only the functional component of an ongoing multimodal dataset, the future integration of diffusion-weighted MRI will allow us to assess whether the observed functional changes are accompanied by changes in microstructural plasticity. Together, the developing dataset promises to clarify how perceptual and memory systems interact during rapid learning and the extent to which early visual areas contribute to forming and stabilizing new feature-based engrams.
