Introduction

Hemispheric asymmetry and anterior-posterior (AP) gradients of degeneration are hallmark spatial features of Parkinson’s disease (PD). These patterns are central to models of disease progression and clinical presentation. In vivo, they are mostly assessed using dopaminergic PET or SPECT imaging (e.g., DaTSCAN), which captures hemispheric differences and posterior-dominant striatal involvement1,2. Although sensitive and clinically informative, these techniques require radiotracer exposure, are costly, and offer limited spatial resolution for subregional analysis within basal ganglia nuclei.

Recent MRI studies have shown that quantitative and semi-quantitative MRI can capture AP gradients and posterior putamen (PP) asymmetry in PD. These MRI-derived features have also been linked to dopaminergic and motor symptom asymmetry3,4. However, prior work has focused mainly on the striatum. It remains unclear whether similar spatial features can be detected across the broader basal ganglia system using routine clinical MRI contrasts. In addition, MRI signal variability across imaging centers, sessions, and subjects limits comparative analyses without proper harmonization.

Here, we investigated whether harmonized, widely available T1-weighted (T1w), T2-weighted (T2w), and proton density-weighted (PDw) MRI can detect PD-related AP gradients in the putamen and globus pallidus externa (GPe). We further examined whether MRI-derived asymmetry measures predict motor symptom lateralization across a comprehensive set of basal ganglia regions, including the substantia nigra (SN), putamen, caudate, nucleus accumbens, globus pallidus subregions, and subthalamic nucleus (STN).

Methods

MRI data were obtained from the Parkinson’s Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI; www.ppmi-info.org), including 526 imaging sessions from PD and healthy control participants (baseline n = 196; 136 PD, 60 HC). Imaging was performed on 3T Siemens Tim Trio scanners in multiple imaging centers using similar protocols (T1w MPRAGE; T2w/PDw turbo spin-echo). Motor symptom asymmetry was computed as the difference between summed left- and right-hemisphere MDS-UPDRS III item scores.

Images were skull-stripped using SynthStrip and rigidly registered to individual T1w space using FSL FLIRT. Intensity harmonization was applied using a custom bias-correction algorithm that models white-matter intensity as a second-order polynomial bias field and normalizes images by division, yielding consistent whole-brain intensity distributions across subjects and visits.

AP gradients were quantified along the first major axis of the putamen and GPe using the mrGrad tool (https://github.com/MezerLab/mrGrad), as described previously [3, 4]. Spatial effects and PD-related alterations were tested using linear mixed-effects models with Position and Position × Group terms. Post hoc localization of significant interactions was performed using mixed-effects models with categorical Position terms to identify positions where PD alterations differed from baseline.

MRI asymmetry indices were computed for each basal ganglia region as the difference between the median left- and right-hemisphere intensities. ROIs included nuclei segmented using FSL FIRST (caudate, putamen, nucleus accumbens, and globus pallidus), the SN segmented using the MASSP atlas nonlinear registration, and subnuclei segmented using DBSegment (GPe, GPi, SNc, SNr, and STN). In addition, posterior subregions identified by the gradient analyses were included as separate ROIs, specifically the PP and posterior GPe (PGPe). Multiple comparisons were controlled using Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction within each analysis.

To integrate information across contrasts, composite images were generated by summing T1w, T2w, and PDw images with contrast-specific signed weights. For each ROI, asymmetry indices were computed from the resulting composite images. At the baseline visit, simple and multiple regression analyses tested associations between regional MRI asymmetries and motor asymmetry. Partial correlation analyses assessed independent contrast contributions. Longitudinal analysis evaluated replication at 12-, 24-, and 48-month visits.

Predictive modeling used subject-wise Monte Carlo cross-validation (30 repetitions). In each repetition, 20% of subjects were held out for testing, while models were trained using a single randomly selected visit per training subject to ensure independence of observations within and between training and test sets, allowing prediction across visits. Statistical significance of predictive performance was assessed using subject-level permutation testing (500 permutations). DaTSCAN asymmetry in the anterior putamen was analyzed for comparison using identical clinical measures.

Results

Intensity harmonization substantially improved consistency across subjects and visits (Dice coefficients across HC subjects at baseline: T1w = 0.35→0.70; T2w = 0.11→0.76; PDw = 0.01→0.71). Robust AP gradients were detected in both the putamen and GPe, with PD-related alterations localized to the PP and PGPe.

Across the basal ganglia regions examined, three ROIs – PP, SN, and PGPe – showed MRI asymmetries significantly associated with motor asymmetry at baseline after FDR correction (Figure 1). In the PP, asymmetries from all three contrasts were significant (R2 up to 0.31). In the SN, T2w and PDw asymmetries were significant (R2 up to 0.21). In the PGPe, T1w and PDw asymmetries were significant (R2 up to 0.22). Multiple-contrast regression and partial correlation analyses confirmed that these contrasts contributed independent variance within each ROI. Asymmetry indices derived from composite (signed-sum) images further increased explained variance (PP: R2 = 0.39; SN: R2 = 0.32; PGPe: R2 = 0.32).

At the baseline visit, a multi-ROI model combining composite-image asymmetry indices from PP, SN, and PGPe explained 56% of motor asymmetry variance (R2 = 0.56, p < 10-23, n = 136), comparable to DaTSCAN asymmetry alone (R² = 0.58). Associations were replicated at 12 months (R2 = 0.39, p < 10-11, n = 106) and 24 months (R2 = 0.35, p < 10-8, n = 89), and partially at 48 months (R2 = 0.43, p < 10-5, n = 48; PGPe contribution not significant). In cross-validated prediction, the multi-ROI MRI model achieved a mean test-set R2 of 0.37 ± 0.12 and a global cross-validated R2 of 0.40 (permutation p = 0.002). DaTSCAN alone showed higher predictive performance (R2 = 0.58), yet adding MRI asymmetries increased explained variance by 5%.

Conclusion

Harmonized multi-contrast MRI captures AP gradients in the putamen and GPe and detects basal ganglia asymmetries related to motor symptom lateralization in PD. Asymmetry was examined across multiple basal ganglia regions, and significant associations found selectively in the PP, PGPe, and SN. This indicates region-specific sensitivity of routine MRI to PD-related spatial pathology.

When asymmetry information was combined across regions, MRI explained motor asymmetry at baseline to a level comparable to DaTSCAN. In predictive models, MRI also provided complementary information beyond dopaminergic imaging. Together, these results show that widely available clinical MRI, when properly harmonized, can capture key spatial features of PD that are typically assessed using PET or SPECT. This supports the use of MRI-based measures as a non-invasive biomarker for PD research and clinical evaluation.
