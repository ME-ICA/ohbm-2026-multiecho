Introduction

The core of the cerebral cortex is organized into a hierarchical structure. One of the canonical structures underlying this hierarchy is the laminar-specific connectivity pattern, which can be quantitatively estimated using the fraction of Supragranular Labeled Neurons (SLN)—a metric that distinguishes feedforward (FF) pathways, originating largely from supragranular layers, from feedback (FB) pathways primarily emerging from infragranular layers. Conventionally, laminar structure is assumed to dictate a specific functional "counterstream" (Markov et al., 2013): FF pathways are characterized as predominantly excitatory drivers, while FB pathways are viewed as modulatory (Fig. 1a). However, the evidence shows that this assumption is often valid only for the local sensory microcircuit, leaving the true relationship between the structural pathways (FF/FB) and their functional effect (excitatory/inhibitory) unclear at the level of large-scale brain networks (Fig. 1b). Here, we interrogate this question using an advanced biologically informed neural mass model. Notably, we employ a framework where the weights of long-range excitatory and inhibitory connections are trainable, allowing the functional effect to be conceptually decoupled from the fixed structural scaffold. We demonstrate that optimizing these weights to capture effective connectivity (EC), not static functional correlation (FC), retrieves long range connection weights that are congruent with the structural underpinning, from which we recover a biologically grounded "Effective Hierarchy." Crucially, this topology distinguishes itself from the rigid structural scaffold; the functional balance of excitation and inhibition is not a strict linear readout of SLN, but rather a distinct configuration locally optimized to minimize the error in directed interactions.

Methods

We constructed a biologically grounded model using data from tract-tracing experiments (Froudist-Walsh et al., 2021) and resting-state fMRI data from 19 macaques (PRIME-DE; (Milham et al., 2018). The structural scaffold is parameterized by the Fraction of Labeled Neurons (FLN), representing the strength of directed connections, and SLN, representing their laminar origin. We simulated whole-brain dynamics using a neural mass model where long-range connections originate exclusively from local excitatory populations and target both excitatory and inhibitory populations of the receiving node (Schirner et al., 2023). This architecture defines two distinct parameters for every edge (Fig. 1c): Long-Range Excitatory (LRE) weights and Long-Range Inhibitory (LRI) weights. These weights were optimized using a gradient descent-based optimization. Specifically, we minimized the error between simulated and empirical metrics—either FC or EC (via Vector Autoregression)—using a push-pull update rule: for under-expressed edges (target > simulated), LRE was increased while LRI was decreased, scaled by the node's global error (determined by root mean square). To characterize the topology of the optimized weights, we introduced the effective functional ratio. This ratio is defined as the proportion of the LRE weight relative to the total functional weight (LRE plus LRI) of that projection (Fig. 1d). We then estimated the Effective Hierarchy by applying a Generalized Linear Model to the ratio matrix, following a similar methodology used to derive structural hierarchy from SLN (Markov et al., 2014).

Results

To determine if function is strictly dictated by the structure, we first benchmarked a “Fixed-SLN” model where weights were set exactly to the laminar origin: LRE = SLN and LRI = 1 - SLN. Enforcing this rigid constraint significantly degraded the model’s ability to reconstruct the empirical targets compared to a simple excitatory only (LRE-only) baseline (p<0.001; Fig. 2), demonstrating that anatomically prescribed inhibition is insufficient to capture complex brain dynamics. We next employed our trainable framework to resolve this mismatch. We found that the optimization target was critical: minimizing error in static FC led to overfitting, whereas optimizing for EC constrained the model to a generalizable regime that reproduced both FC and EC. Crucially, without exposure to anatomical priors, these EC-optimized weights naturally recovered the latent counterstream architecture (Fig. 2d), where LRE scales with feedforward degree and LRI with feedback depth. This confirms that the counterstream motif is not merely a structural artifact, but a dynamical necessity for supporting the brain's directed functional interactions. Detailed profiling of the EC-optimized weights revealed an interesting pattern in the structure-function relationship. We observed a distinct drop in net excitation at the extreme feedforward end (SLN>0.9), suggesting the recruitment of feedforward inhibition to stabilize strong driving inputs (Fig. 2e). Finally, we reconstructed the cortical “effective hierarchy” from the EC-optimized weights, following an established model from Markov et al. (2014). This data-driven estimate exhibited a significant correlation with the anatomical hierarchy (r = 0.46, p < 0.001), confirming that the physical scaffold imposes a necessary topological constraint. However, the substantial deviation highlights that the effective hierarchy is not a fixed linear derivative of anatomy; rather, it is an emergent topology, shaped by the functional re-weighting of pathways (e.g., via feedforward inhibition) to stabilize information flow.

Conclusion

We demonstrate that the brain's effective wiring is not a direct readout of its structure. While the standard fixed-SLN hypothesis fails to capture whole-brain dynamics, optimizing for directed functional interactions reveals that the counterstream motif emerges spontaneously as a flexible statistical principle. This redefines the canonical rules of feedforward-excitation and feedback-inhibition: they are not fixed anatomical constants, but dynamical necessities—functional mechanisms that the brain exploits to stabilize information flow within a fixed structural scaffold.
