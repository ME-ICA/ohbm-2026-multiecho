Introduction

Attempts to subtype schizophrenia using clustering approaches have been limited by two recurring challenges: weak biological validation and minimal clinical or prognostic utility, despite consistent reports of broad patterns such as cortical impoverishment and subcortical enlargement. Consequently, it remains unclear whether schizophrenia is best characterized by discrete, biologically definable subtypes or by continuous dimensions with overlapping structure.

Here, we sought to address that uncertainty–rather than presuming the existence of stable subtypes, we evaluate whether biologically meaningful organizational structure can be identified at all. Clustering solutions are assessed not by their number or visual separability, but by their reproducibility across datasets, biological distinctiveness as quantified by imaging–transcriptomic divergence, and clinical separability (operationalized as the extent to which cluster assignments explain variance in symptom dimensions while controlling for demographic covariates). The objective is therefore not simply to derive additional subgroupings, but to determine whether reproducible, biologically anchored structure exists within psychosis, and at what level of resolution it emerges. Where discrete structure proves insufficient, the framework supports a hybrid representation in which individuals are positioned along biologically informed dimensions while retaining graded membership relative to cluster-defined reference states

Methods

Structural MRI was processed with the CIVET pipeline to derive vertex-wise cortical thickness (CT), surface area (SA), cortical volume (CV), and mean curvature (MC) across 40,892 vertices per hemisphere. After quality control, the combined sample comprised n = 589 patients with schizophrenia across 10 datasets (FBIRN, COBRE, MCIC, BrainGluSchi, NMorphCH, ds030, ds115, NUSDAST, HCP-EP, TOPSY).

To identify reproducible subgroup structure, we implemented a multiverse clustering framework spanning single-modality and multimodal feature sets and K = 2–10 clusters. Solutions were generated in (i) feature space using k-means, PAM, and Ward hierarchical clustering on a PCA-reduced representation (50 components), (ii) affinity space using correlation-derived subject–subject similarity matrices clustered with spectral and Ward (correlation-distance) methods, and (iii) multi-view spectral clustering by fusing affinity views across modalities and across parcellation-defined subregions (von Economo; Yeo7). 

For each solution, clinical separability was assessed within dataset using linear models testing cluster effects on symptom measures while adjusting for age and sex; statistical significance was evaluated using F-tests. Biological distinctiveness was assessed by imaging–transcriptomic decoding: cluster mean cortical maps were correlated with regional gene-expression profiles from the Allen Human Brain Atlas to derive gene-correlation vectors, and transcriptomic separation was summarized on schizophrenia-risk genes using pairwise agreement metrics (Spearman similarity, top-gene Jaccard overlap). Schizophrenia-gene enrichment (Fisher’s exact test) and pathway analysis (fgsea; MSigDB Hallmark and Reactome) were used to contextualize molecular signatures.

Results

We generated 10,440 clustering solutions across 10 independent datasets, spanning multiple algorithms, feature representations, and parameter settings. Across the multiverse, solutions with k ≈ 3–5 showed the strongest and most consistent clinical separation (Fig2a). Multimodal integration increased the mean clinical signal relative to single-modality clustering (Fig2b), and affinity-based (correlation) approaches outperformed feature-space clustering (Fig2c). Whole-cortex approaches outperformed subregional multi-view clustering on average, while subregional (Yeo7) expanded the upper tail of achievable clinical signals (Fig2d). After thresholding for effect size (F > 2), the highest proportion of solutions occurred at k = 2 using whole-cortex, affinity-based clustering (Fig 2e).

Increasing the number of clusters refined the biological underpinnings of clusters, with increasing transcriptomic disagreement (Fig2f). This biological separation was improved by including multiple modalities (Fig2g). Greater transcriptomic divergence between clusters was associated with stronger clinical separation (Fig2h), and explained more variance in clinical symptoms (correlation between jaccard disagreement and mean R2: Pearson’s R=0.21, p < 2.2E-16).

Meta-clustering of imaging-derived clusters revealed two densely populated regions in transcriptomic space, corresponding to clusters with elevated schizophrenia gene enrichment, embedded within a broader continuum of molecular variation (Fig2i). Pathway enrichment analyses further demonstrated that clusters were characterized by distinct biological profiles, with meta-clusters exhibiting pathway-level “fingerprints” spanning mitochondrial/respiratory processes, synaptic and neurotransmission pathways, proteostasis, and immune-related signaling (Fig. 2j–k).

Conclusion

In a multiverse clustering framework, clinically meaningful structure emerged only within a narrow region of the parameter space. Cluster resolutions between K = 3–5, affinity-based representations, and multimodal integration consistently outperformed alternative strategies, indicating that distributed similarity structure—rather than raw feature geometry—more effectively captures clinically relevant variation. Importantly, the observed association between clinical separation and imaging–transcriptomic divergence supports a biological basis for these solutions, linking symptom differentiation to underlying molecular and neurobiological organization. Taken together, these findings argue against a single canonical clustering solution and instead support a unifying framework that ties together reproducibility, biological separation, and clinical relevance as joint criteria for resolving psychosis heterogeneity.
