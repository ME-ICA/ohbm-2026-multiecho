Introduction

Despite thousands of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies reporting grey matter alterations in psychiatric disorders, the field has failed to converge on robust neuroanatomical phenotypes for any specific diagnosis. Hence, it is important to examine whether current practices will ever converge on such a phenotype, which is essential for tracking illness risk, progression, and treatment.  Following our previous work showing low consistency of grey matter alterations in five psychiatric disorders^1, we test here whether it is possible to identify a robust neuroanatomical signature of major depressive disorder (MDD) using the large ENIGMA MDD cohort^2. The large sample size provided by the ENIGMA cohort allowed us to conduct bootstrapping analyses that revealed how the level of cross-site consistency depends on the sample size of individual studies.

Methods

We used vertex-wise cortical thickness maps derived from T1-weighted MRI of 2384 MDD cases and 2096 controls from 25 sites in ENIGMA MDD Working Group. We ran general linear models, taking into account sex and age as covariates, and obtained a z- statistical map for each hemisphere in each site quantifying the vertex-level cortical thickness difference between cases and controls. We then calculated the cross-site consistency (CSC) of these maps by correlating them between every pair of sites (Fig. 1).

To investigate the influence of sample size on CSC, we used a bootstrapping approach, where we combined all data from cases and controls to form two populations. From the case population and control population, we randomly sampled two pseudo-sites, each of which comprises a case group and a control group with a predetermined sample size. We chose sample sizes that are approximately equally spaced on a logarithmic scale; i.e., 𝑁={10,16,25,40,63,100,158,251,398, 631, 1000}. We then computed two 𝑧-maps of cortical thickness differences––one for each pseudo-site––and correlated these two maps. We repeated the sampling procedure 100 times with replacement to obtain the mean and standard deviation of such correlations at each sample size. Repeating the bootstrapping procedure across different sample sizes allowed us to evaluate how quickly the CSCs converge on the most robust correlation estimate that we can obtain with our data. A faster convergence rate is associated with a more robust neuroanatomical phenotype.

Results

The CSC of cortical thickness alterations in MDD is generally low, ranging between -0.17 < r < 0.22 (median = 0.01), which is relatively lower than our previous results obtained for schizophrenia (SCZ) with a range of -0.15 < r < 0.40 (median = 0.16) and Alzheimer's disease (AD), with a range of 0.43 < 𝑟 < 0.71 (median=0.54) for the left hemisphere (Fig. 2a and b).  We obtained similar results for the cortical thickness difference on the right hemisphere and surface area and grey matter volume alterations in each hemisphere. These results provide limited support for grey matter differences, as commonly investigated in MRI studies, as representing robust neuroanatomical phenotypes in MDD.

Bootstrapping analysis indicated that a mean cortical thickness CSC of 0.5 for MDD can we obtained using sample sizes of 1000 subjects per group of cases or controls. This is a much larger sample size required to achieve the same consistency in SCZ (~200 subjects) and AD (~30 subjects). The convergence of the CSCs in MDD is much slower than in SCZ and AD, suggesting that cortical thickness is a less robust neuroanatomical phenotype in MDD.

Conclusion

Our analysis indicates that MRI-based measures of grey matter differences are unlikely to ever converge on robust biological phenotypes for MDD if we continue to rely on multiple, independent studies of small samples. Our findings, supported by the large ENIGMA MDD cohort, also point to the extreme clinical and biological heterogeneity of MDD, even in comparison with SCZ. Our results highlight the need for multi-site collaboration and data sharing for identification of robust biomarker identification.
