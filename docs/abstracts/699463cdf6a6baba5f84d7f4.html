Introduction

Sense of agency (SoA) refers to the subjective experience of controlling one’s own actions and their outcomes.1 Altered SoA is a hallmark of several neuropsychiatric conditions, including Schizophrenia and Borderline Personality Disorder, as well as neurological conditions such as Developmental Coordination Disorder and Functional Movement Disorders. These disruptions underscore the need to understand the neural mechanisms that generate SoA.

A prominent explanatory framework is the comparator model,2 which proposes that SoA arises from comparing predicted and actual sensory consequences of actions. According to this model, SoA consists of two components: a prospective component, emerging before action initiation and reflecting the fluency of action selection; and a retrospective component, occurring after movement execution and driven by sensorimotor prediction error. Although these components have been studied behaviorally in both healthy and clinical populations, and have been shown to be differentially modulated,3 their neurophysiological basis remains unknown.

To address this gap, the present study examined the electrophysiological signatures underlying the prospective and retrospective components of SoA in healthy adults. We hypothesized that the prospective component would be associated with activity in mid-frontocentral regions, whereas the retrospective component would engage temporo-parietal regions, with each component characterized by distinct oscillatory frequency profiles. A further objective was to modulate SoA non-invasively using transcranial alternating current stimulation (tACS), stimulating at specific frequencies and cortical targets derived from our electroencephalograph (EEG) findings, thereby establishing a causal association.

Methods

Experiment 1 (EEG):

Twenty-five healthy adults (mean age 25.08 ± 3.34 years; 13 females), right-handed, with normal vision and no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders were recruited. Written informed consent was obtained from participants. 

EEG was recorded concurrently while participants performed a modified Libet’s clock task, termed the Alphabet Clock Paradigm, in which a red cursor rotated around a clock displaying 42 alphabet characters instead of numbers. They were instructed to press the spacebar at a freely chosen moment and report the alphabet at the time of action. A 1000 Hz tone (100 ms duration) followed keypress in some trials depending on block probability (0%, 50%, or 75%). Five blocks (32 trials each) were presented: two 75%, two 50%, and one baseline (no tone). 

In addition to EEG, Structural T1-weighted MR images of the participants were acquired, and electrode positions were co-registered for source localisation.

For Behavioural analysis, Intentional binding, which is the subjective time compression between action and outcome, was assessed through Judgment Error (JE). JE was defined as the difference between actual and reported press time. Trials were classified into the following conditions: baseline, 50_no_tone, 50_tone, 75_no_tone, and 75_tone, and JE was normalised relative to baseline. Trials with premature responses, invalid responses, or JE > ±2 SD were excluded. A two-way non-parametric repeated measures aligned rank transformation (ART) ANOVA was performed using block and tone as factors. 

For EEG data, a standard preprocessing pipeline was followed. Data was epoched from –3000 to 1250 ms around the button press. Source reconstruction used FreeSurfer segmentation, OpenMEEG BEM forward models, and minimum-norm estimates with Dynamic Statistical Parametric Mapping (dSPM) scaling. Time–frequency was performed using Morlet wavelets on source time-series data and analysed across four predefined time windows: -1500 ms to -500 ms (movt-preparation), -500 ms to 0 ms (pre-movt), 0 ms to 250 ms (post-movt), and 250 ms to 500 ms (post-tone) using cluster-based permutation statistics.

We computed source-space Phase-Amplitude Coupling (PAC) by assessing how Theta phase in right hemisphere seeds modulates Beta amplitude in left hemisphere targets, using PCA-reduced signals and the Modulation Index. We then evaluated prospective and retrospective components using paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests.

Experiment 2 (tACS):

Fourteen healthy adults (mean age 24.43 ± 2.79 years; 5 females) were recruited for the tACS experiment and performed the same behavioural task as experiment 1. Each stimulation session included seven blocks: one baseline, two 50%, and four 75% blocks (24 trials each).

Participants completed four randomised, blinded tACS sessions: theta (6 Hz), beta (25 Hz), theta-modulated beta (25 Hz carrier, 6 Hz envelope), and sham, delivered in antiphase. Saline-soaked sponge electrodes (5 × 5 cm) were placed over C3 (left M1) and P4 (inferior parietal lobule). Stimulation was delivered at 2 mA peak-to-peak (except Sham) with 15 s ramp-up and a 15s ramp-down.

JE was computed as in Experiment 1. A linear mixed-effects model (lme4) accounted for inter-individual variability, followed by a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA (lmerTest) with stimulation frequency and task condition as factors. Post hoc comparisons used Tukey-corrected estimated marginal means. 

Results

Experiment 1(EEG):

For behavioural data, the results showed the least JE for the baseline condition and the most JE for the 75_tone condition, and revealed a significant effect of tone (F(1,57) = 4.40, p = 0.040). However, there was no significant effect of block or its interaction with the tone. 

Source-level analyses of the prospective component showed increased theta power in the right inferior parietal region (p = 0.0404, c = 509, s = 183), beta power in the left precentral, postcentral, and supramarginal regions (p = 0.0164, c = 2180, s = 744), and gamma power in the right superior frontal and precentral regions during the pre-movement window (p = 0.0180, c = 1564, s = 525). During the post-movement window, a bilateral increase in beta power was observed in superior frontal, precentral, postcentral, and superior parietal regions  (LH: p = 0.0088, c = 2368, s = 757; RH: p = 0.0088, c = 2313, s = 750). In the post-tone window, beta power increases were predominantly localised to right precentral, postcentral, supramarginal, inferior parietal, and superior parietal regions (p = 0.0192, c = 1314, s = 469)In contrast, the retrospective component showed a significant increase in theta-band power in the post-tone window, expressed in two clusters -  one encompassing the right rostral and caudal middle frontal, precentral, postcentral, supramarginal, superior temporal, and insular regions (p = 0.0244, c = 3296, s = 1176), and a second cluster localized to the inferior temporal region (p = 0.0487, c = 443, s = 159).

PAC analysis on source time-series revealed significantly increased theta–beta connectivity during prospective sense of agency between theta activity in the right inferior parietal lobule and beta activity in the left precentral gyrus in the pre-movement window (W = 45.00, p = 0.0251), as well as in the left postcentral gyrus in the post-tone window (W = 51.00,  p = 0.0438). Guided by these source and connectivity findings, we targeted the right inferior parietal lobule and left sensorimotor cortices using theta (TT), beta (BT), and theta-modulated beta (TB) stimulation in the subsequent tACS experiment.

Experiment 2 (tACS):

A significant main effect of stimulation frequency (F(3, 195) = 18.61, p < .001), with no significant main effect of condition or interaction, was observed. Post hoc pairwise comparisons showed that, across conditions, theta-modulated beta (TB) produced significantly lower judgment error than theta (TT; t(195) = −5.22, p < .0001), beta (BT; t(195) = −7.16, p < .0001), and sham (SM; t(195) = −3.30, p = .006). In contrast, beta stimulation resulted in significantly higher judgment error than sham (t(195) = 3.86, p < .001). No significant differences were observed between TT and BT or between TT and SM.

No significant effects of frequency, component, or their interaction were observed when comparing prospective (75_no_tone − 50_no_tone) and retrospective (50_tone − 50_no_tone) components of SoA. Nevertheless, a trend was observed for the retrospective component, wherein BT stimulation appeared to facilitate SoA, while TT and TB tended to worsen it. For the prospective component, all active stimulation frequencies (TT, BT, and TB) were associated with worsened SoA relative to sham.

Conclusion

In the present study, we investigated the cortical mechanisms underlying the prospective and retrospective components of the SoA using EEG. We further employed tACS to establish a causal link between SoA and the cortical correlates from the EEG analyses.

Prospective SoA was characterized by increased beta power over left fronto-central regions implicated in motor planning and initiation, along with elevated theta activity in the right inferior parietal region. Following movement execution, bilateral increases in beta power were observed over pre- & post-central, and superior parietal cortices, consistent with the transmission of efference copy signals for outcome prediction, as proposed by the comparator model.4 In the post-tone window, enhanced beta activity was localized to right precentral, postcentral, supramarginal, inferior parietal, and superior parietal regions, encompassing key nodes of the temporo-parietal junction involved in action–outcome comparison.1

Retrospective SoA was primarily associated with increased theta power in right caudal middle frontal, sensorimotor, supramarginal, and angular gyrus regions. These areas have been consistently implicated in action–outcome monitoring.5

Importantly, source-space connectivity analyses revealed enhanced cross-frequency coupling between theta oscillations in the right inferior parietal lobule and beta oscillations in left sensorimotor cortices during prospective SoA, suggesting a mechanistic role for theta–beta interactions in predictive agency, which can again be attributed to the comparator model.

Subsequent tACS study provided preliminary causal evidence for this mechanism. Theta-modulated beta (TB) stimulation enhanced intentional binding, reflected by reduced JE, whereas beta (BT) stimulation alone disrupted binding, increasing JE. Moreover, tACS differentially affected the two components of SoA: antiphase theta stimulation selectively impaired retrospective SoA, while antiphase theta-modulated beta stimulation disrupted prospective SoA.

To conclude, this study is the first to dissociate and localize the neural sources underlying the prospective and retrospective components of SoA using EEG. Moreover, our study provides causal evidence that specific cortical regions and oscillatory frequencies contribute differentially to each component of SoA through neuromodulation with tACS. Together, our findings identify theta–beta connectivity as a promising target for modulating and potentially enhancing sense of agency.
