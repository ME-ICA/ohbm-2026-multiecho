Introduction

The functional connectome (FC), i.e., correlations between brain activity across different regions of the human brain, derived from resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI), is a standard input for identifying brain–behaviour/personality relationships and inter-individual differences. One issue in this regard is that the vast number of input features with strong collinearity makes it hard to assess the relevance of individual features reliably, interpret the findings neuroanatomically, and avoid overtraining. Additionally, it consumes significant computational time. For these reasons, feature selection (FS) or feature reduction methods are introduced.

Here, we are studying whether the average structural connectome can serve as an effective feature selector for FC-based “fingerprinting” 1-4, i.e., the reidentification of a subject in the test-retest sample, using the Human Connectome Project (HCP) young adults S1200 sample. Reducing the number of connections by eliminating those that are not or only weakly structurally connected would yield an anatomically grounded FS that could be applied across datasets for fingerprinting or regression tasks, independent of the target variable. We will compare it to group-averaged functional connectivity magnitude-based FS, which has the disadvantage of including non-anatomical connections and is likely to be less transferable between groups.

Methods

We used preprocessed rs-fMRI test-retest data from N=1018 participants from the HCP S1200 project (2mm isotropic resolution, TR = 720ms)  with FSL’s ICA + FIX noise removal. We corrected further by regressing out six motion parameters, white matter, and cerebral spinal fluid signals.  Where reported, global signal regression (GSR) was applied by adding one more regressor. Then, time courses were band-pass filtered from 0.01-0.1 Hz. Time series from the LR and RL phase encoding directions were concatenated before computing Pearson and partial correlations based on region of interest (ROI) time courses using the DiFuMo brain atlas. We used the atlas with 512 regions and reduced it to either only gray matter (GM) regions (409 regions) or cortical GM (342) to compute FC matrices (FCMs). 

A group-averaged structural connectome (SC) was derived from the HCP S1200 dataset based on MRI diffusion-based tractography5 and resampled to the same atlases. Streamline count between regions were normalized by region volumes before converting to ranks. Averaged FC matrices were computed and converted to ranks based on FC magnitude for Pearson and partial correlation, and with and without global signal regression (GSR). Note that FS on Pearson correlation matrices was only done with the average of the Pearson correlation matrices, and the same for partial correlation matrices, i.e. correlation types were not mixed. Ten random selectors were also employed and accuracies were averaged.

To remove potential temporal sequence (session) effects, FCMs from 509 randomly selected subjects from day 1 were assigned to pool P1, and the corresponding FCMs of the same subjects from day 2 were assigned to P2 and vice versa the remaining 509 subjects: their D1 FCMs were assigned to P2, and their D2 FCMs were assigned to P1. As a similarity measure, Pearson correlation was used between the vectorized FCMs, dropping the diagonal and the lower triangle. The subject with the highest correlation coefficient was selected as the presumed match. 

Results

Results are shown in Fig. 1. When using all features, accuracies range between 95.87% for Partial FC matrices without GSR and the GM atlas and 99.2% for Pearson FC with GSR and the cortical atlas. Generally, the cortical atlas performs better than the larger GM atlas that contains also subcortex and cerebellum. While partial FC matrices reach up to 100% accuracy with about 1% of the connections when using the FC-based FS, the top accuracies for Pearson correlation reach 99.71% (3 mislabelled subjects) with GSR independent of the selector.  Peak values are reached with less than 2% of the edges for a number of configurations. For Pearson correlation, the accuracy rises slowly from FSP = 100% to the maximum and even a random selector stays constant up to a tipping point of about FSP = 5%. Partial correlation accuracies can exhibit a substantial drop in accuracy for intermediate FSP values for FC- and SC-based selection, and accuracy drops off immediately for a random selector. For Pearson correlation, the SC-based selection reaches similar accuracy peaks as FC-based selection but with fewer edges. For partial correlation, the FC-based selection reaches higher peak accuracies than FC-based selection at similar FSPs. When using SC-FS for Pearson and FC-FS for partial correlations, the accuracy attended with all features can be attend with 0.5% of the edges or less. GSR tends to improve performance values. We also looked at atlases with different resolution (data not presented) and observed better performance with more ROIs.

Conclusion

In the HCP S1200 data set, rs-fMRI-based fingerprinting benefited strongly from informed, group-averaged, and outcome-independent feature selection. The use of Pearson correlation over partial correlation is recommended for fingerprinting because it is robust against performance drop even for a random selector. For Pearson correlation, the structural connectome is superior to FC-based feature selection as similar performance can be reached with fewer edges. The number of features can be reduced to 1-2% of all features with a benefit to fingerprinting accuracy. These observations may be biased by the quality and long scan duration of the HCP resting state data.
