Introduction

Childhood and adolescence are phases in which rapid social, emotional and cognitive development occur. During this period the brain undergoes a complex and dynamic multistage maturation process^1. As a result, the brain is particularly vulnerable to adversity and stress during this period, which may explain the observation increased the vulnerability to psychiatric disorders that emerges during adolescence^1. Changes in the developing brain are best captured through longitudinal resting-state functional MRI, but these studies are challenging to carry out in humans due to the time and resources required. Rodent studies offer an experimentally tractable means to examine longitudinal brain development. While analogous functional networks have been reported, no robust, sex-balanced network template exists for developmental rodent studies. The aim of this study was to use a clustering approach to identify multiscale network organisation for tracking developmental trajectories.

Methods

Thirty-six male and female Wistar Han rats were weaned on postnatal day 21. Animals were housed in pairs in individually ventilated cages on a 12-hour light-dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. A Bruker 7T BioSpec 70/30 system equipped with an eight-channel surface coil and 72mm transmit coil was used for all imaging sessions. Animals underwent five MRI scans at approximately postnatal day 28, 35, 49, 70 and 91. Scans were acquired over four waves of longitudinal data collection. The StandardRat protocol^2 was followed for both anaesthesia/sedation and MRI  scanning. Anaesthesia induction was carried out using 4% isoflurane in 1000ml/min oxygen and 400ml/min nitrogen. This was followed by a bolus of medetomidine (0.05mg/kg, subcutaneous). Animals were maintained at 0.4% isoflurane in 200ml/min oxygen and 400ml/min nitrogen, with a continuous infusion of medetomidine (0.1 mg/kg/h, subcutaneous) starting 15 minutes after the bolus. The functional scan began 40 minutes after the bolus of medetomidine had been delivered. For the anatomical scan, a T2w turboRARE sequence was used with TR = 2500ms, TE = 30ms, RARE factor = 8, 18 interleaved axial slices of 1 mm with 0.1 mm gap, FOV = 25.6mm x 25.6mm, and matrix size of 128 x 128. For the functional scan, a gradient echo planar sequence was used with the following parameters: TR = 1000ms, TE = 17ms, 18 interleaved axial slices of 1 mm with 0.1 mm gap, FOV = 25.6mm x 25.6mm, and matrix size of 64 x 64.

Preprocessing and confound correction were carried out using RABIES: Rodent Automated Bold Improvement of EPI Sequences^3. EPI scans were corrected for inhomogeneities then registered to the population average template and resampled to 0.3x0.3x0.3mm. Confound correction was then carried out on the native space EPI timeseries. Voxelwise detrending was applied followed by bandpass filtering (0.01-0.1Hz). Thirty seconds was removed from each edge of the timeseries to account for edge artefacts following filtering. Next, 6 rigid motion parameters and the global signal were regressed from the data. Finally, a spatial Gaussian smoothing filter was applied at 0.5mm full-width at half maximum.

Clustering was carried out as previously described^4, following computation of whole-brain functional connectivity for each of 188 Regions of Interest (ROIs) from the SIGMA rat in vivo atlas^5. The eta-squared statistic was computed for each ROI pair, using the fisher-z-transformed whole-brain connectivity matrix as the input. This yielded an eta-squared matrix for each subject and timepoint. A spectral clustering algorithm was applied to the eta-squared matrices to partition the brain into k clusters, where k ranged from 2 to 15. The individual-level results generated from spectral clustering above were then aggregated to determine a single, stable group-level solution using consensus clustering whereby spectral clustering was applied to the group consensus matrix to produce group-level community assignments for each k.  This process of spectral and consensus clustering was carried out independently for each timepoint to yield age-specific clustering solutions. Metrics used to determine the optimal number of clusters included mean Variation of Information, mean Cramer’s V, Davies Bouldin Index, modified silhouette, mean consensus and percent agreement.

Results

The optimal cluster number was identified using mean agreement across the different timepoints. Based on the metrics used, k = 5 proved to be the optimal k for most timepoints, across almost all metrics. Therefore, the results from k = 5 was selected for further analysis.

The first network identified (Cluster 1; blue, 68 Regions) is a subcortical network consisting of mostly limbic brain regions. This includes majority of the hippocampal formation, namely the cornu ammonis 1-3, dentate gyrus, the fimbria of the hippocampus, and the fornix. This cluster also features the bed nucleus of stria terminals, substantia nigra, hypothalamic region, thalamus and midbrain structures. Network 2 (cluster 2; orange; 41 regions) is composed of majority of the primary and secondary visual cortex, retrosplenial cortex, temporal association cortex and remaining parts of the hippocampal formation.  Network 3 (cluster 3; green; 22 regions) includes the insular cortex and orbitofrontal region; hubs of the rodent salience network. Additionally, a large number of somatosensory regions are members of this cluster such as the secondary and parts of the primary somatosensory dysgranular cortices. Network 4 (cluster 4; red; 32 regions) is primarily a frontal network. This cluster includes medial frontal brain regions including the prelimbic region, as well as the primary and secondary cingular cortices. This combined with the presence of the entorhinal cortex and frontal association cortex in the cluster is indicative of the rodent default mode network. However, the cluster also contains other frontal regions including the striatum and olfactory structures. Network 5 (cluster 5; purple; 25 regions) is a somatomotor brain network. In particular, it features the primary motor and auditory cortices along with a range of somatosensory regions including the primary somatosensory cortices and barrel fields.

Analysis of the longitudinal pattern shows that these brain networks mature in a hierarchical fashion, with more fundamental system such as sensory and motor networks maturing earlier, while higher order networks developing more progressively over time (figure 2). The largest reorganisation of clusters occurs between postnatal day 28 and 35, with 74 regions (39.36% of all regions) changing cluster membership. Clusters become more stable from postnatal day 35 onwards with less than 25 Regions changing membership between later timepoints.  By postnatal day 70, the overall structure of these functional networks is largely established, with minimal changes occurring between this timepoint and postnatal day 91.

Conclusion

A cluster solution of 5 (k = 5) was highly stable across timepoints, with the greatest developmental reorganisation occurring between postnatal day 28 and 35, equivalent to late childhood ~9-11 years of age. Functional networks identified in adult male and female Wistar rats include limbic, visual, salience-somatosensory, default mode and somatomotor. The somatomotor and visual network are largely stable by postnatal day 28, while limbic, default mode and salience-somatosensory networks mature more slowly. These networks will form the basis for future developmental trajectory analyses, and examination  of sex as a biological variable and the impact of stress on functional brain development.
