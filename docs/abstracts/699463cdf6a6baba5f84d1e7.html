Introduction

Over the past decades, MRI has transformed neuroscience, yet linking brain structure to cognition remains difficult. Large-scale studies show that structural MRI metrics explain only a small portion of individual differences in cognitive ability^1. This raises a central question: to what extent do standard MRI measures reflect the neurobiology that actually supports cognition?

Cellular studies show that interindividual cognitive differences relate to variation in pyramidal cell morphology, interneuron density, and glial processes. However, such cellular data are rarely obtainable in humans, making it crucial to determine whether MRI metrics meaningfully capture these cellular properties. Cortical thickness is widely used in neuroscience and relates to cognition across the lifespan. It has been associated with heterogeneous biological processes including neuronal organization (e.g., density, dendrites, synapses), axonal myelination and glial cells. Yet, empirical links between thickness and underlying mechanisms remain inconsistent.

Histology offers direct microstructural measurements, but post-mortem work faces major limitations, including tissue degradation and lack of corresponding MRI or cognitive assessments. To bridge this gap, our study uses resected cortical tissue from living patients, providing a rare combination of ex vivo histology, in vivo MRI, and IQ scores.

Using data from 62 subjects, we test whether histological measures of dendritic structure (total dendritic length, number of branch points, cell body area) explain (1) differences in MRI-derived cortical thickness, (2) differences in IQ, and (3) IQ differences through cortical thickness. Based on prior findings, we expect histology to predict both cortical thickness and IQ directly, and to exert additional indirect effects through MRI measures. This work evaluates how well MRI captures neurobiological differences relevant for cognition and clarifies the cellular basis of cortical thickness.

Methods

Participants

The sample included 68 adults (18–66 years; 31 males, 31 females and 6 missing values) undergoing neurosurgery for conditions affecting subcortical structures (e.g., epilepsy, tumors). Before surgery, all participants completed MRI and IQ testing. During the procedure, a small non-pathological tissue sample (1–1.5 cm) was resected from the middle temporal gyrus (MTG; BA21), 4 cm from the anterior pole. Non-pathological status was confirmed by the surgeon, pre-surgical MRI, and histology. No associations were found between medical history or age and histology or IQ.

Histology, MRI and IQ data have been previously reported^2,3. All procedures were approved by the Medical Ethical Committee of VU Medical Center; all participants gave written informed consent.

MRI processing

MRI scans were acquired on a 3T GE Signa HDXt scanner as part of routine pre-surgical evaluation. T1-weighted images were processed using FreeSurfer 7.3.2. The pipeline included skull stripping, white/gray segmentation, and pial/white surface reconstruction. Many patients had prior temporal resections on the same hemisphere, which can impair automated surface extraction. To minimize segmentation errors, gcut was used to remove dura/skull, followed by automated QC with Qoala-T. Scores ranged 31.3–74.5 (mean = 53.8). Because the scans were atypical, a threshold of 40 was used, instead of the recommended 50. However, raising it to 50 did not change results.

The cortex was parcellated using the Destrieux atlas. Cortical thickness was extracted for MTG in both hemispheres. Thickness values for the resected hemisphere were compared with the non-resected hemisphere to account for bilateral symmetry.

Ex vivo histology

Histological procedures followed Heyer et al. (2022)^3. The morphology of the pyramidal cells were measured through five variables: cell body diameter, cell body area, cell density, total dendritic length (TDL), and number of branch points (NBP). TDL and NBP were measured on basal dendrites. Metrics were averaged across cells and slices per participant.

Biocytin staining was used for diameter, TDL, and NBP, while NeuN staining was used for cell area and density. This results in a lack of overlap between the measures from the two staining. Thus, cell area and density were excluded from modeling to avoid non-convergence.

Intelligence Quotient

IQ scores were previously described and analyzed in Goriounova et al. (2018)^2. Participants completed the Dutch WAIS-III or WAIS-IV, yielding full-scale IQ, verbal IQ, and performance IQ.

Statistical analyses

Pearson correlations assessed associations among histology, cortical thickness, and IQ. Structural equation models (SEM) tested three questions: (1) whether histology predicts MRI-derived MTG cortical thickness; (2) whether histology predicts IQ; (3) whether histology predicts IQ indirectly through cortical thickness. Models were run first with MTG thickness in the resected hemisphere, then the non-resected hemisphere, and at the hemispheric level.

Individual histological predictors (e.g., TDL, NBP) were tested separately, then jointly to assess shared variance. For third question, we compared a model where histology predict IQ directly and indirectly through cortical thickness to a model where the direct paths between histology and IQ were fixed to 0. Plausible scenarios include a partial or full mediation by cortical thickness of the prediction of IQ by histology. The lack of mediation would imply that MRI does not capture information found in our ex vivo histology measures to predict IQ.

Model fit was evaluated using RMSEA (<0.05), CFI (>0.97), and SRMR (<0.05). AIC and BIC informed model comparison. R² values indexed explanatory power. Multiple comparison correction used Benjamini–Hochberg (q < 0.05). SEMs were estimated in R (4.1.0) using lavaan with maximum likelihood and FIML for missing data.

Results

Correlations

Among the three histological metrics, cell body diameter was not correlated with total dendritic length (TDL) or number of branch points (NBP), whereas TDL and NBP were highly correlated (r = 0.90). IQ was moderately associated with TDL and NBP (both r = 0.51).

Mean cortical thickness was strongly correlated across hemispheres (r = 0.85). In contrast, cortical thickness in left and right MTG was not significantly correlated, likely reflecting resection effects. MTG thickness in the resected hemisphere correlated with mean thickness of that hemisphere (r = 0.40), whereas MTG thickness in the non-resected hemisphere correlated with mean non-resected thickness (r = 0.72).

MTG cortical thickness in the resected hemisphere was negatively associated with NBP (r = –0.58), but not significantly with TDL (r = –0.42). No cortical thickness measure correlated with IQ.

Histology predicting IQ

Individual SEMs showed that TDL (β = 0.490, p < 0.001)  and NBP (β = 0.489, p < 0.001) strongly predicted IQ, explaining 36-50% of its variance; cell body diameter showed no effect. Participants with more complex dendritic morphology (higher TDL, higher NBP) had higher IQ.

The joint model including all three metrics fit the data well, but none of the predictors reached significance. High covariance between TDL and NBP (0.9) increased standard errors, suggesting multicollinearity. Thus, we tested models containing two predictors (e.g., TDL + cell area; NBP + cell area). Those models fit the data better, and TDL and NBP each significantly predicted IQ (TDL: β = 0.486, p < 0.001; NBP: β = 0.493, p < 0.001) (Results Figure). Cell body area did not add explanatory value.

Histology predicting cortical thickness

In individual models, NBP (β = -0.658, p < 0.001) and TDL (β = -0.490, p < 0.001) negatively predicted MTG cortical thickness in the resected hemisphere, explaining 24-43% of its variance. These associations were not found in the non-resected hemisphere. Thus, greater dendritic complexity was associated with a thinner cortex.

In the joint three-predictor model, both TDL (β = 0.569, p < 0.001) and NBP (β = –1.133, p < 0.001) predicted MTG thickness, though the positive TDL estimate likely reflects multicollinearity. Two-predictor joint models showed that only NBP significantly predicted cortical thickness (β = –0.658, p < 0.001), whereas TDL did not (Results Figure). No significant effects emerged in the non-resected MTG or whole-hemisphere analyses.

Do histology measures predict IQ through MRI-derived cortical thickness?

We tested whether MRI-derived MTG thickness mediated the histology-IQ associations. Due to multicollinearity, TDL and NBP were each tested in separate models (with cell body area).

Models constraining direct effects (i.e., from histology to IQ) to zero fit poorly (e.g., NBP model: χ² = 12.670, df = 2, p = 0.002; RMSEA = 0.469; CFI = 0.000; SRMR = 0.126). Allowing direct and indirect effects improved fit (ΔAIC = 5; ΔBIC = 1; p = 0.002 for NBP). For NBP, the free model was preferred; for TDL, it was not (Results Figure). This suggests that while histology strongly predicted IQ, cortical thickness did not mediate this relationship, meaning that cortical thickness (and potentially other metrics of MRI) does not capture all the structural information found in the brain.

No significant mediation paths were found when using the non-resected MTG or whole-hemisphere thickness.

Conclusion

In a unique dataset combining ex-vivo histology, MRI and IQ, we examined whether MRI-derived cortical thickness captures the histological information that predicts differences in IQ. We found that dendritic structure (i.e., number of branch points and total dendritic length) predicted IQ, but these associations were not mediated by cortical thickness. This suggests that dendritic architecture contains cognitively relevant information that is not fully reflected in MRI-based thickness.

In line with previous studies in this sample^2,3, both dendritic measures positively predicted IQ, indicating that individuals with more complex arborization tend to show higher IQ. Human pyramidal neurons have large, elaborated dendritic trees that enable extensive synaptic integration and efficient information transfer. Their structure also varies across the cortex, with association areas (e.g., temporal lobe) showing larger, more complex neurons with more connections. These features may help explain how dendritic complexity partly explain differences in IQ (see review^4).

We also found that greater dendritic complexity was associated with thinner cortex in the resected middle temporal gyrus. This supports the idea that dendritic arborization contributes to interindividual variation in cortical thickness, though dendrites alone cannot account for all differences, as cortical thickness reflects multiple overlapping biological processes (e.g., synapse, myelination). These findings are also specific to variation among adults; the mechanisms shaping cortical thickness across development or aging may differ from those driving differences between adults.

However, the negative association we observed contrasts with previous positive findings^2. Because both studies rely on nearly the same underlying dataset (we added 22 patients), the discrepancy is likely due to differences in MRI processing, such as FreeSurfer version, workstation or operating system, which are known to alter cortical thickness estimates^5.

Importantly, cortical thickness did not mediate the link between dendritic measures and IQ. This raises a broader question about how much biological information is captured by MRI-derived metrics. Cortical thickness is a valid measure of laminar distance, but validity does not imply that it reflects all relevant cellular mechanisms. Other MRI measures—such as surface area, grey matter volume or more advanced structural metrics—may capture additional or complementary information. Multivariate approaches may therefore be more informative than relying on a single metric.

These findings should be interpreted with caution. The sample consisted of neurosurgical patients with temporal-lobe pathology, limiting generalizability. The dataset was also small, with relatively few individuals having matched histology and MRI, which reduces statistical power. Furthermore, our cortical thickness measure did not perfectly map onto the resected tissue, as automated parcellations cover a much broader region. More precise matching between histology and imaging, ideally using manually defined regions, would likely improve comparisons.

Overall, our findings highlight the gap between cellular-level biology and macro-scale MRI metrics. While MRI remains essential for studying human brain structure in vivo, commonly used measures like cortical thickness capture only part of the underlying neurobiological variation. Advances in high-resolution imaging, new acquisition sequences, and multi-scale integration of histology and MRI will be crucial to better understand what MRI metrics reflect. Although our study does not offer a definitive account of what cortical thickness measures, it underscores the need to clarify the biological processes captured by structural MRI and to combine multiple metrics to better represent the complexity of cortical organization.
