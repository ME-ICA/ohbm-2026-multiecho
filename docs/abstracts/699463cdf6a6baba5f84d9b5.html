Introduction

Mechanical pressure pain (MPP) is widely used in noxious-stimulation fMRI because it reliably evokes activity across pain-related regions in brain, brainstem, and spinal cord1. Yet most spinal and whole-brain analyses model entire stimulus epoch with a canonical hemodynamic response function (HRF), implicitly assuming neural activity is sustained and a fixed HRF captures timing across tissues. This approach can blur rapid components, especially near stimulus onset, and can mix nociceptive responses with later task components such as rating. Recent pressure-pain work suggests responses may be strongest near onset2, but temporally resolved coupling between spinal cord and cortex remains under-characterized. We therefore combined simultaneous spinal cord–brain fMRI with finite impulse response (FIR) modeling to estimate pressure-evoked BOLD responses without imposing an HRF shape on pain epoch. We asked whether FIR would reveal onset-locked brain–cord correspondence consistent with ascending nociceptive processing, and whether conventional full-HRF block modeling would obscure this temporal structure.

Methods

Five healthy volunteers underwent simultaneous spinal cord–brain T2*-weighted EPI with dynamic per-slice shimming3 at 3T (brain/cord FOV 22/8 cm, TR/TE 2.5 s/30 ms, 43 slices total: 31 brain and 12 spinal cord slices centered at C5; slice thickness/spacing 5/0 mm). Each 11:32-min run comprised 21 cycles with pseudo-randomized anticipation (3.5/4.5/5.5 s), 10 s pressure pain, 7 s rating, and 10 s baseline. Pressure was delivered to left thumbnail nailbed using a custom pneumatically driven device (4.5 kg/cm²). After each stimulus, participants rated both pain intensity and unpleasantness using right hand to control a visual analog scale (Fig. 1a). Cardiac and respiratory recordings were collected.

Preprocessing included RETROICOR physiological noise correction4, slice-timing and motion correction, mean CSF signal removal for spinal cord, spatial smoothing (brain 5 mm FWHM; cord 2×2×5 mm³), and spatial normalization (brain to MNI152; cord to PAM50). First-level modeling used FSL FEAT5 with high-pass filtering (100 s) and prewhitening. Anticipation was modeled as a boxcar convolved with a double-gamma HRF. Pressure pain and rating epochs were modeled with FIR basis functions: eight unconvolved regressors spaced at TR (2.5 s) spanning 0–20 s relative to pressure onset. Analyses emphasized four FIR bins expected to capture pressure-evoked BOLD given HRF latency (Fig. 1b). For comparison, we re-analyzed same data with a conventional full-HRF model (boxcars convolved with a double-gamma HRF for anticipation, pressure, and rating as shown in Fig. 1c). Group maps were summarized across whole brain and cervical cord (segment-level visualization centered on C6).

Results

FIR modeling revealed predominantly phasic, onset-locked responses during MPP. In earliest post-onset window (pressure-bin-1), robust activation was observed in contralateral primary somatosensory cortex, insula, parietal operculum, and dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, together with ipsilateral cerebellar activation. Critically, this bin also showed ipsilateral dorsal horn activation in spinal cord, consistent with temporally specific spinal–supraspinal engagement at stimulus onset (Fig. 2a). Pressure-bin-2 showed no significant effects in brain or cord (Fig. 2b). Pressure-bin-3 revealed spinal cord activation (contralateral ventral horn and central dorsal regions) without significant supraspinal activation (Fig. 2c). Pressure-bin-4 exhibited activity in bilateral frontal operculum, contralateral cerebellum and premotor cortex, supplementary motor area, ipsilateral primary motor cortex, and visual cortices, accompanied by contralateral hemicord activation, consistent with recruitment of visuomotor networks during rating behavior (Fig. 2d).

In contrast, conventional full-HRF modeling yielded broader, temporally smeared activation patterns. In brain, activation included contralateral cerebellum and visual cortex, bilateral frontal operculum and insula, and ipsilateral somatosensory cortices. In spinal cord, activation was more centrally located and biased ventrally relative to dorsal horn–weighted pattern isolated by FIR at onset, reducing visibility of onset-specific cord–cortex correspondence (Fig. 2e). Statistical display and inference followed original analyses: brain maps were cluster-corrected (p < 0.05; Z > 2.3); spinal cord FIR maps were displayed at uncorrected Z > 1.6; spinal cord full-HRF results were cluster-corrected (p < 0.05; Z > 1.6). Spinal cord FIR effects did not survive multiple-comparison correction, but spatial patterns aligned with expected brain–cord relationships (ipsilateral dorsal horn with contralateral sensory cortex in bin-1; contralateral hemicord with ipsilateral motor areas in bin-4).

Conclusion

Using simultaneous spinal cord–brain fMRI, FIR modeling indicates mechanical pressure pain is dominated by transient, onset-locked responses with coordinated engagement of contralateral cortical sensory regions and ipsilateral dorsal horn. By estimating pain-evoked time course without imposing an HRF shape, FIR better isolates early nociceptive components and distinguishes them from later rating-related activity, revealing brain–cord correspondence that is less apparent with canonical full-HRF block modeling. Later bins primarily reflected visuomotor and motor network engagement with accompanying hemicord activation. Although spinal FIR effects were not correction-resistant in this small sample, observed temporal structure emphasizes that pain responses are not uniformly sustained across stimulus window and motivates temporally flexible modeling when interpreting spinal–supraspinal coupling in human pain neuroimaging.
