Introduction

Alzheimer's disease is a chronic neurodegenerative disorder that gradually impairs memory, cognition, learning and organizing skills. While extensive research has focused on these cognitive aspects, less attention has been given to the significant visual problems experienced by older adults with Alzheimer’s disease. In fact, Alzheimer's disease is a major disorder causing visual problems in the older population 1. Visual disorders can hinder object recognition and discrimination, directly impacting patients’ quality of life. Although the occipital lobe is the primary visual processing center, structural MRI studies have shown that occipital regions are often spared from atrophy in Alzheimer’s disease, with the frontal, temporal, and parietal lobes being more commonly affected2. Multiple resting-state functional MRI (fMRI) studies have demonstrated abnormal functional connectivity in the occipital lobe, but the direct relationship between brain activity and concurrent cognitive task performance remains unclear due to the task-free nature of these studies 3,4. In this study, we collected high-resolution mnemonic similarity task (MST) 7-Tesla fMRI data from cognitively normal (CN) participants and patients with amnestic mild cognitive impairment (MCI). Our goal was to evaluate abnormal brain activity in MCI and their relevance to cognitive performance, which might provide insights into the biological mechanism at the prodromal stage of Alzheimer’s disease.

Methods

The imaging data were acquired using a 7T Siemens MAGNETOM Terra MR scanner. Both MST fMRI data and structural MRI data were collected from 46 cognitively normal elderly participants and 37 subjects with amnestic mild cognitive impairment (MCI). During the encoding phase of the MST task, participants responded with “indoor” or “outdoor” to 66 everyday objects. In the recognition phase, they judged objects as “same,” “similar,” or “new,” with stimuli including exact repeats (targets), similar objects with varying degrees of difference (small, medium, large lures), and entirely novel objects (foils) (Figure 1a). Preprocessing of fMRI data included slice-time correction, distortion correction, realignment, co-registration, and normalization to the standard MNI template. A general linear model (GLM) was applied with seven conditions, including “new” response to foils (foil), “same” response to targets (hit), correct “similar” responses to each of three types of lures (small-, medium-, and large-difference lure correct rejections, LureCR), false “same” response to all lures combined (lure false alarm, LureFA), and one condition for all others. Only the hit and the three LureCR conditions were included in our statistical analysis, since these four conditions involve recalling previously seen objects. Two-way mixed analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied on beta coefficients derived from GLM to evaluate the impact of clinical diagnosis (cognitively normal vs MCI) and task conditions (hit and the three LureCR conditions). We exclusively focused on the regions showing difference between clinical diagnosis in this abstract, the analysis with task conditions was conducted in a separate project. With the regions showing significant difference between clinical diagnosis after cluster-wise correction, a linear mixed effect (LME) model was constructed to examine if the regional beta value was associated with task performance and if such an association was mediated by clinical diagnosis. The whole-brain functional connectivity with the significant regions was characterized by Pearson’s correlation, and then the difference between whole-brain functional connectivity was examined after Fisher’s r-to-z transformation.

Results

Participants with MCI had significantly lower correct response rate compared to CN, with the correct rate for target, small-, medium-, and large-difference lures as 82.8±11.8%, 54.7±16.4%, 61.9±17.3% and 60.7±14.6% respectively for CN and 67.8±21.8%, 33.5±13.8%, 38.5±14.1%, and 41.8±15.9% for MCI (Figure 1b). In the mixed two-way ANOVA, bilateral inferior occipital lobes were identified to show significant difference between clinical diagnosis (p<0.01, 484 and 432 voxels for left and right inferior occipital lobe respectively, Figure 2a), and the interaction term between clinical diagnosis and similarity levels did not show significant clusters. The average beta values of these two clusters were significantly lower in participants with MCI than CN across all four conditions, suggesting weakened brain activation in bilateral inferior occipital lobe. The average beta values of hit and large LureCR conditions at left inferior occipital lobe were shown in Figure 2b, the plots for right inferior occipital lobe or other conditions were omitted due to their similar pattern. LME analysis showed that the regional beta coefficients in both clusters were strongly associated with participants lure discrimination performance and such an association remained consistent across groups, without showing distinct association. The scatter plots between behavioral lure discrimination and beta values at left inferior occipital lobe, together with the 95% confidence interval, were shown in Figure 2d for the hit and large-difference LureCR conditions. Functional connectivity analysis showed that inferior occipital lobe was functionally connected to parietal lobe and precentral gyrus in both groups, and the functional connectivity did not show difference between them (Figure 2c).

Conclusion

The functional activity of inferior occipital lobe was substantially impaired in patients with MCI compared to CN, and the weakened activity was associated with their deteriorated performance in object mnemonic discrimination, suggesting that hypoactivity in the inferior occipital lobe might be an early sign of Alzheimer’s disease and it could be a major factor influencing cognitive decline during the prodromal stage. However, the functional connectivity of bilateral inferior occipital lobe likely remains integral considering that there was no difference between CN and MCI in the whole-brain connectivity analysis, which might be because the pathological tau did not substantially undermine the neuronal connections with inferior occipital lobe. Diffusion MRI studies could potentially further validate the findings. In summary, inferior occipital lobe hypoactivity may be a critical factor in cognitive impairment in the prodromal stage of Alzheimer’s disease.
