Introduction

A homogeneous B0 field is crucial for to reduce EPI distortion and boost image quality for functional MRI under high magnetic fields (7T and above). When using the 7T Terra.X scanner for fMRI studies, the scanner by default supports automatic B0 shimming and manual interactive B0 shimming. Various automatic shimming options were provided, including DESS standard, DESS advanced, GRE brain, but it was not clear which is the best sequence to use, how many times each sequence should be run to converge to a good and stable B0 homogeneity, and how the automatic shimming quality compares to that of manual interactive shimming.

We sought to find an optimal shimming procedure combining automatic and interactive B0 shimming, tested the abovementioned 3 automatic methods, each followed up by an interactive shimming. We characterized the shimming quality by 2D EPI BOLD image quality (blurriness) and distortion (local similarity to anatomical T1 image), and B0 phase map frequency distributions within the brain mask (distribution shape and center, median, standard deviation, normalized root mean square error versus 0 Hz). 

Methods

In one healthy subject (sex: female, no dental wires, minimal head motion), we compared three automatic B0 shimming options (DESS standard, DESS advanced, GRE brain), with the same adjust volume box tightly touching the edges of the brain (instead of the scalp).

For each automatic shimming option, we started from the scanner tune-up settings (“Load tune-up” button), performing 3 automatic shimming iterations, followed by a 4th manual interactive shimming. For each iteration per option, we acquired one B0 map (Siemens product sequence gre_b0map, 2 mm isotropic), and one BOLD 2D EPI sequence (CMRR multiband=2, iPAT=3, TR=2000 ms, TE=21 ms, spatial resolution=1.2 mm isotropic, 74 slices, 20 TRs, phase-encoding direction: PA, FatSat turned off, no EPI distortion correction). We also acquired the same B0 and 2D EPI sequences for the tune-up setting, and attempted interactive shimming directly from tune-up. This turned out to be very difficult, thus we performed interactive shimming after the GRE brain automatic shimming.  

For the interactive shimming, we always aimed to maximize the T2* (as close as 20 Hz) and minimize the FWHM (below 40 Hz), similar to our practice on the 7T Magnetom. After each shimming, we also observed the system resonance frequency distribution in the frequency tab.

To quantitatively evaluate the B0 shimming quality, we examined the B0 phase map’s median and standard deviation within the brain mask, the FWHM of the distribution, the normalized root mean square error (NRMSE) versus zero Hz, and the BOLD EPI distortion compared to a T1-weighted MPRAGE anatomical sequence (0.8 mm isotropic) by Neighborhood correlation^1 in ANTs (ImageMath). The B0 shimming influence on the tSNR of the BOLD sequence (mean signal divided by standard deviation) was also examined after standard preprocessing (slice-scan time correction, motion correction, temporal high-pass filtering, coregistration to the anatomy).

Results

For the EPI BOLD sequences, bad shimming induced large EPI distortion in the phase-encoding direction, and bad image quality including blurry anatomical structures, intensity fluctuations inside the brain, and dirty background outside the brain (Figure 1A). However the peak of the tSNR distribution was minimally affected by different B0 shimming options.

For the B0 phase maps, bad shimming induced a broad frequency distribution within the brain mask, with median far away from zero Hz and large standard deviations (Figure 1B, metrics quantified in Figure 1C). Good shimming induced much sharper (narrow and smooth) distribution with smaller FWHM, see e.g. results of the GRE Brain iterations, although most of the frequency distributions were not centered on zero Hz. The various B0 shimming iterations produced B0 phase maps with different spatial variations, i.e. they did not converge to a single best shimming. This variability across iterations may be dependent on the brain position in the magnetic field modulated by head motion, compounded by frequency drift of the scanner after a period of scanning.

Across all 3 automatic shimming options (Figure 1C), GRE brain was the quickest and most stable sequence that converged to a narrow resonance frequency distribution before interactive shimming. This was reflected in all B0 and EPI distortion metrics tested. Interactive shimming in general provided better B0 homogeneity, when the T2* and FWHM in the interactive shimming tab were properly maximized/minimized. The T2* and FWHM values for interactive shimming following each automatic shimming option are, DESS standard: 18 ms, 31.7 Hz; DESS advanced: 14 ms, 41.5 Hz (not optimally maximized/minimized, quantitatively reflected in Figure 1C); GRE brain: 17 ms, 35.3 Hz; GRE brain after tune-up: 17 ms, 35.2 Hz.

During the scan, we observed that the resonance frequency distribution graphs in the frequency tab (|Max| and FWHM) were a better indicator of the B0 shimming quality than the T2* and FWHM values in the interactive shimming tab. In the frequency tab, a tall, narrow and smooth water peak with a small bump of the fat component (high |Max|>50000 and low FWHM<30 Hz) is desirable (verified in a later scan, Figure 2). A bad B0 shimming would produce a noisy frequency distribution worse than the ones showed in Figure 2. Thus it’s worth manually checking the frequency tab before starting actual data acquisition, rather than letting the scanner perform it automatically without showing the graph.

Conclusion

We found that the GRE brain is the fastest-converging and most stable automatic B0 shimming method. Proper interactive shimming is usually advantageous, but takes time, and may have inter-operator variability. We plan to further compare multiple other automatic shimming options (although may not be existing default options provided in the Terra.X system), and further explore the B0 shimming inter-subject variability in a more diverse group of subjects, including males/females with more/less scalp fat, bigger/smaller head sizes, with/without dental inserts e.g. wires behind the teeth.

Based on our current observations, we propose the following optimized automatic plus manual B0 shimming procedure:  

Step 1. Start with the GRE brain automatic shimming, check the frequency distribution in the frequency tab. Perform a few (1-3) iterations until the FWHM is below 70 Hz, which gives a good starting point for manual interactive shimming. Record the |Max| and FWHM values as a reference point. If there is no further desire to do interactive shimming, it is better to perform a few more iterations until the FWHM converges and no longer reduces (usually can converge around 50-60 Hz).

Step 2. Start interactive shimming, aim for a T2* > 17 ms and a FWHM < 40 Hz, whenever possible. Check the frequency distribution again, and verify if the FWHM < 40 Hz while the |Max| is higher than before.

The automatic + interactive shimming procedure could be done within 10 minutes, and provides very good B0 shimming quality.

We hope our investigation will be useful for all users at 7T or higher fields, with high requirements of B0 homogeneity (fMRI, diffusion imaging, spectroscopy).
