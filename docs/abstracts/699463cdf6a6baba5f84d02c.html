Introduction

Sometimes, our mind feels empty, as if there is nothing there, even when awake and alert; a mental state known as Mind Blanking (MB)1. Understanding the neural correlates of MB is crucial, as it not only challenges assumptions about a content-full nature of thinking, but it can also provide insights about atypical cognitive function, such as in ADHD2. 

So far, MB research has primarily used probe-caught methods, where participants are interrupted at random times to report their mental state, either during tasks or while at rest1. At rest, probed MB (pMB) is reported uniformly across time, iti s associated with an all-to-all positive functional connectivity brain pattern3, and is characterized by whole-brain deactivations compared to other mental states with content4. Importantly, fMRI scans around MB reports are associated with increased global signal. As the global signal tracks arousal, it suggests there is a mediation of low vigilance when reporting MB3,5.

It should be noted that probe-caught methods rely on externally timed cues, thus MB episodes that can occur between probes may be missed. Instead, self-caught methods rely on the person’s subjective evaluation to report a mental state when they realize they are in this particular state. This method has been applied in mind wandering (MW) research, but not yet to MB. Here, we investigated whether catching oneself with an empty mind (self-caught MB, scMB) represents the same brain-based phenomenon as it being detected by probes. We specifically focused on the hyper-connected brain pattern3,5, hypothesizing that this brain configuration will also emerge as a relevant pattern of the scMB.

Methods

Twenty-two individuals participated in the study (45% female; mean age = 29 ± 4 years). All were right-handed, native French speakers with academic backgrounds. The study consisted of a within-subject design, in which participants performed two experience-sampling tasks one with the self-caught, and the other with the probe-caught methodology. The tasks were performed in a 3T MRI scanner (Magnetom Allegra, Siemens Medical Solutions, Erlangen, Germany) utilizing standard EPI fMRI sequences. In the self-caught task (8 min duration), participants were asked to press a button whenever they felt they had a MB state. In the probe-caught task (approx. 19 min duration), participants were interrupted at random times by auditory and visual probes (n=25, inter-probe interval: 30-60s), inviting them to use button press to report their immediate mental state choosing among MB, MW and Sensations. All participants completed first the self-caught task, among other cognitive tasks not described there, and after a pause, they performed the probe-caught task. During both tasks, participants were in a resting state with eyes open, fixating on a black screen. Following previous studies3,5, the mental states (scMB, pMB, MW, and Sensations) were defined as the 10 seconds before the probe or the button press for scMB. Rest periods were defined as 30 seconds of uninterrupted resting activity in both tasks.

We analyzed both behavioral and brain correlates. Behaviorally, we assessed the consistency of individual MB reporting across conditions by correlating the number of MB reports per subject in each condition. We also examined the temporal distribution of MB reports across time. Acquisition time was divided into 5 equal-duration bins, and the number of MB reports in each bin was counted. We then compared the resulting distribution against a uniform distribution using a χ² test, and calculated effect size with the Phi coefficient.

Brain-wise, fMRI data was preprocessed following the fMRIPrep (version 24.1.1) pipeline: after parcellating the brain with Schaefer-100 atlas (7-network version), we calculated the instantaneous phase functional connectivity (FC) at each volume, applying the second-order Hilbert transform. Following previous pipelines applied to MB3,5, we performed k-means clustering on the FC of the rest periods of both tasks, utilizing the cosine distance. The number of clusters (k) was predefined based on previous research that finds four stable brain patterns during resting state3. We then computed the cosine distance between each FC associated with each mental state to the four centroids. We fitted a generalized linear mixed-effect model with a gamma distribution to explain the distances to each FC centroid, with mental state as fixed effect and subject as a random effect. Pos-hoc comparisons were calculated using estimated marginal means with Tukey’s HSD adjustment for multiple comparisons. The same statistical analysis was applied to compare the global signal amplitude across the mental states. 

Results

Participants reported on average 5 scMB (range = 0-31, N = 119) and 2 pMB episodes (range = 0-7, N = 55). Individual MB reporting was consistent across methods, with the number of reports per subject significantly positively correlated between tasks (r = .47, p = .03).  The temporal distribution of MB reports across acquisition time did not significantly deviate from a uniform distribution in either task (scMB: χ2= 1.13, p = .89, φ = .10; pMB: χ 2=5.27, p = .26, φ = .31).

Brain-wise, scMB showed significantly higher global signal compared to MW (Δ = -0.001, 95% CI [-0.002, -0.0001], z = -2.81, p = .025). Regarding the functional connectivity, k-means clustering identified a hyperconnected pattern characterized by an overall high inter-areal coherence (Pattern 3). Analysis of distances to this hyperconnected pattern revealed that only scMB reports organized significantly closer to this centroid compared to all other mental states (scMB-pMB: Δ = -0.02, 95% CI [-0.03 -0.008], z = -4.61, p < .0001; scMB-MW: Δ = -0.02, 95% CI [-0.03 -0.02], z = -7.71, p < .0001 ; scMB-Sens: Δ = -0.02, 95% CI [-0.03 -0.02], z = -6.29, p < .0001). In contrast, pMB, MW and Sensations showed no significant differences in their distances to the hyperconnectivity pattern (p < .05). 

Conclusion

We employed fMRI experience sampling of MB using self-caught and probe-caught approaches (scMB and pMB, respectively). Behaviorally, scMB and pMB showed similar profiles, with individual MB reporting consistent across approaches. Brain-wise, scMB was associated with the expected hyperconnected pattern and higher global signal, resembling past pMB studies3,5. We propose that the self-caught methodology is adequate to detect MB events, at least those mediated by meta-awareness. In that respect, pMB reports might reflect a broader aspect of MB, as it could catch both events with and without meta-awareness. In fact, a recent study found pMB being associated not only with the hyperconnected brain pattern, but also with an anticorrelation pattern in the context of a sustained attention to response task5. Here, the intrinsic heterogeneity in pMB, combined with pMB being reported less than half as frequently as scMB in our study, likely resulted in insufficient power to detect its neural effects.

Taken together, scMB successfully replicated previous findings on MB, suggesting it captures the MB phenomenon. These findings validate self-caught methodology as a complementary approach for studying MB, enabling new experimental protocols, where probe-caught approaches might be disrupting the stream of thought. Overall, our work advances our ability to investigate content-less experiences that are of both theoretical and clinical relevance.
