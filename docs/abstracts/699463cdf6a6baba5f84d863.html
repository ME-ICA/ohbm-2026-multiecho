Introduction

Characterizing directed interactions between brain regions is a central challenge in network neuroscience. While structural connectivity provides anatomical constraints and functional connectivity captures statistical dependencies, effective connectivity (EC) aims to infer causal influences between neural populations. Dynamic Causal Modeling (DCM)1 offers a principled, biophysically grounded framework for EC estimation, but it requires explicit model specification, strong prior assumptions, and becomes computationally intractable for large-scale networks. An alternative approach is the multivariate Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (mOU)2 model, which describes whole-brain activity using linear stochastic dynamics and estimates EC by matching empirical covariance structures through Lyapunov optimization. The mOU framework is highly computationally efficient and scalable to whole-brain parcellations, yet its performance critically depends on the choice of the global dynamical time constant τ. In recent applications, τ is often estimated from a limited portion of the autocorrelation function, potentially leading to biased estimates and unreliable EC matrices. A systematic investigation of the role of τ, its impact on directionality, the scalability of mOU-derived EC and its consistency with established methods such as DCM is currently lacking.

Methods

We studied the role of the dynamical time constant τ in the mOU model applied to resting-state fMRI data from the Human Connectome Project (HCP). We analyzed data from 1,024 healthy subjects, using the minimally preprocessed resting-state fMRI pipeline provided by the HCP. This preprocessing includes correction for spatial distortions, head motion, and gradient non-linearities, registration to standard space, and intensity normalization. Regional time series were extracted by parcellating the cortex using the Schaefer atlas at multiple spatial resolutions (62, 100, and 200 regions). The parcellization with 62 ROIs was obtained in previous works through a consensus clustering algorithm3, starting from the Schaefer atlas with 100 ROIs, in order to deal with a reduced number of ROIs, suitable for DCM models.

The mOU model describes regional activity as a linear stochastic process governed by a Jacobian matrix composed of a global decay term −1/τ and a directed effective connectivity (EC) matrix C. Model parameters were estimated by minimizing the discrepancy between empirical and model-predicted zero-lag and lagged covariance matrices using Lyapunov optimization. The lag of the empirical covariance was fixed to one repetition time, consistent with standard practice in fMRI analyses.

We analytically decomposed the Jacobian into symmetric and antisymmetric components, showing that τ explicitly controls their relative contribution and thus regulates the emergence of directionality in EC estimates. Then we applied the mOU model to the HCP dataset, we explored a wide range of τ values, and identified regimes in which the optimization converges and yields stable EC matrices. To validate the inferred EC, we compared mOU-derived connectivity with Sparse DCM4 estimates obtained from the same data, which have been analyzed in another work3. Scalability and robustness were assessed through cross-parcellation and coarse-graining analyses. Network properties were characterized using graph-theoretical measures such as in-strength, out-strength, and community structure. Finally, to assess subject-specificity, we performed connectivity fingerprinting, quantifying how accurately each subject could be identified across different scanning sessions (REST 1 and REST 2 scans of the HCP) based on the similarity of their EC or functional connectivity pattern5

Results

We found that τ strongly influences the structure of inferred EC matrices. For small τ values, EC becomes overly symmetric and loses directionality, while for large τ values the inferred matrices are highly asymmetric but noisy and biologically implausible. We identified a broad range of τ values (typically between 0.6 and 1 TR) for which the model converges reliably and produces EC matrices that balance symmetric and antisymmetric components. Within this range, EC exhibits non-trivial correlations with empirical functional covariance, while remaining distinct from purely symmetric solutions.

EC matrices inferred in this regime show good agreement with Sparse DCM estimates, despite fundamental differences in model assumptions (sparsity prior) and inference strategies. The mOU approach scales efficiently to large parcellations, successfully estimating EC for networks exceeding 80 regions, beyond the practical limits of current DCM implementations. Coarse-graining analyses reveal consistent EC patterns across spatial resolutions, supporting the robustness of the method. Moreover, EC matrices substantially improve subject fingerprinting accuracy compared to functional connectivity alone, especially with low-resolution parcellization: we find that the fingerprinting accuracy through EC is consistently over 0.80% with any, also low-resolution, atlas, while FC-based fingerprinting needs at least 200 ROIs to achieve  0.70% of accuracy (with 1024 subjects).

Conclusion

Our results demonstrate that appropriate selection of the dynamical time constant τ is crucial for reliable whole-brain EC estimation with the mOU model. By clarifying the mathematical and empirical role of τ, we provide a principled strategy to obtain consistent, directed, and biologically interpretable EC matrices. The proposed framework is computationally efficient, scalable to large networks, and yields results consistent with Sparse DCM while enabling applications that are currently infeasible with traditional model-based approaches. This work establishes the mOU model as a practical tool for large-scale effective connectivity analyses in human neuroimaging.
