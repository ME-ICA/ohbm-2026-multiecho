Introduction

Multi-site neuroimaging enables larger, more representative cohorts, but technical differences between scanners can still obscure true biological variation. Prior same-subject and large multi-site studies demonstrate that systematic scanner and site differences contribute measurable variability to structural MRI metrics despite harmonized acquisition and calibration.1 When identical hardware, software, and harmonized high-resolution protocols are used, site-related variability can be substantially reduced, yet its residual impact remains poorly quantified. To address this, we conducted a within-subject, same-day dual-scanner study using matched Siemens Prisma 3T systems and a standardized multi-modal protocol.2 Our study isolates scanner-related variability under near-optimal conditions and establishes benchmarks for the reliability of structural metrics derived from FastSurfer, assessing cortical thickness as well as cortical and subcortical volumes.

Methods

Analyses were conducted on a pilot cohort of 16 healthy adults (mean age 38.6 ± 15.4 years, 62.5% female) using the protocol and scanners from the Rhineland Study, a population-based prospective cohort based in Bonn, Germany. Each participant completed MRI sessions on the same day at two separate sites, equipped with matched Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma 3T systems running software version VE11C. Same-day scans were separated by at most 3 hours, ensuring minimal physiological or circadian variation between sessions, with randomized site order across participants. To ensure scanner stability and early detection of hardware or software deviations, both sites performed a standardized daily quality-assurance protocol using dedicated phantoms. The harmonized multi-modal imaging protocol comprised T1-weighted images (0.8 mm isotropic), T2-weighted images (0.8 mm isotropic), and T2-FLAIR images (1.0 mm isotropic), diffusion-weighted imaging, resting-state fMRI, and an abdominal Dixon sequence for adipose tissue quantification.2 All imaging parameters and sequence versions were aligned across sites. For the present analysis, we focused on T1w-derived structural phenotypes processed using a standardized longitudinal FastSurfer pipeline (v2.4.2).3 Cortical thickness, grey matter volume, and subcortical volumes were extracted using consistent software versions and identical parameter settings. Inter-site agreement was evaluated using paired t-tests, intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC, two-way agreement), and relative site differences referenced to the mean of both measurements. We also included same-session repeat T1w scans from a different subset of participants (N = 90) to estimate the intrinsic test-retest variability of a single scanner and directly compared absolute percentage differences between intra-scanner and inter-scanner conditions using Wilcoxon-signed rank test. This internal benchmark allowed us to determine whether inter-site differences exceeded normal within-scanner fluctuation and head position or movement influences.

Results

Paired analyses of FastSurfer-derived morphometric measures from two nominally identical Siemens Prisma 3T scanners (N = 16) revealed high overall agreement. Subcortical volumes demonstrated excellent reproducibility, with an average ICC of 0.99 ± 0.008 [range: 0.96-1.00]. However, paired t-tests identified significant site differences in 15 of 22 subcortical regions after FDR correction, including cerebral white matter (left: PFDR = 1.8x10-4; right: PFDR = 1.7x10-3). These differences were consistently biased, with site A reporting volumes larger by up to 2% relative to the structure’s volume. Grey matter volume estimates were similarly robust, with a mean ICC of 0.99 ± 0.014 [range: 0.93-1.00] across 62 regions. Despite this, 17 regions exhibited significant inter-site differences after FDR correction, mostly showing larger values at site A, although seven predominantly frontal regions demonstrated the opposite direction of bias. Relative differences in cortical volumes were small with mean relative difference of 0.69% ± 0.89%.  Cortical thickness showed the lowest, yet still excellent, reliability, with a mean ICC of 0.94 ± 0.049 [range: 0.76-0.99]. Few regions, including bilateral pericalcarine and lateral and medial orbitofrontal sulci, showed reduced ICC values. Unlike subcortical and cortical volume measures, no cortical thickness region differed significantly between sites after FDR correction, with relative differences ranging bidirectionally from approximately -1.58% to +1.55%. 

Intra-scanner test-retest analyses (N = 90) demonstrated that cortical thickness showed lower reliability than volumes, with a mean ICC of 0.89 ± 0.07 and regional patterns of reduced stability strongly overlapping those observed in the inter-scanner comparison. Sixteen of 62 cortical thickness regions differed significantly between repeated scans after FDR correction, and their relative percentage differences matched the magnitude and spatial distribution of inter-site thickness biases. Grey-matter volumes exhibited higher repeatability (mean ICC = 0.98 ± 0.01), with only one region showing a significant test-retest difference and relative differences similar to those seen across scanners. Subcortical volumes were the most stable measures (mean ICC = 0.99 ± 0.006), with no significant test-retest differences and relative changes well below 1%. 

Direct comparison of region-wise absolute percentage differences confirmed that inter-scanner variability modestly exceeded within-scanner test-retest variability for both grey matter volumes (mean absolute difference: inter = 0.92% ± 0.65%, intra = 0.50% ± 0.35%; one-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p = 1.4x10-5) and subcortical volumes (inter = 1.10% ± 0.49%, intra = 0.20% ± 0.11%; p = 2.1x10-5). In contrast, absolute cortical thickness differences did not differ significantly between inter- and intra-scanner conditions (inter = 0.45% ± 0.37%, intra = 0.41% ± 0.39%; p = 0.37).

Overall, the intra-scanner benchmark confirmed that the inter-site effects observed in the main analysis modestly exceeded normal within-scanner variability, while generally remaining small.

Conclusion

FastSurfer produced highly reproducible morphometric estimates across harmonized Siemens Prisma 3T scanners, with near-perfect agreement for subcortical and cortical volumes and strong reliability for cortical thickness. Although several volumetric regions showed systematic inter-site differences, their magnitude was small and closely mirrored the spatial patterns seen in the intra-scanner test-retest dataset. The within-scanner benchmark confirmed that cortical thickness is intrinsically less stable than volumetric measures and that some regional vulnerabilities reflect general measurement sensitivity rather than site-specific artifacts. This reduced stability is consistent with prior work showing that within-session head motion increases over time and can induce spurious cortical thinning,4 suggesting that the observed intra-scanner effects may partly reflect motion-related variability rather than scanner instability.5 Importantly, inter-site variability exceeded same-session repeatability but remained modest in magnitude. These findings indicate that FastSurfer-derived metrics are suitable for large-scale, multi-center applications, provided that statistical models account for residual scanner bias. The dual-scanner, same-day design used here isolates pure scanner effects with high precision and offers a framework for future extensions to additional imaging modalities and processing pipelines.
