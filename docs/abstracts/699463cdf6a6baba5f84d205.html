Introduction

Time is gone forever. Although human beings do not have specific physiological organs to receive time information, they can still accurately perceive temporal duration—ranging from milliseconds to seconds, minutes to hours and other scales. Temporal duration perception is extremely important for the survival and development of individuals, and it is also the basis of various psychological activities, including motor timing (the ability to coordinate movements with temporal cues, a core behavioral manifestation of temporal processing). ^1 However, the relationship between subjective time and objective time is very complicated due to numerous influencing factors.

Methamphetamine has become one of the most prevalent illicit stimulants today, imposing a heavy economic, health, and safety burden on society. ^2 Long-term methamphetamine use severely impairs people's cognitive function, brain function, and motor timing abilities. ^3 Previous studies have found that the brain damage mechanisms of methamphetamine-dependent (MUD) individuals overlap with those underlying motor timing in terms of neurobiology and brain circuits. ^4 A large number of experiments have also shown that methamphetamine can accelerate the internal temporal clock, which in turn affects motor timing and other behavioral responses related to temporal duration perception, leading to impulsive decision-making. ^5

However, research in this field involving human participants remains insufficiently in-depth. The roles of key factors such as abstinence duration and intake dose in MUD-related impairments of motor timing are still poorly understood. The neural mechanisms based on resting-state EEG underlying these deficits remain unknown.

Methods

Participants

MUD participants were recruited from the drug rehabilitation centers in China, with all being adult males. Inclusion criteria for the MUD groups followed our previous research (Zhang et al., 2019). Short-term abstinence group (1–8 months of abstinence, mean = 3 months) and long-term abstinence group (>9 months of abstinence, mean = 16 months). The healthy control group was recruited from local communities in China, consisting of adult males who were right-handed, had normal or corrected-to-normal visual acuity, self-reported no major physical/psychiatric illnesses, and no history of drug or psychotropic medication use. All participants signed an informed consent form prior to participation and received appropriate monetary compensation or equivalent daily necessities post-experiment. The study was approved by the local Ethics Committee. 31 healthy controls, 40 short-term abstinence participants, and 44 long-term abstinence participants were included in the final analysis.

Procedure

Participants first completed a series of questionnaires, followed by resting-state EEG (rEEG) recording and a duration reproduction task.

EEG Recording

Data were collected using the EGI NA400 amplifier with 128 electrodes. Electrode impedance was maintained below 50 KΩ, with a sampling rate of 500 Hz and online reference at Cz. The recording session consisted of three consecutive phases: 5 minutes of eyes closed, 5 minutes of eyes open, and 5 minutes of eyes closed. Only eye-closed data were analyzed in this study.

Duration Reproduction Task

During the encoding phase, an ellipse was displayed for 1, 2, 3, 4, or 5 seconds, and participants were required to memorize its duration. The inter-stimulus interval was 600–800 ms. During the reproduction phase, an identical ellipse appeared, and participants pressed the spacebar when they perceived its duration as matching the encoded ellipse, triggering the disappearance of the second ellipse and the start of the next trial. The formal experiment included 50 trials (10 trials per duration).

Behavioral Data Analysis

Prior to statistical analysis, reaction times exceeding the group-specific mean ± 3SD for each condition were excluded. Two dependent variables were analyzed: (1) Duration Reproduction Ratio (DRR), an index of temporal accuracy calculated as the ratio of reproduced duration to target duration; (2) Coefficient of Variation (CV), an index of temporal variability calculated as the ratio of the standard deviation to the mean of a participant’s responses for each condition.

EEG Data Analysis

EEG preprocessing was done in MATLAB R2016b with EEGLAB 2020. Two eye-closed datasets were merged; bad channels were interpolated, and 39 facial/neck electrodes were removed. Data were bandpass-filtered (1–40 Hz) with a 50 Hz notch filter, downsampled to 250 Hz, and segmented into 2-second epochs. Bad epochs were manually rejected, ICA was used to remove ocular/artifacts, and the TBT plugin handled automatic artifact rejection/correction (±80 μV threshold). Participants with <80% usable epochs were excluded. Microstate analysis was conducted using the MST 1.0 plugin for EEGLAB. Four microstate metrics were extracted for each participant: mean duration (ms), occurrence (n/s), coverage (%), and transition probability.

Statistics

Statistical analyses included one-sample t-tests, independent samples t-tests, univariate analysis of variance (ANOVA), and repeated-measures analysis of covariance (ANCOVA). Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied when Mauchly’s test of sphericity was violated, and post-hoc comparisons were Bonferroni-corrected. Additionally, Pearson correlation analyses were performed. To explore whether microstates played a key mediating role, mediation analyses were conducted using the Bootstrap method via the PROCESS 3.5 plugin for SPSS.

Results

First, regarding EEG microstate differences across the three groups, the results showed that relative to the healthy control group, the short-term abstinence MUD group exhibited no significant abnormalities in microstates A, C, and D, while microstate B showed significantly decreased occurrence frequency and time coverage ratio—and despite unaltered average duration, its overall neural activity was attenuated. In the long-term abstinence MUD group, microstate A remained unaltered, all metrics of microstate B were significantly reduced, all indicators of microstate C were significantly elevated, and the occurrence frequency of microstate D was diminished. Therefore, microstate A remained unaffected across all MUD subgroups whereas microstate B activity was consistently attenuated. As the abstinence period prolonged, the attenuation of microstate B was accompanied by enhanced microstate C activity and further diminished microstate D activity.

Second, for the association between drug use history and microstate-related brain function in MUD groups, the results showed that abstinence duration was significantly positively correlated with all microstate C indicators and significantly negatively correlated with all microstate D metrics, pre-abstinence drug use duration was uniquely positively correlated with the transition rate from microstate D to microstate B, and pre-abstinence monthly drug consumption was significantly negatively correlated with all microstate A indicators.

Third, for the potential resting-state EEG correlates of abnormal motor timing, microstate B exerted a partial mediating role in the duration reproduction ratio for 1-second difference between the short-term abstinence group and the healthy control group. A mediating effect that was also observed for the duration reproduction ratio for 1-second difference between the long-term abstinence group and the healthy control group. In other words, irrespective of abstinence duration, microstate B played a mediating role in the 1-second temporal perception difference between MUD groups and the healthy control group. Interestingly, microstates C and D jointly exerted critical mediating effects on the duration reproduction ratio for 4-second difference between the long-term and short-term abstinence groups.

Conclusion

This study explored brain microstate characteristics, their associations with drug use history, and potential EEG mechanisms of abnormal motor timing in MUD populations with different abstinence durations. Key findings indicated that microstate B activity was consistently decreased in MUD groups, with prolonged abstinence linked to increased microstate C and decreased microstate D activity, while specific drug use history variables differentially correlated microstate dynamics. Additionally, microstates B, C, and D mediated temporal perception differences, providing potential electrophysiological markers for evaluating abstinence progress. These findings further advance our understanding of the neural basis of brain function impairments in MUD individuals during abstinence and lays a foundation for the development of targeted clinical intervention strategies.
