Introduction

Prediction is essential in executing voluntary actions, relying on internal models like the forward model, which forecasts sensory feedback from movements. This aids in real-time corrections and smoother motor functions. The forward model also anticipates external outcomes of one's own actions, with key brain regions such as the dorsal premotor cortex and superior parietal lobe playing significant roles in predicting the actions of others. Higher neural activity in these areas correlates with improved prediction accuracy.^1 However, existing research tends to overlook the prediction of one's own action, although behavioral studies show individuals are more accurate at forecasting their own actions compared to others'. 

An impaired internal model can diminish the ability to predict actions. Developmental coordination disorder (DCD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by difficulty in performing coordinated movements without underlying physical issues. Individuals with DCD may struggle with predicting their movements due to impaired internal models, leading to challenges in motor control and learning.^2 Consequently, individuals with motor coordination issues might have difficulties in predicting both their own and others' actions, along with abnormalities in related neural activity. 

The present study examined the neurological features linked to observing and predicting one’s own actions, focusing on temporal information processing using electroencephalography (EEG). It was hypothesized that individuals would be more accurate in predicting their own actions compared to others, and that neural activity in the motor cortex would be more pronounced for self-prediction. Moreover, individuals with impaired motor coordination were expected to exhibit lower neural activity in predicting both their own and others' actions compared to those with normal motor coordination.

Methods

A total of 19 adults participated in the experiment. Two participants were excluded due to significant EEG artifacts, resulting in 17 participants (6 males, mean age = 22.2 ± 1.6 years, all right-handed) being included in the analysis. The study received approval from the Research Ethics Committee of Keio University, Japan (#22009-1), and all participants provided written informed consent.

In the action prediction task, participants watched first-person perspective videos of a ball being thrown towards a target and made two judgments: a self-other judgment (determining if the action was their own or someone else's) and a hit-miss judgment (predicting whether the ball hit or missed the target) (Figure 1). Each trial began with a 500-1000ms fixation point, followed by a 2300ms video, and responses were made using a keyboard after a 500ms wait. The task consisted of four conditions: hit movements performed by the participant (Self-Hit), missed movements performed by the participant (Self-Miss), hit movements performed by others (Other-Hit), and missed movements performed by others (Other-Miss). All video stimuli were created based on recordings made using an HMD prior to the experiment. The experiment consisted of a total of 450 trials (number of trials; Self-Hit/Other-Hit = 75/150; Self-Miss/Other-Miss = 75/150), which were conducted in 5 blocks. During the task, EEG and electrooculogram (EOG) signals were recorded using the Biosemi Active2 system (2048 Hz) from 32 electrodes, according to the 10–20 system, with CMS/DRL placed at AF1/AF2 and the earlobes serving as references.

              Behavioral performance was assessed by calculating the accuracy of self-other judgments (proportion of correct identifications for Self-Hit/Miss and Other-Hit/Miss) and hit-miss judgments (correct predictions of action outcomes). A two-way ANOVA was used to analyze accuracy with self-other and hit-miss factors.

              Regarding the EEG analysis, the acquired EEG data were band-pass filtered between 0.1 and 90 Hz and notch filtered at 50 Hz. After re-referencing using earlobe electrodes, the analysis segment was extracted from 500 ms before to 2500 ms after the video stimulus presentation. Artifacts related to eye movements were corrected using principal component analysis, followed by visual inspection to exclude any additional artifacts. After averaging the data for self (i.e., Self-Hit and Self-Miss) and other (i.e., Other-Hit and Other-Miss) conditions, time-frequency analysis using a continuous wavelet transform was performed on the C3, C4, and Cz electrodes. For the EEG time-frequency analysis data, a t-test was used to compare values at the corresponding time and frequency points between the two conditions of self and other conditions. To address the multiple comparisons problem, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) procedure for false discovery rate (FDR) control at a significance level of 0.05.

To investigate neural activity in individuals with impaired motor coordination, participants were classified into two groups based on their MABC-2 scores, a standardized test to assess motor coordination ability: the MABC-low group and the MABC-control group. The mean EEG power values were calculated for each group during the time interval (2000-2300 ms) and frequency range (5-10 Hz) where significant differences in neural activity were observed between the self-trial and other-trial conditions (see Results). The EEG power values were examined using a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with group factor (MABC-low/MABC-control) and condition factor (Self/Other). All analyses were conducted using MATLAB 2022b and the toolbox EEGLAB v2024.2.1.

Results

Self-other judgement accuracy was 0.60 ± 0.02 (mean ± SEM). The self-identification accuracy was 0.54 ± 0.06, while the other-identification accuracy was 0.64 ± 0.03. Regarding the prediction accuracy, a two-way ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of the self-other factor (F(1,16) = 6.07, p < .05, η² = .09), a significant main effect of the hit-miss factor (F(1,16) = 7.74, p < .05, η² = .14), and a marginal significant interaction (F(1,16) = 4.07, p = .06, η² = .02). Post hoc analysis showed that the accuracy of Self-Hit prediction (0.62 ± 0.06) was significantly higher than that of Other-Hit prediction (0.41 ± 0.06; p < .05). Conversely, for other predictions, the accuracy in hit trials (Other-Hit) was significantly lower than that in miss trials (Other-Miss, 0.66 ± 0.04; p < .01). No significant difference was observed between Self-Hit and Self-Miss prediction accuracy (Self-Miss, 0.73 ± 0.04; p = .17), nor between Self-Miss and Other-Miss prediction accuracy (p = .18).

A comparison of the neural activity during the action prediction task revealed that the neural activity was significantly higher in the self-trial condition (Figure 2). Moreover, prominent neural activity was observed in both conditions 2000 ms after the onset of the video stimulus presentation, with significantly higher neural activity in the self-trial condition, particularly at C3 and Cz. Differences in power values between the self and other trial conditions were particularly pronounced in the 5-10 Hz frequency range during the 2000-2300 ms time window (i.e. the period during which the ball trajectory was visible). Correlations between the power value difference (self vs. other) and the Self-Hit prediction accuracy were significant only at C3 (C3, r = 0.50, p < .05; Cz, r = 0.02, p = .93; C4, r = 0.34, p = .19).

A two-way ANOVA was used to analyze the mean neural activity power values in the 5–10 Hz range during the 2000–2300 ms time window for the MABC-low (n = 4) and MABC-control (n = 13) groups. At C3, the main effect of condition and interaction were significant (condition effect, F(1,15) = 10.43, p < .01, η² = .10; interaction F(1,15) = 4.58, p < .05, η² = .05) while the main effect of group was not significant (F(1,15) = 0.28, p = .60, η² = .01). Post hoc analysis showed that, in the MABC-control group, neural activity during the self-trial condition was significantly higher than during the other-trial condition (Self condition, 1.34 ± 0.20 μV², Other condition, 0.52 ± 0.15 μV², p < .001). In the MABC-low group, there was not significant difference between self and other trial conditions (Self condition, 0.84 ± 0.26 μV², Other condition, 0.68 ± 0.22 μV², p = .26). At Cz and C4, only the main effect of the stimulus condition factor was significant (Cz, F(1,15) = 12.93, p < .01, η² = .18; C4, F(1,15) = 16.43, p < .01, η² = .21), while the main effect of group and the interaction were not significant (Cz, main effect of group, F(1,15) = 0.92, p = .35, η² = .03, interaction, F(1,15) = 2.09, p = .17, η² = .03; C4, main effect of group, F(1,15) = 0.01, p = .94, η² = .00, interaction, F(1,15) = 0.43, p = .52, η² = .01). The power value difference between Self and Other conditions were compared between groups, and the MABC-low group showed a significantly lower values at C3 (MABC-control, 0.82 ± 0.16 μV², MABC-low, 0.17 ± 0.12 μV², t(13.18) = -3.25, p < .01). No significant differences were found at Cz (MABC-control, 0.91 ± 0.64 μV²; MABC-low, 0.39 ± 0.30 μV²; t(5.28) = -1.50, p = .19) or C4 (MABC-control, 0.40 ± 0.08 μV²; MABC-low, 0.29 ± 0.40 μV²; t(4.16) = -0.57, p = .60).

Conclusion

The present findings indicated that individuals are more accurate in predicting their own actions compared to others'. Neural activity associated with predicting one's own action was significantly greater than that associated with predicting others' action, correlating with the accuracy of predicting one's own action. Notably, individuals with impaired motor coordination exhibited no significant differences in neural activity between self and other trial conditions, unlike those with normal coordination. 

 The prediction accuracy of Other-Hit trials was lower than that of Self-Hit trials. The participants recognized Other-Hit trials as “miss” because the others' action, whether it was a hit or miss, was different from their own “hit” action. In other words, they used their own “hit” action as a reference for the prediction. Importantly, positive correlation was found between the accuracy of self-identification and self-action prediction, especially for hit trials (i.e., Self-Hit prediction accuracy), suggesting that both self-identification and action prediction are commonly based on the forward model. Previous studies have also reported a higher prediction accuracy for one's own action than others' actions.^3 However, self-identification accuracy does not necessarily improve action prediction. Bischoff et al. (2012)^4 reported high prediction accuracy of one's own action even when the participants were not informed that their own actions were included in the judgment task. Thus, self-identification and action prediction are based on the forward model but may operate independently.

The EEG power during the prediction of one's own action was significantly higher than that during the prediction of others' actions. Variability in neural activity may stem from the activation level of the mirror neuron system and the familiarity with the actions　(i.e., well-learned or high-frequency). Calvo-Merino et al. (2005)^5 indicated that the mirror neuron system was more active when individuals were proficient in a particular action and observed the action. First-person simulations generated greater activation in the somatosensory cortex compared to third-person simulations. Visual information concerning actions activates the motor and somatosensory cortices through the mirror neuron system, facilitating internal action representation. In addition, the supramarginal gyrus and superior parietal lobule, which receive input from S1, are activated during action observation. S1 activity encodes the predictive sensory consequences of an action, whereas M1 activity is modulated by the kinematic properties of the observed action (e.g., velocity). Furthermore, the ability to extract kinematic features from action observations is associated with the accuracy of action prediction, and M1 inhibition reduces the accuracy of action prediction. These findings suggest that the accuracy of action prediction is based on both the S1 and M1 via the mirror neuron system; therefore, the neural activity for predicting one's own action was enhanced in the present study.

In the MABC-low group, minimal differences were observed between neural activities for Self and Others' conditions, with these differences correlating to the Self-Hit prediction accuracy. The reduced neural activity during the prediction of one's own actionsuggests an insufficient activation of the mirror neuron system, impacting the ability to generate internal action representations or map them effectively to motor and sensory cortices. This insufficiency may contribute to impaired motor coordination skills, affecting tasks like posture maintenance and movement control. Effective motor coordination requires balancing body movements and regulating muscle forces, which relies on modifying motor commands based on sensory feedback. In cases of inadequate mirror neuron system activation, coordinating actions becomes challenging, leading to motor coordination impairments such as DCD.
