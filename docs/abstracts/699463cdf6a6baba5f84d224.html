Introduction

The normative modelling framework establishes a reference standard based on a large number of healthy participants to predict a particular metric of interest, starting from individual demographic variables such as age and sex. Individual observations (e.g., from psychosis patients) are then benchmarked against this prediction to obtain a personalised score of deviance from the expected biological metric (Rutherford, 2022). In our previous work, we developed a method to build voxel- or vertex-wise normative models of mean diffusivity (MD) and mean kurtosis (MK) derived from multi-shell diffusion MRI data (dMRI), thus providing a powerful resource for researchers and clinicians to gain deeper insights into voxel-wise individual-level deviations in brain microstructure (Pavan, 2025). However, the greatest challenge to normative modelling is the handling of the complex harmonisation problem, which is particularly pronounced in multi-shell diffusion data. Non-biological variability introduced by different scanner hardware and acquisition protocols can mask true biological deviations. One powerful method to achieve harmonisation is Generalised Additive Models for Location, Scale, and Shape (GAMLSS; Rigby, 2005). GAMLSS is an univariate distributional regression framework that allows modelling any non-Gaussian distribution, accounting for location, scale, skewness and kurtosis, which has long been used for normative modelling of brain data and by the World Health Organisation for growth charts. Here, we report the results of our investigation on the dependence of MD and MK on different continuous acquisition parameters (AQP) across diverse open source and local datasets.

Methods

To evaluate the impact of different continuous acquisition parameters, we utilised multi-shell diffusion MRI data from 2033 healthy participants (HC) from 6 datasets (Human Connectome Project (HCP), 304 HCPdevelopment, 512 HCPaging, 1032 HCPyoung-adults, 56 HCPearly-psychosis; and Lausanne Psychosis (LSP) 59 LSPPrisma, and 70 LSPTrio) aged 15 to 70 years old. The HC group was split into 1362 HC for the model training (in-sample HC) and a test set (out-of-sample HC) composed of 671 randomly-sampled HC (~33%). The acquisition parameters taken into account were: dataset (Fig. 1A), echo time (Fig. 1B), repetition time (Fig. 1C), individual scanner (Fig. 1D), voxel volume (Fig. 1E), and total number of diffusion encoding directions (measurements, Fig. 1F). The table in Fig. 1 summarises the dataset counts for each value of the parameters .
After standard pre-processing (MP-PCA, Gibbs unringing, distortion and motion correction), individual MD and MK maps were computed using constrained weighted least squares (max b-value=3000 s/mm2) and registered to a study-level template, where the white matter (WM) was regularly sampled, including 5% of voxels (~8400 voxel count), to yield average MD and MK values that were used to simulate the cross-validation (CV) procedure of our previous voxel-wise method.
To evaluate the impact of each AQP, we started from the best CV-selected voxel-wise model for MD and MK, plus all the AQP mentioned above, included as independent random intercepts. The best GAMLSS models, before adding the random intercept for the ACQ, included: MD/MK~sex+spline(HCmale)+spline(HCfemale)+spline(volume)+re(dataset), where re stands for random effects, and the spline's basis dimension was 5. From each full model, we ran a feature selection that penalises all model terms (except sex), retaining only the relevant terms in the final model. The model selection procedure is based upon the mgcv R package (select=TRUE), which includes an extra shrinkage penalty to every smooth term (note that random effects are considered smooth terms in mgcv). Additionally, shrunk terms are fully removed when falling below thresholds based on two criteria: the estimated degrees of freedom (EDF<0.9) and the percentage of the predictor range covered (<0.2, i.e. the effect of the variable relative to the response) of the shrunk term (see the gam.selection and select_gamlss2 documentation of the mgcv and gamlss2 R packages for more details). Penalties were calculated based on the generalised (approximate) cross-validation (GCV).
A proper harmonisation must return z-scores that are independent of the acquisition site or scanner, net of undercoverage or sampling biases. Therefore, to evaluate the success of the harmonisation procedure, we estimated the z-scores for MD and MK in the out-of-sample group of HC not included in the training part, grouping the individual deviations by AQP retained as a random effect in the model selection procedure. Then, we tested the post-harmonisation pairwise differences between relevant AQP with ANOVA, Tukey's HSD, and compared marginal means when demographic differences within the scanners were suspected. Finally, we utilised the same approach in relation to a group of early psychosis patients (110 HCPearly-psychosis, 98 LSPPrisma, and 52 LSPTrio).

Results

GCV-penalised model selection returned two distinct but very similar models for the two diffusion metrics. MD and MK location (μ) models were similar, including: sex+spline(HCmale)+spline(HCfemale)+spline(volume)+re(dataset)+re(scanner). Only the scale (σ) model differed between MD and MK, with the MD model retaining: re(dataset)+re(scanner); while MK retained only: re(dataset). Overall, these results indicate that factor AQP, i.e. dataset and scanner variables, were preferred over continuous AQP (TE, TR, etc.) to address harmonisation problems within our current set of datasets. The results of the harmonisation on the training dataset can be seen in Fig. 2A-D (in-sample). Specifically,  Fig. 2A, C show MD and MK colored by dataset before the harmonisation, while Fig. 2B, D show the two metrics after the correction, with the contiguity between datasets preserved. Likewise, Fig. 2E, G and Fig. 2G, H show the effect of the harmonisation on the out-of-sample group of HC for MD and MK, respectively. Subsequently, we tested the post-harmonisation pairwise differences between datasets and scanners. Fig. 2I, J show the results of the ANOVA analysis in the out-of-sample HC for MD and MK z-scores grouped by dataset. For both MD and MK z-scores, the effect of the dataset was correctly estimated and corrected. Both ANOVA show a non-significant p-value (p=0.46 and 0.71 for MD and MK, respectively) and negligible effect size (generalised eta-squared, 𝜂2g=0.005 and 0.003, respectively). Post-hoc Tukey's HSD did not find any significant pairwise difference. Similarly, Fig. 2K, L show the results of the ANOVA analysis in the out-of-sample HC for MD and MK z-scores grouped by individual scanners. ANOVA across scanners returned a non-significant p-value (p=0.73 and 0.91 for MD and MK z-score, respectively) and small effect size (𝜂2g=0.01 and 0.02). Post-hoc Tukey's HSD did not find any significant pairwise difference either. However, despite ANOVA suggesting the effect of individual scanners was estimated correctly, some scanners show a distinct visual offset in Fig. 2K, L (see ‘prisma_66109’ in 2L, for example). Noticeably, the scanners that are misaligned are scanners featuring a smaller number of HC, both during training and in the out-of-sample population (N<16). Finally, we applied the same procedure to a group of early psychosis patients (Fig. 2M-P). Regarding the between-dataset harmonisation, patients’ ANOVA analysis showed a non-significant p-value for MD z-scores (Fig. 2M, p=0.68); however, patients’ MK showed a significant difference (Fig. 2N, p=0.002). Significant differences were also found between z-scores in both MD and MK across individual scanners (Fig. 2O, P, p<0.001), indicating that datasets and/or scanner effects could be found in both diffusion metrics in patients. The largest effect size was found for the MD between-scanner ANOVA (𝜂2g=0.1, Fig. 2O). Notably, most of the differences in early psychosis seem to be caused by the participants scanned in ‘prisma_66027’. To rule out the possibility of a sampling bias due to the demographics of the participants on each scanner, we compared the marginal means for the datasets and scanners, while controlling for age, sex, and dataset or scanner when the opposite AQP was included. Controlling for age only improved the scanner differences in MK, reducing the number of significant pairwise associations, but not fully removing all the differences. Only covarying by scanner controlled for the effect of the dataset in MK z-scores (p=0.1), but not vice versa.

Conclusion

With this study, we aimed to evaluate the impact of different acquisition parameters on the between-site and between-dataset harmonisation of multi-shell diffusion MRI data that underlies successful and usable normative modelling of MD and MK quantitative maps. We found that within our current cohorts, factors AQP are more relevant random effects than continuous AQP, most likely because 4 datasets included in this study are part of the Human Connectome Project, thus having already similar ACQ (except for connectome1), and limiting their added value. We do not exclude that by adding additional datasets, continuous AQP could have a higher impact on the harmonisation and provide better corrections than just factors. For now, both the individual scanner and the dataset have been retained in the models, indicating that both capture a non-negligible influence on the dMRI metrics. Individual scanner effects may capture differences in scanner homogeneity, gradient performance, coils and vendor model, for instance, while dataset effects include differences in the aggregated effects of acquisition and preprocessing parameters. GAMLSS models, similarly to other harmonisation techniques like ComBat, can perform both a location and scale correction. The GAMLSS model showed the ability to effectively correct for both when tested in out-of-sample HC for MD and MK z-scores grouped by dataset or scanner. Qualitatively, both harmonised curves show a biological-plausible trend as MD is expected to dip around 35-40 years of age before slowly increasing due to ageing, while MK reaches its maximum later in the mid-forties before decreasing due to ageing (Beck, 2021). However, the correction is not fully generalised to patient data, as the testing yielded differences dependent on datasets and scanner effects. One possible explanation is sampling bias. It is likely that, given the complexity of working in a clinical setting, patients scanned at different sites do not represent a sample underlying population due to variations in demographic characteristics and the inherent heterogeneity of psychosis diseases. Another possible reason is that the HC sample size for a particular scanner/dataset may explain this discrepancy. In particular, HCPearly-psychosis, LSPprisma and LSPtrio datasets had the lowest number of HC (HCPearly-psychosis: 24 prisma_66027, 6 prisma_66074, 26 prisma_66109; LSP: 59 prisma_fit_67014, 70 trio_35016). The lower number may impede the correct estimation of the scanner random effect, resulting in the model overfitting to the specific characteristics of these few control subjects rather than capturing the true scanner variability, leaving residual scanner bias in the final z-scores. Thus, our results could suggest that a larger number (at least >70) of subjects is required for between-site harmonisation of MD and MK derived from multi-shell dMRI data. Studies considering correcting site effects only in one clinical group (e.g. HC) should carefully consider the possibility that the correction may over- or underfit, thus not be applicable to the matching clinical cohort (e.g. patients), which could have serious implications in normative modelling. Future work will focus on further increasing the number of datasets and controls up to N=3000, and implement a leave-one-site-out CV, as training the model to generalize across sites could improve the ability of the model to disentangle scanner/dataset effects from the true biological variability in new situations of an unseen control/patient or small dataset. Ultimately, a new voxel-wise model based on these findings will be trained and publicly released, enabling researchers to assess regional deviations in WM microstructure in individual patients, after harmonising their matching controls to our model.
