Introduction

The thalamus is central to sensory processing  and higher-order cognition 1. Yet the human thalamic neuronal diversity, particularly of inhibitory interneurons (ITIN), is not well characterised. This is especially important since in contrast to the commonality of the reticular thalamic nucleus ITINs, it has been shown that ITIN density  increases drastically from rodent to primates 2,3, suggesting that intrathalamic ITINs are critical for supporting more complex thalamic functions. Despite prior efforts, we currently do not have a complete map of ITINs across all thalamic nuclei in humans. This limits our understanding of their potential role in health and disease. To address this gap, we aim to develop a novel 3D mesoscale atlas of the human thalamus that integrates structural and connectional organisation with detailed ITIN mapping. Advances in brain mapping increasingly integrate traditional histology with ultra–high-field MRI, transcriptomics, and connectomics, to generate more comprehensive, multiscale views of neural architecture. Our approach adds to these efforts by aligning light-sheet high-resolution 3D microscopy with MRI within the same spatial framework, improving cytoarchitectonic delineation and providing a more reliable anatomical reference.

Methods

Our work is focused on optimising MRI and light-sheet microscopy and Figure 1 summarises the methodology we deploy. We acquire our human brain samples from the Institute of Anatomy at the University of Zurich. Thalami are excised from fixed post-mortem human brains, placed in a custom 3D-printed vessels, and scanned on a Bruker 9.4T small animal MRI scanner. To avoid susceptibility artifacts arising from tissue-air interfaces at the edges of the brain, samples are immersed in Fluorinert, a proton free fluid, though  a complete removal of all air bubbles from our samples is not achieved. A proton density weighted (PD-W) sequence (200 µm isotropic resolution) provides structural images for delineating nuclei, while multi-shell diffusion weighted imaging (b-values 2000–8000 s/mm²; 300 µm isotropic resolution) enables visualisation of microstructural features and fibre pathways. 

As our samples are obtained from donors aged 60+ years and preserved in formalin for extended periods of time (typically >1 year prior to acquisition), conventional clearing protocols developed for mouse tissue or thin human brain sections are not suitable for our purpose. The prolonged fixation and high lipid density of aged human tissue present significant clearing challenges, as previously reported 4,5.  Therefore, following MRI acquisition, samples undergo tissue clearing using the CleaLight protocol (Wyss Center, Geneva), which employs alcohol and detergent for delipidation and permeabilization, followed by photobleaching to reduce autofluorescence. Tissue is then labelled with NeuroTrace™ for neuronal visualization or with antibodies against ITIN markers. Cleared tissue is  imaged using light-sheet microscopy, enabling deep 3D visualization of neuronal architecture.

Results

We successfully acquired high-resolution MRI data from unilateral thalami of six post-mortem donors. Individual thalami were manually delineated (Figure 2.a) from the PD-W dataset and registered to a common space to generate a population-averaged template. Tensor fitting was applied to the DWI, (Figure 2.b), generating scalar maps that further differentiated major nuclear, such as the pulvinar, mediodorsal and lateral-motor nuclei, based on their distinct diffusion properties. Fiber tracking was subsequently performed using manually delineated nuclei as seed regions of interest (Figure 2.c), allowing for the reconstruction of major white matter pathways within the thalamus. The resulting averaged anatomical template would serve as a reference for integrating DTI tractography data with complementary light-sheet microscopy datasets, enabling multi-modal characterization of thalamic connectivity at microscopic resolution.

Optimization of the clearing and staining protocol was essential for imaging aged, formalin-fixed human thalamic tissue. We systematically evaluated multiple antibodies with and without pretreatment using clearing solutions and assessed the penetration depth of both dyes and antibodies (Figure 2d). These optimization experiments determined the optimal tissue thickness for effective labelling and imaging, while minimizing the number of tissue blocks required. This approach reduces tissue loss, structural distortions, and alignment difficulties when reconstructing the complete thalamus dataset. Following this optimization, the clearing and staining protocol enabled high-quality imaging of 1-cm tissue blocks, revealing neuronal densities with NeuroTrace™ (Figure 2e,f). While identifying compatible antibodies for aged, formalin-fixed human brain tissue that are also compatible with the clearing protocol remains challenging due to both binding efficiency and tissue penetration limitations, our systematic approach has established a foundation for successful multi-modal thalamic imaging.

Conclusion

Our datasets provide new insights into thalamic organisation and establish a foundation for mesoscale brain mapping. By integrating high-resolution connectional mapping with ITIN characterisation, this atlas aims to capture features of thalamic architecture absent from current resources and will offer a precise, anatomically grounded tool for neurosurgical planning and mechanistic studies of thalamic function.
