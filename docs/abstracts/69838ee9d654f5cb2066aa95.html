Introduction

A word can evoke multiple meanings depending on the linguistic context in which it is encountered. For instance, integration might refer to a mathematical operation in a math textbook or to a social process in an article on multicultural societies. Meaning selection also depends on a reader´s prior experience: a math professor may automatically think of the mathematical sense, whereas non-mathematicians may think of the social one. Grounded cognition accounts propose that such conceptual flexibility is implemented through context-dependent recruitment of brain systems that support individual experience with a concept’s referent1,2. To date, context-dependent grounding mechanisms and their modulation by experience have been examined mainly for concrete concepts, such as action concepts which flexibly recruit action-related brain networks depending on context and individual action expertise. Here, we use fMRI to test whether these mechanisms generalize to the abstract domain. Among abstract concepts, mathematical concepts (e.g., integration, logarithm) constitute a suitable category for studying the experiential grounding of abstract knowledge: prior work suggests that their processing recruits a magnitude-related brain network whose engagement varies with individuals’ experience with mathematical concepts3. Using a cueing paradigm, this fMRI study investigated whether the recruitment of this fronto-parietal magnitude network in semantic processing is modulated by context and mathematical expertise. Specifically, we tested whether (i) processing mathematical sentences engages this network more than processing non-mathematical sentences; (ii) processing ambiguous abstract probes flexibly recruits this network when preceded by mathematical versus non-mathematical cues; and (iii) individual expertise modulates the engagement of this network. This study has been preregistered: https://osf.io/8uh5y/.

Methods

Participants

Thirty-two (19 male) healthy young adults (M = 24.4 years, SD = 2.7 years) participated in the study. All participants had completed at least the second semester of a Bachelor's degree in mathematics, informatics, physics or medical physics. 

Experimental paradigm

During the fMRI data acquisition session, participants performed an event-related cued association judgment task. As displayed in Figure 1, each trial began with a contextual cue consisting of two short sentences that conveyed either a mathematical (MAT) or a non-mathematical (NON) meaning. The cue was followed by an ambiguous probe word, which had a mathematical and a non-mathematical meaning. Subsequently, the probe reappeared on the screen together with two target words presented below it, one consistent with the mathematical and one with the non-mathematical meaning of the probe. We created two counterbalanced versions, presenting each ambiguous probe in both contextual conditions across participants. Participants were instructed to select the target word most strongly associated with the probe. From these choices, we computed a behavioural measure of each participant’s individual Bias to prefer the mathematical over the non-mathematical meaning of the ambiguous probes. Each participant completed five fMRI runs of 20 trials each (10 MAT and 10 NON). Outside the scanner, participants completed a mathematical test from which we obtained a behavioural measure of their mathematical skills (Skill).

Data preprocessing and analysis

MRI data were preprocessed and analyzed in SPM12 v7771 running in MATLAB R2021b. Functional images underwent distortion correction, realignment, slice-timing correction, normalization via the segmented structural image, and spatial smoothing with an 8 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel. First, to investigate brain activation in response to processing MAT vs. NON cues, at the single-subject level we defined a contrast with a weight on +1 for the experimental regressor modelling MAT cues and a weight of -1 for the regressor modelling the NON cues. Second, to investigate brain activation in response to processing ambiguous probes, at the single-subject level we defined a contrast with a weight of +1 for the experimental regressor modelling probes preceded by MAT cues and a weight of -1 for the regressor modelling the probes precede by NON cues. In both analyses, the resulting t-contrast images were tested at the group level by applying a one-sample t-test. Further, we tested whether interindividual differences in Bias and Skill (see above) modulate the brain response to MAT vs. NON conditions by modelling Bias and Skill as covariates. Analyses were firstly restricted within a single mask pooling together the regions of interest (ROI) constituting the math-responsive network (Figure 2A), with each region consisting of a spherical small volume of 6 mm radius centered on the target coordinate taken from the meta-analysis by Sokolowski and colleagues4. For control purposes, exploratory whole-brain analyses were conducted.

Results

Brain activation during mathematical versus non-mathematical cue processing

Processing MAT compared to NON cues elicited significantly greater activation in the left supplementary motor area, left inferior parietal lobule, and left insula within the magnitude network ROI-mask (Figure 2B). No regions within the magnitude-network ROI mask showed stronger activation for NON relative to MAT cues. At the whole-brain level, enhanced activation for MAT versus NON cues was observed in the left inferior frontal operculum, left superior parietal lobule, and bilateral inferior temporal gyrus (Figure 2C). In contrast, processing NON cues was associated with greater activation in the right precuneus, middle temporal gyrus, middle frontal gyrus, medial frontal operculum, angular gyrus, supramarginal gyrus, as well as the left middle temporal gyrus and left postcentral gyrus. No significant effects of Bias and Skill were found.

Brain activation during disambiguated probe processing

Processing probes preceded by disambiguating MAT vs. NON cues elicited significantly greater activation in the left inferior parietal lobule within the magnitude-network ROI mask (Figure 2B). No regions within the magnitude network ROI mask showed stronger activation for probes preceded by NON vs. MAT cues. At the whole-brain level, enhanced activation for probes preceded by MAT (vs. NON) cues was observed in the left inferior parietal lobule and the left middle frontal gyrus (Figure 2C). In contrast, processing probes preceded by NON (vs. MAT) cues elicited greater activation in the right middle frontal and medial orbitofrontal gyrus. No significant effects of Bias and Skill were found.

Conclusion

By using a cueing paradigm, this fMRI study investigated context-dependent grounding mechanisms and their modulation by experience in the abstract mathematical domain. Our analyses of MAT vs. NON sentential cue processing replicated previous findings3 showing a stronger activation in the left supplementary motor area, left inferior parietal lobule, and left insula within the magnitude network. As a novel finding, we found a stronger activation of the left inferior parietal lobule within the magnitude network selectively for processing ambiguous abstract probes that were preceded by MAT cues. Notably, this activation of the magnitude-related network was independent of the specific word form given that in our counterbalanced design, we used each probe in both contextual conditions across participants. According to the hypothesis, the processing of NON cues and of probes following them did not engage any areas within the magnitude network.  

Against the hypothesis that individual mathematical expertise might modulate the recruitment of the magnitude-related network in processing MAT meanings, we found no evidence for a modulation by participants’ Skill or Bias on MAT vs. NON cue or probe processing. This lack of evidence for an interaction may in part reflect limited variability within our all-expert sample, which may have reduced our sensitivity to detect subtle expertise-related modulations. Alternatively, experience might modulate brain activation in a non-linear way, with a reduction of activation driven by neural efficiency at high expertise levels, resulting in a u-shaped relation, as has been previously shown for functional connectivity during mathematical conceptual processing5.

The overall pattern of result indicates that the semantic processing of mathematical word meaning in participants who have mathematical expertise recruits the math-responsive network in a flexible, context-dependent fashion, in line with the hypothesis of experience-specific grounding in the abstract domain.
