Introduction

Sleep profoundly transforms most aspects of neurophysiology and behavior, including major alterations to motor control, cognition, and consciousness. During wakefulness, distinct subcortical neuronal populations produce and release neuromodulators such as norepinephrine, dopamine, serotonin, acetylcholine, and histamine, supporting waking behaviors and stimulating cortical and thalamocortical circuits while inhibiting sleep-promoting regions. The subcortical regions which regulate this process make up the ascending arousal network (AAN) and several nuclei have been separately shown in rodents to initiate arousal state transitions1-2. However, several technical challenges have limited our understanding of how this network generates and sustains sleep-wake states in the human brain. More specifically, it remains unclear how AAN activity evolves at transitions between sleep and wake in humans. Additionally, the lack of simultaneous recordings from multiple neuronal populations in prior rodent studies has left unresolved whether there are key neuronal populations that orchestrate activity across the rest of the circuit to trigger an arousal state, as well as how coordinated activity across this network contributes to fluctuations in arousal state.

Methods

To investigate the role of the human AAN on sleep-wake transitions, we used high-spatial-resolution imaging using 7 Tesla (7T) fMRI simultaneously with EEG to track electrophysiological arousal state. Participants arrived at the lab around their bedtime (range=10:00 PM-12:00 AM; mean=10:22 PM). Sessions began with an MPRAGE scan (voxel size=0.75mm isotropic), and we then performed a resting scan of up to 4 hours, with a sleep opportunity while collecting a sagittal partial-brain EPI acquisition covering the brainstem and midline cortical structures (voxel size=1.2mm isotropic; TR=0.98s; SMS=2, GRAPPA=4; 38 slices) (Fig. 1A-B). A 7T-compatible EEG technology3 was used to track sleep-wake state throughout the scan (Fig. 1D). Participants (n=20; 16 females, 20-40 years old) spontaneously transitioned between sleep-wake states during the scans and performed a self-paced behavioral task4 to capture the loss and recovery of behavior across sleep-wake state transitions.

Anatomical segmentation of the AAN was performed within each individual using a probabilistic segmentation algorithm5 (Fig. 1C). To delineate arousal dynamics within the AAN, we extracted BOLD activity in each region of interest (ROI) (Fig. 1C). at the transitions between sleep-wake states. We defined behavioral arousals as the first button press after at least 20 seconds of unresponsiveness4 and arousal dips as the last button press before at least 30 seconds of unresponsiveness. We further separated behavioral arousals into distinct types to investigate how AAN dynamics contribute to the maintenance of wakefulness: sustained (at least 5 button presses following arousals) and transient (two or fewer button presses)2. We also separated behavioral arousals into distinct types to investigate the prior sleep period on AAN arousal dynamics: arousals from prolonged (>60 seconds unresponsiveness before arousal) and from short (<40 seconds unresponsiveness before arousal) unresponsiveness. Significant changes in BOLD during arousals and arousal dips were identified with a linear mixed effects model at each time point to calculate significant differences in BOLD from zero, with a random effect of subject. Significant differences between different arousal types for each ROI were with a linear mixed effects model at each time point to calculate significant differences between the average responses between the arousal types, with a random effect of subject. P-values were corrected for number of time points and ROIs tested using FDR correction.

To investigate the timing of arousal responses across AAN ROIs, we determined the onset time of each subcortical ROI’s arousal response by fitting a linear combination of two Gaussian curves and defined the onset as the time the model fit reached 10% of its maximum amplitude. Bootstrap resampling was completed to create a confidence interval of onset time. The peak timing of ROI responses was additionally computed.

Finally, to investigate how coordinated activity across the AAN and other ROIs changes during arousal state transitions, we performed a dynamic functional connectivity analysis. BOLD data from all AAN regions, thalamic nuclei, and cortical regions was extracted from 40 seconds before to 40 seconds after arousal transitions. The correlation between all ROI pairs was calculated in 20 second windows (step size=1 second). Sliding-window correlations were averaged within groups of ROIs (i.e. within groups of AAN regions) and between groups of ROIs (i.e. between AAN nuclei and thalamic nuclei). Connectivity time series’ were baseline normalized to the average 30 to 20 seconds prior to the arousal transition. Significant changes in connectivity were calculated with a time-binned, paired t-test (5-s bins, 2-s step size) between the empirical connectivity time series and a null distribution calculated from random time points during the scan. P-values were Bonferroni corrected to account for the number of time bins and ROI groups tested.

Results

We found that awakenings from sleep were accompanied by electrophysiological changes consistent with a sleep-to-wake transition (Fig. 2B), and were locked to coordinated activation across the AAN prior to the moment of awakening (Fig. 2A-C). We next investigated if key regions of the AAN activate earlier than others or if the entire AAN activates simultaneously during the process of awakening from sleep. We found that the onset of the arousal response across all AAN ROIs began several seconds before the moment of arousal, with several ROIs, namely the dorsal raphe, basal forebrain, and pedunculopontine tegmentum, activating earliest during the arousal response.

We next investigated how these arousal response dynamics compared to the sleep onset process. During arousal dips, we observed EEG changes consistent with transitions into sleep, with a decrease in occipital alpha and beta power (Fig. 2E). We further observed an inverted pattern of BOLD responses across ROIs, with significant deactivation across the AAN during transitions into sleep (Fig. 2B).

We next tested whether specific regions of the AAN were linked to the subsequent maintenance of arousal state and we found that a select group of AAN regions, namely the median raphe and mesencephalic reticular formation, were more strongly activated following sustained arousals (p<0.05; linear mixed effects model), while other AAN regions exhibited no difference between sustained and transient arousal responses (p>0.05; linear mixed effects model). When separating arousals by the duration of the prior sleep period, we found that a different subset of AAN regions, specifically the locus coeruleus, median raphe, mesencephalic reticular formation, pedunculopontine tegmentum, and ventral tegmental area, exhibited significantly lower BOLD responses in the 30 seconds preceding arousals from prolonged compared to arousals from short unresponsiveness. Overall, we observed heterogeneity with which regions of the AAN reflected the maintenance of wakefulness and the sleep duration preceding arousals. 

Finally, we investigated how the coordinated activation of the AAN and the rest of the brain evolves at transitions between sleep and wake. From this connectivity analysis, we found that the synchronization of activity across the AAN and between the AAN and thalamic nuclei increased before behavioral arousals, whereas synchronization of activity across the cortex increased after the arousal moment.

Conclusion

Using simultaneous EEG-fMRI at 7 Tesla, we identified distinct patterns of subcortical neuromodulatory dynamics that reflect the moments before, during, and after transitions between sleep and wakefulness. We observed that regions of the AAN activated before sleep-to-wake transitions, with cholinergic and serotonergic regions activating earliest across the entire network. These behavioral arousals were further characterized by an early synchronization of activity within the AAN, as well as between the AAN and thalamus, whereas cortical synchronization followed the moment of awakening. During transitions from wake-to-sleep, these activity patterns inverted and the AAN exhibited deactivation responses. Distinct subsets of AAN regions displayed activity linked to the maintenance of wakefulness and the duration of sleep preceding awakening, revealing heterogenous properties of these regions in arousal state transitions. These findings define the arousal dynamics of the human AAN and uncover novel ways by which subcortical neuromodulatory systems contribute to sleep-wake states.
