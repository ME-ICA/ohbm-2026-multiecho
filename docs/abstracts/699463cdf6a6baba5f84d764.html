Introduction

The left hemisphere’s fundamental role in language processing has long been recognized 1. The right hemisphere, long regarded as non-dominant, has been associated with peripheral functions such as pragmatics and prosody. Closely tied to hand dominance, this organization of language processing has been hypothesized to be reversed in left-handers. As a result, language deficits following right hemisphere damage (RHD) were interpreted as the result of inverted lateralization in left-handers or atypical lateralization in right-handers 2. However, growing evidence has challenged exclusive left-hemisphere dominance in language processing, including: a) developmental equipotentiality of hemispheres in language processing with leftward shift by maturity 3; b) large cohort fMRI evidence of bilateral language processing, with significant population variability in the degree of bilaterality and independence of hand asymmetries 4; c) genome-wide association studies confirming independence of language and handedness asymmetries 5. 

As such, the apparent rarity of language impairment after RHD may stem from biased sampling, driven by hemisphere-specific neuropsychological assessments and reliance on handedness as a proxy for language lateralization.

In this study, we disregarded diagnostic labels and handedness information and used a data-driven approach to examine language deficit prevalence in an unselected cohort of patients with unilateral stroke. i) What is the prevalence of language deficits following RHD patients, compared to LHD patients?; ii) Do patients with RHD and LHD differ in terms of language impairment pattern and severity, sociodemographics handedness, and broader behavioural profiles?; iii) Does stroke topography differ between RHD and LHD patients?; iv) Do variables that best predict the degree of language impairment differ in RHD and LHD patients?

Methods

This study included patients drawn from two cohorts, both recruited at Washington University in St. Louis and evaluated using identical inclusion criteria and clinical assessment batteries. The patient group consisted of 250 individuals with unilateral stroke (132 males, 118 females). The mean age was 56.3 years (range: 19–95) and the mean education level was 13 years (range: 5–20). Among them, 123 patients had LHD lesions and 122 had RHD lesions. A healthy control group (n = 62) was matched to the patient cohort on age, gender, and education. Five control participants were excluded due to missing data or behavioral performance suggesting cognitive impairment.

Patients were assessed approximately two weeks post-stroke. Healthy participants were tested twice, three months apart, and their scores were averaged to establish normative performance. Five language tasks were available across both cohorts and were therefore included: naming, sentence reading, reading comprehension, nonword reading, and semantic fluency. Additional measures of verbal and visuospatial memory and attention were also administered.

Language impairment definition

Because patients were not assessed for a formal aphasia diagnosis, we did not classify them as aphasic or non-aphasic. Instead, language impairment was defined as impaired performance on at least two of the five language tasks, minimizing the risk of misclassifying individuals with a single deviant score. Specifically, impaired performance was defined as a score at or below 2 SD below the control group mean.

Neuroimaging

Stroke lesion masks were manually delineated on high-resolution T1-weighted sequences using Analyze biomedical imaging software. MP-RAGE T1 weighted images were acquired on a 3 T Siemens Tim-Trio scanner (School of Medicine of the Washington University in St. Louis, US) with the following parameters: TR/TE=1950/2.26 ms; flip angle=9°; voxel size=1 mm isotropic. We also estimated the probabilistic disconnection of language-relevant white matter tracts using the Lesion Quantification Toolkit (LQT), which employs a white matter atlas derived from 842 healthy Human Connectome Project participants. For each tract, we extracted disconnection severity, defined as the proportion of streamlines intersected by the lesion. 

Analyses

Subsequent analyses focused on tracts consistently identified as critical for language processing. Two-tailed independent-samples t-tests (α = 0.05) were used to compare the prevalence and pattern of language deficits in RHD and LHD patients, as well as differences in sociodemographic characteristics, lesion volume, and motor-cognitive performance. Global differences in lesion distribution between language-impaired and language-unimpaired patients (separately for RHD and LHD) were assessed with voxel-wise t-tests using 1,000 permutations in FSL’s randomise. Threshold-Free Cluster Enhancement (TFCE) was applied, with significance set at α = 0.05.

To investigate whether distinct language impairment profiles could be identified and whether they corresponded to specific lesion patterns, we performed hierarchical agglomerative clustering on z-scored language scores. Finally, to examine predictors of language performance in LHD versus RHD patients, we fitted stepwise linear models using bidirectional AIC-based selection. Separate models were estimated for each language task and each group, with z-scored language scores as outcomes. Full models included age, education, and tract-level disconnection severity for left or right language-related white matter tracts, depending on lesion side.

Results

Prevalence of language deficits and behavioural patterns

Of the 250 patients, language assessment data were available for 228 individuals (RHD: 110; LHD: 118). As expected, language impairment was more common in LHD than RHD (Figure 2 - Panel 1). Specifically, 25% of RHD patients (28/110) showed language deficits, compared to 49% of LHD patients (58/118). Impairments on individual tasks among RHD patients were not negligible, ranging from 10% to 34%. Overall, RHD patients exhibited significantly less severe language deficits than LHD patients, except on the nonword reading task (Figure 2 - Panel 2A). No sociodemographic differences emerged in age or education. Although left-handedness was more common in RHD language-impaired than in LHD language-impaired patients, most RHD language-impaired individuals were right-handed (Figure 2 - Panel 3B). No group differences were observed for NIHSS admission scores, verbal memory, or motor deficits (Figure 2 - Panel 3A). By contrast, RHD language-impaired patients showed significantly greater impairments in spatial attention and visuospatial memory (Figure 2 - Panel 2B).

Lesion topography

We next examined differences in lesion topography between LHD and RHD groups. Lesion data were available for 64 of the 86 language-impaired patients. In the left hemisphere, lesion differences were extensive, spanning large cortical regions except for the corticostriatal tract and motor systems. In contrast, the right hemisphere did not show significant differences in lesion distribution between language-impaired and unimpaired patients (Figure 2 - Panel 4C).

Hierarchical clustering

All patients with available language data were included in the hierarchical agglomerative clustering, using cosine similarity and complete linkage. Clustering based on the five language measures identified four distinct groups. Cluster 1, the largest (136 patients), consisted of language-unimpaired individuals, whose lesion maps showed mainly left-hemisphere widespread involvement. Cluster 2 (46 patients) showed poor performance across all tasks and consisted predominantly of LHD patients, with 25% of RHD patients. Cluster 3 (18 patients) showed a selective pattern of deficits in nonword reading and naming and included both LHD (8) and RHD (12) patients. Cluster 4 (20 patients) was characterized by primarily semantic deficits, particularly in semantic fluency and reading comprehension, and also included both LHD (12) and RHD (8) individuals. See Figure 2, Panel 5 for clustering results.

Prediction models

Finally, stepwise forward linear regression models assessing predictors of language performance revealed distinct hemispheric patterns. In LHD patients, three left-hemisphere tracts consistently emerged as significant predictors (R2 range: 0.46-0.54). The left AF negatively predicted naming (β = -2.03), nonword reading (β = -2.71), reading (β = -6.19), and reading comprehension (β = -4.89). The left FAT negatively predicted naming (β = -2.63), reading (β = -7.56), reading comprehension (β = -6.63), and semantic fluency (β = -2.50). The left IFOF was associated with naming (β = -1.71), nonword reading (β = -1.39), reading (β = -4.51), and reading comprehension (β = -6.44). Education positively predicted naming and nonword reading. In RHD patients, predictive effects were generally weaker (R2 range: 0.1-0.23); the right SLF III negatively predicted naming (β = -0.83), the right AF negatively predicted nonword reading (β = -1.06), and the right IFOF negatively predicted reading (β = -2.95). Education was a positive predictor for naming, nonword reading, and semantic fluency. No significant predictors were identified for reading comprehension. Overall, right-hemisphere tracts explained less variance and showed smaller effects than left-hemisphere tracts. See Figure 2, panel 6 for prediction models’ results.

Conclusion

Behavioural and anatomical characterization of RHD patients with language impairments

The primary aim of this study was to determine the prevalence and profile of language deficits following RHD in an unselected acute stroke cohort. Contrary to long-standing assumptions that such deficits are rare, we found impairments, milder than those in LHD patients, in nearly 25% of RHD patients. Previous prevalence estimates often relied on case series or aphasia-unit samples, introducing selection bias. Our population-based approach shows that right-hemisphere language deficits, though typically mild, are not negligible.

The RHD and LHD groups were sociodemographically comparable aside from a higher proportion of left-handers in the RHD impaired group. However, because most impaired RHD patients were right-handed, handedness asymmetry cannot explain their language deficits.

Comparisons of RHD and LHD impaired patients revealed distinct behavioural and anatomical profiles. RHD-related impairments were generally milder and more selective, affecting specific tasks rather than causing global dysfunction. This aligned with the clustering analysis: few RHD patients fell into the global impairment Cluster 2, while more belonged to modular dysfunction Clusters 3 and 4.

Although milder, RHD deficits often co-occurred with impairments in other right-hemisphere–dominated functions (left motor function, left spatial attention), suggesting that language impairment in RHD rarely occurs in isolation and may reflect broader cognitive-motor involvement.

Anatomically, LHD impairments reflected strong vulnerability of the left hemisphere, with the exception of small regions near the corticospinal tract and internal capsule. In contrast, the right hemisphere showed no clear anatomical separation between impaired and unimpaired patients, consistent with variability in bilateral language organisation.

Across both groups, predictors of language deficits differed: in LHD, tract disconnection within dorsal and ventral language networks, particularly AF, FAT, and IFOF, was strongly associated with outcomes. In RHD, effects were weaker and more task-specific, yet still indicate that right-hemisphere homologues contribute to selective aspects of language processing.

Language system in the right hemisphere 

The apparent discrepancy between bilateral language representation in healthy individuals and the predominantly left-hemispheric language deficits observed after stroke has been previously noted. Our findings, based on language assessment in a behaviourally unselected sample, confirm the presence of mild language deficits in approximately a quarter of RHD patients and point towards under-detection as one of the factors leading to the apparent absence of such impairments.

Extrapolating from our stroke cohort, we propose that: (i) bilateral language representation is more common than previously thought; (ii) left-hemisphere substrates typically preserve core computational functions; (iii) language bilaterality arises independently of handedness or other lateralized behaviors; (iv) several language homologous white-matter tracts in the right hemisphere are relevant; and (v) the degree of language bilaterality varies widely across individuals, and this variability in functional asymmetries influences stroke outcomes.

Clinical implications

Language deficits after RHD are not rare and they occur largely independently of handedness. As such, right-handed RHD patients cannot be assumed to be language-intact. Systematic neuropsychological screening for language deficits on all unilateral stroke patients is warranted.

If cerebral functional asymmetries are to be used to screen for behavioral deficits in neuropsychiatric populations, a much clearer understanding of their variability is needed.
