Introduction

Early developmental mechanisms have clear macrostructural predictions: processes linked to cortical column generation and areal expansion are expected to be expressed across the full thickness of cortex and therefore similarly on the pial and white surfaces1. Adult training, by contrast, offers no obvious route to large-scale areal expansion and may instead be expressed in properties that are especially sensitive to the gray-white matter boundary2,3. This motivates two aims. First, we seek to separate experience-related change from the stable trait-like components of cortical anatomy that is strongly shaped by genetics. Twins provide a natural test: by tracking within-pair cortical anatomical similarity over time in monozygotic (MZ) and dizygotic (DZ) pairs, we can ask whether training alters similarity in a zygosity-dependent manner, consistent with environmental influence on a highly heritable baseline. Second, we aim to localize any training related signal along the cortical mantle, whether it is distributed across the cortical ribbon or concentrated at a specific surface such as the gray white matter boundary.

We use "brainprints", multivariate neuroanatomical fingerprints constructed from standard morphometric features4, to quantify within pair similarity in MZ and DZ twins. We test whether immersive virtual reality (VR) navigation training can measurably change cortical brainprints, and whether any training related effects preferentially localize to the white matter gray matter (WM GM) interface, consistent with frameworks linking microstructural organization, tensile stress, and cortical curvature5.

Methods

Adult twins (71 MZ and 34 DZ pairs; n = 210 individuals; age range 16 to 78 years) completed up to three MRI waves in an counterbalanced ABB/BAB (train-rest-rest/rest-train-rest) 10 week design. Participants randomized to training navigated in a virtual town ("Plasti City", approximately 5 km²) using immersive VR in the lab (cybercycle setup) and a laptop based homework version, searching for targets with levels (0 to 12) opening sequentially according to navigation performance (Figure 1A). Only time spent actively navigating in search of given targets in Plasti City was counted as training time. One hundred eighteen participants accepted training; for the 100 participants with more than 2 hours of training and MRIs post training, mean training time was 14.64 hours (range 2.05 to 55.13; SD 11.04), including 5.48 hours in immersive VR (SD 2.86; range 0.28 to 14.14).

MRI was acquired on a 3T Siemens Prisma with a 32 channel head coil: T1w MPRAGE (0.8 mm isotropic; TR 2400 ms; TE 2.22 ms; TI 1000 ms), T2w SPACE (0.8 mm isotropic; TR 3200 ms; TE 5.63 ms), and multi shell DWI (1.5 mm isotropic; b = 1500 and 3000 s mm²; 92 to 93 directions and 6 b0 per shell; TR 3230 ms; TE 89.2 ms; multiband factor 4; additional b0 PA volumes). Structural data were processed with FreeSurfer v7.3.2 in a longitudinal framework. Anatomical brainprints were constructed per participant session by extracting cortical area, thickness, volume, and integrated rectified mean curvature for each of 68 Desikan Killiany regions (272 values). Brainprint similarity between twins was computed as 1 minus mean relative difference over brain features (Figure 1B).

Diffusion data were preprocessed with the HCP Diffusion Preprocessing Pipeline (QuNex v1.0.3; TOPUP; Eddy with outlier replacement). DTI (FA, MD, AD, RD) and NODDI (ODI, ICVF) metrics were sampled along the FreeSurfer WM/GM interface and plus or minus 1 mm (into WM and into GM), averaged within parcels, and converted to diffusion brainprints. Diffusion similarity was computed using the same 1 minus mean relative difference metric. To test whether diffusion brainprints carried explanatory signal for the curvature brainprint effects, we compared a baseline GAMM for WM surface curvature similarity (training status + zygosity + s(age) + zygosity by training status, random intercept for twin pair) with an extended model adding diffusion similarity and diffusion similarity by training status. We quantified diffusion signal as increased model fit, defined as change in adjusted R² and AIC, and tested model improvement by likelihood ratio tests with FDR correction across sampling depths within surface.

Results

Longitudinal training analyses included 488 MRI observations, yielding 244 within pair brainprint similarity estimates from 105 twin pairs (71 MZ, 34 DZ); 30 MZ and 14 DZ full pairs had more than 2 hours of training and post training MRI. Across waves, 104 pairs contributed baseline observations, 44 pairs had observations immediately post training, and 21 pairs also had observations approximately 10 weeks after ending training. A significant zygosity by training interaction on cortical brainprint similarity was present immediately post training (t = −2.780, p = .006): the brainprints of genetically identical twins stayed similar or became more similar with training, while those of fraternal twins diverged. The interaction was attenuated after approximately 10 weeks of rest (t = −1.514, p = .131). Aside from one effect on right precentral thickness, six of seven features showing FDR significant zygosity by training interactions were cortical curvature measures (Figure 2A-C).

Directionality analyses showed a positive effect of training status on global cortical curvature at the white surface (immediately post training: t = 2.103, p = .0360; approximately 10 weeks post training: t = 2.775, p = .0058), and a positive effect on total cortical area immediately post training (t = 2.624, p = .0090), which was not significant after approximately 10 weeks (t = 1.029, p = .3041). Equivalent models for pial surface mean curvature and area showed no significant or near significant training effects.

Across regions, the distribution of zygosity by training status interaction effects (partial r from the GAMMs) differed significantly across feature categories in the adult environment (Kruskal Wallis H = 41.32, p < .0001). Dunn’s post hoc tests (Bonferroni corrected) showed curvature differed from area (p < .0001), volume (p < .0001), and thickness (p < .0001).

Control analyses showed that training time discordance predicted reduced similarity immediately post-training (t = −7.201, p < .0001), but discordance did not differ by zygosity (U = 160, p = .212), and the zygosity by training effect on cortical curvature remained after excluding discordant pairs and after adjusting for within-pair training time difference.

Diffusion analyses included 70 MZ and 33 DZ pairs with valid DWI. Adding diffusion similarity and its training interaction improved model fit for predicting WM surface curvature similarity when diffusion was sampled at the WM GM interface and 1 mm into WM, but only a weak trend 1 mm into GM (adjusted delta R² = 7.7 percent, 6.3 percent, 3.2 percent; AIC favoring diffusion model by 12.7, 6.9, 2.2; FDR corrected p = .004, .027, .15; Figure 2D). As a negative control, replacing WM surface curvature with pial surface curvature removed the diffusion effect. Regionally, parcels with stronger immediate training-related effects on curvature similarity were those where adding local diffusion similarity improved model fit the most (Spearman’s rho = −.46, p < .0001), with no such spatial correspondence for pial curvature (rho = −.02; Figure 2E)

Conclusion

Using an immersive VR navigation intervention in adult twins and a multivariate brainprint framework, we show that cortical brainprints can still be altered in adulthood in response to current environmental influence. Training was associated with zygosity-dependent change in brainprint similarity, driven predominantly by cortical curvature, with effects localizing to features defined on the white matter surface rather than the pial surface. Direction of effect analyses, increased white surface curvature and a transient increase in white surface area with no detectable pial effects, suggest that training did not express as general areal expansion across the cortical ribbon but instead acted at the gray white matter boundary. Diffusion-derived microstructure brainprints sampled at and just below the WM/GM interface explained additional variance in WM surface curvature similarity and tracked the spatial distribution of training effects, while the same was not true for pial curvature. Together, these results indicate that microstructural organization at or near the WM/GM boundary is linked to macrostructural curvature measured at the white matter surface.
