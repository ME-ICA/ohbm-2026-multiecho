Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the most prevalent type of dementia, accounting for 80% of dementia worldwide. It commonly occurs in the elderly population causing a progressive decline of cognitive domains, including attention, learning, memory and planning abilities. 

Cognitive impairment associated with dementia is a major source of burden, deterioration in life quality, and reduced psychological well-being, in turn resulting in large and increasing social costs. The etiology of AD remains unknown. The diagnosis of dementia is mainly made on a clinical basis, lacking appropriate biomarkers that can provide an unequivocal diagnosis and characterize in vivo the metabolic and microstructural events associated to early disease development. We  develop and exploit new MRI techniques based on heteronuclear imaging in combination with quantitative MRI and X ray techniques  to identify potential biomarkers of the disease and to explore the physiopathological processes underlying the microstructural tissue damage and the cognitive deterioration. 

Sodium has a fundamental role in many physiological and biochemical functions [1]. In particular, sodium homeostasis is associated to neuroinflammation [2-3], with potential sensitivity to vascular and metabolic alterations. Currently, no non-invasive MR imaging tools are available to reliably detect subtle neuroinflammation. We  develop a multimodal approach based on heteronuclear MRI as a tool to investigate neuroinflammation associated with AD. 

The viability of sodium biomarkers to investigate inflammation and neurodegeneration was assessed, and the relevant role as a marker of cognitive impairment was studied. The outcomes of this study have a significant impact potential on clinical research and on clinical practice in terms of new knowledge about neuroinflammation and neurophysiological alterations in AD, and of availability of the relevant neuroimaging tools.

Methods

We developed a multimodal approach (figure 1) combining in vivo and ex vivo 23Na MRI with high-resolution X-ray–based techniques to validate sodium compartmentalization across scales. The study included controlled in vitro experiments, in vivo MRI in patients and healthy subjects, and ex vivo human brain tissue analysis integrating MRI, X-ray phase-contrast tomography (XPCT), and X-ray fluorescence (XRF).

Participants and in vivo MRI.
In vivo MRI was performed in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) patients and healthy subjects (total n = 10). Sodium MRI was acquired at 3 T using an Ultra-Short Echo Time (UTE) 23Na sequence based on spiral k-space trajectories, optimized to maximize signal-to-noise ratio and minimize relaxation losses. Trajectory corrections were applied to enable quantitative reconstruction of total sodium concentration (TSC) and intracellular fraction (IF) maps, which were reconstructed and processed using MATLAB.

Auxiliary 1H MRI included two-shell high angular resolution diffusion imaging (HARDI) optimized for the NODDI protocol, enabling microstructural characterization. Diffusion data were processed in MATLAB and used to support the interpretation of sodium compartmentalization.

In vitro samples.
A 1.0 M NaCl saline solution was prepared in distilled water and used as a diluent for polyethylene glycol (PEG 400). PEG–NaCl solutions were prepared at volumetric concentrations of 95%, 90%, 82.5%, 81%, 80%, 78.5%, 75%, 60%, and 50% (v/v) and loaded into NMR capillary tubes. These controlled samples were used to characterize sodium relaxation behavior under varying viscosity and compartmental constraints.

Ex vivo human brain samples.
Human cortical tissue was obtained from AD donors (n = 2) and healthy controls (n = 3). Biopsies (~20 mm) were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen immediately after collection. All procedures were conducted at the University of Namur Biobank (Belgium) with institutional ethics approval. Frozen samples were initially scanned by MRI to assess tissue integrity and identify focal damage, then fixed in paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and prepared for X-ray imaging. Five-micron sections were cut for histology and XRF analysis.

NMR experiments.
All NMR acquisitions were performed on a Bruker Avance 400 MHz (9.4 T) spectrometer equipped with a micro-imaging probe (10 mm inner diameter) and a double-tuned 1H /23Na 8 mm RF coil. T2T_{2}T2 relaxation was measured using a CPMG sequence (D20_{20}20 = 50 μs, 256 echo times from 200 μs to 54 ms, NS = 1024, TR = 300 ms). T1 relaxation was quantified using an inversion-recovery sequence (32 inversion times from 0.1 to 60 ms, NS = 1024, TR = 300 ms). Data were processed using TOPSPIN and ParaVision 5.0. Relaxation analysis employed mono-, bi-, and tri-exponential fitting (Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm) and Laplace transform analysis (CONTIN).

X-ray imaging.
X-ray measurements were performed at the Elettra Synchrotron (Trieste, Italy). Three-dimensional cortical imaging was achieved using in-line free-space propagation X-ray Phase Contrast Tomography  at 25 keV with a pixel size of 0.9 × 0.9 μm², enabling micrometric visualization of neurons, vessels, and cytoarchitecture. Tomographic reconstruction was performed using the SYRMEP Tomo Project (STP) with phase retrieval.

X-ray fluorescence (XRF).
Elemental mapping was carried out by exciting sodium K-fluorescence lines using 2 keV photons, allowing simultaneous detection of Na, Mg, and P. Regions of interest (~100 × 100 μm²) were scanned at 1 μm resolution with 3 s exposure per pixel. Data were analyzed using PyMCA, and sodium maps were normalized to the elastic scattering peak. Three to four regions were analyzed per sample.
All sodium data acquired in controlled samples and phantoms were combine to quantify the  sodium contributions, enabling biologically informed interpretation of TSC and IF maps.

Results

Results

Using a combination of in vivo human MRI and ex vivo validation techniques, we characterized sodium relaxation and compartmentalization across controlled systems and human brain tissue.

In vivo human results.
A pilot study for the optimizazion of UTE 23Na MRI at 3 T was successfully implemented in Alzheimer’s disease patients and healthy subjects (n = 10), providing whole-brain maps of total sodium concentration (TSC)   (Figure 1: (A) Sodium map of the brain. (B) sodium map of the white matter. (C) sodium map og the gray matter. (D) sodium map of the cerebrospinal fluid). 

Controlled samples and modeling.
In vitro PEG–NaCl phantoms showed systematic changes in Sodium relaxation times  revealing a clear phase transition around 80% PEG, consistent with viscosity data . The evolution of the three T₂ and two T₁ components reflects PEG-induced modulation of electric field gradient fluctuations around the quadrupolar sodium nucleus. These findings validate PEG-based phantoms as effective models for micro-enviromental influences on sodium relaxation.

The relaxation behavior with increasing PEG concentration, reflecting restricted environments and reduced sodium mobility. Multi-exponential and Laplace-based analyses revealed distinct relaxation components associated with free and constrained sodium pools. 

Ex vivo human tissue validation.
Ex vivo 23Na MRI of human cortical samples preserved tissue contrast and enabled identification of focal alterations prior to histological processing. High-resolution X-ray Phase Contrast Tomography (XPCT)  provided three-dimensional visualization of cortical cytoarchitecture at micrometric resolution, allowing differentiation of neurons, vessels, and surrounding tissue (figure 2 A). X Ray Fluorescence (XRF) elemental mapping revealed heterogeneous sodium distributions in the neuron cell  and between AD and control samples (figure 2 B). Regions exhibiting elevated sodium concentration in XRF corresponded spatially to structural alterations observed in XPCT and to sodium MRI–derived metrics, supporting the biological relevance of the in vivo findings. XRF sodium mapping further revealed increased Na content in AD tissue relative to controls (figure 2 C), consistent with impaired ionic homeostasis and neuronal degeneration

Conclusion

The combined NMR, XPCT, and XRF findings showed that sodium relaxation and spatial distribution serve as sensitive markers of tissue integrity and cellular viability in neurodegenerative disease. This multimodal framework establishes a quantitative link between ²³Na NMR parameters, microstructural and elemental tissue features, providing new insight into sodium homeostasis alterations in Alzheimer’s disease.
