Introduction

Recent work has suggested that a substantial fraction of large-scale brain activity can be explained by a low-dimensional latent variable related to arousal. Building on this insight, we develop a non-autonomous whole-brain dynamical systems model in which arousal is explicitly represented through time-dependent modulation of system parameters. This framework moves beyond traditional stationary models by allowing the global working point of the brain to evolve over time, thereby capturing large-scale cortical interactions. Our modeling pipeline enables the extraction of time-dependent working points governing whole-brain dynamics. Applying this approach to human resting-state fMRI, we show that a temporally varying cortical excitability parameter provides the best account of the observed spatio-temporal organization of functional activity, outperforming stationary formulations. These results suggest that arousal-related modulation of excitability acts as a low-dimensional operator organizing resting-state dynamics. We further hypothesize that age-related cognitive decline arises, in part, from alterations in this low-dimensional arousal-driven control of brain dynamics. To explicitly test this hypothesis, we analyze a large cross-sectional cohort of approximately 1,000 adults spanning the full adult age range (the 1000 Brain dataset). Preliminary analyses reveal robust age-associated declines across a battery of 19 cognitive tests, accompanied by systematic age-related changes in the temporal statistics of dynamic functional connectivity. Ongoing work applies our non-autonomous modeling pipeline to estimate age-dependent, non-stationary working points and to quantify their relationship with chronological age and cognition. Together, this work provides a mechanistic framework linking arousal, whole-brain dynamics, and cognitive aging, and highlights low-dimensional control parameters as potential targets for understanding and mitigating age-related cognitive decline.

Methods

Dataset

We analyzed resting-state fMRI and cognitive data from the 1000 Brain Project1, a large cross-sectional adult lifespan cohort. Participants underwent eyes-open resting-state fMRI acquisition and completed an extensive neuropsychological battery spanning 19 cognitive measures assessing domains including memory, attention, executive function, processing speed, and reasoning. Standard preprocessing pipelines were applied to fMRI data, and regional time series were extracted using an atlas-based parcellation. We access the dataset via our participation in the PEPR Brain Health Trajectory project. In addition we developed our modeling pipeline on a separate dataset comprising of 200 resting state fMRI recordings from the Human Connectome Project dataset2. 

Functional Connectivity Dynamics

Dynamic functional connectivity (dFC) was computed for both empirical and simulated BOLD signals using a sliding-window approach. Time series were segmented into overlapping 60-s windows advanced in 2-s steps. Within each window, functional connectivity (FC) was estimated using Pearson correlations between regional time series. Functional connectivity dynamics (FCD) matrices were constructed by correlating the upper-triangular elements of all windowed FC matrices. FCD speed was quantified as the dissimilarity between FC matrices from successive non-overlapping windows. To increase sampling density, speeds were pooled across window lengths ranging from 55–65 s. Static FC was computed over the full recording. Time-resolved structure–function coupling was estimated by correlating instantaneous FC matrices with the structural connectivity matrix, and the resulting distributions were summarized using percentiles. All analyses used the MATLAB-based *dfcWalk* toolbox. Additionally, we analyze empirically observed streams of dynamic functional connectivity  to assess their capacity to support information flow across spatially organized brain networks. By thresholding time-resolved FC frames, we identify momentary pathways for communication and quantify complementary integrative and segregative properties, reflecting global information sharing and local information retention, respectively. Empirical FCD optimally balances these properties, whereas aging is associated with a concurrent degradation of both integrative and segregative information flow.

Whole-Brain Modeling

Each brain region was modeled using a mean-field neural mass model describing NMDA synaptic gating dynamics, with inputs arising from local recurrence, long-range structural coupling, constant external drive, and additive noise. Autonomous models used fixed parameters, while non-autonomous models allowed selected global parameters (global coupling, local recurrence, gain, or noise amplitude) to vary slowly over time according to a truncated Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, enforcing a baseline arousal level. Simulations were run for 950 s (1 ms time step), discarding initial transients. BOLD signals were approximated by low-pass filtering firing-rate time series below 0.2 Hz. 

Model Fitting

Model parameters were optimized by fitting simulated dynamics to empirical FCD and FCD-speed distributions. Feature vectors consisted of selected percentiles of these distributions. Parameter estimation was performed using a genetic algorithm minimizing Euclidean distance between empirical and simulated features, with bounded parameter ranges informed by prior sweeps.

Results

1. Modelling pipeline suggests a prominent role of arousal fluctuations in on-going patterns of functional connectivity dynamics.

We investigated the dynamical mechanisms underlying the temporal organization of functional connectivity dynamics (FCD) observed in human resting-state fMRI. Empirically, FCD exhibits heavy-tailed step-length distributions, with long epochs of slowly evolving connectivity punctuated by abrupt, large-scale reconfigurations. To explain these features, we compared a conventional autonomous whole-brain mean-field model—operating at a fixed dynamic working point—with a family of non-autonomous models in which key neural parameters fluctuate slowly in time, mimicking arousal-related neuromodulatory influences. A systematic parameter sweep of the autonomous model revealed sharp transition boundaries separating regimes of slow and fast FCD. These boundaries correspond to qualitative changes in the underlying phase space organization, including the emergence of rate instabilities. While autonomous dynamics could reproduce average functional connectivity and some aspects of FCD variability, they failed to account for the extreme, infrequent events that dominate the tails of empirical FCD and FCD-speed distributions. Introducing slow stochastic modulation of model parameters enabled the system to dynamically traverse these critical boundaries. Among all parameters tested—including neural gain, noise amplitude, and local recurrence—fluctuations in a global excitability-like coupling parameter emerged as the most effective control knob. Non-autonomous modulation of this parameter allowed the system to glide between dynamical regimes, producing intermittent slowing and rapid reconfiguration events closely resembling empirical FCD. We fit both autonomous and non-autonomous models to resting-state fMRI data from the Human Connectome Project using a genetic algorithm, targeting the full distributional structure of FCD and FCD speed through multiple percentiles. Arousal-driven models consistently outperformed autonomous formulations across subjects. Crucially, the largest improvements arose from better capturing the tails of the distributions—reflecting rare but pronounced dynamical events—while mean values were similarly reproduced by both model classes. Beyond fitting targets, the non-autonomous models also better predicted additional spatiotemporal properties of the data, including static FC structure, time-resolved structure–function coupling, and meta-connectivity patterns, indicating improved generalization. Analysis of fitted trajectories revealed a dominant dynamical motif in which the system operates near, but typically below, instability boundaries, making frequent excursions into slower regimes.  Together, these results demonstrate that slow arousal-related fluctuations in global cortical excitability provide a parsimonious and mechanistically grounded explanation for the temporal richness of resting-state functional connectivity dynamics. 

2. Aging is associated with cognitive decline and is indexed by temporal properties of resting functional connectivity dynamics

To what extent do low-dimensional arousal fluctuations explain cognitive decline ? In order to answer this question we first sought to characterize the relationship of cognitive scores with age and found significant decline with age spanning a number of cognitive domains (memory, attention, executive functions, language, visuospatial abilities). Alongside, we found significant associations between temporal features of resting state functional connectivity dynamics (using a temporal network approach) with age (see figure). Younger groups show faster inter-regional communication and stronger segregation, indicating a more efficient balance between global integration and local processing. The next phase of this study involves using our modeling pipeline to explore how arousal fluctuations impact these graph theoretic temporal features.

Conclusion

In this work, we investigated the dynamical mechanisms underlying the rich temporal organization of functional connectivity dynamics (FCD) observed in human resting-state fMRI and examined their relevance for cognitive aging. Our initial results provide converging empirical and modeling evidence that slow, low-dimensional fluctuations—interpretable as variations in arousal—play a central role in shaping large-scale brain dynamics.

Using a whole-brain mean-field modeling framework, we demonstrated that conventional autonomous models operating at fixed working points are insufficient to explain the full temporal structure of empirical FCD. While such models can reproduce average functional connectivity and capture some degree of temporal variability, they fail to account for the heavy-tailed distributions of FCD and FCD speed that are dominated by rare but pronounced reconfiguration events. Systematic exploration of model parameter space revealed sharp dynamical boundaries separating regimes of slow and fast connectivity evolution, reflecting qualitative changes in underlying phase-space organization. However, when constrained to a single operating point, the system remains confined to a limited dynamical repertoire.

Introducing slow stochastic modulation of key neural parameters fundamentally altered this picture. Non-autonomous models allowed the system to dynamically traverse these critical boundaries, generating intermittent epochs of stability interspersed with rapid, large-scale reconfigurations. Among all parameters tested, fluctuations in a global excitability-like coupling parameter emerged as the most effective control mechanism, providing a parsimonious account of the observed dynamics. When fitted to empirical data, arousal-driven non-autonomous models consistently outperformed autonomous formulations, with the largest gains arising from accurately capturing the tails of FCD and FCD-speed distributions. Importantly, these improvements generalized beyond the fitting targets, yielding more accurate predictions of static functional connectivity, time-resolved structure–function coupling, and meta-connectivity. Together, these findings suggest that resting-state brain activity operates near critical instability boundaries, with slow arousal fluctuations enabling flexible exploration of dynamical regimes while maintaining overall stability.

We then asked whether this low-dimensional arousal-driven control of brain dynamics is relevant for understanding cognitive aging. Analysis of a large adult lifespan cohort revealed robust age-related declines across multiple cognitive domains, including memory, attention, executive function, language, and visuospatial abilities. In parallel, we observed systematic age-associated alterations in temporal properties of resting-state functional connectivity dynamics, as quantified using a temporal network framework. These changes indicate that aging is accompanied not only by static reorganization of functional networks but also by a degradation of the temporal structure through which information is integrated and segregated over time.

Taken together, our findings support the hypothesis that arousal-related modulation of global brain dynamics constitutes a mechanistic link between large-scale neural activity and cognitive performance. Ongoing work will leverage our modeling pipeline to explicitly estimate age-dependent changes in non-stationary working points and to relate them to alterations in dynamic graph-theoretic properties and cognitive outcomes. More broadly, this framework highlights low-dimensional neuromodulatory control as a key organizing principle of resting-state dynamics and suggests that age-related cognitive decline may, in part, reflect a reduced capacity to flexibly regulate arousal-driven excursions across dynamical regimes.
