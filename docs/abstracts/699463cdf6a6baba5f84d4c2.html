Introduction

Neurobiological theories emphasize that the brain—measured as brain structure, microstructure, and/or function—influences the development of psychopathology in youth.1 In particularly, these brain measures within the corticolimbic, corticostriatal, and executive function networks, which are involved in stress response, affective processing, reward processing, learning, and behavioral control, appear disrupted in youth with increased psychopathology. However, almost all research has examined the associations between individual brain measures (structure or microstructure or function) and youth psychopathology separately. As these brain networks are distributed across the cortex/subcortex and interacting with each other, studying them in isolation limits our ability to capture their shared and distinct relevance across psychopathology domains. Similarly, focusing on individual brain measures within each of these networks constrains our ability to identify combinations of brain measures that drive their relationships with domains of psychopathology. Here, we identified patterns of brain structure, microstructure, and function from corticostriatal, corticolimbic, and executive function networks that relate to psychopathology domains in an integrated manner during adolescence.

Methods

Data: We used imaging and psychopathology data at baseline from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study (9-10 years, n=9,127).2 The imaging data were derived from structural, diffusion (microstructural), and task-based functional MRI across corticostriatal, corticolimbic, and executive function networks. The Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL) was used to assess domains of psychopathology: aggression, rule-breaking, anxiety, depression, somatic complaints, attention problems, social problems, and thought problems.3 Preprocessing: Standard MRI preprocessing was performed by the ABCD Study Data Analysis, Informatics & Resource Center.4 Structural MRI measures captured gray matter properties including cortical thickness, surface area, and cortical/subcortical volumes from brain regions defined by Desikan-Killiany parcellation and automated segmentation procedures. Diffusion MRI measures indexed white matter properties in the form of fractional anisotropy, mean diffusivity, axial diffusivity, and radial diffusivity from white matter tracts labeled using a probabilistic atlas. Task-based functional MRI measures corresponded to brain activations from the emotional N-back, monetary incentive delay, and stop-signal tasks for brain regions defined by Desikan-Killiany parcellation and automated segmentation procedures. The imaging and CBCL data were normalized by z-scoring all brain measures and CBCL subscales. All imaging and CBCL data were residualized for participant sex assigned at birth, race/ethnicity, and scanner site. Structural MRI measures were further residualized for mean cortical thickness, total surface area, and total intracranial volume. Diffusion and functional MRI measures also were residualized for mean head motion. We then performed principal component analysis to reduce the dimensionality of the imaging data for each brain network and measure and balance the ratio of the brain variables to CBCL subscales. Principal components (PCs) were chosen based on the amount of explained variance and their statistical significance via permutation testing across 5,000 iterations to ensure they reflect true underlying structures rather than noise. Analysis: We employed canonical correlation analysis (CCA) to identify modes of covariation between brain measures and CBCL subscales.5 Each mode represents linear combinations of brain measures and CBCL subscales that are maximally correlated using Pearson’s r. Statistical significance of the modes was obtained via permutation testing across 5,000 iterations and FDR-corrected for multiple comparisons. We then computed the cross-loadings as the Pearson’s r between brain-derived modes and CBCL subscales. We also derived brain loadings to examine the primary contributors of brain measures for each brain-derived mode, and therefore driving the associations with CBCL subscales. Brain loadings reflect the Pearson’s r between brain measures and brain-derived modes. We interpreted the strongest brain loadings with |r|>0.20 based on effect size guidelines.

Results

We obtain 39 significant PCs across brain networks and measures, yielding a brain-to-CBCL variable ratio of approximately 5:1. CCA identified 6 significant modes of covariation: Mode 1 (r=0.13, P=0.001), Mode 2 (r=0.11, P<0.001), Mode 3 (r=0.09, P<0.001), Mode 4 (r=0.08, P<0.001), Mode 5 (r=0.07, P=0.02), and Mode 6 (r=0.06, P=0.03). These modes were validated using 5-fold cross validation and random splitting of the ABCD data into 85% train-set and 15% test-set across 100 times. Cross-loadings indicated that brain-derived modes and psychopathology domains were significantly associated—albeit with varying magnitudes (Fig.1A). The dominant brain-derived mode, i.e., Mode 1, was most strongly associated with attention problems (r=0.12, P<0.001). Mode 1’s strongest loadings corresponded to the corticostriatal microstructures and activations, corticolimbic activations, and executive function structures (Fig.1B). Mode 2 was most strongly associated with thought problems (r=0.04, P<0.001). Mode 2’s strongest loadings corresponded to the corticostriatal microstructures and activations as well as corticolimbic and executive function structures and microstructures (Fig.1B). Mode 3 was most strongly associated with rule-breaking (r=-0.05, P<0.001). Mode 3’s strongest loadings corresponded to the corticostriatal structures and activations as well as corticolimbic and executive function structures and microstructures (Fig.1B). Mode 4 and Mode 5 were most strongly associated with social problems (Mode 4: r=0.05, P<0.001; Mode 5: r=0.03, P=0.004). Mode 4’s strongest loadings corresponded to the corticostriatal and corticolimbic structures and microstructures as well as executive function microstructures (Fig.1B). Mode 5’s strongest loadings corresponded to the corticostriatal and corticolimbic microstructures and activations in addition to executive function structures, microstructures, and activations (Fig.1B). Mode 6 was most strongly associated with somatic complaints (r=0.04, P=0.001). Mode 6’s strongest loadings corresponded to the corticostriatal structures and microstructures, corticolimbic structures, and executive function structures and activations (Fig.1B). The findings showed that these modes captured both shared and distinct contributions of brain measures and networks to different domains of psychopathology.

Conclusion

We captured associations between brain-derived modes from structural, diffusion, and functional MRI and psychopathology domains during adolescence. These independent modes reveal the extent to which brain patterns exhibit common and distinct associations with psychopathology domains. These modes could elucidate how different levels of brain organization influence their relationships with specific domains of psychopathology.
