Introduction

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) is an established treatment for movement disorders and epilepsy. In recent years, it has been increasingly explored as a therapeutic option for treatment-resistant psychiatric disorders, including schizophrenia, for which the first contemporary clinical trials and cases have been published in the last five years. While numerous brain network abnormalities have been reported in schizophrenia, the causal mechanisms of the disease are not fully identified. Hence, the first DBS trials explored a variety of surgical targets, but the best candidates and overall suitability of DBS remain uncertain. Over the years and across indications, retrospective studies of anatomical correlates of treatment response and serendipitous clinical observations have played a key role in defining and refining DBS targets. Here, we build on both approaches to investigate potential targets for treatment-resistant schizophrenia. Specifically, we gathered cases in which DBS either induced or alleviated psychotic symptoms and characterized the anatomical structures engaged by stimulation in each of these cases. As a second step, we interpreted these anatomical findings in the context of existing models of schizophrenia, hypothesizing that stimulation sites causing or improving psychotic symptoms would map onto a limited number of brain circuits implicated in these models (1).

Methods

We performed a systematic review of the literature to identify all reported cases where deep brain stimulation modulated (i.e., caused or improved) psychotic symptoms such as auditory or visual hallucinations and/or delusions, irrespective of surgical target and indication. We included patients receiving DBS for treatment-resistant psychosis, as well as patients who received DBS for different indications but presented with new onset or improved psychotic symptoms following stimulation. Importantly, for incidental cases, we only included cases with evidence of possible causal relationship between stimulation and change in symptoms. For example, we included cases for which symptoms started shortly after a change in stimulation settings and quickly improved upon further change in settings or discontinuation of stimulation, but not cases in which symptoms were already present before surgery and worsened or re-occurred under DBS. Authors of suitable publications were contacted to gather individual case data. When possible, DBS electrodes were reconstructed based on the preoperative and postoperative imaging using a state-of-the-art, publicly available neuroimaging pipeline (Lead-DBS toolbox) (2). Stimulation volumes were modeled based on available stimulation parameters using the OSS-DBS software. 

To identify the stimulated white matter pathways and structures, the volumetric overlap between the stimulation volumes and surrounding structures was computed based on 3-D anatomical atlases and normative connectomes available in Lead-DBS. Anatomical findings were interpreted in the context of current neurobiological theories of schizophrenia to identify candidate circuits consistently modulated across cases. Given the small and heterogeneous sample, this step of the analysis was qualitative and exploratory, aiming at integrating the empirical anatomical findings with existing models, rather than performing data-driven statistical testing.

Results

Thirty-six cases were included in this case series (Fig. 1). 

DBS for schizophrenia - Sixteen patients received DBS for treatment-resistant psychosis. Six patients with schizophrenia received DBS of the nucleus accumbens, with a mean improvement of 28.2% on the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS). In patients with available electrode reconstructions (N=3), better outcomes were seen for stimulation volumes covering the NAc and surrounding limbic pathways, rather than the caudate. Of note, an additional patient with psychosis secondary to traumatic brain injury received DBS of the NAc, with a 64.5% improvement on the PANSS. Four patients with schizophrenia received sgACC-DBS, with more modest benefit (19.6% improvement on the PANSS). No obvious relationship between anatomical substrates and outcomes could be derived for these patients (N=4). 

Two patients with schizophrenia received DBS of the habenula, with one patient experiencing a 31.7% improvement on the PANSS, and one patient worsening by 9.5% and being withdrawn from the study after 10 months. The patient who improved had larger stimulation volumes engaging the habenula and surrounding thalamic nuclei, including the mediodorsal nucleus. Finally, three patients received DBS of the substantia nigra pars reticulata. Long-term clinical outcomes were only reported for one of them so far, with an 84% improvement in psychosis-related Brief Psychotic Rating Scale (BPRS) scores.

Incidental cases - We found 20 cases in which DBS applied at various targets and for various indications triggered (N=18) or improved (N=2) psychotic symptoms. Seven of these cases concerned patients with epilepsy and DBS of the anterior nucleus of the thalamus, who all experienced delusions. Symptoms could be relieved by DBS reprogramming in five cases. Analysis of available electrode reconstructions (N=6) showed that reprogramming led to decreased engagement of the mediodorsal nucleus. Another patient with epilepsy experienced auditory hallucinations with CM-DBS, which were hypothesized to result from off-target stimulation effects. 

Six patients with Parkinson’s disease and subthalamic deep brain stimulation developed psychotic symptoms. In patients with available electrode reconstructions (N=5), stimulation volumes overlapped with the limbic subthalamic nucleus and substantia nigra pars reticulata. Stimulation settings associated with symptom occurrence (rather than resolution) had stronger overlap with these structures, as well as with the nigrothalamic efferents to the mediodorsal nucleus and limbic portion of the hyperdirect pathway. 

Delusions were observed after DBS of the nucleus accumbens in two cases (one major depressive disorder, one obsessive compulsive disorder) and could be relieved by reprogramming or discontinuation of the stimulation. Two patients with GPi-DBS developed psychosis (one Parkinson’s disease, one dystonia). In one case, reproducible occurrence of symptoms was related to the stimulation of dorsal contacts which resided in the vicinity of a small lesion. The presence of this lesion might have distorted the current flow, leading to unexpected stimulation effects at a distance from the stimulating contact, particularly in the surrounding white matter (internal capsule and pallidothalamic projections to the mediodorsal nucleus). In the second case, symptoms occurred after migration of the left electrode resulting in unintended stimulation of the amygdala and hippocampus. Symptoms were resolved by electrode repositioning. 

Finally, two patients with DBS of the nucleus basalis of Meynert for Parkinson’s disease dementia experienced “ON-OFF” improvement of pre-existing visual hallucinations. These cases were characterized by more antero-ventral electrode placement as well as stronger involvement of surrounding white matter pathways (anterior commissure, ventral amygdalofugal pathway) as compared to non-improvers. 

Conclusion

Our case series describes the largest collection to date of cases in which DBS modulated psychotic symptoms (N=36). Detailed characterization of the anatomical structures engaged by stimulation was performed in 29 cases with available electrode reconstructions. Of note, emergence of psychotic symptoms could be linked to off-target DBS effects in a majority of cases. By qualitatively integrating our anatomical findings with current neurobiological models of schizophrenia, we identified two candidate circuits that may be causally involved in psychotic symptoms and might constitute promising targets for DBS (Fig. 1). 

First, six cases could be tied to the involvement of the dopaminergic VTA-NAc loop and its hippocampal input, consistent with the known implication of this circuit in psychosis. Specifically, the hyperdopaminergic state resulting from disruption of this circuit in schizophrenia is believed to result in aberrant salience signaling, which is hypothesized to directly contribute to hallucinations and delusions (3). 

         Second, twenty cases could be mapped to the MD or its main subcortical afferents (i.e., projections from the amygdala and GPi/SNr). Numerous lines of evidence have implicated the mediodorsal nucleus in psychosis (4). In contrast to the VTA-NAc loop, no comparably strong theoretical model has been proposed to explain the involvement of this circuit. Consistent with the key position of the mediodorsal nucleus as the thalamic relay of the associative and limbic basal ganglia loops, and with its role in monitoring, maintaining and updating relevant cognitive (associative) and affective (limbic) representations, we propose that MD dysfunction or disruption may interfere with reality filtering, a cognitive process that bears direct relevance for psychotic symptoms (5). 

         The proposed preliminary theoretical framework, linking two candidate circuits to the emergence and improvement of psychotic symptoms, yields testable hypotheses that may guide future mechanistic and clinical studies.
