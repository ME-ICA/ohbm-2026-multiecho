Introduction

Half of the world population will go through the menopausal transition at midlife. This period, characterized by a decline in levels of circulating ovarian hormones that support cognition, has been theorized as a major contributing factor to the greater prevalence of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) in women than men (Jacobs et al., 2016; Nevel et al., 2018). However, the exact mechanisms of menopause effects on brain health and the extent to which negative effects can be ameliorated by menopausal hormone therapy (MHT) are still not sufficiently understood. This is in part due to a lack of attention paid to hormonal composition of MHT (Puri et al., 2025) as well as individual risk factors for AD, such as the presence of apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 allele, the strongest genetic predictor of sporadic AD onset and progression, which is particularly predictive of AD in women (Gamache et al. 2020).

Methods

The current set of studies examined interacting effects of MHT type and presence of APOE ε4 risk allele on neurocognition. Behavioral outcomes were predicted in participants from the National Alzheimer’s Coordinating Centre (NACC; age = 70 ± 8.3) either taking no MHT (n=10,645), or one of the four most commonly studied types of MHT: estradiol only (E2; n=625), combination estradiol and progesterone (E2+P4; n=105), conjugated equine estrogens (CEE; n=313), or a combination of CEE and medroxyprogesterone acetate (CEE+MPA; n=86). Cortical thickness, subcortical volumes and white matter hypointensities (WMH) were predicted in a subset of NACC participant with neuroimaging data (age = 70.7 ± 7.3 ;no MHT n=1,077, E2 n=82). Analyses were corrected for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate. Extracted structural data was obtained from NACC. Original cortical parcellation and WMH quantitation was performed by the IDeA Lab at UC Davis (De Carli et al. 2023). Briefly, cortical thickness and brain volumes were extracted using Freesurfer. WMH quantification relied on a Bayesian framework that first segmented T1 images into gray matter, white matter, and CSF using an iterative algorithm. FLAIR data were then incorporated to generate voxelwise WMH probabilities and thresholded to create masks which were then transformed back to native space for volume calculation.

Results

Behavioral analyses indicated worse episodic memory performance in participants taking CEE relative to participants taking E2 (p = .001) or no MHT (p = .023). Neuroimaging analyses indicated significant interactions between E2 MHT use and APOE genotype in predicting average thickness of inferior parietal (p = .004), supramarginal (p = .004), and entorhinal areas (p = .002); brain stem (p = .014), accumbens (p = .005), ventral diencephalon (p = .006), and hippocampal (p = .010) volumes, and white matter hypointensities (p = .009). Interaction effects showed the same pattern across brain regions, with more favourable outcomes (higher thickness, volume; lower white matter hypointensities) seen in ε4 carriers taking E2, but not in non-carriers (Fig. 1).

Conclusion

The current results highlight the importance of considering differences in MHT composition when assessing its cognitive impact, and suggest that estradiol therapy may have neuroprotective effects in individuals at higher genetic risk for late-life Alzheimer’s disease. This is particularly important as most studies on the neural and cognitive effects of MHT either do not consider effects of APOE genotype and MHT composition or only include these factors as covariates. One limitation of the current data is that no information was available on duration of MHT use, so the results only present effects of current MHT use. Our results have significant implications for precision approaches to menopause care, and are of particular interest to researchers considering the potential of MHT as an AD risk intervention.
