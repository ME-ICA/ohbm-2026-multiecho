Introduction

Whole-brain models based on coupled nonlinear oscillators provide a principled generative framework for understanding how large-scale brain dynamics emerge from interactions between distributed neural populations1. Within this framework, generative effective connectivity (GEC) has recently been shown to capture fundamental organisational principles of brain dynamics that go beyond statistical dependencies measured by functional connectivity2. In particular, GEC has been used to characterise cross-species similarities and differences in large-scale organisation, to derive subject-specific dynamical fingerprints, and to estimate the computational capacity and competitive interactions that shape emergent whole-brain behaviour across brain states3.

These advances highlight the growing importance of scalable and robust methods for inferring GEC at the individual-subject level. However, current approaches typically rely on computationally expensive iterative simulation combined with naïve gradient-descent or heuristic optimisation strategies2,3. Here, we address this limitation by introducing a differentiable reformulation of the canonical whole-brain Hopf model. By embedding the Hopf model within a recurrent neural network (RNN) framework, we enable efficient end-to-end optimisation of GEC via back-propagation through time (BPTT)4, while preserving the original large-scale model5. This approach is designed to directly support emerging applications of GEC that require reliable individual-level inference and repeated large-scale model fitting.

Methods

Data acquisition and preprocessing: Resting-state fMRI and diffusion-weighted imaging data were obtained from Human Connectome Project (N = 20), following the minimally preprocessed pipeline. Functional MRI data were acquired with TR = 0.72 and 1200 timepoints. Standard preprocessing was applied, including bias field correction, functional realignment, motion correction, and spatial normalisation to MNI-152 standard space with 2mm isotropic resampling resolution, followed by temporal band-pass filtering (0.008 to 0.09Hz). Parcellation and time series extraction: The cortex was parcellated using the Schaefer100 atlas with N = 100. Regional BOLD time series were obtained by averaging voxel signals within each parcel. Empirical functional connectivity (FC) was computed as Pearson correlation between regional time series. Lagged FC was computed using time-lagged correlations at lag τ = 3. Structural connectivity: Structural connectivity was derived from Human Connectome Project diffusion MRI data using the reconstruction and tractography pipeline described in Luppi et al.3 Whole-brain structural connectivity matrices were constructed by counting streamlines between parcels and were used to constrain long-range coupling in the Hopf model. Whole-brain Hopf model: Each brain region was modelled as a Hopf oscillator operating near the supercritical bifurcation (a=-0.02). Local dynamics were governed by the Hopf normal form, with intrinsic frequencies estimated from empirical BOLD signals. Long-range interactions were mediated by generative effective connectivity (GEC), constrained by structural connectivity. Model simulations produced multivariate time series from which simulated FC and lagged FC were computed. HopfRNN formulation and optimisation. The Hopf model was reformulated as a recurrent neural network (HopfRNN), in which oscillator states correspond to recurrent hidden states and GEC corresponds to learnable recurrent weights. The model was unrolled in time and optimised using back-propagation through time (BPTT) to minimise the discrepancy between simulated and empirical FC and lagged FC. Parameters were optimised using AdamW with fixed learning rate and gradient clipping, minimising mean-squared error between empirical and simulated FC (plus lag-FC). Traditional GEC estimation using naïve gradient descent served as a baseline (Figure 1).

Results

HopfRNN optimisation converged rapidly and stably across subjects, exhibiting smooth learning dynamics and reduced sensitivity to initial conditions compared to traditional optimisation (Figure 2B, Left). Across subjects, simulated FC and lagged FC showed high correspondence with empirical measures, with median train-set correlations approaching r ≈ 0.9 (Figure 2B, Right), indicating consistent performance across individuals. At the single-subject level, HopfRNN-derived GEC closely matched GEC obtained using state-of-the-art optimisation methods. Visual inspection of GEC matrices revealed similar spatial organisation and sign structure (Figure 2A, Left). Edge-wise comparisons demonstrated strong correlations between HopfRNN and traditional GEC estimates (e.g. r=0.63 for an example subject), despite differences in optimisation strategy  (Figure 2A, Right). At the group level, HopfRNNs consistently outperformed traditional optimisation in FC fitting accuracy  (Figure 2C, Left). Importantly, this improvement did not arise from trivial rescaling or distortion of connectivity weights. Distributions of inferred GEC weights were comparable between methods, with similar means and variances. The relative balance of positive and negative effective connections, as well as their mean magnitudes, was preserved under the HopfRNN formulation (Figure 2C, Middle and Right). Together, these results demonstrate that HopfRNNs recover biologically plausible GEC patterns consistent with established methods, while substantially improving optimisation stability and functional fit. This suggests that differentiable optimisation provides a more efficient inference mechanism for the same underlying generative model, rather than introducing a fundamentally different solution.

Conclusion

We introduce hopfRNNs as a differentiable reformulation of the canonical whole-brain Hopf model, enabling efficient and stable estimation of generative effective connectivity via back-propagation through time. By preserving the original biophysical equations and constraining network interactions using empirical structural connectivity, this approach maintains interpretability while substantially improving inference efficiency. The ability to robustly estimate GEC at the individual-subject level is critical for emerging applications that rely on generative models of whole-brain dynamics, including cross-species comparisons, subject fingerprinting, and the quantification of computational capacity and competitive interactions in large-scale networks. HopfRNNs directly support these applications by reducing computational cost and enabling repeated model fitting across large datasets. More broadly, this work demonstrates that differentiable implementations of biophysically grounded whole-brain models can bridge the gap between mechanistic neuroscience and modern machine-learning optimisation, without sacrificing model transparency. HopfRNNs provide a scalable framework for personalised and comparative whole-brain modelling and offer a foundation for future extensions, including task-dependent modelling, perturbational analyses, and closed-loop optimisation of stimulation protocols.
