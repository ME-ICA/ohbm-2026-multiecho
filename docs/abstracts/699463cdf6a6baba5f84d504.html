Introduction

The cerebellum undergoes rapid development in early life, paralleling major cognitive and behavioral changes. Accumulating evidence suggests that the adult cerebellum plays a critical role in a wide range of cognitive functions, including language and social cognition.1 Although clinical studies have linked early cerebellar disruptions to profound developmental deficits that might disrupt distant neocortical circuits and behavioral outcomes,1 it remains unclear how typical cerebellar maturation supports cognitive development and how it interacts with broader brain maturation. Here, we apply a normative modeling framework to map cerebellar volumetric growth from infancy to young adulthood. Using lobular and functional cerebellar parcellations, we comprehensively characterize typical cerebellar development and examine how it aligns with cerebral development and behavioral outcomes.

Methods

We leveraged open structural (T1-weighted) MRI data from the Baby Connectome Project (BCP) and the Lifespan Human Connectome Project in Development (HCP-D) (N = 751; age range: 1-22 years). Infant scans (<2 years) were pre-processed with iBEAT V2.0, and child and adolescent scans (>2 years) with the standard HCP minimal pre-processing pipelines. We obtained native-space anatomical volumes of the cerebellum using ACAPULCO, a convolutional neural network-based algorithm that parcellates the cerebellum in 28 lobules. For functional models, we leveraged three widely-used cerebellar atlases, derived from cerebellar resting-state networks,2 a multi-domain task battery (MDTB),3 and a parcellation constructed by fusing diverse task-based datasets into a single functional atlas.4 We extracted functional parcel volumes by resampling the MNI-space atlas to each subject’s native space with nearest neighbor interpolation. Three independent reviewers visually inspected each parcellation output for cerebellar over- and under-inclusions via consensus validation, performing manual corrections as necessary. For the cerebral cortex, we extracted parcel volumes for the Desikan-Killiany and Yeo-17 atlases using FreeSurfer’s recon-all, and removed subjects with defects in surface reconstruction (Euler index > |2| median deviations).

We constructed anatomical and functional growth models using hierarchical Bayesian regression (HBR), controlling for sex and scanner site variability by specifying them as batch (random) effects. We generated linear and 3rd-order B-spline models of cerebellar and cerebral growth for each cerebellar and cerebral parcel, after splitting the dataset into a training (90%) and test set (10%). Inference was performed with Markov chain Monte Carlo methods (4 chains with 2,000 samples). We performed model comparison of linear and B-spline models via leave-one-out cross-validation. 

We then examined whether cerebellar parcel growth predicts cerebral parcel growth (HBR-derived participant z-scores) with regularized regression. We compared Ridge, Lasso, and ElasticNet regression models with nested 10-fold cross-validation, and performed significance testing of cerebello-cerebral associations via 10,000 permutations. We further associated cerebellar growth with participants’ behavioral scores across sensorimotor and association domains with Partial Least Squares (PLS) regression across 10 latent variables. Significance of the PLS loadings was assessed by 10,000 iterations of bootstrap resampling. We ran post-hoc mass-univariate regressions, predicting behaviors from cerebellar parcels (controlling for age and sex) to further unpack the multivariate PLS associations. Lastly, we evaluated the relative contribution of cerebellar versus cerebral growth to behavioral development by testing how well cerebellar-only, cerebral-only, and combined cerebello-cerebral normative z-scores predicted each behavioral measure. To this end, we used regularized regression to estimate out-of-sample predictive performance (R²) of cerebellar-only, cerebral-only, and combined cerebello-cerebral models for each behavior across ten cross-validation folds. As in cerebral associations, we selected the optimal regression model by comparing Ridge, Lasso, and ElasticNet regression models with nested 10-fold cross-validation.

Results

Linear and B-spline HBR models performed equally well based on leave-one-out cross-validation. For parsimony, we here describe linear models. Across cerebellar parcellations, all parcels demonstrated age-related volumetric increases (Fig. 1A-B). Intriguingly, across parcellations, cerebellar anterior regions (lobules I–V), associated with sensorimotor processing, demonstrated smaller age-related effects (i.e., flatter growth trajectories) compared to posterior regions (lobules VI–IX), associated with higher-order cognitive functions (e.g., attention and language). For instance, left S1 of the fusion atlas (related to linguistic processing) showed the highest age effect (βage = 0.42; 95% CI = [0.32, 0.52]), while left M4 (related to motor processing) showed the lowest (βage = 0.08; 95% CI = [-0.01, 0.32]) (Fig. 1B). We also observed subtle sex differences across parcellations, with age-related coefficients being overall slightly higher for males than for females. 

Given the consistency observed among cerebellar parcellations, we henceforth focus on z-scores derived from the fusion atlas for cerebral and behavioral associations. In cerebral associations, the Ridge model marginally outperformed Lasso and ElasticNet based on cross-validated R² and was therefore selected. Cerebellar and cerebral areas with similar functional roles demonstrated coordinated maturation. For example, cerebellar right S3 (social/resting-state) was positively associated with social and resting-state regions [e.g., precuneus (Desikan-Killiany); default mode network (Yeo1-7)], and right D2 (working memory) was associated with memory and executive control regions [e.g., entorhinal (Desikan Killiany); frontoparietal network (Yeo-17)] (Fig 1C). 

In behavioral associations, the 1st out of 10 latent variables significantly captured 78% of the covariation between parcel-wise normative z-scores and behavioral scores (p = .010, 10,000 permutations). PLS loadings for the 1st latent variable demonstrated selective covariation between higher association cerebellar parcels (multi-demand, socio-linguistic) and individual differences in the development of socio-linguistic behaviors (significant after 10,000 bootstraps). Lastly, cerebellar parcels emerged as a unique predictor of atypical social behavior, over and above cerebral and combined cerebello-cerebral parcels (in both Desikan-Killiany and Yeo-17 atlases), as evidenced by model fit (R²) of ElasticNet models (selected model type) of each feature set across behaviors (Fig 1D).

Conclusion

This study establishes a comprehensive normative framework for human cerebellar development, providing critical reference benchmarks for identifying atypical cerebellar maturation from infancy through adulthood. We show that posterior cerebellar regions follow steeper, positive developmental trajectories, consistent with their increasing involvement in higher-order cognition. Furthermore, the domain-specific co-maturation of cerebellar and cerebral regions suggests that disruptions to particular cerebellar territories may confer vulnerability to dysfunction in their cerebral targets, reflecting the extensive reciprocal connectivity between these systems.5 Finally, the unique predictive value of cerebellar growth for socio-linguistic behaviors, especially atypical social functioning, underscores the cerebellum’s central role in neurodevelopment and its susceptibility to social-cognitive disruption. Overall, our work advances understanding of cerebellar development in relation to both cerebral maturation and emerging behavioral skills, providing a foundation for precision medicine approaches in pediatric neuropsychiatric conditions involving cerebellar dysfunction.
