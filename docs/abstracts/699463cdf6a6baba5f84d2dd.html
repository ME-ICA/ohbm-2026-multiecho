Introduction

Functional connectivity refers to the statistical dependence between brain signals originating from spatially separated regions. In recent years, neuroscientists have developed and applied an increasing number of methods to assess the dynamics of functional connectivity derived from signals acquired using functional magnetic resonance imaging during resting state. Instead, less attention has been dedicated to examine functional connectivity changes during varying task conditions. The most popular method, due to its theoretical simplicity and ease of implementation, is temporal linear correlation. However, an alternative class of metrics—phase-based measures derived via the Hilbert transform—offers a complementary perspective on connectivity by quantifying synchrony in the instantaneous phases of brain signals.1 Phase synchronization methods have been used extensively in electrophysiology and, to a lesser extent, resting-state fMRI; yet their application to task-related connectivity remains poorly explored.2,3 Here, we aim to evaluate the utility of phase locking,4 a phase synchronization measure, as a method to estimate task-related functional connectivity changes, and compare it to temporal linear correlation. Phase locking has the advantage of being independent of constant temporal delays, such as those introduced by sequential slice acquisition—potentially obviating the need for slice-timing correction. Using synthetic task fMRI data, we examined how phase locking and linear correlation behave under different task designs, slice-timing shifts, non-linear interactions, as well as measurement noise and filtering. We conclude by offering practical guidance for implementing phase-based connectivity analyses in task fMRI.

Methods

By modeling brain area interactions using a dynamic causal modelling framework,5 we simulated realistic blood-oxygenated-level-dependent responses from two putative brain regions, under different task conditions. We implemented a two-regions model in which activity in region x1 is driven by task inputs u(t) (Figure 1 A-B). A connection from x1 to x2 was specified, such that activity in x2 could only result from a functional connection with x1. The simulated task comprised two identical blocks. During the first block, regions x1 and x2 were not connected, resulting in input-driven activity in x1 exclusively. Conversely, during the second block, a connection from x1 to x2  was present, causing input-driven activity in x1 to elicit activity in x2 (Figure 1C). Task-block effects were regressed out from signals in order to ensure that driving inputs would not affect connectivity estimates. Phase locking was estimated by extracting the instantaneous phases of the signals through the Hilbert transform and taking the modulo of the mean phase difference unit vector. Temporal linear correlation was estimated using the Pearson correlation coefficient. To evaluate how linear correlation and phase locking depend on task parameters, we assessed the respective functional connectivity measures under different block lengths, trial-lengths and inter-trial intervals. Then, we generated synthetic data with varying slice-timing differences, and assessed the robustness of each method to such temporal offsets. Additionally, we investigated how well the two methods were able to detect functional connectivity changes under the presence of non-linear interactions between the regions. Hence, we employed non-linear dynamic causal modelling to generate data with varying degrees of non-linearity, and estimated functional connectivity using both methods. Finally, we explored how each method’s estimates depend on the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the data, as well as on temporal filtering. Therefore, we generated data with different levels of SNR by applying independently distributed zero-mean gaussian noise, and assessed functional connectivity before and after low-pass filtering. SNR was defined as the variance of the signal during task periods, divided by the variance of noise. For each unique condition, we generated 1000 synthetic time-series and compared the methods using point estimates of connectivity and contrast-to-noise ratios defined as the median difference between the two blocks divided by the interquartile range of the difference.

Results

Both phase locking and correlation showed improved ability to detect functional connectivity changes with longer blocks, longer trials, and increased inter-trial-intervals (Figure 2 A-C). Overall, task parameters had similar effects on both connectivity measures. Phase locking demonstrated greater robustness to slice-timing shifts (Figure 2 D). Results showed that even small temporal shifts of 0.5 seconds had a noticeable effect on correlation, and shifts of 1.5 and 2 seconds degraded any ability to detect connectivity changes. In contrast, phase locking remained largely unaffected in the tested range of temporal offsets (from 0 to 2 seconds). Both measures detected functional connectivity changes in the presence of non-linear interactions and agreed on which non-linear coefficients was associated with a loss of connectivity (i.e. -0.8 Hz). The latter corresponds to the point at which positive linear effects and negative non-linear effects balance and cancel each other. The only difference between the two methods was observed for strong negative non-linear effects below this balance point (Figure 2 E). Compared to correlation, phase locking exhibited a more symmetric behavior for non-linear strengths around the balance point. Finally, noise had a similar degrading effect on both methods, with no observable difference between the two methods. However, after low-pass filtering, phase locking exhibited generally higher contrast-to-noise ratios for SNRs above one (Figure 2 F).

Conclusion

Overall, our results suggest that phase synchronization offers a reliable alternative to linear correlation for detecting task-related functional connectivity changes with the advantage of greater robustness to timing differences and noise. Phase locking showed marked robustness to slice-timing differences, preserving accurate estimates under temporal offsets that significantly degraded linear correlation. Potentially, this can obviate the need for slice timing correction in functional connectivity analyses. Additionally, phase locking demonstrated more consistent estimates under the presence of non-linear effects. These results are expected, as phase locking quantifies the dispersion of the phase difference between two signals regardless of phase shifts or the type of relationship between the signals. We suggest that phase-based connectivity measures should complement current methods that are based on the relationships of signals in their time-domain. Our data suggest that phase locking and linear correlation behave similarly for the tested task parameters. Therefore, the same task design principles should be considered when designing fMRI tasks where task-related functional connectivity is to be assessed. For the same reason, we encourage researchers to use phase locking in combination with linear correlation methods, to further substantiate their conclusions.
