Introduction

The detection of pathological anomalies in brain MRI is crucial for diagnosis and treatment planning. While supervised deep learning models have achieved remarkable success, they are typically confined to specific lesions from their training data and require expensive pixel-level expert annotations, lacking the generalization capability for unseen pathologies. Generalizable anomaly detection may overcome these barriers, satisfying the growing demand for efficient screening tools in AI-integrated clinical workflows. For instance, such tools can extract pathological "key slices" from 3D volumes for vision-language models. 

In this study, we propose a generalizable anomaly detection framework that focused on T2-weighted (T2w) brain MRI using self-supervised masked autoencoders (MAE).1 Designed for slice-by-slice analysis, our approach integrates ensemble reconstruction with a hybrid anomaly score to capture multi-scale irregularities. By learning normal anatomical features from multi-source unlabeled data, this framework aims to achieve stable, label-free detection across diverse pathologies and acquisition variations.

Methods

This study used publicly available T2-weighted (T2w) MRI images, comprising 1183 volumes for training and 983 volumes for testing. All data used were de-identified. The training set aggregated non-pathological axial T2w slices from three heterogeneous sources: IXI, MPI-LEMON2, and SSL3D. Model performance was evaluated on three distinct pathological datasets in the testing set: (1) BraTS 20203 (glioma, 369 volumes), (2) MSLesSeg4 (multiple sclerosis, 115 volumes), and (3) BraTS 2023-MEN5 (meningioma, 499 volumes). All volumes underwent skull stripping, reorientation, and resampling to 1mm3 isotropic resolution. We extracted 2D slices (train: > 175k, test: > 136k) containing brain tissue, padded or cropped to 224 × 224, and normalized intensities to [0, 1]. In contrast to the unlabeled training data, for test set evaluation, slices were labeled as anomalous if they contained any lesion pixels (e.g., tumor or multiple sclerosis) in their ground truth masks.

We trained a MAE (ViT-B/16) for 1500 epochs with a mask ratio of 0.75, using image augmentations such as resolution adjustments, spatial shifts, and scaling (Figure 1, left). During inference (Figure 1, right), we applied an ensemble-reconstruction strategy: for each input, k masked variants were generated using stochastic mask sampling, reconstructed independently, and then aggregated using a median operation to suppress outliers. The anomaly map was derived by comparing the input with the median reconstruction. We defined an ensemble hybrid anomaly score (E-HAS) combining pixel-wise L1 error DL1 and structural difference DSSIM, and expressed as E-HAS = DL1 + DSSIM. Model performance was evaluated using the AUROC, the AUPRC, and the normalized Matthews correlation coefficient (NormMCC)

Results

Figure 2A presents qualitative evaluations for glioma, multiple sclerosis, and meningioma. For each pathology, the left block displays a non-pathological slice and the right block displays a slice with a visible lesion. From left to right, columns display the input image, reconstruction from the MAE model trained only on normative data, the difference map between the input and the reconstruction, and the calculated E-HAS value. In the non-pathological slices, the reconstructions are visually similar to the inputs, the difference maps remain almost clean, and the E-HAS values stay low (0.1558, 0.1363, and 0.1347, respectively). In the anomalous slices, the original images contain focal lesions highlighted by orange arrows. These lesions disappear in the MAE reconstructions, which instead resemble healthy tissue. As a result, the difference maps show bright high intensity regions that corresponding to the lesion locations, and the corresponding E-HAS values become markedly higher (0.2312, 0.1983, and 0.1906, respectively), creating a clear separation between normal and abnormal slices.

Figure 2B reports the quantitative comparisons. Random mask sampling combined with median aggregation consistently improves AUROC, AUPRC, and NormMCC over the single pass baseline. The gain in AUPRC is particularly important under strong class imbalance, and NormMCC reflects more stable decisions at the chosen threshold. For datasets with large lesions, the proposed E-HAS together with ensemble reconstruction achieves the best performance. On BraTS 2020, AUROC increases from 0.8550 to 0.8776, and on UCSF PDGM from 0.8265 to 0.8446. Datasets with small or heterogeneous lesions also benefit. AUROC on BraTS 2023-MEN rises from 0.7083 to 0.7252, and on the challenging MSLesSeg dataset the method maintains robust stability with a NormMCC of 0.7202. Collectively, these qualitative and quantitative results support that the proposed ensemble reconstruction strategy effectively mitigates mask-induced variability, enabling E-HAS to demonstrate superior and robust generalizability across diverse brain pathologies compared to the single-pass baseline.

Conclusion

This study introduces a self-supervised anomaly detection framework for brain MRI that obviates the need for lesion specific training data. Trained only on lesion free images, the MAE model reconstructs abnormal slices such as tumor images into "pseudo healthy" versions, thereby suppressing pathological features. The resulting discrepancies are directly quantified by the proposed ensemble hybrid anomaly score (E-HAS), which integrates pixel-wise L1 error and SSIM to robustly detect anomalies. In addition, utilizing MAE-based ensemble reconstruction with median aggregation further increases inference stability and mitigates outliers arising from stochastic masking. Experiments across three independent datasets indicate that the method generalizes across diverse disease patterns and acquisition settings. Since the model is trained on multi source data and operates on individual slices with simple spatial augmentations, it is well-suited for clinical workflows where full 3D volumes or accurate registration are often unavailable, offering a promising tool for automated prescreening tool that flags diverse brain pathologies for further expert review.
