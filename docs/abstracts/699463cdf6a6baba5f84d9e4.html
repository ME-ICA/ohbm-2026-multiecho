Introduction

Monitoring brain responses to naturalistic stimuli is a powerful way to understand brain circuit dynamics. Movie-watching paradigms offer rich, ecologically valid stimuli that capture complex temporal patterns of neural activity [1,2] and neuroimaging data collected during such paradigms have been successfully linked to clinical applications [3]. However, current studies have yet to fully decompose brain signals during movie-watching using agnostic, data-driven approaches. We address this gap by decomposing the Dynamic Functional Connectivity (DFC) to define reliable brain states and developing a forecast model of future brain states. Leveraging unsupervised and supervised machine learning methods to identify temporal dependencies.

Methods

We address this by decomposing the DFC and developing a forecast model of future brain states. We analyzed data from the Naturalistic Neuroimaging Database, derived from 86 participants who watched 10 full-length movies (average duration of 117 minutes). DFC was estimated using a sliding window approach, computing connectivity every 5 minutes with overlapping windows. Dimensionality reduction methods were applied to decompose dynamic functional connectivity (dFC), followed by clustering to identify groups of similar connectivity patterns, referred to as brain states. We systematically evaluated multiple dimensionality reduction approaches (autoencoder, PCA, and UMAP), a range of latent dimensionalities (2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, 34, and 55), clustering algorithms (k-means, DBSCAN, and threshold-based clustering), and varying numbers of clusters (2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, 34, and 55). To validate our approach, we applied clustering metrics and trained supervised machine learning models to classify latent components, testing whether latent variables were properly encoded into brain states. Finally, to assess the stability of brain state dynamics, we forecasted the next 5 to 50minutes of brain activity based on previous states, latent components, movie, age, and gender. We employed a tree-based modelling framework jointly with SHAP to enable interpretable forecasting.

Results

Different latent spaces emerged from our methods, each showing distinct predictive strengths for inter-individual versus intra-individual brain state variability. Brain states were most accurately predicted using a threshold-based clustering strategy with 8 clusters defined by the median of a three-dimensional latent space, achieving an F1 score of 70.0% (±1.7%) when forecasting 5-50 minutes into the future. Using k-means clustering, best performances were obtained using 3-5 latent dimensions combined with 5-13 clusters, that yielding a complete encoding of latent variables with high separation between states and low within-state variability, as quantified by Silhouette and Davies–Bouldin indices. This approach achieved a predictive performance of 62.7% (±2.8%) F1. Across both strategies, moving averages of the first three latent variables from the dFC decomposition emerged as the most important predictors at short forecasting horizons. For longer horizons (>40 minutes), contextual and demographic features - movie, age, and gender - became increasingly influential. In contrast, UMAP combined with DBSCAN produced a distinct latent structure that clustered participants instead of brain states, highlighting inter-individual differences rather than within-participant dynamics, with a clustering performance of 99% F1.

Conclusion

These findings highlight that the dFC incorporated into the forecast model captures both group-level and individual-specific brain dynamics. The dynamic functional connectivity framework demonstrated success in predicting brain states across extended periods, validating the presence of reliable temporal patterns. Our framework provides a new benchmark for modelling brain activity under naturalistic stimuli, with important implications for precision neuroimaging, as it enables more accurate, individualized predictions of brain states. In addition, this approach offers the potential to investigate the neural basis of clinical symptoms, their temporal dependence, and variability across individuals, which could inform early detection strategies and the development of personalized interventions.
