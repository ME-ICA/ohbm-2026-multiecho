Introduction

Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS) is a rare and progressive neurodegenerative disorder. It manifests with remarkable heterogeneity both in terms of the site of origin, as well as in the pattern and rate of progression of the disease.1 Traditionally, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has had limited utility in the study of ALS, with studies not finding MRI to be a sensitive or specific indicator or ALS.2 The lack of a definite neuroimaging exam to identify ALS leads to significant delays in its diagnosis. This coupled with the rapidity of disease evolution poses a big challenge in the clinical management of ALS. Accurate early diagnosis and prognosis are critical for effective patient management and their tailored treatment approaches. Recent advances in ultra-high field (7T) MRI have strengthened our ability to peek into the human brain for more detailed investigations, by offering much finer spatial resolutions. This holds promise for improving ALS characterization through cutting-edge neuroimaging approaches.3 It is in this context that this project has been developed, with the acquisition of multimodal imaging at 7T, and their analysis via suitable machine learning (ML) approaches to help reveal MRI-based biomarkers capable of supporting both the clinical diagnosis and prognosis of ALS patients.

Methods

In this study, we carried out a comprehensive feature-level evaluation of MRI-derived biomarkers. Advanced 7T neuroimaging techniques were employed to acquire images from a total of 30 subjects, comprising 16 ALS patients, and 14 healthy controls. Images were acquired across three domains: (1) high-resolution structural imaging via T1-weighted (T1w) images, (2) diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), and (3) 23Na imaging. Nine different parametric maps were extracted using these images, namely: quantitative T1 (qT1) map via T1w, fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity (MD), axial diffusivity (AD) and radial diffusivity (RD) via DTI, and total sodium concentration (TSC), sodium T2*short (T2s) and T2*long (T2l) relaxation times, as well as  the sodium signal fraction (fNa), representing the sodium signal from the short component via the 23Na images. The brain was parcellated, using FreeSurfer, into 332 regions of interest (ROIs) using the Destrieux atlas, including both gray matter (GM) and white matter (WM). For each ROI, we extracted ten statistical descriptors: mean, standard deviation (SD), median, Huber mean (Hmean), the 5th and 95th percentiles (Q05, Q95), the interquartile range (IQR), the number of voxels (numVoxels) within the region, and measures of distribution shape including skewness and kurtosis. These values were used as representative values of the ROIs. The various permutations of modalities, statistical descriptors and ROIs resulted in roughly 30,000 candidate features.

We adopted our previously published ML pipeline for ALS classification that employs the leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) framework for robust performance estimation, and compared against chance-level predictions.4 Based on previously reported outcomes, the classification was undertaken using an L2-regularized multinomial logistic regression model that was found to be appropriate for our classification study. We focused our attention on  three clinically relevant tasks: (1) 2-Class: controls vs patients, (2) 3-Class: controls vs slow ALS progressors vs fast ALS progressors, (3) 2-Class: slow ALS progressors vs fast ALS progressors. Various performance metrics were evaluated, and we finally ranked the features by using the Balanced Accuracy (BA) and Z-score (Z) as the primary and secondary metric, respectively. To rigorously evaluate the discriminative value of individual features while avoiding potential bias, we developed a two-tier evaluation strategy. In Case 1, classical leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) was employed across all 30 subjects provided baseline estimates of single-feature performance. In Case 2, designed specifically to assess subject-level robustness, we excluded one subject at a time from the dataset and performed LOOCV on the remaining 29 subjects, repeating this process for all subjects. This generated 30 independent performance estimates for each feature, thereby enabling evaluation of feature-level stability metrics. This paves the way for identifying the most informative and robust subset of features that can be selected towards multimodal classification, with the expectation of improved outcomes owing to potential synergy between the selected features. The selection is performed on a per fold basis, based on corresponding findings from Case 2 where the same subject was excluded, to ensure no data leakage that might introduce bias. Initial results from the current multimodal investigation indicate better performances than those previously reported via simple agglomeration of all features and ML-based feature selection approaches.

Results

Single-feature classifiers were found to exhibit highly variable performance. For the 2-class task, mean BA was 0.45 (median: 0.43, chance: 0.50), indicating many features performed below chance level. The 3-class task achieved a mean BA of 0.33 (median: 0.31, chance: 0.33), thus largely at chance level. The Slow vs Fast classification showed mean BA of 0.31 (median: 0.38, chance: 0.50). But when restricting our focus to the top performers, we find promise in identifying MRI-based biomarkers. For the 2-class task, the best features achieved a BA of 0.83-0.87 with Z of 1.8-2.6. For the 3-class task, top performers achieved accuracies of 0.58-0.64 (Z: 2.6-5.3). The Slow vs Fast task yielded the most impressive results achieving accuracies up to 0.88-0.94 with Z of 2.8-4.4.

An interesting finding that emerged from the feature overlap analysis was that the biomarkers informative for each task are fundamentally distinct. Zero overlap was observed among the top 100 features across for all three tasks, and only 22 features matched in the top 1000. Cross-task feature consistency evaluation further confirms that a feature good for detecting ALS is not necessarily good for predicting progression (Pearson coefficient of BA = -0.04, Z = -0.02), demonstrating that disease detection and progression prediction rely on different neural signatures and we therefore cannot use a single feature set for all tasks.

Modality-level performance is found to be relatively balanced when considering broader feature sets. This supports their multimodal integration rather than favoring any single modality. All modalities show a monotonic decline in performance as we include increasing numbers of features. By around Top 1000, differences between modalities largely diminish (see Fig. 1A), and by around Top 15000, everything converges close to chance level. As seen in Fig. 1B, the mean ROI intensity value seems to be a consistently important descriptor, while the numVoxels findings suggest that atrophy/volume related changes are relevant, particularly for tracking progression.

The paracentral region (motor leg area), central sulcus, and corticospinal tract-adjacent WM appear prominently, aligning with known ALS pathophysiology.5 For Slow vs Fast classification, CSF/ventricular regions are found important, suggesting atrophy-related ventricular expansion correlates with the disease progression rate. WM ROIs are found most important for diagnosis, while GM regions were found to be more informative towards prognosis, as indicated in Fig. 1C.

When comparing results between Cases 1 and 2, where we applied a dynamic-threshold based stability framework (90th percentile BA + Z in Case 1, ≥50% consistency in Case 2), a central theme across all three tasks is that many single features that appear highly discriminative in Case 1 do not remain robust under subject-level exclusion. A substantial fraction of these features exhibited large variability in balanced accuracy when individual subjects were removed. This was particularly amplified for the Slow vs Fast task, as the total number of subjects here were even more limited (n=16).

Conclusion

Analysis of the global distributions of ~30,000 individual features found most single features to be poor discriminators, with large clusters around the chance level. The 3-Class task was found to be the most difficult to resolve. It showed a narrow distribution clustered at chance level indicating that very few single features could handle this simultaneous detection and stratification challenge. But there exists a tail of informative features that hold potential to be part of neural signatures to address each of our classification tasks. It was also found that disease detection and progression prediction involve fundamentally different neural signatures. Features that distinguish controls from patients are fundamentally different from those that distinguish progression rates, thereby necessitating task-specific feature selection. When moving towards a multimodal classification setting, feature selection should target 50-100 features using composite scoring incorporating both accuracy and reliability measures, with diversity and redundancy focused constraints.

Our systematic feature-level stability analysis provides a rigorous and reproducible framework for identifying reliable unimodal MRI biomarkers. Furthermore, they lead us to effective multimodal investigations by helping select the most informative and robust features. This is especially critical when dealing with extremely high-dimensional feature spaces and limited datasets, where prior results have demonstrated that ML-based feature selection and dimensionality reduction techniques are often inadequate. Our work highlights the value of stability-focused approaches to feature selection, and sets the stage for robust multimodal classification models that are capable of providing both diagnostic and prognostic value in clinical settings for ALS, as well as other rare diseases.
