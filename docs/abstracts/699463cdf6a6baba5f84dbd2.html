Introduction

Visuospatial neglect affects approximately 30% of stroke patients, yet mild forms often escape detection by conventional clinical assessments^1. The NIHSS inattention item, while useful for bedside screening, provides a binary classification and may be confounded by aphasia, hemianopsia, or reduced consciousness ^2. Eye-tracking technology has demonstrated a good sensitivity to neglect ^3, offering objective quantification of oculomotor behaviour that may detect network dysfunction missed by clinical examination. The anatomical substrates of eye movement control—including frontal eye fields, posterior parietal cortex, and insular regions—are frequently affected in middle cerebral artery territory strokes^4, yet the relationship between specific lesion patterns and oculomotor abnormalities remains incompletely characterized.

This case series addresses two questions: (1) Are eye-tracking parameters more sensitive than clinical assessments in detecting visuospatial and attentional deficits after acute ischemic stroke? (2) How do specific neuroanatomical lesion characteristics, including hemispheric lateralization, cortical-subcortical involvement, and regional localization, correlate with quantifiable eye movement abnormalities? 

We hypothesized that eye-tracking would reveal deficits across fixation stability, saccadic metrics, and blink rate even when clinical assessments show negative or non-specific findings, and that these oculomotor patterns would correspond to distinct neuroanatomical substrates ^5. By examining three patients, we demonstrate how eye-tracking complements clinical assessment and provides neuroanatomically grounded insights into attention network dysfunction. We specifically examined dissociations between NIHSS inattention scores at admission and during eye-tracking assessment versus objective eye movement parameters.

Methods

Participants. Three patients were selected from 112 patients enrolled within 2 weeks from stroke onset at Azienda-Ospedale Università degli Studi di Padova. Participants were eligible for inclusion if they were 18 years of age or older and had experienced a first symptomatic stroke of either ischemic or hemorrhagic etiology. All patients were awake, alert, and able to adequately understand and participate in the research procedures and provided informed consent per local ethics approval. 

Clinical Assessment. Patients were administered with neuropsychological assessment, including the Oxford Cognitive Screen (OCS) egocentric neglect subtests, to assess visuospatial function through standardized clinical measures. NIHSS was performed at admission and repeated at the time of neuropsychological evaluation and eye-tracking data collection after 7 days. The NIHSS extinction and inattention item (item 11) specifically evaluates spatial neglect through bilateral simultaneous stimulation (0=no neglect, 1=visual/tactile/auditory inattention, 2=profound hemi-inattention). Clinical records from the hyperacute phase (<24 hours) were reviewed when available. 

MRI Acquisition and Lesion Mapping. The patients were studied with MRI scans which were acquired 6±3.5 days post-stroke. Subacute lesions were segmented from FLAIR and diffusion-weighted imaging sequences. Binary lesion masks (dimensions: 182×218×182) were manually delineated by a board-certified neurologist using ITK-SNAP software and normalized to MNI152 standard space (1mm³ resolution) using SPM12. Lesion location was characterized by: (1) affected hemisphere (right vs. left), (2) tissue type, (3) specific anatomical regions, enabling precise neuroanatomical correlation with oculomotor metrics.

Eye-Tracking Protocol. Participants completed a free-viewing task while seated 60 cm from a calibrated eye tracker (EyeLink 1000 Plus; sampling rate: 1000 Hz). Stimuli consisted of 20 images (1920x1080 pixels) presented for 10 seconds. Images were balanced for visual content across left/right hemifields to avoid stimulus-driven biases. Raw gaze coordinates were recorded continuously throughout each trial.

Eye movements analysis. Nine eye movement parameters were extracted: mean fixation horizontal position and first fixation horizontal position (quantifying lateral spatial bias), mean fixation vertical position and first fixation vertical position (quantifying vertical spatial bias), fixation duration (reflecting attentional engagement), mean saccadic amplitude and mean saccadic peak velocity (measuring oculomotor exploration), mean saccadic duration, and blink rate (indexing arousal regulation). Raw data underwent quality control to ensure adequate capture. Individual patient metrics were z-scored against healthy individuals (n=17, matched for age and education) who completed identical procedures.

Statistical Analysis. Z-scores exceeding ±2 standard deviations were considered clinically significant deviations from normal performance. Eye-tracking abnormalities were systematically compared to: (1) NIHSS extinction/inattention scores at admission and eye-tracking timepoint, (2) OCS egocentric neglect performance, and (3) precise lesion neuroanatomy in MNI space. This approach enabled direct assessment of eye-tracking sensitivity relative to clinical measures and neuroanatomical correlation of oculomotor deficits.

Results

First patient: A 52-year-old accessed the emergency room with altered visual-related functions, facial palsy, reduced left arm strength, dysarthria, and inattention. NIHSS was 6 at admission. MRI showed extensive right temporo-parietal cortico-subcortical lesion with posterior insula involvement (treated with endovascular thrombectomy). Eye-tracking revealed marked leftward deviation in mean fixation (z=-3.25) and first fixation (z=-2.91), indicating severe rightward spatial neglect. Vertical fixation showed downward bias (mean z=-0.80, first z=-1.54). Fixation duration was substantially shortened (z=-2.07), while saccadic amplitude (z=1.06) and velocity (z=0.86) remained preserved. Blink rate was markedly elevated (z=3.57). NIHSS inattention was positive at admission but negative at eye tracking, indicating clinical recovery. Eye-tracking provided quantitative severity grading beyond NIHSS, revealing multiple severe abnormalities (z<-3) and additional deficits in fixation stability and arousal regulation not captured clinically. The posterior temporo-parietal-insular involvement corresponds to classic right hemisphere neglect neuroanatomy.

Second Patient: A 58-year-old man presented with altered consciousness, abnormal gaze deviation, altered visual-related functions, facial palsy, dysarthria, and inattention. NIHSS was 11 at admission. MRI showed right hemisphere ischemic lesion affecting insula and frontal regions secondary to right internal carotid artery dissection (treated with rt-PA and endovascular thrombectomy). At eye-tracking assessment, oculomotor metrics were near-normal: minimal horizontal bias (mean fixation z=-0.48, first fixation z=-0.22), near-normal vertical positioning (mean z=-0.47, first z=0.69), preserved saccadic function (amplitude z=0.86, velocity z=0.22, duration z=0.47), and minimally elevated blink rate (z=0.22). NIHSS inattention was positive at admission but negative at day 7 eye-tracking, indicating clinical recovery. Eye-tracking objectively confirmed complete normalization across all nine oculomotor parameters, demonstrating superior temporal resolution: while NIHSS captured only binary presence-to-absence transition, eye-tracking quantified full recovery of attention network function.

Third Patient: A 66-year-old man presented with altered visual-related functions, facial palsy, mild to moderate aphasia, and dysarthria. NIHSS was 6 (unchanged at eye-tracking). MRI showed extensive left temporo-parieto-occipital cortico-subcortical lesion with posterior insula involvement (no acute treatment). Eye-tracking revealed mild multidimensional abnormalities: rightward horizontal deviation in mean fixation (z=0.33), contrasting with leftward first fixation bias (z=-0.49), suggesting inconsistent spatial orienting. Vertical positioning showed downward bias (mean z=-0.54, first z=-0.45). Saccadic metrics were mildly reduced (amplitude z=-0.39, velocity z=-0.24), and blink rate slightly elevated (z=0.39). NIHSS inattention scored 0 (negative) at both timepoints because hemianopsia likely confounded visual field testing, and aphasia prevented comprehension of bilateral stimulation instructions. OCS egocentric neglect tasks were similarly invalidated (score=-10). The extensive left posterior lesion produced mild rightward spatial bias consistent with literature describing left hemisphere attentional contributions, anatomically heterogeneous and clinically under-recognized compared to right hemisphere neglect.

Neuroanatomical dissociations: Right posterior (temporo-parietal) damage produced severe and persistent neglect with arousal dysregulation. Right anterior (frontal-insular) lesions caused transient deficits with rapid recovery, demonstrating anatomical dissociation between dorsal parietal attention networks and compensatory frontal systems. Left posterior lesions yielded subtle biases masked by confounding clinical deficits, supporting anatomical heterogeneity of left hemisphere attention substrates.

Conclusion

This case series demonstrates that eye-tracking provides good sensitivity to visuospatial and attentional deficits compared to clinical assessments in acute ischemic stroke, with abnormalities corresponding to specific neuroanatomical lesion patterns. We identified three distinct patterns: (1) Eye-tracking revealed persistent deficits when NIHSS showed recovery (First Patient), (2) eye-tracking confirmed complete deficit resolution consistent with clinical assessment (Second Patient), and (3) eye-tracking detected deficits completely missed by NIHSS when confounding impairments invalidated clinical testing (Third Patient).

Regarding sensitivity, eye-tracking revealed abnormalities where clinical assessment was either falsely negative (First Patient with NIHSS=0 despite multiple z<-3 abnormalities) or impossible to perform (Third Patient with hemianopsia and aphasia). Eye-tracking's reduced dependence on language comprehension, visual field integrity, and examiner interpretation enabled deficit detection when clinical measures failed. The method captured persistent attention network dysfunction despite apparent NIHSS recovery, confirmed true normalization when both methods agreed, and quantified multidimensional dysfunction (fixation, saccades, arousal) beyond binary neglect classification.

Regarding neuroanatomical correlations, distinct lesion patterns produced specific oculomotor signatures. Right temporo-parietal-insular lesions caused severe leftward fixation bias (z<-3), shortened fixation duration, and elevated blink rate, corresponding to ventral attention network damage affecting spatial orienting and arousal regulation. Right frontal-insular lesions produced transient deficits with complete recovery by one week, demonstrating frontal system plasticity absent in posterior parietal damage. Left temporo-parieto-occipital lesions yielded mild rightward bias and saccadic slowing, consistent with left hemisphere contributions to spatial attention that are anatomically heterogeneous and less lateralized than classic right hemisphere neglect networks. The cortico-subcortical involvement suggests critical disruption of attention network connectivity beyond purely cortical damage.

Clinical implications are substantial. Eye-tracking represents a valuable assessment tool when traditional testing (NIHSS) is limited by aphasia, hemianopsia, or reduced consciousness, particularly because approximately 30% of neglect cases remain clinically undetected. Eye-tracking provides quantitative metrics for tracking recovery trajectories, informing rehabilitation intensity, and predicting functional outcomes. For left hemisphere strokes, where spatial deficits are mild and anatomically heterogeneous, eye-tracking offers objective quantification unavailable through bedside testing. Hyperacute implementation could identify transient attention network dysfunction predicting subacute disability despite clinical improvement.

Neuroanatomical insights validate right hemisphere dorsal-ventral attention network models, demonstrate anterior-posterior recovery dissociations, and reveal underappreciated left hemisphere attention contributions. The consistent cortico-subcortical involvement suggests that attention network dysfunction requires connectivity disruption beyond purely cortical damage.

Prospective validation in larger cohorts should establish which mismatch patterns predict functional outcomes and recovery trajectories. Serial eye-tracking from hyperacute through chronic phases would characterize temporal evolution of attention network dysfunction and distinguish true recovery from compensation. Standardized protocols with normative databases would enable clinical implementation. Critical questions remain regarding whether eye-tracking-guided rehabilitation produces superior outcomes compared to NIHSS-guided approaches, and whether specific oculomotor biomarkers can predict long-term disability independent of initial clinical severity.
