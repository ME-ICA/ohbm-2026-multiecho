Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a multisystem neurological disorder primarily characterized by hallmark motor symptoms, including bradykinesia, muscular rigidity, resting tremor, and postural instability1,2. Although PD is traditionally considered a paradigmatic movement disorder, it also causes a wide range of non-motor symptoms, encompassing sensory and autonomic dysfunction, affective disorder and cognitive impairments1,2. Dopaminergic replacement therapy (L-DOPA) reliably alleviates motor symptoms; however, its effects on cognitive function, particularly inhibitory control and decision-making, remain poorly understood. Recent studies showed that a considerable subset of patients with PD exhibits impulsivity and impaired decision-making, which are often exacerbated by dopaminergic treatment1. However, the dynamic changes of inhibitory control during the transition from OFF- to ON-medication states have not been well addressed. Moreover, existing studies typically remain descriptive, relying on simple readout and summary measures of behavioral performance, and therefore are unable to mechanistically explain how inhibitory control is impaired in PD. Going beyond descriptive arguments toward an explanatory approach requires formal models to uncover underlying mechanisms at the individual level1,2. This model-based approach in neurological and psychiatric disorders, often referred to as computational psychiatry, has proven valuable in disorders such as PD1. In this project, we aim to provide novel insights into response inhibition impairments in PD by developing a computational cognitive model of decision-making and inhibitory control in a Go/No-Go task in patients with PD, as they transition from the OFF- to the ON-medication state.

Methods

Experimental design and task

Twenty-five patients with PD and 21 healthy controls first completed two task runs (72 trials each) in the pragmatic OFF-medication state (>12 hour withdrawal of antiparkinsonian medication). Subsequently, patients with PD received L-DOPA and completed an additional five runs, resulting in a total of seven runs per patient (see Fig. 1A left panel). Healthy control participants remained OFF medication throughout all seven runs. The current study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the local ethics committee of the Capital Region of Denmark, as part of larger project investigating the cortical and subcortical activity of bradykinesia and dyskinesia in PD (H-22010296). During the pharmacodynamic Go/No-Go task, electroencephalography (EEG) signals were also recorded for all participants (not reported here). Throughout the task, participants responded using a grip-force device to three distinct visual cues presented with equal probability: a left-hand Go cue, a right-hand Go cue, or a No-Go (inhibition) cue (see Fig. 1A middle panel). Based on the response and background (desert or green meadow), participants received a monetary reward or punishment (see Fig. 1A right panel). This additional aspect allowed us to explore the effect of reward context on decision-making and motor responses in patients with PD. The response to options was defined as reaching the 10% of the individual’s maximum grip force for both left and right hands.

Computational Modeling

Decision-making is commonly conceptualized as involving three main stages: sensory encoding, evidence accumulation, and motor execution. While traditional Go/No-Go tasks involve binary button press responses (Go vs. No-Go) with a single hand3, our task introduces three response options per trial: left-hand Go, right-hand Go, and No-Go with continuous force response measures. This triadic design allows the investigation of lateralized decision-making, which is clinically relevant in PD as symptoms are often asymmetric. We developed a new race model called the three-alternative Go/No-Go race model in which sensory evidence accumulates independently for each option (Left-Go, Right-Go, and No-Go) until one accumulator reaches its decision threshold, triggering the corresponding response (see Fig. 1B). The model includes three main latent parameters: 1) drift rate, reflecting the speed of evidence accumulation; 2) non-decision time, representing the combined sensory encoding time and motor execution time; and 3) decision boundary, indicating the amount of accumulated evidence required to initiate a response. In this model design, the likelihood function is analytically intractable; therefore, we employed a deep learning based, simulation-driven inference approach to estimate latent model parameters4, (see Fig. 1C). Bayesian linear mixed-effects models were used in R to examine the effects of group (PD vs. HC) and medication states (run 1 and 2 as OFF, run 3, 4, and 5 as uptake, run 6 and 7 as ON) on reaction time and accuracy, as well as on the latent parameters derived from the model. For the model fit, we estimated five drift rates corresponding to left-hand Go, right-hand Go, No-Go, commission error, and omission error responses; two non-decision time (NDT) parameters for left- and right-hand responses; and two decision boundaries (Go and No-Go), resulting in nine latent parameters. Previous studies indicate that patients with PD often exhibit reduced drift rates, reflecting slower evidence accumulation during decision-making1,2. Also, PD patients may show shorter non-decision times compared with healthy controls1,2.

Movement component

Furthermore, the continuous grip-force recordings allow a precise quantification of response vigor (maximum yank), reaction time (interval between cue onset and force onset), and movement time (interval between force onset and peak force), see Fig. 1D.

Results

Behavioral results are shown in Fig. 2A. Both reaction time and response accuracy are presented dynamically across OFF, uptake, and ON medication states. Overall, patients with PD exhibited faster reaction time than healthy controls in the OFF state, while their responses slowed over time as they transitioned towards the ON state (see Fig. 2A). Also, response accuracy in patients with PD progressively deteriorated with increasing dopaminergic tone. Bayesian statistical analyses revealed a significant interaction effect between group (PD vs. HC) and medication (β = -1.48, 95% CI [-2.05, -0.93]) on reaction time as well as on accuracy (β = 0.04, 95% CI [0.03, 0.05]).  

The latent parameters of the proposed model were precisely recovered (R-squared = 0.9 for each parameter), indicating that parameter estimates derived from the observed data are reliable for the proposed race model (see Fig. 2B). Model results for all nine latent parameters across groups and medication states are shown in Fig. 2C. There was an overall reduction of drift rates for left-hand Go, right-hand Go, and No-Go responses over time in both groups, but this reduction was stronger in patients with PD. Statistically, pooling the three drift rates (left-hand Go, right-hand Go, and No-Go) revealed a significant group × medication interaction (β = -0.68, 95% CI [-1.15, −0.19]). Specifically, the left-hand drift rate showed weak evidence for group × medication interaction (β = −0.69, 95% CI [−1.42, 0.07]). The right-hand drift rate exhibited a significant group × medication interaction (β = −1.00, 95% CI [−1.84, −0.15]), whereas the No-Go drift rate showed no significant interaction effect (β = −0.37, 95% CI [−1.08, 0.33]).  Overall, NDT in patients with PD was shorter compared to healthy controls. Pooling left- and right-hand NDT showed a significant group × medication interaction (β = −0.83, 95% CI [-1.38, -0.30]). Specifically, NDT for the left-hand response showed no significant group difference (β = −0.03, 95% CI [−0.06, 0.01]), while right-hand NDT demonstrated a significant group difference (β = −0.06, 95% CI [−0.09, −0.02]). Boundaries of go responses were higher than those of No-Go response regardless of group (β = 0.62, 95% CI [0.54, 0.72]). For the grip force measures, movement time showed a significant group × medication interaction (β = -0.03, 95% CI [-0.03, -0.02]). Also, maximum yank showed a significant group × medication interaction (β = -0.20, 95% CI [-0.33, -0.06]).

Conclusion

We showed that response inhibition is impaired in patients with PD compared with healthy controls, as evidenced by both behavioral performance and underlying latent mechanisms. These effects are exacerbated over time, which could either be an effect of dopaminergic replacement therapy, an effect of time-on-task (e.g. fatigue) or a combination of both. Patients with PD exhibited higher error rates than controls, exacerbated by increasing dopaminergic levels over time. In addition, patients with PD showed faster reaction times in the OFF-medication state, but their responses slowed over time with increasing dopaminergic tone, approaching those of healthy controls. Information sampling was generally impaired in patients with PD compared with controls, but particularly towards the end of the experiment with increasing dopaminergic tone.  Computational modelling also revealed that all participants, regardless of group, adopted higher decision boundaries in No-Go trials compared with Go trials, reflecting a more conservative decision strategy for No-Go cues.

We corroborated previously reported faster encoding times in patients with PD relative to healthy controls, which have been suggested to reflect a compensatory mechanism1,2. Here, we argue that non-decision time in the model predominantly reflects encoding time rather than motor execution time, as reaction times and initial movement onset were precisely captured using grip-force responses compared to conventional keypress measures. Although this finding may appear counterintuitive given the motor akinesia and mild cognitive impairment typically associated with PD, it aligns well with a previous study1. In contrast, movement time derived from grip-force measures indicated that patients with PD were slower in the motor component of responding compared with healthy controls. Furthermore, patients with PD exhibited reduced response vigor relative to controls.

Overall, this study highlights the benefits of formal computational modelling of behavior for understanding disease mechanisms. We further demonstrated that computational modelling provides greater explanatory power than raw behavioral measures for elucidating latent cognitive mechanisms. These findings, based on individual-level behavioral data and latent parameters, may have important implications for personalized and disease-modifying treatments in PD. Therefore, these preliminary results could provide a foundation for future diagnostic and therapeutic studies.
