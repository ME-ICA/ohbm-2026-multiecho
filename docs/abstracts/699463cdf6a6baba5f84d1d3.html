Introduction

Meta-analytic evidence indicates that transcranial magnetic stimulation of the hippocampal-cortical network enhances episodic memory.1 Specifically, continuous theta-burst stimulation (cTBS) to the right posterior inferior parietal cortex (pIPC) facilitates the formation of new item-location memories (de novo encoding).2 However, real-world demands often require the flexible updating of existing traces. Murine models suggest that the posterior parietal cortex (PPC) governs this process by mediating the interaction between past experience and current sensory inputs.3 Consequently, we hypothesised that pIPC-cTBS delivered following the retrieval of prior traces would induce a reconsolidation inhibition-like mechanism, thereby mitigating proactive interference to facilitate the encoding of new spatial information. We predicted a main effect of stimulation with preferential benefits for updating conditions.

Methods

Thirty-two healthy adults (age 18–35; 22 female) completed a within-subject crossover study (active pIPC v. control S1 stimulation). To establish prior memory traces, participants underwent a 3-day online training protocol before each visit, memorising 128 items: 64 ‘item-only’ (no prior location) and 64 ‘item-location’ (prior location). The active target was defined as the voxel exhibiting maximal resting-state functional connectivity with the right middle hippocampus, constrained within a 15-mm group-level pIPC ROI sphere (MNI: +43, −67, +28), while the control target was the medial primary somatosensory cortex (S1).2 Coil placement was guided using SimNIBS E-field modelling. On the experimental day, participants first performed a ‘reactivation’ assessment (retrieval of existing traces without feedback). Immediately post-assessment, participants received 40s of cTBS (600 pulses; 70% resting motor threshold). Subsequently, participants completed an ‘encoding’ task where all 128 items were assigned new spatial locations. Finally, retention of these newly encoded locations was assessed via a ‘retrieval’ test following a subtraction task. Behavioural metrics – memory success probability (MSP), intrusion probability, and reaction time (RT) – were analysed using ANOVA frameworks. MSP and intrusion probability were estimated via probabilistic mixture modelling relative to target and prior spatial coordinates, accounting for uniform guessing. Stimulation effects (active minus control) were subsequently analysed using multiple linear regression to predict ΔMSP from ΔRT.

Results

Analysis revealed significant Condition × Item History interactions for both MSP (P = 0.024, ηp2 = 0.168) and RT (P = 0.017, ηp2 = 0.187). Despite non-significant post hoc comparisons (Ps > .05), examination of means indicated divergent trends: active cTBS was associated with numerically higher accuracy and faster responses for de novo items. Conversely, this facilitation was absent for updating items, where performance trended towards lower accuracy and slower responses compared to control. Analysis of intrusion probability revealed no significant differences between conditions (P = 0.995). Regression analyses revealed significant correlations between ΔRT and ΔMSP in both item histories (de novo P = 0.003; updating P = 0.039), suggesting a coupling between processing speed and memory success.

Conclusion

Contrary to our hypothesis that pIPC stimulation would mitigate proactive interference via reconsolidation inhibition (‘eraser’ effect), cTBS yielded no benefit for memory updating despite numerical trends favouring de novo encoding. The persistence of intrusion errors suggests that stimulation may not have destabilised prior traces. Instead, our results likely reflect the inverse of the PPC’s critical function in bridging past and present experiences. We propose that the loss of benefit in the updating condition stems from the duality of the stimulation timing: while positioned post-retrieval, cTBS also occurred pre-encoding, likely inhibiting this constructive bridging mechanism. Collectively, these data indicate that item history (de novo v. updating) determines whether pIPC stimulation acts as a facilitator or a disruptor of new spatial memory encoding.
