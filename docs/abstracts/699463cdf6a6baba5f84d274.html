Introduction

Introduction: The brain’s vasculature is critical for sustaining neural function; it also shapes in vivo neuroimaging signals such as BOLD fMRI. The densely packed microvasculature, capillaries, and arterioles is closely tied to sites of neural activation while macrovasculature, larger arteries and veins, supply and drain broader regions. Classical studies using ink injection to visualize brain vasculature note limited penetration into deeper and finer vessels,1 while newer in vivo neuroimaging MRI methods are limited in spatial resolution. To improve vascular visualization, we developed a whole-brain anatomical atlas, combining post-mortem (immuno)histochemistry with high-resolution MRI. With this approach, we captured, quantified, and described the full range of vascular scales in 31 subcortical structures, which could serve as a reference for vascular architecture. 

Methods

Methods: A non-demented donor brain (male, 76y) was obtained through a whole-body donation program (Amsterdam UMC). After 10% formalin immersion-fixation, quantitative MRI maps of longitudinal relaxation rate R1, transverse relaxation rate R2*, and proton density were acquired at 400-μm and 200-250-μm isotropic resolution on a Magnetom 7T scanner (Max Planck Institute for Human Cognitive and Brain Sciences, Leipzig, Germany; Leiden UMC).2,3 The brain was then cryoprotected in sucrose, frozen in TissueTek, and coronally sectioned into 811 slices at 200 μm using a cryomacrotome (-16 °C). Blockface images were captured for MRI alignment and histology registration. Sections were stained alternately with Platelet Endothelial Cell Adhesion Molecule-1 (CD31), Smooth Muscle Actin (SMA), and Bielschowsky Silver to visualize the vascular bed and scanned at 21-μm in-plane resolution. Color deconvolution was performed in Python using scikit-image’s Hematoxylin+DAB matrix, followed by vessel extraction and 3D mapping using Nighres filtering.4 The MASSP 2.0 algorithm was applied to parcellate 31 subcortical structures on the 3D blockface reconstruction.2,5 Labels were projected onto the aligned histology stack to identify anatomical structures. Within each MASSP2.0-defined region, vessel extractions were performed, and densities quantified. Additional microscopic assessments characterized vessel morphology in Smooth Muscle Actin and Platelet Endothelial Cell Adhesion Molecule-1-stained sections.

Results

Results: We developed a comprehensive dataset of vessel densities across all MASSP2.0-defined structures, along with regional vessel morphology and orientation. In general, grey matter regions like the accumbens and thalamus showed denser capillary perfusion than white matter structures, which are dominated by larger vessels (arteries). Platelet Endothelial Cell Adhesion Molecule-1 densities varied greatly across regions, ranging from 33% in the accumbens to 5% in the posterior commissure. Smooth Muscle Actin density percentages varied less, with 16% in the globus pallidus pars externa to 4% in the Cornu ammonis 1. 

Conclusion

Conclusions: The integration of high-resolution post-mortem MRI with (immuno)histochemistry creates a framework that captures the broader anatomical context and fine-scale vascular profiles across (sub)cortical structures, addressing current gaps in vascular mapping. These data will form the basis for future studies aimed to ultimately improve interpretation of BOLD fMRI signals and account for regional vascular variability. The dataset will be made freely available upon publication of the full paper to support its use as a benchmark in future studies.
