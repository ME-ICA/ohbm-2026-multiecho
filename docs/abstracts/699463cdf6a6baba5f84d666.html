Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) has been linked to atypical resting-state functional connectivity (rs-FC), yet robust and generalizable rs-fMRI–based identification remains challenging (Uddin et al., 2013). To facilitate ASD research, the Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange (ABIDE) provides 1,112 rs-fMRI scans from individuals with ASD and age-matched typically developing (TD) controls (Di Martino et al., 2014). Building on prior ABIDE-based machine learning studies (Vidya et al., 2025), we fine-tuned Google’s MedGemma with QLoRA on ABIDE I to develop a resource-efficient ASD classifier for neuroimaging-assisted decision support.

Methods

Dataset

The ABIDE I dataset was used in this study, with data restricted to a single site—New York University (NYU)—to eliminate inter-scanner variability. Subjects were rigorously matched for age and sex, resulting in a final cohort of 158 participants (79 ASD, 79 TD).

Preprocessing

Standard preprocessing pipelines were applied, including realignment, slice timing, coregistration, segmentation, normalization, and smoothing. To ensure signal stability and reduce high-motion artifacts, the first two volumes were discarded from the original 180-volume time series, ensuring head motion remained below 3 mm (within one voxel). The remaining 178 volumes per subject were retained. Following intensity normalization and format conversion, the data were transformed into 2D PNG images suitable for model input. Each subject contributed 178 functional slices, yielding 14,062 images per class and a total of 28,124 images for training and validation.

Data Partitioning and Model Training

To enforce a strict subject-level split and eliminate slice-level data leakage, the dataset was partitioned into 80% training, 10% validation, and 10% testing sets. To minimize computational resource requirements, the 4-bit quantized MedGemma-4b-it model was fine-tuned using QLoRA (rank = 32). Slice-level classification was formulated as a Visual Question Answering (VQA) task. Final subject-level diagnoses were determined using a majority voting strategy: if more than 50% of a subject’s slices were predicted as ASD, the subject was classified as ASD.

Results

The proposed approach achieved robust performance on the held-out NYU test set. As detailed in the confusion matrix (Figure 1A), the model effectively discriminated between groups. Quantitative evaluation yielded a subject-level accuracy of 74% and a weighted F1-score of 0.76 (Table 1). Specifically, the model demonstrated high reliability for the ASD class with a precision of 0.88 and sensitivity (recall) of 0.79. While specificity for the TD class was moderate (0.54), the high precision for the ASD class indicates strong reliability in positive predictions and a minimized false-positive rate for disease identification. Analysis of the training dynamics showed the validation loss converging to approximately 2.2 (Figure 1B). Despite the loss plateau, the model successfully learned a discriminative decision boundary without evidence of overfitting, as confirmed by the balanced predictions in the test set. The strict subject-level split confirms that these results are not attributable to data leakage.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that fine-tuning the MedGemma-4b-it model on the ABIDE NYU dataset achieves 74% subject-level accuracy and approaches 80% sensitivity, highlighting the ability of foundation VLMs to capture complex neurophysiological patterns beyond traditional classifiers. However, to ensure data consistency, the analysis was intentionally restricted to a single site, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader clinical populations. Future work will focus on extending this framework to multi-site data across the full ABIDE dataset to improve robustness and external validity.
