Introduction

Emotions constitute a central component of the affective human experience, shaping cognition, behavior, and social interaction1. Of the contemporary theories of emotion, emerging evidence supports a two-dimensional model proposed by Robinson et al.2 that separates valence (i.e., positivity or negativity of an emotional state) from arousal (i.e., intensity or activation level). To capture time-varying interactions among brain regions encoding affective experience, neuroimaging research has increasingly adopted dynamic functional connectivity (dFC) approaches. A recent study by Ke et al.2 using dFC to investigate neural correlates of emotional arousal and valence during a naturalistic movie-viewing task. They found a generalizable arousal representation characterized by the interactions between multiple large-scale functional networks in cross-sectional data from 51 participants. Here, we build on this work, identifying valence- and arousal-related dynamic coactivation patterns (CAPs) in a precision neuroimaging dataset to better understand how the functional organization of individuals’ brains reconfigure when exposed to naturalistic stimuli. Using a flexible, data-driven approach capable of identifying recurring patterns of instantaneous whole-brain activity (i.e., brain states) across functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) volumes, through the clustering of individual volumes based on their activation profiles4. In the present study, we used CAPs to elucidate the within-individual neural representation of emotional valence and arousal during naturalistic movie-watching. 

Methods

We conducted a CAPs analysis using data from the longitudinal Dense Investigation of Variability in Affect (DIVA) Study5, which included naturalistic film-viewing neuroimaging data from three female participants (“Blossom”, “Bubbles”, and “Buttercup”; age range = 26-31 years) across multiple sessions (66.5min for Blossom, and 91min for Bubbles and Buttercup). The valence and arousal for the film-viewing task was annotated at the TR level. As shown in Fig. 1A, we extracted the normalized BOLD signal time series from 268 ROIs of the Craddock parcellation and concatenated them across all task sessions to generate participant-level matrices (TRs across all sessions x 268 ROIs). Next, we performed k-means clustering on each matrix and used the elbow criterion to determine the optimal solution (k) of CAPs-derived brain states for each participant, which are characterized by unique, yet internally consistent, spatial configurations of co-activated regions. Each CAP was then separated into positively and negatively activated regions to create two weighted spatial masks, which were used to extract the positive and negative CAP time series. To assess the relation between these CAPs and emotion, the emotion ratings were concatenated across runs to create a continuous valence and arousal time series (Fig. 1B)., and correlated separately with the positive and negative CAP time series using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. This approach yielded four correlations per participant (i.e., positive–valence, positive–arousal, negative–valence, and negative–arousal), as shown in Fig. 1C.. Finally, CAPs with statistically significant correlations (p < 0.05) were converted into spatial brain maps and assigned to Yeo networks to identify dominant large-scale systems, and their functional profiles were characterized using Neurosynth decoding.

Results

Using the elbow criterion, we determined the optimal number of CAPs for each participant to be four CAPs for Blossom and Buttercup, and five CAPs for Bubbles. In assessing the relation between these CAPs and emotion, Blossom had significant correlations for both valence and arousal (Fig. 2A), whereas Bubbles exhibited significant associations only for valence (Fig. 2B) and Buttercup, only for arousal (Fig. 2C). Specifically, for Blossom and Bubbles, valence significantly increased with positive activation in CAP 1 and decreased with negative activation in CAP 2. For Blossom, valence also significantly decreased with positive activation in CAP 2. For Blossom and Buttercup, arousal significantly increased with negative activation in CAP 2, and decreased with positive activation in CAP 1. 

All participants had significant correlations for positive or negative activation from CAPs that reflected distinct combinations of large-scale networks and functional domains. For Blossom, CAP 1 had the strongest positive activation in the Control and Dorsal Attention Networks, regions involved in processing  “expression” and “emotion”. CAP 2 had the strongest positive activation in the Visual and Default Mode Networks, with functional decoding revealing terms “error” and “regulation”; and strongest negative activation in Control Network, with functional decoding revealing terms “face” and “emotional”. For Bubbles, CAP 1 had the strongest positive activation in the Limbic and Ventral Attention Networks with functional decoding revealing terms “activity” and “attention”, and CAP 2 had the strongest negative activation in the Default Mode and Dorsal Attention Networks, with functional decoding revealing terms “inhibition” and “response”. For Buttercup, CAP 1 had the strongest positive activation in the Default Mode and Somatomotor Networks, with functional decoding revealing terms “emotional” and “face”, and CAP 2 had the strongest negative activation in the Ventral Attention and Default Mode Networks, with functional decoding revealing terms “attention” and “activity”.

Conclusion

Across all three participants, results showed that significant correlations between CAPs and emotion were functionally involved in emotion processing, attention modulation, and task-relevant control, aligning with the expected dFC fluctuations during naturalistic film-viewing3. Yet, the emotion-related CAPs varied substantially across individuals, with each participant displaying distinct configurations of valence- and arousal-related network engagement. For Blossom, the pattern of significant correlations suggests that their moment-to-moment affective experience was shaped by fluctuations between externally focused attentional control processes and internally directed evaluative and perceptual states. For Bubbles, valence-related CAPs indicate that changes in emotional positivity or negativity were tied to transitions between systems involved in generating emotional responses and those supporting the regulation and monitoring of attention. For Buttercup, arousal-related CAPs reveal that fluctuations in emotional intensity were supported by coordinated shifts between socioemotional processing mechanisms and broader attentional and regulatory processes. Overall, these findings demonstrate that CAPs sensitive to valence and arousal capture meaningful fluctuations in affective experience through flexible patterns of large-scale network engagement and disengagement, rather than isolated network-level responses. In the future, we hope to expand on these findings by applying a dynamic connectome-based predictive model to define generalizable within-individual neural patterns underlying emotion.
