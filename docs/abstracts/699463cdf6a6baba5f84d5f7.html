Introduction

Resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) is increasingly recognized as a promising tool for measuring brain network connectivity and identifying biomarkers for neurological disorders. However, current processing methods often lack the sensitivity and specificity required for clinical applications. To address this, we previously proposed the graph diffusion autoregressive (GDAR) model as a new approach for estimating dynamic functional connectivity from rs-fMRI data.1,2 The GDAR model integrates structural information derived from diffusion tractography with functional imaging data to capture the dynamic signal flow between brain regions. Therefore, the resulting flow signal is fundamentally different from classical functional connectivity based on temporal cross correlations and can provide new insights into brain function and dysfunction. Here we demonstrate how the output of the GDAR model can be decomposed into different spatial and spectral modes to obtain detailed spatio-temporal insights into dynamic functional connectivity patterns of different brain regions and subnetworks. Furthermore, we show how this decomposition can be used to obtain novel functional brain imaging biomarkers of Alzheimer’s disease (AD).

Methods

In the GDAR framework, the spatiotemporal dynamics of neural activity are parametrically modeled on top of a structural brain graph via an autoregressive network diffusion process (Fig. 1a). In contrast to static, undirected functional connectivity measures, our model produces a time-varying directed communication signal on the graph edges, referred to as GDAR flow. Using recently developed methods for signal processing over networks,3,4 the structural connectivity graph underlying the GDAR model can be use to obtain a set of gradient (directional) and curl (rotational) flow basis function with varying degree of spatial smoothness that characterize brain-wide coherent (low-frequency) and chaotic (high-frequency) communication patterns (Fig. 1b and c). The GDAR flow can be projected onto these basis functions to obtain a spatial flow Fourier decomposition akin to the classical Fourier analysis for time series (Fig. 1d). A limitation of this decomposition is that the resulting flow basis functions are generally not localized to individual brain subnetworks. To address this, we designed a subnetwork-specific decomposition of the GDAR flow based on topological Slepians (Fig. 1e-g).5 The resulting spatially and graph-spectrally constrained modes therefore incorporate anatomical knowledge into the analysis of functional brain networks and can provide novel and easily interpretable biomarkers for various neurological disorders. We have previously validated the GDAR model on 23 fMRI sessions form 9 young healthy subjects using one step ahead prediction errors and test-retest correlations. Here we apply the GDAR model and our new post-processing techniques to fMRI data from 15 old-cognitively normal and 15 age matched Alzheimer’s disease (AD) subjects (ages 63-93) to assess changes in GDAR flow, as well as brain-wide and subnetwork-specific communication patterns due to aging and AD (Fig. 2).

Results

We found that in the young healthy group, the GDAR model outperforms alternative autoregressive models and accurately predicts future BOLD activity (average normalized root mean square error of 0.127 for the best model order) while generalizing very well to unseen data. Critically, we also found that the average GDAR flow is not correlated with classical correlation-based functional connectivity estimates demonstrating that the estimated communication signal is fundamentally different from temporal correlation signals. Finally, both the GDAR flow and its derived brain-wide and subnetwork-specific spatial spectra exhibit high within- and across session stability at the subject level, again outperforming correlation-based functional connectivity estimates. 

Comparisons between fMRI data from young and old healthy subjects reveal a significant increase in GDAR flow in older subjects across the entire brain, reflecting the model's ability to capture physiological changes in the brain associated with aging (Fig. 2a). Differences between old healthy and AD subjects on the other hand are confined to a few edges within the network (Fig. 2b) and brain-wide spatial spectra show very comparable magnitudes and shapes between both groups (Fig. 2c and d). However, when using the subnetwork-specific decomposition of the GDAR flow into different spectral modes based on topological Slepians we found a significant increase in gradient and curl flow in several subnetworks and spatial frequency bands (Fig. 2e). Particularly higher-frequency modes in the Basal Ganglia allow for good subject-level separation between the two groups (Fig. 2f). Critically, we were not able to replicate a similar subject level separation using classical correlation based functional connectivity estimates emphasizing the utility of our approach.

Conclusion

We propose a novel method for estimating and analyzing dynamic brain network connectivity that, unlike most functional connectivity approaches, naturally combines structural and functional brain imaging data. Our model captures the dynamics of rs-fMRI signals and, critically, demonstrates superior sensitivity and reliability in analyzing brain networks at the single-subject level, where traditional temporal correlation-based methods often fall short. Furthermore, the decomposition of the network flow signal into different brain-wide and subnetwork-specific modes offers a powerful new way of identifying interpretable network biomarkers for neurological disorders.
