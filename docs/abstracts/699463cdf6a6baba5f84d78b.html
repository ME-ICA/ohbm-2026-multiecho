Introduction

Neural wiring diagrams offer critical insights into brain structure and function across species. Robustness is a key network property that quantifies its fortitude in the face of different types of disruption or harm. A recent comparative study of functional neural patterns in macaque and human brains proposed a tradeoff between robustness and efficiency in neural coding across species, with increased robustness in macaques compared to humans (Pryluk et al. 2019). Here, we test this functional hypothesis on structural data and compare the robustness of neural wiring using connectomes of macaques (Assaf et al. 2020, Suarez et al. 2022) and human subjects (Shamir et al. 2022) reconstructed from diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans. We apply a network analysis approach (Rubinov and Sporns 2010) to structural robustness by iteratively deconstructing the connectomes, eliminating network nodes and edges, representing cortical gray and white matter respectively, and measuring the resulting impact on network connectedness and efficiency. Across all tests, macaque brains showed higher structural robustness than human brains, reinforcing the link between wiring architecture and coding stability across primate species. Our results support a revised tradeoff in neural wiring that entails a balance between robustness and plasticity. While robustness represents the fortitude of the brain in the face of harm or disease, plasticity represents its malleability and capacity for change and learning. This proposed tradeoff is further established by previous evidence indicating that alongside increased resilience to neurological disorders, macaques also exhibit lower levels of neuroplasticity than humans. This study illustrates the benefits of cross-pollination between studies of neural wiring and neural coding for a better understanding of the interplay between fundamental principles of brain structure and function.

Methods

(N=16) structural connectomes of primate species were selected from exsiting datasets, including:

  1. (N=8) macaque connectomes (Assaf et al. 2020, Suarez et al. 2022, analyzed datasets available at: https://zenodo.org/records/7143143) 
  2. (N=8) human connectomes (Shamir et al. 2022, analyzed datasets available at: https://github.com/ittais/Laminar_Connectivity). 

The analysis of the structural connectomes was conducted using the Brain Connectivity Toolbox (BCT), a toolbox for complex network analysis and visualization of brain networks (Rubinov and Sporns 2010, freely available at: https://sites.google.com/site/bctnet/). To examine structural network robustness in a direct and quantifiable manner, we conducted four different percolation tests, involving iterative deconstruction of the network components step-by-step and measuring the resulting effect on network efficiency and connectedness.

Results

Results for all four tests are plotted and compared for both the macaque and human brains (Fig. 1 A1-D1) and areas under the curves were calculated and normalized for each test of each brain and summarized in violin plots (Fig. 1 A2-D2). Results across all four tests and across both pairs of parcellations show that the macaque structural networks are more gradually and less severely affected by the iterative deconstruction process, relative to the human brains. 

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates the value of using complex network analysis to compare human and macaque brains using not only global network measures, but also their behavior when networks are gradually and methodically deconstructed. We show that network efficiency is a more nuanced and complex measure when it comes to structural interspecies comparisons. Structural efficiency relates more to the definition of the shortest path in the network, describing an inverse measure of the average shortest path. In other words, unlike functional efficiency, which measures network dynamics more directly using a set of measures (such as spectral entropy and Laplacian eigenvalues), structural efficiency is more removed from the classic definition of efficiency. Nevertheless, our results within species reiterate a rightward asymmetry that manifests in higher efficiency of the right hemisphere of both macaque and human brains. This pattern repeats for both weighted and binary efficiency measures, and across different cortical parcellations. When it comes to structural robustness, the elevated structural robustness of macaque brains compared to humans repeats across all robustness tests. Our results establish that the higher robustness previously observed in neural coding (Pryluk et al. 2019) is also evident in the neural wiring underlying it. This finding could further explain why macaques may be less inclined to psychopathologies than us humans. 

The findings presented here offer a possible modification to the robustness-efficiency tradeoff previously demonstrated in neural coding (Pryluk et al. 2019). This modified version for neural wiring involves a possible tradeoff between robustness and plasticity. While robustness represents the fortitude of the brain in the face of harm or disease, plasticity represents its malleability and capacity for change and learning. This tradeoff is further established by previous studies indicating humans may have a higher capacity for neuroplasticity than macaques. Such studies have shown an increased and more widespread experience-dependent reorganization during learning and after injury in humans compared to macaques, a longer ‘plasticity window’ during childhood and adolescence in humans compared to macaques, longer dendritic spines, greater dendritic complexity and dendritic turnover in humans compared to macaques, and even later maturation and increased flexibility of the prefrontal cortex in humans compared to macaques.
