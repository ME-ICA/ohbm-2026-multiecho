Introduction

Fear and anxiety are complex biopsychological phenomena that help organisms prepare for immediate or potential threats. Both involve a wide range of finely tuned cognitive and emotional processes that interact in complex ways and can be categorized as threat-specific, such as assessing whether a stimulus is dangerous (threat appraisal), or as valence-unspecific, including the regulation of attentional resources that also occurs in non-threatening situations. 

Numerous functional magnetic resonance (fMRI) studies have shown strong task-related brain network activity in different types of threat processing1–3, but distinguishing whether neural activity reflects true threat-specific processing or general attentional engagement remains difficult due to challenges in disentangling the complex interplay among functional network components and the lack of control conditions in many experimental designs. To partially address this problem, we here introduce a dynamic threat-monitoring task that includes closely matched threatening and non-threatening conditions. Our findings indicate that neural activity, as measured with fMRI in a dynamic threat task, mainly reflects attention-related processing embedded within autonomic activity. Importantly, regions such as the anterior insula (aIns) and the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNST)—of which fMRI activity has been consistently linked to processing dynamic and sustained threats—were also characterized by nonspecific activity, being activated in both threatening and non-threatening experimental conditions.

Methods

The experimental procedure involved two 10-minute runs (T: Threat; NT: No Threat) and was preceded by a resting-state fMRI scan. In both runs, participants were asked to monitor a curve that fluctuates between 0 and 10 or 0 and -10 (Figure 1). During the T run, whenever the curve crossed a predefined threshold value (9 or -9), a previously calibrated painful electric stimulus was “collected”. Collected electric stimuli were administered at half intensity after the T run. No electric stimuli were expected after the NT run. In both conditions, participants were asked to count the number of threshold exceedances to maintain a basic level of attention.

Forty-three participants were recruited for the study. fMRI data were collected throughout the curve presentation, and anxiety ratings were obtained at the end of both the T- and NT-condition. The number of skin conductance responses (nSCR) was assessed during both runs as an index of autonomic activity. fMRI data were acquired with a 3 Tesla MRI scanner and preprocessed with standard procedures optimized for analyzing activity induced by dynamic stimuli and patterns of subcortical activation.

For the statistical analysis, the curve trajectory was divided into eight different phases based on the threat condition (T/ NT), whether the curve was proximal (prox) or distal (dist) to the threshold, and whether the curve was approaching (appr) or retreating from (retr) the threshold (state-dependent analysis). In the fMRI data analysis, the Tprox/appr, Tprox/retr, Tdist/appr, Tdist/retr, NTprox/appr, NTprox/retr, NTdist/appr, and NTdist/retr phases were analyzed using a repeated-measures flexible factorial design. Similarly, repeated measures ANOVAs and paired t-tests were used to examine differences in nSCR and anxiety ratings. The study served as a conceptual replication of a prior study and was preregistered on the Open Science Framework (10.17605/OSF.IO/3SFZ7). In addition to the study-specific analyses, the datasets from the two studies were combined for a pooled fMRI analysis.

Results

Anxiety ratings and nSCRs were significantly higher in the T condition compared to the NT condition (anxiety ratings: t(42)=8.02, p<0.001, nSCR: t(37)=4.15, p<0.001). nSCRs in the NT condition did not significantly differ from those recorded during the rsfMRI scan, indicating that the NT condition did not substantially increase autonomic arousal. Aside from the main effect of threat, the analysis of state-dependent SCRs revealed no additional threat-specific effects. However, when testing for non-specific effects, we observed more SCRs during approach phases than during retreat phases (F(1,38)=13.00, p=0.001).

The fMRI effects induced by the curve were characterized primarily by a strong non-specific prox*dir interaction, indicating a signal increase when the curve was close to the threshold and moving toward it, independent of the threat level ([[Tprox/appr - Tprox/retr] + [NTprox/appr – NTprox/retr]] > [[Tdist/appr – Tdist/retr] + [NTdist/appr – NTdist/retr]]). No threat-specific state-dependent effects were observed, except for a threat*prox*dir interaction in the periaqueductal grey (PAG) when both datasets were combined ([Tprox/appr - Tprox/retr] - [Tdist/appr - Tdist/retr] > [NTprox/appr - NTprox/retr] - [NTdist/appr - NTdist/retr]). Consistent with our hypothesis, we replicated significant non-specific prox*dir interactions in the anterior insula (aIns) and also in the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNST), suggesting that signal variation in these areas can be explained by attention regulation rather than threat-specific responding (aIns: t=4.58, p=0.004, cluster size: 50 voxels; BNST: t=3.57, p=0.004, cluster size: 10 voxels). The non-specific effects in the aIns and BNST were confirmed by a conjunction analysis of the pooled dataset showing significant prox*dir interactions in both the T and NT conditions (aIns: t=4.85, p<0.001, cluster size: 71 voxels; BNST: t=3.3, p=0.009, cluster size: 4 voxels) and also after controlling for autonomic SCR-associated activity (aIns: t=6.72, p<0.001, cluster size: 159 voxels; BNST: t=5.09, p<0.001, cluster size: 9 voxels).

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that dynamic threat-processing primarily causes variations in the fMRI signal, which can be linked to stimulus-dependent attention and autonomic activity. Consistent with recent research, we conclude that even for brain regions heavily discussed in threat-specific processing, interpretations of the signals should be made cautiously when appropriate control conditions are missing.
