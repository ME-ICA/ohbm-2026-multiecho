Introduction

Recent advances in neuroimaging have yielded high-resolution spatial maps of diverse brain properties, from structural and functional architecture to genetic and neurotransmitter profiles. Quantifying associations between these maps is essential for understanding the links between brain phenotypes. However, brain maps exhibit strong spatial autocorrelation (SA), where spatially proximal observations are similar. SA violates independence assumptions, reduces effective degrees of freedom, and inflates false-positive rates (FPR).

State-of-the-art methods (spin tests, BrainSMASH, and eigenstrapping) aim to control SA-induced FPR inflation by generating autocorrelated surrogate maps.^1-3 Each has notable limitations, including high computational cost of surrogate generation, difficulty in handling missing data on the medial wall, and limited generalizability across spatial geometries that hinders applications in region-of-interest and volumetric studies.

Recent work further shows that these methods can inadequately control type-I and type-II errors under spatially nonstationary (heterogenous) SA.^4 When two maps share aligned (or inversely aligned) nonstationary SA patterns, the null distribution of correlation coefficient widens (or narrows), increasing false positives (or negatives). Similar effects arise when data variance is spatially nonuniform. Thus, rigorous inference requires accounting for spatial nonstationarity.

Here, we introduce a fast, surrogate-free approach to assess the significance of brain map associations through effective sample size estimation (ESSE). ESSE accommodates missing data, generalizes across spatial geometries, and account for spatial nonstationarity. Simulations show that ESSE improves specificity and sensitivity under nonstationary SA and variance. We demonstrate evidence that ESSE yields more reliable inference in empirical brain map associations compared to established benchmarks.

Methods

ESSE evaluates the significance of associations between brain maps in two steps. First, it quantifies spatial dependence within each autocorrelated map by estimating its covariance structure (Fig 1A). Empirical variograms are used to characterize semivariance γ as a function of spatial separation h (lag distance).^2 Parametric stable variogram models (Eq 1) are then fitted to obtain a valid, continuous representation of distance-dependent semivariance, which is evaluated at all pairwise distances to construct covariance matrices.

Eq1: γ(h;σ2,φ,α,ϵ)=ϵ+σ2(1-exp{-(h/φ)α}), with σ2 (sill), φ (range), α (shape), and ϵ (nugget) being parameters fitted.

Second, ESSE estimates the effective sample size and derives statistical significance. Effective sample size (Nef) and effective degrees of freedom (effective dof, Nef - 2) are computed from covariance matrices of the two maps (Cx and Cy) using Dutilleul’s derivation (Eq 2), after which test statistics are referenced to their theoretical distributions parameterized by the inferred effective dof (Fig 1B).

Eq 2: Nef = tr(BCxBCy)/(tr(BCx)tr(BCy)) + 1, where B=N-1(IN – N-1JN), and IN and JN denote the N-by-N identity matrix and the N-by-N matrix of ones, respectively.^5

To accommodate spatial nonstationarity in SA and variance, we implement stationary and nonstationary variants of ESSE that differ in the construction of covariance matrices. The stationary ESSE (sESSE, Fig 1A) assumes homogeneity in spatial dependency and variance, estimating and fitting a global variogram using all data points and construct a stationary covariance matrix (CS, Eq 3). In contrast, the nonstationary ESSE (nESSE, Fig 1C) assumes local stationarity and partitions data into random, nonoverlapping parcels. Stationary variograms are estimated and fitted within each parcel, which are subsequently integrated into a global nonstationary covariance function using process convolution (CNS, Eq 4). nESSE thus accounts for spatially varying autocorrelation and variance when inferring effective dof.

Eq 3: CS(i,j) = ϵ+σ2-γ(hij) if i≠j else ϵ+σ2

Eq 4: CNS(i,j) = |∑i|1/4|∑j|1/4|(∑i+∑j)/2|-1/2CS(√Qij), where ∑i and ∑j are the covariance matrices of the Gaussian kernel centered at point i and j, respectively. Qij=(si-sj)T((∑i+∑j)/2)-1(si-sj), in which si represents the spatial coordinate of point i.^6

We evaluate the performance of sESSE and nESSE using simulations and empirical data, benchmarked to spin test (st) and eigenstrapping (50 and 1k modes, es50 and es1000) wherever applicable. For stationary settings, we generate pairs of maps on a spherical mesh (fsaverage 10k vertices) with varying strengths of SA (l=0-50, 10k pairs per l) to evaluate FPR (Fig 1D). To assess performance under nonstationary SA and variance, we simulate spherical maps with hemispheric heterogeneity and evaluate FPR for aligned and inversely aligned nonstationarity patterns (Fig 1E, 1F). We further generate data on a quarter-spherical mesh to simulate missing values and region-of-interest analysis (Fig 1G), as well as data on a cubic lattice to simulate volumetric studies (Fig 1H). Finally, we evaluate associations between detrended empirical brain maps (pairwise association between gene expression PC1, Neurosynth, T1/T2 ratio, cortical thickness, and functional connectivity gradient PC1) using our methods and benchmarks.

Results

When evaluating stationary maps, both sESSE and nESSE perform comparably to existing benchmarks, maintaining FPR near the significance level of 0.05, despite being mildly overconservative under the strong autocorrelations examined (Fig 2A). For nonstationary settings in which SA is aligned across maps, nESSE and eigenstrapping with 50 modes (es50) provide superior FPR control relative to other methods (Fig 2B). However, the improved FPR of es50 is accompanied by elevated false negatives when SA is inversely aligned, while nESSE increases sensitivity under this inversely aligned condition (Fig 2C). Furthermore, nESSE is the only method that improves specificity under aligned nonstationary variance (Fig 2D) and sensitivity under inversely aligned nonstationary variance (Fig 2E). These findings indicate that nESSE offers superior control of type-I and type-II error over its alternatives in the presence of spatial nonstationarity.

For simulations on the quarter-spheres and cubic lattices, we report results for ESSE alone because benchmarks cannot be easily adapted across different spatial geometries. We found both sESSE and nESSE maintain appropriate FPR control in all cases (Fig 2F, 2G), suggesting their utility in the presence of missing values, as well as flexibility in region-of-interest and volumetric studies.

Finally, when assessing associations between empirical brain maps, ESSE yields findings broadly consistent with benchmark methods. A notable exception arises for the association between gene expression PC1 and cortical thickness maps: spin test, eigenstrapping, and sESSE identify a significant association, whereas nESSE does not (Fig 2H). Post-hoc analyses reveal that the two maps exhibit positively correlated (aligned) spatial autocorrelation, measured by Moran’s I (Fig 2I). This result highlights nESSE’s ability to suppress nonstationarity-induced inflation of false-positive findings in empirical analyses.

Conclusion

We introduce ESSE to access the significance of associations between autocorrelated brain maps. ESSE is fast because it is surrogate-free, evaluating maps of a thousand points in a second and maps of ten thousand points in a minute (estimated using MATLAB). It effectively works in the presence of missing values and adapts to region-of-interest and volumetric studies. Simulation and empirical evidence suggest that ESSE improves specificity and sensitivity when data are spatially nonstationary. Collectively, ESSE provides more reliable inference on brain map associations relative to established state-of-the-arts.
