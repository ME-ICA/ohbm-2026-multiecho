Introduction

The insula is a key node of the salience network (SN) which detects and processes salient events and allocates neural resources accordingly. Its structure and function have been found to differ significantly between the anterior and posterior insula. The posterior insula (PI) receives ascending sensory inputs via the thalamus and is involved in the initial processing of low-level interoceptive stimuli, which are then passed to the anterior insula (AI).1 Once at the AI, the salient sensation is integrated with past experiences through emotional, memory and motivational systems where subjective aspects of the stimuli will also be encoded.1 Based on the perceived salience, the AI, together with the anterior cingulate cortex, redirects attention and engages cognitive control processes for top-down behavioural regulation through their integration with higher-order cognitive regions.

Atopic eczema (AE), a chronic inflammatory skin condition, is characterised by persistent itch, particularly at night, which severely impacts sleep quality. Prolonged pathological somatosensory experiences, such as itch and pain, could establish a feedforward loop which progressively alters insula function and connectivity. Previous studies have reported structural and functional alterations of the insula in chronic pain conditions. Of note, itch processing engages similar SN circuitry as pain processing; however, a key difference is that itch is coupled with the itch-relieving scratch behaviour. The insula likely has a key role as it not only detects itch as a salient interoceptive signal but also coordinates the scratch response, integrating sensory input with motor planning and execution. Despite extensive neuroimaging research on evoked itch and subjective perception, the neural correlates of objective scratch behaviour remain largely unexplored. In this study, we examined whether nocturnal scratch measures correlated with resting-state insula connectivity in adolescent AE patients.

Methods

19 AE adolescent patients with a range of disease severity (median age: 14, range: 12-17 years) and 9 healthy controls (median age: 14, range: 12-16 years) were recruited for the study. Participants wore the GENEActiv actigraphy watch on their non-dominant wrist for 2-12 consecutive nights. Raw 100 Hz triaxial accelerometer data recorded from the actigraphy watch were segmented into individual nights and preprocessed with a 0.2 Hz high-pass filter to remove low-frequency drift (Figure 1A).2 Video recordings of participants’ while asleep guided by visual inspection of raw accelerometer signal plots were used to manually label scratch events. The Python package ‘tsfresh’ was used for feature extraction and selection of the windowed triaxial data. Selected features from 11 nights of data collected across 9 participants were used to train a fully connected neural network model (Figure 1B), with a 5-fold stratified group cross-validation. The model was then employed to predict scratch events on the remaining nights and calculate scratch parameters, such as average movement frequency per scratch episode and total scratch duration for each participant. All scratch parameters were averaged across multiple nights and tested for statistical significance between AE and control groups with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. For AE patients, the scratch parameters were also correlated with Eczema Area and Severity Index (EASI) scores.

High-resolution structural MRI scans were acquired with the 3D T1-weighted Magnetization-Prepared Rapid Gradient Echo (MPRAGE) sequence. Blood Oxygen Level Dependent (BOLD)-contrast resting fMRI scans were acquired from the participants with a multiband gradient echo EPI sequence (multiband acceleration factor = 4, TR = 933ms, TE = 32ms, FOV = 221 x 221 mm, resolution = 2.7mm isotropic, flip angle = 60°; 525 volumes). All MRI data were scanned on a 3.0 Tesla GE SIGNA Premier Scanner at the Centre for Neuroimaging Sciences, King’s College London.

fMRI scans were preprocessed using fMRIprep (version 23.2.1) which includes motion correction, susceptibility distortion correction, slice timing correction and registration of fMRI scans to their respective T1 MPRAGE scans and MNI space.3 White matter, cerebrospinal fluid, as well as 24 motion-related confounds, were regressed from the processed scans using Nilearn. The fMRI data were then further preprocessed with FSL (version 6.0.7.6): i) removal of the first 3 volumes, ii) spatial smoothing (5mm FWHM) and iii) high-pass temporal filtering with a threshold of 150s. Single-subject ICA was then carried out using FSL’s MELODIC and components classified as related to physiological noise, motion artefact, or multiband effects were regressed from the data.

Seed-to-whole brain correlation analyses with anterior and posterior insula from each hemisphere as seeds were then performed using masks for the anterior and posterior insula derived from the Hammersmith maximum probability atlas (Figure 1C).4 The randomise function in FSL was then used to associate insula connectivity with average movement frequency per scratch episode in all participants while controlling for age, gender and group effects.

Results

Across 5-fold cross-validation, the scratch prediction model successfully identified 1115 out of 1399 scratch events, indicating moderate-to-high accuracy (precision of 0.70, recall of 0.80, and an F1-score of 0.74 for scratch events). The model was then used to predict scratch events on remaining nights. As expected, AE patients spent significantly longer duration and a higher proportion of time scratching per night (Figure 2A). AE patients also had significantly longer scratch episodes and greater average movement frequency within each episode compared to healthy controls. All scratch parameters also positively correlated with EASI score (Figure 2B).

Next, we tested the functional connectivity of left AI and PI as well as right AI and PI to the whole brain. No differences in functional connectivity were observed for each of the insula subregions between AE patients and healthy controls. Interestingly, we found that only left AI connectivity with multiple regions known to be involved in itch and scratch processing in both hemispheres (for example, somatosensory cortex, motor cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, thalamus, caudate, putamen and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC)) was significantly associated with average movement frequency per scratch episode across all participants (Figure 2C). Next, we tested whether left AI’s associations with scratch behaviour could be explained by generally stronger intrinsic connectivity with the whole brain compared to right AI. We observed that both left and right AI showed stronger intrahemispheric than interhemispheric connectivity in all participants (Figure 2D).

Conclusion

Here, we showed that participants who scratch more vigorously at night have increased resting-state connectivity between the left AI and itch-associated regions. With the development of wearable technologies and machine learning, we were able to objectively measure scratch movements and correlate behaviour with neural features. However, as actigraphy was recorded from one wrist, our scratch measures capture relative scratch burden rather than absolute bilateral scratch activity. The average movement frequency per scratch episode was used to correlate with insula connectivity because it is more robust to any missed scratch events on the non-monitored hand.

The enhanced left AI-DLPFC connectivity could suggest increasing attention towards itch stimuli. As a result, the SN in these patients might have increased sensitivity towards itch sensation as well as enhanced coupling with somatosensory and motor systems where a small itch stimulus could trigger an intense scratch episode. Moreover, stronger left AI connectivity with the right hippocampus suggests that temporary relief from scratching is encoded in memory systems, potentially creating reward-based reinforcement. This may further promote habit formation, leading to a vicious cycle where itch automatically triggers scratching with weaker conscious control.

In particular, our finding that increased left AI connectivity with itch-scratch associated regions specifically correlates with scratch behaviour is noteworthy. Limited work on the lateralisation of insula has proposed that the right AI is more involved in bottom-up processing such as interoception and redirecting attention to salient stimuli whereas the left AI is associated with top-down regulation of behaviour including fine motor control.5 This hemispheric specialisation aligns with our findings where itch-scratch coupling may preferentially engage the left AI for sensorimotor integration, which differs from withdrawal reflexes or passive suffering in pain. Our findings put forward the left AI as a critical integration hub in the SN that processes salient stimuli and drives downstream repetitive motor actions, which might have broader implications in other conditions where persistent sensations drive motor behaviours, for example, tactile/visual stimuli in skin picking disorder.

Collectively, chronic itch and scratching can have lasting neuroplastic effects on itch-scratch circuitry, potentially explaining why scratching becomes increasingly habitual in chronic itch conditions. Given the potential specific role of left AI in coordinating itch signals and scratching behaviour, left AI could serve as a target for neurofeedback or cognitive behavioural interventions to retrain this circuitry and break the vicious itch-scratch cycle.
