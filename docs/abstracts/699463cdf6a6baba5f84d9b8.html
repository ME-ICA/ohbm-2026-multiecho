Introduction

Alexithymia, characterized by difficulties in identifying (DDF), describing (DIF) feelings and externally oriented thinking (EOT), affects 10-13% of the general population and is associated with altered emotional processing. The Toronto Alexithymia Scale-20, measures three distinct dimensions that may engage different neural systems: DDF relates to verbal-emotional processing deficits, DIF involves interoceptive and emotional awareness impairments, and EOT reflects cognitive style preferences.^1. Understanding how these dimensions differentially correlate with brain activity during emotional processing, could reveal distinct neural correlates and inform targeted interventions. Gaze cueing paradigms offer a powerful tool to examine the intersection of social attention and emotional processing, as gaze direction automatically orients attention and facial expressions modulate this effect. Previous studies showed that alexithymia is associated with altered activation in emotion processing regions including the temporal fusiform cortex, parahippocampal gyrus, and anterior cingulate cortex.^3,^4,^5. However, most studies have treated alexithymia as a unitary construct or focused on total scores, potentially obscuring dimension-specific neural signatures. This study investigated the neural correlates of TAS-20 dimensions during an emotional gaze cueing task using fMRI.

Methods

Participants: A total of 31 right-handed (17 females, 14 males), adults volunteers completed the study. Exclusion criteria included neurological or psychiatric disorders, current psychotropic medication use, and standard MRI contraindications. 

Alexithymia Assessment: The Spanish version of 20-item Toronto Alexithymia Scale (TAS-20).^1, was administered to assess three dimensions: Difficulty Identifying Feelings (DIF), Difficulty Describing Feelings (DDF), and Externally Oriented Thinking (EOT). Scores were treated as continuous variables in correlation analyses.

Experimental Design: Participants completed an emotional gaze cueing task (adapted from Galfano et al. 2011. ^2), during fMRI scanning. The task employed a 2×3 factorial design with factors of trial congruency (congruent vs. incongruent) and emotional face (happy, sad, or neutral expression). Each trial began with a central fixation cross (100ms), followed by a face stimulus with direct gaze. After 1000ms, gaze was adverted on left or on right during 200ms. Subsequently, a target (*) appeared in the left or right peripheral location (until response or 1500ms maximum). Participants were instructed to indicate, as soon as possible, identified the target via button press (see Figure 1). 

fMRI Acquisition: Whole-brain fMRI data were acquired using a 3T GE scanner (TR=2s, TE= 40ms, FOV=25.6cm, matrix 64x64, slice thickness = 4mm, slices=34, volumes= 260). 

fMRI Preprocessing: Preprocessing included: motion correction MCFLIRT, slice-timing correction, spatial smoothing, high-pass temporal filtering, and registration to MNI152 standard space.  Each participant’s data were motion and slice timing corrected, and normalized onto MNI152 standard space. Data were smoothed using a Gaussian filter (full width half maximum = 6 mm) and high-pass filtered during analysis. Data were analyzed using FSL 6.

Statistical Analysis: First-level GLM analysis modeled the six experimental conditions as separate regressors. Contrast images were created for main effects of congruency (congruent and incongruent), and emotion-specific contrasts (HC-NC, HI-NI, SC-NC, SI-NI, etc.). Second and third-level correlation analyses examined relationships between TAS-20 scores (DDF, DIF, EOT, TOT) and BOLD signal across all voxels. Cluster-based thresholding was applied (Z>2.3, p<0.05). Anatomical localization was performed using the Harvard-Oxford cortical and subcortical atlases. Data were analyzed using FSL 6.

Results

Behavioral Data: People with more difficulty identifying feelings and a more externally oriented thinking style tend to show longer RTs (especially in the emotional incongruent/congruent conditions HI and SC).

Imaging Data: Difficulty Describing Feelings (DDF): During congruent trials, DDF showed negative correlations with activation in temporal fusiform cortex (x=-34, y=-36, z=-14), parahippocampal gyrus (x=-12, y=-44, z=72), and left cerebral white matter (x=-22, y=26, z=2), indicating reduced visual-emotional integration in individuals with greater difficulty describing feelings. During incongruent trials, DDF negatively correlated with precentral gyrus/supplementary motor cortex activation (x=-4, y=-24, z=54), suggesting reduced motor-cognitive engagement during conflict resolution. Emotion-specific analyses revealed positive DDF correlations in paracingulate/anterior cingulate cortex during HC-NC contrast (x=-10, y=26, z=34), suggesting compensatory prefrontal engagement for positive emotional processing. During sad face conditions (SI-NI, SI-HI), DDF positively correlated with postcentral gyrus, supramarginal gyrus, and angular gyrus activation (x=-62, y=-14, z=40; x=-58, y=-48, z=26), indicating increased somatosensory and mentalizing network engagement during sad emotional conflicts. Difficulty Identifying Feelings (DIF): DIF showed positive correlations with right cerebral white matter during SC-NC (x=22, y=-26, z=24) and NI-NC (x=24, y=-18, z=26) contrasts, suggesting altered structural-functional connectivity. Right caudate activation positively correlated with DIF during SC-NC (x=-16, y=22, z=2), indicating altered striatal processing of sad emotional gaze cues. During SI-NI, DIF positively correlated with right cerebral white matter and inferior frontal gyrus (pars opercularis and triangularis; x=36, y=24, z=16), reflecting increased frontal control recruitment during sad incongruent trials. Externally Oriented Thinking (EOT): EOT positively correlated with left thalamus activation during HC-NC (x=-10, y=-24, z=16), suggesting heightened sensory relay for positive emotional cues. During SC-NC, EOT showed negative correlation with frontal pole (x=14, y=66, z=4), indicating reduced prefrontal engagement for sad emotional processing. During SI-NI, EOT positively correlated with brain stem (x=-6, y=-38, z=-46), precentral gyrus (x=4, y=-18, z=62), and parietal operculum (x=-50, y=-28, z=18), reflecting increased arousal and sensory-motor processing during sad emotional conflicts. Total TAS-20 Alexithymia Scores (TOT): Total scores showed negative correlation with cerebellum during congruent trials (x=22, y=-40, z=-38), suggesting reduced automatic emotional-motor coordination. During SC-NC, total scores positively correlated with right cerebral white matter and caudate (x=20, y=-26, z=26). During SI-NI, total scores positively correlated with insular cortex (x=34, y=0, z=12), parietal operculum (x=-50, y=-26, z=16), and precentral gyrus (x=56, y=-4, z=48), indicating compensatory interoceptive and sensory-motor engagement during sad incongruent trials. Conclusion

Our results demonstrate that TAS-20 subscales have distinct neural correlates during automatic social-emotional processing. DDF primarily affects temporal-limbic visual-emotional integration regions and shows compensatory anterior cingulate engagement for positive emotions. DIF involves altered white matter connectivity and striatal-frontal control systems. EOT modulates thalamic sensory relay and arousal-related brain stem activation. These dissociable patterns support the multidimensional conceptualization of alexithymia and suggest that different components may involve distinct neural mechanisms requiring tailored therapeutic approaches. Valence-specific patterns indicate reduced salience of negative emotional cues or greater cognitive effort in processing sadness. Sad faces engage broader compensatory networks—including white matter, striatum, insula, and sensorimotor areas—compared to happy faces, highlighting challenges in negative emotion processing in alexithymia. These findings indicate alexithymia differentially affects emotional processing dimensions. Overall, the dissociable neural patterns support alexithymia’s multidimensional nature and the need for component-specific interventions.
