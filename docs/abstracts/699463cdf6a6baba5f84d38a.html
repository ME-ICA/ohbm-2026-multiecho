Introduction

Within-site neuroimaging classification of first-episode schizophrenia (FES) has been increasingly explored, yet much less is known about how predictive models behave when applied across independent imaging sites. Differences in scanners, acquisition protocols, and population characteristics can introduce substantial site effects, but the relative impact of these factors and the effectiveness of available correction methods remain unclear.

Our aim is to identify modelling strategies that provide robust and transferable performance for FES classification across imaging sites. We also evaluate potential gains from fusing information from multiple modalities which is relevant for many other classification tasks in neuroscience such as multiple sclerosis1. We systematically evaluate unimodal neuroimaging features and their multimodal fusion, traditional machine-learning models, and neural networks using two independent imaging sites within a nested cross-validation framework. We quantify within-site performance, the degradation caused by cross-site transfer, and the capacity of several site-effect correction approaches, including ComBat, optimal transport, and deep adversarial adaptation, to improve accuracy.

Methods

Data and feature sets. Neuroimaging data were obtained from two sites IKEM (Institute for Clinical and Experimental Medicine, Prague, Czechia) and NUDZ (National Institute of Mental Health, Klecany, Czechia) (IKEM: 94 FES, 90 HC; NUDZ: 128 FES, 77 HC), each providing T1-weighted MRI, diffusion imaging (DTI), and resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) (details in Hlinka et al.2). Acquisition protocols differed substantially (e.g., rs-fMRI TR: 2000 ms at IKEM vs 1000 ms at NUDZ). All rs-fMRI data were preprocessed using the CONN default pipeline, and regional time series from the 90-region AAL atlas were used to compute functional connectivity (FC) and amplitude of low frequency fluctuation (ALFF). Structural features were derived using region-wise voxel-based morphometry (VBM). From DTI we computed fractional anisotropy, mean diffusivity, axial diffusivity, and radial diffusivity. We analysed unimodal feature sets (FC, ALFF, VBM, DTI) and their multimodal combinations.

Models and hyperparameter selection. We compared traditional machine-learning models (ridge regression, SVM, random forests) with neural networks (MLP, RowColCNN, GCN, GAT). For each model, site, and modality, hyperparameters were selected using a nested 10-fold cross-validation scheme. This procedure produced ten independent hyperparameter configurations per model family, site and modality.

Within-site evaluation. To estimate the best achievable performance on each site, configurations selected on one site were applied to cross-validation on the other. Because hyperparameters were chosen elsewhere, this avoids optimistic bias and provides an empirical upper bound on within-site performance.

Cross-site transfer learning. To assess generalisation across sites, each configuration was trained on the full source dataset and evaluated on the target dataset without access to target labels, quantifying accuracy loss due to site-related differences.

Site-effect correction methods. We evaluated three approaches to mitigate distributional mismatch: (1) ComBat3 for statistical harmonisation; (2) adversarial domain adaptation4 (ADDA) to align latent representations using unlabeled target data; (3) Sinkhorn optimal transport5 (OT) to map source features towards the target distribution 

Evaluation. Balanced accuracy (to account for class imbalance) was used for all results, and 95% confidence intervals were obtained via nonparametric bootstrap.

Results

Within-site evaluation showed that FC was the most informative unimodal feature set, with balanced accuracies of ~76–81% across models and sites. Adding structural or diffusion features yielded inconsistent improvements: multimodal combinations occasionally matched FC but did not provide systematic gains. Traditional machine-learning models (ridge, SVM) and simple neural networks (MLP) performed comparably, while more complex architectures (e.g., RowColCNN) offered no clear advantage. 

Cross-site analysis revealed a substantial reduction in accuracy when models trained on one site were applied directly to the other (Figure 1). Direct transfer typically resulted in balanced accuracies around 63–67% for FC and lower values for multimodal inputs, illustrating the magnitude of site-related distribution shift. 

ADDA produced only modest improvements (Figure 1), generally reaching 70–72% depending on model and modality. Harmonisation strategies produced clearer and more stable benefits. ComBat (Figure 1) consistently outperformed both direct transfer and deep adaptation, achieving balanced accuracies of ~69–72% across sites. The strongest improvements were achieved with OT (Figure 1). When applied to FC or FC+ALFF+VBM features, OT increased balanced accuracy to ~77–79% for MLP models and substantially narrowed the gap between cross-site and within-site performance. Ridge regression also benefited from OT, though effects were more variable for multimodal inputs.

Conclusion

Our results show that cross-site performance in FES classification is shaped more by site-related distribution shifts than by model complexity. FC emerged as the strongest and most stable unimodal feature set. Traditional ML models and simple neural networks performed similarly, indicating that increased architectural complexity offers limited benefit in this setting.

Direct cross-site transfer produced substantial accuracy loss, underscoring the impact of scanner and acquisition differences on predictive models. Deep adversarial adaptation reduced this gap only modestly and inconsistently. In contrast, ComBat provided reliable improvements across sites, and optimal transport yielded the strongest and most systematic reduction in domain shift, bringing cross-site accuracy close to within-site performance for both Ridge and MLP models.

Overall, the results indicate that effective site-effect correction is far more important for cross-site generalisation than increasing model complexity. OT emerged as a simple and computationally efficient approach that consistently reduced domain shift. Its strong performance suggests that OT should be considered alongside more traditionally used harmonisation methods in future neuroimaging-based classification studies.
