Introduction

Touch is a fundamental sense that plays a key role in social communication, emotional regulation, and brain development 1. Tactile stimulation is detected by specialised peripheral receptors in the skin and relayed via sensory neurons through the spinal cord, brainstem, and thalamus to a network of cortical regions including the somatosensory cortex. Of these, unmyelinated C-tactile (CT) mechanoreceptors in hairy skin play a key role in processing affective touch 2 and are optimally-innervated by low-force stroking at ~1–10 cm/s and at skin temperature (~32°C). Information from these receptors are then processed via the affective touch pathway, where ascending CT inputs project to thalamic nuclei then onward to the insula and onto social and reward-related brain regions including the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, and medial orbitofrontal cortex 3. In contrast, discrimination of touch features, such as pressure and texture, are encoded by other mechanoreceptors and processed by primary and secondary somatosensory cortices (S1, S2), orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex 4. Although affective and discriminative fibres exhibit reciprocal cortical connections, it is unclear how the human primary somatosensory cortex differentiates CT-optimal from non-CT stimulation. Studying this in a precise manner is challenging due to the difficulties in robustly delivering the optimal stroking speed and pressure, as well as due to the lower spatial resolution and signal-to-noise ratio of fMRI at standard field strengths. To address this, we developed a custom pneumatic MR-compatible robotic system. This system is capable of delivering a robust, reproducible brushing stimulus within the CT-optimal and non-CT range for use in laminar 7T fMRI studies of the circuitry underlying distinct tactile processing 5. 

Methods

Local Ethics Committee approval (HR/DP-24/25-45808) and written consent was provided for data collection. 

Data acquisition

Data were acquired from four adults (Range: 25-49 years, 1 male) using a Siemens 7T MAGNETOM Terra.X system (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, DE) and an 8TX-32RX head coil (Nova Medical, Wilmington MA, USA). BOLD fMRI data were acquired in 3/4 subjects with a partial field of view (FoV) GRE-EPI sequence over 7:01min with parameters: 0.8mm isotropic resolution, SMS 2, repetition time (TR)  2590 ms, in-plane acceleration of 4, echo time (TE) of 25 ms, flip angle of 90°, 72 slices. Data of 1 participant was collected during a whole brain GRE-EPI sequence with a TR of 2990 ms, in-plane acceleration of 4, a flip angle of 90°, a TE of 25 ms, SMS 3, 120 slices with a 0.8 mm isotropic resolution acquired across 8:19 min. 

Tactile stimulus 

Tactile stimulation was provided in 10 TR blocks of back-and-forth movement at either CT-optimal (1-10 cm/s) or non-CT-optimal (> 10 cm/s) frequency on the participants right forearm (Figure 1A-C). Stimulation was interspersed with rest blocks of equal length. Stimulus presentation was achieved using an MRX-1 pneumatic controller and fMRI-compatible skin stimulation device developed by Human Robotix Ltd (humanrobotix.co.uk). The stimulation device is MR-safe, consisting of a laser-cut polylactic base supporting a 3D-printed height-adjustable arm (Figure 1A). A movable piston extends with a carbon-fibre rod with a second carbon fibre rod mounted via a height-adjustable clip holding a 3 × 2 cm effector delivering gentle tactile stimulation to the participant’s skin.  The system is pneumatically actuated and controlled with an Arduino Giga R1 board that regulates two air valves (MPPES-3-1/8-6-010, Festo). Custom-designed C++ Arduino code and Matlab-based functions control the timing, intensity, and duration of robotic movements through controlled opening of the air valves

Data analysis

Data underwent standardise preprocessing, which included slice-time correction, thermal denoising with NORDIC, and motion correction via AFNI’s 3dVolReg, with trials containing volumes with framewise displacement exceeding 0.8 mm excluded and high-pass filtering. Whole cortical thickness S1 ROIs were manually defined posterior to the central sulcus and ROIs were split into ten equi-volume depths using LAYNII. After linear deveining, depth specific BOLD timeseries were extracted and stimulus trials were averaged across participants. The mean percent signal change depth profiles were calculated based on the peak amplitudes above 0.5% during the stimulation period.

Results

Reproducible stimulation was achieved both within and outside the CT-fibre range. At 50% air pressure, the average speed was 7.47 ± 1.87 cm/s, and at 70%, 14.55 ± 4.28 cm/s (Figure 1D, E), corresponding to stimulation inside and outside the CT-fibre range respectively. These speeds were subsequently used in the fMRI acquisition.  

 A positive BOLD response was observed in the contralateral S1 during both CT fibre and non-CT fibre stimulation (Figure 2A). CT-optimal stimulation elicited distinct cortical-depth profiles in S1, with increased BOLD signal changes of ~1.5% in the middle and ~2.25% in the superficial cortical depths, compared to non-CT fibre stimulation, which elicited a ~0.9% amplitude response in the middle and the superficial depths (Figure 2F). The trial-averaged BOLD response was positive across all three cortical depths for both CT and non-CT responses (Figure 2G, H). During the CT stimulation, an initial peak of 3% BOLD signal change was reached after ~10 sec post stimulus onset (Figure 2G). This response was sustained until 18 seconds post-stimulus onset, followed by a post-stimulus undershoot in the superficial depth. In contrast, during non-CT stimulation, the BOLD response was delayed in onset and temporally dispersed (Figure 2H) with an initial peak in BOLD signal change of 1.5% of the middle and superficial depth around 12 sec that remained elevated for the duration of the stimulus before returning to baseline. Finally, more significantly activated voxels in the insular were elicited by CT-optimal stimulation compared to non-CT stimulation. 

Conclusion

Compared with the non-CT stimulation, the CT-optimal cortical-depth-dependent BOLD response exhibits distinct properties including a higher amplitude, faster onset, shorter total duration and a prominent post stimulus undershoot, especially within the superficial cortical depth (Figure 2F-H). CT-optimal stimulation also activated the posterior insular cortex, the primary target of CT afferents within the affective touch pathway 3, demonstrating that the pneumatic device selectively engages affective and discriminative touch circuits (Figure 2B). 

 We describe a novel pneumatic MR-compatible tactile stimulation robot for ultra-high field fMRI studies that can reliably deliver distinct patterns of stimulation for specific activation of the affective and discriminative touch pathways. This system will enable detailed investigation of differences in the underlying functional cortical circuitry of the somatosensory system. The robotics’ modular and adaptable design makes it suitable for studying affective touch processing across critical developmental periods and in neurodevelopmental conditions, providing a generalisable research tool.
