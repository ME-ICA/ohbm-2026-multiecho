Introduction

Language relies on hierarchical structure (e.g., the sentences “she hit [the thief with the bat]” and “she hit [the thief] [with the bat]” have different meanings). The sensorimotor system, on the other hand, can only generate linear sequences. As a result of this, language production requires the transformation of hierarchically structured sentences into linear strings that can be externalised through speech (or sign). Until recently, the methodological challenges associated with speech production have prevented researchers from explicitly testing whether linearisation is supported by the pars triangularis of the left inferior frontal gyrus as suggested by Matchin and Hickok1 on the basis of syntactic deficits in aphasia.

    In the current study, we investigated which brain regions support the linearisation of hierarchical structure during spontaneous speech production using a pre-existing dataset2 where participants recalled the contents of a series of short movies. We measured linearisation by focusing on the opening and maintenance of different kinds of dependency relations. In the “opening” metrics, we measured the number of clausal (“to leave”, “that she liked it”) and non-clausal (“the cake”, “in the park”) arguments associated with each verb. In the “maintenance” metrics, we marked the parts of the sentences where clausal and non-clausal arguments had been initiated but not resolved. Additionally, to control for the effect of lexical predictability, we also calculated word-by-word metrics of word frequency and word predictability given the context (lexical surprisal). To directly compare the word-by-word predictors with BOLD activity, we convolved the linguistic predictors with the haemodynamic response function and resampled it to the 1.5s repetition time. The resulting predictor time series were later entered into a linear mixed-effects model to estimate which brain areas are sensitive to different aspects of lexical and syntactic processing.

Methods

We analysed a pre-existing fMRI dataset2 where participants (n = 19) verbally described what they remembered about a series of short movies they had watched earlier in the session (average speech duration: 26 min, SD: 8 min). The number of clausal and non-clausal dependencies associated with each verb and the parts of the sentence where they had been initiated but not resolved (Figure 1) were calculated using the spaCy3 dependency parse. The controls for lexical predictability were based on SUBTLEX-US (word frequency) and the negative logarithm of the conditional probability of each word computed using transformer model GPT-2 (lexical surprisal).

    The dataset was acquired using a 3T Siemens Prisma scanner with a 64-channel head/neck coil. Functional images were acquired using a T2*-weighted multi-band accelerated EPI sequence with a 1.5s TR. Whole-brain high-resolution anatomical images were obtained using a T1-weighted MPRAGE pulse sequence. The MRI data were preprocessed using fMRIPrep (version 25.1.4). The functional scans were smoothed with a 5mm FWHM kernel and analysed using SPM12. Because linearisation is thought to occur prior to phonological encoding, we computed GLMs with one condition regressor that modelled the onset of every word 500 ms before it was articulated. We then added word frequency, lexical surprisal, and one of the four syntactic predictors as continuous regressors modulating the response to word onset (parametric modulators). We additionally included six motion parameters, frame-wise displacement, DVARS, and 5 aCompCor noise components as nuisance regressors. The resulting contrast images (four per participant) were entered into group-level GLMs that modelled the group response to each effect of interest separately.

Results

We first characterised which brain regions underlie naturalistic language production by looking for areas that showed increased activation when participants produced more words compared to an implicit baseline (i.e., the natural silences during speech). In line with previous studies, word production resulted in bilateral BOLD increases in motor cortices and superior temporal gyri as well as clusters in bilateral superior and left inferior frontal areas (Figure 2a). The response to word production was also strongly modulated by measures of lexical processing. Higher word frequency was associated with decreased responses in bilateral motor cortices, superior temporal gyri as well as the left middle and inferior temporal gyrus (Figure 2b). Lexical surprisal modulated brain activity in a similar network, with higher surprisal eliciting higher responses in bilateral motor cortices and superior temporal gyri (Figure 2c).

    The effects of the syntactic predictors were much more localised. A higher number of non-clausal arguments associated with the verb (e.g., “drank [a beer]” vs. “drank [a beer] [in the park]”) elicited increased response in the left supramarginal gyrus and the left medial precuneus extending into the superior parietal lobule (Figure 2d). In contrast, no brain areas showed increased responses for higher numbers of causal complements associated with the verb (i.e., increased responses for “want [to know]” vs. “want it”). The maintenance metrics, on the other hand, were significant across clausal and non-clausal arguments. Higher linear distance (i.e., maintenance) between a verb and its clausal complement (e.g., “realised [that she was wrong]” vs. “realised all of a sudden [that she was wrong]”) was associated with increased responses in the left superior temporal sulcus (Figure 2g). Instead, higher linear distance between a verb and its non-clausal arguments (e.g., “sat [on a bench]” vs. “sat all day long [on a bench]”) elicited higher responses in the left posterior middle temporal gyrus (Figure 2f).

Conclusion

The present work investigated the neural correlates of spontaneous speech production using a whole-brain approach. As the first study of its kind, the current results contribute to our understanding of the neurobiological infrastructure for language production in three main ways. Firstly, our results demonstrate that the current approach (previously used only in ROI-based analyses4) can significantly capture the neural correlates of spontaneous speech production at a whole-brain level, thus providing a new way to investigate if the language production network extends beyond the areas traditionally associated with language comprehension. Secondly, in line with previous studies, the current results identified a large fronto-temporal network modulated by the lexical predictability (word frequency and lexical surprisal) of the words participants were about to produce. Thirdly, our word-by-word metrics allowed us to isolate different aspects of linearisation (i.e., how many arguments need to be ordered and how long they need to be maintained) and experimentally investigate which brain regions support the externalisation of hierarchical structure during spontaneous speech production.    

    In contrast with the proposal by Matchin and Hickok1 and in accordance with the results of an fMRI study5 investigating the production of sentences vs. word list, the linearisation of hierarchical structure does not seem to be selectively supported by the pars triangularis of the left inferior frontal gyrus. Additionally, our results tentatively suggest linearisation might involve multiple sub-processes, as indicated by the fact that the metrics capturing the linearisation of clausal vs. non-clausal arguments elicited increased responses in different brain areas. The number of non-clausal arguments associated with the verb (anchored 500ms before the onset of the verb) elicited increased brain responses in areas outside the traditional language network (left supramarginal gyrus and medial precuneus extending into the superior parietal lobule). On the other hand, the maintenance of non-clausal arguments (>0 from 500ms before the onset of the verb until 500ms before the onset of the argument head) was associated with higher responses in an area strongly associated with language (the left posterior middle temporal gyrus). This apparent discrepancy might be explained by the fact that during the earliest stages of speech production planning speakers commit to a thematic structure / number of arguments in a very abstract way (as indicated by the effects in areas traditionally associated with linear ordering) and only later start filling these slots with linguistic content. In the case of clausal complements, the first part of this two-step process possibly showed no effects because the presence of clausal complements is either lexically specified or highly predictable (e.g., the verb "promised" is much more likely to be followed by a clausal complement like "to help" than the direct object like "transparency"). As a result of this, the opening of clausal complements elicited no increased responses because the structure may have already been determined by the verb choice. On the other hand, the maintenance of clausal complements (similarly to the maintenance of non-clausal arguments) also elicited increased responses in an area strongly associated with language (in this case the left superior temporal sulcus). The discrepancy between the two maintenance metrics likely stems from the fact that the maintenance of non-clausal arguments captures the moment the thematic structure is filled, while the maintenance of clausal arguments captures verb selection and the construction of the upcoming clause (e.g., “she told her friend that [she was tired]”).
