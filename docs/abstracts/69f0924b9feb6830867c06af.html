Introduction

One of the primary practices of neuroimaging is to chart how features change across the cortex, forming maps that richly annotate the cortical neuroanatomy. Using a variety of imaging acquisitions and statistical techniques, researchers have generated a wide range of maps documenting neurophysiology, functional connectivity, or even evolutionary expansion [1]. With such maps in hand, there are a number of ways that such maps aid our pursuit of knowledge about the brain.

Commonly, cortical maps are used as references for validating novel findings with established cartographic information. In other situations, a researcher might compare maps to confirm if two maps convey similar information. Shared across these applications is the assessment of similarity between spatially aligned data, which is commonly performed with standard Pearson correlation. Recently, we have shown that correlation between signals can be unwrapped in time, to expose an index of instantaneous similarity called edge time series [2]. Analogously, when taking the correlation between spatially aligned data, this calculation can be unwrapped across space, to render a map of spatial similarity. This new map highlights locations that disproportionately influence the final spatial similarity score (i.e., the r value). In a similar way to how time-varying connectivity has the potential to highlight moments when synchrony is elevated, this new spatial information has the potential to show specific areas of elevated similarly between maps.

We use the context of fingerprinting, where the goal is to find features with high-within and attenuate between-subject similarity, as a proof-of-concept for these methods. Additionally, we show how subject-level maps can be compared to a singular reference dataset, showing variable regional similarity. Overall, we show rendering spatial similarity as its vertex-level contributions has the potential to provide novel insights about the landscape of the cortex.

Methods

Data: Resting-state fMRI data (14.4 minutes; 0.72 TR) was obtained from 176 low-motion unrelated subjects from the Human Connectome Project [3]. Minimally preprocessed data (motion correction, distortion correction, anatomical alignment and warping) was further band-pass filtered and had noised removed via ICA-FIX and orthogonalization to the mean traces from both CSF and WM masks. Precomputed myelin surface data (T1w/T2w ratio) was also retained for analysis. Data in the 32k_fs_LR space was down-sampled, via a spatially-weighted mean, to the onavg space (~10k vertices per hemisphere) to mitigate the bias of uneven sampling due to the folding nature of the cortex. We utilized data from separate functional scanning sessions for evaluating test-retest in this study (REST1_LR, REST2_LR). Finally, the primary functional hierarchy data was downloaded for use [4]. 

Calculation: The Pearson correlation can be recast as the average element-wise product of two z-scored vectors. By forgoing the averaging step, a new element-by-element vector of similarity is rendered (Fig. 1a). For convenience, we can refer to this new vector as the spatial similarity map (SSM). Large positive values of the SSM indicate places where map magnitudes are simultaneously above or below their mean, while small values indicate that one or both magnitudes are near their respective mean. An SSM can be generated for every comparison between a pair of data maps. In the context of test-retest data, an SSM map can indicate locations where on the cortex self-similarity is high (coordinate values) or low (discordant). 

Analysis Methods: For each HCP subject, a spatial similarity map can be generated for map data collected from day 1 and day 2, including maps of signal mean, standard deviation, or even time series lag. Comparing day 1 and day 2 maps within subject, we generated an SSM which shows where on the cortex similarity is being driven by. Furthermore, we can compare all subjects SSM maps normative modeling to reveal locations in the cortex that are more similar within-subject, rather than between-subject (Fig. 1b). To do this, at each vertex the within-subject SSM magnitude is z-scored relative to the between-subjects SSM data distribution. Relatively large z-score values indicate that self-similarity is much higher than between-subject similarity and thus can highlight areas where self-similarity far exceed values expected from the comparison of different subjects. This way, high SSM areas potentially drive the efficacy of fingerprinting analyses. We computed four feature maps from each subject’s day 1 and day 2 resting state fMRI scan: temporal signal to noise ratio (TSNR), temporal autocorrelation (lag-1), signal standard deviation, and signal mean. Finally, subject-level myelin maps were gathered and compared to the first canonical functional gradient. 

Results

The relative z-score maps reveal areas where individual subject data is more similar that between subject data. This data does not necessarily reflect where data magnitudes are the highest. For example, in the case of low and high TSNR areas (inferior poles and anterior cingulate respectively), these are areas where most subject have similar values and thus, we can posit these areas contribute nominally to self-similarity (Fig. 1c). Self-similarity patterns vary by mapped feature (Fig. 2a) but do converge onto select areas of the temporal sulci and lateral orbitofrontal cortex (Fig. 2b), potentially suggesting a role for actively generated cognition altering these features to display individualized patterns at the subject level.   Finally, we compared a canonical functional hierarchy to subject-level myelin maps (Fig 2c), to reveal how this well-established relationship is laid out over the cortex. It appears that this negative spatial relationship is driven by discordant relationships (negative SSM) at the respective ends of the functional hierarchy (Fig. 2e).  Values along the posterior cingulate and insular cortex show the opposite relationship and therefore attenuate the primary negative spatial correlation. 

Conclusion

The correlation between spatially aligned features can be unwrapped, to reveal localized similarity patterns. Performing this straightforward calculation has the potential to differentiate different types of spatial correlation patterns. Doing so can potentially reveal how certain areas of the cortex are typically more unique, or contain more robust individualized information [5]. As interest in mapping individual-level data with canonical data sources grows, we can employ the SSM approach to evaluate which areas of cortex contribute disproportionately to map similarity [1]. Future studies might also use these tools to mitigate bias in spatial correlations, by providing an expected similarity value based on simpler feature sets. An advantage of this framework over existing approaches [5] is that an SSM can be recovered for each pairwise map comparison. Future work can leverage this fine-grained data to recover lower-dimensional patterns or clusters of spatial similarity patterning.
