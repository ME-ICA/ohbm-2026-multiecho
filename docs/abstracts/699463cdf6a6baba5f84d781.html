Introduction

Visual perceptual learning (VPL) refers to experience-driven improvements in visual performance1. A hallmark of VPL is its specificity to trained stimulus features and locations2, suggesting the involvement of early visual mechanisms. Attention plays a central role in VPL because it determines which sensory representations are amplified and thus targeted for plasticity. In previous work, attention has been shown to selectively enhance neural responses at low-to-mid contrasts (input gain)3.

Two major theoretical accounts have been proposed to explain the neural basis of VPL. Low-level theory (LLT) posits that learning leads to hard-wired changes in early visual cortex, affecting the processing of stimuli even when presented outside the context of the training task4. In contrast, reverse hierarchy theory (RHT) proposes that learning reflects enhanced read-out through increased feedback or recurrent processing, whereby attention grants access to increasingly fine-grained representations5. In this view, a memory trace for visual skill may reside in structurally enhanced recurrent processing relevant for the task. Moreover, for skill learning tasks involving feature-based attention, known to affect the entire visual field, we speculated that enhanced recurrent processing might affect the entire visual field.

Here, we combined visual skill learning at a range of contrasts with ultra-high field 7T fMRI to examine (1) whether training-induced improvements are accompanied by MR signal changes consistent with input gain, and (2) whether these changes are better explained by low-level plasticity or by reverse hierarchical processing.

Methods

Participants

14 healthy volunteers (age 18-25) with normal or corrected eye acuity participated in the study.

Overall experimental design

Each participant completed a 2–3 week protocol consisting of two behavioral training phases—interleaved with 2 two-hour 7T fMRI sessions (Fig. 1A), followed by a final behavioral generalization test.

Stimulus and task at behavioural training sessions

A 3° diameter sinusoidal grating (sf = 5 c/d) was presented at 5° eccentricity in the lower-left quadrant while participants fixated centrally. Participants discriminated clockwise versus counterclockwise deviations from a 135° reference orientation using left/right arrow keys (Fig. 1B). Eye position was monitored with an eyetracker (Arrington Research Inc., Scottsdale, USA).

Gratings were presented for 500 ms with a 1 sec response window and a 500 ms inter-trial interval. Feedback was provided by colouring the fixation dot (green: correct, red: incorrect responses). Trials with eye deviations >1.5° or missing responses were aborted and replaced.

All thresholds were determined using a staircase procedure, converging to 84% correct. Staircase measurements were terminated after 14 reversal points or when a total of 120 trials was reached. The threshold was computed as the geometric mean of the last 10 reversal points. All thresholds reported here are one-sided geometric means from the 135° reference orientation.

Participants performed in two main behavioral tasks. First, they carried out orientation training using staircase threshold measurements in which the orientation difference was made dependent on performance. The training extended over five days and yielded an asymptotic orientation-discrimination threshold. Second, at a fixed orientation difference corresponding to that threshold, subsequent contrast training was carried out for 7 days, using staircase measurements making Michelson contrast dependent on performance.

Stimulus and task at MR sessions

Each 2h MR session included eight functional runs and two localizer runs. In the first session, anatomical image acquisition was additionally acquired. In functional runs, to control for the locus of attention, participants conducted two tasks: an orientation discrimination task (ODT) and a T/L fixation task (TLT). ODT was similar to the orientation task in the behavioral lab except that stimuli were presented at 2 Hz in a block design. In TLT, letters ‘T’ or ‘L’ tilted in four angles (0°, 45°, 90°, 135° clockwise) were presented at 4 Hz and participants were to respond to one target (45° tilted ‘T’), see Fig. 1D. Grating stimuli were presented in 12 sec blocks separated by 14 sec intervals. At the beginning of a block, an arrow was presented to indicate which task the participant should perform. Four Michelson contrasts (3%, 5%, 13%, 32%) per task (eight conditions) and two blocks per condition were included in a run with pseudo-randomized order (~8 min).

In the localizer run, we mapped the location of the stimulus, and locations at mirrored distances from X and Y axis in the other three quadrants, by contrasting responses to disk and ring shaped stimuli made of 16 Hz flickering checkerboards using a 10 sec on and 16 sec off block design. Each stimulus was repeated eight times per run (~7 min). The lower and upper quadrants were mapped in fMRI session 1 and 2, respectively.

Scanning

We used a Siemens 7T MAGNETOM scanner with a 1TX/32RX head coil at Scannexus (Maastricht, NL). For functional and localizer runs, we used a 2D GE-BOLD protocol (0.8 mm isotropic, 52 slices, TR = 2 sec). Anatomical images were acquired using a MP2RAGE protocol (0.7 mm isotropic).

Analysis

Nine out of 14 participants' data have been analysed so far. BrainVoyager 23.2 (Brain Innovation, Maastricht, NL) was used for fMRI data analysis. Standard preprocessing was applied. Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs) nonlinear alignment was used for across-session alignment.

Results

Participants improved in both the orientation and contrast training, with thresholds reaching asymptotic performance towards the end of the learning curve (Fig. 2A&B). Learning was highly specific to the location of the stimulus and to the reference orientation (Fig. 2B).

GLM analysis of the localizer runs, contrasting disk to ring stimulus (red for disk, blue for ring) yielded four regions of interest (ROI) in primary visual cortex (V1) in all nine participants analysed so far (see Fig. 2C for two ROIs in one hemisphere).

For analysis within these ROIs, we computed MR signal change while taking for each stimulation block the mean of data points collected 6-14 sec after stimulus onset, and after grouping low and high contrasts (low: 3% & 5%; high: 13% & 32%). In the trained quadrant (Fig. 2D upper panels), Session 1 showed signal enhancement as a function of both contrast (TLT) and attention (ODT). In Session 2 (after completion of training), attention tended to selectively boost the response to the lower contrasts (paired t-test p < 0.05). This points to a training-induced enhancement of the effect of attention, limited to the lower contrast range. Notably, the training did not affect the response to the stimulus presented outside the task context (during TLT). 

In the untrained/unstimulated quadrants (Fig. 2D lower panels), there was (as expected) no effect of stimulus contrast. However, an attention-related signal in Session 1 was selectively boosted in Session 2 while participants performed the ODT at low contrasts (paired t-test p < 0.05). This is in line with a selective, training-induced boost of recurrent processing affecting the entire visual field.

Conclusion

We performed an fMRI experiment designed to pit LLT4 and RHT5 against each other. According to LLT, the result of visual skill learning is laid down in plastic changes in visual areas at the lowest level of the visual hierarchy where the trained stimuli are well-represented. Although attention is involved in the skill acquisition, at the end of learning the low-level plastic changes are thought to automatically affect stimulus processing4. According to RHT, the result of visual skill learning can be expected to be laid down in enhanced connectivity among different hierarchical levels in the visual system, to permit better readout by higher levels in the visual system of relevant stimulus parameters from lower levels where these parameters are best represented. This form of learning can be likened to attentional learning, and can be expected to influence stimulus processing whenever attention is directed to the stimulus in the trained task context. We investigated these two theoretical perspectives in a task that required participants to become better at orientation discrimination with gratings presented at both lower and higher contrasts. Based on earlier data showing that selective attention selectively boosts responses to lower contrasts3, we expected an analogous boost at lower contrasts due to skill learning. According to LLT, the expected training-induced enhancement of neural responses to low contrast gratings should remain present even when the stimuli are presented outside the context of the grating orientation discrimination task (during the T/L fixation task). Thus, the effect of learning should be revealed automatically, merely upon stimulus presentation. According to RHT, a training-induced enhancement of neural responses to low contrast gratings would be revealed only during performance of the orientation discrimination task, and not upon mere stimulus presentation. In addition, because task-related attention was at least in part feature-based (focusing on a small subset of orientations), we also entertained the possibility of a training-induced enhancement of feedback in retinotopic cortex beyond the stimulus representation. Our data supported the RHT-based predictions. 

Based on the analysis so far, it appears that not all of the results conformed to our expectations. Attention alone (Fig. 2D top left panel) did not yield the expected selective enhancement of responses at low contrasts. This makes it less straightforward to associate the selective training-induced response enhancement to low contrasts with input gain effects at low contrasts due to attention prior to training. Further analysis is required to verify our present analysis outcomes. Furthermore, a potential issue with the interpretation of our fMRI data would arise if, due to the staircase procedure used during training, our participants would have been exposed over the duration of the 7-day training to many more trials presenting stimuli at lower contrasts compared to higher contrasts. An analysis of our behavioral data excluded this possibility. The experimental design also equated the numbers of target presentations and button presses between the two task modes, excluding an influence of these extraneous variables on our results.

Our data provide support for enhanced feedback and recurrent processing as a learning mechanism in the present visual skill learning task, in line with concepts and data in other studies. This doesn’t exclude contributions from plastic changes in V1 to learning, even if they were not evident in the analyses conducted so far. Further analysis will differentiate activity among V1 layers to test the hypothesis of training-induced enhanced signal, especially at low contrasts, in the feedback-receiving superficial and deep layers.
