Introduction

Multimodal integration of neuroimaging techniques has gained increasing scientific attention in recent years. Among the available modalities, Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), functional MRI (fMRI), and electroencephalography (EEG) are the most widely employed in neuroscience research. These modalities operate on different spatial and temporal scales: MRI offers excellent spatial resolution (<1 mm) but limited temporal sensitivity; EEG provides millisecond-level temporal precision but poor spatial localization; and fMRI serves as an intermediate solution, balancing spatial and temporal resolution. Anatomical data derived from MRI are fundamental for EEG source reconstruction, allowing individualized modeling to account for inter-subjects' variability in brain morphology. Functional data from fMRI and EEG can be integrated to scout for neuronal populations active in both techniques. The aim of this preclinical study is to integrate anatomical and functional MRI data with EEG source reconstruction of cortical evoked activity during a silent naming (SN) task in healthy subjects. We propose that our framework can detect individualized areas of significant task-related activation, that could represent a potential target for neuromodulation or could be used in longitudinal follow-up after interventions such as rehabilitation. 

Methods

Fifteen healthy controls (40% female, mean age: 28.13 ± 4.25 years) underwent 3 Tesla 3D T1-weighted MRI (Philips) and 64-channel EEG acquisition (BrainProducts BrainAmp). Electrodes were placed according to the international 10-20 system with FCz as reference. Electrode impedances were maintained below 5 kΩ using Abralyt HiCl gel. EEG data were sampled at 500 Hz and recorded using BrainVision Recorder software. An auxiliary head scan of participants was acquired to estimate actual electrodes’ locations on the scalp. 

EEG SN task consisted of 60 trials presenting common objects to name silently once, displayed every 3080 ms (2 TR). Thirty rest trials displayed random pixel matrices during which participants were instructed to perform a countdown task. The complete sequence included 20 stimulus trials interspersed with 10 rest trials, repeated three times. Stimuli were presented, sending triggers to EEG at each TR. 

Initial event management removed duplicate SN events from the second TR of each activity trial and separated SN from rest events. EEG data preprocessing in EEGLAB included: ECG channel removal, bad channel interpolation, SN and rest epoch extraction, bad epoch removal, average re-referencing, infomax ICA, and manual IC component inspection and rejection. 

An fMRI activation map for the naming task was downloaded from Neurosynth (MNI space) and loaded into MATLAB. DBSCAN clustering (epsilon = 2, min_pts = 10) returned functional activity clusters. 

3D T1-weighted MRI, fMRI activation map, the auxiliary head scan, and EEG data were imported into Brainstorm. MRI scans were segmented using CAT12. fMRI clusters were imported and expanded on cortical surface using KNN search (K = 2) to create broader scouts with limitrophe edges. Overlapping and adjacent scouts were merged when possible. Scouts exceeding 10 cm² were subdivided into smaller 10 cm² regions (roughly the field of a TMS impulse with H-coils). To ensure anatomical consistency across participants, scouts from one reference subject were projected onto all other subjects' cortical surfaces.  

EEG epochs for both SN and rest conditions were imported, and electrode locations were determined using a custom MATLAB script based on an auxiliary head scan. Electrode positions were extracted determined through a clustering algorithm, which used color information and scalp curvature to identify clusters. The EEG cap contained two types of electrodes, white and green: green electrodes were directly identified by their color, whereas white electrodes were detected as clusters of scan points exhibiting high curvature. Any missing electrode coordinates could be added manually through a dedicated GUI. 

An initial group-level electrode analysis compared Event-Related Potentials (ERPs) and Event-Related Synchronization/Desynchronization (ERSD) between SN and rest epochs.  

Based on these findings, scout-level features were extracted: Inter-Trial Phase Coherence (ITPC) for temporal consistency across SN trials; theta synchronization for working memory/semantic processing2; beta desynchronization as a cortical activation marker3; and ROI-level ERP and ERSD for task-related frequency changes. ITPC was computed only for SN trials, while other features were extracted for both SN and rest trials. For all features except ITPC, SN trial features were also computed in contralateral scouts. 

Ten features were extracted across all scouts. A feature ranking algorithm was implemented to identify ROIs with the greatest number of relevant features. ROI impact was defined as the number of features present in the ROI. For ROIs that showed the same number of features, ties were resolved assigning weights to the features. To beta and theta desynchronization / synchronization (compared to rest trials or contralateral activity) were assigned higher weights, followed by high value ITPC (> 0.5) presence and feature beta/theta desynchronization / synchronization presence in a ROI.

Results

At electrodes level, significant differences were observed in left temporooccipital and right frontocentral electrodes at 70-150 ms, with low-beta (14-20 Hz) desynchronization and theta (4-8 Hz) synchronization reaching statistical significance. 

At a group ROI level, consistent ERSD beta desynchronization in left-temporal (LT) lobe and theta synchronization in right-central (RC) lobe were found, both comparing ROI SN activity with ROI resting-state (p < 0.05) and ROI SN activity with contralateral activity (p < 0.005). Comparisons were assessed with Wilcoxon rank sum test. GroupROI-level ERPs further showed significant left frontotemporal lobe activity approximately 70-150 ms after stimulus onset (Wilcoxon rank sum, p < 0.001), with ITPC values greater than 0.5.  

A total of 153 ROIs were extracted among the participants, and their positions were compared to the Desikan atlas available through CAT12. No restrictions were implemented while scouting for relevant active ROIs. Neurosynth activation map contained also occipital regions, known to be very active during visual tasks as SN for late response information processing (> 200 ms after stimulus onset). Even so, out of 153 ROIs, only 16 of them were located partially in the occipital lobes (15 in the left lateraloccipital L, 1 in the right lateraloccipital R), supporting the hypothesis that chosen EEG features are characteristic of information processing related to the SN naming task. Occipital regions showed a predominant alpha band activation around ~200 ms after stimulus onset^1. 

SN task is commonly known to show a left-frontotemporal BOLD fMRI activity^4. Although Neurosynth activation maps contained also right frontocentral clusters of activity, among all the relevant ROIs, 111 (76.55%) were in the left hemisphere. Among these, the most common are: left middletemporal L (counts: 23, 15.03%), left precentral L (counts: 18, 11,76%), left rostralmiddlefrontal L (counts: 17, 11.11%) and the already mentioned left lateraloccipital L (counts: 15, 9.80%). Among the 42 right hemisphere ROIs, the most common are: right precentral R (counts: 14, 9.15%) and right supramarginal R (counts: 12, 7.84%).

Conclusion

The fMRI-informed EEG approach reliably identified ROIs engaged during SN. LT beta desynchronization and RC theta synchronization reflected local activation and cognitive/semantic processing, with ITPC and ERP supporting task-related consistency. The appearance of features prevalently in left frontotemporal and bilateral frontocentral Desikan ROIs, without a priori exclusion of temporal ROIs, may indicate the suitability of chosen EEG features. In this study, we showed that SN can elicit a distinctive and reproducible EEG ERP in healthy subjects. Furthermore, we used salient features of the group level ERP to develop an algorithm that extracts source level ROIs of maximum activation within the framework of a fMRI activity atlas. Across subjects, ROIs were largely within the area of the left frontotemporal lobe, evidencing the reproducibility of our framework.  

This study presented a multimodal approach to identify cortical ROIs active at both EEG and fMRI levels. These ROIs may serve as targets for neuromodulation treatments such as rTMS These ROIs may serve as targets for further characterization of evoked responses, such as those elicited during motor imagery paradigms, or for neuromodulation interventions including repetitive Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (rTMS), supporting future clinical applications in neurodegenerative or lesional disorders of speech such as Primary Progressive Aphasia or stroke.
