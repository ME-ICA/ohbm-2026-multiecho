Introduction

Getting older poses challenges to functional independence and quality of life due to decreases in motor and cognitive control. Motor control arises from communication between a widespread network of brain regions. Therefore, changes in the structure, function and connectivity of these regions have been linked to age-related decline in motor control. For example, older adults show decreased grey and white matter volume, hyperactivation of cortical areas, increased between-network connectivity and white matter structural deterioration, among others.1

The role of the cerebellum in aging has only recently gained attention. The cerebellum communicates extensively with the cortex via a closed-loop system, where the cerebellum sends forward models to the cortex, and optimizes these predictions based on a feedback loop originating from the cortex. This feedback loop plays an essential role in motor coordination and motor learning, among other functions.2 

Existing research shows decreased cerebellar grey and white matter volumes in older adults. However, observations regarding the cortico-cerebellar functional connectivity are mixed.2 Furthermore, studies on the cortico-cerebellar white matter connections are scarce.

To address this knowledge gap, the current study examines age-group differences in the reciprocal cortico-cerebellar pathways: the efferent dentato-rubro-thalamic pathway (DRT) and the afferent cortico-ponto-cerebellar pathway (CPCT).3 

Methods

This cross-sectional study investigated structural differences in the cortico-cerebellar tracts using fixel-based diffusion MRI metrics in 46 younger (median [IQR] age: 26.0 [7.75] years) and 46 older adults (median [IQR] age: 67.5 [6.50] years). All participants were cognitively healthy and did not report any psychological or physical disorders. 

Data were pooled from two datasets, both consisting of younger and older adults and collected on the same 3 Tesla MRI scanner. Multi-shell diffusion MRI data were collected using the following parameters: voxel size = [2 x 2 x 2 mm3], flip angle = 90°, FOV = [128 x 128 x 60/64 mm3], b-values = [0/400/1000/2600], directions = [8/24/48/68], multiband = 2. T1-weighted (T1w) data were collected with voxel sizes of [1 x 0.89 x 0.89 mm3] or [0.8 x 0.8 x 0.8 mm3]. Furthermore, one dataset contained T2-weighted (T2w) images [1 x 1 x 1 mm3], while the other dataset contained FLAIR images [1 x 0.98 x 0.98 mm3]. 

T1w images were processed using fMRIPrep (v24.0.1). White matter hyperintensities (WMH) were identified by SamSeg (FreeSurfer v7.2.0) and manually corrected if necessary. Periventricular spaces (PVS) were identified by SHiVAi (PVS-v2).  Diffusion MRI data were preprocessed using MRtrix3 (corrected for Gibbs ringing, eddy currents, geometric distortion and intensity bias, and upsampled to an isotropic resolution of 1.3 mm). 

Fiber orientation density (FOD) images for each subject were estimated using multi-shell multi-tissue constrained spherical deconvolution; joint bias field correction and intensity normalization were applied. A single template white matter (WM) FOD image and a derived fixel template were created by taking the median across 40 participants (10 per group and dataset). Subject-specific FODs and fixels were registered to the template and three fixel-based metrics were computed: fiber density (FD, assessing intra-axonal microstructural integrity), log-transformed fiber cross-section (logFC, to look into macrostructural morphological properties) and fiber density and cross-section (FDC, to simultaneously examine micro- and macrostructural properties).4 These metrics were smoothed using a fixel-fixel connectivity matrix generated from the WM FOD template (iFOD2, 20,000,000 streamlines, minimum/maximum length = 10/250, max angle = 22.5) and filtered to 2,000,000 streamlines using SIFT. 

IFOD2 Tractography of the DRT and CPCT was performed on the template. Inclusion ROIs for the DRT: ipsilateral frontal lobe, ipsilateral ventrolateral thalamus, ipsilateral red nucleus and contralateral nucleus dentatus; exclusion ROIs: corpus callosum, contralateral cerebellar region IX, ipsilateral cerebellar hemisphere and contralateral cerebral hemisphere. Inclusion ROIs for the CPCT: ipsilateral frontal cortex, ipsilateral internal capsule, bilateral pons, contralateral middle cerebellar peduncle and contralateral cerebellum; exclusion ROIs: contralateral superior cerebellar peduncle, ipsilateral ventro-lateral thalamus, ipsilateral red nucleus, ipsilateral cerebellar hemisphere and contralateral cerebral hemisphere. All resulting tracts were visually inspected and spurious fibers excluded. 

Each metric (FD, logFC, FDC) was reduced to a single value per subject and tract (DRT, CPCT) using the median. Posteriorly, a linear mixed model, with subject as a random factor, was computed for each metric with age group (younger/older), tract (DRT/CPCT), normalized total intracranial volume (TIV; only for logFC and FDC metrics) and the interaction between age group and tract as predictors. Post-hoc analyses were False Discovery Rate corrected (emmeans package in R). An additional fixel-based general linear model was employed per metric for group comparison using the MRtrix3 fixelcfestats command; results were corrected using family-wise error rate (FWE). Given the focus on healthy-aging, fixels within WMH or PVS were excluded from all analyses. Significance level was determined with α < 0.05.

Results

FD was significantly predicted by the interaction between tract and age group (F[1, 90] = 7.51, p = 0.007), with furthermore a significant effect of tract (F[1,90] = 2574.50, p < 0.001), but not of age group (F[1, 90] = 2.43, p = 0.123). Post-hoc analyses revealed reduced FD in older adults compared to younger adults in the CPCT (T[110] = -2.35, p = 0.021), but not in the DRT (T[110] = -0.61, p = 0.544). 

After controlling for TIV (F[1, 89] = 50.06, p < 0.001), logFC was significantly predicted by tract (F[1, 91] = 56.08, p < 0.001) and age group (F[1, 89] = 10.78, p = 0.001), but not by their interaction (F[1,91] = 0.46, p = 0.501). LogFC was lower in the CPCT than the DRT (estimate = -0.03, T[91] = -7.49, p < 0.001) and lower in older compared to younger adults (estimate = -0.04, T[89] = -3.28, p = 0.002).

After controlling for TIV (F[1, 89] = 29.25, p < 0.001), FDC was also significantly predicted by tract (F[1, 91] = 1531.73, p < 0.001) and age group (F[1, 89] = 11.88, p < 0.001), but not by their interaction (F[1,90] = 0.04, p = 0.844). FDC was lower in the CPCT than the DRT (estimate = -0.076, T[91] = -39.14, p < 0.001) and lower in older compared to younger adults (estimate = -0.025, T[89] = -3.45, p = 0.001).

Fixel-specific analyses in the cortico-cerebellar tracts revealed that older adults showed mainly decreased, but also increased FD in several fixels compared to younger adults. LogFC solely showed decreased values in older compared to younger adults. FDC was higher in older adults in a limited number of fixels within the midbrain and superior part of the pons (Fig. 1).

Conclusion

This study investigated age-group differences in the structure of cortico-cerebellar white matter tracts. To our knowledge, this study is the first to examine and reconstruct the reciprocal connections between the cortex and the cerebellum using diffusion MRI in a population of both younger and older healthy adults. 

FDC metrics were indicative of altered white matter structure in the older versus younger adults in both cortico-cerebellar tracts to an equal extent. This suggests that age-related white matter structural decline also affects the white matter tracts connecting the cortex to the cerebellum.  At a macrostructural level, both tracts displayed these age-group differences to a similar extent as examined with logFC. However, lower FD in older adults was observed only in the CPCT.

Additional fixel-wise analyses showed both decreased and increased FD in older compared to younger adults. This is in line with previous studies also reporting both negative and positive associations between FD and FC metrics, and age.5 In most areas of increased FD, the FDC was not higher. Hence, these areas likely present a relatively preserved microstructure (FD) when compared to the macrostructural fiber shrinkage (lower FC).5 Interestingly, higher FDC in older adults was observed in fixels within the midbrain and superior pons, which may indicate region-specific ageing effects rather than uniform decline. 

Overall, our results point towards a lower overall white matter integrity of the cortico-cerebellar tracts in older compared to younger adults. However, it seems that for the DRT this decrease is primarily driven by a decrease in macrostructural bundle size (i.e., lower FC) rather than a decrease in the bundle density (FD). Our results might be indicative of a slightly better resistance of the efferent DRT than the afferent CPCT to aging, given that the FD decrease was only seen in the CPCT; however,  this claim is made with great caution, since the magnitude of the age-group difference did not differ in terms of overall white matter integrity (FDC). 

In conclusion, this study evaluated group differences in white matter integrity between younger and older adults to increase our understanding of the role of the cerebellum in aging. Our results show that the cortico-cerebellar tracts also show age-related decline. Further analyses are necessary to determine the impact of these age-related declines on motor performance.
