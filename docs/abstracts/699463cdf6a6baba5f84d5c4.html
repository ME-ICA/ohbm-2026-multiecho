Introduction

Neurodegenerative and psychiatric disorders are more closely linked than their distinct clinical presentations suggest. Individuals with depression or schizophrenia have up to four-fold higher risk of developing dementia later in life, while 65% of patients with neurodegenerative diseases experience psychiatric symptoms.¹ Recent genetic studies reinforce this connection: significant genetic correlations exist across these disease groups, and 30% of neurodegenerative disease causal proteins are shared with psychiatric disorders.¹ Such convergence raises a fundamental question: do these diseases share biological mechanisms affecting the brain?

Brain structure offers a window into this question. Both neurodegenerative and psychiatric disorders show characteristic cortical and subcortical abnormalities with regional overlap.² Critically, Abbasi et al. demonstrated that Parkinson's disease (PD) polygenic risk predicts cortical structure in healthy individuals—suggesting genetic risk shapes brain vulnerability before disease manifests, positioning it as an intermediate phenotype rather than disease consequence.³ Since neurodegenerative and psychiatric disorders share genetic architecture, pathways contributing to brain vulnerability in one context may similarly operate across disease contexts.

Identifying these pathways requires moving beyond genome-wide PRS. Bandres-Ciga et al. screened 2,199 pathways and identified 46 predicting PD risk—encompassing synaptic transmission, vesicle trafficking, and chromatin organization.⁴ These are fundamental neuronal processes, not disease-specific mechanisms. We hypothesized some represent general brain vulnerability pathways. We computed pathway-PRS using GWAS from 11 neurodegenerative and psychiatric disorders and tested associations with brain structure. If a pathway shapes vulnerability generally, it should show consistent brain associations regardless of which disease provides genetic weights.

Methods

Pathway-PRS calculation

We computed pathway-specific PRS for 46 pathways using GWAS summary statistics from 11 neuropsychiatric disorders: Parkinson's disease, Alzheimer's disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, major depressive disorder, anxiety disorder, ADHD, autism spectrum disorder, alcohol use disorder, post-traumatic stress disorder and insomnia. Posterior effect sizes were estimated using PRS-CS. For each pathway, only SNPs mapping to genes within that pathway were included in PRS calculation. This yielded 46 pathway-PRS × 11 disorders = 506 pathway-PRS scores per individual.

Imaging data and preprocessing

Neuroimaging phenotypes were derived from UK Biobank (N≈30,000). We examined regional cortical thickness (CT), cortical surface area (SA), and subcortical volumes from T1-weighted MRI processed with FreeSurfer. Normative modeling was applied to imaging data.5 To minimize confounding from existing brain pathology, we excluded individuals with diagnosed neurological diseases.

Statistical analysis

Associations between each pathway-PRS and imaging phenotypes were tested using linear regression, adjusting for age, sex, genetic principal components and other confounding variables suggested by UKB’s team. To assess spatial consistency of pathway-brain associations across disorders, we performed spin tests (spatial permutation testing), which evaluate whether two cortical maps are more similar than expected given spatial autocorrelation.⁵ We correlated effect size maps (pathway-PRS → imaging associations) across disorders for each pathway, testing whether the same pathway shows consistent spatial patterns of brain associations when genetic weights derive from different disease GWAS. Multiple testing correction was performed using FDR.

Results

Identification of transdiagnostic pathways

Among 46 pathways tested, two showed consistent associations with brain structure across multiple neuropsychiatric disorders: P38_GAMMA_DELTA and ACTIVATION_OF_AMPK_DOWNSTREAM_OF_NMDARS (Figure 1). For these pathways, the spatial pattern of associations with cortical surface area, cortical thickness, and subcortical volumes was significantly correlated across disorders (spin test pFDR<0.05), indicating that the same pathway produces similar brain-wide effects regardless of which disease GWAS was used to weight the PRS.

The cross-disorder consistency included both convergent and divergent patterns. For some disorder pairs, pathway-PRS showed effects in the same direction. For other pairs, effects were in opposite directions but spatially correlated—suggesting the same brain regions are affected, though the direction of effect differs by disease genetic architecture.

Pathways showing disease-specific effects

The majority of the 46 pathways did not show consistent cross-disorder associations. For these pathways, the spatial pattern of brain associations differed substantially depending on which disease GWAS provided genetic weights, consistent with disease-specific genetic architectures.

Conclusion

We identified two biological pathways—P38_GAMMA_DELTA and ACTIVATION_OF_AMPK_DOWNSTREAM_OF_NMDARS—that demonstrate consistent associations with brain structure across multiple neuropsychiatric disorders, supporting a shared-vulnerability model for a subset of biological mechanisms. These pathways, originally discovered through their association with Parkinson's disease risk, appear to represent general brain vulnerability mechanisms rather than disease-specific processes.

The P38 gamma/delta pathway is involved in cellular stress responses, inflammation, and neuronal survival. AMPK activation downstream of NMDA receptors regulates synaptic plasticity, energy homeostasis, and neuronal metabolism. Both pathways govern fundamental aspects of neuronal function that are plausibly relevant across brain disorders. Their consistent spatial effects on brain structure—regardless of which disease's genetic architecture is used to weight the PRS—suggests these pathways influence brain through common mechanisms that are tapped by the genetics of multiple disorders.

However, most pathways showed disease-specific rather than transdiagnostic associations, indicating that shared vulnerability operates through specific biological processes rather than being a general feature of all disease-relevant pathways. This partial convergence suggests a model wherein certain core neurobiological mechanisms create general brain vulnerability, while other pathways contribute to disease risk through more specific routes.

These findings have implications for therapeutic development. Pathways with transdiagnostic effects may represent targets whose modulation could benefit patients across diagnostic categories, while disease-specific pathways may require targeted interventions.
