Introduction

How do we know if a fact we just read is reliable, if we truly recognized a friend in a crowded street, or if a belief we hold is grounded in evidence rather than assumption? These everyday situations rely on our ability to evaluate both the world and our own judgments. On one hand, we make veracity judgments, assessing whether information is likely true or false. On the other hand, we monitor our certainty through confidence, a window into metacognition, the capacity to evaluate the reliability of our own cognitive processes. While veracity judgments target external information, confidence reflects the internal sense of accuracy. Investigating how the brain encodes both what we perceive as true and how confident we are in that perception is therefore key to understanding the neural mechanisms that support decision-making and metacognitive evaluation.

Previous studies investigating the neural correlates of veracity and confidence judgments1,2 showed that veracity judgments activate the ventromedial prefrontal cortex for belief, the insula and regions in the frontal and parietal lobes for disbelief, and the anterior cingulate cortex for uncertainty, with machine-learning classifiers able to decode belief versus disbelief from fMRI patterns. Confidence judgments engage overlapping regions, including vmPFC, insula, and parietal cortices, and both domain-general and domain-specific patterns have been reported within the prefrontal cortex3,4,5. Despite this progress, it remains unclear whether the neural mechanisms supporting veracity and confidence judgments are shared across cognitive domains or exhibit domain-specific specialization. Here, we ask whether veracity and confidence judgments rely on shared or domain-specific neural representations across perceptual and semantic domains. To address this, we use a whole-brain multivariate decoding approach that evaluates distributed voxel-wise patterns to detect information about veracity and confidence across domains.

Methods

Participants

Eleven healthy adults (age range: 20–30 years; 7 males) participated in a pilot 3T fMRI study. All participants provided written informed consent (in accordance with Helsinki Declaration), and the study protocol was reviewed and approved by the local ethics committee at NeuroSpin (CEA, France). Participants received monetary compensation for their involvement. While this sample is smaller than recommended in recent neuroimaging reproducibility guidelines, the objective of this study was not to derive group-level inferences. Instead, each participant’s data were analyzed independently at the single-subject level.

Experimental design

We designed a paradigm (Figure 1-A) to investigate veracity and confidence across two domains: visual perception and written sentences (knowledge and opinions). Across all domains, participants performed the same task: deciding whether a stimulus was true or false and rating their confidence at the same time, ensuring a common currency for cross-domain comparisons.
 Perceptual trials used random-dot kinematograms in which participants judged whether the arrow direction matched the global motion of the dots; motion coherence and onset delay for global motion were varied to modulate difficulty. Written trials consisted of incrementally presented statements with some items paired with their logical opposites to assess consistency.
 Responses were collected via two MR-compatible controllers mapping True/False to left/right hands, each with three buttons mapping for high, medium and low confidence; both hand and finger mappings varied across runs.
 Task difficulty was adjusted online to obtain balanced responses. Propositions were pre-classified based on a separate sample, and adaptively sampled to counteract response biases. For perception, coherence level and coherent-motion duration were varied orthogonally to maintain a broad response distribution, and to avoid confounding them with a single visual feature.
 The experiment comprised eight 9.5-min runs (52 trials each), presented in pseudorandom order, with self-paced breaks between runs.

Acquisition and preprocessing

Data were acquired on a 3T Siemens PrismaFit. Each session included a T1w MPRAGE (1-mm) and whole-brain EPI fMRI (1.75-mm isotropic, TR=1850ms, SMS=3, fat saturation applied) with additional reverse phase-encoding images for distortion correction. Data was BIDS-organized and preprocessed with fMRIPrep: structural images were corrected, segmented, and normalized; BOLD data underwent slice-timing, motion and distortion correction, and co-registration. 

Univariate analysis

First-level GLMs were performed with Nilearn. Events were modeled with onsets 1s before the response and a 2s duration. Veracity and confidence were included as parametric modulators, with confidence orthogonalized by mean-centering to address collinearity between the main effect regressor and confidence levels (Figure 1-B). Motion parameters, framewise displacement, and anatomical CompCor components served as nuisance regressors. All regressors were convolved with a canonical HRF. Analyses were performed in native and MNI space, with 5-mm smoothing. Run-wise estimates for each condition and stimulus type were combined using fixed-effects models.

Multi-voxel pattern analysis

We used whole-brain decoding to test whether activity patterns predicted trial-by-trial veracity and confidence. For feature extraction, brain components were derived using ICA or DictLearning. Loadings served as features, with 50 components providing the best trade-off across tested dimensionalities. Features were standardized, reduced using RFECV, and evaluated in a leave-one-run-out scheme. Linear and non-linear classifiers were tested. As a complementary approach, single-trial activation maps were obtained from a GLM, where each event yielded a whole-brain z-map. Linear classifiers were used combined with a percentile-based voxel screening retaining only the most informative voxels.

Results

Univariate results

For confidence, exploratory first-level analyses revealed a positive association between confidence ratings and activity in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC), alongside negative correlations within prefronto-parietal network. For veracity judgments, trials judged as true showed increased activity in the vmPFC, whereas false/disbelief judgments were associated with greater engagement of the anterior insula and anterior cingulate cortex. These effects were broadly consistent with networks previously implicated in subjective valuation and belief formation, although between-subject variability limited the possibility of drawing strong group-level conclusions. Importantly, the confidence analyses were based on a small cohort (n=9), while veracity-related effects were examined in 11 participants, as the last two subjects performed only the true/false judgment without confidence ratings.

Decoding results

Prediction accuracies, whether for veracity judgment or confidence level, exceeded the empirical chance level estimated using a dummy classifier for most subjects and runs.

For intra-domain decoding, several participants achieved significant above-chance performance. However, substantial variability was observed both across participants and across runs within the same participant. For instance, successful decoding in one stimulus domain did not necessarily generalize to the other within a given participant : participant 7 exhibited robust intra-domain veracity decoding for perception stimuli, whereas decoding performance for sentence stimuli remained largely at chance level. (Figure 2-A). A similar pattern was observed for confidence decoding: participant 9 showed above-chance decoding for sentence stimuli but not for perception stimuli (Figure 2-B).

The two feature-extraction approaches, Independent Component Analysis (ICA) and Dictionary Learning, yielded comparable decoding performance overall, with neither method consistently outperforming the other. Their relative effectiveness depended largely on the individual participant and the specific run.

For confidence decoding, feature construction based on z-maps fitted per stimulus led to superior performance (Figure 2-C), suggesting a more effective capture of task-relevant neural information. Linear classifiers generally provided more stable and higher decoding accuracies than more complex models such as Random Forests, particularly for this feature-extraction approach. This likely reflects the suitability of linear models for high-dimensional, low-sample-size fMRI data, where simpler decision boundaries help mitigate overfitting.

Finally, inter-domain decoding (training on perception stimuli and testing on sentence stimuli) proved more challenging. Nevertheless, decoding accuracies exceeded chance in many cases (Figure 2-D), suggesting the existence of partially shared neural representations supporting true/false judgments across stimulus modalities.

Conclusion

The present study provides preliminary evidence that distributed whole-brain activity patterns carry information about both veracity judgments and confidence levels. Although decoding performance was modest and variable across participants and runs, accuracies consistently exceeded chance, indicating that stable neural signatures of these cognitive processes can be captured using data-driven feature extraction (ICA, DictLearning) combined with linear classifiers. The fact that intra-domain decoding was more robust than inter-domain generalization, and that decoding success did not systematically transfer across stimulus types within individuals, highlights the complexity and subject-specificity of the underlying neural representations. Methodologically, the superiority of linear models over more complex classifiers aligns with known constraints of high-dimensional fMRI data, and the comparable performance of ICA and DictLearning suggests that both approaches provide viable low-dimensional embeddings for decoding confidence and veracity-related information. Taken together, these findings constitute a proof of concept rather than definitive evidence, and performance remains limited by multiple factors, including the optimality of the experimental paradigm, data quality, participant engagement, preprocessing choices, and the intrinsic constraints of MVPA on small samples. As a next step, we will acquire new data at 11.7T using the same paradigm, with the expectation that higher spatial resolution and improved SNR/tSNR will strengthen the reliability and sensitivity of the decoding analyses.
