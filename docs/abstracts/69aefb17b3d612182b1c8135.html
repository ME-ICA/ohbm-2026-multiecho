Introduction

Physical exercise is known to have long-term benefits for both physical and mental health. However, the physiological and neural responses to exercise depend on its characteristics, such as intensity and duration. Endurance exercise, such as running, places substantial demands on aerobic energy production and induces physical strain, including microdamage to skeletal muscle. These responses are driven by the need to maintain bodily homeostasis and sustain performance.

During exercise, the brain continuously monitors bodily states and regulates processes required for ongoing movement. Neural mechanisms underlying these processes can be studied through brain oscillatory activity. In particular, sensorimotor cortex exhibits characteristic alpha (8–13 Hz; mu) and beta (13–30 Hz) rhythms associated with movement planning, sensory processing, and execution. Frontal brain regions are involved in higher-order control processes, such as evaluating bodily resources and regulating effort during exercise. It has been proposed that during sustained physical activity, neural resources may be redistributed, leading to reduced frontal activity, a phenomenon known as transient hypofrontality. This process may contribute not only to changes in cognitive function but also to exercise-induced affective states.

Endurance exercise is also associated with central fatigue, defined as a reduced ability to voluntarily activate muscles, suggesting that prolonged running may influence neural rhythms related to motor control and descending drive.

The present study aimed to examine how acute 90-minute endurance exercise affects brain oscillatory activity. We also investigated how the bodily physiological state in terms heart rate and blood lactate levels, relate to state of the brain. Specifically, we explored where and how prolonged endurance exercise modulates resting-state brain oscillations.

Methods

A total of 19 18-45 years old participants (9 females (35.9 ± 6.0 yrs) and 10 males (36.4 ± 5.1 yrs)) reporting themselves active and healthy were recruited and completed the study protocol. 

Study protocol consisted of three separate visits (see Figure 1). 
During the pre-test visit, participants completed background questionnaires and an incremental treadmill test. The test consisted of three-minute running stages, with the speed increasing by 1 km/h after each stage, until volitional exhaustion. Results from the incremental test were used to determine each participant’s running speed corresponding to the first lactate threshold, which was then applied for the 90-minute run.

Exercise and control visits were conducted in the MEG-laboratory (Centre for Interdisciplinary Brain Research (CIBR), University of Jyväskylä) at the same time of the day (± 2 hours). Visits were similar in content (see Figure 1) and consisted in short: 1) PRE-measurements of performance and resting-state brain activity 2) 90-min treadmill running (with individually preferred music) at a speed of the first ventilatory threshold OR control session including 90-min reading/music listening 3) immediately after cessation POST-measurements of performance and resting-state brain activity (POST10) 
4) Performance tests and resting-state brain measurements conducted 45 minutes after cessation (POST55).

Resting-state measurements were conducted at PRE, POST10, and POST55 and included brain activity recorded with magnetoencephalography (Elekta Neuromag TRIUX, Elekta Oy, Helsinki, Finland) alongside bodily measures (heart rate and ECG-derived respiration). Measurements lasted 8 minutes with eyes closed. Fingertip blood samples were also collected to determine blood lactate levels.

Data analysis. 

To study alterations and the spatial distribution of the brain activity, dynamic imaging of coherent sources ^1 was implemented using custom scripts in MATLAB (R2020b, The MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA, USA). First, computing forward solution using a single-compartment realistic boundary element model (fsaverage-5.1.0 template brain) was done, which was then used for all subjects. Then, calculation of cross spectral density (CSD) matrices were performed using the Welch’s method. Setup used was frequency range of 2 – 45 Hz, window length 2048 samples, 50 % overlap, Hanning window. In the next step customized version of the Destrieux anatomical parcellation (lähde!), having 69 parcels per hemisphere, was used implemented in the calculations. A frequency-domain beamformer, using common weights approach was performed (Note: this step was done separately for PRE-POST10 (N=19) and PRE-POST55 (N=17)). Individual beamformer weights were estimated across the whole frequency band of interest (2 – 45 Hz) for each participant. For all participants, power-spectral estimates at the parcel-level across the whole brain in each condition (run/con x timepoint) by applying the beamformer weights across the frequency bins within the 2 – 45 Hz range. Finally ”Fitting oscillations and one over f” (FOOOF) algorithm [38] was used to extract both periodic (i.e., power) and aperiodic components of the power spectra in each parcel within the frequency range of 2 – 45 Hz. For periodic activity, the power within frequency bands of interest (e.g. alpha band 8 – 13 Hz) was defined from the flattened spectra that was calculated by subtracting the aperiodic activity from the original spectra.

Statistical analysis
MEG: Exercise-specific effects in the brain oscillatory power were studied by contrasting both within- and between session changes, e.g: RUN (POST10-PRE) – CON (POST10-PRE) and utilizing a source-level cluster-based permutation test with a maximum-statistics approach with MATLAB (R2020b, The MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA, USA). 

Results

To clarify exercise-specific effects on resting-state brain oscillations in the alpha-, low-beta-, and high-beta bands, we contrasted both within- and between-session changes (e.g., RUN (POST10-PRE) – CON (POST10-PRE)). Psychophysiological measures are shown to provide context for the observed brain effects (See Figure 2A).

10 minutes post-exercise, running induced a significant decrease in alpha and low-beta power (See Figure 2B). Alpha-band decreases were observed in left frontal (prefrontal and motor-related), temporal, and insular regions. Low-beta decreases occurred in the left temporal, insular, parietal, and postcentral regions, and in the right frontal, orbitofrontal, insular, temporal, parietal, and subcentral regions. In contrast, high-beta power increased in the right sensorimotor cortex, medial superior frontal cortex, and cingulate cortex.

55 minutes post-exercise, alpha power increased in right frontal regions, including inferior and middle frontal gyri, while low-beta power decreased in right frontal and insular regions, including orbitofrontal and anterior insular cortex (See Figure 2B).

These results indicate that prolonged endurance running transiently modulates resting-state oscillations across frontal, sensorimotor, and temporal regions, with effects varying across frequency bands and recovery time.

Conclusion

Our preliminary results suggest that a 90-minute running bout induces acute, exercise-specific effects on brain oscillatory activity, mainly in sensorimotor and frontal regions, as well as near the lateral sulcus.

Psychophysiological measures (Figure 2A) show that running produces bodily responses clearly distinct from the control session of passive rest. Heart rate remains elevated even an hour after exercise, and lactate levels indicate a markedly different metabolic state. These findings underline that recovery is still ongoing and affects bodily physiology.

Acute effects in the sensorimotor areas are expected, given the sustained use of the motor system—including movement planning and execution—during the 90-minute run. Consistent with our findings, previous work showed that a fatiguing isometric task (100 × 10–12 s contractions) increased resting-state beta power in the motor cortex ^2. The observed increase in high-beta power may reflect a cortical state of enhanced inhibition. Since motor commands originate from this area, such inhibition could help reduce neuronal activity, effectively “pausing” motor output and supporting recovery of the motor system after prolonged exercise.

Frontal regions are important for evaluating the costs and benefits of continuing exercise^3 and are closely linked to movement planning and orchestration^2. After the 90-minute run, decreased power in the alpha and low-beta bands is observed in frontal areas, particularly near the lateral sulcus and insula. These oscillatory changes may reflect altered activity in frontal networks responsible for cognitive control during the fatiguing running task. Frontal regions have been highlighted especially in endurance activities ^4 and are actively engaged in processing internal bodily signals ^5, such as respiration and cardiac function, which are strongly affected by exercise. Thus, the post-exercise oscillatory responses could result from prolonged activation of frontal networks combined with modulated sensory input from the body.

After one hour, as recovery progresses, alpha power in the right frontal lobe is increased, whereas low-beta power remains decreased in the right frontal and insular regions. Interestingly, exercise-induced positive changes in affect have been linked to modulations in frontal activity, particularly in regions involved in perception and higher-order cognition.

In conclusion, our results demonstrate exercise-specific modulations in oscillatory power across several brain regions. These modulations appear to follow a distinct timeline, likely reflecting ongoing recovery processes in both the body and brain after a demanding bout of exercise. It is important to note that the effects of exercise are influenced by factors such as intensity and duration. As long-term adaptations result from the repeated physiological responses to individual exercise bouts, understanding the effects of a single exercise session on the brain is important for interpreting exercise-induced adaptations over time.
