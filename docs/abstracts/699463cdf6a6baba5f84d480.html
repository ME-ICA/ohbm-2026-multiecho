Introduction

Adolescent major depressive disorder (MDD) is a leading cause of disability and is characterized by substantial clinical heterogeneity, encompassing diverse depressive symptoms, cognitive impairments, and psychosocial dysfunction. White matter (WM) microstructure plays a critical role in neural network maturation during adolescence, and abnormalities in WM have been implicated in MDD pathophysiology. However, how WM alterations relate to multidimensional clinical, cognitive, and psychosocial profiles in adolescents remains unclear, limiting mechanistic understanding and personalized intervention strategies.

Methods

We recruited 77 drug-naïve adolescents with first-episode major depressive disorder (MDD) and 57 age- and sex-matched healthy controls (HCs). All participants underwent comprehensive clinical, cognitive, and psychosocial assessments, including the Hamilton Depression and Anxiety Rating Scales, the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test, the Adolescent Self-Rating Life Events Checklist, and the Family Environment Scale. Diffusion tensor imaging data were acquired using a 3T MRI scanner, and white matter tracts were segmented using the TractSeg framework. Fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity (MD), radial diffusivity (RD), and axial diffusivity (AD) were extracted for each tract. Group differences were examined using ANCOVA with false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Regularized generalized canonical correlation analysis (RGCCA) was then applied to investigate multivariate associations between white matter microstructure and clinical, cognitive, and psychosocial measures. The stability of canonical correlations and variable loadings was assessed using 1,000 bootstrap resamples. To determine whether the identified brain–behavior–environment associations differentiated subgroups within the MDD cohort, we performed k-means clustering using the first canonical variate pair from RGCCA as input features. The optimal number of clusters was selected using 26 indices from the NbClust package in R (version 4.1.0). Cluster stability was evaluated via bootstrap resampling (n = 1,000) using the Jaccard similarity coefficient. 

Results

Univariate analyses revealed no significant group differences in white matter (WM) measures after FDR correction. In contrast, multivariate RGCCA identified two robust brain–behavior–environment association patterns in adolescents with MDD, each characterized by a distinct WM microstructural signature. The first canonical variate—linked to interpersonal stress and adverse family environment—showed widespread reductions in FA and region-specific alterations in MD, RD, and AD, primarily involving extensive cortico–cortical and cortico–cerebellar pathways. The second canonical variate, associated with somatic and anxiety-related symptoms, demonstrated heterogeneous WM changes with both increases and decreases across regions, suggesting complex reorganization within fronto–posterior networks and cortico–striato–cerebellar circuits. K-means clustering revealed two distinct MDD subtypes (Subtype 1: n = 40; Subtype 2: n = 37), supported by the highest silhouette coefficient (0.51) and strong stability (Jaccard similarity = 0.91). Demographic characteristics did not differ between subtypes or relative to HCs. Subtype 1 exhibited higher interpersonal stress compared with Subtype 2 and HCs, whereas Subtype 2 showed higher levels of family functioning, including cohesion, emotional expression, achievement orientation, organization, and moral–religious emphasis, as well as greater independence. White matter alteration patterns differed markedly between subtypes. Subtype 1 showed only subtle and localized abnormalities, limited to left-hemispheric long-range associative tracts and characterized by mild increases in diffusivity, indicating modest microstructural disruption. In contrast, Subtype 2 exhibited widespread and pronounced WM abnormalities, encompassing language-, emotion-, and cognitive-control–related pathways, as well as fronto–parietal, visual–emotional, and premotor circuits, reflecting substantially greater neurobiological burden.

Conclusion

This study identified two adolescent MDD subtypes characterized by distinct combinations of white matter microstructural alterations and clinical symptom profiles. These findings deepen our understanding of the biological and clinical heterogeneity of adolescent depression and demonstrate that integrating neuroimaging features with multidimensional clinical, cognitive, and psychosocial measures can reveal meaningful latent structure within the disorder. By delineating robust brain–behavior–environment association patterns, this work provides a foundation for future efforts to develop biologically informed and clinically relevant classification frameworks for MDD.
