Introduction

Methamphetamine Use Disorder (MUD) is a common and debilitating neuropsychiatric disorder that responds only partially to treatment. Chronic MA use can lead to an array of abnormalities in brain structure, biochemistry and function. These problems, along with cognitive control deficits, impulsivity and cue-induced craving compound poor treatment response and the maintenance of drug use. While there is information on intrinsic resting state functional connectivity (RSFC) in abstinent MUD (one to 6 months) there are no data on the effects of MA in chronically using individuals. Trace Amine Associated Receptor 1 (TAAR1), coupled to G proteins Gs and Ga13), is expressed in dopaminergic and other monoaminergic brain regions.  While a non-functional TAAR1 allele leads to increased MA consumption in rodents,1 the situation in humans is less clear.  We2 previously reported that a synonymous SNP in a valine codon in human TAAR1 (rs8192620) at amino acid position 288 resulted in increased TAAR1 production in cell culture and was associated with self-reported increased craving in active MUD subjects.  We sought to confirm the craving result and investigate resting state functional connectivity using precision functional mapping.

Methods

Participants were recruited through the Veterans Affairs Portland Health Care System (VAPORHCS) and Oregon Health & Science University (OHSU) (Portland, Oregon, USA). The research was approved by a joint Institutional Review Board at OHSU and VAPORHCS.  Participants (n=14, age = 37.75 ± 13.06, 2 female) MUD subjects completed 2 separate sessions in a double-blind cross-over study. Either MA, 0.3 mg/kg or a matching placebo (PBO) were orally administered in two sessions separated by at least 3 days to allow for drug washout. Subjects received a baseline scan and then a scan initiated 1.5 h after receiving double blind MA or PBO.  MA serum levels were measured from saliva collected before and after the scan.  Ten subjects (Fig. 1) with a minimum of 30 minutes resting state volumes were included in further analysis of within network connectivity (WNC).

Scanning was performed on a 3.0-T Siemens Prisma Scanner using a 32-channel head coil at the Advanced Imaging Research Center at OHSU. During each scan, high resolution T1w and T2w images and field maps were obtained. BOLD data (TR: 800 ms, TE: 0.03, slice thickness: 2.4mm, flip angle: 52°) for RSFC were collected in 5 minute blocks FIRMM software in real-time to collect 30 to 45 min worth of low motion (FD<0.2mm) volumes.  BOLD data for each participant was processed similarly to the ABCD-BIDS pipeline. Time series data from each session were treated as separate tasks to ensure that BOLD data was mapped to an identical surface mesh generated from a common T2w image.

Network templates were produced from 164 participants in a separate study using the template matching procedure. This resulted in 14 different network templates.  Precision functional maps were constructed for each participant. The spatial similarity (eta²) at each grayordinate was calculated to each of the network templates and, in each individual participant, the grayordinate was assigned to the network template that had the highest eta² value. 

Craving was rated using the Stimulant Craving Questionnaire (STCQ) before each baseline scan, immediately after administering the treatment and after the post scan. TAAR1 genotype was determined from DNA extracted from buccal cells using performed on an Applied Biosystem (ABI, Foster City, CA) StepOnePlus real-time PCR system using an ABI Taqman SNP genotyping assay for rs8192620 (Assay ID: C_25962567_10, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). 

Statistical analyses were performed using SYSTAT (v13.1, Grafiti, LLC), IBM SPSS for Windows (v30; IBM Corp.) and R statistical software version (v4.5.1; R Core Team, 2025).  We performed multivariate general linear models with all within network connectivity measures as dependent variables in an initial analysis, followed by mixed effects analyses to confirm significant effects on individual networks.  Our primary analytic tool was the linear mixed model, with fixed effects of interest (diagnosis, treatment, genotype, age, sex ) as primary predictors along with subject as a random effect.  

Results

As hypothesized, chronic MA subjects with the CV TAAR1 genotype (7 subjects, homozygotes and heterozygotes combined) expressed higher craving on the STCQ than WT 7 subjects) (3.76 ± 0.397 vs 1.850 ± 0.396; Figure 2A). A linear mixed-effects model with random intercepts for Subject and an AR1 repeated structure showed a significant main effect of genotype on STCQ, F(1, 24.5) = 5.62, p = .026. Neither the main effect of time, F(5, 65.99) = 0.99, p = .429, nor the genotype X time interaction, F(5, 65.99) = 1.07, p = .386, was significant.  Similarly, neither age nor sex nor (natural log transformed) MA level were significant covariates.

The fMRI analysis recovered 15 networks for which we calculated within network connectivity (WNC). A general linear model (fixed effects only) of the effect of genotype on the set of within network connectivity measures was multivariate significant (Wilk's Λ = 0.269, p = 0.001) found Auditory, Parietal Occipital and Dorsal Somatomotor connectivity higher in the CV subgroup, although none of these differences were significant in a mixed effects analysis.

 We examined the effect of genotype on the relationship of craving to within-network connectivity with a multivariate general linear model (4 craving scores per subject, one after each scan) and found a significant effect of genotype on the relationship of STCQ score with WNC (Wilk’s Λ = 3.2, p < 0.009) in the seven networks (Auditory, Cingulo-Opercular, Dorsal Attention, Default Mode, Fronto-Parietal, Parieto-Occipital and Dorsal Somatomotor.  Interactions in three of these networks (Cingulo-Opercular, Dorsal Attention and Dorsal Somato-Motor) were significant at p < 0.01 when we performed a mixed effects regression analysis with a random intercept and AR1 variance structure (B,C,D Figure 2).  In all cases, increased craving was associated with increased WNC in the CV subjects, but with decreased connectivity in the WT. 

Although most subjects were scanned with high MA levels (Fig 1), two had low enough initial levels to test the effect of acute MA on WNC.  While two subjects did not allow statistical evaluation, inspection of pre-post WNC indicated that placebo administration had no effect on connectivity, but MA lowered network connectivity in Auditory, Cingulo- Opercular, Dorsal Attention, Fronto-Parietal, Middle Temporal, Salience, Dorsal Somatomotor, SCAN and Ventral Attention networks.  

Conclusion

These findings confirm the previous finding that   the common variant TAAR1 genotype differentially modulates stimulant craving.  The results further demonstrate that craving dependence on WNC responds differentially to TAAR1 genotype.  Craving increases in the CV subjects with greater WNC, while it decreases with WNC in WT individuals.  This suggests that the cerebral mechanism linking network connectivity to behavior differs between the groups.  We hypothesize that increased rates of translation of the CV results in increased TAAR1 signaling in response to chronic MA use (a TAAR1 agonist) and results in amplification of TAAR1 signaling and subsequent alteration of WNC.  While the mechanism of this effect is not known, we note that chronic MA use results in decreased dopamine transporter (DAT) numbers in striatum, while cocaine use does not.3  This could be a result of MA-induced amplification TAAR1 induction of DAT trafficking.4  Confirmation of this mechanism will require further investigation.

These findings have implications for treatment of MUD, as individuals with the CV or WT genotypes may be differentially responsive to TAAR1 ligands.
