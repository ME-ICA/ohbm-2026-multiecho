Introduction

Large-scale functional connectivity (FC) is increasingly viewed as a dynamic process rather than a static property of brain organization. Even during rest, brain networks reconfigure over time, giving rise to structured patterns of dynamic functional connectivity (dFC) that are reproducible within individuals and informative about behavior and clinical state [1]. Characterizing these temporal dynamics is therefore essential for understanding both normative brain function and its alteration in psychiatric disorders [2].

Many existing approaches to dFC, including sliding-window analyses, state-based clustering, and sequence modeling, have provided valuable descriptive insights [3]. However, these methods often neglect temporal structure or rely on black-box models with limited interpretability. As a result, the principles governing transitions between connectivity states and individual differences in network regulation remain poorly understood.

Optimal control theory provides a principled framework for modeling brain dynamics as the evolution of a low-dimensional system constrained by intrinsic dynamics and control costs. While control-theoretic approaches have been applied to structural and task-based networks, their relevance for resting-state dFC and psychiatric symptoms has received limited attention.

Major depressive disorder (MDD) has been associated with altered functional connectivity and reduced network flexibility, yet most prior work has focused on static connectivity or categorical group comparisons [4]. Here, we test whether resting-state dFC can be modeled as an optimal control process and whether subject-specific control parameters relate to depressive symptom severity. We further compare this interpretable, theory-driven approach with recurrent neural network models to assess whether optimal control modeling can capture brain dynamics while offering mechanistic insight into depression-related alterations.

Methods

Resting-state fMRI data were analyzed from 2,770 adults, including 823 individuals diagnosed with MDD, drawn from the Marburg–Münster Affective Disorders Cohort Study [5]. Imaging was conducted at two sites in Germany: the University of Marburg and the University of Münster. At Marburg, data were acquired on a 3 Tesla Siemens Tim Trio scanner with a 12-channel head coil, and at Münster on a 3 Tesla Siemens Prisma scanner with a 20-channel head coil. High-resolution T1-weighted structural images were collected using a magnetization-prepared rapid gradient echo sequence with 1.0 mm isotropic resolution. Resting-state fMRI data were acquired during an 8-minute eyes-closed scan.

Structural images underwent bias field correction and brain extraction. Resting-state fMRI preprocessing was performed using FSL and included motion correction, brain extraction, spatial smoothing with a 5 mm full-width at half-maximum Gaussian kernel, high-pass temporal filtering (100 s cutoff), and registration to individual structural images and standard MNI space. To reduce noise, within-participant spatial independent component analysis was performed using MELODIC, followed by automated classification and removal of noise components with FSL-FIX, mitigating motion-related, physiological, and scanner-related artifacts.

Following preprocessing, BOLD time series were extracted from 32 cortical regions defined by the CONN network atlas, spanning eight canonical resting-state networks. dFC was estimated at each time point using instantaneous phase synchrony. Regional BOLD signals were transformed into analytic signals via the Hilbert transform, and framewise connectivity between regions was defined as the cosine of instantaneous phase differences. This approach yields temporally resolved connectivity estimates without the use of sliding windows.

To obtain a compact representation of dFC, framewise connectivity patterns were clustered using k-means clustering, identifying four recurrent connectivity states. Each time point was then embedded as a continuous four-dimensional mixture over these states using a similarity-based weighting scheme, resulting in a low-dimensional time series that captures gradual transitions between connectivity configurations.

The temporal evolution of these state-mixture embeddings was modeled using inverse optimal control. Linear state dynamics were assumed, regulated by a Linear–Quadratic Regulator (LQR) in which control inputs govern transitions between connectivity states while minimizing a cost that penalizes both deviations from preferred states and control effort. Subject-specific feedback dynamics and control cost parameters were inferred via inverse optimization, yielding interpretable measures of how strongly individual brain dynamics are regulated.

As a benchmark, gated recurrent unit (GRU) neural networks were trained on the same state-mixture embeddings to predict next-time-point connectivity states. GRU models were optimized using standard training procedures and served as a flexible, non-interpretable comparison for sequence prediction.

Model performance was evaluated on held-out data using Pearson correlation, coefficient of determination (R²), and mean squared error (MSE) between predicted and observed state-mixture vectors. In addition, the number of transitions between dominant connectivity states was computed for each participant as a summary measure of dynamic switching. Depressive symptom severity was assessed using the Beck Depression Inventory–II (BDI-II), and associations between symptom severity, transition measures, and inferred control parameters were examined at the individual level.

Results

Both the inverse optimal control model and the GRU benchmark achieved high predictive accuracy for next-time-point dFC states. Across participants, median held-out Pearson correlation between predicted and observed state-mixture vectors was approximately 0.90 for both approaches, with comparable performance across additional metrics including coefficient of determination (R²) and MSE. No systematic advantage of the GRU over the control-theoretic model was observed, indicating that the interpretable inverse optimal control framework captured the essential temporal structure of dFC as effectively as the flexible deep learning baseline.

Prediction accuracy varied substantially across individuals. For both models, performance decreased as the number of transitions between dominant connectivity states increased, such that participants exhibiting more frequent state switching showed lower predictability. This relationship suggests that richer or more exploratory connectivity dynamics are inherently harder to predict, regardless of modeling approach, and highlights inter-individual variability in the temporal organization of resting-state networks.

Behavioral relevance emerged primarily at the level of dynamic properties rather than categorical diagnosis. Higher depressive symptom severity, as measured by the BDI-II, was associated with fewer inter-state transitions, indicating reduced switching between connectivity states. In addition, higher BDI-II scores were linked to larger inferred control penalties in the inverse optimal control model, reflecting greater estimated effort required to steer connectivity dynamics away from preferred states. Together, these findings are consistent with “stickier” and more strongly regulated network dynamics in individuals with more severe depressive symptoms.

Notably, these continuous associations with symptom severity were stronger and more consistent than group-level comparisons between healthy controls and individuals diagnosed with major depressive disorder. While modest group differences were observed in some dynamic measures, symptom-related effects accounted for more variance than diagnostic category alone. This pattern suggests that individual differences in dynamic brain regulation may be better captured by dimensional measures of depressive severity than by binary diagnostic labels.

Across analyses, relationships between depressive symptoms, state-transition rates, and inferred control parameters were robust to model choice and persisted when accounting for overall prediction accuracy. These results demonstrate that interpretable control-theoretic parameters derived from resting-state dFC are meaningfully related to behavioral variation and provide information beyond standard diagnostic distinctions.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that resting-state dynamic functional connectivity can be effectively modeled as a low-dimensional optimal control process. An interpretable inverse optimal control framework achieved predictive performance comparable to state-of-the-art recurrent neural network models, indicating that much of the temporal structure of brain-wide connectivity dynamics can be captured by simple, principled control mechanisms rather than highly parameterized black-box models.

Beyond predictive accuracy, the control-theoretic approach provides meaningful parameters that characterize how strongly individual brain dynamics are regulated. Variation in inferred control penalties and state-transition behavior was systematically related to depressive symptom severity, with higher symptom burden associated with fewer connectivity state transitions and greater estimated effort required to reconfigure brain networks. These findings suggest that depression may be characterized by altered controllability of large-scale brain dynamics, manifesting as “stickier” network states that are harder to shift over time.

Importantly, associations with depressive symptoms were stronger than categorical distinctions between healthy controls and individuals diagnosed with major depressive disorder, highlighting the value of dimensional, symptom-based approaches to understanding brain–behavior relationships. By focusing on individual differences in dynamic regulation rather than static connectivity or diagnostic labels, control-theoretic modeling offers a complementary perspective on psychopathology that aligns with transdiagnostic and precision psychiatry frameworks.

More broadly, this work bridges systems neuroscience and clinical neuroimaging by linking dynamic connectomics to interpretable dynamical systems models. The inverse optimal control framework used here is general and can be extended to other cognitive states, clinical populations, and imaging modalities. By providing mechanistically meaningful parameters that scale with behavior, optimal control modeling represents a promising avenue for understanding how brain network dynamics support mental health and how their dysregulation may contribute to psychiatric disorders.
