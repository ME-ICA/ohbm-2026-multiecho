Introduction

Understanding the neural substrates of complex social behaviours poses a substantial challenge, particularly when such behaviours emerge following focal brain damage. Lesion studies have historically provided critical insights into brain-behaviour relationships; however, most behaviours of interest do not localize to single regions. Instead, they appear to arise from disturbances to distributed neural systems supporting cognitive control, social cognition, and affective regulation. This is especially relevant for behaviours that are ethically, socially, and clinically sensitive, where simplistic localization risks overinterpretation.

Lesion network mapping has emerged as a powerful framework for addressing this challenge. Rather than focusing exclusively on the anatomical location of a lesion, it examines the functional connectivity profile of the damaged region using normative resting-state fMRI data1. Converging evidence suggests that lesions associated with similar behavioural syndromes, despite being anatomically heterogeneous, often map onto common functional networks. This network-based perspective provides a principled way to reconcile anatomical variability with behavioural specificity.

A seminal application2 of this method demonstrated that lesions temporally associated with criminal behaviour were functionally connected to a shared network involving frontal and subcortical regions implicated in decision-making, impulse control, and social behaviour3, proposing that disruption of specific large-scale networks may increase vulnerability to maladaptive behavioural outcomes under certain conditions. 

Building on this framework, the present study applies lesion network mapping to a new cohort of patients with documented temporal associations between focal brain lesions and criminal behaviour, and aims to identify neural systems that may be disproportionately affected in these individuals, without implying inevitability or moral determinism.

Methods

Data acquisition

In order to perform Lesion Network Mapping, lesions from 7 defendants were used as seeds for a seed-based connectivity analysis of resting-state fMRI data. Regions with activity positively or negatively correlated with activity at each lesion site were identified. Finally, lesion network maps from each patient were overlapped to determine brain regions significantly connected to all or most lesions.

Scanning was carried out at the IRCCS San Camillo Hospital, Venice (Italy), using a 3T Ingenia Scanner with a 32-channel receive head coil. High-resolution 3D T1-weighted MRI scans were acquired using a magnetization-prepared rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE) sequence (TR=8.1ms, TE=3.7ms, flip-angle=8°, voxel dimensions=1mm isotropic). Approximately 10 minutes of resting-state functional images were collected using a T2*-weighted echo planar imaging (EPI) sequence with whole-brain coverage [TR=1000 ms, TE=23ms, flip-angle=50°, Multiband acceleration=4, voxel dimensions=3mm isotropic], along with diffusion images for distortion correction.

Preprocessing and analysis

Analyses of fMRI data were performed using CONN4 (22.v2407) and SPM (v25.25.01.02).

Functional and anatomical data were preprocessed using a modular preprocessing pipeline including realignment with correction of susceptibility distortion interactions, outlier detection, direct segmentation and MNI-space normalization, and smoothing (6 mm FWHM). 

Functional data were denoised using a standard denoising pipeline including the regression of potential confounding effects characterized by white matter timeseries, CSF timeseries, motion parameters and their first order derivatives, outlier scans, session effects and their first order derivatives, and linear trends within each functional run, followed by bandpass frequency filtering of the BOLD timeseries between 0.008 Hz and 0.09 Hz. The effective degrees of freedom after denoising averaged 96.1 across subjects.

Seed-based connectivity maps were estimated characterizing the patterns of functional connectivity with 39 ROIs from 7 patients. Functional connectivity strength was represented by Fisher-transformed bivariate correlation coefficients from a weighted general linear model (weighted-GLM, defined separately for each pair of seed and target areas, modeling the association between their BOLD signal timeseries. In order to compensate for possible transient magnetization effects at the beginning of each run, individual scans were weighted by a step function convolved with an SPM canonical hemodynamic response function and rectified.

Group-level analyses were performed using a General Linear Model. For each individual voxel a separate GLM was estimated, with first-level connectivity measures at this voxel as dependent variables, and groups or other subject-level identifiers as independent variables. Voxel-level hypotheses were evaluated using multivariate parametric statistics with random-effects across subjects and sample covariance estimation across multiple measurements. Inferences were performed at the level of individual clusters (groups of contiguous voxels). Cluster-level inferences were based on parametric statistics from Gaussian Random Field theory. Results were thresholded using a combination of a cluster-forming p < .001 voxel-level threshold, and a familywise corrected p-FDR < .05 cluster-size threshold.

Lesion Network Mapping

Positive and negative correlations were thresholded at T > ±12 to create a binarized map of functionally connected regions to each patient’s lesion site (p < 10-17 uncorrected). Maps from all patients were combined to form a group map showing the number of patients with lesions functionally connected with each individual voxel2.

Results

Seven patients with a documented temporal relationship between brain lesions and criminal behavior were identified. The lesions were anatomically heterogeneous, involving but not limited to the frontal gyri, cingulate cortex, insula, pre- and postcentral gyri, temporal gyri, and cerebellum.

For each lesion, functional connectivity was estimated using a normative resting-state fMRI database. Regions showing significant positive or negative correlations (thresholded at T > ± 12) with each lesion site were identified (Figure 1). Individual lesion connectivity maps were then binarized and combined to generate group overlap maps, highlighting regions functionally connected to most or all lesion locations.

Across patients, lesions associated with criminal behavior showed positive functional connectivity with the medial frontal gyrus; inferior, middle, and superior frontal gyri; caudate nucleus; pre- and postcentral gyri; insula; temporal gyri; lentiform nucleus; thalamus; cuneus; and cerebellum. In contrast, negative functional connectivity was observed primarily with the precuneus (Figure 1).

Conclusion

Results indicate that lesions temporally associated with criminal behaviour, although anatomically diverse, converge on a shared functional network when examined through lesion network mapping. This convergence supports a network-level interpretation of behavioural vulnerability, consistent with prior work2 demonstrating that focal damage can disrupt distributed systems underlying complex social and executive functions.

Across patients, lesion sites were positively functionally connected with medial and lateral frontal cortices, striatal and thalamic structures, insula, and sensorimotor regions. These areas collectively participate in circuits involved in action selection, behavioural monitoring, interoceptive awareness, and cognitive control. Disruption within such networks may plausibly alter the balance between impulse generation and regulatory processes, particularly in emotionally or socially charged contexts.

Importantly, the observed convergence does not imply that damage to this network is sufficient, or even necessary, for criminal behaviour to occur. Rather, the findings suggest that lesions affecting nodes functionally embedded within these systems may bias behavioural regulation in ways that interact with premorbid personality traits, psychosocial stressors, and situational factors. 

The observation of negative functional connectivity between lesion sites and the precuneus is noteworthy. The precuneus is a central hub of the default mode network, implicated in self-referential processing, autobiographical memory, and moral reasoning. Reduced or altered coupling between regulatory frontal systems and default-mode regions could reflect disruptions in the integration of internal models with external behavioural demands. While speculative, such alterations may influence reflective processes that ordinarily constrain socially inappropriate actions. However, caution is warranted. Negative functional connectivity in normative datasets does not directly translate to inhibitory or antagonistic relationships at the individual level, particularly in patients with structural lesions. These findings should therefore be interpreted as descriptive features of network organisation rather than mechanistic explanations.

Conclusions must be interpreted in light of the study’s limitations. First, the sample size is necessarily small, reflecting the rarity and ethical complexity of well-documented cases with clear temporal relationships between lesions and behavioural change. Second, lesion network mapping relies on normative resting-state data, which may not capture individual-specific network reorganisation following injury. Third, the categorisation of “criminal behaviour” encompasses heterogeneous actions influenced by legal, cultural, and contextual factors that cannot be fully operationalised neurobiologically. Moreover, the temporal association between lesion onset and behaviour does not establish causality. Behavioural changes may emerge gradually, be modulated by environmental responses to illness, or reflect pre-existing vulnerabilities unmasked by neural injury.

Given the sensitivity of the topic, it is essential to emphasise that these findings do not support deterministic or reductionist interpretations of criminal behaviour. Neural network disruptions should not be construed as explanations that negate agency or responsibility5. Instead, they offer a framework for understanding how brain injury may alter behavioural constraints, potentially informing clinical assessment, rehabilitation, and risk management in a nuanced and ethically responsible manner.
