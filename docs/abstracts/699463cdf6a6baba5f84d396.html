Introduction

Psychosocial stress alters cognition, yet its effects on time perception remain poorly understood. Stress distorts perceived duration, often by making intervals feel longer, but the neural mechanisms remain unclear.¹ A fundamental question concerns whether stress globally disrupts shared magnitude-processing systems, or selectively targets specific domains like temporal cognition.

Temporal and spatial perception recruit partially overlapping frontoparietal networks, consistent with theories proposing a common neural code for magnitude.² Dopaminergic signaling within frontostriatal and prefrontal circuits strongly modulates interval timing, and these same systems are highly sensitive to psychosocial stress.³ Neurobiological models suggest stress reallocates cognitive resources toward evaluation and threat monitoring, potentially at the expense of internally generated representations such as interval timing. While initial evidence demonstrates stress effects on timing,⁴ critical gaps remain: Does stress uniformly degrade magnitude processing across domains, or selectively disrupt temporal versus spatial cognition within overlapping networks? Moreover, the molecular substrates of individual stress responsivity remain unexplored.

Here, we tested whether psychosocial stress selectively disrupts neural timing representations using task-based fMRI combined with endocrine and epigenetic profiling. Participants performed temporal and spatial reproduction tasks under controlled and stressed conditions while cortisol responses were tracked. We hypothesized that psychosocial stress would impair timing-specific frontoparietal networks while sparing spatial processing, and explored whether individual variability in stress- and dopamine-related gene methylation modulates neural stress effects. This multimodal approach tests competing predictions regarding domain-general versus domain-specific stress effects on magnitude cognition.

Methods

Participants: Forty-five healthy right-handed adults completed scanning (23M; age 23.73±3.85). Sessions were scheduled 12:30–16:00 to minimize diurnal cortisol variation. Participants refrained from exercise, tooth-brushing, eating, caffeine, and alcohol for 24h pre-session (compliance assessed via checklist). Females were scanned during luteal phase; none reported oral contraceptive use. Participants using medications affecting the nervous/endocrine systems were excluded.

Task Paradigm: During fMRI, participants performed visual reproduction with two trial types: temporal and spatial. Each trial began with a 1s cue (clock or ruler icon) indicating whether to reproduce the duration or the size of the stimulus. Stimuli were centrally presented circles on gray background (durations: 2.0, 2.5, 3.0, 3.5s; radii: 100, 125, 150, 175px).

  • Temporal trials: Circle appeared for target duration; participants reproduced it by button-press to re-present the circle and second press to stop when elapsed time matched target.
  • Spatial trials: After target offset, participants reproduced size by button-press to initiate circle expansion and second press to stop at target radius.

Each run: 40 trials (20 time, 20 space). Response window capped at 200% of target to maintain constant run duration. ITI/ISI: 0.8, 1.0, 1.2s (pseudo-randomized, log-distributed).

Stress Induction: Psychosocial stress was induced using evaluative threat adapted from the Montreal Imaging Stress Task.5 Participants completed four runs: two control (no feedback), then two stressed. Stressed runs displayed a mock performance bar (always below "group average") updated after each trial, plus brief "low performance" warnings (5×/run). Unfriendly verbal warnings were delivered between stressed runs with observers present. Due to cortisol kinetics (slow rise, prolonged elevation), control runs always preceded stressed runs to prevent carry-over; subjective stress ratings and cortisol responses validated manipulation effectiveness.

Cortisol Sampling: Five saliva samples collected (tA: entering scanner, tB:pre-task, tC:pre-stress, tD:pre-exit, tE:post-recovery). Cortisol assayed via ELISA; individual response curves computed. Participants were classified as responders (n=22) versus non-responders (n=23) based on cortisol change direction.

DNA Methylation: Pyrosequencing quantified methylation of five genes: glucocorticoid receptor (NR3C1) and dopamine-related TH (synthesis), SLC6A3 (transport), COMT (degradation), DRD2 (receptor); motivated by established dopaminergic modulation of interval timing and stress-sensitive frontostriatal circuitry.

MRI Acquisition: 3T Siemens Magnetom Trio, 32-channel head coil. T1-weighted: 0.9mm isotropic, TR 2150ms, TE 2.99ms. Functional EPI: TR 1000ms, TE 34.4ms, 2.10mm isotropic, 60 slices, multiband factor 6. Each run: 486 volumes (4 runs = 1,944 volumes). Standard SPM12 preprocessing steps applied.

Statistical Analysis: First-level GLMs modelled task conditions (canonical HRF, high-pass filtering, motion regressors). Group-level voxel-wise repeated-measures ANOVA tested Task × Condition effects; significance: FWE-corrected p<0.05. Percent signal change extracted from interaction clusters for visualization only (no re-testing). A second set of a priori ROIs (SMA, insula, IPS, hippocampus, caudate; 10mm spheres) was examined for endocrine/epigenetic associations. Mixed-effects models tested Gene × Task modulation of stress-related PSC changes (FDR correction); molecular analyses planned as exploratory hypothesis-generation given sample size.

Results

Manipulation Check: Stress manipulation increased subjective ratings during stressed versus control runs. Salivary cortisol showed marked inter-individual variability. Post-hoc classification: responders (n=22) versus non-responders (n=23) based on cortisol change direction. No sex differences in cortisol response (F(1,38)=0.774, p=0.385); baseline cortisol did not differ between groups (t=0.869, p=0.390).

Behavioral Performance: Across tasks and conditions, responses showed systematic underestimation consistent with central tendency effects in supra-second reproduction. A 2×2×2×2 mixed ANOVA (Task × Condition × Group × Sex) revealed no robust main effects of stress after correction. While a four-way interaction emerged (F(1,39)=4.43, p=0.042), follow-up contrasts yielded no significant stress effects within task/group/sex combinations after FDR correction (all adjusted ps>0.3). This pattern suggests stress-related neural changes can precede or occur independently of overt behavioral impairment.

Whole-Brain fMRI: Main Effects: Voxel-wise analyses (FWE-corrected p<0.05) revealed:

Timing main effect (Time>Space): Robust activation in supplementary motor area, caudate, intraparietal sulcus, superior temporal gyrus, cerebellum, and insula, replicating established timing networks.

Stress main effect: Widespread activation including visual cortex, insula, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, bilateral posterior cingulate, temporal cortex, and hippocampus, consistent with stress-regulatory networks.

Group effect: Responders versus non-responders differed only in the occipital cortex.

Whole-Brain fMRI:  The Condition × Task interaction was the only interaction surviving whole-brain FWE correction. Stress was associated with reduced activation during temporal reproduction in a distributed frontoparietal network: inferior frontal gyrus, ventrolateral prefrontal cortex, intraparietal sulcus, and superior temporal gyrus. Critically, activation during spatial reproduction was comparatively preserved in these regions. Percent signal change plots confirmed selective timing-network disruption under stress, with spatial processing unaffected. Visual association cortex showed different patterns reflecting task-specific visual demands rather than magnitude processing.

Endocrine and Epigenetic Findings: Molecular analyses were conducted as exploratory hypothesis-generation. DNA methylation did not reliably predict cortisol reactivity across genes (all p>0.25; FDR q>0.84). The largest non-significant trend: TH methylation (β=0.168, p=0.125).

Brain-methylation correlations (uncorrected): Left caudate PSC during stressed-time positively correlated with SLC6A3 methylation (r=0.44, p<0.01); left hippocampal PSC during control negatively correlated with COMT methylation (r=−0.34, p<0.05).

Mixed-effects models testing Gene × Task modulation of stress-related PSC changes yielded nominal effects that did not survive FDR correction: NR3C1 in left insula (p=0.039, q=0.321; robust to outlier removal), SLC6A3 in left caudate (p=0.012, q=0.290; outlier-driven) and left insula (p=0.030, q=0.321). These findings warrant replication in larger samples but suggest a framework in which glucocorticoid receptor and dopamine transporter methylation may modulate timing-network stress responses.

Conclusion

This study provides convergent evidence that psychosocial stress selectively disrupts neural representations of time while sparing spatial processing, despite partial overlap in supporting networks. Rather than inducing global suppression of magnitude-related cognition, stress was associated with domain-specific reduction of frontoparietal activity during temporal reproduction, including inferior frontal, ventrolateral prefrontal, intraparietal, and superior temporal cortices, while spatial reproduction remained largely unaffected. These findings constrain theoretical accounts by demonstrating that psychosocial stress can selectively target temporal representations within overlapping magnitude-processing systems.

The observed Task × Condition interaction provides direct neural evidence for selective temporal vulnerability under stress. This dissociation challenges proposals of a fully unified neural code for time and space by showing differential stress modulation within shared frontoparietal networks. This pattern aligns with models proposing that stress reallocates cognitive and neural resources toward evaluation and regulation, reducing availability for internally generated temporal judgments. Notably, spatial processing, which may rely more on automatic or earlier-stage sensory mechanisms, remained relatively intact.

Behavioral performance did not show robust stress-related impairments, highlighting dissociation between neural sensitivity and overt performance. Systematic underestimation across conditions reflects central tendency effects commonly observed in supra-second reproduction. This neural-behavioral dissociation suggests stress-related network disruptions may precede measurable performance deficits, underscoring the value of neural measures for detecting subtle cognitive changes.

Cortisol responses confirmed effective stress induction but showed substantial inter-individual variability. Classification into responders versus non-responders did not reveal robust group differences in primary neural effects, suggesting selective timing-network disruption reflects stress-related modulation observable across participants rather than a subgroup-specific response.

Exploratory epigenetic analyses suggest individual differences in glucocorticoid and dopaminergic signalling pathways may modulate timing-network stress responses. Higher glucocorticoid receptor (NR3C1) methylation was nominally associated with greater stress-related insular activity during temporal judgments, consistent with altered stress regulation (effect survived outlier removal but not multiple-comparison correction). Nominal associations involving dopamine-related genes align with established dopaminergic modulation of interval timing. However, as no gene-brain associations survived FDR correction, these findings remain hypothesis-generating and require replication in larger samples.

Together, these findings advance understanding of how psychosocial stress dynamically reshapes human cognition. By integrating fMRI with endocrine and epigenetic profiling, this work identifies neural timing networks as a selective locus of stress vulnerability and highlights biological variability as a potential modulator. The results emphasize that stress does not uniformly impair magnitude cognition but can selectively disrupt internally driven processes such as temporal reproduction. Future research should examine whether similar selective vulnerabilities extend to other temporal processes, test whether interventions targeting stress regulation can restore timing-network function, and clarify the molecular mechanisms linking glucocorticoid and dopaminergic signalling to timing-network stress sensitivity.
