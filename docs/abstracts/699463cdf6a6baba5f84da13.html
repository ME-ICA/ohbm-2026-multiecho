Introduction

Machine learning models that predict “brain age” from fMRI are increasingly used as biomarkers of neurodevelopment, aging, and disease risk, with the gap between predicted and chronological age often interpreted as “accelerated” or “resilient” brain aging¹. However, reported accuracy varies widely across studies because functional-connectivity features are highly sensitive to site, scanner, sequence, and paradigm differences (rest vs task), and because benchmarking protocols are often inconsistent (different splits, baselines, and leakage-prone evaluation)⁴,⁵. As large multi-study datasets become the norm, there is a need for a simple, reproducible benchmark that quantifies (i) in-distribution performance under a clean train/validation/test protocol, (ii) the impact of rest vs task acquisitions, and (iii) the drop in performance under true external generalization.

Here, we conduct a large-scale benchmark on classical machine-learning approaches for functional brain-age prediction using parcelwise functional connectivity features derived from a consistent preprocessing pipeline and harmonized using a Consistent Reference External Batch (CREB) bundle learned on training data and applied to all evaluation sets. We compare principal component regression (PCR), support vector regression (SVR), ElasticNet, and a residual-learning PCR+XGBoost hybrid, and evaluate each model across train/validation/test splits as well as independent external cohorts. Performance is summarized with MAE, correlation, and R², and interpreted relative to a simple constant-age baseline with statistical testing, providing a practical reference point for future functional brain-age models and a direct estimate of the generalization gap despite harmonization.

Methods

Methods. Data and splits: We benchmarked functional brain-age prediction from fMRI across multiple public studies with Rest and Task acquisitions. Data were organized into Train (Rest, Task), Validation (Rest, Task), Test (Rest, Task), and independent external cohorts (Aging: Rest; CamCAN: Rest and Movie).

Preprocessing and features: Preprocessing matched our CREB pipeline (fMRIPrep v23.2.3 + XCP-D v0.10.5). Frames with framewise displacement (FD)>0.5 mm were censored/regressed; data were band-pass filtered (0–0.15 Hz, order 2) and smoothed (8 mm FWHM). Parcelwise time series were extracted with the 4S456 atlas (456 parcels). For each run we computed the 456×456 Pearson-correlation functional-connectivity (FC) matrix and vectorized the upper triangle as features. FC feature matrices were stored as wide Parquet tables; predictors were all non-metadata columns (excluding at minimum filename, age, SITE, and Sex_F). Rows with missing age or any NaNs in predictors were removed.

Harmonization: All FC features were harmonized using a CREB bundle learned on Train-Rest+Train-Task and then applied to all other splits. CREB was fit with biological covariates (age, sex) to preserve age-related variance while estimating additive and multiplicative site effects from training data only; the learned bundle was then used to harmonize each evaluation set without joint access to other splits.

Training protocol: To reduce selection leakage, we used two models per algorithm. Model 1 trained on Train-Rest and was evaluated on Val-Rest. Model 2 retrained on Train-Rest+Val-Rest and was evaluated on Test-Rest, Test-Task, Train/Val-Task, and both independent cohorts.

Models: (1) Principal component regression (PCR): IncrementalPCA was fit in chunks with n_components=min(n_samples,n_features); LinearRegression was fit on PCA scores; inference was chunked. (2) Support vector regression (SVR): StandardScaler was fit on training data and applied to evaluation sets; hyperparameters were selected on Train-Rest via successive-halving grid search (3-fold CV; R²) over kernel, C, gamma, and epsilon, then refit for Model 1 and Model 2. (3) ElasticNet: alpha and l1_ratio were selected via successive-halving grid search (5-fold CV; negative MAE) and refit for Model 1 and Model 2. (4) PCR+XGBoost hybrid: a PCA+LinearRegression model was fit first; residuals (y−ŷ_linear) were modeled with an XGBRegressor trained on the raw FC features (n_estimators=2000, max_depth=3, learning_rate=0.05, subsample=0.6, colsample_bytree=0.6, min_child_weight=5, reg_alpha=0.5, reg_lambda=1.0, tree_method='hist', max_bin=64). Final predictions were ŷ=ŷ_linear+ŷ_resid. (5) ZeroR baseline: for each dataset, predictions were the dataset’s mean age (within-set mean predictor).

Evaluation and statistics: Primary performance was mean absolute error (MAE, years); Pearson r and R² were also reported. For each dataset/model, improvement over the ZeroR baseline was assessed using a one-sample t-test on per-subject absolute errors with H0: mean(|ŷ−y|)=MAE_ZeroR; significance was annotated on boxplots.

Results

Results were computed on CREB-harmonized functional-connectivity features. Across internal splits, principal component regression (PCR) and ElasticNet were the most accurate and stable, support vector regression (SVR) was consistently weaker, and a PCR+XGBoost residual hybrid did not improve over the linear baselines. In Fig. 1, boxplots of absolute error show PCR achieving the lowest median error across train/validation/test groupings (with study-level dispersion). Asterisks in Fig. 1 mark one-sample t-tests against the ZeroR dummy baseline; across splits and external cohorts, models significantly outperformed ZeroR (p<0.001). Task data generally increased error and reduced correlation relative to rest.

Within-distribution performance: On Train–Task, PCR achieved a mean absolute error (MAE) of 5.79 years with a Pearson correlation (r) of 0.923 and an R-squared (R²) of 0.850; ElasticNet achieved an MAE of 6.30 years with Pearson r of 0.909 and R² of 0.823; and SVR achieved an MAE of 7.43 years with Pearson r of 0.883 and R² of 0.755 (ZeroR MAE 16.40 years). On Validation–Rest, PCR achieved an MAE of 6.98 years with Pearson r of 0.897 and R² of 0.804, while SVR achieved an MAE of 8.42 years with Pearson r of 0.878 and R² of 0.729 (ZeroR 18.16 years). On Validation–Task, PCR achieved an MAE of 6.14 years with Pearson r of 0.917 and R² of 0.840; ElasticNet achieved an MAE of 6.48 years with Pearson r of 0.908 and R² of 0.824; and SVR achieved an MAE of 7.92 years with Pearson r of 0.884 and R² of 0.742 (ZeroR 16.64 years). On Test–Rest, PCR achieved an MAE of 6.91 years with Pearson r of 0.898 and R² of 0.806; ElasticNet was nearly identical (MAE 6.94 years, Pearson r 0.899, R² 0.807); the hybrid achieved an MAE of 7.16 years with Pearson r of 0.891 and R² of 0.794; and SVR achieved an MAE of 8.15 years with Pearson r of 0.879 and R² of 0.736 (ZeroR 17.50 years). On Test–Task, errors increased: PCR achieved an MAE of 7.74 years with Pearson r of 0.876 and R² of 0.766; ElasticNet achieved an MAE of 7.85 years with Pearson r of 0.873 and R² of 0.762; the hybrid achieved an MAE of 8.00 years with Pearson r of 0.869 and R² of 0.753; and SVR achieved an MAE of 8.90 years with Pearson r of 0.858 and R² of 0.703 (ZeroR 16.88 years).

External generalization degraded relative to internal testing. On Aging–Rest, ElasticNet achieved an MAE of 9.69 years with Pearson r of 0.866 and R² of 0.725; PCR achieved an MAE of 10.21 years with Pearson r of 0.862 and R² of 0.700; the hybrid achieved an MAE of 10.18 years with Pearson r of 0.862 and R² of 0.699; and SVR achieved an MAE of 11.94 years with Pearson r of 0.828 and R² of 0.623 (ZeroR 21.45 years). On CamCAN–Rest, ElasticNet achieved an MAE of 8.46 years with Pearson r of 0.834 and R² of 0.681 and PCR achieved an MAE of 8.62 years with Pearson r of 0.838 and R² of 0.673, while the hybrid achieved an MAE of 8.86 years with Pearson r of 0.827 and R² of 0.654 and SVR achieved an MAE of 10.12 years with Pearson r of 0.797 and R² of 0.557 (ZeroR 15.88 years). On CamCAN–Movie (task), PCR achieved an MAE of 9.21 years with Pearson r of 0.738 and R² of 0.545; ElasticNet achieved an MAE of 9.23 years with Pearson r of 0.737 and R² of 0.543; the hybrid achieved an MAE of 9.35 years with Pearson r of 0.729 and R² of 0.531; and SVR achieved an MAE of 10.13 years with Pearson r of 0.709 and R² of 0.462 (ZeroR 14.01 years). Overall, moving from Test–Rest to independent cohorts incurred roughly a 2–4 year MAE penalty, quantifying a substantial out-of-distribution generalization gap despite harmonization.

Conclusion

These benchmarks provide a practical reference point for fMRI-based brain-age modeling with classical approaches and quantify expected error inflation when moving from held-out test data to truly independent datasets. Future work should (i) report standardized evaluation protocols that separate tuning from final evaluation, (ii) use strict baselines (e.g., train-mean) in addition to within-set dummy predictors, (iii) characterize domain shift explicitly (scanner/sequence/paradigm effects) and test traveling-subject or harmonization robustness analyses, and (iv) explore model families that target external generalization (domain adaptation, reweighting, or uncertainty-aware calibration) rather than optimizing in-distribution MAE.

Conclusion: Using CREB-harmonized functional-connectivity features, classical machine-learning models achieved consistent within-distribution performance for functional brain-age prediction (MAE ~6–8 years across validation/test splits), but performance degraded on independent external cohorts (MAE ~8.5–12 years), revealing a clear generalization gap even after multi-site harmonization. Across models, PCR and ElasticNet were the strongest and most stable; SVR was consistently weaker; and a PCR+XGBoost residual hybrid did not yield meaningful gains over the linear baselines, suggesting that most predictive signal in these features is captured by low-dimensional linear structure. Despite this gap, all models substantially outperformed a constant-age baseline (within-set mean predictor; MAE ~14–21 years), and improvements over the baseline were significant (p<0.001) across internal splits and independent cohorts. Predicted vs. actual age exhibited a strong global linear trend (overall R²=0.852), but with increasing dispersion at older ages, consistent with regression-to-the-mean and distribution shift.
