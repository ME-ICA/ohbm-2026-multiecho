Introduction

The interplay between body physiological signals and decision-making is crucial for adaptive behaviour, particularly when decisions concern food and eating1. Indeed, on the one hand it is documented that interoceptive capacity contributes to healthy eating, with data showing reduced interoceptive accuracy in individuals with obesity, and altered interoception at behavioural and brain level in patients with eating disorders. On the other hand, dysregulated eating has been related to high impulsivity, difficulties in inhibitory control and altered brain responses to reward in decision-making. Despite this evidence, the brain mechanisms underlying the interaction between interoception and impulsivity, when faced with different types of reward, have yet to be elucidated. The insula and dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC) are considered core regions for interoceptive processing and motivated behaviour, thus a better understanding of their potential role in decision-making behaviour has an important translational impact2. This study investigated whether modulating the right insula and dACC with high-definition transcranial direct current stimulation (HD-tDCS) could affect interoceptive accuracy and impulsivity with food and monetary rewards. 

Methods

Model-based HD-tDCS montages were defined to target the right insula and dACC via 6 circular electrodes (9.5 mm radius, 3 anodes x 3 cathodes, total current: 3 mA). Two behavioural paradigms were employed: i) the heartbeat detection task (HBD), to assess interoceptive as well as exteroceptive accuracy; ii) two versions of the delay discounting (DD) task with food and monetary rewards, as indexes of impulsivity. Heart rate variability (HRV) was also measured as autonomic physiological marker. HD-tDCS was administered online during the HBD, while DD tasks were performed offline. Twenty-four participants completed 4 sessions in a within-subject design: baseline DD tasks, anodal HD-tDCS targeting the insula, dACC or in sham mode. 

Results

Accuracy in the HBD task was higher for the exteroceptive than interoceptive trials. Interestingly, a significant three-way interaction HD-tDCS condition x trial type, x synchronous condition (χ²(2) = 52.9, p < .001), revealed that HD-tDCS applied both to the right insula and dACC interfered with participants’ capacity to recognize synchronous heartbeat, while improving accuracy in exteroceptive trials. The repeated-measures Anova on hyperbolic estimation of delay discounting index revealed a significant main effect of reward type (F(1,23) = 8.89, p = .007) and HD-tDCS session (F(1,23) = 3,54, p = .019). Overall impulsivity in DD task was higher for food than monetary reward, and individual discounting rate significantly increased following insula stimulation. Analyses on participants’ choice for sooner vs later options revealed that HD-tDCS sessions differently affected DD task in relation to reward type (the session × reward type interaction was significant (χ²(3) = 7.99, p = .046).When faced with food, impulsive choices increased in the three HD-tDCS sessions compared to the baseline. Whereas in the monetary condition a significant increase of impulsive choices resulted in the sham condition compared to baseline, but not following the insula and dACC stimulation.

Conclusion

Findings suggest a differential role of dACC and insula in interoceptive and exteroceptive accuracy, supporting the effect of HD-tDCS combined with interoceptive task in modulating impulsive decision-making3. The engagement in the interoceptive task increased impulsive decision-making, in particular when food rewards were presented. Interestingly, HD-tDCS to the right insula and dACC mitigated the impulsivity with monetary rewards. These differences in reward type sensitivity highlight the relevance of stimulus type when assessing interventions for impulsive eating behaviours.
