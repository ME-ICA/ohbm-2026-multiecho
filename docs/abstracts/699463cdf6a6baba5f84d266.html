Introduction

Adaptive fear is critical for survival and performance. However, over-generalized or persistent fear can become pathological and promote mental disorders, including fear-related disorders and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD).1 From a temporal perspective, the fear processing cycle encompasses three critical stages: fear exposure and subsequent fear consolidation. Developing innovative strategies to effectively diminish the subjective experience of fear during exposure and attenuate fear memory consolidation may provide a key candidate therapeutic pathway for fear-related disorders. 

Recent studies indicated that Oxytocin (OT), a neuropeptide produced in the hypothalamus, has the capacity to regulate fear during both acquisition and consolidation stages.2 Notably, the field has largely utilized static fear stimuli or classical fear conditioning models. While methodologically rigorous, these approaches may not fully capture the dynamic fear experiences in daily life, limiting the translational interpretation of the findings. 

To address the aforementioned issues, the present study employed a pre-registered, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled pharmaco-fMRI design. 67 male participants underwent a dynamic fear movie-watching paradigm, flanked by two resting-state fMRI scans (eyes open) performed before and after the task. The objective is to investigate whether a single dose of intranasal OT can attenuate subjective fear in dynamic environments and affect fear consolidation under close to real-world conditions. To systematically examine OT's attenuation of subjective fear during these stages, we applied a local Multivariate Pattern Replay analysis3 and a global integrative framework involving Dynamic Hidden Semi-Markov Model (HSMM)4 clustering and K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) mapping.

Methods

Participants

Sixty-seven right-handed male participants (age  21.03  1.95 years) completed the study. Participants were recruited in accordance with approved ethical protocols (UESTC-1061423041725893), and the study was pre-registered (NCT05896553). Informed consent was obtained from all participants. Participants were randomized to receive either intranasal oxytocin (24 IU) or placebo under double-blind conditions. fMRI data were acquired 45 minutes after treatment administration. To control for baseline differences, participants completed mood and mental health questionnaires. Double-blinding was confirmed by non-significant treatment guesses ( χ2 = 0.39, p = 0.53).

Dynamic Fear Movie-watching Paradigm and Rest-fMRI Acquisition 

The experiment commenced 70 minutes post-treatment (Fig. 1A & 1B). Participants watched a 10-minute horror movie to induce dynamic fear. Two 8-minute resting-state fMRI scans were acquired before (Rest_pre) and after (Rest_post) the movie to assess baseline and fear consolidation, respectively. To quantify oxytocin's effects on subjective fear, participants rated their subjective fear levels after each session using a 9-point Likert scale (1 = no fear, 9 = extreme fear). An independent sample (N=20) provided continuous fear ratings (2-sec resolution) to temporally segment the horror movie into low, medium, and high fear levels (LF/MF/HF) in a data-driven manner. A mixed ANOVA was used to assess treatment effects on subjective fear across the three sessions (between: treatment; within: session), followed by post-hoc comparisons of group differences at each session.

MRI Data Acquisition and Preprocessing

Data were acquired on a 3T GE Discovery MR750 system and preprocessed with fMRIPREP. At the first level, a GLM was estimated with the three fear conditions (LF/MF/HF) of interest, while rigorously regressing out motion artifacts, physiological noise, and signal trends.

Multivariate Pattern Replay Analysis

We first conducted a first-level analysis to generate high-fear activation maps for all participants, followed by a second-level analysis to identify between-group differences. After applying peak-level FWE correction, we found that OT significantly reduced activation in the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC), and this activation pattern was used as the encoding template. For each participant, the left dlPFC template was correlated with the corresponding multivariate pattern at each TR during both pre- and post-encoding rest, yielding a similarity time series for each rest run. Replay was defined as the number of TRs with correlation between 1.5-3 SD and above the participant’s mean correlation within a given run (Fig. 1D). 

Replay values were compared between OT and PLC using two-sample t-tests for pre- and post-encoding rest, respectively. For brain-behavior analyses, post-encoding replay was correlated with subjective fear ratings (two-tailed Pearson correlations) and additionally tested as a mediator in a simple Treatment→Replay→Fear model.

Dynamic HSMM Clustering and KNN Mapping Approach

For each subject, BOLD time series from 463 ROIs were averaged into 9 networks to obtain 9‐dimensional network time courses. Dynamic functional connectivity among these 9 networks was estimated using a Dynamic Conditional Correlation (DCC) model, and task DCC matrices were submitted to HSMM to identify three recurring connectivity states and their state-wise centroids. 

These task-derived centroids were then used as templates in a K-nearest neighbors (KNN) classifier to map pre- and post-task resting data into the same three-state space, yielding discrete state time series for both rest periods (Fig. 1E). 

From these sequences, we computed fractional occupancy (FO), mean dwell time (DW), switching rate (SW), and state transition probability matrices for each subject and run, and compared group differences using two-sample t-tests (and permutation tests where appropriate).

Results

Demographics and Potential Confounders

The OT (n = 33) and PLC (n = 34) groups exhibited comparable socio-demographics, mental health indices, and pre-treatment and post-treatment mood assessments, suggesting that pre-treatment group differences and non-specific treatment effects were not present (all p values > 0.07).

OT Decreased Subjective Fear in Dynamic Fear Exposure and Consolidation

Repeated ANOVA results showed that the interaction between treatment and session was significant (F = 3.12, p = 0.048), and the main effects of treatment (F = 7.41, p = 0.008) and session (F = 245.30, p < 0.001) were also significant. Further post-hoc tests revealed that OT decreased subjective fear in dynamic fear (t = 2.08, p = 0.041) and the following rest (t = 2.82, p = 0.006, Fig. 1C).

OT Reduced Left dlPFC Replay During Fear Consolidation

Group-level analyses confirmed that OT significantly reduced activation in the left dlPFC in dynamic fear (fear encoding, Fig. 2A), and replay quantified from this region showed clear treatment effects. OT did not differ from PLC during rest-pre (t = 0.133, p = 0.355), but exhibited significantly lower replay during rest-post (t = -2.618, p = 0.011, Fig. 2B). In post-encoding rest, dlPFC replay was negatively associated with subjective fear ratings following OT treatment (OT: r = -0.56, p = 0.001; PLC: r = -0.08, p = 0.664, Fig. 2C). Mediation results indicated a small indirect effect (a*b = 0.378, p = 0.036, Fig. 2D), implying that dlPFC replay provides supportive but not primary contributions to OT-induced fear reduction.

SW Played a Causal Role in Reducing Subjective Fear

Dynamic connectivity modeling identified three HSMM-derived states with distinct FC patterns (Fig. 2G). Compared to PLC, OT enhanced state1 FO (t = 2.38, p = 0.020) and accelerated whole-brain SW (t = 2.62, p = 0.011), decreased state2 FO (t = -2.19, p = 0.032) and DW (t = -2.47, p = 0.016, Fig. 2E). Transition analyses further revealed that OT reduced persistence in State 2 (t = -2.37, permutation p = 0.022, Fig. 2F). Higher SW predicted lower fear ratings in OT group (OT: r = -0.76, p  0.001; PLC: r = 0.06, p = 0.728, Fig. 2H). Mediation testing showed a significant indirect effect of SW on fear (a*b = –0.313, p = 0.014, Fig. 2I), establishing SW as a behavioral-relevant dynamic signature through which OT reduces subjective fear.

OT Accelerated SW During Fear Consolidation Rest

Using centroid-based KNN mapping, task-defined HSMM states were projected onto the two resting runs. During rest-pre, OT and PLC did not differ in FO, DW, or SW (all p > 0.409), indicating comparable baseline state dynamics before encoding. In contrast, rest-post revealed clear OT-related alterations in spontaneous dynamics. OT significantly increased whole-brain SW (t = 2.24, p = 0.029) while PLC showed no such change (p = 0.836, Fig. 2K), suggesting enhanced dynamical flexibility following encoding under OT. In addition, OT enhanced FO in state1 (t = 2.20, p = 0.032), further transition probability analyses identified selective OT-related modulation of state transitions (t1→1 = 2.78, permutation p = 0.005; t2→1 = 2.10, permutation p = 0.028, Fig. 2J), indicating that OT reshaped the post-encoding resting-state flow towards state 1.

We next tested whether SW served as a behaviorally relevant signature linking OT to reduced subjective fear in fear consolidation. Mediation analysis showed a significant indirect effect (a*b = –0.316, p = 0.022), with OT increasing SW (a = 0.051, p = 0.029), and SW in turn predicting reduced fear (b = –6.183, p = 0.017). Although a direct treatment effect on fear remained (c′ = –1.042, p = 0.034), the mediation pattern indicates that SW partially contributes to, but does not fully account for OT-induced fear reduction (Fig. 2L).

Conclusion

The present study investigated how single-dose intranasal OT modulates subjective fear during both exposure and subsequent consolidation using an ecologically valid dynamic fear paradigm. OT consistently reduced subjective fear during the task and in the subsequent resting period, despite comparable baseline characteristics across groups. These behavioral effects indicate a genuine treatment impact rather than pre-existing differences or nonspecific mood changes.

At the regional level, OT reduced left dlPFC activity during fear encoding and selectively dampened post-encoding replay. Although replay during rest-post negatively predicted fear in the OT group and showed a small indirect effect, the preserved direct treatment effect suggests that dlPFC replay plays a supportive rather than primary role in OT-induced fear reduction.

In contrast, whole-brain dynamic connectivity revealed a more robust mechanism. OT increased the fractional occupancy of an adaptive state, reduced the engagement of a maladaptive state, and accelerated the switching rate during fear processing. Importantly, SW showed a strong negative association with fear only in the OT group and mediated part of the treatment effect, highlighting large-scale dynamical flexibility as a key mechanism through which OT alters fear experience.

By mapping task-defined states onto resting data, we further demonstrated that these effects extend beyond stimulus engagement. Rest-pre dynamics were comparable across groups, ruling out baseline differences. Rest-post, however, showed increased SW, higher occupancy of state 1, and selective transition changes under OT, indicating that OT reshapes spontaneous network flow during consolidation. The convergence between task-evoked and rest-post dynamics supports the idea that OT modifies the brain’s functional landscape, preventing prolonged engagement of fear-related configurations.

These findings collectively suggest that OT promotes rapid reconfiguration among large-scale networks while suppressing replay-linked consolidation signals, thereby weakening the persistence of aversive experiences. SW emerges as a strong candidate mechanism, with replay representing a complementary, region-specific contributor.

Several limitations should be noted. Participants were healthy men receiving a single OT dose, limiting generalizability to women or clinical populations. Although naturalistic stimuli improve ecological validity, they do not encompass all fear contexts. Finally, mediation estimates rely on observational associations and do not establish definitive causality.

Despite these constraints, the present work offers important translational implications. First, SW and state-transition profiles may serve as mechanistic biomarkers for monitoring pharmacological modulation of fear circuits. Second, identifying individuals with rigid network dynamics may guide personalized OT-based interventions.5 Third, the integrated analytic framework, combining replay, HSMM modeling, and cross-state mapping, provides a scalable approach for probing how neuromodulators reshape brain dynamics under conditions closer to daily life.

In summary, OT reduces subjective fear through coordinated modulation of large-scale dynamical flexibility and targeted suppression of prefrontal replay. These multi-level effects move beyond static activation accounts and highlight network-based dynamic signatures as promising targets for the development and translation of mechanism-guided treatments for fear-related disorders.
