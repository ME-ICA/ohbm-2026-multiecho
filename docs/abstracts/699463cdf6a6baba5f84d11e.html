Introduction

Language development depends on the maturation of neurostructural systems supporting auditory processing, phonological encoding, and frontotemporal integration.1 Music participation is a rich auditory-sensorimotor experience that engages neural systems central to speech and language processing.2 Children involved in musical activities often show stronger phonological awareness, auditory discrimination, and literacy-related skills. Neuroimaging research parallels these behavioral findings, demonstrating that musical experience is associated with altered cortical thickness, gray-matter morphology, and strengthened structural connectivity within auditory, motor, and frontotemporal pathways.3 These findings suggest that music may influence language development through experience-dependent changes in neurostructural systems.

Despite this evidence, the underlying neural pathways remain poorly characterized. Many prior studies rely on small samples, cross-sectional designs, or limited control of socioeconomic, behavior, and familial confounding, making it difficult to separate effects of musical experience from preexisting individual differences. To address this gap, we leveraged longitudinal MRI and cognitive data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study. We tested whether cortical structure mediated the association between baseline music participation and language skills two years later, adjusting for demographic, socioeconomic, prenatal, perinatal, behavioral, and enrichment-related factors.   

Methods

Data were drawn from the ABCD Study, Release 3.0, which includes ongoing longitudinal follow-up of U.S. children aged 9-10 years at baseline across 21 sites. For the present analysis, we used data from the baseline visit and the 2-year follow-up assessment (ages 11-12). 

Music participation was measured at baseline using parent reports from the Sports and Activities Involvement Questionnaire, with sustained participation defined as involvement in organized musical activities for ≥4 continuous months. Language outcomes at follow-up were assessed using the NIH Toolbox Crystallized Composite, an age-adjusted standardized index of vocabulary knowledge and language-based abilities. 

Structural MRI data were collected at 21 sites on 3-T Siemens, GE, and Philips scanners using   standardized imaging protocols developed by the ABCD Imaging Acquisition Workgroup. Each participant completed high-resolution 3D T1-weighted and T2-weighted imaging.  Centralized preprocessing was performed by the ABCD Data Analysis, Informatics, and Resource Center (DAIRC), including gradient nonlinearity correction, intensity inhomogeneity correction, and registration to a reference template. Cortical surface reconstruction and subcortical segmentation were completed in FreeSurfer, followed by manual quality review by trained DAIRC technicians for motion artifacts, intensity inhomogeneity, white matter underestimation, pial overestimation, and magnetic susceptibility artifacts. Global cortical measures were derived from T1-weighted structural MRI data processed using standardized ABCD Study pipelines. Total surface area was computed as the sum of vertex-level areas, total cortical volume as the gray matter volume between the pial and white matter surfaces, mean cortical thickness as the average distance between these surfaces, and mean sulcal depth as the average displacement relative to a spherical atlas. These preregistered global cortical measures were selected to capture broad variation in cortical size and shape relevant to higher-order cognitive functions. 

Analyses adjusted for demographic factors (age, sex, race/ethnicity), socioeconomic indicators (income, parental education, marital status), prenatal and perinatal factors (preterm birth, prenatal alcohol or tobacco exposure), behavioral characteristics (CBCL ADHD Problems, Social Problems, Internalizing, Externalizing), participation in non-music enrichment activities, and study site. 

We used linear mixed-effects models with family level random intercepts to account for within-family clustering, evaluating both direct associations between baseline music participation and later language skills as well as mediation pathways through cortical structure. Each exposure-mediator-outcome pathway was modeled separately to minimize collinearity and reflect theoretical distinctions. The conceptual mediation framework is illustrated in Figure 1. Multiple comparisons were controlled using the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR procedure (q<0.05). All analyses were conducted in Python 3.11 using statsmodels.

 

Results

From the full ABCD baseline cohort (N = 11,878), 5,482 participants were excluded for missing 2-year language outcomes, 511 for missing baseline demographic or socioeconomic data, and 67 for missing MRI cortical measures, yielding a primary analytic sample of 5,818 participants with complete crystallized cognition scores. A χ² automatic interaction detection (CHAID) analysis of baseline characteristics found no evidence of selection bias between participants retained in the final analytic sample and those who were excluded due to missing data. Among the included participants (baseline mean age 10.0 years; SD 0.6; 47.1% female), the sample included 2.1% Asian, 10.8% Black, 18.3% Hispanic, 58.9% White, and 9.9% multiracial or other race individuals. At baseline, 42.7% of children engaged in sustained music participation. Children who participated in music differed from non-participants primarily in sociodemographic characteristics: they were more likely to come from households earning ≥$50,000 annually, from parents with at least some college education, and from married-parent households. They also participated more frequently in other enrichment activities, including creative arts and individual sports. Perinatal history, prenatal exposures, and child behavior measures were largely comparable between the two groups, with standardized mean differences generally <0.20. 

Sustained music participation at baseline was positively associated with higher crystallized cognition two years later (β = 4.38; 95% CI, 3.52-5.24), corresponding to approximately a 0.29-0.30 SD advantage on an age-standardized scale with a mean of 100 and SD of 15. In mediation models, global cortical surface area and global cortical volume accounted for statistically significant, though small, portions of this association. Surface area mediated 4.1% of the total effect (indirect β = 0.18; 95% CI, 0.05-0.31; q = .005), and cortical volume mediated 4.3% (indirect β = 0.19; 95% CI, 0.06–0.32; q = .005). No significant mediation was detected for global cortical thickness or sulcal depth (all q ≥ .94). Full mediation estimates are presented in Table 1.

Conclusion

Sustained engagement in music during early adolescence was associated with modest but developmentally meaningful gains in crystallized cognition two years later, even after extensive adjustment for demographic, socioeconomic, perinatal, prenatal, and behavioral factors. The magnitude of this association is comparable to typical enrichment effects and aligns with evidence that music participation supports the acquisition and consolidation of language-based skills. 

Mediation analyses indicated that global cortical surface area and volume accounted for a small but statistically significant portion of the longitudinal association between music participation and crystallized cognition, whereas cortical thickness and sulcal depth did not. This pattern aligns with evidence that surface area and thickness reflect partly distinct genetic and developmental processes and supports prior work implicating surface-area reductions in youth with developmental language disorder.4 The mediation observed for cortical volume likely reflects its strong surface-area contribution. The absence of mediation via thickness or sulcal depth suggests that experience-dependent influences of music on language ability may correspond more closely to expansion-related aspects of cortical morphology than to maturational thinning or cortical folding patterns. These findings are consistent with the shared sound-processing hypothesis, which proposes overlapping neural mechanisms for processing acoustic complexity in both music and language.5 Even small improvements in crystallized cognition at the individual level could translate into meaningful population-level benefits during adolescence, a developmental period marked by enhanced neuroplasticity and rapid academic growth. 

Several limitations should be considered. First, the mediated proportions were small, indicating that although global brain morphometry accounted for a statistically significant portion of the music–language association, most of the effect likely operates through other neural pathways or experiential factors not captured in this study. Second, music participation was measured via parent report and may be subject to recall or reporting bias. Third, the mediation analyses focused exclusively on global cortical metrics and did not incorporate regional cortical features, white-matter microstructure, or functional network properties, potentially overlooking more localized or circuit-level mechanisms. Finally, despite extensive covariate adjustment, residual confounding and selection bias are still possible, and mediation in observational data cannot establish causality. Future research should investigate the durability of these effects and assess whether different types or intensities of music engagement produce distinct neural or educational outcomes.

In summary, sustained music participation is associated with higher crystallized cognition in early adolescence, and global cortical expansion partially explains this relationship. Music participation may represent an accessible experiential factor capable of supporting language-related development. Future studies using randomized or advanced causal-inference approaches, together with multimodal longitudinal imaging, including structural, diffusion, and functional MRI, are needed to strengthen causal inference and clarify the neural mechanisms through which music engagement shapes cognitive growth.
