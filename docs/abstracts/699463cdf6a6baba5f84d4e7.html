Introduction

Dynamic functional network connectivity (dFNC) captures the brain’s intrinsic organization as a fluid, continuous evolution of network configurations. Despite this temporal complexity, common analysis pipelines often force data into rigid, discrete states (e.g., via k-means). These "hard" classifications create instability: negligible fluctuations near boundaries trigger spurious transitions, while substantial intra-state functional reorganization is ignored.

To resolve this instability, we introduce geometric profiling—a framework that quantifies the shape, efficiency, and excursion of the dynamic trajectory (e.g., Total Distance, Straightness) without relying on rigid boundaries. We evaluate these metrics directly in the unreduced High-Dimensional (HD) space. We chose this native space to eliminate the confounding influence of dimensionality reduction parameters (e.g., selecting model order or component number). While reduced models enhance interpretability, they inevitably introduce projection errors or information loss. By analyzing the raw covariance structure, we establish a "ground truth" benchmark: a parameter-free baseline that validates the biological utility of geometric analytics before their future application to more interpretable continuous latent models (e.g., from ICA).

Methods

We analyzed baseline data from 11,221 participants (ages 9-11) in the ABCD Study.1 Preprocessing included rigid-body motion correction, distortion correction, normalization, and smoothing with 6mm3 kernel. We employed NeuroMark, a hybrid ICA framework,2 leveraging the NeuroMark 1.0 functional template, which is constructed from the Human Connectome Project and Genomic Superstruct Projects datasets and includes 53 spatially reproducible intrinsic networks arranged into seven functional domains. Subject-specific time courses were back-reconstructed and further denoised via motion regression, detrending, despiking, and bandpass filtering (0.01–0.15 Hz).

dFNC was estimated using sliding windows (length = 40 volumes). To standardize analysis duration, we restricted analyses to the final 300 windows. We defined the dynamic trajectory in the native High-Dimensional (HD) space (1,378 unique pairwise correlations). A preliminary validation against mean framewise displacement (FD) revealed strong motion confounds in the full sample (r=0.43); however, restricting the sample to runs with mean bandstop-filterd FD < 0.2 mm substantially mitigated this (r=0.18). Consequently, all subsequent analyses were restricted to this high-quality subset (N=9,017). Derived metrics were averaged across the surviving low motion runs to improve stability.

We calculated Total Distance (sum of Euclidean distances between successive windows) and profiled the trajectory shape using Displacement (distance between first and last window), Straightness (Displacement/Total Distance), and Radius of Gyration (RMS distance of all windows to the run centroid). We used linear mixed models to compare the predictive utility of these continuous HD metrics against standard discrete state transition counts (derived from k-means clustering, k=5). We assed how these measures of brain dynamics relate to individual characteristics in the ABCD Study data including sex and scores from the Child Behavior Checklist, a parent-rated measure providing broad-band indices of child psychopathology: Internalizing problems, Externalizing problems, and Total Problems.

Results

Sex difference analyses provided initial validation of sensitivity: females exhibited higher dynamism than males. Critically, while discrete state transition counts failed to detect this robust effect (p>0.05), the Total Distance metric successfully captured it (t=4.91, p<.001), confirming that the preservation of continuous information outperforms hard discretization. Displacement (t=4.67, p<0.001), Straightness (t=2.67, p=0.007), and Radius of Gyration (t=3.91, p<0.001) were also sensitive to sex differences.

Regarding behavioral prediction, discrete state transitions showed no significant associations with any CBCL scale (all p>.05). In contrast, Total Distance significantly predicted Internalizing (r=0.032), Externalizing (r=0.038), and Total Problems (r=0.034; all p<.001). Radius of Gyration was also sensitive to all three measures(r=0.025, 0.051, 0.04 respectively, all p<.001). Displacement significantly predicted Externalizing (r=0.04, p<0.01) and Total Problems (r=0.03, p=0.004). Finally, Straightness exhibited a positive association with Externalizing symptoms (r=0.025, p=0.017).

Conclusion

Geometric profiling offers a fundamentally more stable lens for analyzing dynamic trajectories than rigid discrete states, avoiding spurious transitions triggered by negligible fluctuations near boundaries. We demonstrate that quantifying trajectory shape in the native, unreduced space captures behaviorally relevant information that discrete transition counts miss.

While the HD space provides a parameter-free benchmark, it lacks network-specific interpretability. Therefore, future work should apply this validated geometric framework to continuous latent variable models (e.g., ICA). This extension is desirable because it would combine the interpretability of known, overlapping brain networks with the proven mathematical stability of continuous geometric profiling.
