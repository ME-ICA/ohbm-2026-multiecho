Introduction

Adolescent major depressive disorder (MDD) shows substantial clinical heterogeneity in symptoms and treatment responses, highlighting the need for objective neurobiological markers. Neuroimaging studies have revealed alterations across large-scale functional networks in adolescents with MDD, yet most work relies on node-centric connectivity and may miss informative edge-level interactions.1

Edge-centric functional connectivity offers a higher-order characterization of brain organization by describing interactions between region pairs. Nodal entropy, which quantifies the diversity of edge module affiliations, reflects functional integration and may sensitively capture subtle network disruptions underlying heterogeneous presentations of MDD.2

Integrating neuroimaging with transcriptional architecture further enables linking macroscale abnormalities to molecular mechanisms. Spatial gene-expression profiles from the Allen Human Brain Atlas provide a framework for associating altered connectivity patterns with transcriptional signatures implicated in psychiatric disorders.3

Here, we used individualized edge-centric networks to identify neurobiological subtypes of adolescent MDD based on nodal entropy. We characterized subtype-specific clinical and neural features, mapped entropy abnormalities onto gene-expression patterns, and evaluated diagnostic performance using machine-learning models. This integrative framework aims to elucidate the biological basis of clinical heterogeneity and advance imaging-driven precision psychiatry for adolescent MDD.

Methods

We recruited 110 adolescents with first-episode MDD and 87 age-matched healthy controls (HCs). All participants were 12-18 years old, right-handed, and assessed by trained psychiatrists using DSM-5 criteria. Depression severity was evaluated with the HAMD-17; patients with scores ≥17 and no psychiatric comorbidities were included. Exclusion criteria for all subjects included neurological or major medical illness, developmental disorders, substance abuse, and MRI contraindications. Sixteen patients and nine HCs were excluded due to excessive head motion.Resting-state fMRI data were collected on a GE 3.0-T MR750 scanner. Preprocessing followed standard pipelines, including motion correction, normalization, nuisance regression, and filtering.

The brain was parcellated into 200 Schaefer ROIs to obtain z-scored time series. Edge time series (eTS) were generated by pairwise dot products, producing 19,900 unique eTS. K-means clustering (25 repetitions) partitioned eTS into 7 edge communities, which were mapped back to nodes to form individualized edge-centric networks.Normalized entropy was calculated for each region to quantify the distribution of its edges across communities, reflecting functional integration versus specialization.

Nodal entropy of MDD patients was clustered via nonnegative matrix factorization (NMF). k values from 2-10 were tested (50 repetitions each), and the optimal k was determined by the highest silhouette coefficient. The final model yielded two subtypes. Group differences in demographics and clinical measures were tested using t tests or χ² tests. Nodal entropy differences relative to HCs were assessed using linear regression controlling for age, sex, and motion (FDR-corrected p < 0.05). Three XGBoost classifiers were trained to distinguish (1) MDD vs HC, (2) subtype 1 vs HC, and (3) subtype 2 vs HC. Features were selected using t tests (p < 0.05). Data were split 80/20 for training/testing, with 10-fold cross-validation for tuning. Performance was evaluated using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and AUC. Permutation testing (1,000 iterations) assessed significance. SHAP analysis provided model interpretability.

Left-hemisphere gene expression data from the AHBA were preprocessed using abagen to generate a 100 × 15633 matrix. Partial least squares (PLS) regression linked entropy t-maps to gene expression. Model significance was tested using spatial permutation (10,000 iterations), and gene weights were bootstrapped (1,000 iterations) to derive PLS1-positive and PLS1-negative gene lists. GO/KEGG enrichment of PLS1-associated genes was performed via Metascape (FDR-corrected p < 0.05). Cell-type enrichment (neurons, oligodendrocytes, astrocytes, microglia, endothelial, ependymal cells) was evaluated via permutation testing. Spatial correlations between entropy alterations and neurotransmitter distributions were assessed using JuSpace.

Results

A total of 94 adolescents with MDD and 78 HCs were included. No significant group differences were found in age, sex, education, or BMI. 

NMF clustering identifies two adolescent MDD subtypes. Across candidate cluster numbers (k = 2–10), k = 2 yielded the highest silhouette score (0.16), dividing patients into subtype 1 (n = 60) and subtype 2 (n = 34). The two groups separated clearly along the first two NMF components. Although global patterns of nodal entropy were similar, several regions showed distinct mean differences. PCA visualization further supported subtype separation. Both subtypes showed reduced whole-brain nodal entropy relative to HCs. However, their spatial alteration profiles diverged. Subtype 1 exhibited lower entropy in Visual Network (VN) / Somatomotor Network (SMN) and higher entropy in Ventral Attention Network (VAN) / Frontoparietal Network (FPN) / Default Mode Network (DMN). Subtype 2 showed the opposite pattern, with higher entropy in VN/SMN and lower entropy in DAN/VAN/FPN/DMN (FDR-corrected p < 0.05). Clinically, subtype 2 displayed a longer illness duration (p = 0.016).

Subtype-based classification improves discriminability. In the testing dataset, the overall MDD vs HC model performed poorly (AUC = 0.652; accuracy = 0.514). After subtype stratification, performance improved markedly. Subtype 1 vs HC achieved an AUC of 0.760 and accuracy of 0.750, whereas subtype 2 vs HC reached an AUC of 0.939 and accuracy of 0.870. SHAP analysis showed that subtype 1 classification relied on entropy in VN, SMN, and FPN, while subtype 2 classification depended on SMN, DMN, and DAN.

Transcriptional architecture linked to subtype-specific entropy changes. PLS1 explained 20.28% and 15.18% of spatial variance in subtype 1 and subtype 2, respectively. PLS1 scores correlated positively with case–control t-values for both subtypes (r = 0.600 and r = 0.580; pspin < 0.0001). Subtype 1 showed 1,567 PLS1+ and 924 PLS1– genes; subtype 2 showed 1,200 PLS1+ and 610 PLS1– genes. Molecular pathways and cellular signatures. In subtype 1, PLS1+ genes were enriched in neurodevelopmental and synaptic pathways, whereas PLS1– genes involved metabolic and hormonal regulation. Subtype 2 showed strong enrichment of immune and inflammatory pathways among PLS1+ genes, with PLS1– genes linked to reduced neural structural remodeling. Cell-type enrichment revealed astrocytes and neurons driving subtype 1, whereas subtype 2 showed microglial involvement. Neurotransmitter correlates of nodal entropy. Subtype 1 entropy changes were positively associated with 5-HT4, D2, GABAa, SERT, VAChT, and VMAT2. Subtype 2 showed positive associations with SERT and VAChT and negative associations with 5-HT2a, 5-HT4, and GABAa. In both subtypes, entropy reductions were strongly associated with decreased cerebral blood flow (p < 0.001).

Conclusion

This study integrates an edge-centric connectome with transcriptional and neurochemical data to reveal mechanistic substrates of adolescent MDD heterogeneity. Two subtypes showed distinct nodal entropy patterns across functional networks, accompanied by subtype-specific molecular, cellular, and neurotransmitter signatures, offering a multilevel framework linking macroscale network alterations to underlying biology.

Both subtypes exhibited reduced whole-brain entropy but diverged in network-specific alterations. Subtype 1 showed lower entropy in sensory–motor networks and higher entropy in attention and executive networks, suggesting reduced flexibility in primary processing with compensatory engagement of higher-order systems. Subtype 2 demonstrated the opposite pattern, greater sensory-motor entropy but reduced attentional and executive entropy, indicating diminished top-down control. The longer illness duration in subtype 2 suggests differing developmental trajectories, with subtype 1 possibly reflecting early stage compensatory reorganization and subtype 2 reflecting chronic maladaptation. Subtype based modeling markedly improved diagnostic discrimination, underscoring the importance of accounting for biological heterogeneity. SHAP analyses confirmed subtype specific predictive features that aligned with their distinct entropy profiles, supporting the interpretability and clinical relevance of these subtypes. Transcriptomic analyses revealed mechanistic differences. In subtype 1, PLS1+ genes enriched in neurodevelopmental and synaptic pathways pointed to maturational vulnerability affecting circuit integration, consistent with altered entropy in sensory and associative networks. PLS1– enrichment in metabolic and hormonal pathways suggested systemic constraints influencing network efficiency. In subtype 2, PLS1+ enrichment in immune and inflammatory pathways indicated neuroimmune involvement, aligning with reduced flexibility in higher order networks. PLS1– enrichment in synaptic maintenance pathways suggested limited capacity for structural remodeling under chronic stress. Cell type signatures further differentiated the subtypes: subtype 1 involved astrocyte neuron interactions affecting synaptic and metabolic support, whereas subtype 2 showed microglial involvement consistent with inflammatory modulation of network organization. Neurotransmitter associations also diverged. Subtype 1 displayed widespread positive associations with serotonergic, dopaminergic, cholinergic, and inhibitory systems, reflecting coordinated neuromodulatory influences on network flexibility. Subtype 2 showed mixed serotonergic and cholinergic effects, with opposing receptor specific associations, indicating altered neuromodulatory balance. The consistent negative associations with CBF across both subtypes highlight interactions between vascular and neurochemical factors.4,5

Several limitations warrant caution. The modest sample size requires replication in larger, multisite cohorts. The cross sectional design precludes conclusions on developmental change or treatment response. Transcriptomic data from AHBA are limited to a small number of adult donors and hemispheric coverage. More comprehensive clinical phenotyping is needed to refine symptom subtype relationships.
