Introduction

Understanding the genetic architecture of cortical folding is crucial for elucidating the biological basis of human brain organization that influences cognitive and behavioral functions.1 Among structural features, sulcal morphometry provides a detailed representation of cortical folding patterns that are highly individualized yet genetically independent. Heritability estimates from large population datasets have shown that a substantial proportion of variability in sulcal morphology can be attributed to genetic factors, highlighting cortical folding as a promising endophenotype for genetic neuroimaging.2

Growing evidence indicates that brain folding plays a fundamental role in shaping functional brain architecture and can serve as stable anatomical anchors for functional networks.3 Variability in sulcal morphology has been linked to individual differences in connectivity patterns, network centrality, and cognitive performance.4 However, the extent to which different sulci share common genetic influences remains unclear. Identifying genetic correlations among sulci can reveal the underlying architecture of shared genetic effects that may drive coordinated folding patterns across cortical regions. In addition, sulcal measures have shown genetic correlations with several neuropsychiatric disorders.1 Mapping these genetic relationships onto known functional networks offers a novel opportunity to examine how the brain’s genetic organization aligns with its functional networks.

In this study, we analyzed a large sample from the UK Biobank to estimate heritability and pairwise genetic correlations of sulcal width across the whole brain. Building on the functional brain networks framework, we conceptualize the high-dimensional genetic correlation structure to examine the network-level organization of genetic influences shaping cortical folding. As a relevant example, we further explore the potential association of these network-level genetic patterns with schizophrenia.

Methods

In this study, we used summary statistics from the UK Biobank (https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/), a large population-based cohort of adults aged 40-69 years at recruitment. As only summary statistics were used, no further ethical approval was required. The original GWAS of sulcal morphology included a discovery cohort of 26,530 and a replication cohort of 13,639 participants. The brain imaging protocol included isotropic T1-weighted MPRAGE images (1 mm³ voxel size; field of view = 208 × 256 × 256 mm) that were bias-field corrected during acquisition. Full MRI acquisition details are available in the UK Biobank imaging protocol. 

Sulcal-based morphometry was conducted in the previous study following the ENIGMA-Sulci protocol (https://enigma.ini.usc.edu/) [1]. Briefly, T1-weighted images were preprocessed with FreeSurfer 7.1.1 (https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/), and sulcal extraction and quantification were performed using the BrainVISA 5.1.2 (https://brainvisa.info/). A total of 123 cortical sulci were labeled, and four morphological descriptors (width, mean depth, surface area, and length) were computed. Only measures with high reliability (intraclass correlation > 0.75) from prior test-retest validation were included in the summary statistics used in our analysis.2

To identify the large-scale functional networks associated with each sulcus, we mapped the BrainVISA anatomical landmark atlas (123 sulci; 2 mm, MNI space) onto the Schaefer-400 parcellation with Yeo’s-7 functional networks (2 mm, MNI space). For each sulcus, voxelwise probabilistic maps were overlapped with the Schaefer atlas, and the proportion of each sulcus’s voxelwise probability shape falling within each Schaefer-400 parcel was computed and normalized to sum to one. These parcel-level overlaps were then aggregated according to Yeo’s seven canonical functional networks. The network with the highest cumulative overlap was defined as the primary functional network of that sulcus, while the complete 7-network mixture profile was retained to capture cross-network contributions (Figure 1). Across all sulci, the primary network proportion ranged from 0.32 to ~1.00, indicating that most sulci exhibited a clear dominant functional network affiliation. All assignments were additionally visually inspected and manually corrected when necessary to ensure anatomical and functional consistency.

We estimated the genetic correlation (rg) between each pair of sulcal width measures using GWAS summary statistics with the cross-trait LD Score regression (LDSC) software obtained from https://github.com/bulik/ldsc. We used genome-wide LD scores computed by the Pan-UK consortium on UK Biobank samples of European descent (https://pan.ukbb.broadinstitute.org/): scores were available for 1,094,844 SNPs which met their exporting criteria, most notably after filtering for HapMap3 SNPs, MAF > 0.01 and removing the human major histocompatibility complex (MHC) region. Genetic correlations between the disorder (i.e., schizophrenia) and sulcal width measures were estimated using the same approach, based on schizophrenia GWAS summary statistics obtained from the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium (https://pgc.unc.edu/).

We used the Mann-Whitney U test to compare intra-network and inter-network genetic correlations, assessing whether regions within the same functional network shared significantly stronger genetic similarity than those belonging to different networks. Between-network similarity: for each network, we computed a connectivity profile representing the average genetic correlation of its constituent regions with all other regions in the brain. Between-network similarity was then quantified as the Pearson correlation between these network profiles. Self-correlations were excluded. To emphasize robust relationships, weak correlations with |r| < 0.1 were excluded from the analysis.

Results

The mapping between sulcal regions and functional networks revealed that only one sulcus was assigned to the salience/ventral attention network; therefore, this network was excluded from subsequent analyses. After multiple-comparison correction, we observed predominantly positive genetic correlations (rg) across the whole brain (Figure 2A). The limbic network was the only exception, showing no significant  genetic correlation. The average intra-network genetic correlations were as follows: frontoparietal control (rg = 0.55), default mode (rg = 0.54), somatomotor (rg = 0.36), visual (rg = 0.50), and dorsal attention (rg = 0.71). 

Intra-network genetic correlations (mean rg = 0.53) were significantly higher than inter-network correlations (mean rg = 0.31), indicating that sulci within the same functional network share a stronger genetic influence than those belonging to different networks (p = 0.008, Cohen’s d = 1.78, Figure 2C). To further explore inter-network organization, we quantified the similarity between networks based on their external genetic connectivity profiles (Figure 2D). These results indicate that default, dorsal attention, and somatomotor networks share partially overlapping genetic connectivity patterns (r = 0.68-0.78), whereas frontoparietal control and visual networks exhibit more distinct profiles, with some anti-correlations (e.g., dorsal attention vs frontoparietal control, r = -0.52). This pattern reflects a network-specific organization of genetic influences on sulcal morphology, where some networks co-vary genetically, and others are more independent or oppositely patterned. The visual network appeared genetically distinct from other systems, consistent with its early developmental specialization.

Genetic correlation analyses between sulcal width measures and schizophrenia identified three significant sulci: the left occipital sulcus (rg = 0.11, p = 0.011), the left posterior lateral fissure (rg = 0.11, p = 0.017), and the superior postcentral intraparietal sulcus (rg = 0.10, p = 0.033) (Figure 2B). These genetic correlations did not show significant differences when compared across functional networks.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that the genetic architecture of cortical folding mirrors the organization of large-scale functional networks. This extends the “core genetic network” model of sulcal morphometry from individual features to a network-level framework.5 The dorsal attention and frontoparietal control networks, encompassing regions such as the intraparietal sulcus, superior parietal lobule, and lateral frontal cortex, showed the strongest intra-network genetic correlations. These regions are heavily interconnected and developmentally coupled, with a strong genetic covariance. Multiple twin and genome-wide studies have reported high heritability and shared genetic architecture across these parietal-frontal cortices.1 

Recent work has shown that sulci act as functional units rather than passive anatomical features, with functional connectivity clustering according to sulcal morphology rather than spatial proximity.4 Consistent with evidence that individual sulcal morphology constrains functional connectivity and network centrality,4 our findings emphasize that this functional specialization is mirrored at the genetic level. The strong intra-network genetic correlations observed suggest that the same organizational principles linking sulcal morphology to functional architecture also shape the genetic structure of cortical folding.

Genetic correlations between sulcal width and schizophrenia were modest and limited to a small number of specific sulci, suggesting regionally localized rather than network-wide genetic effects. These sulci likely correspond to later-developing secondary or tertiary folds, which exhibit greater inter-individual variability and are thought to be more susceptible to subtle neurodevelopmental perturbations. This pattern is consistent with prior imaging genetics studies indicating that schizophrenia-related genetic influences on cortical folding preferentially affect higher-order sulcal architecture rather than early-established primary sulci.1

It should be noted that the study relied on probabilistic sulcal-network match, which may oversimplify regions spanning multiple functional boundaries. Some sulci may correspond to multiple networks, as they often lie along the boundaries between adjacent anatomical regions. This could be addressed in future work by refining sulcal-network mapping. In addition, networks with few assigned sulci, such as the salience/ventral attention and limbic networks, had limited representation, reducing statistical power and interpretability of the genetic correlations.

This work represents the first steps toward understanding the genetic background of cortical folding in relation to functional brain networks, providing a novel perspective on the genetic organization of cortical folding. In future extensions, we will also incorporate other sulcal metrics, including depth, surface area, and length, to better characterize the sulcal shape and associated genetics. These genetic and functional network architectures will also be compared with the sulcal phenotypic network to assess their convergence. Furthermore, as a first step, we examined genetic correlations with schizophrenia, which showed the highest genetic correlation with width in previous studies.1 Subsequent analyses will extend this framework to compare sulcal genetic architectures with those underlying other neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative disorders to identify potential endophenotypes.
