Introduction

Population ageing has emerged as a global public health challenge, driving an urgent need to address age-related cognitive decline and neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease. Non-pharmacological interventions (NPIs), defined as structured, nonchemical therapies grounded in theoretical frameworks1, are increasingly recognized for their potential to delay cognitive deterioration and reduce dementia incidence in older adults through enhancing brain neuroplasticity2,3. Previous NPIs studies in ageing neuroscience have largely focused on intervention-specific regional effects, with limited understanding of generalizable network-level mechanisms. Since these single-intervention designs with small cohorts exhibited substantial inter-study variability while failing to establish a unified explanatory framework, no scalable, cross-cultural target for NPIs in older adults has yet been identified. Future advances require identifying shared neural mechanisms underlying NPIs at the brain system-level, which are essential to both decipher late-life neuroplasticity and refine theoretical models of how targeted interventions remodel aging brain function. Thus, in this study, we explored whether a convergent brain network explains multi-domain cognitive gains across heterogeneous NPIs in older adults.

Methods

We pooled data from four pre-registered intervention trials of 171 community-dwelling adults aged 53–80 years (112 training, 59 control; Chinese Clinical Trial Registry). Interventions in the training group (TG) comprised cognitive training, square dance, aerobic-video-game combination, or multimodal Tai Chi, plus cognitive training and group counselling, delivered over 4–16 weeks. Similarly, trial-specific protocol-matched activities were engaged in the control group (CG), including health lectures, maintenance of baseline lifestyle, or strenching exercise. After providing informed consent and demographic information, all participants completed neuropsychological assessments and MRI acquisition (structural MRI and resting-state functional MRI) both pre- and post-intervention. Fig.1 illustrates the recruitment and exclusion process of four registered interventions we used. We investigated intervention effects on the behaviour and brain. The primary outcome was global cognition measured by Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), while the brain functional organisation was measured with functional gradients and network dedifferentiation (indexed by within- and between-network functional connectivity strength). We first constructed parcel-parcel resting-state functional connectivity (rsFC) matrices for all participants based on the Schaefer parcellations with 400 parcels4, which were mapped into 7 networks. Then we separately generated group-level Fisher-z transformed FC (FCz) matrices that harmonized the multi-site effect for TG (n = 112) as well as CG (n = 59). On this basis, we computed within-network functional connectivity strength (within-FCS; sum of FCz between parcels belonging to the same network) and between-network functional connectivity strength (between-FCS; sum of FCz between parcels belonging to different networks) as well as functional gradients pre- and post-intervention. Finally, we implemented a new analysis procedure called “virtual lesion”5 by directly excluding network parcels from the post-intervention matrix in TG and rederived functional gradients to derive network necessity for functional gradient maintenance, and regression models to link network connectivity change to cognitive change, with age, education, sex, and head motion as covariates. 

Results

A significant interaction effect of group (TG vs. CG) × period (pre- vs. post-intervention) was found for all participants measured MoCA (TG/CG: n = 97/46; F(1, 141) = 4.31, p = 0.040, partial η2 = 0.030). Simple main effects analysis further revealed that the interaction effect was driven by increased MoCA scores in TG after intervention (post-pre: 0.95, 95% CI = 0.59-1.31, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.162), with no significant change observed in CG (post-pre: 0.28, 95% CI = -0.24-0.81, p = 0.286, partial η2 = 0.008).

Comparative analysis of FCS changes pre-to-post intervention between the TG and CG revealed an intervention-induced within-FCS enhancement in TG (TG-CG: 0.60, 95% CI = 0.43-0.78, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.40), whereas between-FCS changes showed no significant group difference but a decreasing trend in TG (TG-CG: -0.25, 95% CI = -0.53-0.03, p = 0.079, Cohen’s d = -0.08). Before intervention, both TG and CG (TG1/CG1) exhibited highly similar gradient patterns, characterized by primary gradients (G1) that extended from unimodal (somato/motor and visual) to transmodal (association) areas (S-A axis), and secondary gradients (G2) spanning the somato/motor-visual axis. However, after the intervention, TG participants (TG2) retained this gradient organization, whereas CG participants (CG2) exhibited a swapped gradient topology. Specifically, primary gradients in TG1, CG1, and TG2 closely matched the secondary gradient in CG2.

When the dorsal attention network (DAN) was selectively removed from TG2, the rederived primary gradient now closely resembled the primary gradient of CG2 (r = 0.96, pspin < 0.001, 1000 permutations). Similarly, the rederived secondary gradient in DAN-dropped TG2 shifted to match CG2’s secondary gradient (r = 0.96, pspin < 0.001, 1000 permutations). The general intervention-driven optimization of DAN may play a core role in maintaining hierarchical cortical organization.

Linear mixed-effect models predicting MoCA scores across all participants revealed that only within-FCS in DAN showed significant associations with MoCA after Bonferroni correction (b = 0·10, 95% CI = 0·03–0·17, p = 0·008). As sensitivity analyses, linear regression models predicting MoCA scores changes only across TG participants also revealed a trend that higher Dwithin-FCS in DAN was associated with MoCA change (b = 0·10, 95% CI = 0·01–0·19, p = 0·031). Finally, in the post-intervention TG2 subgroup, linear regression models showed that within-FCS in DAN remained significantly predictive of MoCA (b = 0·18, 95% CI = 0·05–0·32, p = 0·010). Conclusion

A pressing priority of heterogeneous non-pharmacological interventions in aging neuroscience is to reveal their universal brain pattern and underlying mechanism attenuating age-related cognitive decline, then furtherly identify the core convergent large-scale functional network central to their neuroplasticity in the elderly’s brain. In the current study, we constructed an NPI dataset based on four different types of intervention in older adults and investigated the intervention-general effects on both brain and behaviour. Besides improving cognition, diverse NPIs help strengthen functional integration within the same network and maintain the original gradient pattern against aging, reflecting a significant intervention effect on brain hierarchical organization. Through virtual lesion analyses, we first uncovered that the dorsal attention network may be linked to the intervention-induced gradient maintenance in the cerebral macroscale organization. Next, we split the post-intervention training group into high- and low-subgroups according to the within-FCS changes median in DAN and found that elderly brain hierarchical organization can be effectively maintained by enhancing intra-network integration in DAN after intervention. Finally, the link between global cognition and within-FCS in DAN demonstrated that both the behavioral and brain intervention effects converge on DAN, once again indicating dorsal attention network is the core convergent hub across diverse non-pharmacological interventions attenuating age-related cognitive decline.

In summary, across four independent non-pharmacological interventions, the maintenance of cortical hierarchy and cognitive function converged on the same mechanism—enhanced integration within the dorsal attention network. This discovery reframes non-pharmacological intervention research from fragmented behavioural evidence to a mechanistically grounded, globally scalable approach with a unified network-based framework of neuroplasticity for promoting healthy brain aging and mitigating the rising worldwide burden of neurological disorders. By establishing dorsal attention network connectivity as a functional biomarker, this work enables biomarker-guided stratification and monitoring in future large-scale prevention trials. Longitudinal and cross-population replications integrating molecular and behavioural metrics are needed to determine whether dorsal attention network plasticity mediates long-term resilience against neurodegenerative trajectories.
