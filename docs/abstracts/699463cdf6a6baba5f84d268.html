Introduction

The human prefrontal cortex (PFC) is vital for higher-level cognitive functions. It is a structurally and functionally heterogeneous brain region, including multiple subregions that integrate various cognitive functions such as working memory, executive control, emotional regulation, planning, decision, and social behaviors.1 Clarifying its internal functional organization is crucial for understanding how the subregions of PFC support these cognitive functions. The “What-How, Abstraction, Cold/Hot (WHACH)” model, which was proposed by O’Reilly,2 provides a key framework for deciphering the functional organization of PFC. The WHACH model suggests three functional axes in human PFC, the ventral-dorsal axis, the rostral-caudal axis, and the medial-lateral axis. Although the model has been verified by various empirical studies, it is unclear whether the model accounts for the functional gradient characteristics of these axes. Functional gradient analysis (FGA) offers a quantitative tool for filling this gap. Previous studies have shown that FGA can be used to quantify the transition of macroscopic continuous hierarchies by reducing the dimensionality of brain features.3, 4 For instance, the human lateral prefrontal cortex (lPFC)3 and left inferior frontal gyrus (lIFG)4 exhibit a dual-gradient structure along the rostral-caudal axis and the dorsal-ventral axis. Although these studies have revealed important continuous organizational principles within human PFC, they are largely derived from the resting-state fMRI data. While the cognitive functions of the PFC have been extensively investigated using diverse paradigms in task-based fMRI research, its task-based functional gradients remain unexplored. Therefore, we used FGA to reveal the functional continuity and hierarchy of the human PFC based on the task-fMRI data. Specifically, we collected the activation patterns of PFC under different task paradigms to characterize its task-based functional gradients.

Methods

Search strategy

We searched for articles in the Neurosynth dataset from January 2002 to July 2018 with 59 search terms related to the PFC and its subregions separately. After removing duplicates, we screened the articles by the following criteria. The inclusion criteria were: (1) applying task-based fMRI, (2) involving healthy adults aged 18-60 years, (3) employing whole-brain analysis, and (4) reporting the PFC coordinates in MNI or Talairach space. Studies were excluded if they were: (1) resting-state fMRI studies, (2) animal studies or studies not including healthy subjects, (3) studies based on ROI analysis, and (4) review or meta-analysis articles. We defined an experiment as a contrast between conditions in each article, and extracted task paradigms, contrasts, number of subjects, and peak coordinates from each experiment. Then, we classified and organized these coordinates according to their spaces (MNI or Talairach space) for subsequent analysis.

Activation likelihood estimation (ALE)

First, Talairach coordinates were converted to MNI space using the tal2icbm transform in GingerALE 3.0.2. Then, the experiments were categorized into 10 functions: attention, perception, cognition, emotion, decision, memory, execution, social, language, and thinking. A single dataset meta-analysis was conducted using GingerALE to obtain the ALE map of each function. The ALE analysis involved 4 steps: modeling activation maps, converging the activation probability, constructing an empirical zero distribution, and identifying true activations. The statistical threshold was set at p < 0.01 (uncorrected voxel-level) and volume > 200 mm3.

Functional specificity analysis

We resampled all ALE maps into the Brodmann atlas via Nilearn, achieving voxel-level alignment through continuous interpolation. The PFC includes 12 Brodmann areas (BA): 6, 8, 9, 10, 11, 24, 25, 32, 44, 45, 46, 47. For each function in each of these BA regions, we extracted the voxel-wise ALE values within the region and calculated the mean ALE value. This process was repeated for all functions and all BA regions in the PFC, generating a mean matrix of “BA regions × functions” (12 × 10). Based on this matrix, we calculated the specificity index (SpecIdx) of each BA region (Fig. 1a). For a given BA region, if its SpecIdx in a certain function is higher than that in the others, we infer that this BA region prefers that function.

Functional diversity analysis

To measure which PFC regions have multiple functions, we converted each function’s ALE map to a binary image with a cut-off threshold of 0.001, where the voxels with ALE > 0.001 were set to 1 and the others to 0. Fig. 1b shows the Dice coefficient (0-1 range) for each pair of functions for a given BA region in the PFC. The higher the Dice coefficient, the greater the spatial overlap between the ALE maps for this pair of functions within the region, indicating that this region is jointly recruited by both functions.

Functional gradient analysis (FGA)

First, we mapped the activation peak coordinates to a 3 mm MNI152 space via an inverse affine transformation. A PFC mask (16,046 voxels) was generated according to the Brodmann atlas. For each voxel, we constructed a 1 × 1,150 activation vector representing the 1,150 experiments. Each component of the vector represents whether the voxel is activated in each experiment, with 1 standing for activation and 0 for no activation. Then, the cosine similarity between every pair of the vectors was calculated (Fig. 1c) to obtain a 16,046 × 16,046 similarity matrix. We used the diffusion embedding algorithm5 implemented in the BrainSpace toolbox to reduce the dimensionality of the similarity matrix, and generated functional gradients of the PFC with default sparsity (retaining the top 10% similarity of each row in the matrix) and diffusion parameter (α = 0.5). In the current study, we report the first three gradients because they can explain more variance compared to the others.

Results

We included 831 articles covering 1,150 experiments, with a total of 16,708 subjects and 6,084 coordinates. The number of experiments for each of the 10 functions was as follows: execution (231), memory (188), language (145), emotion (116), thinking (111), social (103), decision (92), cognition (57), attention (56), and perception (51). Fig. 2a shows the ALE results of the 10 functions. Fig. 2b shows the functional specificity of the 12 BA regions in PFC. For example, BA 45 (Broca’s area) exhibits a preference for language function. Fig. 2c reflects the functional diversity of the BA regions in PFC. Taking BA 45 as an example again, this region is highly involved in multiple functions, including language, memory, and perception. Fig. 2d shows the first three functional gradient axes of the PFC, all of which mainly extend along the rostral-caudal (abstract-concrete) axis, corresponding to one axis of the WHACH model. Unfortunately, we failed to capture the independent leading role of the other two axes in this model. However, this limitation has been partially compensated for in the first gradient. The extension direction of the first gradient is not strictly distributed along a single axis, but presents the direction across two axes: the rostral-caudal axis and the medial-lateral (hot value representations-cold cognitive calculations) axis. We infer that this cross-axis distribution reflects the inter-axial interactions emphasized in the WHACH model. When representational capacity is constrained along one axis, other axes can provide complementary and synergistic support, thus achieving multi-dimensional representation and dynamic compensation.

Conclusion

The current study analyzed the functional gradients of task-based fMRI data from healthy human adults. We found that different PFC regions exhibit common activation features across different task paradigms, reflecting functional characteristics of specialization and diversity. Furthermore, the task-based functional gradients of PFC are predominantly organized along the rostral-caudal axis, and there exists a comprehensive organizational pattern of multi-axis interactions, which is consistent with the WHACH model hypothesis. Our findings provide new insights into the task-based functional organization of the PFC.
