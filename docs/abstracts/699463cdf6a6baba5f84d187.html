Introduction

Higher-order interactions (HOI), i. e. interactions involving more than two brain regions, are increasingly recognized as a fundamental substrate for large-scale brain activity (1–3). HOI can be investigated by measuring informational properties, as synergy, i. e. information available in the joint activity of brain regions, that cannot be recovered from single areas. These informational properties have been shown to change systematically across states of consciousness, age, and cognitive conditions, and at rest, they align with established functional and anatomical hierarchies (2, 3).

While recent literature has deeply investigated higher-order informational properties in brain dynamics, it remains unclear how the brain balances the contribution of different interaction orders and how this relates to neurochemical and metabolic organization. While this distinction is critical for understanding how collective information processing emerges in the human brain, we lack computational tools able to disentangle the contribution of different orders.  State-of-the-art metrics, such as the O-information, do not have this ability by construction, while approaches, such as Partial Entropy Decomposition, which can, in theory, provide more nuanced decompositions, often imply high computational costs (4). 

Here, we develop a new information-theoretic metric to address this gap: the irreducible O-information, which disentangles the contribution of the group of brain regions under study from subsets of them, allowing us to measure order-specific synergy. To illustrate the properties of this new metric, we analysed BOLD resting state time series, showed how irreducible synergy is spatially organized across the cortex, evaluated its alignment with large-scale functional gradients, and tested its association with neurochemical and metabolic brain organization. Our results reveal a structured and biologically meaningful architecture of high-order integrative processing.

Methods

O-information for each quadruplet was estimated following established multivariate information-theoretic formulations, employing the Gaussian copula estimator and the open source Python toolbox, HOI, https://github.com/brainets/hoi (5). Irreducible O-information was computed as the difference between the absolute value of the O-information of the quadruplets minus the maximum absolute value across subtriplets (Fig.B).We validated our metric using analytical benchmarks and generative models, and then applied it to resting-state BOLD data to measure order-specific synergy.  We focus on order 4, measuring the irreducible O-information (Ωir) of all possible quadruplets of brain regions. Our choice is motivated by the fact that synergy is present only in groups of three or more variables; hence, order 4 is the lowest order at which it is possible to observe synergies at different orders and where it is meaningful to investigate order-specific synergy. Order-specific synergy was defined as negative irreducible O-information, given that  Ωir, as the O-information (Ω), measures the difference between redundancy and synergy. Conceptually, this approach separates interactions that genuinely require all four variables from those that can be assembled from simpler subsystems (Fig. A, B).To validate the measure, we first constructed synthetic systems in which the relative contributions of 3- and 4-variable synergy could be precisely manipulated. In more detail,  the ratio λ₃/(λ₂ + λ₃) controls the relative contribution of order 4 (ruled by parameter λ₃) with respect to order 3 (ruled by parameter λ₂) XOR. In the logic-gate setting, we combined overlapping XOR interactions while holding total O-information constant. This allowed us to test whether irreducible O-information selectively increases proportionally to λ₃/(λ₂ + λ₃).We next evaluated the metric in parametric generative models commonly used to study multivariate interactions: correlated Gaussian systems, multivariate Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes, and small Ising networks. For each model class, the ratio λ₃/(λ₂ + λ₃) controls the relative contribution of order 4 (ruled by parameter λ₃) with respect to order 3 (ruled by parameter λ₂) coupling. We varied λ₃/(λ₂ + λ₃) from 0 (only order 3 interactions) to 1 (interactions only at order 4), estimating for every configuration the order-specific synergy among the 4 variables to assess whether the metric increases monotonically with the proportion of genuine order 4 coupling.Empirical analyses were conducted using resting-state BOLD data from the Human Connectome Project (S1200 release). We employed minimally preprocessed time series subjected to ICA-FIX denoising, regression of motion parameters, and temporal filtering (0.01–0.1 Hz). Cortical signals were parcellated using a standard multi-network atlas, Schaefer 100 regions with 16 subcortical regions. Individual subject maps were obtained by summing for each region r,  order-specific synergy across all quadruplets containing r, and then the average across subjects is computed and plotted (Fig.B). Regions were grouped into the seven canonical resting-state networks. For each network, we calculated mean order-specific synergy across all constituent nodes, yielding group-level summaries of irreducible synergy. To relate irreducible synergy to cortical biology, we correlated regional Ωir values with multiple micro-architectural datasets: neurotransmitter receptor and transporter densities, metabolic markers (e.g., glycolytic index), cortical microstructure (T1w/T2w, myelination, thickness), evolutionary expansion maps, and cell-type-specific gene expression gradients, Fig.D. All these maps were taken from the Python toolbox, neuromaps (https://pypi.org/project/neuromaps/). Associations were quantified using Spearman correlations with spatial-autocorrelation-robust null models.

Results

We first validated the irreducible O-information on controlled systems known to generate higher-order synergistic interactions (Fig.A). In the left panel, we constructed logic-gate systems where synergy arises from overlapping XOR interactions at different orders. Importantly, we created a system with constant O-information (Ω), with varying relative contribution of 3- vs. 4-variable synergistic interaction. Under these conditions, the irreducible O-information increased specifically when true 4-variable synergy dominated. This demonstrates that the irreducible O-information (Ωir) is sensitive to the order of synergistic structure rather than merely its overall magnitude, isolating the part of synergy that genuinely requires all four variables (order-specific synergy).

In the right panel of Fig.A, we extended this benchmarking to parametric mechanistic models of higher-order synergy (Gaussian, multivariate Ornstein-Uhlenbeck, and Ising systems). Here, synergy increases continuously as the higher-order coupling λ₃ becomes stronger relative to λ₂. Across all model classes, the proportion of order-specific synergy grew monotonically with λ₃/(λ₂ + λ₃), despite their distinct dynamical properties. This convergence confirms that irreducible O-information provides a model-agnostic marker of genuine high-order synergistic mechanisms.

We then applied this framework to resting-state BOLD signals from the Human Connectome Project. For every subject, we computed order-specific synergy, see methods, across all quadruplets of brain regions and projected these values onto the cortical surface (Fig.C). The spatial distribution was highly structured: transmodal association cortices, including default mode, frontoparietal, and dorsal attention systems, exhibited the strongest irreducible synergy, while visual and somatomotor regions showed the weakest contributions. This organization was consistent across participants and parcellations.

When aggregated at the network level (Fig.D), the default mode, frontoparietal control, and dorsal attention networks robustly displayed the highest irreducible synergy, whereas unimodal networks were systematically lower. This indicates that cortical hubs involved in integrative cognition participate most strongly in synergy that cannot be reduced to interactions among subsets of regions. 

Finally, irreducible synergy showed systematic relationships with cortical micro-architecture (Fig.E). Irreducible synergy correlated positively with neuromodulatory receptor and transporter densities, particularly serotonergic and dopaminergic systems, suggesting that these neurochemical gradients may scaffold higher-order integrative dynamics. The negative association with metabolic markers suggests that irreducible synergy preferentially emerges in regions that support efficient, distributed information integration rather than energetically intensive local processing. Additional associations with microstructure, cortical expansion, and gene-expression gradients reinforce the view that irreducible synergy aligns with fundamental axes of cortical biological specialization.

Together, these findings show that irreducible synergy is deeply embedded in the hierarchical, neurochemical, and microstructural organization of the human cortex, revealing a new dimension of large-scale integrative processing.

Conclusion

Previous work has established the relevance of informational properties such as synergy and redundancy for characterizing brain activity, revealing robust links with cognitive functions (3) and with functional and anatomical hierarchies of the cortex (2). However, despite these advances, it has remained unclear how to disentangle the contribution of different orders of interactions and how to isolate genuinely order-specific contributions from those driven by lower-order dependencies. Here, we introduced a new higher-order information-theoretic metric, the irreducible O-information, specifically designed to disentangle synergy across interaction orders. 

By applying this framework to resting-state BOLD activity, we achieved the first whole-brain mapping of irreducible higher-order synergy in humans. Focusing on order 4, the minimal multiplet size at which synergies can coexist at different orders, we isolated the component of synergy that cannot be reduced to any combination of triple-wise dependencies, thereby characterizing the role of brain regions across different interaction orders (1).

Our results show that irreducible synergy is strongly hierarchical, closely following the unimodal-to-transmodal cortical gradient and peaking in association cortices, particularly frontal and parietal regions implicated in cognitive flexibility and integrative processing. The spatial alignment with neuromodulatory receptor distributions suggests that higher-order synergistic interactions may be scaffolded by neurochemical systems supporting flexible and context-dependent coordination. Moreover, the negative association with metabolic markers indicates that regions rich in irreducible synergy operate within metabolically efficient regimes, consistent with a form of distributed integration that does not rely on energetically intensive local processing.

From a conceptual perspective, irreducible synergy captures a form of information integration in which multiple brain regions complement each other to generate emergent informational structure that cannot be recovered from any subset alone. The prominence of frontal and parietal cortices in our results aligns with their well-established role as integrative hubs supporting abstract cognition and large-scale coordination.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Our analyses were restricted to a single dataset and to resting-state conditions, and focused exclusively on order-4 interactions. While order 4 represents the first level at which irreducible synergy can be meaningfully defined, extending this framework to higher orders, task states, developmental trajectories, and clinical populations will be essential to build a comprehensive taxonomy of higher-order brain organization. Finally, while in this paper, we mainly focus on irreducible synergy, our work can be extended to the systematic investigation of irreducible redundancy and other informational properties.

Overall, these findings advance our understanding of how the human brain supports high-order information integration beyond pairwise and triple-wise interactions. They demonstrate that irreducible synergy is not diffusely distributed, but instead reflects the brain’s macroscale functional, neurochemical, and energetic architecture. As such, irreducible synergy provides a powerful new axis for characterizing large-scale brain organization and the emergence of collective computation in the human cortex.
