Introduction

Emotions are central to human well-being, and the ways in which people regulate them shape resilience, mental health and everyday functioning. One of the most studied strategies is cognitive reappraisal, in which people reinterpret situations to alter their emotional impact. Neuroimaging research has identified a robust network of prefrontal and parietal regions engaged during reappraisal.^1 Theories often assume that variation in these neural responses underpins individual differences in regulation capability and, in turn, well-being. Such effects are commonly thought to be mediated via a down-regulation of the amygdala by these networks. Yet the extent to which brain activity during reappraisal provides a reliable marker of individual differences in actual emotion regulation capability remains unclear. Thus far, evidence has been mainly been based on few non-preregistered studies with small sample sizes. This highlights the need for more robust large-scale efforts to test a neurobiological theory, which has received considerable attention in psychology, psychiatry, and the broader public.

Methods

We initiated a new consortium on the Neurobiology of Individual Differences in Emotion Regulation (NIDER). Potential members were identified over a systematic literature search and academic mailing lists. Within this consortium effort, comprising 40 fMRI datasets (N=2,175), we examined the relationship between neural activation during reappraisal tasks and three core individual difference indices of reappraisal capabilities: (1) trait questionnaires, (2) task-based affective ratings, and (3) amygdala down-regulation. Each study team performed standardized second-level correlation analyses for these three associations on their existing datasets, which were aggregated by the core team for further analysis. Study-level images were analyzed using Frequentist and Baysian random effects models, together with comprehensive simulations, indicating sufficient power for small effect sizes (r=.10). These analyses were preregistered on PROSPERO (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD42021243155.&nbsp). We further examined the generalizability of important methodological results to other psychological domains, beyond emotion regulation, using a publically available dataset (N=270), comprising 18 studies covering 9 psychological domains.^2 

Results

Stikingly, there was no shared overlap across the three common indices of emotion regulation capability (Figure 1). Only a very weak correlation emerged between amygdala down-regulation and task-based affective ratings (r=0.08, 95% CI = [0.02, 0.15]). Detecting this effect size in a single study with 80% power would need N>1000, far above common sample sizes in this research area. The other two correlations were statistically not significant and the null hypothesis favored by Bayes factors (ratings and questionnaires: r=0.05, 95% CI = [-0.00, 0.10], BF01=6.25; questionnaires and amygdala: r=0.01, 95% CI = [-0.05, 0.06], BF01>100). Responses in canonical emotion regulation networks (e.g., prefrontal cortex) were not associated with established trait questionnaires or task-based affective ratings measuring emotion regulation capability (|r|<.03, BF01>47). Moreover, amygdala down-regulation, often interpreted as a stable individual marker, was confounded by person-specific whole-brain responses: A person with larger task-based amygdala responses than other people was also much more likely to have larger responses in most other parts of the brain. The multi-domain dataset showed this was not restricted to the amygdala or emotion regulation, but rather a general feature of task-based fMRI across regions, research teams, psychological domains, and common preprocessing strategies.

Conclusion

Our findings challenge prevailing assumptions about the neural correlates of interindividual capabilities in cognitive reappraisal and raise fundamental questions about how emotion regulation is measured across levels of analysis. The weak convergence between common outcome measures and their inconsistent associations with canonical reappraisal networks underscore the need for better-aligned behavioral, trait-based, and neural constructs for them to capture a shared core of cognitive reappraisal. This is especially relevant given that extremely large-scale resting-state efforts have failed to reveal replicable neural correlates of emotion-related traits.^3^4 Employing tasks that are well-designed to elicit the individual differences of interest might lead to larger effects. Still, the negligible association between questionnaires and task-behavior suggests that either common emotion regulation tasks or questionnaires do not sufficiently capture their target construct. Moreover, we demonstrated that interindividual differences in whole-brain responses during tasks are a methodological issue that likely affects the vast majority of the task-based fMRI literature, beyond emotion regulation. Ultimately, identifying robust and generalizable neural markers of self-regulation requires both methodological progress and conceptual clarity in defining what we seek to measure. Until then, we should refrain from strong statements concerning a person’s emotion regulation capabilities based on their prefrontal cortex, amygdala, or any other specific brain region or network.
