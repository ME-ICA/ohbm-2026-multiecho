Introduction

Wilson disease (WD) is a rare autosomal-recessive disorder of impaired copper metabolism that leads to toxic copper accumulation, primarily in the liver and brain.1 Damage to these organs can result in severe hepatic dysfunction, neurological impairment, and cognitive decline. The mechanisms that render specific brain regions, such as the deep gray matter nuclei, particularly susceptible to copper toxicity, as well as the microstructural alterations underlying neuropsychiatric symptoms, remain insufficiently understood.1

Neurite Orientation Dispersion and Density Imaging (NODDI) has recently emerged as a promising advanced neuroimaging technique for assessing brain microstructure from diffusion-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) by quantifying the morphology of neurites, such as their density and spatial organization.2 Initial findings using NODDI suggest microstructural disruption in the dorsal striatum and thalamus among WD patients with neurological manifestations.3,4 However, the current evidence base is limited, and the association between NODDI-derived metrics and neurocognitive function in WD is yet to be explored.

With this study, we aim to advance the currently limited knowledge by characterizing NODDI-derived microstructural alterations in the striatum and thalamus and examining their relationships with neurological and cognitive symptoms in WD patients with distinct clinical phenotypes.

Methods

Thirty WD patients were recruited, including nineteen patients with a neurological phenotype (neuro-WD; age M = 43.5 ±11.4, education M = 15.6 ±2.5, disease duration M = 24.2 ±11.0; all in years) and eleven patients with a hepatic phenotype (hep-WD; age M = 34.3 ±12.3, education M = 15.2 ±2.7, disease duration M = 17.5 ±9.4; all in years). In addition, thirty age- and sex-matched healthy controls (age M = 40.1 ±12.3, education M = 14.8 ±1.9; all in years) were included. All patients met a Leipzig score of ≥ 4, suggesting an established diagnosis of WD, and were receiving decoppering treatment.

Clinical assessments comprised the Unified WD Rating Scale neurological subscale (UWDRS-N) to evaluate neurological severity, the Digit Span Test (DST) to assess working memory, the Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT) to evaluate sustained attention and mental processing speed, and the Color-Word Interference Test (CW-INT) to assess executive function.

Structural and multi-shell diffusion MRI data were acquired at 3T (MAGNETOM Prisma, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) using a protocol adapted from the Lifespan Human Connectome Project (HCP) in Aging.5 Diffusion data were processed using the CUDA Diffusion Modelling Toolbox (cuDIMOT; https://users.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/~moisesf/cudimot/) within the FMRIB Software Library (FSL; v6.0.2) to derive NODDI metrics, including the neurite density index (NDI; indicating the packing density of neurites), orientation dispersion index (ODI; indicating the spatial configuration of neurites), and isotropic volume fraction (ISOVF; indicating free water).2 An overview of the diffusion MRI processing is provided in Figure 1.

Group differences in clinical scores and NODDI metrics within the dorsal striatum (caudate nucleus and putamen) and thalamus were assessed using Kruskal-Wallis tests followed by post-hoc Dunn’s test. Within the patient cohort, associations between clinical scores and diffusion metrics were examined using partial Spearman correlations, controlling for age and sex, and additionally for UWDRS-N scores in analyses involving neuropsychological measures. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics (v29), with a level of significance set at a Bonferroni-Holm-corrected p ≤ .05. Boxplot figures were created using R (v4.4.1).

Results

There were no significant differences in age, sex, education, or, where applicable, disease duration between groups. Patients with neuro-WD showed significantly higher UWDRS-N scores (Mdn = 6 [IQR 5–15]) compared with hep-WD patients (Mdn = 2 [IQR 0–4], p = .003) and healthy controls (Mdn = 0 [IQR 0–0]), p < .001). Neuro-WD patients also performed worse than healthy controls on the SDMT (z = - 0.55 ±0.85 vs. z = 0.55 ±0.79, p < .001) and CW-INT (z = 0.14 ±0.67 vs. z = 0.66 ±0.64, p = .008). Performance on the DST did not differ between groups (neuro-WD z = -0.25 ±0.88, hep-WD z = -0.53 ±0.97, controls z = -0.10 ±1.01; p = .451). No significant differences in clinical scores were observed between hep-WD patients and healthy controls.

Group differences in diffusion metrics are shown in Figure 2. Neuro-WD patients exhibited increased NDI in the striatum compared with hep-WD patients (caudate nucleus and putamen p < .001) and healthy controls (caudate nucleus p = .008; putamen p = .006). Thalamic NDI was also elevated in neuro-WD patients relative to healthy controls (p = .043). Striatal ODI was reduced in neuro-WD patients compared with both hep-WD patients (caudate nucleus p < .001; putamen p = .001) and healthy controls (caudate nucleus p = .035; putamen p = .001). In contrast, hep-WD patients demonstrated lower NDI (p = .044) and higher ODI (p = .035) in the caudate nucleus compared with healthy controls. Furthermore, ISOVF in the striatum and thalamus was higher in neuro-WD patients than in hep-WD patients (caudate nucleus p < .001; putamen p = .004; thalamus p = .002) and healthy controls (caudate nucleus p = .002; putamen and thalamus p < .001).

Within the patient cohort, UWDRS-N scores were significantly correlated with caudate NDI (rs = .576, p = .005), ODI (rs = -.614, p = .002), and ISOVF (rs = .599, p = .003). DST scores showed a significant correlation with caudate NDI (rs = .468, p = .048) and with microstructural alterations in the putamen (NDI rs = .496, p = .034; ODI rs = -.569, p = .009; ISOVF rs = .460, p = .048). The association between SDMT performance and caudate ISOVF did not remain significant after correction for multiple comparisons. No significant associations were observed between NODDI metrics and CW-INT scores.

Conclusion

We demonstrated diminished neurite orientation dispersion within the striatum and increased free water content in the striatum and thalamus of WD patients with a neurological phenotype, consistent with previous reports.3,4 These microstructural alterations may hint to reduced dendritic complexity, spongiform degeneration, edema, and/or neuroinflammation in the affected regions. The unexpected increase in striatal and thalamic neurite density, previously reported only in the globus pallidus of WD patients,4 may reflect compensatory or treatment-related microstructural reorganization.

Beyond prior work focusing exclusively on neurological WD, our findings further revealed reduced neurite density and increased orientation dispersion of neurites in the caudate nucleus of patients with hepatic WD, suggesting the presence of subclinical brain alterations before the onset of overt neurological impairment. Moreover, greater neurological symptom severity was associated with altered neurite density, simplified spatial organization, and increased free water content in the caudate nucleus. In contrast, after controlling for covariate effects of neurological function, no significant correlations were observed between microstructural changes and cognitive deficits. Nevertheless, preserved working memory performance in WD patients was related to neurite density, dendritic arborization, and free water content in the putamen.

Taken together, these findings indicated that WD is associated with phenotype-specific microstructural alterations in deep gray matter nuclei, which appear to preferentially contribute to neurological rather than cognitive manifestations of the disease. NODDI may therefore serve as a sensitive imaging marker for early or subclinical brain involvement of WD.
