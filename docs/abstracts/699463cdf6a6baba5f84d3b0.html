Introduction

Diffusion MRI (dMRI)-based tractometry is a vital method for quantifying tissue properties of white matter tracts in living humans. Tractometry exhibits high test-retest reliability when data were collected on the same MRI scanner using the same acquisition protocol¹. However, the extent to which tractometry is affected by scanner and protocol differences, a known source of variability in dMRI measurements, remains unclear. Here, we employed a traveling subject approach, in which the same subjects participated in experiments using different scanners, to evaluate reproducibility within the same scanner and protocol, as well as generalizability across scanners and protocols for tractometry. We defined generalizability as the agreement of tractometry metrics between datasets collected under different machine and/or protocol conditions. Our experimental design enabled comparisons across progressively different conditions: within a scanner, between scanners of the same model, between different scanner models, and between distinct acquisition protocols.

Methods

Five healthy male volunteers (mean age = 34.0) traveled to be scanned with three different scanners: two Siemens 3T Verio at the National Institute for Physiological Sciences (NIPS) and a 3T PrismaFit at Tamagawa University (Figure 1). Two volunteers also participated in an additional scan on a Siemens 3T PrismaFit at the Basque Center on Cognition, Brain and Language (BCBL). We used two acquisition protocols, based on the UK Biobank and HARP. Acquisition parameters were identical in all scans except for echo time, which was set to the minimally achievable value. T1-weighted images were acquired to generate semi-quantitative T1w/b0 maps2 and to define regions of interest (ROIs) for tract identification. dMRI data were processed using the RTP2 pipeline3, including preprocessing and identification of 20 major white matter tracts. For each tract, we computed 5 tract profiles using the tensor-based metrics fractional anisotropy (FA) and mean diffusivity (MD), the NODDI4 metrics (inter-cellular volume fraction [ICVF], orientation dispersion index [ODI], and isotropic volume fraction [ISoV]), and T1w/b0. Each metric was then averaged along the tract to obtain a summary metric. Reproducibility and generalizability were assessed by calculating the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) across datasets, treating data from each tract as independent samples. We also computed the within-subject standard deviation (SD) across all scanners and protocols to estimate non-biological variability for each tract. SD provides a proxy for the minimum detectable differences; thus, a smaller SD indicates higher detectability when datasets are polled across scanners and protocols.

Results

Tractometry results for MD showed high reproducibility when datasets were acquired with the same protocol on the same scanner (Figure 2A, upper left panel). Generalizability decreased when comparing datasets from different scanners of the same model and further declined when comparing datasets from different scanner types (Figure 2A, upper middle panel). The lowest generalizability was observed when different acquisition protocols were used (Figure 2A, upper right panel). Other metrics showed the same overall pattern, although FA and orientation dispersion index maintained relatively high generalizability across scanners and protocols. Generalizability improved after ComBat harmonization5, but did not reach the level observed for test-retest reliability on the same scanner and protocol (Figure 2A, lower panels).

To assess tractometry detectability when data acquired with multiple scanners and protocols were pooled, we examined the within-subject SD of MD across all datasets (Figure 2B). As shown by the distribution, variability was clearly tract-dependent. The uncinate and cingulum hippocampus showed relatively large variability, indicating lower detectability, whereas the optic radiation exhibited much smaller variability, indicating higher detectability. For all examined tracts, ComBat harmonization reduced within-subject SD.

Conclusion

We found that tractometry is highly reproducible when the same scanner and acquisition protocol are used. However, its generalizability decreases when scanners or acquisition protocols differ. These results indicate that tractometry is reliable under controlled experiments, but that quantifying scanner- and protocol-dependent effects is crucial for large-scale multisite studies. Moreover, scanner and protocol effects varied across metrics and tracts, suggesting that the smallest detectable differences in a multisite dataset depend on both metrics employed and the specific tracts under study.
