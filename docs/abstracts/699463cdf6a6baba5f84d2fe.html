Introduction

Head motion during functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) scans is a significant challenge in neuroimaging research, often leading to data quality issues and participant exclusions especially among pediatric and clinical populations1. Head motion exclusions in MRI studies, while necessary for ensuring data quality, may inadvertently introduce biases by disproportionately excluding certain sociodemographic groups2. In the present study, we first examined how the first-level quality control inclusion criterion for resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rsfMRI) and subsequent high-head motion exclusions for subjects who pass first-level quality control relate to sociodemographics at baseline. We then assessed the longitudinal impact of these artifacts, specifically examining whether high-motion status at baseline predicts participant attrition over the 2-year follow-up period.

Methods

Data were obtained from the ABCD Study Data Release 5.1, a large, longitudinal, population-based cohort in the United States3. Our analyses focused on participants with available rsfMRI data at baseline (ages 9-10) and the 2-year follow-up (ages 11-13). We employed both frequentist and Bayesian approaches to examine how head motion-related exclusions and participant retention shape sample composition over time. 

Results

At baseline (N=8,561), high-motion exclusion disproportionately affected Black (48.3%), Hispanic (44.8%), and Asian (43.5%) youth. Adjusted models confirmed higher odds of exclusion for Black (OR=1.23, 95% CI [1.04,1.45], p=.013), Hispanic (OR=1.34 [1.16,1.56], p<.001), and Asian (OR=1.50 [1.07,2.12], p=.020) participants (χ²(4)=21.13, p<.001), though Bayes factors favored the reduced model (BF₁₀=5.2×10⁻⁴). These disparities were absent at the 2-year follow-up (χ²(4)=2.5, p=.64; BF₁₀=1.0×10⁻⁷). Retention analyses showed lower odds of follow-up participation for Black (OR=0.63 [0.52,0.75], p<.001) and Other-identified youth (OR=0.80 [0.67,0.95], p=.012), with strong evidence for modeling Black participants separately (BF₁₀=2189.6). Baseline head motion predicted non-retention (OR=0.63 [0.50,0.80], p<.001), but motion × race interactions were nonsignificant (χ²(4)=6.55, p=.16; BF₁₀=3.6×10⁻⁷). Longitudinal models (N=4,658) showed no racial differences in high-motion status across visits (χ²(4)=5.81, p=.21; BF₁₀=2.1×10⁻⁷), but a decline in motion-related exclusion, consistent with decreased motion across child development as well as increased exclusion and loss to followup for high-motion individuals (baseline vs. follow-up OR=2.70 [2.41,3.02], p<.001; BF₁₀=1.39×10⁷²).

Conclusion

The present study combined cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses to examine relationships between head motion exclusion and demographics in the ABCD study. These results highlight the disproportionate exclusion of Black and Hispanic participants, raising concerns about the generalizability of neuroimaging research. Exclusion criteria based on head motion may inadvertently introduce sampling bias. While motion distortion and artifact correction are crucial for data quality, these practices may conflict with efforts to include groups that are disproportionately affected by current exclusion criteria within neuroimaging research4. Together, these results also highlight the high statistical power of ABCD, and also the fact that methods with different assumptions may not agree, as when frequentist and Bayesian analyses differed in their conclusions. Echoing Dick et al.,5 we think that a more nuanced approach to statistical hypothesis testing is necessary in the context of large datasets like ABCD.
