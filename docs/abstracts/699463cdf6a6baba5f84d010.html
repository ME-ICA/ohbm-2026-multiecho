Introduction

Subtle motor dysfunction is a recognized early feature of many neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease, often preceding cognitive decline by several years.1 Although previous studies have mainly focused on gait and balance impairment, emerging evidence suggests that poor fine motor performance, demonstrated by daily activities requiring coordination, dexterity and precision, is also closely associated with cognitive impairment.2 Additionally, this relationship might reflect a shared reliance on integrated large-scale brain functional networks. However, to which extent specific kinematic features, such as reduced precision or slower speed, relate to network-level neurodegeneration and domain-specific cognitive decline remains to be elucidated. To address this gap, we employed a digitized Archimedes spiral drawing test, comprehensive cognitive tests and resting-state fMRI to explore the common neural basis of fine motor and cognitive functions.

Methods

Analyses were based on data from the Rhineland Study, a prospective, population-based cohort study in Bonn, Germany. A total of 7,086 participants (age range: 30-95 years) with baseline data available on fine motor function, cognitive performance and brain MRI were included, 3,564 of whom had also completed their first follow-up visit. Fine motor function was measured by a digital spiral drawing test on tablets and quantitatively assessed on two dimensions: tracing precision (deviation area) and tracing velocity.3 Global and domain-specific cognitive functions were assessed with a comprehensive cognitive test battery for episodic memory, working memory, processing speed and executive function. Resting-state functional MRI was acquired using two identical 3T Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma MRI scanners.4 After preprocessing and quality control, within-network functional connectivity was generated using the Gordon et al.’s parcellation scheme.5 Series of linear mixed-effect models were conducted to investigate the association between baseline fine motor performance and longitudinal cognitive decline, as well as the association between within-network functional connectivity and fine motor function or cognition. Potential confounders such as age, sex, education, visual acuity and hand grip strength were adjusted. False discovery rate was applied to account for multiple testing.

Results

Over the follow-up time (median: 5.1 years), poor fine motor function at baseline was associated with significantly faster declines in global cognition (tracing area: β = -0.047; 95% CI: -0.062, -0.032, tracing velocity: β = 0.045; 95% CI: 0.030, 0.060) as well as each of the four cognitive domains. Altered functional connectivity within the default mode network, auditory network, cinguloparietal network and frontoparietal network was related to changes in both fine motor and cognitive functions (Figure 1). Further adjustment for brain grey matter volume did not materially change any of these findings. 

Conclusion

Overall, our longitudinal findings from the large, population-based Rhineland Study demonstrate that poor fine motor function is associated with future cognitive decline and that widespread disruptions in brain functional networks, ranging from sensory to high-order cognitive system, might underlie this association. These findings suggest that assessment of fine motor function may help identify individuals in the preclinical stage of neurodegeneration, who are at high risk for subsequent cognitive impairment.
