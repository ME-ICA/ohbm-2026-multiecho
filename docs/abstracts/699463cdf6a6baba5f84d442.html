Introduction

The diffusion MRI (dMRI) signal at low diffusion weightings (b-values) is influenced by the intravoxel incoherent motion (IVIM) effect driven by cerebral blood flow (CBF)1. Recent work indicates that the IVIM effect may persist even at moderate to high b-values (i.e. 1000–2000 s/mm²). A proposed strategy to mitigate perfusion-related dMRI signal contamination is to fit the monoexponential signal model with a non-zero low-b shell (instead of b0). The rationale is that diffusion-weighted signal attenuation at a low b-value predominantly reflects fast-moving spins from blood flow, and applying a minimal diffusion weighting should suppress these contributions2. Depending on local vascular density, replacing b=0 s/mm2 with b=250 s/mm2 causes a reduction in mean diffusivity (MD) estimation of 4.5%, 7.8%, and 4.3% on cortical grey matter (GM), white matter (WM), and thalamus, respectively2. In this study, we used a vasodilatory paradigm to evaluate to what extent this non-zero low-b shell approach suppresses the perfusion contribution to MD estimation. 

Methods

Hypercapnia was used as a vasodilatory stimulus (inhaling 4% CO2) to induce temporary hemodynamic changes in the subject (Figure 1a)3. We included 19 healthy young subjects in this study. dMRI data was collected with AP+PA phase-encoding directions under both baseline and hypercapnic conditions using a Siemens Prisma 3T scanner with TR/TE=3.1s/0.064s, 1.5mm isotropic resolution, 13xb=0s/mm2, 6xb=250s/mm2, 50xb=1000s/mm2, 50xb=2000s/mm2. dMRI data was corrected for motion, eddy current, and susceptibility-induced distortions using FSL’s TOPUP, then used to compute MD using different shell combinations, either using b of 0 as the low-b shell (b0+b1000, b0+b2000) or b=250 as the low-b shell (b250+b1000 and b250+b2000) (Figure 1b). CO2-induced percentage differences in MD were calculated across shell combinations separately on cortical GM and WM as they have different levels of CBF by nature. Since hypercapnia-induced cerebral blood volume (CBV) change may produce a secondary volume change in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), the free-water signal fraction (ffw) was estimated by using the free-water DTI model on the b0+b1000 shell4, and voxelwise CO2-induced ffw change was quantified.

Results

Statistics revealed an overall increase in MD under hypercapnia (Figure 2a, 2b), while brain maps showed heterogeneous CO2 effect with the effect directions and magnitude varying across tissue types and b-values (Figure 2c). MD estimated using shell combinations including b=250s/mm2 as the low-b shell (MDb250+bxxxx) was consistently lower than MD estimated with a low-b value of 0s/mm2 (MDb0+bxxxx). This is true on both cortical GM and WM, yielding positive MDdiff (MDb0+bxxxx - MDb250+bxxxx) across all shell combinations and gas conditions (Figure 1c). 

The differences between MD estimated using a lower-b shell of 0 and 250 s/mm2 were larger under hypercapnia, with significant increases on cortical GM (p=0.0233 and 0.0134 for MDb0+b1000 and MDb0,+b2000, respectively) and in WM (p=0.008 and 0.002 for MDb0+b1000 and MDb0+b2000, respectively). Representative axial slices (Figure 1d) show predominantly positive MDdiff,  with localized negative MDdiff in deep WM and subcortical GM regions. These negative MDdiff values were more pronounced at upper-end b-value=1000s/mm2 and under baseline conditions. 

Identifying regions of positive and negative CO2-induced MD change (∆MD) for the b0+b1000 combination, we examined how a change in the lower-b shell affected ∆MD in these same regions. Positive ∆MD was significantly higher with b250+bxxxx (Figure 2c): In WM, positive ∆MD for b250+b1000 was higher than for b0+b1000 by 74.78% (p<0.0001) and, higher by 21.58% for b250+b2000 than for b0+b2000 (p<0.0001). On cortical GM, this increase in ∆MD was only significant for b250+b1000 versus b0+b1000 (65.34%, p<0.0001). More pronounced negative ∆MD was also observed for b250+bxxxx (Figure 2c): in WM, the mean negative ∆MD was more pronounced by 86.23% (p<0.0001) for b250+b1000 compared to b0+b1000, and 23.6% (p<0.0001) more pronounced for b250+b2000 compared to b0+b2000. On cortical GM, b250+b1000 generated a more pronounced negative ∆MD by 29.96% (p=0.0002) than b0+b1000. Voxelwise analysis revealed a systemic CO2-induced elevation in ffw across the brain, with the effect highlighting the WM regions (Figure 2f).

Conclusion

DTI model fitting with b250+bxxxx combinations reduced overall MD estimates compared to b0+bxxxx combinations (i.e. MDdiff > 0), more so under hypercapnia (Figure 1c), likely reflecting increased cerebral blood flow (CBF) and therefore larger contribution from fast-moving spins during hypercapnia. MDdiff was also lower when upper b-values=2000s/mm2 (Figure 1c), consistent with reduced sensitivity to fast flow at higher diffusion weightings. These findings reflect a suppression of perfusion contributions with b=250 as the low-b shell. However, MDdiff was not always positive, suggesting that blood did not only contribute to MD through velocity. Blood T2 likely also played a role. The negative MDdiff observed in deeper tissue layers (Figure 1d) was more pronounced at baseline. These negative values were also emphasized for b1000 than for b2000 combinations, translating to lower MD estimated at b0+bxxxx than b250+bxxxx. This is contrary to the widely assumed MD over-estimation from perfusion contribution, and may reflect a contribution by blood T2 at b0. If blood T2 is lower than tissue T2, and at baseline than at hypercapnia, which is expected due to blood-oxygenation effects, then the T2-weighted signal at b=0s/mm2 (S(b=0)) could be lower than S(b=250), leading to this finding. This effect appears confined to deeper brain tissue, whereby blood flow is lower and thus may contribute more to S(b=250) and S(b=1000) than faster flow. Indeed, this negative-MDdiff effect is reduced when using b=2000 instead of 1000, and many of the impacted voxels were no longer negative during hypercapnia (Figure 1d). This reversal during hypoxia may reflect an increase in S(b=0) due to increased blood T2, but another possibility is an increased  free water signal contribution during hypercapnia, as suggested by the observation of increased ffw during hypercapnia on the same regions that the reversal was found (Figure 2f).

Moreover, contrary to expectations of general MD increase, hypercapnia either increased or decreased MD estimates, and in a manner depending on tissue types. MD reductions were more commonly observed on cortical GM despite the CBF increase (Figure 2c). This could be explained by a displacement of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) secondary to vasodilation. MD increases, however, may reflect a combination of hemodynamics and a dynamic in microscopic CSF flow. Based on CO2-induced ∆ffw results, the positive ∆MD could partilly be attributed, in addition to an increased perfusion, also to an increase in signal from the free water compartment (i.e. interstitial fluid, perivascular spaces) in WM cortical GM. Interestingly, this effect was more pronounced when using a non-zero low-b shell (Figure 2d), suggesting that MDb250+bxxxx may still retain a higher contribution from CSF relative to from blood to MD. To clarify the mechanisms underlying the hypercapnia-related increase in ffw, additional analyses will focus on characterizing CBF changes induced by CO₂. Overall, while using a non-zero low-b shell can reduce perfusion-driven overestimation of MD by suppressing contributions from fast flow, it does not eliminate perfusion contributions entirely, as blood can also contribute to MD estimates through T2. Moreover, some portion of CSF flow that is slower than blood flow remains, and may still contribute to MD biases that mirror blood volume, adding to the residual perfusion contribution. Consequently, the sources of MD are complex especially in regions of higher blood or interstitial CSF volume.
