Introduction

Source credibility plays a critical role in how persuasive messages are perceived, particularly in healthcare contexts where false information can lead to harmful decisions1. Traditionally, source credibility comprises two primary dimensions: expertise (perceived capability to make accurate assertions) and trustworthiness (perception that the communicator believes in their statements' validity). While highly credible sources are generally more persuasive, the relative contribution of expertise versus trustworthiness remains debated, especially in medical communication.

The current study examines how these dimensions affect persuasiveness of medical information. By manipulating expertise (working experience) and trustworthiness (customer ratings) among fictional doctors providing cues for medical statements evaluation, we isolate each dimension's relative impact for the communicators from the same professional field. We also examined whether cognitive reflection, need for cognition, and conformity modulate credibility evaluation.

To investigate underlying neurocognitive mechanisms, we employed EEG—an approach remarkably scarce in source credibility research2. We focused on the feedback-related negativity (FRN), a frontocentral negative deflection occurring 200–300 ms post-feedback, enhanced when feedback contradicts expectations.

We hypothesized that higher doctor credibility would lead to greater opinion change and cue persuasiveness. Neurophysiologically, we expected stronger FRN responses to "False" cues from highly credible doctors, representing violation of source-based expectations for accuracy.

Methods

The study consisted of two main parts: EEG study and a post-experimental session during which participants completed several questionnaires. After providing informed consent and reading the instructions, participants evaluated medical statements both before and after being shown a doctor's profile and the doctor's cue, and rated the cue's persuasiveness. After the EEG experiment, participants completed three questionnaires: the CRT, the NFCS, and the CS.The study was approved by the local university ethics committee and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki developed by the World Medical Association. 

Participants 

Thirty-six right-handed participants (28 females, aged 18–35, M = 20.4, SD = 1.78) were included into the study, after excluding 7 due to noisy EEG recordings. All had normal/corrected-to-normal vision, no neurological/psychiatric history, and confirmed no medical education or employment background.

Stimuli 

The stimulus set comprised 120 validated medical statements (true/false, positive/negative valence) covering vaccines, vitamins, GMOs, lifestyle, and alternative medicine3. We created 16 fictional male doctor profiles systematically varying in expertise (work experience: high 18–22 years vs. low 1–5 years) and trustworthiness (patient ratings: high 4–5 stars vs. low 1–2 stars), creating four experimental conditions through factorial combination. Doctors' cues ("True"/"False") always accurately reflected statement veracity. Three additional filler doctors with moderate values (8–12 years experience, 3 stars) were included to mask the experimental design. Doctors' cues ("True"/"False") always accurately reflected statement veracity.

Procedure 

Participants first evaluated medical statements on a 7-point Likert scale (1="absolutely disagree" to 7="absolutely agree"). After a fixation cross (1000 ms), a doctor's profile card displaying initials, work experience, and patient rating appeared (5000 ms). Following a variable fixation (250–500 ms), the doctor's cue ("True" or "False") was presented (1500 ms). Participants then re-evaluated the statement and rated cue persuasiveness (1="absolutely non-persuasive" to 7="absolutely persuasive"). This sequence repeated for 120 counterbalanced trials. Participants also completed the Cognitive Reflection Test, Need for Cognition Scale, and Conformity Scale.

EEG Recording and Processing 

EEG was recorded using 64 actiCap electrodes (Brain Products) in a standard 10–20 montage with mastoid references (TP9/TP10), digitized at 500 Hz. Data were preprocessed in MNE-Python (v.1.8.0): band-pass filtered (1–45 Hz), notch-filtered (50 Hz), bad channels interpolated, and artifacts removed via ICA (infomax algorithm). ERPs were computed from epochs (−400 to 1000 ms) time-locked to cue onset, baseline-corrected (−400–0 ms), and grand-averaged.

Statistical Analyses 

Behavioral: Friedman tests examined differences in opinion change and persuasiveness across credibility conditions. Pairwise comparisons used t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Bonferroni correction. Linear mixed regression models assessed predictors (experience, rating, cue type, CRT, NFCS, CS) of opinion change and persuasiveness.

ERP: Cluster-based permutation F-tests (1024 permutations) examined main effects and interactions of cue type and credibility conditions, with cluster-forming threshold p < .05. Repeated-measures ANOVA tested effects of cue type, experience, and rating on ERP amplitude in the 150–210 ms time window. LORETA source localization (MNI152 template) identified cortical sources of observed effects.

Results

Behavioural results

Opinion change. The Friedman test revealed significant differences in opinion change across four doctor credibility conditions (χ²(3) = 28.874, p < 0.001). Pairwise comparisons (Bonferroni-corrected) demonstrated that high experience/high rating doctors produced significantly greater opinion change than low experience/low rating doctors (p < 0.001), high experience/low rating doctors (p < 0.001), and low experience/high rating doctors (p = 0.016). Low experience/high rating doctors also elicited greater opinion change than low experience/low rating doctors (p < 0.001). This pattern was more pronounced for "True" cues (χ²(3) = 30.277, p < 0.001) compared to "False" cues (χ²(3) = 6.380, p = 0.09). Linear regression revealed significant effects of experience (β = −0.200, p = 0.016), rating (β = −0.441, p < 0.001), cue type (β = −0.382, p < 0.001), and conformity (β = 0.114, p = 0.047). Rating demonstrated a stronger effect than experience, and higher conformity scores were associated with greater opinion change.

Persuasiveness. The Friedman test revealed significant differences in persuasiveness ratings across credibility conditions (χ²(3) = 64.633, p < 0.001). High experience/high rating doctors received significantly higher persuasiveness ratings compared to all other conditions (all p < 0.001). This pattern was consistent for both "True" and "False" cues. Linear regression showed that both experience (β = −0.671, p < 0.001) and rating (β = −1.499, p < 0.001) significantly predicted persuasiveness, with rating demonstrating a stronger effect than experience.

EEG Results

Cluster-based permutation analysis. For "False" cues, a significant difference in ERP amplitudes was found between high experience/high rating and low experience/low rating doctors within the 158–214 ms time window (F-mean = 9.99, F-max = 13.62, p = 0.02), with high-credibility doctors eliciting a stronger negative deflection, similar to an early FRN response. A resembling effect emerged when comparing high experience/high rating versus high experience/low rating doctors (162–204 ms, F-mean = 6.30, F-max = 7.88, p = 0.047). Critically, no significant clusters were observed for "True" cues in any comparison (all p > 0.05), indicating that credibility modulation of ERP responses was specific to "False" cues. No significant differences were found between other credibility condition comparisons for either cue type.

ANOVA. Repeated-measures ANOVA on ERP amplitude (150–210 ms) revealed a significant Cue type × Rating interaction (F = 9.446, p = 0.004), supplementing the cluster-based permutation analysis results. No significant main effects were observed for cue type (F = 1.632, p = 0.210), experience (F = 0.065, p = 0.799), or rating (F = 1.166, p = 0.288).

Source localization. LORETA analysis (170–180 ms) revealed distinct activation patterns for credibility-modulated feedback processing. For "False" cues, the difference map (high credibility minus low credibility) showed negative activation predominantly in left temporal and inferior frontal regions, consistent with an FRN-like response. A similar pattern emerged when comparing high experience/high rating versus high experience/low rating doctors, with negative activation in bilateral frontal and left temporal regions.

Conclusion

Both expertise (work experience) and trustworthiness (patient rating) significantly influenced opinion change and persuasiveness evaluation regarding medical statements. However, rating demonstrated a stronger effect than experience across both behavioral measures. This finding aligns with patient-centered research suggesting that while a healthcare provider's expertise is often assumed, trustworthiness must be earned and serves as a primary determinant of patient acceptance of medical advice4. In the context of complex medical information where outcomes are personal, participants likely relied on trustworthiness as a heuristic cue to manage uncertainty and perceived risk. 

Additionally, conformity as a personality trait significantly predicted opinion change. Individuals higher in conformity demonstrate greater susceptibility to social influence and are more likely to alter their opinions to align with perceived experts. Crucially, the conformity tendency may be directly harnessed by the trustworthiness dimension: a high star rating serves as a salient expression of aggregated peer consensus, meaning that more conforming individuals are particularly influenced by this credibility factor when deciding to change their opinion.

At the neurophysiological level, "False" cues from highly credible doctors elicited a significant negative deflection resembling an early FRN response (150–210 ms), and this effect was specifically modulated by rating rather than experience. We interpret these findings as reflecting expectation-based mechanisms in source credibility processing. When evaluating information from highly credible doctors, participants form stronger expectations about information accuracy. When such sources provide "False" cues, this represents a profound violation of source-based expectations, engaging surprise detection and conflict monitoring systems manifested as enhanced FRN amplitude5. The earlier latency compared to the typical FRN (200–300 ms) likely reflects rapid expectation formation based on sequential presentation of credibility information followed by feedback cues .

To summarise, our results demonstrate that doctors' credibility characteristics modulate both behavioral responses and neural processing of evaluative feedback on medical statements. Trustworthiness (rating) emerged as a more influential dimension than expertise (experience) in driving opinion change, persuasiveness evaluation, and ERP modulation. These findings contribute to understanding how source credibility dimensions differentially affect medical information processing and highlight the neurocognitive mechanisms underlying credibility-based information evaluation.
