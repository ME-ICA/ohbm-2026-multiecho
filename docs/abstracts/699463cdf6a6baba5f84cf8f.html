Introduction

Schizophrenia (SZ) and bipolar disorder (BP) are severe mental illnesses that typically emerge in late adolescence and early adulthood and are increasingly understood as disorders of disrupted neurodevelopment. Ventricular volumes and subcortical structures, including the thalamus, hippocampus, amygdala, and basal ganglia, show robust volumetric abnormalities in SZ and BP,1 implicating atypical development of circuits involved in affect regulation, motivation, and cognition. Adolescents at familial high-risk for SZ (FHR-SZ) or BP (FHR-BP) carry substantial inherited liability but have not yet reached the typical window of illness onset, offering a unique opportunity to investigate early neurodevelopmental deviations. MRI findings at age ~12 in this cohort showed sex-specific subcortical differences in the thalamus, hippocampus, amygdala, and nucleus accumbens that were not explained by pubertal stage or psychopathology (Hansen et al., in prep), suggesting early markers of familial vulnerability. Although cross-sectional studies in FHR offspring have identified subcortical alterations,1 it is not known whether maturational slopes diverge in adolescents at FHR. Adolescence is a period of pronounced, sex-dependent subcortical maturation in the general population,2 and SZ and BP differ by sex in age of onset and clinical expression.3 Thus, familial risk may interact with typical development in sex-specific ways, yet no study has prospectively examined subcortical development in FHR youth prior to the typical onset window of SZ or BP. Here, we investigated longitudinal age-related changes in subcortical volumes between ages ~12 and ~16 in FHR-SZ, FHR-BP, and population-based controls (PBC). We tested whether developmental slopes differ by group and whether these patterns are sex dependent. We hypothesised that FHR adolescents would show altered maturational slopes in subcortical structures and ventricular volumes relative to controls, with effects differing by sex.

Methods

This longitudinal study is part of the Danish High Risk and Resilience Study (VIA) cohort. The first wave (VIA 7) comprised 522 children at age 7 born to a parent with SZ (FHR-SZ, n = 202), BP (FHR-BP, n = 120), or neither of these disorders (PBC, n = 200). The follow-up waves included MRI with 297 children at age 11-12 (VIA 11; FHR-SZ: 107; FHR-BP: 71; PBC: 119; 49.2% female) and 252 adolescents at age 15-16 years (VIA 15; FHR-SZ: 94; FHR-BP: 62; PBC: 96; 50.4% female) with good quality structural scans being included in the current study sample. Behavioural problems and general functioning were assessed with the Child Behaviour Checklist (CBCL–caregiver report) and the Children’s Global Assessment Scale (CGAS), respectively. Pubertal stage was determined using the self-report Tanner scale. Structural MRI was performed on 3T Siemens scanners at two sites in Copenhagen (Danish Research Centre for Magnetic Resonance; DRCMR) and Aarhus (Centre of Functionally Integrative Neuroscience; CFIN). High-resolution T1-weighted images were derived from 3D Magnetisation-prepared 2 Rapid Gradient–Echo (MP2RAGE) sequences with harmonised parameters at the two sites. Images were inspected by trained personnel and were excluded for artefacts and pathology. The longitudinal FreeSurfer pipeline (v7.4.1) was used to segment the bilateral caudate, putamen, pallidum, accumbens, thalamus, hippocampus, and amygdala, as well as the lateral and third ventricles, to extract their volumes. Total brain volume (TBV) was also extracted. We conducted rigorous quality control to ensure segmentations were of good quality. Left and right regions-of-interest (ROIs) were averaged to derive bilateral volumes for each region and time point. Our statistical analysis plan is preregistered at the Open Science Framework (doi: 10.17605/OSF.IO/TFK5N). To investigate longitudinal changes in subcortical and ventricular volumes, we used linear-mixed effects (LME) models implemented in lmer (lme4 package) in R (version 4.5.0). Fixed effects and interactions were evaluated using anova (lme4). All models included fixed effects for MRI site and total Euler number (TEN), a proxy for image quality, and random intercepts for participants nested within families to account for repeated measures and sibling relatedness. Age was mean-centred separately for VIA 11 and VIA 15 to facilitate interpretation of model intercepts. Descriptive models assessed age-by-sex interactions and age effects within each sex for each ROI. We implemented three model sets. Note that lower-order terms are included automatically: 

Model 1: ROIvolume ~ Group × Age + Group × Sex + Age × Sex + Site +TEN — to test whether age-related developmental slopes differed across FHR-SZ, FHR-BP, and PBC groups and whether group effects varied by sex. 

Model 2: ROIvolume ~ Group × Age + Site + TEN — to examine group-by-age interactions separately in males and females to further characterise potential sex differences.

Model 3: ROIvolume ~ Group × Age × Sex + Site + TEN — evaluated sex-dependent group differences in developmental slopes.

When group-by-sex or group-by-age interactions were not significant, interaction terms were removed to test only for the main effects of group. All analyses were repeated with and without TBV adjustment. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied separately to primary (four a priori ROIs: thalamus, amygdala, hippocampus, accumbens) and secondary (nine ROIs: caudate, putamen, pallidum, lateral and third ventricles, plus the four a priori ones) hypotheses. Partial η² (effectsize package) indexed effect size, with ≈ 0.01, ≈ 0.06, and ≥ 0.14 interpreted as small, medium, and large effects, respectively. Post-hoc estimated marginal means and age-related slopes were obtained using emmeans and emtrends, with pairwise contrasts (Cohen’s d, 95% CI, p) for both mean group differences and developmental slopes. Pairwise tests and exploratory follow-ups were not FDR-corrected.

Results

Demographic analyses confirmed that groups matched on age, sex, and site. At VIA 11 and VIA 15, both FHR groups showed higher CBCL total scores (FHR-SZ: MVIA11/15 = 20.9/23.3; FHR-BP: MVIA11/15 = 20.0/18.7) and lower CGAS scores (FHR-SZ: MVIA11/15 = 67.7/67.1; FHR-BP: MVIA11/15 = 69.6/70.6) than PBC (CBCL: MVIA11/15 = 12.0/13.1; CGAS: MVIA11/15 = 75.6/77.7; all p ≤ 0.032), indicating greater symptom burden and poorer functioning. Descriptive LMMs testing for sex-by-age effects showed that males had larger volumes in all ROIs compared to females (F ≥ 13.40, p < 0.001). Sex-by-age interactions were identified in the thalamus, amygdala, hippocampus, caudate, putamen, and pallidum (F ≥ 12.92, p < 0.001, η2p ≥ 0.061), replicating previous findings showing that subcortical structures follow distinct age-related growth patterns in males and females.2 Examining our hypotheses, we found no significant group-by-age or group-by-sex interactions in the full sample in Model 1 (F ≤ 2.99, p ≥ 0.052, η2p ≤ 0.017). Likewise, Model 3 showed no significant group-by-age-by-sex effects (p’s ≥ 0.067). When stratifying our sample by sex, no group-by-age effects were observed in males before or after TBV adjustment (p’s ≥ 0.176). In females, we observed a group-by-age interaction in the thalamus (F = 4.09, p = 0.020, qFDR = 0.078, η2p = 0.073) and in the hippocampus (F = 4.46, p = 0.014, qFDR = 0.056, η2p = 0.080), the former before and the latter after TBV adjustment. Next, we tested main effects of group in the sex-stratified analyses. In males, group effects at the uncorrected p-level were detected in the thalamus, hippocampus, accumbens, caudate, and pallidum (3.18 ≤ F ≤ 4.90; 0.018 ≤ p ≤ 0.044; 0.059 ≤ q ≤ 0.066, 0.035 ≤ η2p ≤ 0.042). These effects disappeared after TBV adjustment. We did not observe any group effects in any of the ROIs in females (p ≥ 0.126). None of the tests survived FDR correction. Figure 1A–B show the volumetric slopes in all ROIs with separate lines for each group in males and females. 

In follow-up analyses, we performed pairwise post-hoc comparisons of estimated marginal means for the observed group effects in males and estimated marginal mean volumetric change for the observed group-by-age interactions in females. In males, FHR-SZ participants had smaller thalamic and hippocampal volumes than PBCs (t ≤ -2.217, p ≤ 0.028, d ≤ -0.374), while FHR-BP males had larger caudate volumes than PBCs both before and after TBV correction (t ≥ 2.23, p ≤ 0.027, d ≥ 0.422). FHR-BP males also showed larger thalamus, hippocampus, accumbens, caudate, and pallidum volumes relative to FHR-SZ males (t ≥ 2.04, p ≤ 0.042, d ≥ 0.395). In the thalamus, FHR-SZ females had slower age-related decline compared to PBC females (t = 2.85, p = 0.005, d = 0.227). In the hippocampus, FHR-BP females had attenuated age-related increase compared to PBC females (t = -2.97, p = 0.004, d = -0.263). Figure 1C–D present bar plots of effect sizes for FHR-SZ vs PBC and FHR-BP vs PBC comparisons in the nine ROIs, illustrating mean volumes in males (bottom left) and volumetric change in females (bottom right).

Finally, we evaluated whether pubertal maturation contributed to the observed effects by adding Tanner stage and the interval between Tanner assessment and MRI as fixed effects in the models with significant findings (at the uncorrected p-threshold). All effects persisted: in females, both the thalamic (F = 4.42, p = 0.015, η2p = 0.084) and hippocampal group-by-age effects (F = 3.13, p = 0.048, η2p = 0.062) remained significant. Similarly, in males, all observed group effects remained (3.79 ≤ F ≤ 4.54, 0.012 ≤ p ≤ 0.024, 0.042 ≤ η2p ≤ 0.049).

Conclusion

This longitudinal study suggests that being at FHR for SZ and BP is associated with sex-specific patterns of subcortical development during early to mid-adolescence. In males, group differences in subcortical volumes that were already present at age ~12 remained stable through age ~16, particularly in the thalamus, hippocampus, accumbens, caudate, and pallidum. These male-specific effects disappeared after adjusting for total brain volume, suggesting that in males, familial risk may influence global brain growth that occurs earlier in development, rather than region-specific subcortical changes during adolescence. FHR-BP females also showed early volumetric differences at age ~12, specifically larger thalamic and caudate volumes relative to PBC females. However, these effects were not evident at age ~16. Instead, females exhibited divergent developmental slopes: FHR-SZ females showed attenuated age-related thalamic decline, while FHR-BP females showed reduced hippocampal growth compared to PBC peers. These findings suggest that familial liability in females is associated with deviations in maturational trajectories in early to mid-adolescence, consistent with earlier subcortical brain maturation in females. As the third neuroimaging wave at ~19–20 years (VIA 19) is currently underway, it will be possible to test whether divergent trajectories emerge later in males, aligning with delayed male neurodevelopment. 

The thalamus and hippocampus are two of the most commonly affected subcortical regions in both SZ and BP, typically showing reduced volumes in patient samples, in particular, lower hippocampal and thalamic volumes in SZ,4,5 with some effects showing sex specificity in males.5 These structures are key hubs that support sensory integration, affect regulation, memory, and cognitive control, functions compromised in SZ and BP. In our cohort, we observed differences in early to mid-adolescence, well before typical illness onset, suggesting that these regions are particularly sensitive to familial risk across sexes. They therefore represent important targets for future microstructural and connectivity analyses to probe underlying neurobiological mechanisms.

Our observed effects were at an uncorrected threshold; however, similar regional differences were present at baseline (VIA 11), which supports their potential relevance. Importantly, adjusting for pubertal maturation did not change our findings, suggesting that observed group differences are not driven by puberty per se and may reflect endophenotypic neurodevelopmental signatures. We plan to extend these analyses and assess whether these group effects remain independent of broader psychopathology and whether behavioural or clinical measures moderate or mediate group-specific trajectories. Of particular interest is the prevalence of psychotic experiences, i.e., subclinical expressions of psychotic-like symptoms that fall below the threshold for a diagnosable disorder. Given that only a subset FHR adolescents will transition to psychosis, incorporating psychotic experiences will allow us to determine whether individuals who exhibit these early clinical signs show altered development. Overall, our findings suggest that familial vulnerability to severe mental illness may affect subcortical development through distinct sex-specific pathways from early to mid-adolescence. Such patterns may contribute to known sexual dimorphisms in the onset and expression of SZ and BP and may index early neurodevelopmental mechanisms through which inherited risk unfolds.
