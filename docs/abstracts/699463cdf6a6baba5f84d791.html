Introduction

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) plays a central role in diagnosing, monitoring, and assessing risk for Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias (ADRD). With the recent approval of amyloid-targeted therapies, MRI has also become essential for treatment eligibility and for monitoring possible adverse effects.1 Despite this, most imaging evidence still comes from research cohorts that follow strict MRI protocols and include selected participants, which limits population diversity and generalizability. At the same time, large numbers of MRI scans are routinely acquired in health care settings for clinical purposes.2 These real-world imaging data represent a substantial but underused resource for ADRD research. Their use is limited by the absence of established methods for locating, extracting, and processing heterogeneous clinical MRI scans, and by uncertainty about whether such scans can provide reliable volumetric biomarkers. Routine MRI differs from research imaging in pulse sequences, acquisition protocols, image quality, and artifacts, which raises concerns about whether standard dementia-related markers can be derived with sufficient accuracy.

In this study, we assessed whether real-world MRI can support ADRD research by comparing volumetric biomarkers extracted from routine clinical scans in the population-based OSTPRE cohort of Finnish women with the same markers extracted from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) research cohort, where imaging follows a standardized protocol. We also describe the key steps required to assemble and quality-check real-world MRI data and to link them with cognitive status using the health register information. Full methodological details are available in the published paper.3

Methods

We used brain MRI data from the population-based OSTPRE cohort, which includes all women born in 1932–1941 and living in Kuopio County, in Finland  (N=14,220). Originally, the study aimed to find risk factors for osteoporosis. The use of a total population ensures diversity, equity, and inclusion in the target group. From the regional picture archiving and communications system (PACS) maintained by Kuopio University Hospital, we identified 4,422 head MRI visits for 3,076 participants between 2003 and 2022. After extracting DICOM files, pseudonymizing them, and converting them to NIfTI format, we identified T1-weighted scans through metadata searches and resolution checks. Scans were organized using the Brain Imaging Data Structure (BIDS).

T1-weighted images were processed with CAT124 for segmentation, normalization, and volumetry. Automatic quality control (≥68%) and comparison with SynthSeg5 were used to exclude low-quality or discordant scans. This resulted in 2,434 high-quality scans from 1,885 participants (Fig. 1). Cognitive status at the time of imaging was determined from national and local health registers based on diagnostic codes and classified as no memory complaints (NMC) (n=967), subjective memory complaints (SMC) (n=479), mild cognitive impairment (MCI) (n=202), or dementia (n=786). ADNI served as the reference cohort and included 916 White female participants with baseline MRI, classified as NMC (n=231), SMC (n=158), MCI (n=380), or dementia (n=147), as the OSTPRE cohort consisted solely of White women. Both datasets were processed using the same pipeline.

We analyzed associations between volume measures (total gray matter, hippocampus, ventricles) and cognitive status using a linear regression model. Standardized volume was the dependent variable, and cognitive status and cohort were the main independent variables. Models were adjusted for age, total intracranial volume (TIV), scanner manufacturer, and field strength. Non-linearity for age and TIV was modeled using cubic B-splines. Because some OSTPRE participants contributed multiple scans, sandwich estimators were used to adjust for clustering. Predictive margins of measures for cognitive status and cohort were used in the comparisons of contrasts of interest (between cognitive status categories within and between OSTPRE and ADNI. 

Results

The pattern of volumetric differences across cognitive status groups was similar in OSTPRE and ADNI (Fig. 2). In both cohorts, hippocampal and gray matter volumes were lower, and ventricular volumes higher, with more advanced cognitive impairment, although variance was larger in the real-world data (Fig. 2a).

In ADNI, standardized volume measures did not differ between NMC and SMC, but NMC was significantly different from MCI and dementia (p ≤ 0.002), and MCI differed from dementia (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2b). In OSTPRE, NMC and SMC differed in total gray matter and hippocampus volumes (p < 0.001), but not ventricle volume (p = 0.161). NMC differed from both MCI and dementia for all measures (p < 0.001), while MCI differed from dementia for all measures except total gray matter (p = 0.242). In both cohorts, the direction of effects followed expectations: NMC ≥ SMC > MCI > dementia for gray matter and hippocampus, and NMC ≤ SMC < MCI < dementia for ventricles (Fig. 2b).

Between-cohort comparisons showed similar overall patterns, with differences in effect sizes for specific contrasts. NMC–SMC and NMC–MCI differences in total gray matter and hippocampus volumes were more pronounced in OSTPRE, whereas MCI–dementia differences were larger in ADNI. Nonlinear age and total intracranial volume effects were also consistent with expected trajectories, with hippocampus and gray matter volumes declining and ventricular volume increasing with age.

Conclusion

Routine clinical MRI acquired over 20 years can provide quantitative volumetric markers that align with those from a standardized research cohort. These results support the use of real-world imaging data for ADRD research and demonstrate that established volumetric biomarkers derived from heterogeneous clinical scans retain their expected relationships with cognitive status.
