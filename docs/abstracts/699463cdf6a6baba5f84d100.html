Introduction

The longitudinal study of neurodegenerative diseases presents substantial methodological challenges, as these conditions manifest through progressive brain changes. To characterise disease progression in individual patients, researchers acquire imaging, biological, and neuropsychological data at multiple time points, since isolated assessments provide insufficient information. This approach enables quantification of crucial biomarkers, notably annualised rates of brain atrophy, which serve as sensitive indicators of disease progression.

However, quantifying these biomarkers is challenging. Given that annualised atrophy rates during early disease stages typically measure around 2%1, reliably detecting such subtle structural changes requires image processing methods of high precision. Longitudinal preprocessing aims to address this challenge by leveraging temporal dependencies across multiple scans from a single subject to maintain anatomical consistency, contrasting with cross-sectional approaches that treat timepoints as independent images. Longitudinal workflows typically create a subject-specific template by coregistering and averaging images, which is then processed through a standard cross-sectional pipeline to initialise subsequent analyses. While this core principle remains consistent, implementations vary in registration and segmentation strategies. Despite numerous approaches being developed and compared, no consensus exists on which methodology offers superior accuracy and reliability.

To address this gap, we conduct a comprehensive evaluation of several widely-used, freely-available software tools for both cross-sectional and longitudinal processing of T1-weighted MRI data, namely FreeSurfer2, SAMSEG3, ANTs4, ANTsPyNet5, SPM126 and CAT127, using test-retest datasets for robust assessment. Our objective is to identify which methods demonstrate the greatest robustness and provide evidence-based recommendations for users in clinical research settings.

Methods

Evaluated Methods 

Longitudinal preprocessing typically follows a general framework consisting of: (1) creation of a subject-specific template through iterative coregistration of timepoints, (2) cross-sectional processing of the template, and (3) processing of individual timepoints aligned to the template space. While numerous longitudinal preprocessing methods implementing this framework exist in the neuroimaging literature, we focused our evaluation on a subset of widely-used tools.Our selection criteria required that methods process T1-weighted images, handle any number of timepoints (i.e. more than 2), provide whole-brain analysis, and provide openly available, open-source code. Six tools met these criteria: FreeSurfer, SAMSEG, ANTs, ANTsPyNet, SPM12, and CAT12. Each method was tested using default parameters where possible, supplemented by relevant documentation and code. For incomplete specifications, assumptions were made to complete the pipelines.

Evaluation of Preprocessing Methods

To assess the reliability and reproducibility of the six preprocessing methods across cross-sectional and longitudinal approaches, we designed a test-retest experiment. This evaluation framework is based on anatomically identical T1-weighted MRI scans acquired from the same subjects within short time intervals. The underlying principle is that a reliable preprocessing method should yield highly consistent results between test and retest sessions for the same subject.

We employed the absolute symmetric percentage change (ASPC) as our primary reproducibility metric, defined as ASPC = 100 × (x₂−x₁)/[(x₁+x₂)/2], where x₁ and x₂ represent measurements (e.g., regional volume) obtained from test and retest scans, respectively. This metric quantifies the relative change between repeated measurements, with lower ASPC values indicating superior reproducibility and method robustness.

We evaluated each method at two complementary levels of analysis. At the tissue level, we compared whole-brain gray and white matter volumes, outputs available across all six methods. At the regional level, we analysed structures particularly relevant to neurodegenerative diseases. Due to atlas compatibility constraints, we used the Desikan-Killiany-Tourville (DKT) atlas for FreeSurfer, SAMSEG, ANTs, and ANTsPyNet, while the Neuromorphometrics atlas was used for SPM12 and CAT12. We selected regions common to both atlases that are either typically affected by neurodegeneration or expected to remain stable across short intervals: lateral ventricles, thalamus, hippocampus, putamen, caudate, pallidum, amygdala, and brainstem.

At both tissue and regional levels, we assessed if cross-sectional and longitudinal ASPC differed significantly for each method using a Welch t-test with Benjamini-Hochberg correction.

Study Datasets

We employed three publicly available datasets to evaluate preprocessing robustness, using exclusively test-retest T1-weighted scans. The MIRIAD8 dataset comprises repeated scans acquired at 1.5T magnetic field strength from 23 cognitively healthy control subjects (48% female, mean age 69.7 ± 7.2 years) and 46 individuals diagnosed with Alzheimer's disease (59% female, mean age 69.4 ± 7.1 years). Both KIRBY219 and HCP10 datasets were acquired at 3T. KIRBY21 includes 21 subjects (11 male, 10 female, age range 22–61 years), while the Human Connectome Project (HCP) dataset comprises 30 subjects (age range 22–35 years). Together, these datasets provide comprehensive coverage of field strengths, scan quality levels, subject populations (including both healthy controls and disease populations across different age groups), and temporal intervals relevant to clinical and research applications.

Results

Tissue-Level Analysis

In MIRIAD, most mean ASPC values remained below 2%, except FreeSurfer which exceeded 3% for white matter (WM) and 1.5% for grey matter (GM). No single method consistently showed significant improvements of longitudinal over cross-sectional (CS) versions for both tissue types. SAMSEG's longitudinal version was significantly better for WM but showed no significant difference for GM. ANTsPyNet's longitudinal version was significantly better for both WM and GM. FreeSurfer's longitudinal version was significantly better for GM but not for WM. ANTs, CAT12, and SPM12 showed no significant improvements for either tissue type.

In KIRBY21, mean ASPC values remained below 1.5% across all methods, substantially better than MIRIAD. We observed a similar pattern to MIRIAD with limited improvements between CS and longitudinal pipelines. ANTsPyNet's longitudinal version was significantly better for both WM and GM. FreeSurfer's longitudinal version was significantly better for GM but not for WM. SAMSEG, ANTs, CAT12, and SPM12 showed no significant improvements for either tissue type.

In HCP, most mean ASPC values were below 1%, except FreeSurfer's CS version which reached 2% for both tissues, showing greater variability than in KIRBY21. We observed improved method performance between CS and longitudinal pipelines compared to MIRIAD and KIRBY21. FreeSurfer's longitudinal version was significantly better for both WM and GM. SAMSEG's longitudinal version was similarly better for both WM and GM. ANTsPyNet's longitudinal version was significantly better for WM but not for GM. ANTs, CAT12, and SPM12 showed no significant improvements across all three cohorts for either tissue type.

Regional-Level AnalysisLongitudinal versus Cross-sectional Comparison

At the regional level, longitudinal methods generally outperformed their CS counterparts in subcortical GM and brainstem regions across all three cohorts.

In MIRIAD, brainstem and lateral ventricle mean ASPC values remained below 2% except for CAT12, while subcortical GM values clustered around 2.5% across all methods. FreeSurfer's longitudinal version was significantly better in subcortical GM, brainstem, and lateral ventricles. SAMSEG's longitudinal version was significantly better in subcortical GM and brainstem. ANTs, ANTsPyNet, SPM12, and CAT12 showed no significant improvements across any region.

In KIRBY21, mean ASPC values remained below 2% for brainstem and below 2.5% for lateral ventricles and subcortical GM, substantially better than in MIRIAD, except for CAT12 which exceeded these thresholds. Regarding longitudinal versus CS, we observed a similar pattern to MIRIAD. FreeSurfer's and SAMSEG's longitudinal versions were significantly better in subcortical GM and brainstem. ANTs, ANTsPyNet, SPM12, and CAT12 showed no significant improvements across regions.

In HCP, mean ASPC values remained consistently below 2.5% across all regions and methods, comparable to KIRBY21 and substantially better than in MIRIAD, except for CAT12 which exceeded these thresholds across all regions. Regarding longitudinal versus CS, we observed a consistent pattern across all three regions. FreeSurfer's and SAMSEG's longitudinal versions were significantly better in subcortical GM, brainstem, and lateral ventricles. ANTs, ANTsPyNet, SPM12, and CAT12 showed no significant improvements across regions.

Performance of Longitudinal Pipelines

Among the longitudinal pipelines in MIRIAD, SAMSEG and SPM12 exhibited the highest robustness and reliability across regional assessments. FreeSurfer, ANTs, and ANTsPyNet demonstrated similar intermediate performance levels, while CAT12 showed comparatively weaker results.

Across both KIRBY21 and HCP, SAMSEG demonstrated consistent robustness. In contrast, SPM12 performed well in KIRBY21 but showed poorer results in HCP. CAT12 continued to show weak performance across both cohorts, failing to demonstrate improvement in regional-level assessments.

Conclusion

In this study, we demonstrated that while longitudinal preprocessing methods often exhibit greater robustness than their cross-sectional counterparts on test-retest T1-weighted MRI data, this advantage is not uniform across all methods, anatomical regions, and scales of analysis. Furthermore, even among longitudinal methods, the observed volume changes in some instances approach or exceed the annualised atrophy rate of approximately 2% observed in early stages of neurodegenerative diseases, raising concerns about their sensitivity for detecting clinically meaningful changes.

To ensure the robustness of our findings, we evaluated three publicly available datasets spanning different field strengths: MIRIAD at 1.5T and both KIRBY21 and HCP at 3T. However, despite this multi-dataset approach, the overall sample sizes remain relatively small, and our findings may not fully generalise to broader contexts involving different acquisition protocols, scanner manufacturers, or demographic characteristics. To address this limitation, we plan to extend our evaluation to the CoRR^11 (Consortium for Reliability and Reproducibility) dataset, which offers considerably larger sample sizes and diverse imaging acquisitions.

Regarding the metrics used for evaluation, while ASPC effectively quantifies volume changes and provides insight into pipeline robustness, complementary metrics such as the Dice coefficient could evaluate segmentation overlap, offering spatial information beyond volume-based measurements alone. Another limitation is the reliance on two different atlases (DKT and Neuromorphometrics) due to method-specific constraints, which may introduce subtle biases in regional comparisons across methods.

Most critically, our current experiment evaluates robustness exclusively under a no-change condition, where ideally no variation should exist between repeated scans. While this approach assesses reproducibility, it does not address the accuracy of each method in detecting meaningful anatomical changes. A method may appear highly robust simply because it consistently produces the same results, whether correct or incorrect. To comprehensively evaluate method performance, future work should incorporate controlled-change scenarios that assess both reproducibility and accuracy in detecting simulated pathological changes.
