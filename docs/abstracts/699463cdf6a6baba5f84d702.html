Introduction

Recent neuroimaging research has increasingly highlighted the limitations of null-hypothesis significance testing, particularly in large samples where very small effects can reach statistical significance. This issue, often referred to as the “fallacy of the null hypothesis”, has motivated a shift toward effect-size approaches, which offer more interpretable and potentially more reproducible results across studies.

These concerns are especially relevant for voxel-based morphometry (VBM) analyses in psychiatric populations, where between-group differences are typically subtle, spatially distributed, and sensitive to modeling choices. In obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD), VBM studies have reported heterogeneous findings, including increased gray matter volume in subcortical regions and reductions in prefrontal cortex. However, results are usually presented as thresholded statistical maps, providing limited information about the magnitude and spatial uncertainty of the underlying effects.

Spatial Confidence Sets (CSs) offer a principled alternative for mass-univariate inference by focusing explicitly on effect sizes. Rather than identifying voxels exceeding a statistical threshold, CSs allow researchers to assert which brain regions exceed a given effect-size threshold. This enables explicit statements about both effect magnitude and spatial extent, addressing key limitations of conventional voxelwise inference. Although originally developed for functional MRI, the CS methodology is applicable to any parameter estimated within a general linear model.

We extend the CSs framework to structural neuroimaging by applying it to VBM-derived effect-size maps from a large sample of OCD patients and healthy controls. By constructing confidence sets at multiple Cohen’s d thresholds, we illustrate how effect-size–based spatial inference can complement classical p-value-based results and provide a more interpretable characterization of structural differences in OCD.

Methods

Our sample consists of 116 OCD patients (mean age = 43 years, SD =11.8; 52 women) and 118 healthy controls (mean age = 33 years, SD = 12.8; 62 women). Patients with OCD were consecutively recruited from the Department of Psychiatry at Bellvitge University Hospital (Barcelona, Spain) and diagnosed based on the DSM-IV-TR criteria for OCD. 

Structural MRI data were acquired on a Philips Ingenia 3T scanner with a 32-channel head coil at the Imaging Diagnostic Institute (Hospital Duran i Reynals, Barcelona). For each participant, a high-resolution T1-weighted anatomical image was obtained (233 axial slices, TR = 10.46 ms, TE = 4.79 ms, FA = 8°, 0.75 mm isotropic voxels, FOV = 24 cm, matrix = 320 × 318, acquisition time = 5:04 min). Voxel-based morphometry (VBM) analyses were performed in SPM12 (Welcome Centre for Human Neuroimaging, UCL) implemented in MATLAB R2023b. Preprocessing included tissue segmentation, correction for intensity inhomogeneity, normalization to MNI152 space with modulation, and smoothing with an 8-mm Gaussian kernel. Group differences and group-by-age interactions in cortical and striatal gray matter were tested using full-factorial GLM, including total gray matter volume and sex as covariates. Given significant age differences between groups (p < 0.001) and evidence for differential age-related gray matter trajectories in OCD, 1 2 age was modeled categorically rather than as a continuous covariate. Empirical age cut-offs were estimated within the control group using segmented linear regression, yielding thresholds of 32 years for cortical gray matter and 36 years for striatal gray matter. These cut-offs were applied to stratify both groups into Young and Adult subgroups. 

Effect-size maps were derived from VBM second-level t-maps using a custom MATLAB script following Bowring et al., 2021, and extending their one-sample methodology to the general linear model (GLM).3 4 For each contrast, design matrices, contrast vectors, and effective residual degrees of freedom were extracted from the SPM.mat files, allowing computation of Cohen’s d for arbitrary GLM contrasts while accounting for cell sizes and covariates. Effect sizes were bias-corrected using the standard small-sample correction, 5 although the adjustment was negligible (J≈0.9967). 

Spatial confidence sets (CSs) were then constructed on the effect-size maps following Bowring et al. 3  (See Figure 1). To accommodate the non-constant variance of Cohen’s d across voxels, variance-stabilized d-maps were computed prior to inference. Voxelwise residual fields from the group-level GLM were used to estimate boundary variability and to generate wild-t bootstrap distributions (5,000 sign-flip iterations). Upper and lower CSs were obtained at multiple effect-size thresholds (Cohen’s d = 0.5, 0.8 and 1.2), enabling spatial inference of both the magnitude and extent of structural effects. 

Robustness across thresholds was assessed by quantifying spatial overlap between upper CS using voxelwise intersections and Jaccard indices, testing whether higher thresholds yielded spatially nested subsets of lower-threshold effects. 

Results

Groups differed significantly in age and total gray matter (GM) volume, but not in sex distribution. Patients were older than healthy controls (mean ± SD: 43.0 ± 11.8 vs. 33.0 ± 12.8 years; Mann–Whitney U = 3,815, p < 0.001) and showed greater total GM volume (728 ± 76.8 vs. 665 ± 84.0 mL; t₂₃₂ = −5.9, p < 0.001). The proportion of female participants did not differ significantly between groups (controls: 26.5%; patients: 22.2 %).

VBM analysis revealed that, when comparing the brain regions where patients showed lower GM volume than controls using the cortical cut-off age, a cortical cluster, specifically in the left ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (PFC) emerged as significant (peak voxel: x=-51 y=38 z=-10; Height threshold=4.59; kE=159; pFWE =0.032). However, when testing the Interaction Age × Group contrast to assess whether there was an interaction between age and group, no significant results were found (p>0.05). In contrast, when testing this same interaction contrast using the subcortical cut-off age, a significant subcortical cluster was found in the right putamen (peak voxel: x = 26, y = 6, z = 9; height threshold = 3.79; kE = 151; pFWE = 0.043).  

Effect-size-based spatial inference was performed using Cohen’s d thresholds of 0.5 (medium), 0.8 (large), and 1.2 (extra-large). For each threshold, upper confidence sets indicate regions where d>c, lower confidence sets indicate regions where d < c, and the point estimate set represents voxels exceeding the threshold in the observed data. 3 

For the Interaction Age × Group contrast, the upper CS revealed regions in the right putamen and premotor cortex that could be asserted with 95% confidence to reach at least a medium effect size (d≥0.5). Only the right putamen also reached the large effect-size threshold (d≥0.8). For the Patients < Controls contrast, the upper CS identified regions in the ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (vlPFC), insula and hippocampus reaching d≥0.5, with the bilateral vlPFC also exceeding d≥0.8. No regions reached the largest threshold (d≥1.2). Only clusters within the upper CS are reported, as point estimates outside this set are not corrected for multiple comparisons.

Bootstrap validation showed well-behaved supG distributions. For the Interaction Age x Group contrast, supG ranged from 1.35 to 5.49 (mean 2.70), with strictly increasing percentiles (80th 3.08, 90th 3.34 and 95th 3.57). For the Patients <Controls contrast, sup G ranged from 1.83 to 5.37 (mean 2.96), with smoothly ordered percentiles (80th 3.31,90th 3.57 and 95th 3.80), supporting the validity of the bootstrap procedure. 

Sensitivity analyses showed robust spatial nesting across thresholds. For the Age x Group contrast, the upper CS included 1,805 voxels at d≥0.5 and 234 voxels at d≥0.8, with full containment of the stricter threshold (Jaccard index=0.13). The right putamen was the only region surviving at d≥0.8. For the Patients<Controls contrast, the upper CS comprised 3,944 voxels at d≥0.5 and 307 voxels at d≥0.8, again showing spatial nesting (Jaccard index=0.08). No upper CS was observed at d≥1.2. 

Comparison with classical inference is shown in Figure 2. FWE-corrected maps identified significant clusters in the right putamen for the AgexGroup contrast and in the vlPFC for the Patients<Controls contrasts. Confidence sets further refined these findings by demonstrating that the right putamen reaches a large effect size (d≥0.8) and that bilateral vlPFC shows moderate-to-large effects, despite only the left side surviving classical inference.

Conclusion

In this work, we extend the spatial confidence sets framework to voxel-based morphometry analyses and demonstrate its utility for effect-size–based spatial inference in obsessive-compulsive disorder. By constructing confidence sets on Cohen’s d maps derived from standard second-level GLM contrasts, we show that confidence sets can be applied to structural neuroimaging data without requiring individual-level images, relying instead on voxelwise residual fields from group-level analyses. 

While conventional FWE-corrected maps identify statistically significant clusters, confidence sets allow explicit statements about which brain regions can be asserted, with a given level of confidence, to exceed predefined effect-size thresholds. Thus, our results illustrate how confidence sets provide information that goes beyond classical voxelwise inference. In OCD, this approach revealed that age-by-group interaction effects in the right putamen robustly reached large effect sizes, while group differences in cortical regions, including the ventrolateral prefrontal cortex, were characterized by moderate-to-large effects. Importantly, confidence sets also highlighted regions with substantial effect sizes that were only partially captured or appeared more spatially restricted under traditional p-value based inference. 

Together, our findings highlight how confidence sets complement classical inference, or could replace it, by characterizing not only the statistical significance of clusters but also the magnitude, robustness, and spatial extent of the underlying effects. This approach directly addresses limitations of null-hypothesis testing in neuroimaging, particularly in contexts where effects are spatially distributed and sensitive to modeling choices. Moreover, our work demonstrates the applicability of the confidence sets framework to structural neuroimaging studies, enabling researchers to move beyond dichotomous thresholded maps toward spatial statements based on effect sizes.
