Introduction

Arousal is a continuous spectrum (wakefulness to anesthesia) modulated by complex neural networks. The brain requires dynamic changes to support specific behaviors. While FC differs across states, most existing research focuses on the differences in BOLD signals or functional connectivity across distinct states, neglecting the overall synergistic change patterns (coupling) among functional connections during arousal modulation1.

Synergistic changes reflect subsystem coupling/decoupling. Modular structure is the optimal tool for decomposing arousal-modulated dFC patterns into modules. This is crucial for verifying if the brain modulates arousal systematically, uncovering fine-grained organizational principles and which connections synchronize.

Despite structural symmetry, arousal changes involve marked lateralization2. Evidence (first-night effect) suggests asynchronous hemispheric activity3. While lateralization is reported at nodal levels, it is unknown whether functional connection modules associated with arousal modulation possess a lateralized organizational feature (structural bias toward one hemisphere).

This study investigates the modular structure of Arousal-dFC coupling to systematically characterize its hemispheric organization and the specific roles of each module. We first decompose the coupling into functional communities, and then assess their hemispheric organizational principles using Integration and Segregation metrics. By also quantifying the Spatial Heterogeneity of modulation effects within these modules, we seek to understand the community-specific and lateralized nature of the effect. Ultimately, we aim to reveal if arousal modulation is achieved via an asymmetric, highly structured network synergy, offering a new perspective on large-scale brain mechanisms.

Methods

Methods and Materials


Datasets:This study primarily relied on resting-state functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI) data from the Human Connectome Project (HCP) S1200 release (7T scans) from 184 participants, yielding 723 initial resting-state scans. Key scanning parameters included a TR of 1 second and a voxel size of 1.6 × 1.6 × 1.6 mm3.


Data Preprocessing:

  1. Eye-tracking Data Preprocessing: Steps included removing misaligned samples, detecting and interpolating blink artifacts, applying a 200 ms Hanning window for temporal smoothing, and finally downsampling to 1 Hz to match the fMRI temporal resolution.
  2. fMRI Data Preprocessing: Building upon the HCP minimal processing, additional steps included linear detrending, regression of nuisance signals . The data was temporally band-pass filtered between 0.01 - 0.1 Hz.
  3. Quality Control (QC): Motion scrubbing was performed, removing and linearly interpolating frames with Framewise Displacement (FD) exceeding 0.2 mm. Based on criteria including FD and eye-closure proportion, 485 resting-state scans were retained.

Estimation of Arousal and Dynamic Functional Connectivity

  1. ROI Parcellation: Preprocessed BOLD signals were spatially averaged within the 400-ROI homotopic atlas4 to obtain Regions of Interest (ROIs) with left-right hemispheric symmetry.
  2. Dynamic Functional Connectivity (dFC): Estimated using a sliding-window approach (30 s window, 5 s step). Pairwise Pearson correlations between ROI time series were computed within each window and Fisher-z transformed.
  3. Arousal Estimation: Pupillometry data was processed with the same sliding-window parameters, and the mean pupil size within each window served as the index of moment-to-moment arousal level.
  4. Arousal-dFC Coupling: Defined as the Fisher-z transformed Pearson correlation between the time-varying dFC sequence of each functional connection and the corresponding time series of pupil-derived arousal estimates.

Community Structure and Network Asymmetry Analysis

  1. K-means Clustering: Arousal-dFC coupling values were standardized across edges and used as input. Each edge served as an observation and each participant as a feature dimension. The number of clusters k ranged from 2 to 21, with the clustering procedure repeated 250 times with random initializations, selecting the solution with the lowest average silhouette score.
  2. Network-Level Analysis: Clustering results were projected onto the classic Yeo7 network parcellation to visualize the edge-based community spatial distribution at the system level.
  3. Hemispheric Asymmetry: Based on the hemispheric location of connected nodes, edges were categorized into four types: left-left (LL), right-right (RR), left-right (LR), and right-left (RL). Two indices were calculated for lateralization5:

    1. integration:  (LL+RR)-(LR+RL), measuring whether intra-hemispheric and cross-hemispheric connections are more concentrated within one hemisphere. A positive value indicates that bilateral interactive connections are more concentrated in the left hemisphere, and a negative value indicates in the right hemisphere.

    2. segregation: (LL-LR)-(RR-RL), measuring whether connections are more inclined towards intra-hemispheric links rather than cross-hemispheric links (i.e., independence or cohesion). A high positive value means the connections are more concentrated internally within the left hemisphere, while a high negative value indicates within the right hemisphere.

  4. The indices were averaged across three types of network interactions: unimodal-unimodal (U-U) (Somatomotor network and Visual network), heteromodal-heteromodal (H-H) (Default Mode Network, Fronto-Parietal Network (FPN), Limbic Network (LIMB), Ventral Attention Network (VAN), and Dorsal Attention Network (DAN)), and heteromodal-unimodal (H-U).

Statistical Testing: Statistical significance was assessed via comparison with a null model and corrected using FDR multiple comparison correction (α < 0.05).

Results

To systematically characterize the overall modulatory pattern of arousal states on dynamic functional connectivity in the brain, we first performed K-means clustering on the arousal-dFC coupling matrix (400×400 edges × 485 runs) from 485 subjects, using edges as samples and subjects as features to identify potential stable coupling patterns. The results indicate that arousal's modulation of dFC is not a singular process but is collaboratively accomplished by seven modular communities, each possessing unique characteristics (Fig. 1C). These communities collectively form the structural basis for functional network reorganization under fluctuating arousal states.

we further explored whether these arousal-modulated communities exhibit hemispheric lateralization characteristics. We are interested in whether these communities participate equally in both hemispheres under arousal modulation and display systematic hemispheric preferences. We systematically assessed the hemispheric asymmetry of the arousal-regulated communities using integration and segregation as two lateralization indicators at the network pair level, network level, and overall level.

At the network-pair level, by comparing with a null model and conducting tests after false discovery rate (FDR) multiple comparison correction (pFDR < 0.05), we identified network pairs with significant lateralization effects (Fig. 2A, B). The results indicate that the lateralization of functional connectivity patterns modulated by arousal does not manifest as a global distribution but rather as local features of specific network pairs within communities. For example, in community 7, the connections between the ventral attention network (VAN) and multiple heteromodal networks exhibited a significant rightward integration bias, indicating that the bidirectional connections (intra-hemispheric and inter-hemispheric) of this network pair are primarily concentrated in the right hemisphere. In contrast, the connections between the VAN and unimodal networks in community 1 exhibited a significant leftward segregation.

To further investigate whether the characteristics of arousal modulation lateralization are "community-specific" or "network connection level-specific," we averaged the integration and segregation of the seven communities across the three types of network connection levels, thereby obtaining the lateralization status of each community at the network connection level (Fig. 2C, D). The results support that the lateralization of each community is not driven by the network connection level but rather exhibits community specificity. For instance, the H-H connections in community 7 show a rightward integration, while the H-H connections in community 3 exhibit a leftward integration. Overall, the lack of a significant overall bias among different network connection types further supports the community-specific nature of lateralization.

Finally, considering the potential commonalities in lateralization among communities, we conducted a meta-analysis of the spatial patterns of arousal modulation lateralization for each community. By aggregating the lateralization indicators of all significant network pairs, the results confirmed that the lateralization of arousal modulation is community-specific: communities 2, 3, and 6 show leftward integration and segregation; communities 1, 4, and 7 show rightward integration and leftward segregation; while community 5 exhibits both rightward integration and segregation (Fig. 2E).

Conclusion

In summary, the modulation of functional connectivity by arousal exhibits significant hemispheric asymmetry at the network level, and this asymmetry is not determined by the large-scale functional networks to which they belong (e.g., DMN or FPN) or the network connection levels, but rather relies more on the community to which they belong. Furthermore, the dual indicators of integration and segregation reveal the unique spatial organizational patterns of lateralization for each of the seven communities, emphasizing that the lateralization driven by arousal in dynamic brain connectivity does not manifest as a simple whole-brain effect but rather as a complex process with modular characteristics.
