Introduction

Previous reports have highlighted cognitive changes after the end of chemotherapy for breast cancer. These cognitive alterations are associated with structural brain – both volume loss and white matter integrity - alterations  but these changes remain to be fully characterized prior to adjuvant treatments ^1–3.  We recently proposed that sleep modifications following cancer and its treatments could contribute to neurocognitive functioning of patients ^4. However, despite frequent sleep complaints in breast cancer patients, it is unknown whether sleep modifications are related to both their brain structural and cognitive changes. We aim to investigate sleep modifications along with cognitive and brain changes in breast cancer patients, compared to controls without cancer, after surgery but prior to any adjuvant treatments.

Methods

Preliminary data from 34 women with insomnia complaints have been collected for now. This includes 18 breast cancer patients (52 ± 11 years old) evaluated at least one month after surgery but before the beginning of adjuvant treatments and 16 controls without cancer (52 ± 8 years old). Among patients, 12 (67%) underwent a lumpectomy and 6 (33%) underwent a mastectomy, 4 (22%) had stage I cancer, 12 (67%) stage II cancer and 2 (11%) stage III cancer. All participants filled in questionnaires related to quality of life such as cognition, fatigue, pain and sleep complaints, and anxio-depressive factors and underwent a dedicated neuropsychological test battery to evaluate memory, executive functioning, and attention. Sleep structure was measured using polysomnography. MRI scans were acquired on the 3T GE MRI scanner at the Cyceron Center for Neuroimaging (Caen). Sequences included T1 and DTI.  Voxel-based morphometry was used to generate gray matter volumes with the CAT12 toolbox and tract-based spatial statistics (TBSS) measures – fractional anisotropy (FA) and mean diffusivity (MD) - were generated using FSL pipelines. Comparisons between groups were done using ANCOVA, controlling for age. 

Results

Compared to controls, patients perceived their cognitive abilities as lower than those of controls (p=0.04) and had reduced activity and lower motivation (p=0.006 and p=0.07, respectively). Pain and sleep complaints as well as global fatigue did not differ between groups. Inhibition scores were higher – less efficient (p=0.05) and short-term memory performance was lower (p=0.04) in patients than in controls. Other cognitive scores did not differ between groups. Sleep structure was similar in both groups except for a higher number of awakenings in patients compared to controls (p=0.05). MRI analyses showed atrophy in the right lingual gyrus (cluster size = 1132 voxels) in patients compared to controls (p uncorrected, <0.001) and higher FA in three main clusters (tfce corrected, clusters’ size of 441, 208 and 123 voxels) falling into the middle cerebellar peduncle, the right medial lemniscus, the brain stem and the left cerebellum Crus II. MD values did not differ between groups. 

Conclusion

Results showed that even before adjuvant treatments, breast cancer patients have poorer cognitive functioning along with mild brain structural and sleep modifications. Patients have lower inhibition and short-term memory abilities, but other cognitive domains were unaffected in the current sample. Atrophy in the lingual gyrus – involved in episodic memory retrieval - concord with previous reports after chemotherapy ^1. Altered white matter integrity was found in regions involved notably in cognitive functioning (i.e., cerebellum Crus II) and in sleep regulation (i.e., brainstem). An increased FA has been suggested to reflect oedema in traumatic brain injury ^5. Brain modifications even before adjuvant treatments may partly explain both cognitive and sleep modifications in breast cancer patients. Although subtle, these alterations are in line with patients’ complaints and underline the need for early tackling of both cognitive and sleep difficulties in breast cancer.
