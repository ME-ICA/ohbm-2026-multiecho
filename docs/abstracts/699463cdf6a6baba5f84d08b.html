Introduction

Drug-resistant focal epilepsy is common and can be effectively treated with resective surgery but that requires accurate localisation.1 Simultaneous acquisition of Electroencephalography and functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (EEG-fMRI) is well suited for this purpose, since it combines the high temporal resolution of EEG, which captures epileptic dynamics and the precise timing of events, with the high spatial resolution of fMRI, which can localise the sources of these signals.2 At present, EEG-fMRI has limited clinical translation owing to the complexity of its data acquisition and analysis. 

The first key challenge to address is identifying when epileptiform events are occurring. Manual annotation is time-consuming and subjective. Automated detection of events from EEG time-series may therefore be considered a critical enabling step, prior to localisation of these signals.  

Despite growing interest in machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) tools for EEG event detection, existing studies vary widely in their choice of models, evaluation protocols, and experimental conditions, making direct comparison difficult. As a starting point, this work benchmarks candidate ML and DL frameworks for automated event detection. We focus on generalised spike-wave discharges (GSWs), since these are a well-characterised electrophysiological marker of absence epilepsy (AE) that are relatively similar across patients. By using GSW detection as a benchmark, this work’s objective is to test which methods are most robust to behavioural-state variability i.e. generalise across rest and task recording conditions, and investigate how well they perform with and without manual denoising pipelines. 

Methods

EEG recordings from five patients with genetic generalised epilepsy, originally acquired in a simultaneous EEG-fMRI study,3 were analysed. Each participant completed three or four 10-minute EEG-fMRI runs in resting and cartoon-watching conditions (three participants underwent two rest and two cartoon runs; two underwent two cartoon and one rest run). EEG was recorded using a 64-channel MRI-compatible cap (62 EEG electrodes, one electrocardiogram (ECG) electrode, one reference), arranged according to the extended 10-20 system and referenced to FCz. 

Scalp EEG was acquired concurrently with fMRI at 5,000 Hz, synchronised to the scanner’s 20 kHz gradient clock, with 0.5 µV resolution and 16-bit digitisation. MR gradient and pulse-related artefacts were removed offline using template artefact subtraction,4 after which the EEG was downsampled to 250 Hz. This minimal preprocessing was necessary, as preliminary experiments showed that models failed to learn meaningful patterns without basic artefact removal. GSW events were visually identified and manually annotated for each run. Data from the ECG electrode were removed because excluding it experimentally improved accuracy, leaving 63 EEG channels. 

The objective of this study was to benchmark different ML and DL methods on the task of GSW event detection from EEG. The methods chosen include classical ML frameworks that learn from hand-engineered features (AdaBoost and logistic regression) and DL frameworks that learn directly from timeseries: a 1D convolutional neural network (1D-CNN), EEGNet,5 and a hybrid 1D-CNN + biLSTM model. For each framework, the task was to classify given inputs as positive (1 = GSW event) or negative (0 = non-event). A summary of the workflow is shown in Fig. 1. 

Training and testing samples were generated by partitioning each recording into 2-second windows with 1-second overlap. Each window constituted a single classification instance and was labelled as GSW (class 1) if more than 50% of its duration overlapped with manually annotated GSW activity. Windows were normalised per channel to reduce amplitude variability across subjects and sessions, allowing classifiers to focus on the temporal and spectral structure of GSWs rather than signal magnitude.  

For the feature-based ML approach, a set of commonly used EEG descriptors was extracted from each channel of each sample. These features comprised of spectral power (delta, theta, alpha, beta, gamma) and time-domain statistics (mean, variance, standard deviation, skewness, kurtosis, root-mean-square amplitude, and peak-to-peak voltage). The median value across channels was then taken for each feature; this approach was taken with the view that GSW are brain-wide events, thus averaging allows us to boost signal-to-noise, while avoid any need for channel-wise feature selection that may overfit in small cohorts. 

In contrast to ML models, the time-series from all EEG channels were provided as inputs to DL models, with view that they should learn their own features. The 1D-CNN captures short-term temporal patterns and local spatial relationships across channels. EEGNet, a widely used architecture in EEG applications, employs temporal and depth-wise convolutions to learn features that separately encode how events spread out in space or time providing a more efficient alternative to convolving across both space (channels) and time concurrently. Convolutional models, however, have limited ability to capture longer-duration temporal dependencies. To address this, a hybrid 1D-CNN + biLSTM model was included to also incorporate LSTMs for learning extended temporal context. 

Together, these models allow us to assess how different temporal and spatial feature representations influence automated GSW detection across brain states.

Results

All models were evaluated under four complementary settings to assess within-subject, cross-subject, and cross-state generalisation, and to examine how additional artefact removal affected performance: 

(1) Within-subject 5-fold cross-validation, assessing detection performance within the same individual.

(2) Leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) validation, training on four patients and testing on the held-out patient to assess cross-subject generalisability.

(3) Within-subject state-transfer testing, training on one behavioural state (rest or cartoon) and testing on the other to evaluate cross-state generalisability.

(4) Artefact-reduced state-transfer testing, repeating (3) after additional independent component analysis (ICA)-based muscle and ocular artefact removal to assess the contribution of noise versus neural state differences.

The dataset is highly imbalanced, with most epochs being non-GSW; therefore, performance was evaluated using the area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC), which reflects how well models identify true GSW events (recall) while avoiding false positives (precision). Performance variability was reported as the standard deviation of AUPRC across folds or test subjects. 

1. High within-subject classification performance across all models 

As shown in Fig. a), across resting, cartoon-watching, and combined-state conditions, all DL and classical ML models achieved high within-subject performance, with mean AUPRC values typically in the 0.90-0.99 range. This indicates that within a given individual and state, GSWs exhibit consistent temporal–spatial patterns that are readily learnable by a wide range of ML and DL architectures. 

2. Robust Cross-Subject Generalisation with Deep Learning Models 

As shown in Fig. b), When models were tested on unseen subjects, performance decreased as expected (AUPRC 0.85-0.95), and variability increased across all methods. Despite this drop, the DL models, particularly EEGNet, retained high performance and showed robust cross-subject generalisation. These results underline that - despite the fact that GSWs display a well characterised global signature – they still display clear inter-individual variability that can confound automated detection methods. For this problem it seems that convolutional models offer the greatest potential learning features that generalise beyond individual patients. 

3. Asymmetric Cross-State Generalisation 

As shown in Fig. c), cross-state generalisation was asymmetric: models trained on cartoon data transferred well to rest (AUPRC 0.87–0.98), whereas models trained on rest performed poorly on cartoon data (AUPRC 0.73–0.88, with higher variability). This suggests that cartoon-state GSWs are more diverse or noisy, enabling models to learn broader representations, while rest-state GSWs appear more homogeneous and generalise less well to the more complex cartoon-state EEG.

4. Effects of Artefact Removal on Cross-State Generalisation 

As shown in Fig. d), Applying further artefact removal did not uniformly improve performance. Instead, an asymmetric pattern emerged in which cartoon-to-rest transfer performance decreased after cleaning across all models, whereas rest-to-cartoon transfer generally slightly improved. This suggests that cartoon-state EEG contains more movement-related artefacts, and cleaning removes misleading noise that otherwise hampers rest-to-cartoon generalisation. In contrast, rest-state EEG may contain subtle low-frequency or microstate-related features that are inadvertently removed during cleaning, reducing cartoon-to-rest performance. Notably, convolutional models showed minimal change following artefact removal, indicating relative robustness to noise. 

Nonetheless, even after artefact removal, cartoon-to-rest generalisation remained relatively low (AUPRC 0.85-0.90), indicating that the cross-state generalisation gap is likely driven by neurophysiological rather than purely artefactual differences. 

Conclusion

This study provides a systematic benchmark of classical ML and DL approaches for automated GSW detection across subjects, behavioural states, and preprocessing conditions. While both ML and DL models achieved high within-subject performance, deep learning methods showed more robust generalisation across individuals and states. Importantly, cross-state evaluation revealed a marked asymmetry, with models trained on cognitively engaged EEG generalising better than those trained on resting data, an effect that persisted even after additional artefact removal and thus likely reflects state-dependent neurophysiological differences. 

Feature-based ML models achieved reasonable performance for GSW detection, consistent with the highly stereotyped and global nature of these events. However, their reliance on hand-crafted global summary features limits their flexibility and scalability. As epileptiform activity becomes more heterogeneous, state-dependent, or spatially focal, ML approaches require increasingly careful channel selection and feature engineering, which is both labour-intensive and prone to overfitting in small cohorts. In contrast, DL models operate directly on multichannel EEG signals and are inherently better suited to learning spatially specific and state-dependent patterns, enabling more robust generalisation across patients and conditions without explicit feature design. 

Together, these findings highlight behavioural state as a key factor in automated EEG event detection and motivate training strategies that incorporate diverse recording conditions. As a next step, extending these approaches to focal epilepsy will require explicit modelling of spatial heterogeneity in EEG signals and closer integration with fMRI-derived activation patterns. Unlike GSWs, focal epileptiform activity is spatially localised and may involve only a subset of electrodes, with propagation patterns that vary across patients. This places greater emphasis on preserving channel-level information, learning spatially specific features, and linking EEG events to focal haemodynamic responses observed in fMRI. Combining robust automated EEG event detection with fMRI activation maps could therefore support more precise localisation of epileptogenic and inform surgical targeting in drug-resistant focal epilepsy.
