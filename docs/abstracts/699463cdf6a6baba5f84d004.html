Introduction

Music is found in all human societies, and one of the main reasons for its existence is the experience of pleasure it elicits. Previous neuroimaging studies have consistently shown that music pleasure activates reward-related brain regions 1,2 but the full network associated with musical pleasure is not established.

              Recent affective neuroscience studies have suggested that emotions are encoded as distributed population codes in the brain 3. ﻿Although specific regions of interest are often well supported by neuroimaging research, whole-brain multivariate predictive models yield broader effects than univariate measures. Activity related to musical pleasure m­­­ay be reflected in ﻿a distributed pattern throughout the whole brain.

              Time series of neural responses are also important for the affective experience of music because music unfolds over time. Music listening experiments ask participants for real-time emotion ratings, whereas many other types of experiments examine the static ratings after stimulus presentation. Real-time music pleasure experiences may reveal dynamic changes in whole-brain activation patterns at finer temporal scales associated with temporal variations in affective response.

              Musical pleasure may be represented in the brain in both common and culture-specific ways. To date, several studies from multiple countries have investigated musical reward using real-time pleasure-rating paradigms 1,4 but no study has examined the generalizability of brain activity across cultures. Cross-cultural neuroscience research would reveal universal neural responses to music reward.

              Here, we examine a predictive model of the human brain's responses to musical pleasure based on whole-brain activity. We used pattern recognition and brain decoding techniques to characterize brain responses across six fMRI studies from three countries (total n = 165) to develop an fMRI-based brain music reward signature (MRS) and test whether it generalizes across populations and stimuli.

Methods

Participants: The present research used the pooled data from n = 165 healthy participants collected across six independent neuroimaging studies from Japan (JP), Canada (CA), and Israel (IS). All participants provided written informed consent and were compensated for their participation in the study. The human ethics research committees of NICT, McGill University, and Tel Aviv University approved the studies.

Stimuli: Participants listened to music self-selected as pleasurable, as well experimenter-selected musical pieces drawn from lists of preferred music. Various music genres were included, such as pop, rock, jazz, classical, and electronica. Since participants from each of the three countries preferred their own country's music, the stimuli included music from several cultures and languages.

Experimental procedure: In the fMRI scanner, participants performed one or two listening fMRI runs . The order of song presentation was counterbalanced, alternating between self-selected and experimenter-selected songs. The duration of the musical stimuli varied from 45s to 4.5 min.

Participants were required to indicate their emotional responses in real time while listening to music during scanning by pressing one of several buttons on an MRI-compatible response pad, except for the Is2 dataset, in which ratings were obtained in a separate session outside the scanner. In the experiments, participants respond to three or four levels of pleasure (JP: 1 = neutral, 2 = pleasure, 3 = chill; CA and IS: 1 = neutral, 2 = low pleasure, 3 = high pleasure, 4 = chill (or peak pleasure)). 

﻿fMRI acquisition: BOLD data were collected across five 3T MRI scanners using echo-planar sequences with varying acquisition parameters. We performed standard pre-processing in SPM12 and CONN, including slice-time correction, motion correction, co-registration to the structural image, normalization to MNI space, and smoothing with a 6-mm FWHM kernel.

GLM analysis: A 1st-level GLM model was used to obtain beta images for the prediction analysis. In this model, we included three or four separate stick-function regressors time-logged to each onset of real-time pleasure ratings during music listening. The implicit baseline is set to the overall average BOLD signal to identify pop-up regions during the experimental periods.

Whole-brain decoding analysis: ﻿ We made the MRS model using the Jp 1 cohort (N = 38) because it was the largest sample size in the six cohorts. LASSO–PCR with 10-fold cross-validation was used to predict the level of music reward from whole-brain voxel-wise grey matter scores in fMRI 1st-level beta images. In addition to using the stick function to capture momentary neural activity, the beta images were made by shifting the rating onsets by 1 TR earlier to cover for a response delay of the emotional rating about the musical events. The statistical significance of the cross-validated prediction accuracy was assessed using 10000 permutations (p < .05). Next, we tested the whole-brain decoding model on a five-independent validation dataset obtained from three countries (Ja 2, n = 11; Ca 1, n = 20; Ca 2, n = 27; Is 1, n = 34; Is 2, n = 29, removing six participants due to having too few emotional responses). The MRS response was estimated for each test subject in each test condition by taking the dot product of the normalized BOLD activation after smoothing (Z) with the signature pattern (W), i.e., (MRS score = W*Z), yielding a single scalar value. The pattern expression scores were averaged across subjects in the test datasets. This method enables us to predict a time-series of music reward levels around the onset of a pleasure rating.

Results

For whole-brain decoding using LASSO-PCR, the MRS model training identifies a pattern of weights across voxels such that the weighted average activity is optimized to predict music reward in a training sample of participants, and the predictive accuracy is validated in an independent sample of participants. For the Study 1-JP1 dataset, the trained pattern resulted in a cross-validated prediction outcome correlation of r = 0.79 (p < .001, Figure A). The average within-subject correlation between predicted and actual musical reward was r = 0.89, p < .001. Furthermore, the two-alternative forced-choice whole-brain decoding accurately classified chill versus neutral responses (Accuracy = 96%, area under the curve = 0.98). To characterize the time course of MRS responses during and after each emotion response onset (button press), we applied the MRS pattern (Figure B) to the time series BOLD signal from smoothed images and examined neuromarker scores at each time point (Figure C). Phasic increases with music reward experiences began at approximately 2 s following response onset and decreased at 6 s. In that period, the order of neuromarker scores was chills, pleasure, and neutral from highest to lowest. These reconstructions of pleasure level results validated the adequacy of the brain hemodynamic response model.

Moreover, as shown in Figure D, E, F, and G, the MRS pattern significantly  predicts the time-series BOLD signal before and after response onset, depending on the levels of subjective pleasure from three countries (Study 2-Ja 2: r = 0.57, p < .001, Study 3-Ca 1: r = 0.46, p < .001, Study 4-Ca 2: r = 0.24, p = .03, Study 5-Is 1: r = 0.37, p < .001). The MRS reacts with a latency of approximately 2 s after response onset, which corresponds to the timing of the hemodynamic response function following response onset by 1 TR earlier. Therefore, the results showed considerable generalisability of the MRS model by the Jp1 dataset. Importantly, the Japanese dataset measured the music reward by neutral, pleasure, and chills responses. But in the Canada and Israel datasets, the reward response format used four categories: neutral, low-pleasure, high-pleasure, and chills. Since the prediction was successful under different response formats, we conclude that the MRS model can describe a musical pleasure vector in general. Note that the dataset from Study 6 (IS 2) failed the generalization, probably due to the huge difference in the experimental method.

We further took a cross-decoding approach to show the generalizability of the whole-brain decoding model for each country. Using the 1st level beta map of response onset before 2 seconds, cross-decoding was performed by training each of the five datasets of the whole-brain grey matter regions and testing each of the remaining four datasets. A successful cross-dataset prediction would corroborate evidence for common neural resources shared by the different populations. As shown in Figure H, whole-brain decoders have considerable generalizability (22 out of  30, pFDRs < .05), although prediction accuracy for the own dataset in the diagonal line showed the highest accuracy, as might be expected. Note that all decoding models significantly predicted at least one dataset from other countries. As for generalizability, training using four datasets rather than one dataset is better for testing datasets, i.e., the mean prediction accuracy for five datasets is highest for the leave-one-dataset-out procedure (named “All” in Figure H, mean score for line plot, r = 0.59; each dataset score for heatmap, Jp 1: r = 0.68, Jp 2: r = 0.59, Ca 1: r = 0.64, Ca 2: r = 0.48, Is 1: r = 0.57).

Conclusion

We created an MRS neural decoder model using the Japan dataset, which can track the moment-to-moment intensity of musical pleasure, and showed that it generalizes to new samples across various music stimuli, experimental procedures, scanners/scanning parameters, and countries. Unlike in other neuroimaging experiments, predictions can be made even when individual music stimuli differ. It has been suggested that certain aspects of behavioral and emotional responses to music are shared across cultures 5; we first observe similar neural activity in this regard. Although people prefer music from their own country, musical pleasure can elicit common neural responses across preferences/genres and languages. In addition, we demonstrated that all neural decoders from Japan, Canada, and Israel can predict neural responses of musical pleasure in other countries by a cross-decoding approach. Integrating data from multiple countries for decoding also yielded higher prediction accuracy. These results strongly supported the shared neural resources of musical reward across countries. 

              However, our results also indicated that the whole-brain activity of music reward may have culture specific aspects. As shown in the Figure, the JP1 decoder predicts the JP2 dataset particularly well. The prediction accuracy of JP1 decoder is also best for JP2. Therefore, something in the music or in the people tested in the Japan dataset is more similar than in other countries. The brain distinctiveness of a nation may also be involved in the construction of its musical culture. In the future study, if we will examine neural responses to musical pleasure across several other countries, we could gain further insight into cultural generalizability and specificity.

﻿              As for the predictive brain weight, positive weight areas in the MRS (Figure B) mainly include the striatum (NAcc, caudate, putamen), vmPFC, ACC, and insula included BG or salience network. These regions have been broadly implicated in reward and pleasure across species, and several prior studies and meta-analyses have demonstrated their central role in pleasurable responses to music 2. These activation regions are validated the decoder activation patterns for musical pleasure.

              In conclusion, our findings suggest that the unique rewarding experiences elicited by music, depend largely on common neural activation patterns across cultures, which may explain why humans are motivated to listen to music everywhere and throughout time.
