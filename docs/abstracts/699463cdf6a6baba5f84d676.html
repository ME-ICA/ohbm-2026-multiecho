Introduction

The ability to process numbers is a key skill for everyday life and academic development. Previous research has indicated that numerical abilities are associated with a fronto-parietal brain network.1 Neural specialization for digits emerges early in development with preferential responses to digits observed in the intraparietal sulcus (IPS) by preschool age, and continues to refine during formal schooling.2 In particular, the left IPS has been shown to be involved in Arabic numeral processing and its activation correlated with age, suggesting that it becomes more tuned to symbolic number processing with maturation.3 Additionally, the left IPS has been found to process symbolic number representations across sensory modalities. However, the developmental trajectories of neural responses to numerical stimuli still remain poorly characterized. More specifically, only few studies have investigated neural trajectories of digit processing in different modalities and across time points in a longitudinal setting, especially in children in primary school.4 This gap limits our understanding of how numerical processing emerges and refines over development.

In the current study, we aimed at clarifying the developmental trajectories of unimodal (auditory or visual) and bimodal (audiovisual) digit processing and their relation to math fluency. Therefore, we examined brain activation from first to fifth grade at four time points (middle and end of first grade, second grade, fifth grade) using an implicit audiovisual digit processing task. This design allowed us to characterize age-related changes in sensory-specific and multisensory processing and to test whether math fluency explains variability in these neural responses.

Methods

A sample of 96 children participated in this study at least on one of the following four time points: middle of first grade (TP1, 7.3 yo), end of first grade (TP2, 7.6 yo), second grade (TP3, 8.4 yo), and fifth grade (TP4, 11.4 yo). Datasets that satisfied the stringent fMRI data quality criteria were used for mixed longitudinal and cross-sectional group analyses (nTP1=35, nTP2=36, nTP3=28, nTP4=74). A subgroup of 13 children had high-quality longitudinal data at the third and fourth time point. Math fluency was operationalized with the percentile scores of the arithmetic part of the Heidelberger Rechentest 1-4 (HRT).

Children performed an implicit audiovisual target detection task with four conditions, in which they were presented with single digits in a visual, auditory, audiovisual congruent and audiovisual incongruent fashion. The children were asked to press a button whenever a target image or sound of a shovel appeared. For each condition, 60 trials, including six target trials, were presented in four blocks with 15 trials each. Unimodal and bimodal blocks alternated pseudorandomly and were separated by fixation periods of 6 or 12 s. Each trial was presented for 613 ms, followed by an interstimulus interval of 331 ms or 695 ms. The total task duration was 6min15s.

The stimuli were Arabic numerals from 1 to 6, presented via goggles for the visual and audiovisual conditions and spoken by a female speaker, digitally recorded, normalized and presented over in-ear headphones for the auditory condition.

MRI data was acquired in a simultaneous EEG-fMRI session on a Philips Achieva 3 Tesla scanner using a 32-channel head coil. T2 * weighted functional images were acquired with a whole-brain gradient echoplanar image sequence (repetition time TR = 1.98 s, echo time TE = 30 ms, 31 slices). Standard preprocessing including slice-time correction, realignment, coregistration and segmentation was implemented in MATLAB (version R2023a) toolbox SPM12 (7219). After preprocessing, scans with excessive motion were repaired with ArtRepair and unrepaired scans surrounded by scans with motion were flagged. Children with more than 20% repaired and flagged volumes were excluded from analyses.

The first-level analysis included a general linear model using an event related design with condition onsets (events of interest) convolved with the canonical hemodynamic response function and six realignment parameters as well as an additional regressor of no interest for the flagged scans.

For the whole sample, second-level analyses included one-sample t-tests per time point and regression analyses with math fluency for the third and fourth time point. For the fully longitudinal sample, a repeated measures analysis of variance (rmANOVA) was conducted to investigate the main effect of time point and condition and the interaction of timepoint and condition (2x2 rmANOVA). We report results using a cluster-based family-wise error corrected (FWEcorr) threshold of p < .05 with a cluster-defining threshold of p < .001.

Results

One-sample t-tests revealed similar activation at each time point in bilateral occipitotemporal regions for visual and in bilateral superior temporal gyri for auditory processing of digits. Within time point observations showed that starting from second grade (TP3), bilateral superior parietal lobules were also activated during visual digit processing. During auditory processing, additional frontal regions showed activation at the end of primary school (TP4).

Congruency (audiovisual congruent > incongruent digit processing) effects and incongruency effects (audiovisual incongruent > congruent digit processing) were mainly present at the end of primary school (TP4). The one-sample t-test at TP4 revealed congruency effects in the middle cingulate cortex and incongruency effects in bilateral middle frontal gyri and the left middle temporal gyrus. Additionally, only the one-sample t-test in first grade (TP1) showed significant congruency effects in the right entorhinal area.

No significant correlations were found between math fluency and brain activation during unimodal (auditory, visual) or bimodal (audiovisual congruent, incongruent) digit processing at either time point three or time point four.

Lastly, the rmANOVA in the longitudinal sample showed a main effect of time in the right occipital fusiform gyrus, which was due to stronger activation in older children (TP4 vs TP3). We also found a main effect of condition in bilateral frontal brain regions and in the left superior parietal lobule, which was driven by an incongruency effect in these brain regions.

Conclusion

The present findings align with and extend current knowledge about numerical processing development by providing evidence for the specialization of neural networks involved in digit processing over the course of primary school. While unimodal auditory and visual processing consistently activated occipitotemporal and superior temporal regions at all timepoints, the emergence of superior parietal activation during visual digit processing starting in second grade suggests growing reliance on spatial-numerical and attentional processes. This shift toward greater parietal engagement is consistent with prior cross-sectional work showing the critical role of the intraparietal sulcus in symbolic number processing.1 The IPS has been discussed as becoming increasingly left-lateralized for digit processing over development, an effect that was not directly observed in the present data.

Notably, audiovisual congruency and incongruency effects were observed predominantly at the end of primary school, suggesting that multisensory integration of numerical information matures later than unimodal processing. The incongruency effects observed in bilateral middle frontal gyri and left middle temporal gyrus may reflect engagement of cognitive control networks necessary for resolving cross-modal conflict. These findings align with literature on development of cognitive control and multisensory integration, showing that conflict resolution and higher-order processing in frontoparietal control networks undergo protracted maturation throughout childhood and adolescence. Conversely, the congruency effect in the middle cingulate cortex at time point four, at the end of primary school, may reflect more efficient integrative processing when auditory and visual numerical information align. The presence of congruency effects in the right entorhinal area specifically in first grade warrants further investigation. It might reflect early memory-based binding of audiovisual digit representations that precedes the maturation of specialized numerical processing networks.

In the longitudinal subsample, increased activation in the right occipital fusiform gyrus from second to fifth grade might represent the fine-tuning of symbol-specific processing as formal math instruction advances.5 The main effect of condition, marked by greater incongruency effects in frontal and parietal areas, further highlights that conflict-related processes become more consistently represented in control networks with age.

Contrary to expectations, we found no significant correlations between mathematical fluency and brain activation during unimodal or bimodal digit processing. Certain factors may explain the absence of brain-behavior correlations: The implicit digit processing task may insufficiently engage the cognitive processes that differentiate mathematical abilities, and the restricted range of abilities in our sample may have limited statistical power to detect correlations. Individual differences may be better captured through functional connectivity analyses, which will be examined in follow-up analyses. Additionally, region-of-interest analyses targeting core numerical regions (intraparietal sulcus, inferior frontal gyrus, and precentral gyrus) are planned to detect also more subtle developmental trajectories that the current whole-brain exploratory approach may have overlooked.

In summary, these results suggest developmental trajectories with progressive recruitment of parietal and frontal regions for numerical processing. Multisensory integration and conflict resolution processes mature later than unimodal processing, emerging robustly only by the end of primary school. These findings underscore the importance of longitudinal designs in capturing the dynamic nature of numerical brain development.
