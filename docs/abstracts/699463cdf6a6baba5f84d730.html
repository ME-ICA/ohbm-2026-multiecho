Introduction

Delirium is an acute disturbance of attention, cognition, and arousal that affects up to half of older hospitalized patients and is linked to increased mortality, prolonged hospitalizations, and accelerated long-term cognitive decline. Contemporary “cognitive disintegration” models posit that delirium emerges when functional brain networks lose integrative capacity and fall below critical connectivity thresholds1. While perioperative EEG and resting-state fMRI studies support reduced feedback connectivity and network dysregulation during delirium, less is known about preoperative neural signatures that confer vulnerability before the surgical and inflammatory insult. The ascending arousal system (AAS)—particularly the noradrenergic locus coeruleus (LC) and cholinergic nucleus basalis of Meynert (nbM)—is essential for attention, learning, and adaptive network dynamics, and exhibits early pathology in aging and neurodegenerative disease. Noradrenergic activity is associated with global network integration, whereas cholinergic modulation often supports selective, segregated processing2. Here, we tested whether preoperative alterations in static and dynamic connectivity within AAS circuits and whole-brain network topology predict postoperative delirium. We further integrated cortical cholinergic gene-expression maps to link molecular substrates with network organization. As such, dysfunctional noradrenergic and cholinergic neuromodulation could underly vulnerability to delirium, with early degeneration of the locus coeruleus and subsequent non-linear compensatory hyperactivity of the cholinergic system. Given the cognitive disintegration theory, we hypothesise that preoperative participants that go onto develop delirium will have dysfunctional network topology, with a greater degree of segregation (disintegration) related to disturbed function of the ascending arousal system.

Methods

Older adults (≥65 years) undergoing elective non-intracranial surgery under general anaesthesia were enrolled in a multi-site perioperative cohort (IPOD-B3; IRB 2015-0374). Exclusions included dementia and nursing-home residence. Of  296 recruited, 120 patients with usable preoperative resting-state fMRI were analyzed after rigorous preprocessing and denoising. Delirium was assessed twice daily for up to four postoperative days using 3D-CAM/CAM-ICU; severity was indexed by DRS-98 and 3D-CAM-S. Pre- and postoperative EEG (eyes-closed rest) was acquired; Oz delta power (1–3.5 Hz) was quantified. Blood pTau181 was measured by NULISA. Cognitive testing (MoCA, TMT, Boston Naming, DSST, COWAT) and perioperative risk indices (ASA, NSQIP-SC/NSQIP-D) were obtained.

Preprocessed and denoised resting-state fMRI was acquired across 5 GE scanners with standard T1-weighted anatomy and T2*-weighted EPI (TR=2600 ms, TE=22 ms, 40 slices, 3.5 mm isotropic voxels). Time series were extracted for 400 cortical ROIs (Schaefer 17-network parcellation) plus LC (probabilistic atlas) and nbM (cytoarchitectonic map). Static functional connectivity (FC) was computed via Pearson correlations between ROI time series; dynamic functional connectivity (dFC) used Multiplication of Temporal Derivatives with 5-TR windows3. Graph topology was estimated per time point using Louvain community detection and participation coefficient (PC), indexing integration (higher PC) versus segregation (lower PC) relative to Yeo network modules. K-means clustering (k=2; k-means++) on region-wise time-resolved PC identified recurrent integrated vs segregated states across concatenated subject-time matrices (multiple initializations for stability).

Next, we explored time-varying features of brain connectivity by using a eigen-mode linear discriminant analysis (LDA) to determine specific spatio-temporal patterns that are unique to the delirium and non-delirious population for noradrenergic dFC and cholinergic dFC. The dFC matrices were concatenated across subjects and time, scaled and balanced-class priors for LDA was used to identify features contributing to maximal separation between the groups. Using the highest-order magnitude (top 5%, based LDA coefficients) time-points across the dFC that separate the two groups, the LDA-embedded average dFC were compared for noradrenergic and cholinergic connectivity between delirious and non-delirious groups. We were interested in determining whether there are time-dependent relationships between the global brain integration/segregation compared to ascending arousal BOLD dynamics. Leveraging previous approaches4, we calculated the cross-correlation between the phasic peaks (defined by 2-standard deviations) of the BOLD signal for the LC and nbM against the average (global) participation coefficients for each subject (lagged windows of -5 to +5 TR around the peaks) and significant group differences derived from permutation-based t-tests. Lastly, we explored whether regional network topological segregation related to gene expression derived from the Allen Human Brain Atlas5.  We selected expression for nicotinic (CHRNA/CHRNB subunits) and muscarinic (CHRM1–5) receptors, synthesis/transport (CHAT, SLC5A7, SLC18A3, SLC44A1/2/5), and degradation enzymes (ACHE, BCHE). After probe filtering, donor aggregation, spatial normalization to MNI, and z-scoring per ROI, we performed PCA to capture low-dimensional cholinergic expression components explaining ~80% variance. Pearson correlations between regional average PC and PCA loadings were tested with spin-permutation (1000 iterations) to control for spatial autocorrelation and FDR correction. Logistic regressions assessed whether preoperative PC and its interaction with postoperative EEG delta change predicted delirium, with model selection via AIC. Additional analyses examined nbM FC relationships with pTau181 and cholinergic PCA components (see Figure 1 for methods illustration).

Results

Delirium occurred in 32.4% of scanned participants. Groups were matched across most baseline characteristics; notable differences included operative severity and postoperative delirium severity indices. Preoperatively, patients who later developed delirium exhibited lower average participation coefficients across the cortex (mean PC delirium ≈0.39±0.05; non-delirious ≈0.41±0.04), indicating more segregated network topology, with pronounced segregation within salience/attentional and default-mode networks. K-means clustering revealed that delirium-vulnerable participants spent more time in a segregated states than non-delirious participants. Logistic regression demonstrated a robust interaction between preoperative participation coefficient and postoperative EEG Oz-delta power in predicting delirium: as PC decreased and delta increased, delirium probability rose. 

We observed significant LC hypoconnectivity with prefrontal and anterior cingulate regions in delirious compared with non-delirious participants, alongside nbM hyperconnectivity with posterior and superior temporal cortices. Logistic regression showed that reduced LC connectivity with salience/attention (p=0.041) and control networks (p=0.034) predicted delirium. Delirium was also associated with reduced nbM connectivity to peripheral visual (p=0.017) and default mode networks (p=0.045), and increased connectivity to the central visual network (p=0.016). In addition, we found distinct spatio-temporal patterns of LC and nbM dFC as we found greater anti-coupling between the LC and frontal regions in the delirium population compared to non-delirious. The nbM had greater coupling across posterior-temporal regions and distinct regions within the default mode network (DMN) in the delirium group compared to non-delirious, and there was anti-coupling across regions within the salience attentional and somato-motor networks. Phasic cross-correlation analyses aligned with prior mechanistic literature4; wherein post-phasic LC activity was positively correlated to greater integration and post-cholinergic activity was related to more segregation. However, the cross-correlation between nbM and global PC occurred in bursts: there was greater segregation for the delirium population for time-lag 0 (p=0.04) and time-lag +1 (p=0.015) compared to non-delirious, but the relationship became flipped at greater time-lags. This finding is consistent with a hyperactive cholinergic response in the delirium population driving greater segregation. 

We found a strong negative relationship between the second principal component of the cholinergic related gene expression correlated with the average PC across both the delirium (rho=-0.456, pspin = 0.001, p-FDR=0.004) and non-delirious (rho=-0.396), pspin = 0.001, p-FDR=0.004) participations (Fig. 2B-C). Importantly, the second principal component embeds nicotinic receptors (nAChRs) and cholinergic neurotransmission gene-related expression (Fig. 2A) with higher gene loadings across frontal cortical regions (Fig. 2D). In addition, when observing the individual cholinergic gene expression related to the average PC; several genes that contribute to the loading in the second principal component are significantly negatively related to PC across both groups (Fig. 2E - H), across nicotinic receptors (Fig. 2-F -G) and butyrylcholinesterase (Fig. 2E). Interestingly, two distinctive genes that have negative loadings in the second principal component show a positive relationship with average PC (Fig. 2I – Ji) and the gene expression is inverted with low expression across frontal and high loadings across posterior cortex compared to all the other expression maps (Fig. 2I - J). This is additional evidence that there is a mechanistic relationship between regional cholinergic gene expression and segregated network topology. 

Conclusion

Older adults who later develop postoperative delirium exhibit a preoperative brain state characterized by more segregated functional topology, LC-to-cortex hypoconnectivity, and nbM hyperconnectivity, together with dynamic signatures showing LC-linked integration and cholinergic phasic activity that acutely biases global topology toward segregation. These convergent findings support and refine the cognitive disintegration theory by situating vulnerability in disrupted neuromodulatory control of network integration versus segregation prior to surgery, rather than solely in intraoperative or postoperative insults. The imaging-transcriptomic evidence implicates frontal-biased nicotinic receptor and cholinergic neurotransmission gene-expression landscapes as molecular substrates of regional segregation, aligning mechanistically with selective attentional modulation, normalization processes, and enhanced signal-to-noise in targeted circuits.

Clinically, preoperative network segregation and AAS dysfunction appear to sensitize the brain to postoperative neuroinflammatory stressors, with EEG delta increases further tipping the system into delirium. This suggests a two-hit model: baseline neuromodulatory imbalance plus postoperative electrophysiologic slowing. The observed nbM hyperconnectivity and its positive relationship with pTau181 echo emerging evidence that presynaptic cholinergic adaptations (e.g., VAChT upregulation) may initially support resilience but also mark a compensatory regime vulnerable to perturbation. Together, these results motivate precision risk stratification using neuromodulatory network metrics, and raise testable hypotheses for targeted interventions—such as nicotinic receptor–focused strategies, dose-dependent cholinergic modulation, or approaches that bolster LC function to restore integrative capacity.

Taken together, these findings lead us to speculate that the cholinergic system predisposes to selective vulnerability in part due to early presynaptic adaptations aimed at maintaining plasticity and network function in the face of other neuronal pathologies. Given that postoperative delirium is associated with later-life cognitive decline, and neuronal injury, a postoperative delirium event might act as a further insult to an already vulnerable ascending arousal system. Ultimately, delineating preoperative cholinergic–noradrenergic signatures of segregation may enable proactive, mechanism-based strategies to reduce delirium incidence and mitigate its downstream cognitive consequences.
