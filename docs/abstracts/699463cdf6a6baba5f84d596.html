Introduction

Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) is a method for non-invasive brain stimulation. Specific TMS protocols are used in clinical settings to achieve neuromodulatory effects, such as for example in major depressive disorder (MDD) ^1. However, in light of heterogeneous clinical results, a better understanding of the underlying principles and effect determinants for optimal application of TMS is necessary ^2.

A potential and understudied influencing factor is coil orientation (CO). The directionality of the induced electric field by TMS determines the distribution of stimulated cortical areas. Our study aimed to examine how the CO parameter influences changes in cerebral blood flow (CBF) and functional connectivity (FC) in continuous theta-burst stimulation (cTBS), a widely applied clinical neuromodulation protocol.

Methods

Twenty healthy volunteers (8 females; mean age 25.7 ± 2.7 years) participated in this prospective, single-blind, sham-controlled crossover study comprising three neuronavigated cTBS sessions. Each visit, separated by at least two weeks, followed a fixed design consisting of two magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) sessions interleaved by one cTBS session. The sessions differed only in CO during cTBS (Fig. 1): CO which yielded the highest MEPs out of seven COs, deemed optimal for motor-evoked potential (MEP) generation (OPT), 90° rotated vice versa OPT (90), and a sham paradigm (45° rotated with a 7.3 cm spacer).

Our TMS protocol comprised forty seconds of cTBS (600 pulses) in between two blocks of resting motor threshold (rMT) determination and MEPs at the M. abductor pollicis brevis (APB) motor hotspot (Fig. 1). RMT was determined according to the Nexstim threshold hunting algorithm. Stimulation occurred on the left hemisphere, except for two left-handed subjects who received stimulation on the right hemisphere. Electric-field simulations for the OPT and the 90 CO were generated from the stimulation points and individual T1-weighted and DWI data using SimNIBS ^3.

The MRI acquisition included a high-resolution T1-weighted scan (TR/TE: 9/4 ms, 1 mm isotropic resolution, covering the whole head), a pseudo-continuous arterial spin labeling (pCASL) sequence (TR/TE 4500/13 ms, post-label delay [PLD]: 1800 ms, labeling duration: 1800 ms, voxel size: 3 mm x 3 mm x 5.5 mm, 29 axial control-label pairs covering the whole brain), resting-state functional MRI (fMRI, 2D EPI, TR/TE: 1200/30, 42 axial slices covering the whole brain, voxel size: 3 mm isotropic resolution, acquisition time: 7:05 min), and single-shell diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) (TR/TE: 7500/100 ms , voxel size: 2 mm isotropic resolution covering the whole brain, 60 directions at b=1500 mm/s^2, 6 interleaved b0 volumes, added phase-reversed b0 volume for topup correction)

The ASL data were processed into whole-brain quantitative CBF maps using ExploreASL (version 1.11.0) ^4. Steps included motion correction, alignment of control-label pairs, slice-wise PLD difference correction, pair-wise subtraction of control and label images, quantification, vascular-artifact compression, resampling to a cubic 1.5 mm grid, registration to MNI space, and smoothing with a Gaussian kernel of 6 mm full-width at half maximum.

The fMRI data were processed according to a standardized pipeline ^5 into seed-based intrinsic FC maps using a 15 mm spherical seed region centered on the voxel with maximum e-field value as per the SimNIBS simulations. Analysis steps included quality control and head movement correction, resampling to a cubic 1 mm grid, deformation to MNI stereotaxic space using a study-specific template (computed from all 20 subjects), spatial smoothing using a 8 mm full-width at half maximum Gaussian Filter, de-meaning and voxel-wise removal of linear trends, and temporal bandpass filtering (0.01 < f < 0.08 Hz). After the networks were computed using the seed-based approach, the maps were Fisher r-to-z transformed and globally thresholded for |z(r)|≥0.40.

The CBF and FC maps of the two subjects, who received stimulation on the right hemisphere, were hemispherically flipped. 

Finally, both CBF maps and FC maps were analyzed using a voxel-wise GLM in SPM12 assessing the time (PRE/POST) × condition (OPT/90/SHAM) interaction. For statistical significance, a peak-level threshold of p<0.001 and a cluster-level threshold of p<0.05 (corrected for the false detection rate) was applied.

Results

After exclusion of images with artifacts, 112 CBF volumes and 114 FC volumes were analyzed. 

Orientation-specific effects emerged, as 90 cTBS produced a significant pre-to-post CBF increase in the right superior cerebellar peduncle, whereas OPT cTBS elicited significant pre-to-post CBF increases near the stimulation site (Fig. 2A) and in the right superior frontal gyrus.

The OPT vs 90 interaction identified significant clusters (Fig. 2B) in the cingulate gyrus and parietal white matter, and smaller clusters in the left internal capsule, left inferior frontal gyrus, and right insula. In these clusters, OPT induced a CBF pre-to-post increase whereas 90° produced a decrease.

In a pooled analysis across all CO conditions, no significant pre-to-post CBF increase was observed. Instead, distributed CBF decreases were observed (Fig. 2C).

 When testing the verum cTBS conditions (OPT & 90) against sham, multiple clusters across the brain showed significant differences in increases and decreases. Specifically, OPT vs sham revealed a significant pre-to-post increase of CBF at the stimulation target compared to a decrease in the sham paradigm.

For our FC maps, the voxel-wise GLM showed no clusters surviving FDRc. However, the ∆OPT >∆ 90 contrast yielded a cluster (Fig 2D) close to our significance threshold in the left insula (p = 0.061 FDRc).

Conclusion

Different COs during cTBS produced distinct patterns of perfusion changes, with only OPT yielding increases at the stimulation site. We observed multiple clusters with significant time x condition interaction effects, demonstrating CO-dependent modulation of CBF after cTBS. Although FC changes did not reach statistical significance, GLM analyses suggested a trend toward coil-specific modulation of network connectivity for a left insular cluster. Our findings may underline the concept that the application of different COs during neuromodulation significantly alters the way TMS affects the brain. This could in part help to explain variability observed in TMS neuromodulation, given that the choice of CO could be optimised on an individual level.
