Introduction

Accurate segmentation of white matter bundles is essential for preoperative planning of glioma resection, as it helps minimize the risk of damage to critical tracts and consequent postoperative neurological deficits. The current gold standard, manual segmentation, is labor-intensive, requires high levels of expertise, and is affected by substantial inter-operator variability. Consequently, there is growing interest in automating white matter bundle segmentation.

Previous studies have proposed automatic segmentation methods for glioma patients based on approaches originally developed for healthy populations. These methods typically assume a canonical spatial distribution of white matter pathways. In the presence of glioma, however, this assumption is violated, because tumor growth deforms and displaces the surrounding anatomy. As a result, bundle trajectories in glioma patients often deviate markedly from the canonical anatomy observed in healthy individuals.

This work aims to address this gap. To the best of our knowledge, we propose the first supervised learning method that explicitly accounts for anatomical alterations induced by tumor growth.

Methods

Bundle identification in glioma patients cannot rely on canonical white matter anatomy, as tumor growth distorts and displaces peritumoral pathways. In this context, automation cannot be framed as a standard supervised learning task that predicts bundle labels based solely on healthy-reference tractography. Instead, we redesign the problem by explicitly conditioning bundle identification on the presence of the tumor and its local impact on fiber trajectories.

Rather than asking whether a fiber belongs to a given bundle in a healthy brain, we cast the task as follows: given a tumor and a fiber, determine whether that fiber can be interpreted as a deformed pathway of a target bundle. Accordingly, bundle identification becomes a conditional decision problem, where the anatomical plausibility of a streamline is evaluated under the additional constraint imposed by the tumor. To operationalize this idea, fibers encoded as polylines are mapped into a deformation-feature space that captures their relationship to both canonical anatomy and tumor geometry.

Each fiber is represented as a sequence in which the spatial coordinates of each point are replaced by three deformation features: (i) the minimum distance from the point to a skeleton streamline representing the target bundle, derived from healthy population data; (ii) the ratio between the tumor radius and the distance from the point to the tumor’s center of mass; and (iii) the absolute difference between the local angle of the streamline at the point and the corresponding angle on the tumor surface. Taken together, these deformation features quantify how likely the local anatomy is to have been modified by tumor growth, enabling an anatomy-aware, tumor-conditioned approach to bundle identification in glioma patients (Figure 1). A geometrical deep learning model based on edge convolution1 is trained accordingly, named GlioDeF.

The proposed method relies on two restrictive assumptions that narrow its range of applicability. First, it assumes a predominantly rapid, mass-effect pattern of tumor growth, as typically observed in high-grade gliomas, in which the lesion displaces the peri-tumoral tissues rather than diffusely infiltrating the underlying white matter architecture. Under this condition, the affected fiber pathways, although spatially distorted, remain sufficiently preserved to be reconstructed by tractography algorithms despite their deviation from canonical anatomy, thereby enabling the subsequent deformation-aware analysis.

Results

The empirical analysis is conducted using two large publicly available neuroimaging resources. The first is a reference benchmark of brain tumor patients (N = 500) from the BRATS collection2, which provides realistic tumor anatomy and heterogeneity. The second is TractoInferno3, a manually curated dataset that includes whole-brain tractograms and expert annotations of major white matter bundles, serving as a high-quality reference for canonical anatomy. 

To study how tumors distort white matter organization, a derivative dataset was created by estimating the deformation of tractography pathways induced by BRATS tumors. A biophysically inspired brain tumor simulator4 was employed to model the displacement of tissue in the peri-tumoral region, generating deformation fields that warp the TractoInferno tractograms into individual-specific distorted configurations. The analysis focuses on five major association and projection bundles: the inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (IFOF), arcuate fasciculus (AF), inferior longitudinal fasciculus (ILF), pyramidal tract (PT), and frontal aslant tract (FAT). For each of these bundles, an annotated dataset of deformed anatomy was obtained, in which the degree of deviation from canonical trajectories depends on both tumor size and spatial location. A bundle specific GlioDef model was tuned by partitioning training, validation and test sets as follow: AF (4997, 105, 370), FAT (3408, 76, 251), IFOF (4522, 94, 333), ILF (3962, 76, 310), PT (4049, 81, 317). Numbers of data derivatives differ because individual bundles are considered only when their pathways are affected by the specific tumor.

On this benchmark, a comparative evaluation was performed between a state-of-the-art bundle segmentation method, RecobundlesX, an extended version of Recobundles5, and the proposed deformation-aware approach, GlioDeF. Bundle identification performance was quantified using the Dice similarity coefficient, computed over streamline sets to capture the overlap between predicted and reference fibers (see Figure 2.A). Empirical results show a substantial improvement in identifying bundles within the peri-tumoral region when using GlioDeF: the learned deformation-feature space effectively captures the relationship between canonical anatomy, distorted pathways, and tumor properties such as size and location. The superiority in performance of GlioDef compared to RecoBundlesX was also confirmed by a one-sided Wilcoxon Rank test, with p-value<0.01 for all the bundles. A further analysis examined how performance varies as a function of distortion severity, measured by the magnitude of the warp induced by the simulated tumor displacement. Figure 2.B illustrates a clear performance advantage for GlioDeF as fiber displacement increases, together with a consistently higher recall, indicating a superior ability to recover deformed but anatomically plausible pathways.

GlioDeF models trained on data derivatives were then applied to a clinical dataset (N=21), where bundles affected by gliomas were manually annotated by a neurosurgeon from a csd probabilistic tractography (see Figure 2.C). When the canonical pathways of bundles are more displaced by the tumor, GlioDeF significantly improved the DSC compared to the RecoBundlesX model, as confirmed by a one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test with a p-value < 0.01.

Conclusion

This work introduces a novel method for white matter bundle segmentation in glioma patients, specifically designed to operate when tumor growth causes anatomy to deviate from canonical bundle models. The proposed approach addresses the challenge of recognizing distorted pathways by conditioning bundle identification on tumor-related contextual information, such as lesion size and location, and extensive empirical analyses demonstrate that a model can be effectively trained to detect these altered structures. At the same time, further investigation is needed to understand how biases of tractography algorithms influence the model’s generalization capabilities, particularly in cases where tumor-affected fibers are not adequately reconstructed. Consequently, the method does not solve scenarios in which the underlying tractography is incomplete or severely corrupted. Preliminary results on a clinical dataset are encouraging and demand extended assessment. Overall, the main contribution is to show that bundle identification in glioma patients cannot rely solely on canonical definitions of white matter anatomy, but must explicitly incorporate tumor-induced deformations into both representation and anatomical assessment.
