Introduction

Prior work described homeostatic and hedonic mechanisms of eating behavior1,2; however, food choices are also guided by memories of past experiences. The underlying neurobiology of this intricate association between food memory, hedonic, and homeostatic processes has so far remained underexplored.

Methods

We examined the neuronal correlates during food memory encoding and retrieval using 3 Tesla task-based functional MRI (Figure 1) in 55 healthy adults with overweight (19 females, age 28±6.5, BMI 25-30 kg/m2) of a double-blind crossover randomized clinical trial (NCT03829189). Hormonal and microbial markers 16S rRNA analysis were assessed in fasting blood and feces. We also assessed the effects of a prebiotic dietary intervention (30g inulin/day for 14 days) versus an equicaloric placebo on food memory. Statistics were done according to linear mixed effects modelling, up to 4 time points per participant × 240 stimuli on memory response accuracy, and on voxel-wise BOLD signal using the sandwich estimator toolbox3 with threshold-free cluster enhancement (TFCE) family-wise-error correction (FWE) of multiple comparisons and covariates of sex and wanting of individual stimuli.

Results

In this preregistered analysis (osf.io/whbc8), food memory accuracy was enhanced by its subjective reward value, eliciting activation in specific brain regions during encoding and retrieval, including the nucleus accumbens (NAc), pallidum, caudate, cingulate, dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (dmPFC), ventral tegmental area (VTA), the right hippocampus, and left amygdala. Higher neural activations during food encoding were related to higher fecal short-chain fatty acids acetate levels (rho=-0.231, pFDR=0.011) and Actinobacteriota abundance (rho=0.226, pFDR=0.019; see Figure 2 for an overview of results). Memory encoding further elicited activations, including in the hypothalamus, which were inversely related to ghrelin levels (rho=-0.202, pFDR=0.031) and directly to creatinine levels (rho=0.189, pFDR=0.039). Activations in reward-related and homeostatic brain areas further partially correlated with insulin (rho=0.172, pFDR<0.05), glucagon-like peptide-1 (rho=0.227, pFDR=0.011), leptin (rho=0.224, pFDR=0.016), and thyroid-stimulating hormone levels (rho=0.211, pFDR=0.028).

Following prebiotic intervention, neural activations related to food memory decreased in areas associated with reward valuation (dmPFC, orbitofrontal cortex, anterior cingulate, caudate, thalamus, VTA), homeostatic regulation (hypothalamus), and the hippocampal body. The decrease of hippocampal activity during food encoding was related to changes in gut microbiota abundance (Firmicutes; rho=0.42, pFDR=0.012).

Conclusion

This study shows interactions between neural activations in reward areas, hypothalamus, and mediotemporal lobe, indicating that food memory encoding and retrieval rely on hedonic and homeostatic processes. Building on our earlier work4,5, we further provide first evidence that microbial, metabolic, and hormonal body signals interfere with memory-related brain signaling in these brain areas, and we offer a proof of concept that a prebiotic intervention alters neural correlates of food memory in humans. The findings reveal new insights into how gut-brain interactions may shape eating behaviours and raise implications for the treatment of obesity and substance use disorder research.
