Introduction

Background: Dissociation, defined as a disruption in the normal integration of consciousness, memory, identity, and emotion, affects a significant proportion of patients with Schizophrenia (SCZ), with reported prevalence rates ranging from 9% to 50%.^1 Clinically, higher levels of dissociative symptoms are associated with greater positive symptomatology, lower self-esteem, and increased resistance to treatment, underscoring their critical importance. 

Existing neuroimaging research suggests the human Orbitofrontal Cortex (OFC) plays a crucial role in the mechanisms underlying both dissociative symptoms and SCZ.^2 Prior studies have observed reduced OFC volume in SCZ patients and decreased regional cerebral blood flow in the orbitofrontal regions in dissociative identity disorder. Furthermore, resting-state functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (rsfMRI) studies in SCZ have consistently reported a reduction in the resting-state functional connectivity (rsFC) involving the OFC.^3

Research Gap: Despite the structural and functional impairments of the OFC noted in both SCZ and dissociation, the specific neural correlates linking OFC dysfunction to dissociative symptoms within the SCZ population remain largely unclear, constituting a critical research gap.

Research Importance and Hypothesis: This study, using rsfMRI, aims to systematically investigate the association between OFC functional connectivity and the severity of dissociative symptoms in SCZ patients. We hypothesize that reduced functional connectivity of the OFC will be significantly associated with higher levels of dissociative symptoms in patients with SCZ, and we will clarify whether this association is independent of other co-occurring factors.

Methods

Participants and Clinical Assessment

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board, and all participants provided written informed consent. We recruited patients with chronic, stable Schizophrenia (SC group) and age-matched Healthy Controls (HC group) (ages 20–65). SCZ diagnosis was confirmed by two trained specialists using DSM-5 criteria and the Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview. Exclusions for both groups included history of substance abuse (within 6 months), claustrophobia, or metallic implants. Dissociative symptoms were assessed in all participants using the Cambridge Depersonalization Scale (CDS) (29 items, 5-point Likert scale).^4 For the SC group, two psychiatrists assessed positive and negative symptoms using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) and functional capacity using the Personal and Social Performance scale (PSP). Depressive symptoms were self-rated using the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI).

fMRI: Image Acquisition and Preprocessing

Resting-state fMRI (rsfMRI) and high-resolution T1 structural MRI scans were performed on a 3.0-T MRI scanner (GE Inc., USA).

rsfMRI Acquisition: Images were acquired using a gradient echo planar imaging sequence (TR = 2000 ms, TE = 30 ms, flip angle = 90°, 40 slices). Participants were instructed to keep their eyes open and fixate on a cross.

Preprocessing: Data were preprocessed using SPM12 and DPABI. Steps included slice timing and head movement correction, normalization to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) template, and spatial smoothing (6-mm FWHM Gaussian kernel). To reduce variance, head motion parameters, along with mean signals from the lateral ventricles and deep white matter, were regressed out. A band-pass filter (0.01–0.08 Hz) was applied. Image scrubbing was performed, excluding image frames with Framewise Displacement (FD) > 0.5 mm and one preceding and two subsequent frames.

Functional Connectivity Analysis

Region of Interest (ROI) Selection: Based on our hypothesis regarding the OFC's pivotal role, we used 12 MNI coordinates derived from an unsupervised K-means clustering study to define 12 spherical ROIs (6-mm radius) within the OFC (Figure 1).^5

Functional Connectivity (FC): FC was calculated as the Pearson correlation coefficient of the average time courses for each pair of OFC ROIs. Fisher’s z transformation was applied. The averaged connectivity z scores from significant OFC clusters were used to represent the overall FC–OFC for subsequent statistical analysis.

Statistical Analysis

The Mann–Whitney and Fisher’s exact tests compared group differences. Pearson correlation analysis explored correlations between FC–OFC and clinical characteristics. Multiple linear regression models were employed to examine the correlation between CDS scores and FC–OFC, controlling for age, BDI scores, and PANSS Positive scores as covariates. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Group Characteristics and Psychometrics

The study included 61 participants (SC, n=41; HC, n=20). The groups did not differ significantly by mean age (SC: 40.1 yrs; HC: 36.4 yrs; p = 0.130) or sex distribution. The SC group, however, exhibited significantly lower mean education levels (13.0 ± 3.0 yrs) and higher Body Mass Index (BMI) (26.3 ± 4.9) compared to the HC group (17.1 ± 4.1 yrs and 23.4 ± 3.0, respectively). Crucially, the SC group showed significantly higher dissociative symptoms (CDS scores: 55.9 ± 40.5) and lower FC of the OFC (0.36 ± 0.12) than the HC group (CDS: 14.8 ± 20.7; FC-OFC: 0.44 ± 0.13). The SC group had a mean illness duration of 13.6 ± 9.2 years, with average PANSS total scores of 80.2 ± 15.8 and BDI scores of 14.5 ± 9.4.

FC-OFC and Dissociative Symptoms

Correlation Findings

A significant negative correlation was found between CDS scores and the FC of the OFC across all participants (n = 61; correlation coefficient = -0.426, p = 0.0006). This indicates that lower OFC connectivity is associated with worsening dissociative symptoms. This negative correlation remained significant within the SC group (correlation coefficient = -0.338, p = 0.0307). The correlation was negative but not statistically significant in the HC group (Spearman rank correlation coefficient = -0.311, p = 0.1816), suggesting the finding was primarily driven by the patient data (Figure 2-1).

Multivariate Analysis

Univariate analysis in the SC group showed that CDS scores significantly correlated with FC-OFC and BDI scores (correlation coefficient = 0.395, p = 0.0107).

To account for potential confounding factors, a multiple linear regression model was performed using CDS scores as the dependent variable and FC-OFC, age, BDI scores, and PANSS Positive (P) scores as independent variables. In this multivariate model, only the FC of the OFC achieved statistical significance (correlation coefficient = -112.0222, p = 0.0259). Age, BDI scores, and PANSS P scores were not significantly associated with CDS scores in the final model (Figure 2-2).

Brief summary

The findings suggest that decreased FC of the OFC plays a key role in dissociative symptoms in patients with SCZ, independent of key clinical covariates including age, depressive symptoms, and psychotic symptoms. Further research is needed to replicate these correlational findings and explore underlying mechanisms.

Conclusion

This study provides the first demonstration of a significant association between the reduced FC of the OFC and the severity of dissociative symptoms in patients with SCZ. Importantly, this relationship remains statistically significant in multivariate analysis and cannot be explained by co-occurring factors such as age, depressive symptoms (BDI scores), or psychotic symptoms (PANSS P scores).

Key Findings and Implications

Novel Neurobiological Link: Our results suggest that decreased FC within the OFC plays a key role in the underlying neural mechanism of dissociation in SCZ, supporting models where OFC dysfunction contributes to the disintegration of self-monitoring and consciousness.

Clinical Relevance: We found that 36.6% of the SCZ group had CDS scores ≥ 70, indicating clinically significant depersonalization-derealization, aligning with previous reports of high dissociation prevalence in SCZ (up to 50%). 

OFC Role: The OFC clusters analyzed represent subregions with similar functional connectivity profiles, and reduced average FC-OFC may reflect compromised internal communication within the OFC. This impairment may disrupt adaptive behavior and socioemotional processing, allowing dissociative phenomena to emerge as a coping mechanism.

Limitations

Symptom Scale: We used the Cambridge Depersonalization Scale (CDS), which focuses on depersonalization and derealization, lacking measures for other dissociative features like amnesia or absorption (captured by scales like Dissociative Experiences Scale).

Trauma History: Trauma history, which is strongly associated with dissociation, was not collected. Although trauma-related symptoms (e.g., depression) were accounted for by the BDI and PANSS, potential confounding effects of past trauma cannot be ruled out.

Sample Size and Generalizability: The modest sample size (n=61) from a single center, including only chronic and stable outpatients, limits the generalizability of these findings to other SCZ subtypes (e.g., acute or untreated patients).

Medication Effects: Participants were not drug-naïve. Although chlorpromazine (CPZ) equivalents were analyzed as a covariate, the residual effects of chronic medication on neuroimaging and dissociative symptoms cannot be completely excluded.

Causality: As this is a correlational study, causality between reduced FC-OFC and dissociative symptoms cannot be determined.

Future Directions

Additional research, ideally using longitudinal designs or larger, multi-site samples, is required to replicate these findings, determine the causality of the OFC-dissociation relationship, and better understand the underlying mechanisms in SCZ.
