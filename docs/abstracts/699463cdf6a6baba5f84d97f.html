Introduction

Population based average templates are the foundation of neuroimaging analysis, serving as stereotaxic registration targets, yet most widely used templates are derived from mixed-sex cohorts with undefined sex ratios1, challenging assumptions of anatomical homogeneity. Indeed, recent evidence shows that sex-specific tractography templates yield superior registration accuracy and reveal robust anatomical differences that are obscured by unisex templates2. While many structural sex differences are subtle, studies have demonstrated persistent, region-specific dimorphisms in cortical thickness and grey matter volume, even after correcting for intracranial volume3. In studies of neurological and psychiatric disorders where sex differences in disease prevalence, phenotype and neuroanatomical patterns are well established, the use of unisex templates can potentially introduce bias, reduce sensitivity to sex related effects and compromise reproducibility1-4. Together, these findings provide a strong rationale for the creation and use of sex-specific templates in neuroimaging analyses.  Motivated by these findings, we developed sex-specific Minimum Deformation Templates using a large cohort from the UK Biobank. We further characterized these templates using deformation-based morphometry to assess population-level anatomical differences. 

Methods

Participants and Acquisition: Data used to build the sex-specific Minimum Deformation Templates were extracted from the UK Biobank. All T1-weighted MPRAGE MRI scans were acquired on a Siemens Skyra 3T scanners using a standard 32-channel head coil, providing a 1.0 x 1.0 x 1.0 mm isotropic resolution. Following initial quality control and preprocessing steps, a final cohort of 14,000 subjects (7,000 female, 7,000 male) was retained for template generation. 

Preprocessing pipeline: The following preprocessing steps were applied to all T1-weighted scans: 1) Brain mask estimation using SynthStrip 2) Initial Inhomogeneity Correction using N3 and brain mask, and 3) Linear Alignment to  stereotaxic space with 9-parameter linear transform, using brain mask. Following linear registration, all subjects underwent a manual visual quality control to exclude any scans with gross alignment errors, scaling errors, or failed brain extraction. Finally, images were resampled to a common, fine resolution (1mm3) using a B-spline interpolation algorithm (ITK) prior to the non-linear template generation procedure. 

Template Construction: Minimum Deformation Templates were constructed using a previously published Symmetric Groupwise Normalization (SyGN) algorithm, as implemented in antsMultivariateTemplateConstruction.sh script from the Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs) package5. The process iteratively minimizes a total energy function to produce an unbiased template that is centrally located in the deformation space. This is achieved by simultaneously maximizing intensity similarity and transformation regularization across the entire image set. The process was repeated until convergence.

Results

The Minimum Deformation Template for males and females (Male n = 7,000, Female n = 7,000) converged successfully after 5 iterations. We estimated voxel-wise Jacobian determinants and modelled male-female structural differences, revealing marginal differences confined almost exclusively to the lateral and third ventricles (p   ~ 6 %, FDR-corrected). The βsex maps indicate that, relative to the SyGN template’s center, males exhibited larger proportional volume of the ventricular system compared to females. No other significant sex differences were observed across the cerebral or cerebellar parenchyma indicating that local morphology of the brain tissue was highly comparable between sexes. 

Conclusion

Our observation of proportionally larger ventricles in the male template is consistent with prior neuroimaging literature1-3, which shows that after normalization for overall brain size, males retain larger cerebrospinal fluid space. Crucially, the absence of widespread, significant structural dimorphism across the parenchyma suggests that previously reported differences may be highly sensitive to the selection of subjects used in the cohorts studied and the normalization strategy employed. Studies using fixed-reference templates with uneven male-female ratios may introduce subtle, systematic biases that conflate true structural variance with residual effects of overall brain size and global shape differences. 

By contrast, our use of the SyGN algorithm to generate an unbiased Minimum Deformation Template provides an accurate estimate of the core geometric average of each sex. Our findings indicate that once the confounding effects of global size and systematic registration bias are minimized, sex differences in the brain are much smaller and highly localized. As such, the use of sex-specific templates are beneficial only when cerebrospinal fluid compartments are the primary regions of interest (e.g. in aging studies when atrophy is common). However, for the majority of neuroimaging studies the use of separate templates may yield only marginal benefits. The high degree of structural homology observed supports the use of a single, high-quality, sex-neutral unbiased template for structural normalization in large-scale studies1,5. Such that, sex-disaggregated analysis at the template level may be an unnecessary complication for most brain regions, and the focus should be on functional or microstructural differences instead.
