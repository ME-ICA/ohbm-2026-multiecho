Introduction

Functional connectivity is spatially constrained: nearby cortical regions tend to show stronger coupling than distant regions, yet long-range connections support distributed integration across regions.1 This distance dependence reflects fundamental anatomical and metabolic constraints (e.g., wiring cost) while still enabling integration between distant cortical regions. Although functional connectivity is widely used to study cognition and aging, its interpretation is often made without considering that coupling strength is strongly shaped by inter-regional distance. Across the adult lifespan, aging is associated with distance-dependent shifts in the functional brain organization.2 However, it remains unclear whether these shifts relate to age-related declines in cognitive abilities, such as executive functions and fluid reasoning.

Here, we quantify functional connectivity distance—the cortical-surface geodesic distance from each vertex to its strongest functional connections3—as an individual-level marker of a person’s tendency toward spatially distributed (long-range) intrinsic connectivity. We then asked whether age and connectivity distance covary with cognitive performance across adulthood. Using a multivariate latent-variable approach, we assessed whether connectivity distance and age jointly relate to performance on a cognitive battery spanning executive function and intelligence. This framework allows us to capture latent shared variance across measures and to test whether distance-dependent functional organization tracks meaningful inter-individual variability in cognition across the adult lifespan.

Methods

Participants were drawn from the publicly available NIMH dataset.4 Given heterogeneity in the neuroimaging protocol, we included only participants with consistent imaging availability: T1-weighted images (SCIC-reconstructed acquisitions), 2D FLAIR images acquired using the ADNI-2 protocol, and EPI-based fieldmaps. Cognitive measures included NIH Toolbox assessments of inhibitory control, cognitive flexibility, working memory, and episodic memory, as well as the KBIT-2 verbal (vocabulary) and nonverbal (riddles) IQ subscales. The final sample comprised N=118 participants (n=73 female), with mean age 34.4 years (range 20–72).

Preprocessing was performed using fMRIPrep version 25.2.3. The anatomical workflow included FreeSurfer cortical surface reconstruction incorporating available T1w, T2w, and FLAIR images. Functional data underwent susceptibility distortion correction using EPI-based fieldmaps, head motion correction, and co-registration to the T1w anatomical reference. BOLD images were normalized to MNI152NLin6Asym volumetric space and resampled to fsLR surface space (32k vertex density). Postprocessing was performed using XCP-D version 0.12.0 in ABCD mode and included nuisance regression using the 36-parameter model (36P), followed by bandpass filtering (0.01–0.1 Hz, 2nd order Butterworth). A notch filter (12–18 Hz, 4th order) was applied for motion artifact removal. Spatial smoothing was applied with a 6 mm FWHM kernel.

Geodesic cortical distances were computed on each subject's midthickness surface mesh registered to the fsLR 32k template space from which the medial wall had been removed. For each hemisphere, the custom pipeline employed pygeodesic to calculate pairwise geodesic distances between all cortex vertices, resulting in vertex-by-vertex geodesic distance matrices. Geodesic distance matrices were normalized by the average distance of the upper triangle to remove individual differences in surface size from the distances. Pairwise functional connectivity between cortex vertices was computed from resting-state fMRI data by correlating each vertex's BOLD timeseries with all other cortical vertices. To obtain a single connectivity distance value per vertex,3 the geodesic distances to its top 2% functionally connected vertices were averaged. All processing was orchestrated using Snakemake (v9.11.5) on an HPC cluster with Apptainer containerization for reproducibility.

To identify latent multivariate associations between neurobiological variables and cognitive variables, we used symmetric Canonical Partial Least Squares Analysis as implemented in scikit-learn PLSCanonical. To control for another, potentially confounding distance-dependent cortical variable related to cognitive ability, the neurobiological variable set not only included age and bilateral mean connectivity distance per subject, but also bilateral surface area. Model reliability was assessed using bootstrap resampling (100,000 iterations) to estimate 95% confidence intervals for canonical correlations and loadings. Statistical significance was evaluated via permutation testing (100,000 permutations) for canonical correlations and explained variance. Model generalizability was assessed using Monte Carlo cross-validation (1,000 iterations, 70/30 train-test split, stratified by age). Component matching across resampling iterations was performed using cosine similarity with sign correction to ensure consistent interpretation.

Results

The results revealed two latent canonical variates (CVs) with statistically significant canonical correlations under permutation resampling and stable loading patterns under bootstrap resampling (i.e., the 95% bootstrap CI excluded zero). Canonical variates beyond the first two dimensions did not yield statistically significant canonical correlations.

The CV1 was characterized by a pattern of variables that had positive loadings for age (0.55, CI [0.34, 0.88]) and connectivity distance (left: 0.45, CI [0.12, 0.61]; right: 0.48, CI [0.14, 0.64]) on the neurobiological set, and had negative loadings for all cognitive variables on the cognitive set, except for verbal intelligence (inhibition: -0.34, CI [-0.54, -0.005]; flexibility: -0.4, CI [-0.56, -0.1]; working memory: -0.51, CI [-0.64, -0.37]; episodic memory: -0.55, CI [-0.72, -0.4]; nonverbal IQ: -0.42, CI [-0.66, -0.11]). CV1's canonical correlation between the neurobiological and cognitive latent dimensions was r=0.44 (CI [0.30, 0.56], p<0.00001). In other words, a latent pattern of older age with a tendency towards more distributed functional connectivity was associated with a latent pattern of lower performance in the majority of cognitive tests (CV1).

CV2 had reliable loadings that were associated with a pattern of positive loadings on age (0.5, CI [0.008, 0.84]) and negative loadings on connectivity distance (left: -0.47, CI [-0.7, -0.06]; right: -0.45, CI [-0.7, -0.04]) on the neurobiological set side, and with positive loadings on the nonverbal (fluid) intelligence variable (0.55, CI [0.03, 0.94]) on the cognitive set side. CV2's canonical correlation between the neurobiological and cognitive latent dimensions was r=0.36 (CI [0.2, 0.52], p<0.0001). In other words, a second latent pattern with higher age and a tendency towards less distributed functional connectivity was associated with a higher nonverbal, fluid intelligence (CV2).

The cross-validation resampling analysis assessed out-of-sample canonical correlations of unseen data projected into the latent space to assess generalizability. Across cross-validation repeats, 98.7% of test-set canonical correlations were >0 for CV1, and 80.8% were so for CV2.

Conclusion

Here, we used functional connectivity distance as an individual-level marker of the extent to which a person’s intrinsic functional architecture is characterized by spatially distributed (long-range) patterns, and asked whether this tendency captures meaningful variance in cognition across adulthood. Using a multivariate latent-variable framework that jointly modeled age and distance-related cortical features, we identified two reliable canonical patterns linking age-related cortical organization to cognitive performance.

The first latent pattern indicated that older age accompanied by more distributed connectivity was associated with poorer performance across multiple cognitive domains, most prominently executive functions and memory (inhibition, flexibility, working memory, episodic memory). Furthermore, the second latent pattern showed that older age paired with less distributed connectivity covaried with higher nonverbal (fluid) intelligence. Together, these multivariate associations suggest that, in older age, a functional organization that relies less on spatially distributed coupling may be comparatively advantageous, whereas a stronger reliance on distributed connectivity may index vulnerability across several cognitive domains.

A tendency toward less distributed intrinsic connectivity could confer benefits by reducing the need for costly long-range interactions, potentially lowering metabolic demands and limiting delays associated with signal transmission. More broadly, it may reflect a functional topography that more efficiently embeds high-dimensional representational structure within the constraints of the cortical sheet, thereby requiring less coordination between distant cortical territories to support complex cognition.

Importantly, both associations demonstrated out-of-sample robustness in cross-validation, supporting generalizability beyond the observed sample. Still, the present results are correlational and cross-sectional; longitudinal follow-up will be essential to disentangle within-person aging trajectories from cohort effects and to test mechanistic accounts (e.g., compensatory recruitment versus dedifferentiation). Future work could investigate whether specific cortical territories drive individual differences in connectivity distance and evaluate whether this measure provides predictive utility beyond other established network metrics associated with cognitive ability.5 Overall, connectivity distance offers a spatially grounded marker of intrinsic organization that may capture constraints shaping cortical function across the adult lifespan.
