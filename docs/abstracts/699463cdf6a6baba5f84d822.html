Introduction

Sleep spindles are a characteristic signature of non-rapid eye movement sleep. They are transient 10–16 Hz oscillations driven by cortico-thalamic circuits and the density of spindles (events/min) during sleep is associated with sleep stability, cortical plasticity, and memory consolidation. But thalamic mechanisms of spindle generation in the healthy human brain remain elusive.

Previous work has shown that some thalamic neurons exhibit resonance phenomena that manifest as frequency specific responses to subthreshold inputs1, but thalamic resonance phenomena have not been described for the generation of spindle events. We hypothesised that additional spindle events can be evoked at an optimal frequency that relies on intrinsic properties of thalamic neurons. 

Here, we employed computational modelling and non-invasive electrical brain stimulation to delineate subthreshold thalamic mechanisms that underlie spindle generation in the healthy human brain.

Methods

We employed a biophysically realistic cortico-thalamic circuit model2 that recapitulates sleep spindle oscillations. The model included excitatory cortical neurons, inhibitory cortical interneurons, excitatory thalamic relay neurons, inhibitory thalamic reticular neurons, with Hodgkin Huxley type ion channel dynamics. We injected subthreshold sinusoidal currents to thalamic cell populations to evaluate the effect of subthreshold stimulation on spindle generation, assessed as changes in spindle density. 

We tested model predictions using Temporal Interference Stimulation (TIS)3,4 in healthy human participants during sleep. TIS enables targeting the thalamus directly using a pair of high-frequency current sources with a small difference in frequency (f1=2000 Hz, f2= 2005 Hz) producing a low-frequency envelope at the difference frequency (df=5 Hz).

We recruited twenty-five healthy young adults for a TIS afternoon nap study at the Surrey Sleep Research Centre. 

TIS was applied during NREM sleep, each participant was subjected to three counterbalanced conditions: (1)  df=5 Hz  (2) df=1 Hz  (3) no-stimulation (OFF). TIS was delivered with 2 kHz carrier frequency, 2 mA amplitude (peak-to-baseline) to two stimulation electrode pairs at 10/10 EEG positions: E1a:F7,  E1b:F8;  E2a: P5, E2b:P6 to maximize modulation bilaterally in the thalamus. We recorded simultaneous 32-channel EEG. Sleep stages were manually scored, and spindle events were detected automatically using the YASA toolbox5. We employed cluster-corrected repeated univariate testing and linear mixed models to assess changes in spindle density across conditions. 

Results

The modelling demonstrated an increase in spindle density after 5 Hz subthreshold stimulation. In comparison to neighbouring stimulation frequencies, 5 Hz elicits spindle events with minimal delay, implicating a resonance-based mechanism. Further investigation showed that the optimal ability of 5 Hz stimulation to evoke spindle events critically depends on the ratio of T-type calcium and H-current in thalamic relay neurons. 

Experimentally, thalamic TIS delivered at 5 Hz produced a significant increase in spindle density compared to both the 1 Hz stimulation and no-stimulation conditions. 1 Hz stimulation did not differ from control, consistent with model predictions. 5 Hz stimulation showed significantly reduced times to spindle event onset relative to stimulation onset. In a topographical analysis, 5 Hz stimulation showed increased spindle density at central and frontal electrode locations.

Sleep architecture did not differ between conditions, and no stimulation-related sensations were reported.

Conclusion

These combined modelling and experimental results demonstrate that TIS can selectively augment sleep spindle activity non-invasively in healthy human participants. 

Our evidence suggests that the frequency-specific effects observed here are driven by a subthreshold resonance mechanism dependent on intrinsic properties of thalamic relay neurons.

This work establishes a mechanistically informed framework for non-invasive neuromodulation of sleep spindles and suggests future applications for causally probing the role of spindles in cognition and developing targeted sleep spindle interventions in patients with neuropsychiatric disorders.
