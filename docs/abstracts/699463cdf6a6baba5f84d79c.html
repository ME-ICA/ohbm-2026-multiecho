Introduction

Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) is an emerging neuromodulation technique for managing chronic pain. Recently a randomized, sham-controlled, single-blind, parallel-group pilot trial was conducted assessing accelerated intermittent theta burst stimulation (aiTBS) with precision connectivity-guidance targeting of the ldlPFC in chronic knee OA pain 1. Primary clinical outcome analyses indicated that aiTBS was a safe, well tolerated, feasible, and acceptable intervention in chronic pain patients. Here we explore arterial spin labelling (ASL) measurements from that trial analysing changes in cerebral blood flow (CBF) to provide mechanistic support of future interventional neuromodulation trials for the treatment of chronic pain. 

Methods

Study design: A total 45 participants (active n=33/ sham n=12) were randomized to the BoostCPM trial. Eligible participants were aged 18–75 years and had chronic knee pain for a minimum 6-months duration. The trial had ethical approval (REC 21/SW/0079) and listed on the ISRCTN registry (ISRCTN15404076). The treatment schedule consisted of a 4-day course of accelerated iTBS delivered at 9000 pulses per day (5 daily sessions of 1800 pulses) with a total 36,000 pulses per week (Figure 1A).  Participants assigned to the active arm received iTBS at 80% resting motor threshold (RMT) measured as the minimum motor evoked potential (MEP)-amplitude of 50 μV in a completely relaxed abductor pollicis brevis (APB)-muscle in at least 5 out of 10 trials. Sham intervention was given through the same coil, but pulses were delivered at 30% stimulator output and orienting the coil perpendicular to the scalp surface with direct contact. Personalized target and stimulation sites were created using Granger Causality Analysis of rs-fMRI data, which located the peak voxel within the lDLPFC receiving maximal effective connectivity from the right anterior insula (Figure 1B).

MRI acquisition: Brain imaging data were acquired on a GE Premier 3T MR scanner using a 48-channel head coil (GE Healthcare, USA). The acquisition protocol consisted of the following sequences: T1-weighted structural (3D MP-RAGE, sagittal, voxel size=1x1x1mm, FOV = 256x256, matrix = 256, TI = 800 ms, FA = 8°) and perfusion-weighted image (3D pCASL sequence, background suppression, FSE readout module with 8 in-plane spiral interleaves, labeling duration = 1500ms, post-labeling delay = 2025ms, FOV=240x240mm, matrix = 128 × 128, slice thickness = 4 mm. The labeling plane was placed 20 mm inferior to the lower edge of the cerebellum. An M0 calibration image acquired without ASL preparation or background suppression and used to quantify CBF.

Image processing: Data analysis was performed using tools from the FMRIB Software Library (FSL, https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl) and the pipeline scripts are publicly available (version 1.6.9; https://github.com/SPMIC-UoN/BRC_Pipeline). T1 pre-processing included distortion correction and defacing, brain extraction, linear and non-linear registration to standard MNI152 template space, bias field correction and tissue segmentation. The T1 spatial alignments were used throughout the ASL processing pipeline. Brain masks were generated from the T1 structural data using BET and FAST. These were transformed, together with the grey and white matter partial volume maps, to native ASL space after image registration. 

CBF quantification: The perfusion-weighted images were registered to the calibration (M0) data with rigid-body registration using FLIRT. The ASL images were quantified to CBF in each voxel using BASIL tools in FSL and standard single compartment tracer kinetic model following the ASL ‘white paper’ formula 2 [ =0.9ml/g,  =1650ms,  =85%,  =1500msec,  =2020msec]. The perfusion image was used to separate grey and white matter CBF by partial volume error correction (PVEc) using modified least trimmed squares (mLTS) 3.

Regions of interest (ROI): 12 anatomical regions were predefined for constructing the network of interest: 8 regions were identified as central nodes of the descending pain modulatory system (DPMS): left/right anterior cingulate cortex (l/rACC), left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (ldlPFC), left/right dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (l/rdmPFC), left/right ventromedial prefrontal cortex (l/rvmPFC), periaqueductal grey (PAG). We proposed an additional 4 brain regions that may play a role in modulating chronic OA pain: right anterior insular (raINS), posterior cingulate cortex (PCC), left/right mediodorsal thalamus (MDThal). 

Results

The effects of the intervention on CBF, incorporating PVEc, is illustrated in Figure 2. A systematic increase in CBF was observed between PVEc and non-PVEc images of GM masked voxels at 25% and 50% of the tissue-type [GM 25%, 5.83ml/100g/min, Cohen’s d=0.05, p<1e-10; [GM 50%, 8.93ml/100g/min, Cohen’s d=0.06, p<1e-10]. In the active group, the mean CBF changes from baseline was significantly decreased across prefrontal ROIS within the DPMS: lvmPFC (mean change difference -16.23 ml/100g/min [95%CI 7.78], p<0.000394), raINS (-10.29 [5.33], p=0.000851, lACC (-9.45 [5.07], p=0.001195), and rvmPFC (-14.63 [8.76], p=0.003106). After spatial PVEc, significant mean CBF changes from baseline were observed in the lvmPFC (-27.75 (9.54), p=0.000006), lACC (-21.27 [7.53], p=0.000009), rvmPFC (-25.25 [11.67], p=0.000266), ldlPFC (-19.87 [9.71], p=0.000480), rACC (-14.66 [7.40], p=0.000668), ldmPFC (-13.77 [6.96], p=0.000681), raINS (-17.23 [9.80], p=0.002025), and rdmPFC (-11.29 [7.02], p=0.004175). There were no sham effects or group-level differences (active-sham) in the predefined ROIs, although extensive voxel-wise changes were detected in the exploratory analyses.

Conclusion

The findings suggest that aiTBS and targeting the ldlPFC may modulate CBF in the DPMS which could be a putative target for pain relief. The physiological origin of the negative CBF effects, and whether these effects are directly linked to changes in cortical excitability and neuroplasticity is unclear. Similar findings using ASL have been observed in healthy volunteers, with prolonged widespread decreases in CBF being demonstrated after both anodal and cathodal tDCS applied to the ldlPFC 4. While we cannot exclude the possibility of non-specific confounding effects on CBF, such as altered levels of arousal and stress after the intense treatment schedule, the relative stability of PCASL measurements to inter-subject and inter-session variability has been demonstrated previously 5. The application of partial volume error correction (PVEc) alongside conventional ‘uncorrected’ CBF images indicates potential benefits in statistical power by mitigating any changes in perfusion that are co-incident with alterations in brain structure, such as atrophy changes in the patient population or comparisons between subjects across wide age ranges. However, physiological variables that can confound ASL signals should be carefully assessed during experimental designs, and approaches that can better detect and account for sham effects will enhance assessments (e.g. parallel designed trials). This pilot work provides a procedural framework that may be useful in developing standardized imaging protocols for future neuromodulation trials in chronic pain.
