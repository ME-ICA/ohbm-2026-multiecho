Introduction

A detailed understanding of variability in the hemodynamic response function (HRF)—the BOLD signal fluctuation following neural activity—remains elusive, despite its relevance for neural and vascular mechanisms. Most fMRI analyses still rely on a canonical HRF, typically modelled as a gamma function peaking 4–6 s post-stimulus under linearity assumptions1. However, these assumptions fail for short stimuli; canonical models, which are based on sustained stimulation and coarse resolution, risk false negative results2. There is substantial evidence about HRF variability across regions and individuals, and these differences might shed light on underlying neural processes, such as inhibition. 

Recent advances in fast fMRI (TR ≤ 1 s) enable finer HRF characterization. Recent findings reveal earlier, narrower peaks and higher amplitudes than predicted by canonical models, and a coexistence of fast and slow HRFs. Intra-regional differences in voxelwise responses have been suggested in V1, with peak latency following a spatial gradient4. However, despite these improvements in temporal resolution, the use of spatial blurring and large voxels can lead to reduced spatial specificity by amplifying signals from surface veins5. Lastly, much like the early models motivating the canonical HRF, recent work challenging this model relies mainly on visual experiments. Recent work 3 on auditory cortex suggests responses appear relatively stable across time; principal component analyses suggest stereotypical HRFs dominate, although peak latency differs across regions.

The present study uses fast fMRI and a data-driven approach to estimate voxelwise HRFs to short, naturalistic auditory stimuli, controlling for macrovascular effects. We test HRF stability across sessions and acquisition protocols, examine fine-grained temporal differences within auditory cortex, and assess links to cortical myelination.

Methods

We collected fast fMRI data (TR=1s) across two independent acquisition protocols (Dataset 1: 2mm isotropic, 2 sessions of 6 runs each; Dataset 2: 2.5mm isotropic, 4 sessions of 6 runs each; 12 sound presentations per run). Healthy participants (N=5 for Dataset 1, N=6 total for Dataset 2) passively listened to 500ms environmental sounds presented with long interstimulus intervals (≈18.5-23.5s). We also collected multi-parameter mapping (MPM) data to estimate R1 relaxation rates, which served as a quantitative proxy for cortical myelination. We employed a preprocessing pipeline including between-session nonlinear brain alignment with a novel nonlinear warping method and using TSNR masking to exclude voxels near large vessels to mitigate macrovascular artifacts. Hierarchical clustering was carried out on the resulting voxelwise event-related averages; this revealed a wide variety of hemodynamically plausible response shapes. Based on these shapes, we developed six flexible data-driven models based on one, two, or three gamma functions to capture the complexity, including models with initial negative peaks (initial dip responses). To evaluate whether the data-driven models outperformed standard HRF models (SPM, GLM single), we computed Radj² values each cluster mean from an independent session and compared these values across models using paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. We also examined HRF stability over time by testing how the fit changes when the sample size (=number of runs) increases, for both simulated and observed data. To gain a deeper understanding of HRF differences within auditory cortex, auditory regions-of-interest (ROI) were derived from custom labels and one event-related average per ROI was extracted and fitted using the data-driven models. From the best-fitting model per ROI, peak latency was recorded for the first peak and normalised within each participant, and averaged across participants to generate peak delay maps. Finally, ROI-wise R1 values were extracted to assess whether they were predictive of differences in amplitude and peak latency, using a linear mixed model accounting for hemisphere- and subject-level variability.

Results

Clustering revealed several different shapes across cortex that were associated with a hemodynamic response to short auditory stimuli. These shapes included reliable stereotypical responses in and around auditory temporal areas across participants, as well as clusters showing sustained positive or negative responses. Response patterns were generally stable across the two sessions, however, this stability varied across brain regions. Overall, the models derived from this data significantly outperformed common HRF models in capturing the variability of responses in an independent session. After Bonferroni correction, differences remained significant: custom models versus canonical HRF, custom models versus canonical HRF with derivatives, and custom model versus GLM single-basis model. These results suggest that standard HRF approaches may not optimally capture complex response shapes. 

We also assessed how much data (in number of runs) would be required to achieve stable HRF estimates.  Across subjects, the largest increase in goodness of fit occurred between run1 and run2 (0.07  ). The average change in goodness-of-fit in Session 1 when adding consecutive runs to the model (to 6 runs) was 0.2 , whereas the change from Session1 to Session2 (6 runs to 12 runs) was only 0.02 . These results suggest that while goodness-of-fit steadily improves as more data are added, across all participants and voxels, 80% of the maximum fit quality was reached within 4.5 runs, suggesting 4-5 runs are sufficient to obtain stable HRF estimates. 

 We next investigated whether differences in response shape could be detected across auditory regions. Despite notable individual differences, we observed consistent general patterns in peak latency across participants. More specifically, faster responses were localised to medial areas (e.g. in ROIs “A1/R”), whereas slower responses localised to lateral areas (e.g. “RA4”). Crucially, these spatial patterns of peak latency were reproducible in the same participants across scanning protocols, supporting their robustness. Given the known myelination profiles of the ROIs with the fastest observed peak times in the current study (i.e. “A1, R, RT”), we investigated whether R1 values could explain variability in amplitude and peak timing across ROIs. BOLD response amplitude was strongly positively predicted by R1, with a less robust relationship with peak latency: in Dataset1, regions with higher degrees of myelination exhibited faster peak times, but this relationship was not statistically significant  in Dataset2. 

Conclusion

Using fast fMRI to characterize voxelwise responses in the brain when people are listening to short sounds, we find stable and complex responses that are reproducible across participants and sessions. Importantly, many of the observed responses would not be accurately captured using canonical HRF models, even those including temporal and spatial derivatives. Having acquired up to 24 runs per person, we confirm that accumulating data is associated with an improved ability to capture responses reliably. However, our results also show that an average of ~4 runs is sufficient to obtain a stable estimate of the HRF across the brain, and even less data is necessary for estimates to converge in auditory temporal areas. Finally, we found that temporal characteristics of the hemodynamic response exhibit similar spatial profiles across participants and observe a gradient of faster responses in medial auditory areas and slower responses in lateral auditory areas. Peak latency differences across auditory areas could be (weakly) predicted by R1 values as a proxy of myelination in one dataset but not in the other. Our findings provide a framework for moving beyond stereotypical HRF characterizations, which is essential for improving the accuracy and biological interpretability of auditory processing studies in the human brain.
