Introduction

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) and Positron Emission Tomography (PET) are established clinical imaging techniques widely used to detect neurodegenerative changes in disorders. MRI and PET capture complementary aspects of brain integrity—tissue structure and glucose metabolism, respectively. Both modalities exhibit age-related alterations and are therefore essential for understanding brain aging and neurodegeneration. Notably, the spatial patterns of age-related change differ between modalities1,2. However, most studies have examined age effects within a single modality, limiting insight into the shared biological processes that manifest jointly across structural and metabolic domains. 

To address this limitation, we focused on canonical correlation analysis (CCA) as a framework for integrating structural (MRI) and metabolic (PET) information. Because multiple CCA variants exist, we evaluated three commonly used approaches—classical CCA, regularized CCA (rCCA), and decomposition-based CCA (dCCA). Each method yields a pair of highly correlated modality-specific scores, such that each score carries substantial information from the other modality. By comparing these variants, our aim was to determine which method most robustly captures multimodal age-related brain changes, thereby providing insight into shared neurobiological mechanisms and enabling more sensitive imaging biomarkers of brain aging. 

As the results showed, dCCA3 provided the strongest association with age. Thus, we used the dCCA-derived MRI score as a phenotype for genome-wide association analyses (GWAS) in a large cohort. Only MRI was used because MRI is far more widely available in clinical practice and currently only MRI data are paired with genetic information at the scale required for GWAS. Using the dCCA-derived MRI phenotype, we identified genetic variants associated with structural–metabolic aging, uncovering molecular mechanisms that drive age-related brain changes.

Methods

We analyzed paired T1-weighted structural MRI and FDG-PET data from 296 cognitively healthy participants in the ADNI dataset (54.1% females, age 73.79 ±6.15 years). Cortical grey-matter volume (MRI, CAT GMV) and glucose metabolism (PET, SUV ratio) were extracted using the Schaefer 200-parcel atlas (17-network solution). All parcel values were standardized across subjects. We evaluated four dimensionality-reduction approaches— Principal Component Analysis (PCA) used as a unimodal baseline, and three CCA approaches, classical CCA, rCCA, and dCCA. Parcel-wise weights were learned independently by each method: PCA selects weights that maximize within-modality variance, whereas CCA-based methods select weights that maximize the correlation between MRI- and PET-derived scores, ensuring that each modality-specific score carries substantial information about the other. For all methods, participant-level modality-specific scores were computed as weighted sums of parcel values, yielding one MRI score and one PET score per individual. The first component from each method was used, as it captures the greatest proportion of variance (or cross-modal covariance for CCA-based approaches). 

To evaluate and compare the performance of the dimensionality-reduction approaches, we correlated the participant-level scores from each method with chronological age and with ADNI cognitive test scores, enabling assessment of how well each approach captures aging and clinical patterns. As a baseline, we also computed the Spearman correlation between chronological age and each parcel’s GMV or SUV values. This check allowed us to determine whether the scores produced by dimensionality reduction methods explained agerelated variation at least as strongly as any single parcel—a key criterion for establishing validity of multi-region imaging-based aging biomarkers. The method with strongest correlation with age was chosen for further analysis (dCCA), yielding a multimodal aging axis.

Regional contributions of each modality to the multimodal aging axis were characterized by examining the dCCA weight vectors indicating the direction and magnitude of each region’s contribution to the derived score. Modality-specific loadings were computed as Spearman correlations between each parcel’s GMV or SUV value and the corresponding dCCA score. The loadings provide a biologically interpretable measure of how strongly each region reflects the shared multimodal aging axis. Generalizability of this newly derived score was assessed using 32,129 healthy UK Biobank participants (52.5%, age 63.96 ±7.52 years) thereby generating an out-of-sample aging score. For this, we used the ADNI-derived MRI dCCA weight to calculate the scores from UKB MRI data, as PET data were not available in this cohort. This allowed us to evaluate the stability of the aging axis and demonstrated the feasibility of applying the dCCA-derived model to a large, independent unimodal dataset. This score was then subjected to GWAS. Two GWASs were performed on 26,263 unrelated White British UKB participants, a subset of the 32,129 participants used for cross-cohort generalization. Genotypes followed standard quality control procedures. Each GWAS tested 8,105,763 SNPs; one model included covariates age, sex, and 10 genetic principal components (age-corrected), while the second omitted age covariate to assess potential confounding by age. Independent loci were defined using LD-clumping (r² < 0.6, 250-kb window), and genome-wide significance was set at p < 5×10⁻⁸. SNP-to-gene mapping was performed using both positional mapping and eQTL-based approaches implemented in FUMA. Functional annotation was carried out in FUMA using its integrated GWAS Catalog, and eQTL analyses were performed with GTEx.

All analyses were performed in Python 3.10.13 using NumPy, SciPy, and scikit-learn, and dCCA was used from https://github.com/shu-hai/D-CCA.

Results

Among the four dimensionality-reduction approaches, dCCA achieved the strongest correlations with chronological age for both MRI and PET (r = –0.556 and –0.562, respectively). Although rCCA also showed good correlation with age, dCCA consistently outperformed both CCA variants and showed stronger associations with ADNI cognitive scores. PCA applied to MRI performed comparably for several cognitive domains—and better for executive measures. Only dCCA produced participant-level scores whose correlation with age exceeded the age associations observed for any individual parcel’s GMV or SUV values (Fig. 2a). Moreover, dCCA yields MRI- and PET-derived scores that are, by construction, highly correlated, ensuring that both modalities contribute to a shared latent aging dimension, whereas PCA does not enforce inter-modal alignment. Based on these results, we selected dCCA for subsequent analyses.

Examining the dCCA weight vectors revealed a clear spatial gradient in the MRI modality (Fig. 2b). Negative weights were primarily located in frontal and anterior cingulate regions— areas known to exhibit pronounced age-related structural changes—whereas positive weights were concentrated in occipital and posterior parietal cortices, which tend to be relatively preserved with advancing age. This polarity indicates that regions showing stronger agerelated decline contributes inversely to the latent multimodal axis, while structurally stable regions contribute in the opposite direction, together forming a coherent structural–metabolic aging dimension. In contrast, all PET weights were positive and homogeneous across regions, consistent with the globally distributed pattern of age-related reductions in glucose metabolism and highlighting the uniform metabolic contribution to the multimodal aging axis.

The spatial distribution of MRI and PET loadings closely mirrored their respective weight vectors, with negative MRI weights yielding low loadings in frontal parcels and higher values in occipital regions. Because the loading maps reproduced the same spatial pattern, only the weight-based brain maps are shown (Fig. 2b). PET loadings exceeded MRI loadings in most parcels (172/200), reflecting the globally distributed nature of age-related metabolic decline. Parcels with stronger MRI loadings were primarily located in occipital (12/28) and parietal regions (13/28), with only two temporal and one frontal parcel showing this pattern, consistent with the elevated MRI loadings observed for visual networks (Fig. 2c).

Projecting the ADNI-derived MRI dCCA weight vector onto the UK Biobank cohort yielded an out-of-sample age correlation (Spearman r = −0.521) similar to that observed in ADNI (r = −0.556), supporting its robustness and suitability as a quantitative phenotype for genetic analyses. Eight independent loci were identified across the two GWASs, with two loci unique to the age‑corrected GWAS (L1, L2) and two unique to the GWAS without age correction (L7, L8; Fig. 2d–f). These loci were represented by a total of ten genome‑wide significant SNPs (pairwise R 2 < 0.6), six per model with two variants overlapping, indicating that part of the genetic architecture captured by the dCCA MRI score depends on whether age‑related variance is retained. Nine of the ten variants have reported associations with brain‑related phenotypes, including cortical thickness, regional brain volumes, notably rs76928645 near the EGFR gene, Alzheimer’s disease risk. Expression quantitative trait locus analyses showed that six significant SNPs modulate gene expression in cortical, hippocampal, and cingulate regions, affecting genes such as STX6, KIAA1614, PRDM5, and EGFR, the latter being pharmacologically targetable. Since none of the ten SNPs were exonic, we searched for exonic variants yielding six among which rs41314549 was of particular interest as it affects THBS3 expression in frontal cortex, adding a candidate gene for the observed imaging phenotype.

Conclusion

Across four dimensionality-reduction approaches, decomposition-based CCA (dCCA) emerged as the most sensitive and biologically meaningful method for capturing multimodal brain aging. Only dCCA produced participant-level scores whose associations with age exceeded those of any individual MRI or PET parcel, and these scores also reflected cognitive performance across multiple domains. The spatial organization of the dCCA weights recapitulated well-established neuroanatomical aging gradients—strong frontal and anterior decline with relative occipital–parietal preservation for MRI, and a uniform cortical decline for PET—highlighting the method’s ability to integrate complementary structural and metabolic information.

When projected onto an independent cohort of >32,000 UK Biobank participants, the MRI-derived dCCA score reproduced the age associations observed in ADNI, demonstrating strong cross-cohort generalizability and enabling its use as a quantitative phenotype in largescale genetic analyses. The resulting GWAS identified several genome-wide significant loci, including variants influencing STX6, KIAA1614, PRDM5, and EGFR, with convergent evidence pointing to reduced expression of STX6 and EGFR—genes implicated in Alzheimer’s disease progression—as neuroprotective4,5. 

Further genetic analyses such as linkage disequilibrium score regression are warranted to get detailed insights.

Together, these findings establish a robust framework for characterizing coordinated structural–metabolic brain aging. Its phenotypic relevance, genetic anchoring, and scalability to large unimodal datasets underscore its potential for advancing the mechanistic understanding of neurodegeneration and informing future biomarker development.
