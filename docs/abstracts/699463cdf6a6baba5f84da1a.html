Introduction

One of the overarching goals of functional connectivity research is to accurately predict a range of behaviors and phenotypes using connectome-based predictive modeling (CPM). If this is achieved, new clinical applications of fMRI are likely to emerge. As of now, it is possible to predict many behaviors with significant accuracy using CPM. However, for this methodology to have any clinical utility, the effect size of the predictions must be greatly increased. Prior work has demonstrated that caricaturing resting-state data—in other words, removing task-relevant, dominant co-activation patterns—prior to constructing connectomes can improve prediction accuracy by leveraging data typically ignored in connectome construction.1 Furthermore, building models using both the standard and caricatured data combined could lead to even better predictions. Here, we apply caricaturing to a transdiagnostic resting-state dataset with 28 cognitive measures, which were previously predicted using CPM with varying degrees of success.2 We then use CPM to build models using Standard, Caricatured, and Combined data, comparing the performance of the Combined models to the other two to determine for which measures caricaturing adds new information that can be used in the model-building process.

Methods

We used the YaleNeuroConnect dataset for CPM. Briefly, it includes participants with diverse psychiatric backgrounds who were scanned 8 times in various conditions. They completed self-report questionnaires, a psychiatric interview, and a cognitive test battery. More information can be found elsewhere.3 After quality assurance measures2 were satisfied, the data comprised 317 resting-state scans from unique participants. Scans were parcellated with the Shen268 atlas, and pairwise correlations and Fisher transforms were performed to obtain the connectomes. For CPM, we used 28 cognitive measures. From the Wechsler Abbreviated Scale of Intelligence 2nd Edition, the matrix reasoning and vocabulary raw scores (wasi_mr_raw and wasi_vocab_raw) were predicted. From the Delis-Kapan Executive Function Scale, the twenty questions test total weighted achievement raw score (dkefs_20q_was_raw), twenty questions test total questions asked raw score (dkefs_20q_totalQ_raw), twenty questions test initial abstraction raw score (dkefs_20q_abstraction_raw), color-word inference test inhibition condition three raw score (dkefs_cw_cond3_raw), color-word inference test word reading condition two raw score (dkefs_cw_cond2_raw), color-word inference test color naming condition one raw score (dkefs_cw_cond1_raw), verbal fluency test category fluency condition two total correct raw score (dkefs_vf_cond2_raw), verbal fluency test letter fluency condition one total correct raw score (dkefs_vf_cond1_raw), trail making test number-letter sequencing condition four raw score (dkefs_tm_cond4_raw), trail making test letter sequencing condition three raw score (dkefs_tm_cond3_raw), and trail making test number sequencing condition two raw score (dkefs_tm_cond2_raw) were predicted. From the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scales, the cancellation total raw score (wais_canc_tot_raw), letter-number sequencing raw score (wais_LN_raw), coding raw score (wais_coding_raw), and symbol search raw score (wais_symbol_raw) were predicted. From the Wide Range Assessment of Memory and Learning 2nd Edition, the finger windows raw score (wraml_FW_cr), verbal learning intrusion (wraml_vl_intrusion), verbal learning delayed recall raw score (wraml_vl_cr_delay), and verbal learning raw score (wraml_vl_cr_tot) were predicted. From the Wide Range Achievement Test 5th Edition, the word reading raw score (wrat_cr_raw) was predicted. From the Boston Naming Test 2nd Edition, the total score (bnt_totalScore), number of correct responses following a phenomic cue (bnt_cr_withPhonCue), number of phenomic cues (bnt_phonCue), number of correct responses following a stimulus cue (bnt_cr_withStimCue), number of stimulus cues (bnt_stimCues), and number of spontaneously given correct responses (bnt_cr_spon) were predicted.

We used the UCLA Consortium for Neuropsychiatric Phenomics4 to construct the caricature task manifold. After processing and quality control, 136 participants with 6 task scans each remained. Detailed information on the processing, quality control, and caricaturing procedure is provided elsewhere.1

Each measure was predicted using CPM with either the Standard or Caricatured connectomes or the Combined data. We used 1000 iterations of 10-fold cross-validation to obtain an accuracy distribution. Feature selection was performed by considering only edges that were significantly correlated (p < 0.05) with the behavior, controlling for age and sex via partial correlation. To create the predictive feature, the sum of the negatively correlated features was subtracted from the sum of the positively correlated features for each participant. Models were built with linear regression. Spearman’s correlation between the predicted and true measure was used to assess accuracy. To determine whether the combined models outperformed the other models, corrected paired t-tests were used.1 This yielded 56 uncorrected p-values, which were then corrected using the mafdr.m FDR procedure.

Results

Prediction accuracy results are shown in Figure 1. From Figure 1A, we observe a wide range of accuracies that depend on the measure being predicted. We also observe differences in the order in which the model types perform. Caricatured models show numerically greater performance on 19 out of the 28 measures compared to Standard models. One notable finding is that, at least numerically, the Combined model was always superior to the lowest-performing model type. Figure 1B demonstrates the relative performance of the Standard vs the Caricatured models and indicates in which cases the Combined model was significantly more accurate than both of the other model types. The three measures for which the Combined model was superior were from Delis-Kapan Executive Function Scale, namely the initial abstraction score (Combined vs Standard pFDR = 1.00 X 10-7; Combined vs Caricatured pFDR = 0.0024), the color-word interference condition 3 i.e. inhibition (Combined vs Standard pFDR = 2.41 X 10-6; Combined vs Caricatured pFDR = 2.88 X 10-12), and the trail making condition 4 i.e. number-letter sequencing (Combined vs Standard pFDR = 0.022; Combined vs Caricatured pFDR = 2.26 X 10-16). Interestingly, the Combined model was superior only when the Standard model was at least as accurate as the Caricatured model on average. 

Conclusion

Although combining the Standard and Caricatured data sometimes led to significant improvements in predictive accuracy, which would support our hypothesis, this was not the case most often. In other words, at least in this analysis, combining data types does not always improve predictive modeling. When only looking numerically, the Combined models were superior for 11 of the 28 measures. At a minimum, we observed that combining the two data types never produced the worst-performing model across any cognitive measure. This indicates that the combination of data types, although it does not always yield unique or useful information, does not destroy baseline predictive information. Another crucial observation was that the Combined model was only the best-performing model when the Standard model was at least as accurate as the Caricatured counterpart. Although this was not noted in the past,1 it indicates a need for future research to determine why the caricaturing method differentially affects predictive performance across behaviors and how this might affect the output of the combination of the two data types. 

Our work has a few limitations. First, we used the simplest version of CPM, which greatly reduces the information present in the connectomes. For example, in our prior study,1 we used ridge regression, which allows for a more nuanced model to emerge from the information present in each feature. The relative benefits of combining the two data types were likely hindered by the extensive aggregation used in the CPM process. If that were the case, then even the fact that the Combined models were superior three times is impressive. Second, we used Spearman’s correlation to assess model accuracy. Other metrics, such as Pearson’s correlation and mean squared error, may yield different results; therefore, it would be interesting to compare across methods. Finally, although this work provides a great introduction to how caricaturing affects the prediction of cognitive metrics, many other behaviors are of interest to the field. Future work is needed to characterize the nature of caricature and its effects on the prediction of other phenotypes.

In conclusion, this work represents the next step in evaluating the interplay between the caricaturing method and CPM. Though only a few positive results were noted, we argue that future changes to the methodology could result in better performance from the Combined models. Future research will therefore enhance the utility of the caricaturing method in neuroimaging, and promising work will follow from its continued implementation.
