Introduction

Regional heterogeneity of voxel-wise activation is considered an approximation of neural selectivity and tuning within a functional region and has been shown to be sensitive enough to capture behavioral variations.1,2 In this study we aim to quantify whether a regional heterogeneity measure we developed, Hcorr1, has the sensitivity to detect and localize treatment induced changes in functional activity patterns in resting state fMRI (rs-fMRI). We used a public database with rs-fMRI data taken at two time points — before and after repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS). By quantifying the changes before and after rTMS, we were able to localize regions of significant BOLD response changes induced by the rTMS.

Methods

We utilized OpenNeuro Dataset ds0030373 of cocaine use disorder patients, which consisted of 40 total subjects, split into sham (n = 18, age = 33.6±8, 6% female) and treatment (n = 22, age = 35.2±7, 18% female) groups. All subjects underwent a baseline scan with 10 minutes of rs-fMRI (EPI sequence, TR/TE = 2000/30.001ms, flip angle = 75º, FOV = 240mm2, voxel = 3x3x3.33mm, number of slices = 36) and a structural T1-weighted scan (FFE SENSE sequence, TR/TE = 7/3.5ms, FOV = 240 mm2, voxel = 1mm3, number of slices = 180), all on a Phillips Ingenia 3T scanner. Repetitive TMS was then applied over the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC; average MNI [-31, 45, 37]) for 10 days within a 2-week period of 5000 pulses per day (50 trains at 5Hz for two sessions, with 50 pulses/train, 10s inter-train interval and 15min inter-session interval), as described by Angeles-Valdez et al. (2024).3 The acute phase of the rTMS protocol was placebo-controlled double-blind, followed by a maintenance phase of 6 months with an additional 4 MRI scanning sessions. For our study, we only used the baseline (T0) and 2 week follow up (T1) scans to assess the acute impacts of rTMS.

MR image preprocessing was done using SPM25 in MATLAB, including T1 segmentation and preprocessing of rs-fMRI data (realignment, coregistration, normalizing, and smoothing), following the default procedure and parameters. The artifact detection toolbox was then used for motion correction (TRs rejected above a global threshold of z = 6.0 or motion threshold of 1.0 mm), and nuisance covariates were removed (6 motion parameters and first derivatives). Hcorr values were calculated for each subject and time point. Specifically, pairwise correlations were computed between neighboring voxels within a Chebyshev distance of 2 voxels, and then Fisher transformed. The Hcorr value per voxel was calculated as the standard deviation of all pairwise Fisher-transformed correlations with its neighbors. To analyze the difference before and after rTMS for treatment versus sham conditions, we used a linear mixed effects model in AFNI (3dLME) to examinine the interaction between group and time. Age and education were included as covariates, and a random slope for the effect of time was modeled. To test for temporal changes within each group separately, we decomposed the group × time interaction using simple main effects (two tailed t-contrasts). Cluster correction (nearest neighbor = 2, bi-sided) was then performed using Monte Carlo simulation (3dClustSim in AFNI) to adjust for multiple comparisons (p < 0.001, α < 0.05).

Results

There were two significant clusters in the treatment group when contrasting T0 and T1, in each hemisphere of the cerebellum (Figure 1a). Both cerebellar clusters were negative, implicating a decrease in regional heterogeneity at T1 compared to T0. Clusters 1 and 2 both spanned areas of lobule VI and crus I in each hemisphere — cluster 1 within the right hemisphere (1,563 voxels, peak z = -5.05), and cluster 2 within the left hemisphere (788 voxels, peak z = -3.31). There were 3 additional clusters trending toward significance but that were all positive, indicating regional heterogeneity higher in T1 vs. T0 (Figure 1b). These clusters were within the bilateral anterior cingulate cortex (ACC; 913 voxels, peak z = 3.45), left superior medial frontal gyrus (left mSFG; 857 voxels, peak z = 3.36), and left basal ganglia extending into the left superior temporal gyrus (STG; 652 voxels, peak z = 3.15). There were no significant clusters in the sham group contrast, even at the lower threshold (p = 0.002) used to detect additional clusters in the treatment group (Fig. 1b). Additionally, we identified no significant impact of age or education level after cluster correction.

Conclusion

We found both areas of increased and decreased regional heterogeneity following active rTMS. The significant and marginal findings within the treatment group, coupled with the lack of findings within the sham group, suggest that our technique may have the sensitivity to detect rTMS induced temporal changes within rs-fMRI data, including regions near the site of stimulation. Furthermore, regions that were different between time points have previously been implicated in addiction and substance use disorders.4,5 These results suggest that this regional heterogeneity measurement, Hcorr, may serve as a useful neural marker to detect and localize regional functional changes induced by non-invasive brain stimulation such as rTMS or other interventional therapies.
