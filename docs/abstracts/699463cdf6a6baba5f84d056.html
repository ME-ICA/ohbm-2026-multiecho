Introduction

As urbanization and aging overlap, older adults face increasing health challenges, including chronic diseases and cognitive decline. To promote healthy aging, age-friendly urban environments, particularly urban green open spaces (UGOS), play a vital role by encouraging physical activity, enhancing well-being, and improving mental health^1. However, the neurostructural effects of UGOS on brain morphology remain unclear.

The level of exposure to UGOS is crucial for brain health, with higher exposure linked to greater benefits^2. Accessibility is key for older adults, as mobility issues reduce UGOS exposure. Differences in age-related neuroplasticity further influence how UGOS impacts brain health, making age a significant factor in this relationship. Physical activity (PA) also interacts with UGOS accessibility to influence brain health. 

This study explores the association between UGOS accessibility and gray matter volume across different ages and investigates the interaction between UGOS and PA. 

Methods

The present study utilized a moving window approach to examine how the association between UGOS accessibility and gray matter volume (GMV) varied along age in a cross-sectional sample of 717 Chinese older adults^3. In particular, the age windows were defined in ascending order according to the age of the participants, with each window spanning five years and a step size of one year. The processing yielded 27 subgroups. Subsequently, the standardized coefficients of each fixed term within each subset window were bootstrapped 500 times, which were matched to the average value of age across the 27 subgroups. We additionally evaluated the significance of the bootstrapped standardized coefficients of UGOS accessibility within each subset window, using 95% confidence intervals. Subsequently, we explored the potential existence of age thresholds for the UGOS accessibility–GMV relationships along age. Step regressions (linear regressions that modify only the intercept at a given age level), segmented regressions (exhibiting changes only in slope at a given age level), and step-segmented regressions (showing changes in both the intercept and slope at a given age level) were employed for the bootstrapped coefficients of UGOS accessibility along age. Each of these models yields a change point (threshold), which describes the age level at which the relationship undergoes a shift. The optimal threshold model and corresponding threshold were selected using the lowest Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). To further substantiate the validity of the identified thresholds, we conducted bootstrapped (200 times) linear regressions on both sides of each threshold for each variable. Subsequent to this, we utilized the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U-test to compare the slope and the predicted value evaluated prior to and following the threshold. 

To further explore the specific brain regions affected by UGOS accessibility, we analyzed the impact of  UGOS accessibility on GMV in 246 regions using the general linear model and performed enrichment analysis via spin-based permutation testing to identify concentration patterns of associations within specific anatomical structures. Finally, we investigated the interaction between UGOS accessibility and physical activity (PA) on GMV through generalized additive modeling.

Results

We found that higher UGOS accessibility was significantly associated with better maintenance of GMV in older adults, but this positive effect was moderated by age, occurring only within the 61-68 age range and peaking at 65.94 years. Additionally, the primary brain regions affected included the inferior temporal gyrus, fusiform gyrus, superior temporal gyrus, and insular gyrus. More importantly, this effect was also influenced by PA levels; at higher UGOS accessibility, greater PA was associated with larger gray matter volumes.

Conclusion

We found that the positive effect of UGOS accessibility on GMV in older adults was influenced by age, and this effect reached its maximum at 65.94 years. Specifically, within the age window of 61–68 years, UGOS accessibility mainly protected the temporal lobe regions represented by the ITG, FuG, and STG and the insula. Finally, the protective effect of UGOS accessibility on brain structure was influenced by the level of PA. Under high UGOS accessibility, older adults with better PA habits exhibited stronger protective effects of UGOS. In conclusion, these results suggest that improving UGOS accessibility could play a crucial role in supporting healthy brain aging, particularly for specific age stages, and that this effect may be further enhanced by higher levels of physical activity. This understanding may pave the way for identifying more effective environmental protection factors that enhance brain health and promote healthier aging trajectories.
