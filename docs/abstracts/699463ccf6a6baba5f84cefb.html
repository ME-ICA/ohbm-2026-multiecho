Introduction

Suicide is a leading cause of mortality in major depressive disorder (MDD), yet its neurobiological mechanisms remain insufficiently understood. Advances in network neuroscience emphasize the importance of large-scale brain connectivity in suicide vulnerability. This study aimed to identify resting-state functional connectivity (FC) alterations distinguishing suicidal (SD) from non-suicidal (NSD) depression within MDD using a data-driven network-based statistic (NBS) approach, and to examine their associations with suicidality- and childhood trauma-related measures.

Methods

Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) data were acquired from 204 participants (SD = 61, NSD = 62, HC = 81) using a 3.0-Tesla Siemens Trio™ scanner (EPI: TR = 2000 ms, TE = 30 ms, voxel size = 3×3×4 mm³; T1: MPRAGE, 1×1×1 mm³). Whole-brain FC matrices were constructed using the Schaefer 400-node cortical atlas. Group differences were assessed using NBS (Zalesky et al., 2010) with a threshold of T = 3.3 (5,000 permutations) (Bryant et al., 2024), controlling for age, sex, and years of education. Between- and within-network FC differences were examined across Yeo’s seven canonical networks (Schaefer et al., 2018). Post hoc correlations were conducted between mean FC strength extracted from the NBS-identified subnetwork and clinical measures of suicidal ideation (Beck’s Scale for Suicidal Ideation; SSI) and childhood trauma (28-item Childhood Trauma Questionnaire; CTQ—total and subscales), while controlling for age and sex. All analyses were performed using the NBS toolbox implemented within the SPM12 environment running on MATLAB R2021a, and subsequent statistical analyses were conducted in R version 4.4.2.

Results

NBS revealed a significant subnetwork showing reduced FC in SD relative to NSD (p-FWE [family-wise error] = 0.038; 82 nodes, 118 edges), spanning 16 within- and between-network pairs primarily involving the visual (VIS), somatomotor (SMN), dorsal attention (DAN), frontoparietal (FPN), and default mode (DMN) networks. This subnetwork reflected widespread reductions in VIS-related and intra-network FC in SD compared with NSD. In comparison with healthy controls, SD exhibited both decreased (p-FWE = 0.001, 163 nodes, 345 edges) and increased FC (p-FWE = 0.023, 107 nodes, 153 edges), whereas NSD showed no significant alterations. Within the MDD cohort, mean FC across the identified subnetwork (SD < NSD) as well as SMN–VIS and DMN–DMN FC were negatively correlated with SSI scores (r = –0.327, p-FWE = 0.004; r = –0.278, p-FWE = 0.034; r = –0.275, p-FWE = 0.038, respectively). In the SD group, SMN–SMN FC was inversely correlated with CTQ–sexual abuse scores (r = –0.402, p-FWE = 0.027).

Conclusion

These findings demonstrate disrupted integration among sensory–motor, attentional, and higher-order cognitive networks—including the VIS, SMN, DAN, FPN, and DMN—as a network-level hallmark of suicide vulnerability in MDD. The widespread reduction in FC suggests impaired coordination between perceptual, attentional, and self-referential systems, potentially compromising emotional and behavioral regulation in suicidal depression. The associations between reduced FC, suicidal ideation, and childhood trauma underscore a convergent contribution of current suicidal risk and early adverse experiences. Together, these results provide neurobiological evidence for large-scale functional network disruptions as potential connectome-based biomarkers of suicide vulnerability in MDD.
