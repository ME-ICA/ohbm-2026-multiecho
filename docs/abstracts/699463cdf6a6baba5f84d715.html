Introduction

Motor recovery after stroke varies substantially across individuals, complicating patient selection for targeted rehabilitation strategies and neuromodulation. As a potential biomarker of recovery, network alterations have been identified using resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI). However, fMRI is resource-intensive and often inaccessible in routine clinical care, especially in the acute phase and for severely affected stroke patients. To overcome these limitations, normative connectome-based lesion network mapping approaches have been developed to estimate functional connectivity from routine clinical imaging and identify symptom-relevant networks.1 More recently, this method has advanced from dichotomous prediction of symptom occurrence to continuous behavioral prediction. However, regarding the prediction of motor impairment after stroke, functional disconnectome studies have reported inconsistent findings, and to date there is no data on the prediction of motor recovery.2,3

This raises the question of whether the degree of motor recovery can be anticipated from functional disconnection patterns estimated early after stroke. Moreover, as distinct aspects of motor recovery, such as basal and complex upper-limb function, have been shown to rely on different networks,4,5 the question arises whether differences in functional disconnection patterns partly explain task-specific recovery.

To address these questions, we performed functional lesion network mapping in patients with upper-limb paresis. We hypothesized functional disconnection patterns to vary with lesion topology and that distinct functional disconnection networks would explain differential (basal and complex) aspects of motor recovery. Finally, we hypothesized that accounting for corticospinal tract (CST) damage would further refine disconnection-behavior associations.

Methods

Individual lesion masks were manually drawn on routine clinical MR images for 127 acute ischemic stroke patients who presented with upper-limb paresis at admission. Lesion masks were normalized to standard space, and right-hemispheric lesions were flipped to the left along the mid-sagittal axis. Basal (grip strength) and complex (Action Research Arm Test, ARAT) upper-limb motor function were assessed acutely (impairment) and >3 months after stroke. Relative recovery was defined as the difference between follow-up and initial impairment scores divided by the difference between the test’s maximum score and the initial impairment score, reflecting the proportion of recovery achieved relative to the individual’s recovery potential. Lesions were grouped into topological subgroups including cortical, subcortical, brainstem, and combined cortico-subcortical lesions.

Patient-specific functional disconnection maps were generated using LeadDBS (Lead Connectome Mapper) and a normative rs-fMRI connectome from 1000 healthy individuals. For each lesion, the mean BOLD time series of all voxels within the lesion volume was extracted for each healthy subject and used as a seed for whole-brain functional connectivity analysis. Seed–voxel correlations were Fisher z-transformed and averaged across subjects to yield a voxelwise lesion-specific functional disconnection map.1

One-sample t-tests and voxelwise uni- and multivariate regression analyses were performed in Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM) to identify regions that were significantly connected or disconnected across the full cohort, within predefined topological lesion subgroups, and in relation to behavioral variables. Here, we refer to regions with synchronous lesion connectivity as ‘functionally disconnected’, assuming that strong coupling with the lesion site results in functional impairment and disconnection from the healthy network context. To account for corticospinal tract (CST) damage, CST-lesion load (CST-LL) was computed as the maximum cross-sectional lesion-CST overlap weighted by the CST’s cross-sectional area at each slice and included as a covariate in multivariate analyses.

Results

Averaged across the whole cohort, lesions showed significant connectivity to a bilateral motor network comprising primary motor and premotor cortices, basal ganglia, internal capsule, temporal cortices, brainstem, and cerebellum.

Topological subgroups exhibited distinct disconnection profiles. Brainstem lesions were associated with disconnections to bilateral pons and cerebellum. Cortical lesions led to disconnections to bilateral motor and premotor cortices as well as the contralesional cerebellum. Subcortical and combined cortico-subcortical lesions resulted in disconnections within a distributed bilateral motor network involving cortical motor, premotor, and temporal regions together with subcortical gray and white matter, brainstem, and cerebellum. Of note, subcortical lesions showed stronger disconnection to bilateral subcortical structures, whereas cortico-subcortical lesions primarily resulted in functional disconnections to bilateral cortical regions. 

Recovery analyses revealed dissociable functional networks supporting basal versus complex motor recovery. Reduced recovery of complex motor function was associated with functional disconnections to the ipsilesional temporal cortex and bilateral pre- and supplementary motor areas. Conversely, reduced recovery of basal motor function was associated with functional disconnection between the lesion and bilateral subcortical regions, including the basal ganglia, brainstem, and cerebellar structures. When accounting for CST-LL, the associations with basal motor recovery remained robust, whereas no significant relationships persisted for recovery of complex motor function.

Conclusion

We found that the recovery of differential aspects of upper extremity motor function after stroke was associated with distinct functional disconnection networks. Impaired basal motor recovery was consistently associated with disconnections to subcortical, brainstem, and cerebellar regions. From a mechanistic perspective, these structures may represent key nodes that provide access to alternative descending pathways and integrative motor circuits, and hence might be crucial for the reorganization of basal motor control. Importantly, these associations persisted even after accounting for CST-lesion load, suggesting that basal motor improvements may exploit CST-independent compensatory mechanisms.4

In contrast, impaired complex motor recovery was associated with functional disconnections involving cortical (pre)motor and temporal regions, aligning with findings from structural lesion-network mapping, which demonstrate that these areas support visuomotor integration, object-oriented grasping, and coordination.5 The disappearance of these associations after adjusting for CST damage underlines that the behavioral contribution of higher-order motor cortices is contingent on CST integrity as the principal motor output pathway.4

In summary, our findings support the notion that the recovery of distinct aspects of motor control depends on the extent and localization of lesion-induced functional disconnections as well as on the structural integrity of the CST as the main motor output pathway. Our results may help inform individualized non-invasive brain stimulation approaches to enhance post-stroke rehabilitation: while patients with preserved CST integrity may benefit most from stimulation of cortical (pre)motor–temporal nodes supporting complex motor control, those with extensive CST injury may require engagement of subcortical–cerebellar circuits that appear critical for basal motor restoration. Such topology- and task-specific targeting may represent a promising direction for future research to advance personalized neurorehabilitation.
