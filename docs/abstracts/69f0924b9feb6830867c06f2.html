Introduction

Lifespan brain charts provide critical insights into the structural trajectories of cortical development and aging, helping to elucidate how neurobiological organizational hierarchies evolve from childhood through old age 1,2. Cortical thickness captures processes such as synaptic pruning, myelination, and age-related atrophy, yet the macroscopic organization of these lifelong processes remains incompletely characterized. Normative models have been instrumental in characterizing population-wide distributions of cortical phenotypes within healthy populations 3,4. Building on this foundation, Spectral Normative Modeling (SNM) leverages latent graph-spectral representations to efficiently model normative trajectories 5. This facilitates mapping brain charts at higher spatial resolutions, enabling the present study to explore mechanistic growth gradients across the lifespan.

Methods

We aggregated structural T1-weighted MRI from 30 datasets across 189 scanners/sites, yielding 78,405 quality-controlled scans from participants aged 5-95 years (50.6% female; Fig. 1). Cortical thickness was mapped to the fs-LR 32k surface and modeled using SNM, which utilized a multivariate hierarchical Bayesian regression that jointly accounts for age and sex covariates while harmonizing site effects via hierarchical priors. Thickness measurements were projected into a latent spectral space spanned by 1,000 connectivity eigenmodes. The fitted SNM was then used to produce normative centiles across 59,412 cortical vertices (excluding the medial wall) at 1-year increments from age 5 to 95. We computed the derivative of the median trajectories to estimate annual percent change in thickness at each age; these derivatives were averaged over age windows to produce two interpretive maps used throughout the study: Aging Thickness Decline (ATD), reflecting the mean annual percent change averaged from age 40–95, and Developmental Cortical Pruning (DCP), quantifying the mean annual percent change averaged from age 5–20. Finally, principal component analysis (PCA) of the median vertex-wise trajectories yielded the principal thickness growth gradients (TGGs), which were contextualized against established cortical hierarchies, including the Sensorimotor-Association axis (SA), functional connectivity gradients (FCG1–2), and cortical gene expression components (GEC1–3).

Results

We identified three principal cortical growth gradients (Fig. 2, TGG1–3) that together explained >90% of the variance in normative vertex-wise trajectories. Each gradient captured a distinctive growth mechanism. TGG1 reflected the mean cortical thickness pattern across the lifespan and showed very high correspondence with the young adult average thickness map (CT) (r = 0.91, P-spin < 0.0001), indicating that relative regional thickness ordering is largely preserved across life, with sensory cortices remaining thinner than higher-order association areas. TGG2 captured a cortical thinning pattern distinguishing changes in later life and was associated with the aging thickness decline (ATD) map (r = 0.88, P-spin < 0.0001), highlighting the heightened susceptibility of premotor and superior temporal cortices to atrophy in healthy aging. TGG3 primarily indexed early-life pruning and was strongly associated with DCP (r = -0.76, P-spin < 0.0001), delineating regions undergoing the greatest developmental refinement. Data-driven exploratory cluster analysis revealed consistent alignment of these gradients with established cortical hierarchies: TGG1 and CT clustered with SA, FCG1, and GEC1; TGG2 with FCG2, GEC2, and ATD; and TGG3 with GEC3 and DCP. Several associations survived stringent nonparametric permutation spin tests with FDR correction across all pairwise comparisons, demonstrating robust cross-modal convergence across organizational hierarchies and offering insights into neurobiological theories of cortical reorganization across development and aging.

Conclusion

Using SNM to produce vertex-level normative charts across a large, multisite lifespan sample, we reveal three mechanistic cortical growth gradients that dissociate stable global thickness organization from distinct developmental and aging processes. The observed anticorrelation between DCP and ATD offers a nuanced extension of the retrogenesis hypothesis, explaining how cortical reorganization in early life and aging mirror one another, with regions relatively spared by developmental pruning becoming those most susceptible to accelerated thinning in later life. Moreover, our cluster analysis recapitulates the mechanistic interpretations of each gradient: TGG1 captures the stable global thickness organization across life, aligning with principal transcriptional and functional hierarchies, all of which are known to follow the direction of the SA axis, whereas TGG2 and TGG3 reveal distinct aging- and developmental-specific mechanisms. For instance, the clustering of TGG2 and ATD with GEC2, a transcriptional component enriched for metabolic processes, points to a potential role for metabolic demand in late-life cortical thinning. TGG3, in turn, groups with the transcriptional component most strongly associated with synaptic plasticity during and after adolescence, reinforcing its link to developmental pruning mechanisms. Understanding the role of these organizational hierarchies in healthy brain structure may offer new avenues to study neurological and psychiatric pathologies as deviations from healthy trajectories.
