Introduction

Neurofeedback (NF) provides real-time information about neural activity to support self-regulation and induce brain plasticity. Although considered a promising non-invasive alternative to pharmacological treatments, NF faces a significant inefficacy problem: about $38\%$ of participants fail to learn self-regulation1. Motivation and attention are reported to strongly influence success, and disengagement can trigger a cycle of failure. Thus, monitoring cognitive engagement is essential2.

Sustained attention is required in demanding tasks, but it frequently fluctuates due to variations in arousal, often described by an inverted-U relationship. Performance deteriorates under hypo-arousal, associated with mind wandering, and hyper-arousal, linked to distraction. Individually increasing, or decreasing, task difficulty can counteract under-load and enhance engagement. Maintaining optimal performance therefore requires balancing arousal and attentional focus3.

Eye tracking (ET) and electrodermal activity (EDA) provide sensitive indices of attention and arousal. Pupil diameter (PD) reflects cognitive effort, fatigue, and task engagement, and its dynamics reveal shifts toward internal processing or mind wandering4. EDA, composed of tonic and phasic components, indexes sympathetic arousal and increases reliably with cognitive load5. Together, pupil and EDA trace the activity of the locus coeruleus–noradrenergic system.

This study extracts ET and EDA features associated with cognitive engagement and task difficulty during attention-demanding tasks. We examine how these physiological markers evolve across tasks and how they relate to cognitive load, expertise, and performance.

Methods

the Stroop task consists in 2 words, one on the top of the other, and participants have to say if the color name written on the bottom corresponds to the font color of the top word. Then the Addition task shows 3 numbers in a line. Participants have to select among the 2 lateral numbers the one that corresponds to the sum of the central digit with the previous one. For these 2 tasks, 3 task blocks were presented interleaved with 3 rest blocks consisting on a cardiac coherence exercise. For the Stroop task, each trial lasts 1250 ms, with a total block duration of 2 minutes. For addition task, each block lasts 1 minute and participants had 3s to answer during the first block, 2s for the second one, and 1s for the last one. The third task is a Focus task, where participants have to switch between internal focus and mind wandering conditions. For the internal focus condition, they had to think to as many words as possible beginning with a specific letter, while during mind wandering they let their thoughts flow freely. They repeated each condition 3 times, with 10 seconds of instruction in-between.

During the 3 tasks, we recorded the EDA using a BrainVision galvanic skin response set and ET with a Tobii Pro X3-120 eye tracker. We then extracted the phasic component of EDA, called Integrated Skin Conductance Driver (ISCR), using the MATLAB toolbox Ledalab within 10 seconds temporal windows. For the ET signal, we computed the PD, the fixation duration, the blink duration and number, and the gaze divergence (distance between the left and right eyes gaze points). Finally, we also estimated a performance score for the Stroop and Addition tasks between 0 and 1 using both the accuracy and reaction time with the following formula: 

Perf = (stimulus duration - reaction time) / stimulus duration * accuracy

Before processing the statistical analysis of the ISCR and ET features, we removed all the data points outside a range of 3 standard deviations from the mean value for each feature. We then normalized the features (ISCR, PD, blink duration and gaze divergence) for each task and each participant independently using z-score, with the aim of conducting across and within subject analyses to identify features able to accurately monitor task engagement. The first analysis we conducted on Stroop and Addition tasks is a two-way ANOVA including each feature (averaged across blocks for each participant) as dependent variable and the condition (i.e., task or rest) and order (i.e., block 1, 2 or 3) as independent factors. All p-values corresponding to post hoc tests included in this study were Bonferroni corrected. We also investigated the relationship between the performance and the evolution of features along each task using a one-way ANOVA including the performance averaged within each block as a dependent variable and block order (i.e. 1, 2, 3) as an independent factor. As the Stroop task has a constant difficulty level while the difficulty in Addition task increases (less time to answer the trials for each new block) we expected to see an increasing performance and decreasing in physiological features linked to arousal and workload for Stroop task and the opposite for Addition task.

Finally, we tested if the features identified with the analysis described above can be used to discriminate internal focus from mind wandering. We averaged the features of interest on the Focus task across 10-second non-overlapping time windows to get a finer view than using the entire blocks at once, and used it as predictors in a logistic regression predicting the focus condition. We used 80% of data for training and 20% for testing and repeated the process of selecting the training and test sets 30 times to ensure the reliability of results.

Results

The evolution of features for Addition and Stroop tasks are visible in the figure.

Stroop task: The two-way ANOVA for ISCR evidenced a significant effect of condition (F(1,90)=53.54, p<0.0001) and order (F(2,90)=7.854, p=0.0007), and a significant interaction between them (F(2,90)=7.798, p<0.0001): ISCR decreases across blocks and during the rest condition. For PD, the ANOVA also revealed the same significant effect for condition (F(1,96)=119.1, p<0.0001) and order (F(2,96)=165.5, p<0.0001), and a significant interaction (F(2,96)=3.765, p = 0.0267). We also observed an order effect (F(2,96)=3.550, p=0.0326) for gaze divergence, which increases, and a significant difference between conditions for blinks number (F(2.96)=19.265, p<0.0001) and duration (F(2.96)=18.575, p<0.0001), which are more and longer during rest. We understand that the decrease of PD and ISCR, both of them linked with arousal, shows a habituation effect, while the increase of gaze divergence across blocks demonstrates a gain of expertise, as a strategy for this task consists of blurring the visual attention to the upper word, to focus only on the word bellow. This is confirmed with the one-way ANOVA conducted including performance as the dependent variable and order as the independent factor, which showed a significant effect for the order (F(2,48)=6.041, p=0.0046): performance increases across blocks. The decrease of physiological signals combined with the increase of performance supports a habituation effect coupled with a gain of expertise.

Addition task: The two-way ANOVA with ISCR evidenced a significant effect for condition (F(1,90)=70.30, p<0.0001) and for order (F(2,90)=5.251, p=0.0070) but no significant interaction. As for the Stroop task, in the Addition task, ISCR decreases during rest compare to task blocks, but here it increases across blocks. For PD, the ANOVA showed the same significant effect for condition (F(1,96)=226.62, p<0.0001) and order (F(2,96)=5.321, p=0.0064). When considering gaze divergence (F(1,96)=4.174, p=0.0438), blinks number (F(1,96)=14.05, p=0.0003) and duration (F(1,78)=85.978, p<0.0001), we observed only an effect of condition, with gaze divergence being smaller, and blinks being more and longer for the rest. Contraty to what we observed in the Stroop task, PD and ISCR significantly increased across blocks. We understand that the difficulty level manipulation prevented the habituation effect and kept the arousal at an even higher level. Thus, both PD and ISCR may be indicators of cognitive load. Here again, the one-way ANOVA conducted including performance as the dependent variable and order as the independent factor confirms the previous observations. It evidenced a significant effect for order (F(2,48)=40.27, p<0.0001): performance decreases across blocks. The cognitive load required to cope with the task progressively increased through its execution, which prevented the emergence of a habituation effect and stopped participants from developing task expertise.

Focus task: The logistic regression aimed to characterize the difference between internal focus and mind-wandering using all the previously defined features (ISCR, PD, fixation duration, gaze divergence, and blink number and duration). We averaged the metrics got on the 30 different trainings, and it was significant in predicting the condition every time with a mean x2(6)=42.13 and p<0.0001 and a mean accuracy of 63.23% on the test set. Among all the features of interest, PD was the only significant feature (p<0.0001): it seems to be the best physiological signal that reflects the cognitive load of participants. The other features included in the logistic regression model were not able to significantly contribute to the classification, even if condition had a significant effect on most of them on the other tasks. We can hypothesize that the condition has a significant effect on these features only if the cognitive task requires an external action.

Conclusion

Our study examined how physiological markers of engagement fluctuate during cognitive tasks and how they relate to cognitive load and performance. Pupil diameter (PD) and phasic skin conductance responses (ISCR) reliably distinguished task from rest in both Stroop and Addition tasks, confirming their sensitivity to arousal. Blink features and gaze divergence showed weaker but consistent effects. Importantly, the two tasks revealed distinct evolution of features depending on the tasks demands. In the Stroop task, PD and ISCR decreased while performance improved, indicating habituation and reduced cognitive load as participants gained expertise. In contrast, during the Addition task, PD and ISCR remained stable or increased as performance declined, reflecting sustained mental effort driven by rising task difficulty.

Moreover, PD further proved to be a robust indicator of attentional focus, allowing logistic regression models to differentiate internal focus from mind-wandering. These results highlight the value of PD as a non-invasive, real-time measure of attention allocation.

Beyond characterizing the physiological bases of cognitive load, our findings support the development of predictive models that identify task engagement or disengagement. Such models could guide adaptive training for neurofeedback systems, dynamically adjusting task difficulty to maintain optimal arousal and prevent attentional lapses. Because these markers are independent of neural measures, they can be integrated with EEG, fMRI, fNIRS or MEG to enhance personalization and performance monitoring.

Overall, PD and ISCR emerge as sensitive, task-unspecific indicators of engagement. Their integration into adaptive frameworks offers promising outcomes for improving cognitive training and neurofeedback efficacy.
