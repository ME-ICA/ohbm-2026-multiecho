Introduction

The human brain contains a vast network of axonal connections. Despite their prevalence, clinical neuroimaging is largely insensitive to detect abnormalities in the human white matter, underscoring the need for dedicated tools. In this context, advanced diffusion MRI (dMRI)-based quantitative tractography has emerged as a particularly well-suited approach1, allowing to comprehensively probe the density of white matter connections across an individual’s brain. However, the reliable identification of pathological connections critically depends on the comparison of these measurements against a normative reference, which is currently undermined by the substantial reconstruction variance inherent to tractography executed on individual session data2. This motivates the development of more reliable methods specifically tailored to the problem of white matter pathology detection, which has been recently recognised as one of the grand challenges to overcome for the field of tractography3. In this work, we extend the previously introduced differential quantitative tractography framework for the robust quantification of longitudinal structural connectivity differences2 to the domain of normative modelling, allowing inference of abnormal brain structural connectivity in individuals.

Methods

The key innovation of the previously proposed differential quantitative tractography framework is the optimisation of connection density within a subject tractogram that is common across sessions to match local (i.e. fixel-wise) fibre density differences between sessions, thereby robustly quantifying connectivity differences without the need for session-wise tractography3. While originally presented as a longitudinal analysis method, much of this workflow naturally extends to normative analyses, where local fibre density differences projected onto a tractogram encode the difference of an individual to some normative expectation (see Fig 1A). 

The core challenge that needs to be overcome in the context of such analysis is the reliable generation of a “healthy digital twin”, an individualised fibre density reference. To overcome this challenge, we extend recent advances in spectral normative modelling4 to the metric space of fixels: first, we establish fixel-wise correspondence across subjects through mapping of fibre densities onto a common grid within a cohort-average template, and perform quantitative streamline tractography on this template to compute a fixel-fixel connectivity matrix, as per the Fixel-Based Analysis pipeline5. Spectral eigenmodes (here termed "fixelmodes") were obtained via eigendecomposition of the random-walk graph Laplacian derived from this connectivity matrix. For each subject, we project their fixel-wise fibre density data onto this spectral basis, truncation of which allows approximation of the core features of those data through a smaller set of spectral weights (see Fig 1B). The projection of individual fibre densities into this common, anatomically meaningful, but lower-dimensional latent space makes it computationally feasible to train a single normative model based on spectral weights, learning relationships between individual attributes (e.g., sex, age, etc.) and fibre densities across the healthy population. For a given subject, the spectral model can then be queried based on the attributes of that individual, yielding an individualised “healthy digital twin” prediction of fixel-wise fibre densities across the entire brain (see Fig 1C). This reference is used in conjunction with empirical fibre density estimates as input for normative differential quantitative streamline tractography, identifying white matter connections with density deviated from normative expectation (see Fig 1D). 

We trained a spectral normative model (Hierarchical Bayesian Regression, 5000 fixelmodes) on a set of 205 healthy controls scanned across two scanners for the Australian Epilepsy Project, with the model accounting for sex, age, scanner effects, intracranial volume and cross-mode dependencies. We selected from that same project three exemplar clinical cases with known lesions: 1) left hippocampal sclerosis, 2) right hippocampal sclerosis and suspected bottom-of-sulcus-dysplasia in the right superior temporal gyrus, and 3) patient that previously underwent resection of a dysembryoplastic neuroepithelial tumour in the left anterior temporal lobe. The anatomical plausibility of the estimated connectivity differences from normative predictions were then evaluated with respect to known lesion locations.

Results

In the patient with left hippocampal sclerosis, marked connectivity decreases were found in several well-established white matter bundles, but were particularly pronounced in the left inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus, left arcuate fasciculus, and forceps minor (Fig 2A). In the patient with right hippocampal sclerosis and suspected bottom-of-sulcus dysplasia, connectivity decreases were predominantly within short association fibres of the right temporal lobe, with additional decreases observed within bilateral motor system pathways (Fig 2B). In the epilepsy case that underwent resection of the left anterior temporal lobe, we found connectivity decreases in multiple left-hemispheric white matter bundles that were partially resected, including the inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus, arcuate fasciculus, anterior commissure, and optic radiation (Fig 2C).

Conclusion

Normative differential quantitative tractography reliably identified abnormal structural connections in close proximity to known lesional pathologies. Together, these methodological advances provide a robust and scalable framework for individualized dMRI analysis that is data-driven, whole-brain, and quantifies interpretable changes in tract connectivity rather than abstract local image features, enabling the reliable detection of disease-specific white matter abnormalities at the single-subject level. Future work will extend this approach to connectome level statistical inference, establishing a technological foundation for the systematic evaluation of diagnostic and prognostic white matter biomarkers in the Australian Epilepsy Project and beyond.
