Introduction

Minor visual hallucinations (mVH) are frequently reported in Parkinson’s disease (PD) and can precede the development of more complex hallucinations and cognitive decline. Despite extensive research on cortical dysfunction in PD, the contribution of subcortical structures - especially the thalamus - to hallucinatory phenomena remains poorly defined. The thalamus contains multiple nuclei with distinct cortical projections that support sensory integration, attention, motor preparation, and higher-order cognitive processes that can influence perception.1 Prior work has shown that PD patients with hallucinations exhibit abnormal functional interactions within attentional and visual networks,2 but it is unknown whether these abnormalities are linked to dysfunctions in specific thalamic nuclei. Since the thalamus acts as a hub regulating information flow to the cortex, disruptions across its subdivisions could affect the coordination of visuospatial and perceptual processing. Understanding the organization of thalamo-cortical disturbances associated with mVH would provide insight into early vulnerability mechanisms and help identify functional biomarkers for patients at risk of progression. Using a combination of structural Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) and resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) data, we examined nucleus-specific functional and effective connectivity alterations between patients with (PD-mVH+) and without (PD-mVH−) hallucinations, assessed the magnitude and anatomical distribution of functional differences, and evaluated whether demographic, cognitive, or volumetric factors accounted for the observed effects. Moreover, to better characterize thalamo-cortical communication, we also examined directed interactions between specific thalamic nuclei and cortical regions using effective connectivity modeling.

Methods

Data were drawn from 191 PD individuals enrolled in the Parkinson’s Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI) study. Based on structured clinical interviews, 17 participants reported minor visual hallucinations (PD-mVH+), whereas 174 did not (PD-mVH−). All subjects completed resting-state fMRI and structural MRI acquired with harmonized 3T protocols across PPMI sites, including whole-brain T2* echo-planar imaging (40 contiguous slices, 3.5 mm thickness, matrix size =64 × 64, repetition time (TR) =2500 ms, echo time (TE) =30 ms, flip angle=80°) and high-resolution T1-weighted (slice thickness of 1.0 mm, 192 slices per volume, and an in-plane resolution of 1.0 × 1.0 mm²) scans. Thalamic segmentation was performed on anatomical images using a probabilistic atlas incorporating ex vivo MRI and histological priors,3 producing individualized parcellations. To improve interpretability within the spatial resolution of rs-fMRI, nuclei were grouped into eight larger functional divisions: mediodorsal (MD), ventral anterior (VA), ventrolateral anterior (VLa), ventrolateral posterior (VLp), ventral posterior (VP), intralaminar/midline (IT), medial pulvinar (PuM), and sensory pulvinar (PuSe) clusters. Functional preprocessing included ICA-AROMA–based removal of motion components, realignment, slice-timing adjustment, and normalization. Blood-Oxygenation-Level-Dependent (BOLD) data were sampled onto each subject’s native cortical surface and then registered to the fsaverage template for group analyses. Runs were concatenated after excluding the first four volumes of each to ensure signal stabilization. Physiological noise was attenuated by regressing out cerebrospinal fluid activity and the first five white-matter principal components. Data were band-pass filtered (0.01–0.1 Hz), and frames with framewise displacement ≥ 0.5 mm were censored. No additional surface smoothing was applied. For first-level analyses, time series were extracted from each thalamic subdivision and correlated with all cortical vertices to generate seed-based connectivity maps; resulting coefficients were transformed into Fisher z-scores. Group differences were assessed using a general linear model with vertex-wise permutation testing and cluster-level family-wise-error correction. The Yeo 7-network parcellation guided functional localization, while anatomical labels were assigned using the Desikan–Killiany atlas. To infer whether mVHs affected the intrinsic connectivity between regions in PD patients, we used spectral Dynamic Causal Modelling (sDCM), a framework for assessing the direction and the strength of the signal from one region to another, i.e. the effective connectivity. We built and inverted a DCM model for each thalamic nucleus, basing A-matrix constraints on seed-based connectivity results. At the group level, we performed a Parametric Empirical Bayes (PEB)4 analysis to separately model the average connectivity across patients with and without visual hallucinations. We used a posterior probability criterion of 95% for each group-level parameter estimate. Finally, to evaluate whether resting-state functional-connectivity (rs-FC) alterations aligned with cortical microstructural or neurochemical organization, we quantified the spatial correspondence between group-level connectivity differences, cortical thickness maps, and PET-derived neurotransmitter receptor density distributions.5 BrainSMASH technique was applied using the neuromaps toolbox. Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons across the 19 receptor maps, setting the significance threshold at p < 0.0026 (0.05/19).

Results

PD-mVH+ individuals showed widespread, nucleus-specific reductions in thalamo-cortical FC compared with PD-mVH− participants (cluster-forming threshold p < 0.001; cluster significance p < 0.05). Connectivity reductions were even more extensive for the ventral thalamic nuclei. The VA and VL complexes displayed large clusters of diminished coupling spanning middle and superior frontal areas, precentral cortex, and inferior parietal regions. These alterations overlapped with attentional and visuospatial control networks, suggesting that disrupted thalamic gating of perceptual information contributes to hallucination vulnerability. The ventral posterior nucleus also showed reduced connectivity with occipital, parietal, and inferotemporal cortices, indicative of impaired relay of sensory signals across dorsal and ventral visual pathways. Effect sizes across the functional changes of VA/VL nuclei ranged from Cohen’s d=0.30 to 0.50. No increases in connectivity were observed in PD-mVH+, and no volumetric differences were detected across nuclei, indicating a functional rather than structural origin for these disturbances. The VP nucleus showed increased connectivity with regions spanning the DMN, somatomotor, and visual networks, reflecting broad sensory integration deficits associated with mVH. Effect sizes across the functional changes of VP complex ranged from Cohen’s d = 0.30 to 0.45. The MD nucleus exhibited diminished coupling with the right inferior parietal cortex, with medium effect sizes (0.3 < Cohen’s d < 0.4). Pulvinar subdivision showed reduced interactions with fusiform, inferior temporal, supramarginal, and post-central cortices, consistent with weakened integration between thalamic perceptual hubs and cortical regions supporting visual recognition and sensorimotor updating (0.3 < Cohen’s d < 0.4). Furthermore, sDCM demonstrated that the directional architecture of thalamo-cortical interactions diverged sharply between groups. In PD-mVH− individuals, the VA nucleus exerted distributed inhibitory influences across multiple cortical targets, which in turn provided balanced excitatory feedback, forming a stable regulatory loop. In contrast, PD-mVH+ participants showed a markedly restricted inhibitory output from VA, limited to only a subset of cortical areas, with the loss of modulatory projections to the remaining nodes. Excitatory bidirectional coupling among specific associative cortical regions was preserved, suggesting intact intrinsic cortical drive despite weakened thalamic regulation. This combination - attenuated thalamic inhibition and preserved cortical excitability - supports the interpretation that hallucination vulnerability arises from impaired thalamic gating rather than from increased cortical synchrony alone. To determine whether these functional alterations aligned with broader cortical molecular gradients, we evaluated the spatial correspondence between rs-FC effects and PET-derived receptor maps. Significant alignment emerged in the right hemisphere between hallucination-related rs-FC reductions and regional densities of mGluR5, M1, and 5-HT6 receptors (Figure 1). Strong spatial correspondence was observed for MD–mGluR5 (SA-corrected p < 0.001; R = 0.50), with similar relationships present for ventral nuclei: VA (SA-corrected p < 0.001; R= 0.40), VLa (SA-corrected p < 0.001; R = 0.35), and VLp (SA-corrected p < 0.001; R = 0.30). VLa alterations also aligned with cortical M1 receptor density (SA-corrected p = 0.001; R = 0.30) and 5-HT6 expression (SA-corrected p < 0.001; R = 0.35). These findings indicate that the pattern of thalamo-cortical dysfunction in PD-mVH+ patients is shaped in part by underlying neurochemical architecture, particularly gradients of glutamatergic, cholinergic, and serotonergic signaling. Network-based analyses did not identify any significant spatial overlap between regions exhibiting altered thalamocortical connectivity and areas of cortical structural change.

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that mVHs in PD reflect a coordinated disruption of thalamo-cortical communication. Multiple thalamic subdivisions - including MD, ventral, and pulvinar territories - displayed reduced coupling with cortical regions involved in attention, visuospatial evaluation, visual association processing, and sensorimotor integration. This broad distribution suggests that hallucination vulnerability emerges from weakened integration across parallel thalamic pathways that normally support the continuous updating of perceptual information. The effective connectivity analyses provided additional insight into the mechanisms underlying these disturbances. Altered directional influences between thalamic nuclei and higher-order cortical areas point toward a breakdown in the flow of predictive and sensory signals within recurrent cortico-thalamic loops. This pattern is consistent with theoretical accounts proposing that hallucinations arise when internally generated expectations are insufficiently constrained by incoming sensory evidence. This imbalance may originate from impaired thalamic gating. A second line of evidence came from the spatial correspondence analyses: regions showing greater thalamo-cortical dysfunction also aligned with cortical gradients of mGluR5, M1, and 5-HT6 receptor expression. This convergence suggests that the distribution of glutamatergic, cholinergic, and serotonergic systems modulates cortical susceptibility to the observed connectivity disturbances. In other words, molecular architecture appears to shape the location of hallucination-related functional changes, providing a link between network physiology and neurochemical organization. Importantly, these effects were not explained by structural differences in thalamic volume or demographic and cognitive characteristics, indicating that the abnormalities detected here reflect early functional dysregulation rather than degeneration or global cognitive decline. Taken together, these results posit the thalamus as a key locus in the emergence of minor hallucinations and highlight the relevance of integrating functional, effective, and molecular connectivity to understand perceptual disturbances in PD. Such multimodal signatures may help identify individuals at risk for symptomatic progression and inform future interventions aimed at restoring stability within thalamo-cortical networks.
