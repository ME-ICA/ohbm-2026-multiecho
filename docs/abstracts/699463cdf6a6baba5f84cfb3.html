Introduction

Language comprehension is one of the most fundamental human behaviors, allowing for the communication of thoughts and engagement with the world. However, the processing of natural language in real-world settings, such as listening to stories, is highly complex, multidimensional, and context-dependent. Recent advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) offer a powerful, unified computational framework for modeling natural language processing. These models represent language via multidimensional contextual embeddings, capturing context-rich linguistic content.  

Recent research has shown that these LLM embeddings align remarkably well with measured human neural activity during natural language comprehension.1 However, most existing studies primarily focus on predicting brain responses using specific embeddings, while paying relatively little attention to the properties of the embedding models themselves—that is, what kinds of representational features make some models more neurally aligned than others. Moreover, it remains unclear to what extent prediction performance is driven by stimulus-specific characteristics of the narrative (i.e. the transcript) versus by individual differences among listeners, and whether these distinct sources of variance manifest differently across the cortex.

This study utilizes the encoding model framework with publicly available “narrative” fMRI datasets;2 we calculated predicted BOLD signals using a regression model3 based on five distinct linguistic embeddings extracted from the story transcripts. The primary goal was to evaluate the capability of these models to capture linguistic features in neural responses and to assess their sensitivity to narrative content and idiosyncratic activity patterns. 

Methods

We used publicly available narrative fMRI dataset2 (OpenNeuro :https://openneuro.org/datasets/ds002345/versions/1.1.4). Two stories from the dataset were chosen: "Pie Man (PNI)" (piemanpni), and "Running from the Bronx" (bronx) with identical MRI acquisition parameters and participants. The preprocessed clean data from non-smoothed derivatives were used. The details of the preprocessing can be found in the original work,2 briefly, fMRIPrep (v.20.0.5) was run on the functional data and additional temporal filtering was done using the confounding variables. 

For computational efficiency, we parcellated the data using Schaefer4 400 parcels in calculating the connectivity matrices for each participant. Narrative transcripts were annotated with onset times, which were used to convert them in TRs (TR were 1.5s). Six language embedding models were used to get the embedded vectors from the story transcripts - the models were: (1) Word2Vec (w2v: https://huggingface.co/fse/word2vec-google-news-300), (2) GPT-2(gpt2: https://huggingface.co/openai-community/gpt2), (3) E5-large(e5: https://huggingface.co/intfloat/e5-large), (4) Mistral(https://huggingface.co/mistralai/Mistral-7B-v0.1), (5) LLaMA-3(llama3: https://huggingface.co/meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct), and (6) GPT-OSS 20B(gpt-oss: https://huggingface.co/openai/gpt-oss-20b). 

Models were loaded utilizing HuggingFace Transformer (https://github.com/huggingface/transformers), for each unique words in the transcript, embeddings were obtained from the final hidden layer by averaging token-wise hidden states, a zero vector was substituted when the processing failed. The final embeddings were mapped back to the narrative transcripts and averaged within TRs to generate predictor matrices. These matrices were then inputted along with the participant’s BOLD signal in training the BOLD signal prediction model. We used Banded Ridge Regression3 for this prediction, which extends classical ridge regression by assigning an independent regularization parameter to each feature space, enabling differential weighting of embedding models with distinct dimensionality, variance, and predictive strength. The regression model was implemented using the himalaya library (https://gallantlab.org/himalaya/),  this library provides GPU-accelerated solvers and efficient multifeature ridge estimators suitable for large-scale fMRI encoding models. 

The methodological pipeline is illustrated in Figure 1(Method Figure). Finally, for each parcel in each participant, we computed five correlations: (1) ISC:  inter-subject-correlation (as a baseline measure), (2) pred vs actual (SS): model prediction trained with the same story vs. individual actual BOLD signal, (3) pred vs group (SS): model prediction trained with the same story vs. group averaged BOLD signals, (4) pred vs actual (CS): model prediction trained with the other story (cross-story condition) vs. individual actual BOLD signal, and (5) pred vs group (CS):  model prediction trained with the other story (cross-story condition) vs. group averaged BOLD signals. The correlation was plotted on the brain surface (Figure 2A: Results Figure) and Figure 2B shows the distribution of parcel-wise Pearson’s correlation.

Results

We first computed the Inter-subject correlation (ISC) to confirm its consistency with the original work2 and plotted with boxplot (Figure 2B) as a baseline to measure the model efficacy in BOLD signal prediction. Figure 2A shows Pearson's correlation r plotted on 41k_fsavg surface-space and parcellation with the Schaefer 400 obtained while using "Pie Man (PNI)" as the training data. The red line boundary indicates the Yeo’s Default Mode Network (DMN)5 and the blue line indicates the Early Auditory cortex (EAC). Across all language models and evaluating conditions, EAC exhibited the highest correlation, demonstrating a robust correspondence between model-based predictions and auditory cortical responses. 

In the individual prediction vs. actual signal conditions, positive correlation was observed primarily in the EAC and no significant differences were observed between the same-story (SS) vs. the cross-story (CS) conditions suggesting that model predictions primarily captured the uniform stimuli-driven auditory processing in EAC unrelated to the narrative content. However, for prediction in group average, parcels outside of EAC extending to DMN ROIs  showed significant positive correlations.  Although a similar pattern in EAC was observed in both SS and CS conditions, the extent of DMN parcels’ inclusion varied across models. In particular, gpt-oss, llama3, and mistral showed less spatial spread into the DMN in the CS condition compared to SS, whereas gpt2 and e5 maintained largely similar spatial correlation patterns across story conditions. 

Whole brain parcel-wise correlation for this piemanpni with all models, conditions and ISC are summarized in Figure 2B right. Figure 2B left shows the summary distribution for bronx trained predictions. Both stories have similar patterns for models performance across the conditions, however piemanpni story shows higher variability in all models and conditions compared to bronx. The length of the narratives - bronx (06:40) and piemanpni (08:56) - may have contributed to this difference in variance. Across models and stories, w2v consistently showed low correlations, whereas gpt2 and e5 consistently performed better than other models. 

Conclusion

This study examined how word-level embeddings from multiple language models predict parcel-level BOLD responses during naturalistic story listening across different prediction conditions and narratives. Across models, robust prediction performance was observed in the early auditory cortex, largely independent of narrative content, indicating sensitivity to shared stimulus-driven auditory processing. In contrast, group-averaged predictions extended into the default mode network, with model- and story-dependent variability suggesting sensitivity to higher-level narrative representations.

Consistent with prior work, inter-subject correlation exceeded model-based prediction performance, highlighting that shared neural responses during story comprehension remain stronger than those captured by current word-level embeddings. Among models, Word2Vec performed poorest, while transformer-based models, particularly GPT-2 and e5 showed more reliable predictive performance.

Overall, these results indicate that language model embeddings capture meaningful but limited aspects of neural responses to narratives, with performance influenced by both narrative properties and model architecture.
