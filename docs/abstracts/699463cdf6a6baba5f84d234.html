Introduction

A defining feature of subjective experience is the continuous stream of thoughts that occupies our mental lives. These thoughts may focus on the immediate environment but often involve memories, plans, or imagined future scenarios1. Although the content of such thoughts can be potentially limitless, research shows that people repeatedly engage in a relatively small set of dominant thought patterns2. These patterns have been linked both to affective traits and large-scale neural dynamics observed during rest3. What remains less clear is how individual differences in affective traits interact with moment-to-moment brain dynamics, particularly under different emotional contexts, and how such dynamics relate to ongoing patterns of thought.
Here, we integrate experience sampling, emotional context manipulation, and hidden Markov modelling to test how large-scale neural state transitions vary across individuals, and how these dynamics reflect people’s characteristic patterns of ongoing thought.

Methods

We acquired resting state functional MRI from 49 participants. Each participant completed three 5-minute resting state scans: baseline scan before viewing any content, and two additional scans collected immediately after viewing 10-minutes positive and negative emotional video clips. After each scan, participants reported their ongoing thoughts using 19 multidimensional experience-sampling items and rated their current affective state. Following the scan session, participants completed a 2-week experience-sampling protocol (five prompts/day) in which they answered the same thought-sampling questions in their everyday lives. Principal component analysis revealed five stable thought patterns. Pre-processed resting-state data were parcellated using the Yeo-17 cortical network atlas, and HMM was applied to the concatenated time series to identify recurrent neural states. Each state was modelled as a multivariate Gaussian distribution parameterised by a mean activity pattern and a full covariance structure capturing network-wide functional interactions. For each state, we derived fractional occupancy (FO; the proportion of time spent in a state), switching rate, transition probabilities, and mean dwell times. These metrics were used to examine how large-scale neural dynamics relate to patterns of ongoing thought reported during the scan and in everyday life. State spatial maps were decoded using Neurosynth4 to aid functional interpretation. We then assessed how each state was embedded within macroscale cortical functional hierarchies by correlating state maps with the first three cortical gradients from 5. The position of each state in this gradient space was quantified as its Euclidean distance from the origin. To assess whether these distances were greater than expected by chance, we generated 1000 synthetic states by randomly permuting the weights of the 17 Yeo networks and computed their gradient distances, allowing a non-parametric significance test of the real states’ embedding in cortical hierarchy.

Results

The HMM revealed five recurrent large-scale neural states corresponding to canonical systems, including an auditory state (S1), a sensorimotor state (S2), a default mode–like state (S3), a salience-related state (S4), and a visual state (S5) (Fig. 1A). Mapping these states into brain’s macroscale cortical gradients showed that S2, S3, and S5 occupied positions significantly farther from the gradient-space origin than expected under 1000 network-permutation nulls (all p < 0.01), indicating that these states align strongly known cortical hierarchies.

Emotional context modulated overall neural dynamics, with reduced global switching rate and decreased fractional occupancy of both the DMN-like (Fig. 1B, left), and salience-like states after watching positive content relative to baseline rest. Neural state dynamics were systematically related to ongoing thought in the scanner. Greater fractional occupancy of the DMN-like state (S3) predicted lower levels of on-task thinking (Component 2), most strongly during baseline and positive rest (Fig. 1B, middle). Negative intrusive thoughts (Component 1) during negative rest (relative to baseline) predicted increased transition probability from the sensorimotor state (S2) into the DMN-like state (S3) (Fig. 1B, right). Daydreaming-related thought (Component 5) also showed a positive association with state 2 to state 3 transitions, suggesting that movement toward DMN-like processing reflects multiple dimensions of internally oriented cognition.

Thought patterns in daily life also related to stable neural-dynamic signatures observed in the scanner. Participants with higher median negative intrusive thinking across the two weeks showed longer dwell times in S2 and reduced transitions between visual (S5) and auditory (S1) states. Fractional occupancy tracked trait-like tendencies as well: greater FO of the auditory state (S1) predicted lower daily-life intrusive thinking, whereas greater FO of the sensorimotor state (S2) predicted higher intrusive thinking.

Conclusion

Large-scale neural dynamics and patterns of ongoing thought are systematically linked. Neural states that most strongly reflected the brain’s intrinsic cortical hierarchy, particularly the sensorimotor and DMN-like states, were also those most predictive of individuals’ thought patterns. Positive emotional context reduced fractional occupancy of the DMN-like state, consistent with a shift away from internally oriented processing, and individuals who spent more time in this state showed reduced on-task thinking. Conversely, negative intrusive thoughts predicted stronger transitions from the sensorimotor state into the DMN-like state, indicating that movement toward this internally focused state supports the emergence of intrusive negative content. Together, these findings reveal that the organisation of ongoing thought mirrors the hierarchical structure of large-scale brain dynamics and provide a foundation for understanding how internal cognition emerges from coordinated transitions across intrinsic neural states.
