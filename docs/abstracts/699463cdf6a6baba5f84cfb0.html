Introduction

The brain is organized across multiple spatial scales. Genomic gradients shape the spatial arrangement of cell types and laminar differentiation, which in turn determine the wiring of neural circuits and the emergence of patterned neural activity. This complex system is perpetually active, requiring constant exchange of fluids to deliver resources and clear waste products. While vascular supply and metabolism are relatively well studied, the complementary clearance pathway—known as the glymphatic system—is less well understood at the whole-brain level.1

A central component of the glymphatic system is the aquaporin-4 (AQP4) water channel. AQP4 is concentrated on astrocyte endfeet that line perivascular spaces, where it supports the exchange of cerebrospinal and interstitial fluid. Through this pathway, cerebrospinal fluid enters along arteries, moves through the parenchyma, and exits along veins and perineural routes, carrying metabolites and other solutes out of the brain. Despite this key role, the macroscale organization of AQP4 in the human brain remains unknown.

The spatial arrangement of AQP4 is an important question because AQP4 dysfunction has been linked to neurodegenerative disease and brain edema. Mislocalization of AQP4 away from perivascular endfeet slows glymphatic clearance, which can promote the accumulation of misfolded proteins. In parallel, impaired AQP4-mediated water transport can disturb fluid homeostasis and contribute to vasogenic edema, as excess interstitial fluid is not efficiently cleared from tissue.

Here we study the whole-brain organization of AQP4. We first map the expression of the AQP4 gene in cortex and subcortex. Looking at healthy brain function, we ask how the spatial distribution of AQP4 relates to regional differences in normative blood perfusion. Looking at dysfunction, we ask how the spatial distribution of AQP4 relates to regional differences in brain edema and vulnerability to neurodegenerative disease.

Methods

A: AQP4 gene expression was obtained from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (n=6, 1 female, ages 24–57).2 Microarray probes were filtered based on intensity and correlation to RNA-seq data. Tissue samples were assigned to 454 brain regions by combining the Schaefer 400 cortical parcellation with a Melbourne subcortical atlas. Expression values were normalized using robust sigmoid and min–max scaling, then averaged across donors to yield a single whole-brain AQP4 expression map for cortex and subcortex.

B: Normative cerebral blood perfusion was derived from pseudo-continuous arterial spin labeling MRI in the Human Connectome Project Lifespan studies (Development and Aging; 1305 healthy participants).3 Quantitative perfusion maps were preprocessed using the HCP ASL pipeline and summarized into a single perfusion score map. This map was parcellated to the Schaefer–Melbourne atlas, yielding regional perfusion estimates that were z-scored across parcels. We correlated regional perfusion with AQP4 expression.

C: We correlated AQP4 expression with neurodegenerative atrophy patterns. Disease atrophy maps were obtained from a pathologically confirmed dementia cohort including 186 patients and 73 healthy controls.4 Patients were grouped into eight diseases: early-onset AD (n=68), late-onset AD (n=29), presenilin-1 mutation carriers (n=10), dementia with Lewy bodies (n=25), 3Rtau (n=11), 4Rtau (n=17), TDP-43 type A (n=12), and TDP-43 type C (n=14). Voxel-based morphometry was used to compute group-level patient–control t-maps for each syndrome, adjusting for age, sex, intracranial volume, field strength, and site. These t-maps were registered to MNI space and parcellated to yield regional atrophy scores aligned with the AQP4 and perfusion maps.

D: Tumor-associated edema was quantified using pre-operative MRI from two diffuse glioma cohorts with expert segmentations of peritumoral edema (total n=1094). For each individual, T1 and FLAIR images with edema masks were nonlinearly registered to MNI space. Masks were then stacked across individuals to derive a voxel-wise edema frequency map, indexing the proportion of patients with edema at each voxel. This map was parcellated to the Schaefer–Melbourne atlas, and regional edema probability was correlated with AQP4 expression.

E: Structural connectivity and shortest-path distances were derived from diffusion-weighted MRI in 1065 participants from the HCP S1200 release. Data were preprocessed using the HCP Minimal Preprocessing Pipelines, and a consensus structural connectome was constructed using probabilistic tractography implemented in probtrackx2, yielding a weighted, undirected connectivity matrix between all parcels. Connection lengths were defined as the reciprocal of streamline density, and weighted shortest paths between all region pairs were computed using the Floyd–Warshall algorithm. We defined “high-atrophy” and “high-edema” parcels as those in the top 10% of atrophy or edema scores, respectively, and “AQP4 hotspots” as parcels in the top 10% of AQP4 expression. For each dementia disease and for the edema map, we calculated Euclidean and shortest-path distances from high-atrophy or high-edema parcels to their nearest AQP4 hotspot and averaged these distances across vulnerable parcels.

F: All maps exhibited strong spatial autocorrelation. To obtain spatially valid significance estimates, we used Moran spectral randomization to generate 10,000 surrogate maps with matched spatial smoothness for each analysis.5 For pairwise correlations, one map was held fixed while the other was replaced with surrogates to build a null distribution of correlation coefficients. For distance analyses, we randomized the AQP4 map, redefined hotspots for each surrogate, recomputed mean Euclidean and shortest-path distances, and compared observed values to the null distributions to obtain p-values that respect spatial autocorrelation.

Results

We show that:

A: AQP4 expression is highly organized across the brain. We find greater AQP4 expression near the ventricles and in subcortex. In subcortex, medial and lateral amygdala, nucleus accumbens, globus pallidus, striatal territories of caudate and putamen, and ventral thalamic nuclei show high expression, whereas hippocampal body/tail and lateral thalamus are relatively low. In cortex, AQP4 is enriched in limbic and transmodal regions, including cingulate cortex, insula, medial temporal lobe and medial prefrontal cortex, and lowest in unimodal sensory cortices.

B: Regional AQP4 expression is significantly anticorrelated with normative cerebral blood perfusion derived from lifespan ASL data (r = −0.59, p_MSR = 4×10⁻⁴). Highly perfused primary sensorimotor and visual cortices tend to have low AQP4 expression, whereas AQP4-rich limbic and association territories are relatively hypoperfused.

C: Across eight pathologically confirmed dementia diseases, we observe systematic differences in how strongly regional atrophy colocalizes with AQP4 expression. Tauopathies and TDP-43 syndromes with prominent frontotemporal atrophy show the strongest positive correlations, including 3Rtau (r = 0.60, p_MSR = 0.032), 4Rtau (r = 0.56, p_MSR = 0.004), TDP-43A (r = 0.44, p_MSR = 0.021) and TDP-43C (r = 0.54, p_MSR = 0.008). More modest but significant associations are observed for dementia with Lewy bodies (r = 0.26, p_MSR = 0.005) and late-onset Alzheimer’s disease (r = 0.25, p_MSR = 0.006), whereas early-onset Alzheimer’s disease and presenilin-1 mutation carriers show weak, non-significant correlations (|r| < 0.1, p_MSR > 0.3).

D: Beyond correlations, we test whether regions that are most affected by disease lie close to AQP4-enriched territories. Defining AQP4 “hotspots” as parcels in the top 10% of AQP4 expression and “high-atrophy” regions as parcels in the top 10% of disease-related volume loss, we compute Euclidean and structural-connectome shortest-path distances from each high-atrophy parcel to its nearest hotspot. Across syndromes, high-atrophy regions tend to lie closer to AQP4 hotspots than expected by Moran spectral nulls, both in geometric and network space, indicating that vulnerable regions are spatially and structurally embedded near AQP4-rich territories.

E: Finally, we examine whether AQP4 expression colocalizes with regions that frequently develop tumor-associated edema. Edema frequency maps derived from diffuse glioma cohorts are parcellated to the same atlas and show that parcels with higher AQP4 expression tend to have higher edema probability. High-edema parcels (top 10%) also lie nearer to AQP4 hotspots in both Euclidean and shortest-path distance than predicted by spatial nulls.

Conclusion

Conclusion: (<4000 characters)
In conclusion, we show that AQP4 channels are not uniformly distributed but form a highly organized periventricular–transmodal axis across the human brain. AQP4 expression is concentrated in periventricular subcortical nuclei and limbic and association cortices, and is lowest in unimodal sensory regions. This pattern is partly distinct from vascular supply: regions with high AQP4 expression tend to be relatively hypoperfused, whereas highly perfused primary sensorimotor and visual cortices show low AQP4 expression. Together, these findings suggest that the glymphatic system follows a macroscale spatial axis that is not simply a reflection of regional blood flow.

We further show that this glymphatic axis is closely aligned with where neurodegenerative tissue loss emerges. Neurodegenerative syndromes with prominent frontotemporal and limbic atrophy show the strongest colocalization with AQP4 expression, whereas disorders with more posterior or diffuse atrophy show weaker or negligible alignment. Beyond simple correlations, regions with the greatest disease-related atrophy and regions with the highest edema probability both lie closer to AQP4 “hotspots” than expected by spatial null models, in terms of both Euclidean distance and shortest-path distance along the structural connectome. These results indicate that vulnerable regions are not only anatomically close to AQP4-rich territories but are also embedded near them in the structural network.

Altogether, our findings suggest that the macroscale organization of AQP4 channels provides a structural and network scaffold that shapes where vascular burden, fluid imbalance and neurodegenerative vulnerability emerge in the human brain. Mapping this glymphatic axis in vivo may help link molecular clearance mechanisms to large-scale network architecture and could eventually inform how we understand risk, progression and potential intervention targets in edema and neurodegenerative disease.
