Introduction

Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) is a non-invasive technique that uses magnetic pulses to induce electrical fields in neural tissue. Concurrent electroencephalography (TMS-EEG) captures TMS-evoked potentials (TEPs)—positive and negative EEG deflections lasting up to 300 ms post-pulse.¹ Varying TMS pulse intensity modulates the activated brain networks.2 A key question is whether the primary motor cortex retains a memory of TMS pulse sequences beyond the immediate prior pulse (n-1, where n is the current pulse). This approach was inspired by the work of Hernández et al.,3 who submitted subjects to a sequence of auditory stimuli while recording their EEG signals. They used a stochastic chain with memory of variable length to model the dependence from the past characterizing the sequence of auditory stimuli. The idea behind that sequence comes from Rissanen,4 who observed that in most sequences of data, each new data unit is chosen considering a sequence of past units whose length is not fixed; it changes as a function of the sequence of past units itself. In Rissanen’s work, each relevant past was called a context, with the set of all contexts represented by a rooted and labeled-oriented tree. This structure, associated with a family of probability measures, determines, for each context, which can be the next element and is called a probabilistic context tree model (PCTM). By modeling the relationship between the presented sequence and the EEG signals, Hernández et al.3 showed that it was possible to retrieve PCTMs from the EEG signals. This is precisely what we intend to do, employing a sequence of TMS pulses in which the TEPs will vary following a context tree. The aim of this study is to investigate whether cortical networks activated by TMS can adapt to a sequence of pulses that follows a probabilistic structure called the context tree model. If so, it will indicate that the brain acquires a memory or adaptation of the sequence of pulses.

Methods

The study was approved by the local ethics committee (CAAE: 85778925.0.0000.5407). We have recruited 1 volunteer (21 years old; right-handed and healthy) so far. Up to twenty right-handed healthy volunteers will be recruited. The TMS pulse sequence is created using a PCTM with a ternary context tree and transition probability matrix (figure 1). The symbols 0, 1, and 2 represent stimulus intensities of 80%, 100%, and 120% of rMT, respectively. The rMT is defined as the minimum stimulation intensity required for MEPs of 50 µV in 5 out of 10 pulses. Single TMS pulses are applied through an 8-shaped coil positioned at the optimal hotspot above left M1 to elicit MEPs from the right abductor pollicis brevis (APB), using a MagProX100 stimulator (MagVenture A/S, Denmark). Coil positioning is guided by a robot (Han's cobot) integrated with InVesalius Navigator software.5 EMG activity is recorded from the APB via surface electrodes placed using a pseudo-monopolar montage. EMG and EEG (32 channels, NeurOne, Bittium Corporation, Finland) are recorded at 20 kHz sampling rate. EEG signals are preprocessed by: TMS artifact removal using baseline data (-5 to 5 ms), filtering (high-pass 1 Hz, low-pass 250 Hz, notch 60 Hz), epoching (-800 to 800 ms), average reference, bad channel/epoch rejection, ICA for ocular artifact removal, final low-pass filtering (45 Hz), and downsampling (725 Hz). EMG signals are filtered (high-pass 20 Hz, low-pass 500 Hz, notch 60 Hz). Peak-to-peak MEP and TEP amplitudes are calculated offline to investigate sequence effects. For TEPs, the amplitude quantification window is between 10 and 60 ms. The goal is to quantify the direct activation caused by TMS, considering that this window is less susceptible to activation not induced by the TMS pulse. In addition, the amplitudes of the TEPs are calculated for electrodes C3, FC1, FC5, CP1, and CP5, as they are located on or around the primary motor cortex. The context tree algorithm is applied to these amplitudes to retrieve the probabilistic structure for each volunteer, based on  Hernández et al.3 approach.

Results

Considering that only one volunteer has been recruited so far, this section aims to describe the behavior of the data in order to validate it. As observed in Figure 2, the components described in the TEPs by the literature were observed. At the CP5 electrode, a marked negative peak was observed at 20 ms and a positive peak at 40 ms. The peak-to-peak amplitude of the segment was 13.81 µV. At CP1, a negative peak was observed at 18 ms and a positive peak at 51 ms, resulting in a peak-to-peak amplitude of 12.95 µV. At FC1, the signal morphology was considered atypical, with a positive peak at 24 ms followed by a negative peak at 43 ms. Considering that N100 did not shift temporally, it is assumed that there was no delay that would justify this change in TEP fluctuations. The peak-to-peak amplitude was 7.56 µV. Similarly, the electrode at FC5 showed a positive peak at 37 ms, followed by a negative peak at 54 ms, but did not show a negative potential, characteristic of N100. The peak-to-peak amplitude obtained was 7.75 µV. Finally, the C3 electrode showed a negative peak at 19 ms, very close to the N15 described in the literature. This was followed by a positive peak at 34 ms, possibly the P30. Adding the peaks together, a peak-to-peak amplitude of 9.92 µV was obtained for this electrode. These results, although preliminary, show the feasibility of the protocol, considering the known limitations in combining TMS-EEG techniques, such as mechanical artifacts from coil movement, electromagnetic artifacts, muscle artifacts, and acoustic and electrical noise.

Conclusion

This protocol proved to be capable of investigating the effects of cortical network adaptation to a sequence of pulses guided by a context tree model. The signal acquisition and processing protocol proved to be efficient in reproducing TEPs as described in the literature. The next step will be to increase the sample size, applying the context algorithm using the peak-to-peak amplitude of the initial TEP response, allowing us to test the hypothesis that cortical networks can adapt to a sequence of pulses structured by a context tree model (PCTM). If this hypothesis is accepted, it means that the stimulated cortical circuits are capable of storing a memory of the sequence of stimuli.
