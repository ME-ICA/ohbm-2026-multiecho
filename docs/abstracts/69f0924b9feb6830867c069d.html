Introduction

Intracortical microstructure profiling was first developed in the 1960’s as a method for appraising cortical microarchitecture. It emerged in a period of scepticism about the reproducibility of foundational neuroanatomical maps, owing to their dependence on visual evaluation (Lashley and Clark, 1946; Bailey and von Bonin, 1951; Braitenberg, 1962). Researchers today are similarly concerned about reproducibility, especially in the context of multi-site studies and biomarker development. Here, we aim to demonstrate that intracortical microstructure profiling presents a promising way forward for objective characterisation of cortical microarchitecture, relevant to understanding differences across regions and individuals.

In recent years, intracortical microstructure profiling has been reinvigorated by parallel advances in histology and neuroimaging. On the one hand, new alignment techniques enabled reconstruction of histological sections into 3D volumes (Amunts et al., 2013; Alkemade et al., 2022), thereby allowing cortical layers to be visualised across the entire human brain regardless of the cutting plane (Novek et al., 2023). On the other hand, sub-millimetre, microstructure-sensitive magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has recently been made feasible, owing to higher magnetic field strengths, acceleration factors and sequencing innovations (Bock et al., 2013; Dinse et al., 2015; Weiskopf et al., 2021).

The present work outlines a standardised workflow for intracortical microstructural profiling, newly packaged as an open-source toolbox “CortPro” (https://github.com/caseypaquola/cortpro). Then, we explore the utility of central moments as descriptors of profile shape. Using these measures, we quantify (i) the extent to which in vivo MRI can capture laminar differentiation, (ii) the test-retest reliability of profiles, and (iii) their replicability across sites and studies.

Methods

We developed a standardized workflow for extracting microstructure profiles (MPs). The procedure involves: (i) reconstructing the cortical surface, (ii) generating equivolumetric intracortical surfaces with matched vertex indices, (iii) registering a microstructure-sensitive image to surface space, (iv) sampling voxel intensities along the matched intracortical surfaces, and (v) compiling these intensity values into profiles that capture depth-wise variations in tissue properties (Figure 1). To support integration with existing workflows and common imaging sequences, we have incorporated several optional preprocessing steps into the CortPro toolbox, including cortical surface reconstruction using FastSurfer1 and computation of a N3 bias-corrected T1w/T2w ratio image2. Therefore, the minimal requirements for initiating the CortPro workflow are a T1w and T2w image.

The shape of each intracortical microstructure profile is characterised by its central moments. The zeroth moment (m0) is the mean intensity and thus disregards variation in intensities across cortical depths. The higher moments are, in contrast, sensitive to profile shape. The centre of gravity (m1) indicates the balance of intensities in supra-vs-infragranular layers, while variance (m2) reflects the spread of intensities across depths. Finally, skewness (m3) and kurtosis (m4) capture intensity shifts in the tails of the profiles (i.e., uppermost and lowermost depths).

Validity as a marker of cortical lamination: While laminar features (e.g. bands of Baillarger) can be observed with ultra-high-resolution MRI, they are obscured with standard 3T sequences. We tested whether microstructure profiles remain sensitive to regional differences in laminar organisation at these lower resolutions. To do so, we downsampled a 0.25mm T1w volume3 to a range of lower resolutions (0.3-1mm in steps of 0.1) and constructed microstructure profiles on the original and downsampled volumes. Then, we correlated the moment maps derived from downsampled volumes with the moment maps from the original volume to evaluate the effect of voxel resolution on regional variability in microstructure profile shapes. 

Test-retest (or scan-rescan) reliability: We compared individual-specific moment maps derived from 2 sessions within one day (n=17)4. The scanning protocol involved both quantitative T1 mapping (longitudinal relaxation time, “R1”) and non-quantitative acquisitions of T1w and T2w contrasts. We calculated the product-moment correlation between moment maps derived from different scanning sessions for each participant. Additionally, we calculated the intraclass correlation coefficient for each region and each moment as a measure of their internal consistency. The procedure was repeated on two additional datasets that involved longer durations between scans [PNI: n = 10, inter-session interval = 1.9 ± 2.5 months5. MICs: n = 40, inter-session interval = 1.71 ± 1.03 years, inter-session similarity = 0.89 ± 0.056].

Replicability: We examined the similarity of moment maps acquired across 21 sites, including 3 different scanner manufacturers [ABCD dataset7]. Given the substantial sample size (n=5046) and the consistency of age distributions across sites, we expected that any systematic differences in moment maps would primarily reflect scanner-related factors. 

Cross-modal consistency: We calculated the product-moment correlation of moment maps derived from different modalities within the same session for a set of participants. For the Shams dataset4, this involved R1 and T1w/T2w in two sessions per individual (n=17), while in MICA-PNI5 this involved R1 and MTsat in one session per individual (n=10). The cross-modal correlations illustrate the generalisability of the approach across acquisition sequences, while also indicating the degree to which different “myelin-sensitive” protocols capture similar patterns of regional variation.

Results

Comparing MPs across areas, individuals or groups is simplified by using their central moments (Figure 2A). The zeroth to second moments capture distinct patterns of microarchitectural variation (spatial correlation between moments: 0.34 < |r| < 0.60, Figure 2A). Therefore, moment maps offer complementary information on cortical differentiation. For imaging data, m3 and m4 tend to be highly correlated with m2 and m1, respectively (|r| > 0.92).

Validity as a marker of cortical lamination: While the profiles are clearly smoother at lower resolutions, the moment maps are strikingly similar even at 1mm resolution (r > 0.9) (Figure 2B). At 0.7mm resolution, the correlations even exceed 0.95. Higher moments are increasingly sensitive to resolution (r1mm m0 = 1.0, m1 = 0.98, m2 = 0.90), which reinforces a selective focus on m0-m2 in in-vivo imaging studies. In sum, microstructure profiling with 0.7 - 1mm resolution imaging can still capture the regional organisation of cortical laminar. 

Test-retest reliability: Moment maps were very highly correlated across sessions for both R1 (r = 0.95 ± 0.04) and T1w/T2w (r = 0.95 ± 0.11) (Figure 2C), with the exception of m0 in T1w/T2w imaging (r = 0.87 ± 0.23). This effect is likely related to B1+ transmit field biases that are known to influence T1w/T2w intensities. Higher moments (m1-m4) sidestep this issue as their computation depends on relative values within an area, thus being relatively resilient to low spatial frequency variations that are characteristic of the B1+ field. Corroborating these results, we also found that the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), which is more sensitive to the discriminability of individuals, ranged from moderate to high across moments and regions, with the exception of low values for m0 in T1w/T2w imaging [R1: ICC(3,1) = 0.70 ± 0.19, T1w/T2w: ICC(3,1) = 0.70 ± 0.30]. We found similarly high levels of reliability in two additional datasets that involved longer durations between scans [PNI: n = 10, inter-session interval = 1.9 ± 2.5 months, inter-session similarity = 0.95 ± 0.04 3. MICs: n = 40, inter-session interval = 1.71 ± 1.03 years, inter-session similarity = 0.89 ± 0.05 4. 

Replicability: Inter-site replicability of moment maps (product-moment correlation across sites) was very high, especially for m2 (r = 0.95 ± 0.03) and m1 (r = 0.90 ± 0.08) (Figure 1D). Replicability was lower for m0 (r = 0.76 ± 0.13), which as above may be attributed to the influence of B1 transmit field bias. Together, these findings highlight the robustness of intracortical microstructure profiling in producing replicable metrics of cortical architecture that generalise across diverse scanner models and acquisition environments.

Cross-modal comparability: At an individual level, cross-modal correlation of T1w/T2w and R1 moment maps was very high for m2 (r = 0.90 ± 0.03) and m1 (r = 0.87 ± 0.03) (Figure 1E). In line with previous analyses, the consistency of m0 was lower (r=0.71±0.10). We found a similar pattern of results with comparison of more distinct myelin-sensitive sequences [R1 and MTsat,3], whereby m2 (r = 0.47 ± 0.12) and m1 (r = 0.40 ± 0.10) were more similar across modalities than m0 (r = 0.16 ± 0.13). Thus, examination of the shape of the profile can help to overcome idiosyncrasies of specific modalities and potentially bridge across datasets that use different sequences.

Conclusion

With microstructure-sensitive imaging entering the mainstream of neuroimaging research, profiling offers a versatile and reliable approach for indexing cortical lamination. The present findings indicate that the shape of intracortical intensity profiles (as described by m1 and m2) offers a reliable and replicable basis for assessing inter-individual differences in cortical microstructure. Notably, these ICC values position the reliability of microstructure profiles on par with structural volumetry6 and cortical thickness7 and superior to functional MRI8. Future studies would benefit from incorporating quantitative relaxometry (e.g., R1) to facilitate region-wise comparisons of microstructural properties, as reliability and replicability decline when using non-quantitative contrasts, such as T1w/T2w. More broadly, our results suggest that 1 mm isotropic MRI is sufficient to capture meaningful laminar variation, though higher resolutions can provide measurable improvements in sensitivity and precision, and are becoming increasingly attainable with contemporary acquisition protocols (e.g., 0.65mm, MP2RAGE at 3T within ~9 min9).

To support future applications of the intracortical microstructure profiling approach, we developed a generalisable workflow for their extraction (https://github.com/caseypaquola/cortpro) and incorporated the code into an automated data preprocessing toolbox (micapipe). Furthermore, we’re releasing a warehouse of microstructure profiles and moment maps derived from open-access resources (“Microstructural Marketplace”, https://osf.io/e6f7d/). These include a wide range of microstructure-sensitive contrasts (R1, T1w/T2w, MTsat) acquired at various resolutions (0.25mm-0.8mm) and different magnetic strengths (3T and 7T)1–4, as well as post-mortem staining for cells bodies myelin and parvalbumin interneurons. We hope these datasets provide a helpful benchmark for new profiling studies and supports further investigations into the importance of microarchitecture in shaping brain function and cognition.
