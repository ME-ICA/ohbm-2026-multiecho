Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) involves progressive cognitive decline and widespread disruption of large-scale brain networks, yet cortical reorganization of metabolic networks is not well investigated and understood. Reductions in N-acetylaspartate (NAA), a marker of neuronal integrity, are a well-established metabolic feature of AD; however, the spatial organization of neuronal metabolic disruption across the cortex has not been systematically mapped. This study aimed to characterize cortex-wide NAA connectivity gradients in AD and to relate disease-associated alterations to cytoarchitecture, functional specialization, and cognitive impairments.

Methods

We acquired high-resolution whole-brain MR spectroscopic imaging (MRSI) data from 112 amyloid-positive patients with AD or mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and 39 age-matched amyloid-negative cognitively unimpaired controls using the SPICE (Spectroscopic Imaging by Exploiting Spatiospectral Correlation) sequence1. Within a 12 min 35 s acquisition, we simultaneously obtained 3D maps of NAA and other metabolites at a nominal spatial resolution of 2.0 × 3.0 × 3.0 mm³. The group-averaged whole-brain NAA maps and overall analysis pipeline are shown in Figure 1A,B. For each participant, an individualized NAA metabolic network was constructed by computing Jensen–Shannon divergence (JSD)–based similarity between NAA distributions across all pairs of ROIs defined by the 100-ROI Schaefer atlas2. Principal component analysis was applied to each individual connectome to derive eigenvectors (gradients) that capture large-scale patterns of regional similarity in neuronal metabolism. Individual gradients were aligned using Procrustes alignment and averaged within groups. Group differences in gradient topology were assessed using two-sample t-tests, with effect sizes quantified by Cohen’s d. Associations between gradient alterations and global cognitive function, assessed using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), were evaluated using Spearman’s partial correlation, controlling for age, sex, and education. To provide multiscale biological context, we compared NAA gradient alterations with histology-derived cytoarchitectural atlases, including the Economo–Koskinas atlas3 and BigBrain-derived cytoarchitectural gradients4, and further linked affected cortical territories to functional domains using NeuroQuery-based meta-analytic decoding5.

Results

Amyloid-positive AD and MCI patients showed reduced within-network NAA connectivity in the limbic (p = 0.0061) and salience/ventral attention (SAL/VAN) networks (p = 0.0016), along with reduced between-network connectivity between the default mode and visual networks (p = 0.0007) and between SAL/VAN and the frontoparietal control network (FPCN) (p = 0.0089), as shown in Figure 1C. In addition, AD was associated with a marked reorganization of cortical metabolic gradients (Figure 1D). Specifically, gradient 1 (G1) showed significant reductions in the lateral temporal cortex (p = 0.0002), ventral prefrontal cortex (p = 0.0049) and visual association cortex (p = 0.0041), alongside increased values in the posterior cingulate cortex/precuneus (p = 0.0014), consistent with a collapse of hierarchical metabolic differentiation across transmodal regions that are vulnerable in early AD. In contrast, gradient 2 (G2) exhibited more regionally specific alterations, with increased values in the posterior middle frontal gyrus (p = 0.0086), posterior parietal cortex (p = 0.0421), and lateral temporal cortex (p = 0.0330), together with reduced values in visual cortex (p = 0.0291).

Cytoarchitectural contextualization revealed a systematic shift in G2 NAA connectivity changes across Economo–Koskinas cortical classes, with lower median G2 values observed in parietal and frontal cortices (Figure 2A, top panel), suggesting a gradient-based relationship between metabolic reorganization and laminar differentiation. Spatial correlation analysis further revealed that the topography of G2 changes in AD was significantly correlated with the BigBrain anterior–posterior cytoarchitectural gradient (r = −0.423, p = 0.001; Figure 2A, middle panel), indicating that G2 reductions were more pronounced toward posterior cortical regions. In addition, G2 alterations were significantly related to the sensory–fugal gradient (r = 0.214, p = 0.033; Figure 2A, bottom panel), linking metabolic gradient reorganization to the hierarchical transition from primary sensory to higher-order association cortices.

Importantly, mean G1 values within regions showing significant group differences—including the lateral temporal cortex, ventral prefrontal cortex, visual cortices, and posterior cingulate cortex/precuneus—were significantly correlated with individual MMSE scores (r = −0.273, p = 0.0038; Figure 2B), indicating that greater cognitive impairment was associated with a more pronounced loss of neuronal differentiation. Meta-analytic decoding further indicated that areas exhibiting the strongest gradient disruption were preferentially associated with episodic memory, attentional control, and visuospatial processing, which are all cognitive domains prominently affected in AD (Figure 2C).

Conclusion

A large-scale collapse of hierarchical neuronal metabolic organization across the cortex was observed in AD. Disruption of NAA connectivity gradients may provide a biologically grounded framework linking cytoarchitectural susceptibility, network-level degeneration, and clinical cognitive impairment. Our experimental findings may establish neuronal metabolic connectome as a promising systems-level marker for characterizing cortical involvement in AD.
