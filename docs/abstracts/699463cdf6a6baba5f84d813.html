Introduction

Epilepsy is increasingly understood as a disorder of pathological brain-state transitions rather than a static abnormality localized to a single region. Seizures emerge from nonlinear interactions across spatial and temporal scales, yet many neural mass models remain limited in their ability to capture such transitions in high-dimensional systems. In particular, simplified representations of temporal interactions (most commonly fixed conduction delays) restrict the dynamical repertoire of these models and constrain their relevance for epilepsy modeling.

Neural mass models have provided important insights into seizure mechanisms by linking excitation–inhibition balance to oscillatory activity. However, linking biophysically grounded temporal structure to the emergence, stability, and variability of seizure-like dynamics remains an open modeling challenge. Addressing this gap is critical for understanding why similar parameter perturbations can produce qualitatively different outcomes across brain states.

Here, we investigate epileptic dynamics from a dynamical systems perspective using a high-dimensional neural mass framework with biologically grounded temporal interactions. Rather than focusing on methodological development alone, we use this framework to study how epileptic activity arises as state-dependent transitions between physiological and pathological regimes. By explicitly examining how temporal heterogeneity shapes neural mass dynamics, we aim to provide mechanistic insight into seizure susceptibility and variability in large-scale brain models.

Methods

We employ a high-dimensional neural mass modeling framework based on the Jansen–Rit architecture, extended to represent interactions across multiple cortical populations. Each population comprises interacting excitatory and inhibitory subpopulations, generating EEG-like signals through pyramidal cell activity. Inter-population coupling is mediated through distributed conduction delays rather than fixed latencies, enabling heterogeneous temporal interactions across the network.

Distributed delays are derived from axonal features extracted from empirical studies, including axon diameter distributions and characteristic path lengths for short-range and long-range connections. Axon diameters are sampled from a Generalized Extreme Value distribution constrained to physiological ranges, mapped to conduction velocities using a biophysically motivated model, and converted into delay distributions via axonal path lengths. These delay distributions are discretized onto a temporal grid and implemented as coupling kernels that distribute synaptic input over time.

We systematically explore a multidimensional parameter space using Latin Hypercube Sampling, varying synaptic gains, excitation–inhibition balance parameters, and external drive terms. For each parameter set, neural activity is simulated and characterized using features relevant to epilepsy, including oscillatory frequency, amplitude modulation, and spike-like transients. Simulated dynamics are classified into non-seizure and seizure-like regimes based on these features.

Sensitivity analyses are performed to assess the relative influence of model parameters on observed dynamics, while emphasizing that parameter importance is interpreted in the context of the system’s underlying dynamical state rather than as static predictors.

Results

The model reproduces a wide range of neural dynamics, spanning stable oscillatory activity, transient spike-like events, and sustained seizure-like regimes. Introducing axon-informed distributed delays reshapes the temporal structure of inter-population interactions by replacing single-latency coupling with temporally extended kernels. This enables the simultaneous expression of fast and slow processes within the same network configuration, with short-range connections supporting rapid interactions and long-range connections introducing slower, integrative influences.

Seizure-related dynamics arise through state-dependent transitions rather than simple parameter threshold crossings. Identical parameter perturbations can lead to qualitatively different outcomes depending on the baseline dynamical state, including persistence of oscillatory activity, transient epileptiform events, or sustained spike–wave discharges. These results highlight strong nonlinear interactions between excitation–inhibition balance and the temporal structure imposed by distributed delays.

Sensitivity analysis confirms that excitatory and inhibitory gain parameters are key determinants of the overall dynamical regime. However, their effects are strongly modulated by the temporal context of the system. In particular, parameter configurations that generate spike-like discharges under fixed-delay coupling may fail to produce such events when delays are distributed, demonstrating that temporal dispersion can suppress spike-generating dynamics despite identical excitability settings.

Direct comparison with fixed-delay implementations shows that distributed delays alter seizure susceptibility and temporal organization of activity, yielding dynamics that are not readily reproduced with single-latency coupling. These findings underscore the critical role of delay distributions in shaping epileptiform activity and emphasize the importance of incorporating realistic temporal structure when modeling pathological brain dynamics.

Conclusion

This work reframes epilepsy as a problem of state-dependent transitions in high-dimensional neural mass systems with biologically grounded temporal structure. By integrating empirically informed axonal features into neural mass interactions, the model moves beyond fixed-delay assumptions and captures heterogeneous temporal dynamics that shape seizure emergence and variability.

Rather than attributing epileptic activity to isolated parameters, our results emphasize the interaction between excitation–inhibition balance and temporal context in determining seizure susceptibility. This perspective provides a mechanistic foundation for interpreting variability in epileptic dynamics and supports the development of model-driven approaches to understanding seizure generation.

Overall, the proposed framework advances large-scale epilepsy modeling by linking biophysical temporal heterogeneity to emergent brain-state transitions, offering a principled basis for future integration with EEG data and personalized neuromodulation strategies.
