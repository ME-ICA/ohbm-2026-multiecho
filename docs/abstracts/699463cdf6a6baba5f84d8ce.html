Introduction

Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) has become a cornerstone technique for investigating intrinsic brain network organization. Computational models such as The Virtual Brain (TVB) aim to reproduce empirical functional connectivity (FC) patterns by simulating neural dynamics constrained by structural connectivity (SC). However, the blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) signal reflects not only underlying neuronal activity but also complex hemodynamic and vascular processes. While computational modeling efforts have traditionally focused on optimizing neuronal parameters to fit empirical FC matrices, the contributions of hemodynamic factors—particularly blood arrival time (BAT) and the region- and subject-specific shape of the hemodynamic response function (HRF)—remain underexplored. Understanding the relative contributions of vascular physiology versus neural dynamics is critical for interpreting modeling results and avoiding potential confounds in our understanding of brain network organization.

Methods

We employed The Virtual Brain framework to simulate resting-state BOLD fMRI data, systematically evaluating the contributions of different physiological and neuronal factors to FC reconstruction. We generated FC matrices under three conditions: (1) using empirical structural connectivity with biologically inspired neural mass models and standard HRF modelling (Balloon-Windkessel), (2) using blood arrival time variations alone without neural dynamics, and (3) retrieving the subject- and region-specific empirical resting state fMRI and convolving it with the simulated synaptic activity. The similarity between simulated and empirical FC matrices was quantified using correlation coefficients. We assessed whether BAT-derived FC patterns could achieve comparable fits to empirical data as those obtained from The Virtual Brain simulations, thereby evaluating the relative importance of hemodynamic versus neural contributions to observed functional connectivity patterns.

Results

Our analyses revealed that blood arrival time alone produces a functional connectivity matrix with similarity to empirical FC comparable to that obtained from simulated BOLD data generated using empirical structural connectivity and biologically inspired neural models. This finding demonstrates that physiological and hemodynamic factors are non-negligible features of rs-fMRI data that substantially contribute to observed FC patterns. Importantly, these results should not be interpreted as diminishing the value of neural modeling approaches; rather, they indicate that vascular physiology, HRF variability, and neuronal activity contribute to functional connectivity in complementary ways, as confirmed by a mediation analysis. The known coupling between cerebral vascularization and neuronal activity suggests these factors are inherently linked, though modulations in FC are likely partially driven by variations in vascular architecture independent of instantaneous neural dynamics. These findings highlight the importance of considering which empirical FC matrix is being fitted, as preprocessing strategies and physiological nuisance regression approaches will inevitably transform the target connectivity patterns. Our results suggest that future modeling efforts may benefit from fitting alternative data features beyond static FC matrices to better disentangle neural from vascular contributions.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that hemodynamic factors, particularly blood arrival time and HRF variability, exert substantial influence on resting-state functional connectivity patterns and cannot be dismissed as mere nuisance variables in computational brain modeling. The comparable performance of BAT-based and neurodynamically-derived FC matrices in fitting empirical data underscores the need for a more nuanced interpretation of modeling results: successful FC reconstruction does not necessarily validate the underlying neural assumptions of a model. Rather than viewing this as a limitation, we propose that it reflects the biological reality of neurovascular coupling, where vascular architecture and neural activity are intrinsically linked yet contribute distinct variance to the BOLD signal. Moving forward, the neuroimaging community should exercise caution when interpreting FC-fitted models, remain cognizant of how preprocessing pipelines shape the target connectivity matrices, and consider expanding modeling objectives beyond static FC to include dynamic, spectral, or regional BOLD signal features. By explicitly modeling hemodynamic heterogeneity alongside neural dynamics, future computational frameworks may achieve more biologically faithful representations of brain network function and better disentangle the relative contributions of "plumbing" and neuronal communication to emergent functional connectivity patterns.
