Introduction

Extremely preterm birth (before 28 weeks gestation) is associated with elevated risk for adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes. These outcomes vary widely in adolescence; many extremely-preterm-born youth show typical cognitive and mental health outcomes, while others exhibit cognitive deficits and/or psychiatric disorders.1 Early exposure to the extrauterine environment dramatically changes the context of brain development for extremely preterm-born infants. White matter development is particularly vulnerable due to the maturational timing of key neurodevelopmental processes. Neuronal migration, apoptosis and synaptogenesis are ongoing at 28 weeks gestational age, and myelination begins around this time and proceeds through adolescence. White matter differences associated with preterm birth have been observed beyond the neonatal period; diffusion MRI (dMRI) research reveals widespread alterations in white matter microstructure of preterm-born adolescents.2 Microstructure varies over the length of white matter tracts, and tractometry is a method that enables localization of effects by representing microstructural metrics from dMRI (e.g., fractional anisotropy [FA]) at multiple points along white matter tracts as ‘tract profiles’.3 In this study, we aimed to localize white matter differences associated with cognitive outcomes (low vs. typical intelligence quotient) among a sample of extremely preterm-born adolescents. We used tractometry to compare FA tract profiles between extremely preterm-born adolescents with low vs. typical IQ. We predicted that the low IQ group would exhibit reduced FA relative to the typical IQ group in major fiber bundles, but we did not have hypotheses regarding specific bundles due to a paucity of prior evidence in this population. For a subset of tracts, we compared results derived from different tract segmentation approaches to assess the impact of methodological choices on tractometry analysis.

Methods

Forty-two extremely preterm-born adolescents (26M/16F; mean age 15.56 years (SD=.52) at scan; mean gestational age 183.38 days (SD=9.33) (~25 wks) at birth; mean birth weight 826.9 g (SD=184.6) from the Extremely Low Gestational Age Newborns (ELGAN)4 were included in analysis. Participants were grouped into low IQ (<85; n=13) and typical IQ (≥85; n=29) groups based on full-scale IQ measured with the Differential Abilities Scales (DAS)5 at age 10 years. dMRI data (TE = 80 ms, TR = 6.95 s, 15 gradient directions at B=1000 s/mm2, one B=0 s/mm2, 1.67 x 1.67 x 2 mm3 voxels) were acquired using Philips 3T MRI systems at two sites. Preprocessing was performed using QSIPrep with a T2w anatomical reference; preprocessing included B1 field inhomogeneity correction, eddy current correction, motion correction, synthetic susceptibility distortion correction, and registration to the anatomical reference. Diffusion tensor model fitting, automated tractography, and tract profiling were performed using pyAFQ3. Tract segmentation was performed via the AFQ-waypoints method (based on include/exclude regions) and the Recobundles streamlines-based registration method. Main analyses focused on AFQ-waypoints tracts (Fig. 1), including the corpus callosum (CC; orbital, anterior frontal, superior frontal, motor, superior parietal, posterior parietal, and occipital segments), and bilateral corticospinal tracts (CST), cingulum bundles (Cing), anterior thalamic radiations (ATR), inferior longitudinal fasciculi (ILF), inferior fronto-occipital fasciculi (IFO), superior longitudinal fasciculi (SLF), and arcuate fasciculi (AF). FA was sampled at 100 points along each tract of interest and to form a “tract profile”. Group-wise tract profiles were modelled as generalized additive models (GAMs) and group differences by IQ were tested using the Tractable package in RStudio, with site and sex included as covariates. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to account for testing models in 21 tracts. Follow-up analyses were performed on tracts segmented with Recobundles (CC Forceps Minor, CC Forceps Major, CC middle, bilateral CST; Fig. 1) to examine whether results were consistent with the main analysis.

Results

Tractometry analysis showed that the tract profiles of the CC-Motor differed between IQ groups (t = -3.44, p < .001, q = .012)., driven by lower FA in the central portion of the tract in the low IQ group. Several other tract profiles differed at the uncorrected level, including the CC Superior Parietal (t = -1.984, p = .047, q = .249), left ILF (t = -2.47, p = .013, q  = .094), and right ILF (t = -2.60, p = .009, q = .094). All of these tracts showed a pattern of lower FA in the Low IQ group relative to the Typical IQ group. Tract profiles for all tracts by IQ group are shown in Figure 2. 

Qualitative observation of tractography from AFQ-waypoints compared to Recobundles tract segmentation showed less accurate segmentation relative to the AFQ-waypoints method. Major bundles including the CC (Forceps Major, CC Forceps Minor, CC middle) and bilateral CST were accurately segmented with the Recobundles method, but other tracts had errors in many subjects, or showed poor coherence. Tractometry analysis of Recobundles tracts were performed on CC Forceps Major, CC Forceps Minor, CC middle and bilateral CST. Results were consistent with findings from the CC in the AFQ-waypoints method: the CC middle portion (encompassing superior frontal, motor, and parietal segments) differed between IQ groups (t= -2.937, p=.003), with lower FA observed across central nodes in the Low IQ group relative to the Typical IQ group. Trending effects (p < .01) were observed in Forceps Major and Forceps Minor, while no group differences were observed in the left or right CST. 

Conclusion

Extremely preterm-born adolescents with low IQ exhibited reduced FA in several white matter tracts compared to those with typical range IQ, most notably the motor segment of the corpus callosum. Central nodes along this tract were affected, indicating that bilateral connections of the motor cortex may be weaker in those with low IQ. These findings show that variability in brain white matter corresponds to variability in cognitive outcomes (IQ) among adolescents born earlier than 28 weeks gestation. Follow-up analysis in tracts segmented with Recobundles showed reduced FA in the middle segment of the corpus callosum in the Low IQ group that was consistent with the main AFQ-waypoints tract findings. This supports the robustness of these results. Tract segmentation based on the AFQ-waypoints method was more accurate than the Recobundles method in our sample, indicating that additional optimization may be needed to apply Recobundles streamlines-based registration and segmentation within this population. We note that this limitation may arise from the limited number of diffusion directions and single B-value used in this study, which may not be optimal for streamlines-based registration. Additional limitations of our study include small sample size, and absence of a full-term comparison group. We plan future analysis extending these methods to study white matter microstructure in extremely preterm-born adolescents from ten additional sites that collected MRI data for the ELGAN study. In addition, we plan to examine white matter differences associated with other cognitive outcomes in extremely preterm-born adolescents, such as specific learning disabilities in reading and math.
