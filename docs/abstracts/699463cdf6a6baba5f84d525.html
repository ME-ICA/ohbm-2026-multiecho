Introduction

Functional connectivity (FC), defined as statistical interdependence between regional BOLD signals, is widely used to characterize individual variability in brain function and brain-behavior relationship. In contrast, effective connectivity (EC) captures directional interactions between brain regions, but its prediction utility of behavioral scores at the large-network and whole-brain scale has been less examined due to computational limitations. Recent developments in regression dynamic causal modeling (rDCM) allow efficient whole-brain EC estimation,1 enabling a direct comparison of prediction performance between EC and FC. In this study we predict individual age and reaction time measured during a stimulus-response compatibility task using whole-brain and network-specific EC and FC calculated from the resting-state fMRI data.

Methods

We analyzed resting-state fMRI from 265 adults (18–85 years) of the 1000BRAINS cohort,2 where the cortical BOLD time series were extracted using the Schaefer atlas3 of 100, 200 and 400 brain regions. EC was estimated using rDCM for the above numbers of regions considered as network nodes, and FC was calculated by Pearson correlations of BOLD time series of any two brain regions. The latter were also clustered into 7 resting-state networks as suggested for the employed parcellation,3 which were considered together with a task-evoked stimulus-response compatibility (SRC) network4 as well as whole-brain (WB) case based on the considered parcellations. Ridge-regularized regression with repeated 5-fold cross-validation was employed to predict individual age and reaction time (RT) measured during the SRC task. A selected fraction (5%/10%/20%) of FC and EC edges most correlated with behavioral scores across subjects were used as features. The prediction results were confirmed for varying feature-selection thresholds and finer parcellations (100/200/400 parcels).

Results

Both connectivity modalities predicted age markedly better than RT, and EC showed consistently superior performance in the whole-brain models (Fig. 1). In particular, EC achieved averaged prediction correlation between predicted and empirical scores r ≈ 0.72 for age, compared to 0.67 for FC, while RT prediction peaked at r ≈ 0.28 using EC compared to r ≈ 0.19 for FC (Fig. 1). Here we also observed relatively steady accuracy for age prediction with respect to the feature selection threshold, whereas the quality of RT prediction might still be improved when more connectivity edges were involved (Fig. 1). EC outperformed FC in age and RT prediction also across functional networks demonstrating different prediction quality (Fig. 2). In particular, ventral attention and frontoparietal networks relatively well contributed to accurate predictions of age and RT, while the default-mode network performed well for age prediction, but less for RT. On the other hand, limbic and SRC networks showed a weak performance. Prediction patterns were stable across feature selection thresholds and parcellation schemes (Figs. 1 and 2).

Conclusion

These results indicate that resting-state EC captures discriminative neural dynamics underlying inter-individual differences of brain-behavior relationship more effectively than FC, supporting EC as a robust feature space for modeling neuronal correlates of behavior in large-scale brain networks and neuroimaging datasets. In line with other studies,5 our results promote including simulated neuroimaging data, either by whole-brain dynamical models or DCM, into the investigation of the relationship between brain and behavior.
