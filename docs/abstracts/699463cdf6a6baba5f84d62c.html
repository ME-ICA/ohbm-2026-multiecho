Introduction

Sharp-wave ripples (SWR) are bursts of synchronized neuronal activity found in the hippocampus 1. SWRs have been reported to correlate with sleep stages, memory consolidation and specific thoughts, see for example, 2.  Moreover, It has been suggested  that SWR influence the dynamics of the entire brain network, as SWRs have been reported to influence selective activation of the default mode network detected via functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI)3. Here, our focus is on electrocorticography (ECoG). We explore network dynamics, which provide a quantitative framework for analyzing interactions among distributed areas. Rather than assuming a fixed connectivity pattern, network dynamics describe time-varying patterns. In recent years brain networks have been derived from electroencephalography (EEG), fMRI, magnetoencephalography (MEG) and other imaging modalities. While fMRI provides higher spatial resolution, MEG/EEG provide higher temporal resolution. Since SWRs have a very short duration of around 100 – 300 milliseconds, we will take advantage of ECoG’s high temporal resolution and analyze network dynamics before and after the SWR on a short temporal scale. We will compare 1-second intervals before and after the SWR and analyze dynamical network metrics derived from each interval.

Methods

Patients with drug-resistant epilepsy undergo intracranial electrode implantation for surgical planning on a regular basis at our clinic. The electrodes are placed solely on the basis of clinical necessity. Some patients gave informed consent to participate in a study which includes wearing an additional wearable device as well as answering questionnaires regularly. The Institutional review Board at the Department of Neurosurgery at the University of Osaka University Hospital approved this study. 11 patients participated between 2019 and 2025. Intracranial EEG was recorded using a Nihon Kohden multi-channel EEG system at a sampling rate of 10 kHz. Continuous recordings were performed for 5 - 13 days, with the duration varying for each patient. Here, we focus on one exemplary patient and present the results for this patient. 

We determined the SWR of the data recorded by the Stereo electroencephalography (SEEG) electrodes in the hippocampus, following the description in 4. We then excluded SWR that were determined as being during an epileptic seizure. As SWR are also discussed to vary depending on day and night, we chose the day interval defined as 7 am to 10 pm. Then we found 5 second intervals around the SWR. Overlapping intervals were excluded to make sure that each interval only contains a single SWR with the peak in the center of the interval. The ECoG data corresponding to those time intervals were found and used for further analysis. The ECoG data were downsampled to 2000 Hz and noise episodes were excluded. Noise episodes were determined by calculating the analytical amplitude of the Hilbert transform, calculating the mean and standard deviation and defining parts as noise if they are 10 or more standard deviations away from the mean and within vicinity of a signal that is 30 or more standard deviations away from the mean. We applied a Hidden Markov Model (HHM) which assumes that a system moves between unobserved, i.e., hidden, states following Markov dynamics. We made use of the osl dynamics toolbox 5. Time delayed embedding was performed using 301 embeddings. Then a principal component analysis with 80 components was performed, follwowed by a z-transformation. The HMM was configured with 8 states, a sequence length of 8000, a batch size of 32, a learning rate of 0.01 and 20 epochs. We initialized the model with 3 different initializations for 1 epoch each and then fitted the model to the prepared data. 

In order to compare the network dynamics pre and post SWR, we focused on the pre-SWR interval of [-1.25 s,-0.25s] before the SWR and the post-SWR interval of [0.25 s, 1.25s] after the SWR. A SWR has a duration of several 100 ms, so we excluded 250 ms before and 250 ms after the peak of the SWR. We compared those two intervals with respect to fractional occupancy (the time spent in a specific state with respect to the total time), mean lifetime (the average duration of a state), mean interval (the average time between the same states), and switching rate (the average number of state activations per second) of all 8 states found by the HMM.

Results

The dataset of the patient resulted in 7308 SWR with 5-s long surrounding intervals that could be used for the model. The recorded ECoG provided 56 useable channels. The p-values of the comparison between the mean of the fractional occupations, mean lifetimes, mean intervals and the switching rates of the pre and post SWR intervals are shown in Table 1. We see that the fractional occupations differ significantly for state 0, 1, 4, 5, 6, and 7. Also the mean lifetimes of the states differ for state 0, 5, 6, and 7. The mean interval, on the other hand, just differs for state 1. The switching rate is statistically different for state 0, 1, 4, 6, and 7. A closer look at the fractional occupation, see Figure 1, shows that the fractional occupations of state 0, 5, and 7 are higher for the post SWR interval, whereas the fractional occupations of state 1, 4, and 6 are lower. The lifetimes of states 0, 5, and 7 are lower in the pre SWR interval and the lifetime of state 6 is longer. The mean interval of state 1 is longer pre SWR. The switching rates of states 0 and 7 are lower pre SWR whereas the switching rates of states 1, 4, and 6 are higher.

Conclusion

We conclude that the occurrence of SWR influences network dynamics. In our exemplary patients we see that especially the fractional occupations of several states differ between pre and post SWR intervals which means that before the SWR the system occupies certain states more than after the SWR. Also, the mean lifetimes of certain states as well as the switching rates are different. That shows that for the exemplary participant various dynamic network parameters are influenced by SWRs. Here, we presented the results of an exemplary patient. While the number of SWR and surrounding intervals is quite high, we cannot be sure that the results are true on a group level. To make conclusions on a group level it is necessary to compare these results to the other patients included in the study. However, given the high significance of the t-tests, i.e., their quite low p-values, we expect that SWR generally influence network dynamics of ECoG and that these dynamics are worth investigating further.
