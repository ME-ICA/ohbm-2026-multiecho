Introduction

Psychiatric disorders such as schizophrenia, depression, or anxiety disorders are characterized by high heterogeneity in symptoms and wide-spread structural as well as functional alterations of the brain. Neuroimaging, especially (functional) magnetic resonance imaging ((f)MRI) provides information about those alterations and fosters insight into the pathologies of the disorders. Clinical decision support systems for those disorders can benefit from machine learning models based on neuroimaging data for objective diagnosis, prognosis, and effective treatment selection. Deep learning (DL) models such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) specialized for medical image processing show promise for this task since they can detect complex patterns in images without the need for prior information about candidate regions.1 Their downside, however, is the lack of transparency about the decision process. Explainable AI (XAI) methods address this problem and might be helpful in the clinical translation of DL applications as well as potential biomarker indication. The current study qualitatively and quantitatively evaluates seven DL architectures frequently employed in medical image analyses with gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM2) for plausibility. The advantage of Grad-CAMs is their explanation on the individual level which provides transparent and intuitive information in a diagnostic or prognostic clinical setting. These local explanations, however, cannot be harnessed directly to discover the biological underpinnings of a disorder. Therefore, we also develop an approach to translate the saliency maps from the Grad-CAM into universally interpretable anatomical markers of a psychiatric disorder.

Methods

This study is based on 192 structural MRI images from the MCIC collection3 containing 101 schizophrenia patients (SCZ, based on DSM-IV criteria) and 91 healthy controls (HC). Images were skull-stripped and registered to MNI 1mm3 isovoxel with the Python toolbox nypipe. Image intensities in the 1% and 99% percentile were removed, empty image slices deleted, and image intensity was normalized to -1 to 1. Size was then rescaled to 64³,128³, or 192x22x192 voxel resolution depending on the downstream network requirements.

Seven 3-dimensional DL architectures that are frequently employed in medical image analysis were trained to distinguish images of SCZ from HC in a five-fold cross-validation approach with the hyperparameters that were suggested in their respective publications (Fig. 1, left). For local explanation (Fig. 1, middle), class-selective saliency maps for each test image were generated by a 3D adaptation of the original Grad-CAM method2 to quantify the contribution of each voxel to the classification decision. Mass accuracies (MA) of the saliency maps allow for judgement of the plausibility of a DL model with the ratio of attention within the brain area vs. outside the brain. The center of mass (CoM) of each saliency map allows for group comparisons. Based on these metrics and visual inspection, two architectures are selected for global explanation (Fig. 1, right). To capture the brain areas robustly distinguishing between clinical group in each model, the saliency maps were subjected to a mass-univariate group comparison and the resulting t-maps were TFCE-error corrected to account for multiple comparisons. The voxels with sign. difference were then mapped to the regions of the AAL atlas. To obtain reliable biomarker candidates, the intersecting regions between the two models were used. 

Results

On average, all classifiers exhibited a stratified classification accuracy well above 70% and area-under-the-curve (AUC) scores of more than 0.75 without sign. differences in accuracy (F6,28=0.371, p=.891). These accuracies are well within the range of what is expected from an ML-classifier based on structural MRI data. However, only two of the models (BrainID, ResNet18) base their decisions in the schizophrenia classification task on plausible structural brain information (median MA of both clinical groups > 40%). For these two models, the CoM is sign. different between clinical groups as well (both t35>5.000; p<.001). The group comparisons of the saliency maps for these models revealed one contiguous cluster each, spanning 19 and six brain regions, respectively (Fig. 2). The intersection of the regions from the two models to obtain reliable biomarker candidate areas highlights five brain areas: Frontal Inf Tri R, Caudate R, Frontal Sup R, Frontal Sup Medial L, and Precuneus R. 

Conclusion

In this three-stage approach, we demonstrate the necessity and feasibility of transparency in the decision process of DL-architectures for image-based decision support in psychiatry. Furthermore, we demonstrate the value of a post-hoc local explainability method, which is a helpful tool for enriching individual decisions made by a DL-based clinical decision support system, in deriving global anatomical biomarkers for a psychiatric disorder such as schizophrenia.

Saliency maps are most often used for qualitative evaluation and visualisation of the decision process of classification models whereas their quantitative evaluation is scarcely conducted. In image classification tasks in which the face validity of the produced saliency map can be easily assessed visually or via a well-defined ground truth area of interest, a quantitative evaluation might not be necessary or hard. The anatomical changes in schizophrenia are subtle, distributed, and still an ongoing matter of research. The quantitative metrics developed in this study are based on the rather coarse region of interest including the whole brain, the assumption that the saliency should not be uniformly distributed, and that similar brain regions are affected in the majority of patients. Our approach can be generalized to other disorders characterized by subtle, complex brain alterations as it is typical for many psychiatric disorders. For biomarker discovery, further analyses that detect confined clusters of intensity can be a valuable addition to the analyses presented in this study.

The anatomical regions most frequently identified in reviews of neuroimaging studies on schizophrenia patients are frontal, temporal, and subcortical regions. For the data set used in this study, frontal, temporal, and insular grey matter reduction was assessed with voxel-based morphometry. Along this line, frontal regions are highlighted most prominently by the saliency maps of both our classifiers as well as their intersection. Beyond the right superior frontal gyrus and the triangular part of the right inferior frontal gyrus, however, the specific regions vary across classifiers. While the saliency map of BrainID highlights left superior and right temporal regions as well, the map of ResNet18 emphasizes the right insula and putamen. The insula has been discussed for its role in the progression of schizophrenia and has also been highlighted in another study utilizing saliency maps. The basal ganglia and related subcortical structures are affected in several psychiatric disorders with caudate nucleus and putamen, the pertaining regions highlighted in our results, being specifically involved in schizophrenia. Indeed, the right caudate has been found to be enlarged in schizophrenia patients. The correspondence of the saliency maps from this study to findings in the current body of literature confirms the plausibility of our results and supports the approach introduced.

Based on the learnings from the current study, upcoming work might strive for a region-focused saliency map generation by improving the classification performance, construct networks additionally capturing regions of interests e.g. segmentation networks or including saliency map related metrics in the training cost function. Due to the method-inherent smoothing of the Grad-CAM method, the conducted brain region mapping procedure might also be imprecise and thereby lead to slight distortions in region coverage. 

To conclude, the generalizable approach employed in this work is a first step to enable the identification of regions of high relevance during the classification of pathologies by transitioning from local saliency explanations to accumulated global information. The anatomical findings of this study converge with findings of classical imaging studies on schizophrenia patients, giving the approach plausibility.
