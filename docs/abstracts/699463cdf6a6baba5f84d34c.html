Introduction

The human primary motor cortex (M1) is an executive locus of voluntary motor control of a limb, and also receives and processes proprioceptive (kinesthetic) signals from the limb.1 It is shown that the primate’s M1 is capable of integrating proprioceptive signals from a contralateral limb with ongoing motor control processes of the limb with a short latency (fast feedback control).2 In the present study, we examined whether the M1 is also capable of integrating proprioceptive signals from a contralateral limb with motor signals for an opposite (ipsilateral) limb. This is important because if the brain can integrate proprioceptive and motor signals bilaterally, this approach can be an effective way to restore motor functions of paretic limbs of hemiplegic stroke patients. We prepared a bilateral proprioceptive-motor coupling task: participants voluntarily extend and relax the left index finger alternatingly at 1 Hz and an experimenter passively extend the right index finger synchronized (In-phase task) or anti-synchronized (Out-of-phase task) with the extension of the left finger. We measured brain activity when healthy adults performed these tasks and simultaneously recorded electromyography (EMG) from their right passive hands. We tested our hypothesis that EMG is augmented during the In-phase task and the activity of the left M1 hand section increases in relation to augmentation of EMG activity. By proving these, we provide evidence that proprioceptive-motor coupling can occur bilaterally in the M1.

Methods

Forty-one right-handed healthy young adults participated in the study. The study was approved by the NICT Ethics Committee, and the MRI Safety Committee of the CiNet (no. 2003260010). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after full explanation of the study procedures. We measured brain activity with a 3.0-Tesla MRI scanner (TR = 1s, Vida, Siemens, Germany) when the participants voluntarily extended and relaxed the left index finger alternatingly at 1 Hz and the experimenter passively extended the right index finger synchronized (In-phase task) or anti-synchronized (Out-of-phase task) with the extension of the left finger. Simultaneously, surface electromyography (EMG) was recorded from the right index finger extensor using an MR-compatible system (BrainAmp MR Plus, Brain Products GmbH, Munich, Germany). One run composed of fifteen 20-s task epochs with alternating 10-s rest epoch was performed for each task. In the EMG analysis, after denoising fMRI pulses, EMG signals were band-pass (10-400 Hz) filtered and rectified, and root mean square (RMS) values were calculated for each rest and task epoch. In the fMRI analysis, we used SPM12. After preprocessing, we directly compared brain activity between the In-phase and Out-of-phase tasks to identify In-phase-specific brain activity. For EMG, we also calculated RMS value for each EPI volume, and 450-s EMG time series was convolved with the canonical hemodynamic response function and used as a regressor.3 In order to depict brain regions in which activity specifically changes in relation to EMG change in the In-phase task, we directly compared activity in relation to EMG change in the In-phase task with that in the Out-of-phase task. We first conducted single-subject analysis and performed second-level group analysis to identify significant brain activity (height threshold p < 0.005, FWE corrected p < 0.05 for whole brain).

Results

When compared with rest epoch, EMG activity of the right passive hand significantly increased during task epoch both in the In-phase and in the Out-of-phase tasks (p < 0.001; Figure 1A), and no significant difference was observed between the tasks. However, the EMG activity gradually increased through the repetition of the In-phase task, whereas no such gradual increase was observed in the Out-of-phase task (Figure 1B). When we directly compared brain activity between the In-phase and Out-of-phase tasks, we found In-phase-specific activity in the hand section of the left M1 (Figure 1C yellow section). This was the only region in the entire brain. In addition, activity in this region changed in relation to EMG change specifically during the In-phase task (Figure 1C orange section). Viewed collectively, the EMG activity was gradually augmented by proprioceptive-motor coupling only during the In-phase task, and this integration took place in the hand section of the M1 contralateral to the passive hand.

Conclusion

Using simultaneous EMG recording and fMRI measurement, this study provides compelling evidence that proprioceptive–motor integration can occur bilaterally, and the primary motor cortex (M1) is its fundamental locus. Augmentation of muscle activity of the right passive hand through the repetition of In-phase task suggests effectiveness of In-phase task for the restoration of hand motor function for hemiplegic stroke patients.
