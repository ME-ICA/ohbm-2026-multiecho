Introduction

Altered functional brain network organization is an established hallmark of schizophrenia, with numerous studies reporting disruptions in core graph-theoretical measures (GTMs) such as clustering, centrality, efficiency, and assortativity.^1 These findings suggest that topological features capture meaningful disease-related changes and can support the classification of functional connectivity patterns. However, standard GTMs may miss finer-scale structural motifs that better distinguish generative models or pathological network alterations. To address this, we employ graphlets — an extension of the degree distribution that summarizes counts of small induced subgraphs^2 — which provide a more elaborate characterization of local topology. We hypothesize that counts of graphlets for nodes (graphlet degree distribution) offer improved discriminability between functional networks representing patients and controls, thereby enhancing classification performance.

Methods

Resting-state fMRI data from 90 schizophrenia patients and 90 healthy controls (3T Siemens Magnetom Trio, IKEM, Prague) were preprocessed following standard pipelines.^{3,4} Time series were extracted from 90 regions of interest (ROIs) defined by the AAL atlas, and Pearson correlations were computed between all ROI pairs to form functional connectivity matrices. These matrices were then binarized using two thresholding approaches: proportional thresholding, which retains a fixed proportion of the strongest connections, and degree-matched thresholding, which enforces identical degree distributions across groups to control for potential degree-related confounds.

To systematically probe methodological sensitivity, we also generated several classes of simulated parametrized random graph models and created progressively rewired variants of the real functional connectomes, allowing controlled tests of how well different feature sets detect graded changes in network topology.

We constructed multiple feature representations of functional brain networks based on (i) standard GTMs (including degree, clustering, betweenness and eigenvector centralities, assortativity, and global efficiency) and (ii) graphlet-based descriptors capturing counts of all induced subgraphs up to five nodes (see Fig. 1). To evaluate their discriminative power, we first generated a comprehensive simulation benchmark comprising several families of random graph models with systematically varied parameters, designed to create subtle but identifiable differences in topology. For each simulated classification task, Random Forest classifiers were trained on either GTM features, graphlet features, or their combinations, allowing us to assess which representations best distinguished between closely related generative models. Next, we applied the same feature sets to real resting-state fMRI connectomes from individuals with schizophrenia (SZ) and healthy controls (HC).

Results

In the simulations, graphlet-based features clearly outperformed standard GTMs when distinguishing between random graph models, including cases where the differences between classes were very small.  Specifically, graphlets outperform GTM by margins ranging from 2% to 10%, depending on the task. Consistently, in the rewired connectome analyses, graphlets were the most sensitive representation: they detected topological perturbations earlier and more strongly than both GTMs and adjacency-based classifiers. For example, for 5% of rewired edges, an accuracy was around 80%, 70% and 65% for graphlets, GTMs and adjacency respectively (see Fig. 2). In the real fMRI data, all feature sets that preserved local information — graphlets, node-level GTMs, and adjacency features — achieved similar classification performance around 65% to 70% accuracy, whereas global GTMs without spatial detail performed at chance level. This indicates that the discriminative signal in the data is carried by local structure rather than global topology. Degree-matched analyses further confirmed that the observed SZ–HC differences were not driven only by degree: although accuracy dropped slightly, both graphlets and locally informed GTMs remained above chance.

Conclusion

We demonstrate that graphlets provide a powerful extension of degree-based measures for network analysis. Beyond their theoretical value, they offer sensitive detection of group-level differences in resting-state fMRI data. Our findings indicate that schizophrenia patients show altered local graph structures relative to healthy controls, underscoring the potential of graphlets as biomarkers of pathological connectivity and as a tool for identifying deviations from normative network organization.
