Introduction

Dense mapping of cortical connectivity at the voxel/vertex level can provide a more precise view of connectomic organization compared to the conventional approach of studying connectivity at the level of brain areas. This approach has been applied to resting-state functional connectivity (RSFC), providing important insights into the organization of cortical networks1 and boundaries2,3, as well as principal gradients of connectivity variation4. However, RSFC characterizes task-free, spontaneous functional connectivity, and it remains unclear to what extent the connectomic organization observed in the resting state generalizes to task-related conditions. Meta-analytical connectivity modeling (MACM) characterizes consistent task-based co-activations across published neuroimaging studies, and thereby provides a marker of generalized task-related functional connectivity5. While MACM has often been applied to specific regions of interest, a dense whole-cortex characterization of MACM at the voxel/vertex level is lacking. This is partly due to the high computational costs of MACM, prohibiting its large-scale application in dense connectivity mapping. Here, we developed a graphics processing unit (GPU)-accelerated implementation of MACM to calculate a dense connectome across the cerebral cortex, and characterized its network structure in comparison to RSFC.

Methods

We used the BrainMap dataset, including the reported foci of significant activation across 2,206 task-based functional imaging studies (8,264 experiments; 32,538 subjects), to calculate a dense MACM matrix between 40,379 cerebral cortical voxels as seeds (rows) and 211,590 grey matter voxels in MNI space (2-mm resolution) as targets (columns). To do so, for each seed voxel with given MNI coordinates, we filtered the BrainMap dataset to the 50 experiments closest to the seed, and performed specific activation likelihood estimation (SALE) meta-analysis (5000 permutations) on the selected experiments to calculate the MACM map of the seed voxel.

Next, we computed the similarity of MACM patterns across all cortical seeds, resulting in a MACMcorr matrix. Using this matrix, we then performed K-means clustering analysis to assess the MACM network structure. The optimal number of clusters (range: 2-25) was selected based on between-set and between-seed stability of solutions. Furthermore, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to identify the main axes of MACM variation across the cerebral cortex. MACM networks and PCs were compared to RSFC networks and axes reported previously1,4. In addition, we performed a hub analysis, where we calculated the row-wise summation of the dense MACM matrix as MACM strength, which reflects the overall meta-analytical co-activation rate across the cerebral cortex (Fig. 1).

Results

The overall meta-analytical co-activation rate was highest in the frontal medial cortex, middle temporal gyrus, supplementary motor area, posterior cingulate, and precuneus (Fig. 2A). Clustering analysis on MACMcorr using K-means showed relatively stable solutions with 6, 13, and 20 clusters (Fig. 2D). We focused on the six-cluster solution (Fig. 2B), on par with the seven canonical RSFC networks1. There was a broad correspondence between the MACM and RSFC networks (Fig. 2C), with some MACM networks aligning with single RSFC networks (e.g., N4 corresponding to the visual RSFC network), and others spanning multiple RSFC networks (e.g., N6 spanning limbic and default mode RSFC networks). Using PCA, we identified three principal axes that explained 73.2% of the variance (Fig. 2E,F). We found that the first and second principal axes of MACM were largely aligned with the first and second gradients of RSFC (Fig. 2G)4.

Conclusion

Leveraging a GPU-accelerated implementation, we provided a dense, high-resolution characterization of cortical MACM. Using clustering and principal component analysis, we found that task-related co-activation patterns across reported effects in the literature showed a macroscale organization that broadly aligns with task-free RSFC organization, suggesting shared organizing principles of cortical connectivity across different modalities and conditions. At the same time, divergences between MACM and RSFC were observed, which may reflect both biological differences between task-related and resting-state conditions, as well as methodological differences related to using meta-analytical versus subject-level imaging data, and differences in pipelines, highlighting important open questions regarding the origins and implications of these differences. 

To advance understanding of cortical organization, future work should integrate dense MACM with other connectivity modalities, characterizing the unique and shared aspects captured by each approach, and aiming for a comprehensive multimodal mapping of the connectome. The planned release of this dense MACM dataset as an open resource (by June 2026) will enable researchers to perform such analyses and reuse this data for other applications in future studies.
