Introduction

The wide diversity and complexity of brain functions is supported by the biological diversity of its regions. This diversity is exhibited, for instance, in the neurochemical profile of the brain regions as measured by the density of neurotransmitter receptors and transporters (NR/NTs). However, their spatial distributions are heterogeneous across the brain, and the principles of brain neurochemical organization are not fully understood, especially when considering the interaction of many NR/NTs in a multivariate approach. Given that the neurochemical profile of the brain regions determine their function, examining how brain regions differ in their neurochemistry will advance our understanding of the mechanistic bases of brain function and behavior. One way to approach this gap is to study archetypes of neurochemistry, which can be done with the Pareto Task Inference (ParTI) method.1,2 ParTI examines the shape of the distribution of a low-dimensional representation (Principal Component Analysis, PCA) of the observed data. More specifically, it fits a polytope that succinctly captures the geometric shape of the observed data (in this case, neurochemical profiles of different brain regions). The vertices of this polytope correspond to specialized neurochemical profiles and represent archetypes. Hence, brain regions located close to archetypes are considered specialists, while regions located in the middle of the polytope are considered generalists. The examination of such specialized neurochemical profiles can shed light on the mechanisms underlying behavior, given that regions expressing specialized neurochemical phenotypes are expected to be associated with specialized functions. Here, we studied principles of brain neurochemical organization in humans by examining archetypes in the cortical distribution of NR/NTs. The identified patterns were characterized based on multiple features, including brain functional and structural organization, cognition and evolution.

Methods

We used Positron Emission Tomography (PET) data averaged over healthy subjects and openly available through neuromaps.3 We included 21 tracers for NR/NTs covering, among others, dopaminergic, serotoninergic and GABAergic systems (Fig 1a). To examine archetypes of the brain’s neurochemistry, 1000 cortical parcels (Schaefer atlas) were analyzed using ParTI (Fig 1b). Archetypal maps (Archs) were computed as a normalized proxy of the similarity of each brain region to each archetype in the ensuing PCA space (Fig 1c). Here, the normalized proxy of the similarity represented the reversed Euclidean distance and was computed as similarity=max(x)−x+min(x). Also, a generalist-specialist (GS) brain pattern was obtained using the highest similarity of each brain region to any archetype. In order to understand the neurochemical profiles characterizing the archetypes, the resulting Archs and GS maps were compared with all the PET maps using Spearman’s correlation (feature enrichment) (Fig 1d). In addition, the relevance of the Archs and GS maps was also examined with neurobiological characterization and cognitive decoding (Fig 1d). This was done by computing Spearman’s correlation between the Archs and GS maps with several neurobiological features of the brain (such as structural and functional organization, evolution (neuromaps3)), as well as with meta-analytic maps of cognitive tasks activation (neurosynth4). Here, positive associations indicate an increase in the biological feature as the regions are closer (more specialized) to the archetype, while negative associations indicate a decrease in the biological feature as the regions are closer (more specialized) to the archetype. Spin test5 was used to quantify the statistical significance of the correlations while accounting for spatial autocorrelation. Multiple comparisons were corrected to control the false discovery rate. The novelty of the brain organizational principals obtained with ParTI was analyzed by comparing the obtained brain maps with standard methods in the field (gradients from diffusion map embedding).

Results

A polytope with 5 vertices showed the best fit to the data and was statistically significant (p = 0.005), revealing 5 archetypes of brain neurochemistry (Fig 2a). Arch1 was located in the parahippocampal and lingual gyri (Fig 2b) and its neurochemical profile showed negative correlations with several NR/NTs, such as 5HT6, 5HT2A and M1 (Fig 2c). The neurocognitive relevance showed that as regions became more specialized for Arch1 the levels of blood flow, histone deacetylase and synaptic vesicle glycoprotein 2A (synapse marker) decreased (Fig 2c). Interestingly, as brain regions became more specialized for A1 their evolutionary areal expansion decreased (Fig 2c). Arch2 was located in the somatomotor cortex, lingual gyrus, and posterior cingulate cortex (Fig 2b). Similar to Arch1, the neurochemical profile of Arch2 also showed negative associations with several NR/NTs, including 5HT2A, 5HT1A and 5HT6 (Fig 2d). This map was associated with the sensory-association axis, T1w/T2w, and genetic expression (first principal component of the Allen Human Brain Atlas) (Fig 2d). Also, in line with Arch1, as brain regions became more specialized for Arch2 their areal expansion during evolution decreased (Fig 2d). In turn, Arch3 was focused on the primary visual cortex (Fig 2b), with a neurochemical profile showing positive associations with 5HT2A and GABAA and negative associations with VAChT and KOR (Fig 2e). This map showed associations with genetic expression, power distribution measured by magnetoencephalography (positive for alpha and negative for low gamma) and cognitive task activation associated with negative emotions (Fig 2e). Arch4 highlighted associative regions (including temporoparietal junction, middle frontal gyrus and middle temporal gyrus) (Fig 2b). Its neurochemical profile showed mostly positive associations, including mGluR5, 5HT2A and 5HT6, while its neurocognitive characterization showed alignment with the sensory-association axis and genetic expression (Fig 2f). In addition, as brain regions became more specialized for Arch4 their interindividual variability in resting state functional connectivity and evolutionary areal expansion increased, while their functional homology decreased (Fig 2f). Finally, Arch5 was located on insula and anterior cingulate cortex (Fig 2b). In line with Arch4, Arch5 showed mostly positive associations with NR/NTs, such as CB1, 5HT1A and GABAA5 (Fig 2g). Arch5 was aligned with the sensory-association axis and genetic expression and showed a positive association with cortical thickness and negative associations with T1w/T2w and functional homology (Fig 2g). The last map examined in the study, the GS map, highlighted all the specialist regions for all archetypes (Fig 2b), and revealed neurochemical profiles with mostly positive associations, for instance with 5HT6, 5HT2A and M1 (Fig 2h). This map showed associations with blood flow, the sensory association axis and histone deacetylase (Fig 2h). Also, the GS map was positively associated with area expansion during evolution (Fig 2h). Of note, the uncovered Archs did not fully align with gradients, and their neurocognitive profiles were different.

Conclusion

The examination of archetypes of brain neurochemistry revealed five Archs and one GS map, uncovering novel principles of brain organization. The Archs and GS maps explained variability in several features of the brain, including structural and functional organization, gene expression, evolution, and cognition. In other words, the uncovered maps indicated that different archetypes of neurochemical organization underlie different patterns of brain physiology and metabolism, brain structural organization, evolutionary signals, interindividual variability in resting state functional connectivity, and genetic expression. Altogether, our results reveal novel principles of brain organization based on neurochemistry and whose biological relevance is supported by multiple functional, cognitive, structural and evolutionary patterns. This study provides a comprehensive framework for the examination of how diverse organizational principles of the brain converge across multiple biological levels.
