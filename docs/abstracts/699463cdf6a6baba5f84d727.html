Introduction

Isolated REM-sleep behavior disorder (iRBD) confers a substantially elevated risk of developing Parkinson’s disease (PD), multiple system atrophy (MSA) or dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), indicating that it represents a prodromal form of neurodegenerative disease.1 At this prodromal stage, neuroprotective therapies have the potential to delay symptom onset. However, therapeutic developments are hindered by lack of knowledge concerning the time it takes for iRBD patients to phenoconvert, which is needed to determine optimal durations of clinical trials. A potential solution to this problem is to develop biomarkers that reliably estimate short-term phenoconversion risk in iRBD cohorts. Currently, the most established biomarker of phenoconversion in iRBD is the DaT scan, which quantifies dopamine transporter availability in the putamen using radioactive tracers. In this study, we investigated whether MRI-based measurements of an upstream mechanism of putamen dopamine deficits, namely loss of dopamine-producing cells in the substantia nigra (SN), can serve as less invasive alternatives to DaT scans when estimating phenoconversion risk in iRBD.

In PD, SN cell loss precedes time of diagnosis by several years, making it a viable marker of pathology in the prodromal stage.2 This loss can be studied non-invasively using structural MRI techniques. In particular, neuromelanin (NM)-sensitive3 and diffusion-weighted4 (DW) MRI provide sensitive markers of SN degeneration. However, a clear link with iRBD phenoconversion has not yet been established. Moreover, given that RBD is closely associated with midbrain pathology, phenoconversion times may be more reliably estimated when considering additional nuclei, particularly the locus coeruleus (LC).5

We tested whether structural MRI-based measurements of midbrain degeneration can identify and monitor phenoconversion risk in iRBD, using DaT scanning of putamen dopamine as a benchmark.

Methods

Fifty-eight polysomnography-confirmed iRBD patients from the ICEBERG cohort (Paris Brain Institute, Paris, France; clinicaltrials.gov identifier NCT02305147) were followed longitudinally and monitored for diagnostic re-evaluation between May 2015 and September 2025. Nineteen patients (33%) converted to PD (n=11), DLB (n=5), MSA (n=2), or Lewy body disease (n=1). At baseline, patients underwent a comprehensive neuroimaging protocol, including DaT single-photon emission tomography (SPECT) and multimodal structural MRI (NM and DW). DaT SPECT was performed using the 123I-FP-CIT (DaTScanTM) tracer, followed by extraction of standardized uptake ratios (SBR) from the bilateral putamen relative to the lateral occipital cortex. MRI modalities focused on assessing the structural integrity of midbrain nuclei using well-validated analytical pipelines: NM MRI was used to extract signal-to-noise ratios from automated segmentations of the SN and LC, whereas multi-shell DW MRI was used to estimate free water in the posterior SN. Fourteen converters and 16 non-converters had usable imaging data from all imaging modalities. Binomial logistic regression was used to test whether imaging metrics distinguished converters from non-converters, with classification performance measured using receiver-operating characteristics and area-under-the-curve (AUC). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and Cox proportional hazards regression were used to test for associations between imaging metrics and phenoconversion rates. Principal component analysis, applied to MRI metrics with and without DaT, was used to explore the complementary value of measurements.

Results

Putamen dopamine. Reduced putamen SBR distinguished converters from non-converters (χ2[1]=14.13, P<0.001; AUC=0.83) and was associated with elevated phenoconversion risk (adjusted HR=0.20, 95%CI=[0.07-0.58], P=0.003).

Midbrain cell loss. Reduced SN NM SNR distinguished converters from non-converters (χ2[1]=5.52, P=0.019; AUC=0.74), as did SN FW (χ2[1]=8.03, P=0.005; AUC=0.73). NM SNR in the LC showed the same pattern, but at trend level (χ2[1]=3.44, P<0.064; AUC=0.68). All three MRI measurements were associated with time to phenoconversion (SN NM SNR: adjusted HR=0.67, 95%CI=[0.52-0.86], P=0.002; SN FW: adjusted HR=421.20, 95%CI=[1.00-1.77e+05], P=0.049; LC NM SNR: adjusted HR=0.80, 95%CI=[0.67-0.96], P=0.015).

The first principal component derived from all MRI metrics together yielded a slight improvement in classification performance. It distinguished converters from non-converters (Figure 1, top left and right. χ2[1]=10.63, P=0.001; AUC=0.79) and associated with time to phenoconversion (Figure 1, bottom left. adjusted HR=1.71, 95%CI=[1.13-2.58], P=0.011).

Structural MRI combined with DaT SPECT. The first principal component derived from MRI metrics combined with DaT showed improved performance relative to DaT in isolation. It more clearly distinguished converters from non-converters (Figure 1, top left and right. χ2[1]=16.65, P=0<0.001; AUC=0.88) and associated more robustly with time to phenoconversion (Figure 1, bottom right. adjusted HR=1.94, 95%CI=[1.28-2.95], P=0.002). There was a trend towards a significant increase in AUC when MRI metrics were combined with DaT compared to when only MRI metrics were used (D=1.62, P=0.052).

Conclusion

Our findings indicate that higher levels of midbrain degeneration, quantified using widely available structural MRI protocols, confer increased risk of phenoconversion in iRBD, albeit with slightly less precision compared to putamen DaT SPECT. This supports using structural MRI of midbrain nuclei as an alternative to DaT SPECT when estimating phenoconversion risk in clinical trials involving iRBD cohorts.
