Introduction

Approximately 332 million people worldwide are affected by depression,1 including an estimated 21 million adults in the United States (8.3%) with major depressive disorder (MDD).2 MDD imposes a substantial personal and societal burden, contributing $29–$48 billion annually to the national economic cost.3 Although repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) is an effective treatment for MDD,4,5 the neural mechanisms underlying its therapeutic effects, particularly those related to cognitive flexibility (CF) and emotion regulation (ER), remain poorly understood. Disruptions to CF and ER processes may contribute to persistent depressive symptoms and variability in treatment outcomes, highlighting the importance of studying network changes linked to rTMS.

In this study, we examined associations between rTMS treatment outcomes and changes in both the capacity to recruit CF and ER circuits during trained tasks, as well as the spontaneous engagement of these networks during an autobiographical memory task. Using trained decoders and decoder outputs from fMRI, we quantified CF and ER network engagement time-locked to task cues, enabling us to assess how these networks are spontaneously recruited after negative memories, including during subsequent tasks that require sustained attention. We further evaluated whether pre- to post-treatment changes in network engagement were associated with clinical outcomes.

Understanding how rTMS treatment has the potential to modulate both capacity and spontaneous engagement of CF and ER circuitry may inform more precise treatment targeting and improve prediction of clinical response in MDD.

Methods

All data was collected and treatments were performed at the Medical University of South Carolina under an IRB-approved protocol. In this study, twenty individuals with major depressive disorder (MDD) at elevated suicide risks were recruited to participate in a personalized, EEG-synchronized rTMS treatment protocol. One participant withdrew voluntarily and was excluded from all analyses. An overview of the methodology is described in Figure 1.

Depressive symptom severity was assessed before and after treatment using clinician- and self-reported measures: the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17) and the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9). To assess both the capacity to engage cognitive networks and their spontaneous recruitment, participants completed three functional MRI (fMRI) tasks before and after treatment: (1) a CF task adapted from Armbruster et al., (2) an ER task adapted from Ochsner et al., and (3) an autobiographical memory (ABM) task.

The CF task comprised three trial types designed to probe distinct processes: ongoing trials assessed non-interference, distractor trials indexed cognitive stability, and switch trials measured cognitive flexibility. The ER task similarly included three trial types to evaluate regulatory capacity: viewing neutral images for baseline affect, viewing negative images to elicit negative emotion, and reappraising negative images to assess emotion regulation. The ABM task was used to examine the spontaneous engagement of CF and ER networks, presenting participants with negative, neutral, and positive autobiographical memories, followed by cues to either immerse or distance themselves from the memory, or perform a sustained attention to response task (SART).

For the CF task, reaction times and accuracies were analyzed using a two-way ANOVA with post hoc comparisons. Subjects whose accuracies fell more than three standard deviations from the group mean were excluded from further analyses. One subject was excluded due to outlier accuracy.

We first trained decoders on the CF and ER capacity tasks using a linear regression model with ElasticNet regularization. The CF decoder was trained on a predefined functional connectivity mask to differentiate between distractor and switch trials, thereby capturing CF network engagement. The ER decoder, on the other hand, was trained to differentiate between look-negative and reappraise-negative trials, thereby capturing ER network engagement. Decoders were trained individually for each participant and separately for pre- and post-treatment sessions, using a 5-fold cross-validation. Decoding performance was evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic area under the curve (ROC‑AUC). 

After training, each participant’s CF and ER decoders were applied to the ABM task to generate decoder outputs for each TR, reflecting the degree of CF or ER network engagement over time. Higher decoder output values indicated stronger engagement of the corresponding network. We examined the decoder outputs for the TRs immediately preceding and following the SART cue to assess how CF and ER networks were recruited following negative memories. This time-locked analysis provided a measure of spontaneous CF and ER network engagement during the subsequent task period, capturing how individuals naturally recruit these networks when regulating attention in response to emotionally salient stimuli. Pre- and post-treatment sessions were compared to characterize changes in spontaneous network engagement and to examine their associations with clinical outcomes. Partial correlation analyses were conducted to assess relationships between changes in decoder outputs and changes in PHQ-9 and HAMD-17 scores following rTMS treatment.

This approach allowed us to capture both the capacity to recruit CF/ER networks (from the trained tasks) and the spontaneous engagement of these networks in a different, contextually rich task, reflecting trait-like patterns of network recruitment.

Results

An overview of the results is presented in Figure 2.

Clinical Outcome Changes Pre- to Post-Treatment

Pre- to post-treatment comparisons on the PHQ-9 and HAMD-17 revealed significant improvements. Participants showed substantial reductions in depressive symptoms, with PHQ‑9 scores (t(18) = 13.47, p < 0.001) and HAMD‑17 scores (t(18) = 22.69, p < 0.001). These findings indicate that rTMS treatment was associated with robust reductions in depressive symptom severity.

Behavioral Task Performance

A two-way ANOVA with factors task condition (ongoing, distractor, switch) and session (pre, post) was conducted separately for accuracy and reaction time. The analysis revealed a significant main effect of task condition, F(2, 104) = 9.45, p = 0.0002, and a significant main effect of session, F(1, 104) = 8.32, p = 0.0048. The task condition × session interaction was not significant, F(2, 104) = 1.27, p = 0.28. Post hoc comparisons indicated that accuracy was higher for ongoing trials than for distractor trials (p = 0.033) and switch trials (p < 0.001), whereas accuracy did not differ significantly between distractor and switch trials (p = 0.062). Accuracy was also higher post-treatment than pre-treatment (p = 0.0086).

For reaction time, there was a significant main effect of task condition, F(2, 104) = 37.60, p < 0.001, but no significant main effect of session, F(1, 104) = 2.25, p = 0.137, and no task condition × session interaction, F(2, 104) = 0.07, p = 0.931. Post hoc comparisons revealed that reaction times were significantly longer for switch trials than for both ongoing (p < 0.001) and distractor trials (p < 0.001), and longer for distractor trials than for ongoing trials (p = 0.003).

Decoder Training Performance

In total, we trained 18 individuals’ pre- and post-treatment decoders for the CF task, and 19 individuals’ pre- and post-treatment decoders for the ER task. The CF task did not show a significant improvement in decoder training accuracy pre- to post-treatment (t(17) = -1.55, p = 0.14), but almost all subjects showed improvement in decoding ROC‑AUC for the Ochsner task (t(18) = -2.30, p = 0.034).

Correlations Between Network Engagement and Clinical Outcomes

We examined the relationship between changes in spontaneous engagement of ER and CF networks during periods of SART that followed negative memories and changes in clinical scores using partial correlations. No significant associations were observed for CF network engagement with either changes in HAMD‑17 (r = 0.26, p = 0.31) or changes in PHQ‑9 (r = 0.26, p = 0.26), suggesting that changes in CF network engagement were not robustly related to depressive symptom changes. In contrast, ER network engagement showed significant negative associations with symptom change: changes in HAMD‑17 were negatively correlated with ER changes (r = -0.62, p = 0.006), indicating that greater reductions in depressive symptoms were associated with larger spontaneous engagement in ER networks during SART following a negative memory. Similarly, changes in PHQ‑9 scores were negatively correlated with ER changes (r = -0.68, p = 0.002), suggesting that participants with larger decreases in self-reported depressive symptoms showed greater increases in ER network engagement.

Conclusion

Overall, our results demonstrate that rTMS treatment in MDD is associated with improvements across behavioral, neural, and clinical domains. Behavioral analyses revealed that task conditions strongly influenced performance, with switch trials eliciting slower and less accurate responses. Overall, improvements in accuracy were observed after rTMS across all trials. These findings suggest that rTMS may enhance cognitive processes related to task flexibility and attentional control. However, it is important to note that this study did not include a control group, so some observed improvements could reflect practice effects rather than treatment-specific effects.

Decoder analyses revealed that greater clinical improvement was associated with increased spontaneous engagement of ER networks during SART periods following the recall of negative memories. Specifically, individuals who showed larger reductions in depressive symptoms, as measured by HAMD-17 and PHQ-9 scores, exhibited stronger ER network recruitment during a task that required sustained attention, rather than explicit instruction to regulate emotion. In contrast, spontaneous engagement of CF networks during the same post-memory SART periods was not significantly related to clinical outcomes, suggesting that improvements in depressive symptoms were not driven by changes in flexible cognitive control in this context. Together, these findings suggest that symptom improvement is more closely associated with the spontaneous recruitment of ER circuitry in a naturalistic task setting, rather than task-evoked or explicitly instructed regulation processes. Consistent with this interpretation, decoder training performance showed post-treatment gains for ER task decoders but not CF task decoders, suggesting that ER networks may be particularly sensitive to rTMS-related modulation. Further validation is needed to clarify the specific neural processes that these decoder-based measures of network engagement capture.

Taken together, these findings suggest that spontaneous ER network engagement may serve as a meaningful neural marker of individual treatment response, offering insight into the mechanisms underlying symptom improvement following rTMS. Rather than reflecting enhanced performance under explicit regulatory demands, clinical improvement was associated with greater ER network recruitment in contexts that more closely resemble everyday cognitive demands, such as sustained attention following negative memory recall. Future work should extend these findings by examining whether changes in spontaneous ER network engagement generalize across tasks and time points, and whether combining decoder-based neural measures with behavioral and clinical data can improve the characterization of individual treatment trajectories.
