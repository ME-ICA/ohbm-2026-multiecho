Introduction

Schizophrenia is a severe psychiatric disorder with a prevalence of about 1%. Typically emerging in late adolescence, it is characterised by heterogeneity in its symptoms, such as positive (e.g. delusions and hallucinations), negative (e.g. anhedonia and social withdrawal) and cognitive (e.g. impairments in memory and executive functioning) symptoms, which are paralleled by heterogeneity in its underlying brain pathophysiology. This includes, but is not limited to, impaired neurodevelopment, neuroinflammatory mechanisms, aberrant neurotransmitter systems and structural brain alterations1. One main finding of neuroimaging studies across patients with schizophrenia is widespread dysconnectivity between cortical brain regions, with studies reporting on alterations of brain networks measured by resting-state functional MRI (fMRI) as well as aberrant structural connectivity measured by diffusion MRI (DWI), underpinned by alterations in microstructural integrity of white matter2. Fewer studies have also focused on cortical connectivity of subcortical regions such as thalamic nuclei or the striatum and observed alterations in patients; however, whether connections between the subcortical claustrum and the cortex are impaired in schizophrenia remains unclear. The claustrum, a small grey matter structure located between the insula and putamen, is one of the most highly connected regions by volume of the human brain3. It plays an important role in regulating cortical activity and is proposed to be involved in vital functions such as attention, slow-wave sleep and memory consolidation4. Previously, we reported that lowered claustrum volumes in patients with chronic schizophrenia are linked to attentional deficits5. The current study aims to test whether volume and structural connectivity are altered in early schizophrenia, and whether these changes are linked to specific cognitive deficits, including impairments in attention, executive functioning, and memory. 

Methods

All MRI data analysed in this study had been made available via the Human Connectome Project for Early Psychosis (HCP-EP) Release 1.1. Subjects included healthy controls (HC) as well as patients who had been diagnosed with schizophrenia with an onset at most five years prior (E-SCZ). Notably, we excluded all subjects in the affective psychosis group (i.e., subjects with major depressive disorder or bipolar disorder). The final sample included 48 age- and sex-matched subjects each in the HC group and E-SCZ group (n=96). Structural, T1-weighted MRI data (MPRAGE, 0.8mm isotropic resolution) and DWI data (1.5mm isotropic resolution, 46 directions each of b1500 and b3000 s/mm2, 7 images of b0) had been acquired using Siemens 3T scanners at three clinical sites. Cognitive assessments had been conducted at the same study sites. After preprocessing of the structural images using FSL’s Anatomical Processing Script (fsl_anat), we estimated total intracranial volume (TIV) using the registration matrix and denoised the images in MATLAB using an Adaptive Optimised Nonlocal Means filter. The claustrum was segmented using an in-house pre-trained deep learning model; its volume was normalised by dividing byTIV for further analysis. Masks of cortical regions-of-interests (ROIs) were generated based on the Harvard-Oxford cortical atlas. Diffusion-weighted images were corrected for susceptibility-induced distortions (utilising a b0 image acquired with reverse phase encoding), motion and eddy currents using FSL topup and eddy. All MRI data was visually inspected to exclude subjects with insufficient image quality. Fractional anisotropy (FA) and mean diffusivity (MD) maps were fitted using a diffusion tensor model including a kurtosis option for multi-shell data with FSL dtifit. Fibre distribution was estimated using a ball-and-stick model using FSL bedpostx, and probabilistic tractography was run between the claustrum and ipsilateral cortical ROIs using FSL probtrackx. Connectivity outcomes were calculated by dividing the number of streamlines arriving at the selected ROI by the total number of streamlines connecting the claustrum to all cortical ROIs in the same hemisphere, resulting in a measure of claustrum-ROI connectivity normalised by both claustrum volume as well as global per-subject claustrum-cortical connectivity. Prior to statistical analysis, we controlled for possible site effects in the MRI data using neuroCombat. Outliers were removed using the IQR method. Group effects on claustrum structure and connectivity were computed using parametric and non-parametric t-tests. Correlations with cognitive performance in the patient group were calculated with Spearman rank correlations. 

Results

Claustrum volumes normalised by TIV were significantly lower in early schizophrenia compared to healthy controls (Welch’s t-test, p<0.01, Cohen’s d: 0.45). On average, patients had lower claustrum volumes of around 7.9% compared to controls. Overall, the claustrum was most highly connected to the prefrontal cortex (PFC) with group means of normalised connection probabilities of 0.446 and 0.468 in the control and patient groups, respectively. Claustrum connectivity to this region was significantly higher in patients compared to controls (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, pFDR<0.05, Cliff’s d: 0.345). In contrast, claustrum connectivity in patients was lower to both temporal association cortex (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p<0.01 and pFDR=0.053, Cliff’s d: -0.307) and primary auditory cortex (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p<0.05 and pFDR=0.086, Cliff’s d: -0.273), however, the effects did not remain significant after correcting for multiple comparisons. In the patient group, performance on episodic memory assessments (picture sequence memory test) was positively correlated with claustrum volume (Spearman rank correlation, rho=0.382, pFDR<0.05). Neither fluid intelligence (matrix reasoning score of WASI-II), working memory (list sorting working memory test), executive functioning (dimensional change card sorting test), nor attention and response inhibition (Flanker task) scores were significantly correlated with changes in MRI outcomes in the E-SCZ group.

Conclusion

Our results demonstrate that claustrum structure and connectivity are altered in early schizophrenia. 

Firstly, our results demonstrate, on average, a 7.9% smaller claustrum volume in patients with early schizophrenia. While this number is lower than the 13% volume reduction reported previously in chronic schizophrenia5, this could indicate that claustrum structural alterations in schizophrenia are linked to longer disease progression. To better understand the possibly dynamic changes in claustrum volume during the course of schizophrenia, further studies are needed to address (1) whether claustrum alterations are already present before disease onset, i.e. in a prodromal stage, and (2) the possible effect of antipsychotic medication use on claustrum volume. 

Secondly, the results show a significant change in the distribution of claustrum-cortex structural connections in patients with early schizophrenia. In particular, while connectivity between the claustrum and PFC is increased, connectivity between the claustrum and temporal association cortex as well as primary auditory cortex are decreased. Notably, altered functioning of the PFC has been linked to negative symptoms and cognitive deficits in schizophrenia1. Meanwhile, feedforward inhibition of the cortex by the claustrum via activation of inhibitory interneurons has been proposed as a key mechanism in its regulation of cortical activity4. However, due to its technical limitations, it is not possible to ascertain the directionality of altered claustrum-cortex connections or detect cellular-molecular systems underlying dysconnectivity using DWI alone. Additional studies using techniques such as neuroimaging transcriptomics or neurite orientation dispersion and density imaging (NODDI) are required to detect molecular candidates in grey and white matter underlying dysconnectivity in schizophrenia.

Thirdly, we found an association between impaired episodic memory, as assessed by the picture sequence memory test, and claustrum volume in patients with early schizophrenia. Schizophrenia is characterised by marked deficits in cognitive functioning, including learning and memory1. At the same time, through its regulation of slow-wave sleep, the claustrum could potentially play a role in learning and memory consolidation4. Schizophrenia is also associated with decreased performance in tasks involving domains such as attention1, which was found to be mediated by lowered claustrum volumes in chronic schizophrenia5, yet the current study did not find correlations between attentional deficits and changes in structure or connectivity of the claustrum in the current study. This could, however, possibly be attributed to different tasks used to assess attention or due to differences in cohorts and stage of the disorder.  

In conclusion, the current study further strengthens the notion that, despite its small size, the claustrum is a potentially important region of the brain underlying cognitive deficits in schizophrenia. Additionally, our findings suggest that claustrum structural changes emerge early in the disorder and are accompanied by aberrant dysconnectivity to cortical regions important for cognitive functioning.
