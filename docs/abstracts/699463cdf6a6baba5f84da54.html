Introduction

Increases in social affiliation can impact one's internal mental state, specifically by modulating the patterns of mind-wandering, which in turn influences subsequent emotional and behavioral states. It was previously reported that Social Imitation (SI) can modulate a person's subsequent mind-wandering patterns and content. When individuals imitate another's actions, the temporal dynamics and content of their subsequent resting-state mind-wandering are altered.1

The Default Mode Network (DMN) of the brain is intimately linked to mind-wandering, as multiple studies have observed its engagement during mind-wandering and spontaneous thought processing, such as self-reflection and perceiving and inferring others’ intentions.

Based on spatial clustering analysis, electroencephalography (EEG) microstates (short (60-120 ms), quasi-stable states of scalp potential topographies of large-scale neural ensembles) have been used to examine the temporal dynamics of large-scale resting networks across socio-affective and cognitive states in both non-clinical and clinical populations to identify biomarkers of brain disorders. EEG microstates are known to involve the auditory network (A), visual network (B), saliency network (C), the attention network (D), the medial prefrontal cortex (E), the precuneus, anterior and posterior cingulate cortex (F), and the sensorimotor network (G).2

In a previous study, it was reported that engaging in SI can prolong the duration of EEG microstate C and reduce the duration of microstate D; two brain states associated with the DMN and Central Executive Networks, respectively.1 While that study focused on post vs pre SI tasks, in this study, we further assess changes in microstate dynamics that occur during the dyadic social imitation tasks, to better understand the neuro-hormonal mechanism that might involve CEN and DMN and its beneficial effects during SI. And if changes during SI were moderated by oxytocin (OXT) and emotion regulation ability.

Methods

We analyzed 128-channel EEG recordings from 42 young volunteers who engaged in a 3-minute dyadic SI task, in which they imitated an actor's arm movements to induce behavioral synchrony. We compared the SI with a non-social imitation (CTRL) task, which involved imitating the geometry of figures displayed on a computer screen. Salivary OXT samples were collected from the participants before and after the tasks to measure hormonal changes associated with social imitation. After the imitation task, participants completed the Amsterdam Resting-State Questionnaire (ARSQ) which assesses various dimensions of mind-wandering, such as "Discontinuity of Mind", "Self", "Comfort", “Somatic Awareness”, etc., the Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS) questionnaire, with differnt facets of emotionsl regulation such as:  “Goals”, “Impulse”, “Awareness”, and “Clarity”, and the visual analogue scale (VAS) which assessed the perceived stress.

The EEG data were processed in the following order: they were first band-pass filtered between 1 and 40Hz with an additional notch at 50Hz; after performing an artefact removal process, detailed below, we applied interpolation of noisy electrodes using a 3D spherical spline, down-sampled the recordings to 125Hz, and finally, recomputed the EEG to average-reference.

The EEG samples recorded during active movement required a two-step process to accurately remove oculomotor artefacts, including eye blinks, cardiac artefacts, and saccades. To aid in our artefact removal process we first used the GEDAI plugin from MATLAB EEGLAB toolkit (https://eeglab.org/), which employs leadfield filtering to remove any noise and artefacts that diverge from a theoretically defined EEG forward model.3 Using this model, we were able to clean the EEG recordings of most of the eye blinks, cardiac artefacts, muscle and movement artefacts, and saccades. The remaining artefacts were removed using the AMICA (Adaptive Mixture Independent Component Analysis) plugin in the same MATLAB toolkit, which performs independent component analysis (ICA) on the resulting GEDAI data.4 For GEDAI, we used the default parameters as recommended by the authors. For AMICA, we used the Generalized Gaussian model, rejecting unlikely data samples with “num reject”=7, “num LL stdev”=3, “start iter”=2, and “iter btwn rej”=3. The final recordings were then manually marked of any artefacts that might have been missed.

For microstate analysis, we first performed a K-means clustering at the individual level, using only the global field power (GFP) peaks in the clustering algorithm, with an optimal cluster number of 7. The individual clustering calculation was performed using the “pycrostates” Python package (https://github.com/vferat/pycrostates/). The resulting individual topographies were then used as input to a second clustering algorithm at the group level to identify the most representative topographic classes. From the final group clustering results, we fitted the seven microstate classes conventionally labelled as A-G, which explained 56% of the variance and labelled 80% of the total time frames. We applied temporal smoothing (window half-size of 3 = 24ms), a Besag factor of 10, rejection of small (< 3 = 24ms) time frames, and a 0.7 correlation coefficient threshold to exclude noisy periods in the data. The fitting process microstates, temporal parameters, and statistics on mean duration (ms), time coverage (%), global explained variance (GEV), and the occurrence (Hz) for each microstate and subject. All preprocessing steps (except artefact removal) and the microstate analysis were conducted using the Cartool EEG Software (cartool.unige.ch), developed by Denis Brunet.

Results

The fitting process generated seven microstates, which we labeled A-G, based on a comparison with three studies from the literature (Figure 1) alphabetically using the MS Template Explorer developed by Thomas Koenig for the EEGLAB toolbox (https://github.com/ThomasKoenigBern/MS-Template-Explorer). The first two microstates, A-B, had the highest similarity scores with the microstates labeled A-B by Custo et al. Interestingly, microstate C had the highest similarity score, 98.7%, with microstate C from Tomescu et al., which was generated using the pre- and post-data of the same EEG dataset. And the last four microstates D-G had the highest similarity scores with the microstates labeled D-G by Tarailis et al.5

Of the seven microstates, D, E, and G showed statistically significant differences between the social imitation task and the control task (Figure 2A). Microstate D showed a significant decrease during the SI task across all calculated metrics: mean duration (Wilcoxon p=0.037), time coverage (Wilcoxon p=0.027), GEV (Wilcoxon p=0.034), and occurrence (Wilcoxon p=0.034) compared with the non-social CTRL task. Microstate G time coverage (Wilcoxon p=0.032), GEV (Wilcoxon p=0.012), and occurrence (Wilcoxon p=0.029) were significantly decreased during the SI task than during CTRL. Microstate E showed a significant increasing trend, with higher observed occurrence during the SI task (Wilcoxon, p=0.043) when compared to Microstate E, which showed a significant increase, with a higher observed occurrence during the SI task (Wilcoxon, p=0.043) than in CTRL.

In addition to differences in temporal microstate dynamics, we conducted moderation analyses to examine the relationships among salivary oxytocin levels, microstate dynamics, and participants' affective states and mind-wandering content and dynamics (Figure 2B). We observed that oxytocin levels moderate the relationship between multiple ARSQ measures, stress levels, and microstates C, E, F, and G. For example, oxytocin levels after the SI task moderate the correlation between the occurrence and time coverage of microstate C with the “Self” scores reported by the participants after the SI task; the occurrence and time coverage of microstate E with the reported stress levels; the occurrence, time coverage, and GEV of microstate F and the “Somatic Awareness” scores, and the occurrence of microstate G and the “Discontinuity of mind” scores reported by the participants after the SI task. More specifically, in participants with low oxytocin levels after the SI task, low occurrence of microstate G during SI predicts decreased discontinuity of mind after SI (p=0.012), while higher levels of microstate F GEV (p<.001), occurrence (p<.001), and time coverage (p<.001) can predict higher levels of Somatic-related thinking in the participants after the SI task. Moreover, in participants with smaller changes in oxytocin levels after the SI task, the lower occurrence of microstate E (p=0.012) predicts lower stress levels reported after the SI task. 

We also observed a moderating effect of oxytocin levels on the relationship between microstate C occurrence and Self-related thoughts reported after the SI task: in participants with smaller oxytocin changes after the SI task, higher microstate C occurrence predicted reduced Self-related thoughts (p=0.045).

Finally, we found that specific DERS measures can moderate the relation between microstate D and various behavioral aspects. More specifically, we observed that, among participants with high ability to understand their emotions (“Clarity”), a shorter duration of microstate D was associated with higher comfort levels (p=0.004). In contrast, a higher frequency of microstate D was associated with greater sleepiness (p < .001). In participants with a low ability for behavioral control (“Impulse”), higher occurrence of microstate D was associated with higher levels of “Discontinuity of Mind” scores (p=0.065).

Conclusion

We have observed that microstates D, E, and G change significantly during SI as compared to non-social imitation tasks. Microstate D and G essentially decreased while Microstate E increased during SI.

In a previous study comparing the temporal dynamics of microstates before and after the SI task, the occurrence of microstate D decreased after the SI task.1 This decrease was also observed in the current study, confirming that the activity of microstate D decreases during SI. This might point to microstate D as an indicator for the therapeutic effect of social imitation, but further investigations on larger sample sizes are needed. 

We also found that OXT levels can serve as key moderators of the relationship between EEG microstates and specific mental outcomes, including stress, self-related thoughts, and somatic awareness. Specifically, low oxytocin levels moderate the relationship between EEG microstates and the feelings of disconnected mind, self-related thinking, stress, and somatic awareness. When oxytocin is low, low activity in microstates F and G can predict less somatic thinking and less chaotic mind-wandering after SI. In individuals with lower changes in oxytocin levels, lower C and E activity is associated with lower stress and lower self-related thinking. 

Microstate G is regarded as related to the sensorimotor network. Tarailis et al. hypothesize that microstate G reflects well-being arising from appropriate somatosensory activation.5 In our study, when OXT acts as a moderator, and the oxytocin levels are low, decreased microstate G activity during SI predicts a lower discontinuity of mind. This result may indicate a beneficial effect of SI, as it reduces spontaneous thought processing in low-OXT modulators.

Moreover, we observed that emotional regulation abilities interact with microstates to predict behavioral and cognitive states. In participants with better emotion regulation and impulse control, a low microstate D activity can predict lower discontinuity of mind. We also observed that, among individuals with more emotional clarity, lower D activity predicts higher sleepiness levels. Together, these results suggest that individuals with better emotion regulation might benefit more from the positive effect of SI via decreased D microstate.

Ultimately, the results suggest that social brain activity might be shaped by an individual's hormonal state and psychological traits. These results extend previous findings by showing that social imitation not only precedes changes in resting-state dynamics but also actively modulates them during the social task itself. The differential activity in Microstates D and E supports the hypothesis that SI involves a reciprocal modulation between CEN (associated with D) and networks linked to the DMN (associated with E and possibly C).

We consider these results relevant not only to future microstate analyses of EEG recordings of dyadic social interaction, but also for future studies investigating the complex interplay between neuro-hormonal mechanisms, large-scale neural network dynamics, and socio-affective processing. Although further research is needed to further confirm the neuro-hormonal mechanisms underlying the therapeutic benefits of social imitation, our results support the neurocognitive benefits of SI.
