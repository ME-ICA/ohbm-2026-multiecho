Introduction

Idiopathic normal pressure hydrocephalus (iNPH) is the most common reversible dementia in older adults and is characterized by ventriculomegaly with gait, cognitive, and urinary disturbances. Disrupted white‑matter (WM) integrity is well documented in iNPH, but most diffusion MRI studies have used voxel-based approaches, such as tract-based spatial statistics1. Because iNPH affects widespread WM pathways through mechanical and CSF‑related mechanisms, a connectome-level perspective is needed to understand how large-scale structural communication is altered2. Network-based analyses of gray matter morphology have revealed abnormal structural network organization in iNPH3, yet WM connectomes remain largely unexplored. This study tests whether FA‑weighted structural connectomes reveal network‑specific alterations that extends beyond periventricular damage.

Methods

iNPH patients (n = 26; age 80.1 ± 5.9y; 15M/11F) and healthy controls (HCs; n = 41; age 74.9 ± 5.4y; 11M/30F) were recruited at Geneva University Hospitals between 2017 and 2020. All participants underwent T1‑weighted and diffusion MRI on a 3T scanner. Diffusion data were processed with MRtrix3 to obtain fractional anisotropy (FA) maps from tensor fitting. FA‑weighted structural connectomes were generated by sampling mean FA along streamlines from the HCPA‑422 tractogram atlas and aggregating values with respect to 360 cortical regions (Glasser parcellation) and 14 subcortical regions, ensuring that the same anatomical pathways were considered across participants. This resulted in individual 374x374 connectivity matrices weighted by the mean FA along connecting streamlines. Edge-wise group differences in FA were assessed with univariate Student’s t-test controlling for age, sex, and education. Family wise error rate was controlled at the connected component level using network‑based statistics (NBS; q=0.05). For each node, we computed a dominance score (relative percentage of edges showing significantly increased vs decreased FA in patients) and the percentage of significant edges relative to the node degree, capturing the direction and burden of change. Nodes were assigned to resting‑state networks (RSNs) to summarize structural alterations in a functionally defined network space.

Results

Edge-wise analyses revealed widespread FA alterations, with 6.15% of all edges surviving NBS multiple comparison correction and most showing increased FA in patients (74.65% of significant edges), alongside smaller clusters of decreased FA (25.35%). Nodal dominance maps showed that lateral posterior regions, including occipital, posterior temporal, and inferior parietal cortices, were predominantly involved in edges with increased FA in iNPH compared to HCs, while more focal clusters of decreased FA appeared in left medial occipito‑parietal and right ventral temporal areas, indicating heterogeneous remodeling of posterior association networks with extensive FA increases combined with localized FA loss. The proportion of significant edges was highest in posterior and medial parietal regions, with additional hotspots in dorsal occipital and superior temporal areas, suggesting that these posterior association cortices carry the greatest burden of WM remodeling. At the RSN level, dominance scores were highest for visual central, dorsal attention, temporo‑parietal, and control networks, indicating that their connections most consistently showed FA increases in iNPH.

Conclusion

FA‑derived structural connectomes reveal that iNPH is associated with widespread yet spatially organized WM disruptions, with predominant FA increases in lateral posterior cortices and more focal FA decreases in medial and ventral regions. The coexistence of these opposite effects aligns with prior diffusion studies reporting distinctive spatial patterns of WM change in iNPH4. It suggests that FA increases may reflect a combination of compensatory reorganization and mechanical compression of posterior and periventricular pathways, whose alteration can propagate broadly through the connectome. Connectome‑level metrics at the edge, node, and RSN level could improve biomarkers of network vulnerability and complement newer microstructural markers from quantitative relaxometry and susceptibility mapping in iNPH5. A key limitation of this work is the use of an atlas‑defined tractogram, which facilitates between‑subject comparability but reduces tract specificity and may miss specific WM variations. Future work should move toward subject‑specific tractograms, refined tract definitions, and combine these structural measures with detailed cognitive and behavioral profiling and resting‑state fMRI to link structural changes to network dynamics.
