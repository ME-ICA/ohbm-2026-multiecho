Introduction

Dynamic functional connectivity is a method of studying brain networks in transition (Hutchison et al., 2013). It is commonly applied to resting state fMRI. Brain network function can be guided by carefully controlled task paradigms (Diwadkar & Eickhoff, 2021). Applications of DFC are therefore valuable to the understanding of how brain network states undergo transitions induced by changing task conditions. Here, we apply DFC to fMRI data obtained from a group of schizophrenia patients (SCZ) and healthy controls (HC). This is notable in SCZ due to its known effect of creating disordered network dynamics (Baajour et al., 2020).

Methods

Subjects (n=78, 39 SCZ) were recruited from the Detroit area. All subjects were required to perform an established memory formation task (Ravishankar et al., 2019) which cycled between states of Encoding (27s) , subsequent Rest (27s), Retrieval (27s), subsequent Rest (27s). During Encoding, subjects were presented with a 3x3 grid within which images of monosyllabic objects appeared momentarily and one at a time. During both Rest periods the subjects were presented with a black screen and a static white fixation cross “+”. During Retrieval subjects were again presented with the grid. Here, locations in the grid were highlighted momentarily and one-by-one, prompting the subject to declare aloud the object they recalled to be in that location. This cycle was repeated 8 times, object-locations associations remaining constant, with the order of encoding-presentation and retrieval-prompting being random. The goal being for the subjects to successfully learn where each object is located by the end of the task, a total of 864 seconds. fMRI data (3T Siemens Verio) were obtained throughout.

Average time series (TR=3, length=288) were extracted from each of 90 bilateral (AAL) regions from each subject. Within each subject, the following analysis was implemented. Each pair of regions underwent a DFC analysis with a window length of 9 timepoints (matching the length of each task condition) and an overlap of 1 timepoint, potentially maximizing the dissimilarity between adjacent points of the resulting DFC timeseries (length=37). This resulted in 4,005 DFC timeseries per subject. Over all subjects from both groups, this resulted in a pool of 312,390 DFC timeseries. Each of these represents the coherence of a pair of regions within some subject over the course of the task.

All of these DFC timeseries were then forwarded to an unsupervised clustering algorithm called Partitioning Around Medoids (PAM) (Kaufman & Rousseeuw, 1994), a method of clustering wherein the clusters can be defined by their most central element (the medoid) and all elements closest to that medoid vs other medoids. The distance measurement for this analysis was Dynamic Time Warping, a non-euclidean representation of similarity between time series (Berndt & Clifford, 1994). Resultant clusters were investigated for SCZ:HC membership ratios, AAL region-pair composition, and relation to clinical measures in the SCZ group.

Results

After partitioning our data into ten clusters via the above described methods, there are some discernible patterns. Figure 1 is organized such that the columns show data about each cluster from largest to smallest, left to right. The first row shows each cluster’s DFC trend frequency map. In these frequency maps, the X axis is the window within which FC was calculated (37 windows), the Y axis is the Pearson’s r within that window (-1 to 1). The second row (HC) and third row (SCZ) of Figure 1 show the representation frequency of AAL region-pairs within that cluster. AAL regions are ordered anterior to posterior (top to bottom) and separated by memory network (top), non-memory network regions (bottom) (see appendix). The bottom row of Figure 1 contains bar graphs showing the frequency of memory network, non-memory network, and cross network pairs of regions as represented within that cluster. In these bar graphs, red represents HC and blue SCZ.

The membership of the largest five clusters, K1...K5, is more composed of DFC timeseries from controls than from SCZ patients. The remaining clusters, K6…K10, are more populated by DFC timeseries from SCZ patients. K3 is populated by a notably high concentration of DFC trends representing region-pairs from anterior and posterior regions of the memory network (see appendix). This phenomenon can be seen in the heatmap for both HC and SCZ. In the top row of Figure 1, DFC trend frequency maps show high representation of high r-valued, low variance, DFC trends in the larger clusters. These, as stated above, are control-heavy clusters. The smaller clusters have frequency maps showing low r-valued, high variance, DFC trends. These are the clusters which are dominantly populated by trends from SCZ patients.

Conclusion

It is apparent that the conditions of memory formation induce characteristically different dynamic functional connectivity trends in schizophrenia as compared to controls. Carefully applying partitioning algorithms can illuminate these differences. Here we have shown that the partitioning around medoids algorithm is suitable for partitioning dynamic functional connectivity trends into discernibly different patterns. Results of this partitioning show that brain networks in schizophrenia show reduced functional connectivity under these controlled conditions of memory formation, as well as under the transitional states between conditions. This reduced functional connectivity is reflected in the impaired task performance of the SCZ group. Additionally, asymmetries in region-pair representation within clusters are not only between the two groups HC and SCZ, but between the involvement of anterior and posterior AAL regions. The combination of these techniques is a valuable addition to the tools we have available to characterize schizophrenia.
