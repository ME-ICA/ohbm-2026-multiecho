Introduction

Biological ageing does not progress uniformly across the body. Each organ system follows its own ageing trajectory, and chronic diseases can accelerate ageing in specific tissues. While brain ageing has been widely characterised1, much less is known about how ageing in the body relates to ageing in the brain, or how either relates to cellular ageing markers such as telomere length2. Imaging-derived phenotypes (IDPs)3,4 from brain MRI have enabled detailed quantification of brain ageing, whereas studies of ageing across body organs have largely relied on non-imaging clinical or biochemical measures5. As a result, the broader structure of body–brain ageing, and its relationship to chronic disease burden, remains unclear.

Here, we used population-level data from the UK Biobank (UKBB) to develop biological age clocks for major organ systems using body imaging phenotypes together with grey-matter and white-matter MRI features. We examined how body organ clocks relate to each other, to chronic diseases, and to brain ageing, and whether cellular ageing indexed by leukocyte telomere length is linked to these processes. Our study provides a whole-body view of ageing and enables investigations of how body and brain ageing are interconnected at the population level.

Methods

We used data from UKBB participants with brain and body MRI (UKBB project 43822), electronic health records, demographic information, and leukocyte telomere length. Data availability varied across modalities, with imaging data from samples ranging from tens of thousands of participants to biological measures from several hundred thousand participants. 

IDPs for the brain and heart were taken directly from the UKBB imaging release. Brain IDPs included grey-matter (GM) regional volumes and cortical measures (1422 IDPs) and white-matter (WM) microstructural features (443 IDPs). Body IDPs were derived from MR images using automated machine-learning pipelines to segment the liver, pancreas, spleen, kidney (whole, cortex and medulla), lungs, and subcutaneous and visceral adipose tissue (SAT and VAT) (Fig. 1a). Within each organ, quantitative measures were computed, including T1, R2*, fat fraction, and organ volume.

T1 measures in UKBB are acquired using an ECG-gated shMOLLI sequence and are therefore strongly dependent on heart rate, particularly at higher T1 values such as in the kidney. To reduce physiologically induced variability and improve biological specificity, organ-specific shMOLLI heart-rate correction was applied using sequence-derived lookup tables to standardise T1 values across participants (Fig. 1b). Quality control procedures were applied to all derived body IDPs. IDPs were complemented with selected non-imaging measures for lung and cardiac function, and blood-based measures for the immune system (Fig. 1a).

We trained separate age-prediction models for each organ system, and for male and female independently (Fig. 1c). For GM and WM models, dimensionality reduction was performed using principal component analysis (PCA), retaining the first 50 components. For all other organs, the full feature sets were used. Model training included only participants without any major chronic diseases⁴, and the trained sex-specific models were subsequently applied to an unseen set of participants, including those with chronic disease.

For each model, biological age was estimated using 20-fold cross-validated linear regression. Organ age gaps (Δ) were defined as predicted age minus chronological age. Model accuracy was assessed on held-out data using correlations between predicted and chronological age and mean absolute error (MAE) (Fig. 1d).

After deriving organ age gaps, we quantified whether different organs shared a common ageing pattern by applying PCA to the matrix of subject-by-organ age gaps. Only participants with age estimates for at least four organs were included, and missing values were imputed using iterative imputation to preserve shared covariance structure. The first principal component (PC1) was taken as an index of multi-organ ageing, and absolute component loadings were used to estimate the contribution of each organ to this systemic ageing factor.

To identify features contributing most strongly to each organ age clock, we computed permutation-based importance scores. Each feature was shuffled across the training set, and the resulting change in MAE was compared with the baseline model. Scores were normalised to identify the most influential features for each organ (Fig. 1e). For GM and WM, PCA weights were back-projected into IDP space and grouped into anatomical regions to produce region-level feature-importance maps. Importance was estimated separately for male and female models to identify sex-specific contributors to biological ageing.

Finally, organ age gaps were related to chronic disease groups and leukocyte telomere length using regression and correlation analyses adjusted for age, sex and Charlson comorbidity index (CCI), with Bonferroni correction applied for multiple comparisons.

Results

Across all organ systems, the age-prediction models showed strong generalisability on the unseen set (Fig. 1d). The spleen, adipose and pancreas clocks achieved the highest accuracy, with correlations near 0.95 and mean absolute errors of 1.5–2 years. Liver, immune, kidney and lung clocks also performed well. As expected for neuroimaging-based models, GM and WM clocks showed lower accuracy than body clocks. Together, these results indicate that ageing signals vary in strength across organs but can be modelled reliably at scale. Figure 1e summarises the feature-importance profiles driving each organ clock.

Organ clocks did not age uniformly across individuals (Fig. 2a). GM and WM age gaps showed the strongest association (r≈0.56), reflecting a shared neurobiological ageing axis. Outside the brain, liver and spleen showed clear co-ageing, and a broader cardio–renal–pulmonary–adipose pattern was evident: cardiac ageing correlated with kidney, lung and adipose ageing, and kidney and lung also showed consistent positive associations. Adipose ageing was modestly linked to both cardiac and kidney ageing. In contrast, many other pairwise correlations were close to zero, and immune ageing showed minimal coupling with other organs, confirming that biological ageing is largely multidimensional.

A systemic ageing factor was derived using PCA (Fig. 2b). The first principal component explained 18% of shared variance, indicating limited commonality across organs. GM and WM showed the largest loadings, reflecting both their correlation and distinct neurobiological contributions to this shared axis. Lung and cardiac ageing also loaded moderately, suggesting a weaker neuro–cardiorespiratory component. Liver, pancreas, spleen and immune ageing contributed little, consistent with their more independent trajectories.

Chronic diseases showed clear, organ-specific effects on biological ageing (Fig. 2c). Across conditions, the most consistent pattern was accelerated brain ageing: GM and WM age gaps were positive in all disease group and often significant. Immune ageing showed a similar but weaker cross-condition trend. Against this shared pattern, disease-specific effects were evident. COPD showed the largest increase in lung ageing (Δ≈+3.5 yrs), chronic kidney disease showed the strongest kidney ageing (Δ≈+2.5 yrs), and diabetes produced marked liver ageing (Δ≈+1.9 yrs). Cardiometabolic conditions (ischemic heart disease, hypertensive disease and diabetes) consistently elevated cardiac ageing.

Neurological and psychiatric disorders produced broader multisystem patterns. Multiple sclerosis showed large increases in both WM and GM ageing (Δ>+3 yrs). Schizophrenia showed similarly strong brain-ageing effects together with increased immune ageing. Dementia and parkinsonism showed elevated GM/WM age gaps that did not reach significance due to smaller group sizes. Depression and bipolar disorder showed modest increases in GM and WM and weaker, inconsistent effects in peripheral organs. Individuals with multiple chronic conditions showed small, diffuse increases across most organs, suggesting a cumulative ageing burden (last column in Fig. 2c).

Leukocyte telomere length showed organ-specific associations with biological ageing (Fig. 2d). Shorter telomeres were significantly associated with older immune, brain (WM and GM), pancreatic and lung ages after adjustment for covariates, with the strongest effect observed in in the immune system. These findings indicate that telomere shortening is most tightly coupled to ageing in immune, neurobiological and selected metabolic systems.

Fig. 2e shows telomere–age-gap associations within each disease group. The immune system again displayed the most consistent pattern, with significant negative correlations across many diseases. Other organs showed fewer associations, typically limited to isolated groups. This pattern reinforces immune ageing as the most robust telomere-linked component of systemic biological ageing.

Conclusion

This work shows that biological ageing does not follow a single pathway but differs across organs. Using organ-specific age clocks derived from body MRI and brain imaging, we found that only a small proportion of ageing is shared across systems, with most variation reflecting organ-specific processes.

Across chronic health conditions, the most consistent finding was an increase in brain biological age. Grey- and white-matter age gaps were elevated in almost all disease groups, with similar effect sizes in systemic and neurological or psychiatric conditions, except for multiple sclerosis, which showed the strongest brain-ageing effects. In contrast, brain age gaps were only weakly related to peripheral organ ageing, suggesting that accelerated brain ageing in chronic disease is not simply driven by ageing in specific body organs.

Peripheral organs showed clearer disease-specific patterns. Ageing was strongest in organs directly affected by disease, indicating more localised biological stress outside the brain. Together, these results suggest that chronic diseases combine a shared impact on brain ageing with more targeted effects on peripheral organs, rather than producing a single, unified ageing process across the body.

Leukocyte telomere length was most strongly associated with immune ageing and also related to brain ageing at the population level. Notably, these associations were more evident in systemic disease groups than in neurological or psychiatric conditions, suggesting that cellular ageing may reflect systemic disease burden more than brain-specific pathology.

Overall, our findings point to a clear separation between widespread brain ageing across chronic conditions and largely independent ageing of peripheral organs. They support a model in which chronic disease accelerates brain ageing through shared mechanisms, while cellular ageing signals are more closely tied to systemic physiological stress. This highlights the value of combining brain imaging, body imaging and cellular markers to better understand how ageing unfolds across the body and brain.
