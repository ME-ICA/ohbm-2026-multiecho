Introduction

Older adults often exhibit altered postural control, increased fall risk, and reduced adaptability to environmental uncertainty. These deficits may arise from age-related changes in cognitive control, such as slower anticipatory processes and reduced sensitivity to environmental statistics. Predictive coding1 provides a theoretical framework to interpret these effects, describing how expectations are updated probabilistically based on accumulated sensory evidence to guide adaptive postural responses. We examined age-related effects on anticipatory processes during step initiation by manipulating the probability of responses in a go/no-go paradigm. Anticipatory postural adjustments (APAs) were measured, and age-related differences in APA onset time were modelled with the Hierarchical Gaussian Filter2 (HGF), a predictive coding-based belief updating model. Intermuscular coherence (IMC) between lower leg muscles was computed to assess physiological correlates of anticipation. We hypothesized that older adults would show slower APA onsets and slower belief updating, reflecting stronger reliance on priors and reduced precision on prediction errors.  

Methods

In this study, 24 younger adults (22.7 ± 1.6 years) and 25 older adults (72 ± 3.6 years) performed a choice stepping task (Fig 1A) using a go/no-go paradigm where they stepped in response to a priming cue (left/right) followed by an imperative cue (go/no-go). We varied the probability of go and no-go cues between 75% (mostly-go condition) and 25% (mostly-no-go condition) to experimentally manipulate the anticipation of the stepping response. Stepping responses were recorded using force plates underneath the stepping mat, and physiological signals were acquired using mobile EMG (32 channels). Latencies of anticipatory postural adjustments (APA onset time) and movement onsets (lift-off time) were extracted from vertical force profiles (Fig 1B), and statistical differences between groups, conditions, and trial histories were assessed using Linear Mixed Models (LMM). We then fitted a two-level Hierarchical Gaussian Filter (HGF) with a log RT response model to the APA onset times to extract individual model parameters and estimate changes in precision weighting in a trial wise manner (Fig 1C).To look at differences in physiological processes prior to the onset of a go/no-go stimulus, intermuscular coherence3 (IMC) between lower leg muscles was estimated. To assess anticipatory activity, statistical differences of IMC between groups and conditions were assessed 500ms before the imperative cue. 

Results

In behavioural data, older people showed slower APA and movement onsets, and these group differences were larger in the mostly-go conditions (p = 0.011; Fig 2A). When assessing serial dependencies in APA onset times, we observed that reaction times decreased with subsequent go trials and the effect of trial history was reduced in older people (p = 0.008; Fig 2B). The extracted parameters of the HGF model revealed that older participants had significantly lower perceptual learning rate (ω; p<.001) and higher response model coefficient (β4; p = 0.01). HGF simulations based on mean fitted ω and β4 across participants revealed that these group differences in ω and β4 were associated with reduced precision weighting of prediction errors in older people (Fig 2C). In physiological data, IMC at 8–12 Hz was significantly enhanced in the mostly-go condition, particularly among younger participants (p = 0.029; Fig. 2D).

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate age-related differences in anticipatory processes of postural control that reflect separable cognitive control processes. Behavioural measures revealed slower anticipatory postural onsets and reduced serial dependency. Computational modelling captured these age-related effects as reduced statistical learning and stronger influence of unexpected uncertainty, causing slower belief updating with age. Simulations of hidden states in the perceptual model indicated age-related reduction in precision-weighting of prediction errors, suggesting an increased reliance on priors and lesser emphasis on accumulating statistical regularities of sequential inputs to update beliefs. Physiologically, younger people showed increased pre-stimulus IMC between lower leg muscles in the 8-12Hz range during mostly-go condition, in line with the faster stepping responses that are observed in this condition. Together, these behavioural, modelling, and physiological markers suggest that younger people flexibly engage proactive control to adapt postural responses to changing environmental statistics, whereas older people favour stable, prior-driven strategies acquired over the lifespan that promote perceptual stability to avoid fall risks, linking age-related cognitive control mechanisms to adaptive postural control.
