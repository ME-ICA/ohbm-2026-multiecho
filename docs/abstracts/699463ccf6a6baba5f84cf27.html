Introduction

A growing body of research has demonstrated that humans show low accuracy in differentiating real pictures and AI-generated images¹. As the GAN (generative adversarial networks) technology has advanced, GAN-generated faces have become harder to detect. The phenomenon in which AI-generated faces are perceived as more realistic than genuine faces is known as AI-hyperrealism2. The Uncanny Valley hypothesis has recently been challenged because the boundaries of human and artificial intelligence agents have been blurred and leading to the suggestion that human perception might, in some cases, “cross” the valley3. At a subconscious level, our neural circuitry can pick up on subtle irregularities in deepfakes indicated by EEG brain activation patterns, which might correspond to a “prediction error” signal, potentially for scrutiny or conflict detection. Recent neuroimaging studies suggested beliefs about AI images could alter impression formation and related brain activity, such that if a person believes that the image comes from an AI source, individuals treated AI-labelled faces as less attractive, and signals like perceptual emotional response were damped. However, there is still a lack of evidence with the decision-making mechanism underlying the AI-or-not judgment. 

Previous work has primarily relied on experimental surveys and other qualitative methods to assess the multiple human attributes to the perception of the AI-synthesised photos versus real photos. By investigating the situation when we are forming a judgement without a priori label, this fMRI study aims to compare behavioural and neural patterns of individuals by displaying face and scenery pictures (category), either from real existing sources or generated by AI (sources). Meanwhile, we look for such subtle prediction errors and bias underlying such judgments and identify additional factors beyond human-likeliness that could help us understand why humans often struggle to distinguish AI from real pictures.

Methods

Participants. 39 healthy adults (age range:18–26) with normal or corrected-to-normal vision participated. Exclusion criteria included MRI contraindications, neurological/psychiatric history, and excessive head motion (e.g., mean framewise displacement (FD) > 3 mm). 

Stimuli.  The study employed a 2 × 2 factorial design, with semantic category (faces vs. scenery) and ground-truth source (real photographs vs. synthetic images) as within-subject factors. Synthetic human images were adopted from Nightingale & Farid (2022) 4. Face trials comprised 192 real and 192 synthetic images; scenery trials comprised 50 real and 50 synthetic images.  

Task design and procedure. There is a total of 484 trials with two blocks. Each trial consisted of (i) stimulus presentation, where Face and scenery pictures with different ground truth sources interleaved each other for up to 3000 ms (terminated by response), followed by (ii) a fixation inter-trial interval jittered between 500–1500 ms. Participants judged whether each image was real (R) or synthetic (S) via a two-button response; response mapping was counterbalanced across participants. Accuracy-based incentives were provided. An introduction was provided before the scanning to ensure participants understood the task. There were two blocks,121 trials per block. A break screen was shown for 20 s after 1 block to reduce fatigue. Trial order was randomised to balance category and source.

Behavioural analysis. Omissions (no response within 3000 ms) were removed, and subjects with fewer than 400 valid trials were removed. 35 participants are left for analysis. Outcomes included accuracy and RT by category and ground-truth source, plus signal-detection measures: hit rate, false alarm rate (“synthetic” response to real), d′, and response criterion. ANOVA was conducted for reaction time analysis, and accuracy (correct vs. incorrect) was analysed using a mixed-effects logistic regression with fixed effects of Source (Real vs. Synthetic), Category (Face vs. Scenery), and their interaction, and random slopes for Source and Category by participant and by item.

fMRI analysis. We ran a first-level GLM in Nilearn modelling each stimulus as a boxcar event (onset = response onset; duration = 0 s) convolved with the canonical HRF, with high-pass filtering (128 s). Regressors of interest captured the full Category × Stimulus Source × Response design: Face/Scenery × Real/Synthetic stimulus × R/S judgement (8 regressors total: RealFace-R, RealFace-S, SynthFace-R, SynthFace-S; and the same 4 for Scenery). Motion parameters were included as nuisance regressors.

Planned contrasts from each participant’s first-level model were entered into whole-brain random-effects group analyses (one-sample t-tests across participants). Multiple comparisons were controlled using cluster-level FWE correction (pFWE < 0.05). contrasts: (1) Response effect within fixed stimulus: within each specific stimulus condition (e.g., Real Face), Judged Real − Judged Synthetic. (2) Stimulus source effect within fixed judgement: within a given response (“Real” or “Synthetic”), Real stimulus − Synthetic stimulus. (3) Category × Source interaction within fixed judgement: within a given response, compare Real–Synthetic differences across categories: [(Real−Synthetic)_Face] − [(Real−Synthetic)_Scenery].

ROIs were defined in a data-driven manner using Nilearn connected_regions, from significant clusters from the whole brain analysis (cluster level corrected FWE< 0.05), and separate regions with ≥50 connected voxels/vertices. This ROI analysis aims to examine the behavioural correlates of response accuracy rate in each brain region. The regional responsiveness for each task-condition was evaluated by the voxel-average β-coefficient estimates for each participant from the first-level GLM. Covariation between responsiveness changes and behavioural scores was measured using a Pearson correlation, with Bonferroni correction.

Results

Overall, participants were poor at telling real from synthetic images, showing near-chance accuracy—below chance for faces (48.62%) and only slightly above chance for scenery (54.70%), with scenery performance not reliably different from chance. A model including the Source × Category interaction fit significantly better, indicating that the effect of being real vs synthetic depends on whether the stimulus is a face or scenery.

Crucially, participants were much less accurate on synthetic items than real ones, for both categories (synthetic faces: OR = 0.24; synthetic scenery: OR = 0.44), consistent with a strong tendency to mistake synthetic images as real. Signal-detection results reinforce this: sensitivity was lower for faces (d′ = −0.09) than scenery (d′ = 0.28), meaning faces were essentially indiscriminable (or even slightly reversed) while scenery showed modest discriminability. Response bias was negative in both categories (faces c = −0.47; scenery c = −0.22), reflecting a “real” response bias under uncertainty, which is also visible in the error pattern (high miss rate: 59.42% synthetic → “real”, versus a lower false-alarm rate 37.6% real → “synthetic”).

Reaction times did not show a robust 2×2 interaction overall, but a clear qualitative pattern emerged: participants took longer when labelling scenery as “synthetic” than when doing the same for faces, suggesting greater deliberation or uncertainty when rejecting scenery as real.

People in this task generally could not reliably detect synthetic content, especially for faces, and their judgments were dominated by a bias to assume images are real, leading to frequent misses (synthetic judged as real). Scenery offered slightly better cues than faces, but performance still hovered around chance-level discrimination.

Whole-brain fMRI: main effects and interactions. 

For real face stimuli, trials judged as more likely to be real (rather than AI-generated) elicited stronger responses in the right medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and middle temporal gyrus, whereas when the stimuli were judged more as synthetic, they showed greater activity in the right anterior insula. Moreover, significant brain behaviour correlations between MPFC and judgement accuracy rate were found (Pearson r = 0.60, p Bonferroni < 0.001). On the contrary, when the face is synthetic but incorrectly judged as real, the right superior frontal gyrus was more activated than when it is correctly identified. When the face was synthesised but was incorrectly judged as real, the right angular gyrus, right superior frontal gyrus and left supramarginal gyrus elicited stronger responses. When subjects correctly judge the faces as synthetic, additional regions are recruited, including motor cortex, inferior frontal gyrus and cerebellum, which are not activated when they think the faces are real when they are synthesised. These additional regions are suggested to help them judge whether the image is fake or not. For the same contrasts, no significant clusters survived for the scenery condition. Given response is real, when the face is indeed real, the left occipital pole, right hippocampus, and left fusiform gyrus elicited a stronger response than when subjects mistakenly perceived synthetic faces as real, which hinted at an unconscious neural activity despite both kinds of stimuli being judged as real. We did not observe significant clusters for the interaction contrast ([Face(Real–Synthetic)] – [Scene(Real–Synthetic)], within ‘Real’ judgements) at cluster-level FWE (pFWE < 0.05). While some regions (e.g., precentral gyrus) appeared at p < 0.001 uncorrected, these results did not survive correction and therefore are reported only as exploratory.

Conclusion

Overall, this study provides converging behavioral and neural evidence that people struggle to distinguish GAN-generated from real images, with the difficulty most pronounced for faces. Behaviorally, performance hovered around chance and consistently revealed a liberal “real” response bias under uncertainty—participants were far more likely to mistake synthetic images as real than to reject real images as synthetic—supporting the idea of AI “hyperrealism.”

Neurally, judging real faces as authentic engaged regions associated with social evaluation and mnemonic processing, including mPFC and hippocampal–fusiform/occipital circuitry, and mPFC activity tracked individual differences in judgment accuracy. In contrast, labeling a real face as synthetic recruited right anterior insula, consistent with salience/conflict-related processing. Correctly rejecting synthetic faces additionally engaged control- and action-related systems (inferior frontal, motor, and cerebellar regions), suggesting that identifying convincing fakes requires extra attention and top-down control. Together, these findings support a decision mechanism in which default acceptance (“real”) is only overridden when additional error signals and control resources are recruited—offering a neural account for why AI-generated images can feel subjectively more authentic than reality.
