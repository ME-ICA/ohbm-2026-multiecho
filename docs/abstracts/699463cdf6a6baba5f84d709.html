Introduction

The human brain integrates sensory information through a hierarchical architecture, combining inputs across modalities to support perception and cognition. Our previous work introduced a two-dimensional, function-based framework to characterize the sensory integration along the cortical hierarchy.^1 However, these two dimensions are not entirely independent: while one captures a region’s sensory preference, it does not reflect the strength or purity of sensory integration, which instead must be inferred from the second dimension.

To overcome this limitation, we adopt a Compositional Data Analysis framework to separate changes in sensory identity from the magnitude of functional reconfiguration. This approach is motivated by the fact that visual, auditory, and somatosensory contributions are inherently relative, forming a constrained composition whose components sum to a constant. Standard Euclidean statistics are inappropriate for interpreting such data due to the “closure problem”, where the forced dependency between components (as one increases, others must decrease) creates spurious correlations and distorts distance metrics.

Using Aitchison geometry—specifically the centered log-ratio (CLR) transformation—we map these constrained compositions into an unconstrained Euclidean space. This enables rigorous quantification of (i) the complexity of sensory integration (Aitchison variance) and (ii) the magnitude of functional reconfiguration (centroid shift), without the distortions introduced by linear analyses of proportional data.

Methods

We used preprocessed 7T fMRI data from the Human Connectome Project (HCP).^2 Our analyses focused on 167 participants (101 females; mean age = 29.4 ± 3.24 years) who completed all four movie-watching runs and all four resting-state runs. Each participant contributed an equal number of volumes for each scan type. Additional surface-based smoothing (4 mm FWHM) and standardization were applied. To avoid contamination from rest periods embedded within the movie runs, we removed all resting volumes from those runs prior to analysis. The four movie runs and the four resting runs were then concatenated separately.

To estimate the contribution of each sensory modality to each fMRI time series, we fit a non-negative general linear model (GLM) in which the average time series from the primary visual cortex (V1), primary somatosensory cortex (S1), and primary auditory cortex (A1) served as predictors (Fig. 1a and 1b). These primary sensory areas were defined using the Glasser MMP parcellation.^3 The resulting regression coefficients indicate how strongly each modality contributes to the signal at each cortical vertex. We converted these coefficients into three-element compositional vectors (visual, somatosensory, auditory) by normalizing them so that the three components sum to one (Fig. 1c).

Because these normalized coefficients lie in a closed composition (a 3-part vector constrained to sum to a constant), standard Euclidean statistics are inappropriate due to the closure problem. We therefore analyzed the data using Aitchison geometry.^4 All computations were carried out in centered log-ratio (CLR) space, which maps each composition from the constrained simplex into an unconstrained Euclidean vector. The CLR transformation is performed by dividing each component by the geometric mean of the three components and taking the natural logarithm of the resulting ratios.

For each participant, brain state (movie vs. rest), and MMP parcel, we computed two geometric metrics describing sensory integration:

Aitchison Variance (distributional complexity): This metric captures how widely the vertices within a parcel vary in their sensory compositions (Fig 1d). To compute it, we first identified the parcel’s centroid in CLR space, which corresponds to the closed geometric mean of all vertex compositions in that parcel. We then measured the squared Euclidean distance—defined in CLR space—between each vertex and this centroid, and averaged these distances across vertices. Larger Aitchison Variance indicates that the region expresses a broader and more diverse mixture of sensory inputs, whereas smaller variance indicates a more uniform and specialized sensory profile.

Centroid Shift (magnitude of functional reconfiguration): To quantify how much a parcel changes its average sensory identity between resting and movie-watching states, we measured the Euclidean distance in CLR space between the parcel’s movie centroid and its resting centroid (Fig. 1e). This scalar value reflects the magnitude of functional reorientation, such as a shift toward visual-dominant or multisensory-balanced compositions during naturalistic stimulation.

To identify state-dependent changes, we performed paired-sample t-tests comparing movie vs. rest Aitchison Variance across all 360 parcels in the MMP parcellation.

Results

Vertex-level sensory compositions showed clear state-dependent differences (Fig. 2a). During movie-watching, vertices shifted toward more visual-dominant mixtures relative to rest, consistent with strong visual drive from dynamic naturalistic stimuli. This reorientation was not uniform across the cortex but followed the cortical hierarchy, with higher-order regions showing the largest compositional displacement.

Aitchison Variance quantified how widely vertex-level compositions varied within each parcel. We identified cortical regions exhibiting the top 5% largest changes in Aitchison Variance between movie-watching and resting states (Fig. 2b). These regions included early extrastriate cortex (V2), ventral temporal areas (TF, TE2p, FFC, PHA1), transitional visual zones (STV), and orbitofrontal/prefrontal areas (11l, 47m). Critically, V2 was the only parcel to show a significant increase in variance during movie-watching. This indicates that the early visual cortex expands its functional repertoire in response to dynamic naturalistic stimulation, integrating a broader and more diverse set of sensory inputs. In contrast, the higher-order ventral temporal and prefrontal regions, while among the parcels with the largest variance shifts, did not increase their variance. Instead, these regions exhibited a contraction or sharpening of their sensory composition, suggesting that they stabilize or specialize their functional responses to support consistent object recognition and scene evaluation within the movie narrative.

Next, we examined parcels showing the top 5% largest shifts in mean centroid shifts (Fig. 2c). Unlike variance changes, these shifts were predominantly observed in higher-order heteromodal regions. The most pronounced shifts occurred in the lateral temporal cortex, including the Superior Temporal Sulcus (STS) and MT+ complex, reflecting their role in processing audiovisual signals and motion. Additional reconfiguration was seen in parietal and medial regions (PFm, IP2, POS2, p32), likely supporting attentional control and network-level state switching to prioritize incoming sensory information over internal processing.

Interestingly, some ventral temporal regions showed both changes in variance and centroid shifts, indicating that these regions not only refine their sensory selectivity but also adjust their overall composition to track complex scene dynamics. Together, these results reveal a topographic dissociation: early visual areas expand their sensory repertoire, higher-order ventral and prefrontal regions specialize, and distributed heteromodal hubs reorient their mean sensory composition in response to naturalistic movie stimulation.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that sensory integration in the human cortex is state-dependent and region-specific. Early visual cortex (V2) responds to naturalistic movie-watching by expanding its functional repertoire, integrating a broader range of sensory inputs to accommodate complex visual dynamics. In contrast, higher-order ventral temporal and prefrontal regions exhibit functional sharpening, stabilizing their sensory composition to support object recognition and scene evaluation. Meanwhile, distributed heteromodal association areas, including lateral temporal, parietal, and medial regions, show large centroid shifts, reflecting a reorientation of mean sensory composition to prioritize dynamic, externally driven inputs over resting-state internal processing.

Together, these results highlight that sensory integration involves complementary mechanisms across the cortical hierarchy: lower-level regions increase compositional diversity, higher-order areas refine and specialize, and heteromodal hubs dynamically reconfigure to coordinate large-scale brain states. Importantly, our use of Aitchison geometry allows these changes in variance and composition to be quantified rigorously, providing a generalized framework that can integrates information from even more than three sensory modalities, overcoming a main constraint of our prior work.
