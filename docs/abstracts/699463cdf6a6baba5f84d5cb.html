Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) has evolved into a global epidemic, increasingly acknowledged not merely as a metabolic syndrome but as a significant driver of neurocognitive degeneration. Epidemiological data indicate that individuals with T2DM exhibit a 1.5- to 2.5-fold increased risk of developing cognitive impairment and dementia compared to normoglycemic controls.1 While vascular damage and glucose toxicity are traditional explanations, recent focus has shifted to the disruption of brain iron homeostasis. Iron is a double-edged sword in neurobiology; essential for dopamine synthesis and mitochondrial respiration, its excess catalyzes the Fenton reaction, producing hydroxyl radicals that cause oxidative stress and ferroptosis.2 Deep gray matter (DGM) nuclei, having the highest basal iron concentrations, are particularly susceptible to this pathology.

Quantitative Susceptibility Mapping (QSM) represents a paradigm shift in measuring brain iron. Unlike T2*-weighted imaging, which suffers from non-local field effects, QSM provides a direct, voxel-wise metric of tissue magnetic susceptibility.3 However, existing QSM studies in T2DM are often limited by small cohorts or restricted regions of interest, failing to capture the whole-brain pattern of iron redistribution. More importantly, the mechanistic "missing link"—whether iron deposition mediates the relationship between known risk factors (like aging) and cognitive decline in T2DM—remains unproven. This study utilized high-resolution QSM in a large clinical cohort to: (1) comprehensively map iron overload and depletion patterns in DGM nuclei; (2) correlate these patterns with multidomain cognitive deficits; and (3) test the hypothesis that regional iron dysregulation acts as a mediator in the age-related cognitive decline observed in diabetic patients.

Methods

Participants: This prospective, cross-sectional study was approved by the institutional review board. We recruited 291 participants, including 115 patients with clinically diagnosed Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) (mean age: 53.04 ± 10.12 years) and 176 Healthy Controls (HC) (mean age: 50.18 ± 9.41 years). Diagnosis of T2DM according to 2021 ADA criteria. Exclusion criteria were rigorous: history of stroke, head trauma, other neurological disorders (e.g., Parkinson’s, Alzheimer’s), psychiatric conditions, alcohol abuse, or MRI contraindications.

Clinical & Neuropsychological Battery: A comprehensive assessment was administered to all subjects. Descriptive demographic and clinical characteristics were collected, including age, sex, years of education, body mass index (BMI), disease duration, blood pressure (BP), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), fasting insulin (FINS), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C). A battery of neuropsychological tests was administered to assess global cognition, cognitive flexibility, executive function, and the status of anxiety and depression. Global cognition was evaluated using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) and the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE). Episodic memory was measured with the Auditory Verbal Learning Test (AVLT). Executive function, processing speed, and fine motor skills were assessed via the Trail Making Test Part A (TMT-A), Digit Symbol Substitution Test (DSST), and the Grooved Pegboard Test (GPT). The Digit Span Test (DST) was used to evaluate attention and working memory. The Clock Drawing Test (CDT) served to measure visuospatial function. Finally, mood status was quantified using the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS)33 and the Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS).

MRI Acquisition: Imaging was conducted on a 3.0T Siemens Prisma scanner with a 64-channel head coil. Structural images utilized a high-resolution 3D T1-weighted MPRAGE sequence (TR/TE=2530/2.98ms, matrix=256×256). For iron quantification, a 3D multi-echo gradient-echo (mGRE) sequence was acquired: TR=24ms; 8 echoes (TE1=4.6ms, ΔTE=5.5s); Flip Angle=15°; FOV=220mm; voxel size=0.85×0.85×2.0mm³. 

QSM Processing: We employed the SEPIA toolbox (v1.2.1) for QSM reconstruction. The pipeline included: (1) Brain extraction via FSL-BET; (2) Laplacian-based phase unwrapping; (3) Echo combination weighted by signal-to-noise ratio; (4) Background field removal using the V-SHARP method (radius range: 12mm); and (5) Dipole inversion via the iLSQR algorithm with regularization to suppress streaking. Susceptibility values were referenced to whole-brain mean susceptibility.

ROI Analysis: Regions of Interest (ROIs)—bilateralamygdala, hippocampus, thalamus, globus pallidus, caudate nucleus, putamen, red nucleus, and substantia nigra—were segmented from T1 images using FSL-FIRST and registered to QSM space using ANTs (SyN). During the transformation process, we employed nearest-neighbor interpolation to preserve the integrity of anatomical labels. Mean susceptibility was extracted for each ROI. All registration results underwent visual inspection by experienced operators and necessary manual corrections were applied.

Statistical Analysis: Data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 and R. Group comparisons used independent t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests. Partial correlation analyses, adjusted for age, sex, and education, assessed iron-cognition relationships. Stepwise multiple linear regression identified predictors of iron deposition (variables: Age, Sex, BMI, Disease Duration, HbA1c). Finally, a mediation analysis (PROCESS macro, Model 4, 5,000 bootstraps) tested the indirect effect of Age on MoCA scores through iron susceptibility. Significance was set at p<0.05, with False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction for multiple comparisons.

Results

Demographics and Clinical Profile: The analysis revealed that the T2DM group had a significantly higher proportion of males and was slightly older than the HC group. Metabolically, T2DM patients showed significantly elevated levels of HbA1c, FPG and HOMA-IR. Neuropsychological assessments revealed that patients with T2DM demonstrated poorer performance on the Auditory Verbal Learning Test (AVLT) and the Digit Symbol Substitution Test (DSST). The T2DM group also exhibited longer completion times on the Grooved Pegboard Test (GPT) with both hands. Furthermore, psychological assessments indicated higher levels of anxiety in the T2DM patients

Region-Specific Iron Dysregulation: QSM revealed a heterogeneous pattern of iron redistribution. T2DM patients showed significantly increased magnetic susceptibility (iron overload) in the bilateral thalamus (Left: t = 2.80, P = 0.005; Right: t = 2.64, P = 0.009), bilateral putamen (Left: t = 3.79, P < 0.001; Right: t = 2.74, P = 0.007), bilateral substantia nigra (Left: t = 3.33, P < 0.001; Right: t = 3.69, P < 0.001), and the right red nucleus (t = 3.38, P < 0.001). Conversely, the bilateral caudate nuclei exhibited significantly reduced susceptibility compared to HCs (Left: t = -2.83, P = 0.005; Right:  = -3.73, P < 0.001), indicating pathological iron depletion.

 

Cognitive Correlations: Partial correlation analysis (adjusted for covariates) indicated that regional iron anomalies predicted cognitive dysfunction. In the Caudate Nucleus, higher susceptibility correlated with better cognition: left caudate positively associated with MoCA (r = 0.119, P = 0.027) ,GPT-L and CDT, while right caudate correlated with MoCA (r = 0.193, P = 0.001), AVLT (5min and delay), both hands GPT, CDT, and MMSE.
 

In contrast, iron overload in other regions predicted poorer outcomes. The Left Putamen showed negative correlations with MoCA (r = -0.170, P = 0.004), MMSE, AVLT and DSST, alongside positive correlations with GPT time. However, only the correlations between the left putamen and AVLT-immediate and GPT-L remained significant after FDR correction. Similar neurotoxic patterns were found in the SN and RN, where higher iron linked to lower memory scores and slower motor speed.

 

Predictors of Iron Deposition: Multiple linear regression identified diabetes status as the distinct, independent predictor of iron deposition solely in the Substantia Nigra (β = 11.748, P = 0.002). Age exhibited bidirectional effects: it positively predicted susceptibility in the Putamen (β = 0.202, P < 0.001) while showing inverse relationships in the Hippocampus and Thalamus. BMI similarly displayed opposing patterns, positively associating with iron in the Hippocampus and Globus Pallidus, but negatively in the Caudate Nucleus (β = -0.623, P = 0.001). Additionally, female sex significantly predicted lower thalamic iron (β = -2.795, P < 0.001). Other associations, including smoking history and education, did not survive FDR correction.

 

Mediation Analysis: Mediation analysis (adjusted for sex/education) tested whether iron overload mediates age-related cognitive decline. The model identified the  as a critical mechanistic link. Specifically, Age significantly predicted increased  Substantia Nigra susceptibility (Effect = 0.595, 95% CI: 0.226 to 0.963, P = 0.002), which in turn negatively predicted MoCA scores (Effect = -0.014, 95% CI: -0.025 to -0.002, P = 0.019). Crucially, the indirect effect was significant. As the direct effect of Age on MoCA remained significant (Effect = -0.054, P = 0.004), these findings establish  Substantia Nigra iron accumulation as a key partial mediator connecting biological aging to cognitive impairment in T2DM.

Conclusion

The present study provides compelling, in vivo evidence of extensive iron dysregulation within the deep gray matter of patients with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus, utilizing advanced quantitative susceptibility mapping. Our findings delineate a dissociated pattern of iron redistribution: while the putamen, substantia nigra, and thalamus exhibit pathological iron overload consistent with neuroinflammatory and oxidative stress models, the caudate nucleus displays a paradoxical iron depletion. This divergence challenges the simplified view of global brain iron accumulation in metabolic disorders and suggests spatially distinct pathophysiological mechanisms within the basal ganglia. The iron overload observed in the substantia nigra and putamen likely exacerbates dopaminergic neuron loss via ferroptosis, a form of iron-dependent cell death, thereby contributing to the observed motor and cognitive slowing.

 Crucially, this research bridges the gap between neuroimaging biomarkers and clinical phenomenology. We identified the left putamen's iron content as a robust predictor of multidomain cognitive decline, particularly in episodic memory and fine motor speed. The inverse correlation in the caudate nucleus—where lower iron predicts worse cognition—suggests a state of "functional iron deficiency" that may compromise the synthesis of iron-dependent neurotransmitters, further impairing cognitive circuits. This highlights the delicate balance of iron homeostasis required for optimal neural function.

 Most significantly, our mediation analysis establishes a specific mechanistic axis: the Substantia Nigra serves as a critical nexus where biological aging and diabetic metabolic stress converge. We demonstrated that T2DM exacerbates the age-dependent accumulation of iron in the SN, which in turn acts as a partial mediator for global cognitive decline. This implies that diabetes may accelerate the trajectory of "normal" brain aging towards a neurodegenerative phenotype similar to Parkinsonism. These findings validate QSM as a powerful, non-invasive biomarker for monitoring cerebral metabolic health. Clinically, they suggest that therapeutic strategies targeting iron chelation or homeostasis could offer a novel avenue for neuroprotection in T2DM, potentially delaying the onset of cognitive impairment. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to elucidate the temporal dynamics of these iron changes relative to glycemic variability.
