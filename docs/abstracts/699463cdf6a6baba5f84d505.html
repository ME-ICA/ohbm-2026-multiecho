Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is characterized by widespread demyelination, neuroinflammation, and progressive neurodegeneration. While white-matter lesions are central to diagnosis, increasing evidence indicates that cortical neuronal dysfunction is a major determinant of long-term disability.1 Reductions in N-acetylaspartate (NAA), a marker of neuronal integrity, provide a sensitive index of such dysfunction. However, how neuronal metabolic disruption is organized across the cortex at the systems level remains poorly understood. In this study, we aimed to characterize the cortex-wide NAA connectivity gradients in MS and to determine how disease-related alterations relate to cytoarchitectural organization and clinical impairment.

Methods

Whole-brain high-resolution MR spectroscopic imaging (MRSI) was acquired in 75 patients with MS and 52 age-matched healthy controls (HCs) using the SPICE sequence (FOV = 240 ✖ 240 ✖ 120 mm³; nominal resolution = 2.0  ✖ 3.0 ✖ 3.3 mm³; TR = 160 ms; TE = 1.6 ms; scan time = 10:05 minutes).2 Patients were recruited from Huashan Hospital, Shanghai, and diagnosed according to the 2024 McDonald criteria3. For each participant, cortical NAA metabolic networks were constructed by computing Jensen–Shannon divergence–based similarity between NAA distributions across all pairs of regions defined by the 100-ROI Schaefer atlas. Diffusion map embedding was then applied to derive individual NAA connectivity gradients. A common gradient template, derived from the group-averaged connectivity matrix across all participants, was used to align individual gradients via Procrustes alignment.4 

Group differences were assessed at the network level for the first two principal gradients. Clinical relevance was examined by relating gradient measures to neurological disability (Expanded Disability Status Scale, EDSS) and cognitive performance (Symbol Digit Modalities Test, SDMT). To provide multiscale biological context, spatial correspondence between metabolic gradients and cytoarchitectural differentiation was quantified using the two principal histology-derived gradients from the post-mortem BigBrain dataset.5 Finally, NeuroSynth-based meta-analytic decoding was used to link metabolically affected cortical regions to large-scale functional domains.

Results

The first two NAA connectivity gradients accounted for 59.5% and 17.5% of the variance in whole-brain metabolic connectivity, respectively. Patients with MS exhibited a pronounced reorganization of the principal gradient, characterized by reduced metabolic differentiation between transmodal association cortex and primary sensorimotor regions. This gradient collapse was most prominent in dorsal attention, ventral attention, and frontoparietal networks, indicating preferential vulnerability of higher-order associative systems (Fig. 1). 

Importantly, gradient disruption showed robust clinical relevance. Greater metabolic dedifferentiation within the limbic network was associated with higher EDSS scores (r = 0.47, PFDR < 0.001) and lower SDMT performance (r = −0.43, PFDR = 0.004) (Fig. 2), linking neuronal metabolic disorganization to both motor disability and cognitive impairment. Multiscale contextualization revealed that cortical patterns of gradient alteration were significantly correlated with the sensory-fugal cytoarchitectural gradient derived from BigBrain histology (r = −0.31, Pspin = 0.006), suggesting preferential vulnerability in agranular and dysgranular regions. Meta-analytic decoding further indicated that regions exhibiting the strongest gradient disruption were associated with attention, working memory, cognitive control, and sensorimotor integration, which are the functional domains commonly affected in MS.

Conclusion

Our study showed a large-scale collapse of hierarchical neuronal metabolic organization across the cortex in MS. Our results also showed that disruption of NAA connectivity gradients were strongly related to cytoarchitectural vulnerability, functional network degeneration, and clinical disability. These findings suggested that neurometabolic connectome gradients could be a promising biomarker for characterizing cortical pathology and symptom burden in MS.
