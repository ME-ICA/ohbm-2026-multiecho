Introduction

Metabolic processes are essential for maintaining brain homeostasis and structure. Growing evidence identifies metabolic dysregulation as a key mechanism in brain disorders,1 with circulating metabolites serving as biomarkers for diagnosis, prognosis, and monitoring.2 Studies have also shown consistent associations between circulating metabolites and brain structural measures,3 suggesting that metabolic dysregulation may not only correlate with brain structural changes but also bridge the understanding of neuropathological mechanisms in brain disorders.

However, the mechanistic pathways linking metabolic dysregulation to brain disorders through structural changes remain insufficiently understood. Recent studies have mapped metabolite–brain structure relationships in healthy adults,4 yet often without considering downstream clinical outcomes. Conversely, disease-focused studies have connected metabolite profiles to pathological structural changes,5 but usually target single disorders or individual metabolites and rely on modest sample sizes. Moreover, the relative genetic and environmental contributions to metabolites and brain structure remain unclear, limiting progress toward effective prevention strategies.

To address these gaps, we propose a systematic framework leveraging large-scale metabolomic and neuroimaging data from the UK Biobank. We first built a metabolite–brain structure atlas. We then assessed whether brain structure mediates associations between plasma metabolites and major brain disorders. Next, we quantified genetic and environmental influences on metabolite levels and structural measures. Finally, integrating multi-omics resources, we identified potential drug targets linked to metabolite–brain–disease pathways. This study characterizes metabolic signatures associated with brain structure, clarifies their relevance to brain disorders, and advances understanding of metabolic mechanisms that may guide preventive and therapeutic strategies.

Methods

Participants were selected from the UK Biobank with available plasma metabolomics, structural MRI, and covariates. Individuals with major imaging abnormalities or excessive missing data were excluded. The final sample included 36,831 participants, divided into discovery (n=25,846) and replication (n=10,985) cohorts. Figure 1 illustrates the workflow. 

Plasma metabolomics. EDTA plasma samples were profiled using NMR. After quality control, 249 metabolites and 64 derived ratios were retained. Participants with more than 30% missing traits were removed. Extreme values were excluded, and all traits were log-transformed and standardized.

Imaging phenotypes. Structural MRI measures included cortical thickness, surface area, and volume for 68 regions, volumes of 14 subcortical structures, and diffusion metrics (FA and MD) for 27 white-matter tracts. Automated preprocessing and quality control were applied. Participants with excessive missing IDPs or imaging outliers were excluded. Remaining values were normalized.

Clinical outcomes and environmental factors. Neurological and mental health disorders were identified from hospital records using ICD-10 codes, classified as prevalent or incident. Controls had no diagnosis. Environmental and lifestyle variables with acceptable completeness were retained, yielding 117 standardized exposures across multiple domains.

Genetic data and metabolite GWAS. Genotypes were obtained from the imputed dataset following standard quality filters, restricted to unrelated individuals of homogeneous ancestry. To maintain non-overlap with imaging, metabolite GWAS were conducted in participants with metabolomics but without MRI. Models included demographic, technical, and ancestry covariates. Polygenic scores for metabolites were constructed using PLINK2.

External datasets. Independent GWAS summary data for structural MRI traits, brain disorders, and plasma proteins were incorporated to support causal inference while avoiding sample overlap.

Association analyses. In the discovery cohort, metabolite–IDP associations were tested using generalized linear models adjusting for demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, imaging, and biological variables. Significance was determined by Bonferroni correction across all metabolite-brain structure pairs.

Mendelian randomization. Bidirectional two-sample MR estimated potential causal relationships between metabolites and brain structures. Independent instruments were selected by stringent linkage-disequilibrium criteria. The primary estimator was inverse-variance weighting with heterogeneity and outlier checks. Sensitivity analyses included MR-Egger, weighted-median, weighted-mode, and Wald-ratio. Pairs with fewer than three valid instruments were excluded, and results were considered significant after FDR control.

Mediation analysis. For metabolites showing both replicated associations and MR support, mediation models were constructed to quantify indirect effects through structural metrics. Structural mediators were aggregated within major domains (cortical measures, subcortical volumes, diffusion indices). Models adjusted for age and sex estimated direct, indirect, and total effects with FDR-controlled significance.

Integrated gene–environment models. A secondary mediation analysis was employed to assess whether polygenic scores and environmental exposures influenced disorder risk via metabolite levels and structural mediators. 

Drug-target prioritization. Potential targets were identified by combining three sources of causal evidence: metabolite-to-disease MR, protein-to-metabolite cis-MR, and protein-to-disease MR. Proteins showing consistent causal directions across analyses were mapped to druggable gene resources to highlight candidates for therapeutic development.

Results

After quality control, the discovery cohort included 25,846 participants and the replication cohort 10,985 participants. Plasma NMR yielded 313 metabolites, and imaging produced 272 structural phenotypes covering cortical thickness, volume and area, and white-matter FA and MD.

Metabolite–brain structure associations. In the discovery cohort, linear models identified 1,215 significant metabolite–structure associations after Bonferroni correction (Figure 2). Associations were most frequent for cortical thickness (n=546) and volume (n=488), followed by MD (n=130), FA (n=35) and area (n=16). Overlap was greatest across thickness, volume and MD. Lipoproteins and lipids, cholesterol, and metabolic ratios accounted for most associations. Regions such as the superior parietal cortex, cuneus, lingual gyrus, inferior longitudinal fasciculus and corticospinal tract showed the largest association counts. Representative strong effects included apolipoprotein ratios with occipital thickness and LDL-related measures with temporal volumes.

Replication. Of the 1,215 associations, 967 (79.6%) replicated in the independent cohort at FDR < 0.05, with the highest replication rates for thickness and volume, indicating robustness across samples.

Mendelian randomization. Bidirectional MR on replicated pairs identified 153 forward causal associations linking 73 metabolites to 25 structural phenotypes at a threshold of FDR-corrected P < 0.05. The GM volume and thickness exhibited the highest number of significant MR associations. And 46 reverse associations where structure influenced metabolite levels, revealing complementary structural effects on metabolic profiles.

Metabolite–disease associations. Logistic and Cox models identified 1,560 cross-sectional and 2,134 longitudinal metabolite–disease associations after correction. Overlap across analyses yielded 1,044 robust associations. Psychiatric disorders showed a higher burden than neurological diseases. Lipoproteins and lipids and ratio-related metabolites dominated both prevalent and incident disease links. Several metabolites, such as cholesterol fractions in LDL subtypes, were associated with a broad set of clinical outcomes.

Mediation analysis. Structural equation models identified 53 significant metabolite–structure–disease mediation pathways. Cortical volume, especially in the temporal pole, superior parietal cortex and precuneus, accounted for substantial portions of indirect effects in metabolite-brain disorders pathways. Parietal and occipital thickness mediated associations for outcomes such as epilepsy and paralytic syndromes. Only pathways with significant indirect effects and consistent directions were retained.

Genetic and environmental contributions. Polygenic risk score–based mediation identified 24 significant pathways where metabolite PRS influenced disease risk through structural variation, mainly involving lipoprotein and lipid and GM volume. Environmental models identified 875 significant mediation pathways linking modifiable exposures—diet, physical activity, mental health indicators and pollution—to disease risk through metabolite alterations and subsequent structural changes. Most environmental mediation effects were partial but consistent across related exposures and phenotypes.

Drug-target prioritization. By integrating metabolite–disease MR with cis-MR of plasma proteins, we screened 313 metabolites and 1,811 proteins. A total of 186 metabolites showed causal evidence for disease risk. Protein-level analyses prioritized 13 proteins linked to 10 brain disorders, several overlapping with known druggable genes. Targets such as APOE, PARP1, LPA, ABO, FES and MST1 emerged as candidates that may mediate metabolite–structure–disease pathways and offer translational value.

Conclusion

In this large-scale imaging–metabolomics study, we delineate a comprehensive map linking plasma metabolites to brain structure, establish their relevance to a broad spectrum of brain disorders, quantify genetic and environmental contributions, and prioritize translationally relevant therapeutic targets. By integrating 313 circulating metabolites, 272 brain structural phenotypes, multilevel association and Mendelian randomization analyses, phenome-wide disease links, mediation modeling, and genetic and environmental risk decomposition, our findings bridge metabolic dysregulation with structural vulnerability and disease susceptibility at an unprecedented resolution.

We show that cortical thickness and volume—particularly in the superior parietal cortex, temporal pole, and precuneus—are strongly associated with lipoprotein and lipid-related metabolites, and that these regions mediate key pathways between dyslipidemia and psychiatric and neurological disorders. Contrary to long-held assumptions that HDL is neuroprotective and LDL detrimental, our results reveal predominantly negative associations between HDL-related metabolites and brain structure and positive associations for LDL-related molecules, with bidirectional MR supporting potential causal effects. These patterns, replicated in an independent cohort, highlight the nuanced and function-dependent roles of lipid cargo and apolipoprotein composition in maintaining neuronal integrity.

At the disease level, we identify extensive metabolic signatures across psychiatric disorders, substance-use-related phenotypes, depression, movement disorders, multiple sclerosis, diabetic neuropathy, epilepsy, and delirium. Structural mediation analysis demonstrates that dyslipidemia influences these disorders partly through localized GM alterations in high-metabolic-demand regions such as the precuneus and temporal pole, providing a mechanistic basis for region-specific vulnerability.

By quantifying upstream determinants of metabolite–structure pathways, we show that lipoprotein and lipid-related polygenic risk impacts psychiatric disorders through structural remodeling, complementing known shared genetic architectures. Environmental analyses reveal that modifiable factors—including poor mental health, pollution, diet (salt, coffee, dietary diversity), and physical inactivity—affect disease risk partly via metabolic disruption and subsequent reductions in GM volume. Ranking genetic versus environmental contributions to metabolites and structure clarifies how inherited and lifestyle exposures jointly shape neurobiological resilience.

Finally, leveraging cis-MR of ~1,800 plasma proteins, we prioritize 13 druggable targets with potential to modulate disease-associated metabolites. Notably, APOE emerges as a cross-disorder therapeutic node influencing cholesterol, APOB, and lipoprotein metabolism, while PARP1—largely overlooked in psychiatric contexts—appears relevant through inflammatory metabolic pathways. These findings underscore the promise of metabolite-modulating interventions for diverse brain disorders.

Together, this work provides a unified framework connecting metabolic signatures, brain structural architecture, and clinical phenotypes. By integrating genetics, environment, metabolomics, neuroimaging, and disease mechanisms, our study advances etiological understanding and identifies biologically grounded intervention points. These insights may inform targeted prevention, guide metabolic and lifestyle-based treatment strategies, and accelerate translational progress toward precision approaches for brain health.
