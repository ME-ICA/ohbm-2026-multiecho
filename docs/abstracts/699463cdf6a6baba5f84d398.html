Introduction

Spontaneous large-scale brain activities during resting state exhibiting highly reproducible spatiotemporal patterns1 consume the majority of brain energy and are generally considered to maintain the fundamental architecture for information processing and cognition. Elucidating the physiological origins of these large-scale functional features is of great importance for understanding the organizational principles of brain function.

A prevailing view is that macroscopic brain functional activity is jointly shaped by neural dynamics of local circuits and global coupling imposed by the structural connectome. Guided by this perspective, brain network modeling, as a promising approach of whole-brain biophysical modeling, has proposed various potential dynamical scenarios for the generation and dynamic evolution of brain functional networks2. 

Recently, there has been increasing interest in regional heterogeneity of local circuit3. In contrast, the topological features of structural connectivity, such as rich-club organization, which also contribute to heterogeneous neural dynamics, have been relatively underexplored in studies of whole-brain resting-state dynamics. Regarding how brain dynamics at the network level generate novel behaviors together with the interplay of structural network topology and ultimately lead to the emergence of spatiotemporal patterns of brain function, mechanistic research remains lacking.

Methods

Here, we adopted the large-scale modeling framework proposed by Deco et al.4, constructing a homogeneous brain network model based on reduced Wong-Wang model and group averaged structural connectivity from HCP S1200 dataset under Desikan-Killiany parcellation to investigate how the interaction between brain dynamics and heterogeneous structural connectivity topology influences the emergence of resting-state functional spatiotemporal patterns. 

We derived the Jacobian matrix of the network model and analyzed how the excitatory–inhibitory  coupled synaptic dynamics behave under different excitatory–inhibitory ratios, as well as how these dynamics shape the emergent network behavior when interlinked by the structural connectome. Under varying global excitatory–inhibitory ratios, we compared simulated and empirical BOLD signals in terms of multiple spatiotemporal properties—including functional connectivity, dynamic functional connectivity, and functional connectivity gradients—and identified the dynamical regime that yields the optimal fit to resting-state functional data.

Through eigen-decomposition and topological analysis of the structural connectivity matrix, we then delineated a structural core subnetwork that supports this optimal dynamical state. To further probe the physiological and cognitive significance of this core subnetwork, we quantified its associations with gene expression maps and meta-analytic maps of cognitive functions, and assessed these associations using spin-tests. Finally, we examined the robustness of our findings in the context of models incorporating regional heterogeneity and inter-individual variability in empirical HCP S1200 data.

Results

Dynamical analysis shows that when the coupling is sufficiently strong and the excitatory–inhibitory ratio is tuned to a specific balance point, the excitatory–inhibitory coupled synaptic dynamics place the system in a critical regime, characterized by a markedly larger dynamical range and longer intrinsic timescales. When embedded in the structural connectome, the resulting network dynamics are jointly shaped by highly stable local dynamics arising from local circuit and weakly stable global dynamics emerging from structural connectome coupling. 

Under different E–I ratios, the global dynamics are dominated by distinct network modes, and two of these modes can reach criticality. Among them, when the network mode centered on parietal regions is tuned to the critical regime, the system exhibits the optimal fit to resting-state functional connectivity, which significantly enhances interhemispheric spatial coupling in static functional connectivity and global temporal coupling in dynamic functional connectivity, thereby transforming the functional connectivity gradient from an structural connectivity like pattern of cross-hemispheric segregation to a more functional connectivity like pattern of cross-hemispheric integration.

Topological analysis indicates that the critical network modes arise from two structural core subnetworks formed by the strongest edges in the structural connectome, which correspond to the two dominant eigenvectors of the structural connectivity matrix. The parietal core subnetwork, in particular, is associated with gene expression profiles related to mitochondrial and ribosomal function, as well as with top-down cognitive functions such as working memory and planning. Moreover, in models incorporating regional heterogeneity, the critical state of the parietal core subnetwork remains a necessary component of the solution that achieves optimal resting-state functional fitting. Finally, using the autocorrelation function of regional BOLD signals as a proxy for criticality, we validate in the HCP S1200 dataset that the criticality of the parietal core subnetwork is associated with FC spatial and temporal coupling and with structure–function coupling.

Conclusion

Our results delineate how the interaction between local circuit dynamics and global coupling via the structural connectome shapes whole-brain network dynamics, and they provide a candidate mechanistic account of how these dynamics give rise to the emergence of spatiotemporal patterns of brain function. 

Across different excitatory–inhibitory ratios, the network dynamics recruit distinct dominant network modes, while core subnetworks in the structural connectome can drive their associated modes toward criticality. When these core subnetworks become critical, they enable the system to partially escape its structural constraints and organize large-scale spatial and temporal synchronization. Among them, the critical state of the parietal core subnetwork leads to realistic resting-state functional connectivity.

Integrating these dynamical analyses with the physiological and cognitive associations of the parietal core subnetwork, we propose a unified hypothesis for the emergence of resting-state brain function: supported by dense structural connectivity, high metabolic costs, and elevated cellular activity, the parietal core subnetwork—with a specific level of excitatory–inhibitory balance—enters a critical dynamical regime that organizes long-range spatiotemporal correlations in functional networks. This specific network dynamics supports the brain’s top-down cognitive functions and contributes to the spatio-temporal patterns of resting-state spontaneous brain activity.
