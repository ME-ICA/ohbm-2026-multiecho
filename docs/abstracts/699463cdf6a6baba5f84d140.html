Introduction

Musical Mnemonic Training (MMT)1,2 utilizes rhythm and melody to establish a temporal scaffold for verbal learning, facilitating "deep encoding" even in compromised neurological states. This efficacy is hypothetically underpinned by the extensive overlap between brain networks supporting musical and verbal processing. However, existing research predominantly focuses on English speakers, leaving a critical gap regarding the effectiveness of MMT in Chinese-speaking cohorts, where the linguistic characteristics of a tonal language may interact uniquely with musical processing. This study aims to: 1) Delineate the neural mechanisms of MMT across encoding, storage, and retrieval; 2) Evaluate behavioral efficacy in a Chinese-speaking cohort; and 3) Investigate neuroplastic effects supporting aging memory.

Methods

Subjects:
The cohort consisted of 32 native Mandarin-Chinese speakers (aged 55-82 years, residing in Taiwan), randomly assigned to a 'sung' or 'spoken' group. Exclusion criteria included severe psychiatric or neurological disorders, head trauma, cerebrovascular disease, recent general anaesthesia (within 6 months), use of brain-affecting medications, and conditions prohibiting MRI participation. 


General Procedure:
The protocol (fig. 1a) comprised two on-site sessions for behavioral and neuroimaging data collection. Session 1 involved a comprehensive battery of standardized assessments. In Session 2, participants completed four experimental tasks during fMRI. Acoustic stimuli (approx. 85 dB) were delivered binaurally via MRI-compatible headphones, with task presentation controlled by PsychoPy.


ChVLT Task and Stimuli:
Participants performed the Chinese Verbal Learning Test (ChVLT) (fig. 1b) in either the 'spoken' or 'sung' condition, with the 'sung' condition incorporating a simple, repetitive melody mirroring the words' intonation (fig. 1c). Each trial consisted of a uniform list of 9 non-semantically related two-character nouns, presented iteratively across 10 trials. Recall was tested immediately post-presentation, after a distractor, and at delayed intervals (30-minute, 1-day, 1-week).

fMRI Data Acquisition and Preprocessing:MR images were acquired on a 3.0T GE Discovery MR750 scanner at Shuang-Ho Hospital, Taiwan. Functional images used a whole-brain gradient-echo EPI sequence (TR=2000 ms, TE=34.0 ms, voxel size=2.5 x 2.5 x 3.0 mm³). The ChVLT used an alternating block design (10 minutes total). Data were preprocessed with CONN 22.v2407. Scans were corrected for slice timing and motion, co-registered, segmented, normalized to MNI space, and spatially smoothed (8-mm FWHM). 

Results

Demographics & Behavioral Performance
Demographic analysis confirmed that the Sung and Spoken groups were well-matched, with no significant differences in age, years of education, MoCA scores, or years of musical training (all p> .05). Behaviorally, a mixed-design ANOVA on learning trials (A1–A10) revealed a significant main effect of Trial (F = 10.636, p < .001), confirming robust learning across all participants (fig. 2a). However, neither the main effect of Condition (p = .226) nor the Condition × Trial interaction (p = .222) reached significance, indicating that the rate of initial information acquisition was comparable between the Sung and Spoken groups. Crucially, despite similar initial learning curves, post-hoc comparisons (Bonferroni corrected) revealed a specific advantage for memory durability in the Sung condition. The Sung group demonstrated significantly superior recall at 30 minutes (p = .034) and 1 day (p = .009) post-learning, with a trend toward significance at 1 week (p = .058).

Functional Connectivity (Sung > Spoken)
gPPI ROI-to-ROI analysis (controlled for age and MoCA) was conducted to determine the neural mechanism underlying this superior retention (fig. 2b). We seeded the analysis in the right-lateralized melodic network, defined a priori as the Right Thalamus3, Right pSTG4, and Right Frontal Pole1,2,4. This network exerted significant functional connectivity modulation on key executive regions during the Sung condition compared to the Spoken condition. Specifically, robust connectivity was observed between the melodic network and the Anterior Cingulate Cortex (ACC) (p-FDR < .05), consistent with the ACC’s role as a central integration hub. Notably, these right-sided seeds exhibited significant cross-hemispheric connectivity to the Left Frontal Pole (FP l) (p-FDR < .05). This pattern suggests that MMT facilitates memory by establishing a bridge for inter-hemispheric transfer, rather than isolating processing to the right hemisphere.

Conclusion

Our findings reveal a critical behavioral dissociation: while initial learning rates were comparable between conditions, Musical Mnemonic Training (MMT) produced significantly superior long-term retention, suggesting a deeper quality of encoding. This durability is underpinned by Thalamo-Cortical-Auditory integration, where rhythmic drives from the Thalamus enhance attentional engagement ("Spotlight" mechanism) and right-hemisphere melodic structures "scaffold" verbal information. By establishing compensatory connectivity to the Left Frontal Pole (consistent with HAROLD5 model), MMT creates a robust, dual-coded memory trace. Thus, we conclude that MMT does not merely speed up learning, but specifically recruits a compensatory network to protect verbal memories against decay in aging adults.
