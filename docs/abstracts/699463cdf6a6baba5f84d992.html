Introduction

It is well known that movements are preceded by slow cortical (readiness) potentials and modulation of ongoing beta activity in primary motor cortex (MI), followed by evoked responses related to proprioceptive and sensory feedback. Their magnetic equivalents can be precisely localized to the sensorimotor cortex using MEG1 and similar modulation of brain activity has been observed during the simple anticipation of somatosensory input2. Passive (externally generated) movements have been less studied but likely involve sensorimotor changes related to both movement-related feedback as well as anticipated sensory input. Pilot results from a previous study revealed the presence of readiness fields in anticipation of predictable but not unpredictable passive movements3. The objective of the current study was to clarify the existence of readiness fields during passive movements, and understand their functional role in this presumably passive process.

Methods

We recorded MEG in 10 healthy adults during passive movements of the index finger using a custom-built platform containing a pneumatically-driven piston. Passive movements occurred under conditions of predictable (constant = 3.1 s) and unpredictable (random = 1.6-4.6 s) movement intervals, in addition to self-generated movements and (predictable) tactile stimulation of the same finger. SAM and Event-Related Beamformers (as implemented in the BrainWave MATLAB toolbox4, https://github.com/cheynelab) were used to perform source analysis. Time courses of time-locked evoked responses and beta activity (15-30 Hz) in the contralateral sensorimotor cortex were analyzed. In addition, we recorded electromyography (EMG) of agonist and antagonist muscles of the right index finger, force sensor activity between the pad of the finger tip and the pneumatically-driven piston, and accelerometer activity of the right index finger (Figure 1). All three signals were synchronized online with the neuromagnetic activity.

Results

Similar to previous results, slow pre-movement brain activity resembling the self-generated movement readiness field was observed in the predictable, but not unpredictable, passive movement condition (Figure 2). Conversely, early modulation of sensorimotor beta prior to movement onset was observed only during self-generated movement. Force sensor recordings reveal decreased force during predictable over unpredictable passive movement conditions, in the absence of any increases of muscle activity over baseline values (as revealed by EMG recordings).

Conclusion

In the absence of active muscle contraction, we deduce that this reduction in force during passive movements is related to the active relaxation of antagonist muscles (beyond baseline levels) in order to facilitate the expected movement. Thus, premovement activity in MI (that is not related to the anticipation of sensory input) is likely driving control of antagonist muscle tone. Furthermore, onset of this activity was decoupled from beta suppression in the same cortical areas related to active movement planning and predictable sensory input. We conclude that the observed readiness fields prior to passive movement reflect pre-movement activity in MI related to descending output to the antagonist muscles5 suggesting an additional role for the readiness potential / fields in sensorimotor control.
