Introduction

Cerebrovascular reactivity (CVR) reflects the ability of cerebral blood vessels to dilate in response to a vasoactive stimulus, a capacity which is impaired in neurodegenerative disease such as Alzheimer’s1.  

CVR is commonly assessed using BOLD-fMRI signal change, assuming it reflects predominantly cerebral blood flow (CBF) changes, arterial spin labelling (ASL) provides direct measurement of CBF (ml/100g/min). Nevertheless, CBF estimation using ASL relies on the fitting of the data using a kinetic model that is dependent on several parameters such as labelling efficiency (α), a parameter related to the proportion of tagged blood achieved during the labelling process. While a value of α = 0.85 is recommended for pseudo-continuous ASL (pCASL)2, the true labelling efficiency depends on blood velocity through the labelling plane. This may lead to underestimation of CBF change during hypercapnia when flow velocity increases.

Subject-specific estimates of labelling efficiency can be obtained using phase-contrast MRI combined with a sequence-dependent efficiency model 3. In this work, we evaluate the impact of labelling efficiency correction on ASL-derived CVR metrics and compare these with BOLD CVR measurements.  We hypothesised that ASL CVR would show a linear relationship with BOLD CVR across subjects, and that labelling efficiency-calibrated ASL CBF would improve correspondence between ASL- and BOLD-derived CVR.

Methods

Data acquisition

Data from 26 female participants (33.5 ± 4.6 years old) were acquired on a 3T Siemens Prisma scanner. 

pCASL data with six post-labelling delays (PLDs) (250–1500 ms), a 1400 ms labelling duration, and 3.5×3.5×4.5 mm³ resolution were acquired during normocapnia (6 min) and hypercapnia (5% CO₂; 3 min). Data were analysed with FSL tools4, yielding maps of CBF and arterial arrival time (AAT). Right- and left side ICA velocities were calculated from the phase-contrast data and labelling efficiencies estimated using a sequence-dependent model3 .

BOLD fMRI data were acquired using a gradient-echo EPI sequence (2.4 mm isotropic, multiband factor 6, TR/TE = 800/30 ms) during a boxcar hypercapnic challenge with 5% CO₂ (two blocks of 90s normocapnia and 60s hypercapnia). Data were preprocessed using FSL FEAT, including motion correction, spatial smoothing (4 mm FWHM), and high-pass temporal filtering (200 s).

Cerebrovascular reactivity quantification

ASL CVR was computed as the change in CBF between hypercapnia and normocapnia divided by the corresponding change in mean end-tidal CO₂ (PetCO₂). Two ASL-CVR measures were derived: Absolute CVR (ml/100g/min/mmHg) and relative CVR (%/mmHg) normalised by normocapnic perfusion. Both CVR measures were computed with and without subject-specific labelling efficiency correction.

 For BOLD-CVR, PetCO₂ traces were demeaned, peak-interpolated, and temporally aligned to the whole-brain BOLD signal via cross-correlation. CVR maps were estimated using a general linear model with the shifted PETCO₂ regressor sampled at BOLD repetition time. Structural images were segmented to obtain grey-matter masks. For each subject, ASL and BOLD CVR were summarised as the median value within the grey-matter mask in their respective spaces.

Statistical analysis

Associations between ASL CVR and BOLD CVR were assessed across subjects using Pearson correlation and linear regression (statistical significance at p < 0.05). The analysis was conducted both with and without labelling-efficiency correction. 

Results

Linear regression revealed no significant association between BOLD CVR and ASL CVR (Figure 2). ASL CVR expressed as a relative measure showed a weak positive, non-significant correlation with BOLD CVR for both labelling efficiency-calibrated (R² = 0.042, p = 0.32) and uncalibrated measures (R² = 0.048, p = 0.29).  

No association was observed between BOLD CVR and absolute ASL CVR, with negligible correlations for both uncalibrated (R² = 0.002, p = 0.824) and calibrated measures (R² < 0.001, p = 0.947).

Conclusion

Our results show no significant linear correspondence between ASL (absolute or relative) and BOLD CVR across subjects. Additionally, correction for subject-specific labelling efficiency did not improve agreement between ASL and BOLD CVR.

These findings highlight the importance of interpreting CVR measures in light of how the signal originated. Whilst ASL CVR quantifies changes in CBF, BOLD CVR measures a combination of different haemodynamic changes including in CBF, cerebral blood volume and oxygen metabolism. Although it is assumed that BOLD CVR predominantly reflects changes in CBF due to the stimulus, our results further demonstrate that the relationship between ASL and BOLD CVR might be affected by other factors. One such factor is the paradigm design, which differed between modalities: BOLD CVR was estimated using a block design, whereas ASL CVR was derived from a steady-state stimulus, required for the multi-PLD ASL acquisition. Consequently, differences in CVR between modalities may reflect the fact that CBF changes are expected to reach a plateau during steady-state hypercapnia, while responses during shorter block-design hypercapnic periods may vary across participants.

Further work is required to clarify the sources of discrepancy between ASL- and BOLD-derived CVR and to determine the conditions under which ASL provides a reliable assessment of cerebrovascular reactivity.
