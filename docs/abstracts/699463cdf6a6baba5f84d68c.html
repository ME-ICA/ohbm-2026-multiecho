Introduction

Motor recovery relies on the functional reorganization of the motor network, yet underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Previous fMRI research has characterized stroke-related alterations in regional activity and connectivity, consistently demonstrating global overactivation of motor regions during motor task performance, reflecting widespread network reorganization.1 However, it remains unknown whether and how regional representations of motor control for distinct motor functions change after stroke. Representational similarity analysis (RSA) provides a multivariate framework for quantifying regionalneural representations.2 Unlike univariate approaches, RSA assesses the similarity of multivoxel patterns, revealing whether representations of specific motor functions become more distinct or diffuse, thereby indicating reorganization following stroke. Motor recovery has been described to follow specific gradients, e.g., evolving from proximal to distal muscles and engaging basal before more complex muscle synergies,suggesting that distinct neural mechanisms may drive the recovery of different aspects of motor control.3–5 Hence, we hypothesized that applying RSA to motor tasks varying in effector specificity and synergy complexity — spanning hand, forearm, elbow, and shoulder movements — may help to further elucidate mechanisms underlying functional reorganization after stroke.

Methods

Forty-one chronic stroke patients (>6 months post-stroke) and twenty-nine healthy controls completed task-based fMRI while performing four upper limb motor tasks: repetitive hand, forearm, elbow, and shoulder movements of the paretic limb. These tasks were designed to reflect a gradient of effector specificity and synergy complexity, allowing us to assess competing organizational principles within the motor network.

RSA was implemented using a novel maximum-likelihood RSA (MLRSA) approach that supports formal hypothesis testing via multilevel modeling. Two competing representational hypotheses were specified and tested within predefined motor regions of interest (primary motor cortex, dorsal premotor cortex, supplementary motor area): (1) a proximal-to-distal spatial gradient reflecting canonical somatotopic motor cortex organization, and (2) a synergy-complexity gradient — where neural representations organize around distinct complexity and coordination demands of involved motor synergies. Hypotheses were formalized as linear components in the representational model, and statistical inference was performed via leave-one-component-out (LOCO) analysis, quantifying each component's unique contribution by comparing different models. Likelihood ratio statistics and effect sizes were derived to enable formal hypothesis testing and interpretable model comparison.

Results

In healthy controls, multilevel RSA confirmed the expected proximal-to-distal somatotopic organization within the primary motor cortex (M1). In contrast, stroke patients exhibited a reorganized representational pattern characterized by additional synergy-complexity representation in M1, indicating a shift toward coding motor actions according to coordination demands alongside preserved spatial organization. In patients, the dorsal premotor cortex (PMd) showed an amplified proximal-to-distal gradient pattern, suggesting compensatory enhancement of spatial motor coding — possibly supporting the recruitment of alternative descending pathways. Conversely, the supplementary motor area (SMA) demonstrated a pronounced increase in the representation of synergy complexity, indicating increased functional specialization in integrating and coordinating diverse synergies that support post-stroke motor control. Together, these representational changes defined a pattern of functional reorganization in which spatial representations appeared amplified in PMd, while synergy-complexity encoding appeared enhanced in M1 and SMA — a dissociation highlighting complementary roles in post-stroke motor recovery.

Conclusion

This study represents the first application of multilevel RSA to systematically investigate the structural representation of motor network reorganization in chronic stroke patients. By modeling competing organizational principles within the motor network, we reveal that functional reorganization involves a systematic restructuring of neural representations of distinct motor functions — a dimension not captured by conventional univariate or connectivity analyses. Stroke patients demonstrated preserved or amplified somatotopic organization alongside emergent synergy-complexity representations. The distinct regional changes in motor-control representation suggest that motor network reorganization may rely on task- and region-specific transformations of neural motor representations. These findings establish MLRSA as a novel tool and analytical framework to further our understanding of plasticity mechanisms in the human brain and elucidate how changes in neural representational geometry relate to functional reorganization after stroke.
