Introduction

Sensory processing differences are a transdiagnostic feature common in neurodevelopmental conditions such as autism1 and are associated with difficulties in daily functioning, social participation, mental health, and sleep2. The thalamus plays a central role in relaying and integrating sensory input and modulating attention, with altered thalamocortical organisation being implicated in atypical sensory processing3. Functional MRI (fMRI) studies have reported differences in thalamocortical connectivity in autism, although results vary3. This may partly reflect the limitations of traditional resting-state fMRI paradigms, which may not capture the sensory and attentional demands of real-world environments. Here we combine more naturalistic stimuli (i.e., video viewing), with ultra-high field 7-Tesla (7T) fMRI to provide a more ecologically valid approach to examine large-scale network interactions at high temporal and spatial resolution4. We investigated thalamocortical coupling during naturalistic video viewing and tested whether thalamic connectivity with large-scale cortical networks was associated with individual differences in sensory reactivity, measured using the Glasgow Sensory Questionnaire. 

Methods

Participants

Twenty-five adults (10 autistic [5 male], 15 non-autistic [5 male]; mean age (years) = 31.32 years (SD 8.01), median 29, range 20-48) participated in a 7T naturalistic fMRI study.

 

Data acquisition

Data were acquired on a 7T MRI system (Siemens MAGNETOM Terra.X) with 8Tx/32Rx head coil (Nova medical), using a multiband echo-planar imaging sequence (TR = 1 s, TE = 25 ms, flip angle = 45°, voxel size = 1.79 × 1.79 × 1.80 mm³, multiband factor = 5). Six segments were acquired during which participants viewed short video clips varying in social content and cognitive load (Figure 1), totalling 655 seconds (655 volumes). A high-resolution T1-weighted MPRAGE anatomical image (0.65-mm isotropic) was acquired for registration.

To characterise participants’ sensory reactivity, each participant also completed the Glasgow Sensory Questionnaire (GSQ), a 42-item questionnaire investigating both hyper- and hypo-reactivities in seven modalities: visual; auditory; gustatory; olfactory; tactile; vestibular and proprioceptive. Higher scores indicate more sensory challenges.

Data preprocessing

Functional data were denoised using NORDIC and corrected for susceptibility-induced distortions using FSL topup. Preprocessing was performed with fMRIPrep5 (version 25.0.0), including rigid body motion correction and registration to MNI152NLin2009cAsym space. Data were high-pass filtered at 0.008Hz and ICA-based denoising was applied using FSL ICA-FIX with the HCP 7 T classifier, followed by manual verification. Data were spatially smoothed with a 3.6 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel using FSL, and residual white matter and cerebrospinal fluid signals were regressed out using Nilearn.

Average cortical time series were extracted separately for each subject and video segment using the Schaefer 400-parcel atlas. Parcels were assigned to one of the seven canonical Yeo sub-networks (visual, somatomotor, dorsal attention, salience/ventral attention, limbic, control, and default mode), and sub-network-level time series were computed by averaging parcel-wise timeseries within each sub-network at each timepoint. A thalamic region of interest was defined using a mask of the entire bilateral thalami derived from the FSL Oxford Thalamic Connectivity atlas and mean thalamic BOLD time series were extracted for each participant and video segment by averaging across all voxels within the mask. Thalamocortical functional connectivity was quantified by computing Pearson correlations between the thalamic and the 7 Yeo network time series, giving one connectivity estimate per subject and video.

Statistical analysis

Associations between GSQ scores and thalamocortical connectivity were assessed using linear mixed-effects models fitted separately for each Yeo network, with participant and video included as random effects with age, sex, and mean framewise displacement included as covariates. Multiple comparisons across networks were controlled using false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Analyses were conducted across the combined sample, reflecting the transdiagnostic focus on sensory reactivity.

Results

Higher GSQ scores were significantly associated with stronger thalamus-somatomotor network connectivity (β = 0.0068, SE = 0.0020, t = 3.39, p = 0.0029, FDR-corrected p = 0.020). A positive association was also observed between GSQ scores and thalamus-salience/ventral attention network connectivity (β = 0.0032, SE = 0.0012, t = 2.60, p = 0.017), although this effect did not survive correction for multiple comparisons (FDR-corrected p = 0.061) (Figure 2).

Conclusion

Using ultra-high field 7T fMRI during naturalistic video viewing, we found that higher sensory reactivity was associated with stronger thalamus-somatomotor network connectivity. Given the thalamus’ role in sensory relay and modulation, this finding suggests that thalamus-somatomotor coupling may contribute to individual differences in sensory reactivity - a domain that can have a significant impact on everyday experiences and wellbeing and is a prominent feature of neurodevelopmental conditions such as autism. The potential role of thalamic interactions with attentional systems warrants further investigation in larger samples. Future work will examine whether these effects are modulated by stimulus properties such as social content or cognitive load and will explore connectivity patterns using more fine-grained thalamic parcellations.
