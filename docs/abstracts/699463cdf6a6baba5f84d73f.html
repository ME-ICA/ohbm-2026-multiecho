Introduction

Structural similarity network analysis provides a powerful framework to study coordinated patterns of brain morphology in health and disease1. One recent, biologically validated approach to estimate structural similarity is Morphometric Inverse Divergence (MIND), which uses the Kullback–Leibler divergence between multivariate distributions of structural MRI features to quantify similarity between cortical areas2. MIND has been applied to characterise distributed morphological changes across the lifespan and in neuropsychiatric disorders3. 

As MIND is increasingly applied to address a broad range of questions, it is important to understand how input parameters influence its estimation. For example, the number of surface mesh sample vertices at which feature values are extracted can bias similarity estimates between cortical areas3,4, potentially influencing comparisons across subjects and between brain regions. Here, we outline practical and methodological considerations for applying MIND, quantify the effects of key methodological factors on MIND estimation, and recommend tools and sensitivity analyses to mitigate these effects.

Methods

MIND network estimation and bias investigation

MIND similarity between two regions is computed as the inverse of the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between their feature distributions. KL can be computed directly from probability density functions (PDFs), estimated from empirical sample distributions using a k-nearest neighbours (kNN) approach, or computed between two gaussians using a closed-form parametric solution (Figure 1a).

kNN enables efficient estimation of MIND (Figure 1b) from multiple morphometric features. However, accuracy depends on input parameters, including the number of samples used and the distributions of input feature values. To characterise these effects, we simulated underlying feature distributions using gaussians with empirically-derived parameters. For a pair of simulated distributions, we computed “true” MIND via the parametric KL solution and estimated MIND with the kNN approach, then quantified estimation bias as the percentage difference between the two (Figure 1c).

Number of samples

The number of samples (vertices or voxels) from which features are extracted depends on image processing and can vary both between subjects (due to brain size differences), and between regions within a subject (due to inhomogeneous parcellation region sizes). We therefore examined how both global and regional variation in sample number influenced MIND estimation bias.

Duplicate feature values and resampling

kNN estimation involves computing the Euclidean distances between samples. If a sample has a neighbour with identical feature values (a ‘duplicate’), the distance computed is zero. In this case, kNN KL cannot be computed so the sample is selectively excluded. To avoid data loss in the case of high numbers of duplicates, kernel density estimation is used to approximate the probability density and generate unique samples (‘resampling’). Duplicates can arise in empirical data due to true anatomical homogeneity or preprocessing steps, such as rounding. We modelled how increasing proportions of duplicate values affect MIND estimation bias.

Empirical data: dHCP and parcellation atlases

To illustrate how these issues arise in practice, we analysed neonatal data from the developing Human Connectome Project (dHCP5). T2-weighted scans from n=752 neonates, aged 26-43 weeks postmenstrual age, were processed through a pipeline of surface reconstruction and feature extraction (cortical thickness, mean curvature, sulcal depth, surface area and volume).

We also assessed parcellation inhomogeneity in 10 commonly-used adult cortical surface parcellations (Figure 2d), using the Gini coefficient: the mean pairwise difference in number of vertices between parcellation regions normalised by mean number of vertices. A low Gini coefficient indicates that a parcellation is homogeneous, i.e. its regions contain similar numbers of vertices, while a high Gini coefficient indicates that a parcellation is inhomogeneous, i.e. its regions contain different numbers of vertices.

Results

Low sample numbers inflate MIND

Simulations showed that the bias in MIND estimations increased as the number of samples became small (Figure 2a). This relationship was also present between pairs of regions if the number of samples in either region was small (Figure 2b). Empirically, the number of surface vertices increased with age in the dHCP cohort (r = 0.906, p < 0.001; Figure 2c), which would inflate mean MIND estimates for younger neonates. Analysis of common adult surface parcellations also revealed high prevalence of inhomogeneity in regional sample counts (Figure 2d).

Duplicate values influence MIND estimation in the univariate case

Simulations revealed that duplicate values biased MIND estimates at both the edge and network levels in univariate data. For a pair of simulated regions, independently varying the proportion of duplicates produced bidirectional MIND biases, with MIND being strongly inflated when the proportion of duplicate values in both distributions increased (Figure 2e). At the network level, introducing a random proportion of duplicates to each distribution produced a MIND network which was poorly correlated with the original, duplicate-free network (Figure 2f). 

In neonatal data, duplicates were common for younger neonates, particularly in cortical thickness (~10%) and volume (~12%), and decreased over gestational age as gyrification and feature variability increased (Figure 2g). Duplicates were evenly distributed across the cortical surface and rarely overlapped between features, resulting in very few duplicates in the multivariate case. In the univariate case, bias introduced by duplicates was effectively corrected using a resampling approach (Figure 2h).

Conclusion

We conclude with a list of recommendations to researchers using MIND:

  1. Downsample the cohort to a consistent surface or volumetric template space to reduce between-brain sample number differences.

  2. Use a homogeneous parcellation atlas to reduce within-brain sample number differences. 

  3. Resample univariate data to attenuate bias introduced by duplicate values.

Overall, MIND provides a powerful and scalable method for structural similarity mapping. The practical recommendations and sensitivity analyses outlined here can help researchers ensure that their MIND-based analyses are both robust and interpretable.
