Introduction

Aggression frequently occurs during social interactions and can arise from multiple, often overlapping motivations, such as instrumental (obtaining a reward), reactive (in response to threat, provocation, or frustration), or defensive (as a mechanism to avoid harm to the self). These diverse motivations are often difficult to disentangle and may co-occur within the same interaction. Uncovering neural mechanisms may increase our understanding of this complex type of social interaction. However, behavioral and neural aspects of social interactions, including aggression, have, to date, been examined primarily through one-person experimental designs, limiting insight into how these dynamics unfold between interacting individuals. Hyperscanning, which measures brain activity from multiple people simultaneously, offers a way to explore the neural underpinnings of these interactions. Yet it remains unknown whether neural dynamics during real interactions differ meaningfully from those observed in simulated ones. This pilot study examines how reward, provocation, and self-harm avoidance influence reciprocal behavioral and neural dynamics during real versus simulated aggressive interactions. We focus on three core Theory of Mind (ToM) regions implicated in understanding others’ mental states: the right temporoparietal junction (rTPJ), dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (dmPFC), and precuneus (PCun).

Methods

We developed a task, the Self-Harm Aggression Reward Paradigm (SHARP) in which 24 participants (10 women), paired in 12 unacquainted dyads and matched by gender, chose between administering aversive sounds to themself (self-harm) or the other person of the dyad (aggression) in exchange for monetary rewards. Participants played two runs of the task, with 48 trial per run, for a total of 96 trials, while undergoing functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) scanning with active noise cancellation. Each run was divided into two blocks, one real and one simulated interaction block, in this order. The trials presented three different reward conditions: a condition in which the reward for the self-harm option was higher than for the aggression option, a condition in which the reward was the same for the two options, and a condition in which the reward for aggression was higher than for self-harm. An actor-partner interdependence-like model (APIM) was used to analyze behavioral and neural data. The APIM computes the actor effect (how much one person’s current variable is influenced by his/her own past variable, e.g. behavior or brain activation) and partner effects (how much one person’s current variable is influenced by the other’s past variable) for both members of the dyad. Separate models were run for real and simulated interaction blocks.

Results

Behavioral results show that the partner effect was present only in real, not simulated, interactions: participants with greater punishment sensitivity chose aggression less frequently after their opponent chose aggression (OR = 0.64, 95% CI = [0.41, 0.98], p = .041). Reward strongly influenced behavior in both block types: overall participants preferred the self-harm option unless the reward motivated aggression, and higher self-reported sensitivity to reward positively predicted the probability of choosing aggression (OR = 2.12, 95% CI = [1.28, 3.50], p = .004). Participants selected aggression more often in the simulated blocks compared to the real interaction blocks (t (21) = 4.209, p < .001). In real interaction blocks, we observe an actor effect, meaning that participants were more likely to continue selecting aggression when selecting it in the previous trial, especially in the second run (OR = 2.38, 95% CI = [1.03, 5.52], p = .043. The analyses of neural data also revealed partner effects in two ToM regions, the rTPJ [t = -2.23, 95% CI = [-0.13, -0.01], p = .026] and the PCun [t = -3.67, 95% CI = [-0.17, -0.05], p < .001], during real but not simulated interactions: participant’s activation in the rTPJ and PCun in the previous trial negatively predicted the opponent’s rTPJ and PCun activation in the following trial, respectively, suggesting a leader-follower dynamic in decision-making. We further ran exploratory brain-behavior models to predict behavior from the previous neural activation in the three ToM ROIs. These models showed that, in real interaction blocks, participants’ own activation of the dmPFC in the previous trial negatively predicted selecting aggression in the subsequent trial [OR = 0.85, 95% CI = [0.73, 1.00], p = .044], suggesting intrapersonal self-control processes during decision-making. Instead, activation of rTPJ positively predicted partner’s aggression in the following trial [OR = 1.20, 95% CI = [1.01, 1.43], p = .038]. There were no significant results in simulated blocks.

Conclusion

Our findings support the use of hyperscanning for investigating the behavioral and neural correlates of aggressive interactions, revealing clear distinctions between real and simulated interactions. Partner effects emerged only during real interactions, with participants adjusting their aggression in response to the behavior of the other, particularly those with higher sensitivity to punishment. Together with the negative partner effects at the neural level in the rTPJ and PCun, and the results of the exploratory brain-behavior models, our findings suggest a leader-follower dynamic in aggressive interactions in the context of reward, punishment and self-harm avoidance. In this dynamic, one player in the pair shows lower aggressive behavior yet engages the ToM regions, especially the rTPJ and PCun, to anticipate the partner’s aggression and lower ToM engagement, reflecting an asymmetry in perspective-taking or social regulation. Our findings demonstrate that hyperscanning can effectively capture behavioral and neural dynamics that are absent, or not properly detectable, in simulated interactions.
