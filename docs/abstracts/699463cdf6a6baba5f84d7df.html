Introduction

Predicting functional outcomes after stroke is critical during the acute phase, when clinical decisions such as mechanical thrombectomy or intravenous thrombolysis must be made rapidly. While machine learning models have demonstrated promising prognostic performance, most rely solely on clinical features or on neuroimaging combined with clinical data collected hours or days after stroke, limiting immediate applicability [1]. Additionally, deep learning approaches often lack interpretability and are typically unimodal, providing limited insight for clinical decision-making. 
Here, we present an interpretable vision-language model to predict 90-day functional outcomes (modified Rankin Scale, mRS) from acute-phase data, including diffusion MRI (DWI), FLAIR, radiology reports, and clinical features. Our approach aims to (i) improve prognostic accuracy through multimodal integration and (ii) provide interpretability at multiple levels: unimodal interpretability highlights relevant brain regions and key sentences from radiology reports, while multimodal interpretability captures cross-modal interactions, enhancing transparency and clinical utility. 

Methods

This study included 718 patients treated with mechanical thrombectomy between 2015 and 2021 at Lille University Hospital. For each patient, acute-phase data were collected, including baseline FLAIR and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), clinical variables (sex, age, NIHSS, and pre-stroke mRS), and radiology reports. 
Neuroimaging data were preprocessed using SynthStrip for brain extraction, N4ITK bias field correction for FLAIR images, and rigid registration. Radiology reports were cleaned and standardized using the Mistral-Small 3.2 language model, then encoded using DrBERT, a french biomedical language model. Clinical variables were normalized and encoded using a multilayer perceptron (MLP). Data were split into training and testing sets using 5-fold cross-validation. 
The proposed multimodal architecture (Figure 1) consisted of two convolutional neural networks (CNNs), one for each imaging modality, a DrBERT encoder for text representations, and an MLP for clinical features. Modality-specific features were enriched through enrichment module composed of residual gating and cross-modal interaction mechanisms [2], allowing each modality to be contextualized by the others prior to fusion and preventing dominance of any single modality. 
Multimodal fusion was performed using a task-conditioned attention pooling mechanism inspired by Multiple Instance Learning (MIL), enabling interpretable weighting of each modality’s contribution to outcome prediction [3]. The model was trained to predict functional outcome at 90 days using either binary classification (mRS ≤ 2 vs. > 2) or ordinal classification, directly estimating mRS scores from 0 to 6. 
Neuroimaging interpretability was achieved using a framework inspired by U-Net, Feature Pyramid Networks (FPN), and gated attention mechanisms [4]. A top-down pathway generated saliency masks highlighting outcome-relevant brain regions at multiple spatial resolutions prior to pooling. This process was trained using a consistency-based self-supervised loss, enabling unsupervised generation of saliency maps directly from raw images. Similarly, attentive pooling was applied to text tokens to extract sentence-level attention scores, providing interpretable radiology report relevance [5]. 

Results

Multimodal fusion outperformed both unimodal and bimodal approaches. The multimodal model achieved an AUC of 0.885, compared with 0.855 for bimodal models (clinical + neuroimaging or clinical + radiology reports) and 0.83 for the unimodal model using clinical features only. Comparable performance was observed with ordinal outcome prediction, supporting the robustness of the proposed framework. 
Interpretability was assessed at multiple levels (Figure 2). At the fusion level, mean attention weights indicated that clinical features and DWI contributed most strongly to outcome prediction, followed by radiology reports and FLAIR imaging (Figure 2a). Modality-specific interpretations further highlighted model transparency: attention maps over DWI emphasized stroke lesion regions (Figure 2b), while sentence-level attention in radiology reports identified clinically relevant descriptions contributing to the prediction (Figure 2c). 

Conclusion

We present a multimodal vision-language framework for predicting 90-day functional outcomes in the acute phase of stroke. The model integrates neuroimaging, clinical data, and radiology reports collected immediately after stroke, achieving superior performance compared with unimodal and bimodal approaches. Beyond predictive accuracy, the framework provides interpretability at multiple levels, highlighting key imaging regions, report sentences, and clinical features driving each prediction. This combination of robust performance and transparency supports potential clinical utility, offering a decision-support tool to assist clinicians during acute stroke management.
