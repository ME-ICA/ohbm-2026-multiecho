Introduction

Preterm birth is associated with an increased risk of atypical neurodevelopmental trajectories, including a higher prevalence of neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs). Sleep disturbances (SDs) are also highly common in this population and may contribute to later cognitive, emotional, and behavioral difficulties1. Given the role of sleep in synaptic pruning, plasticity, and large-scale network maturation, early sleep disruptions may not only reflect altered brain development but also exacerbate ongoing atypical processes2. Identifying neonatal neural markers that predict later sleep outcomes could therefore improve early risk assessment and guide preventive strategies. Predictive processing mechanisms, such as sensory prediction (SP), reflect the brain’s capacity to anticipate upcoming sensory events and to adjust its responses to repetitive or irrelevant inputs through repetition suppression (RS)3. These mechanisms are thought to reflect the brain’s capacity to form and update internal models and may constitute sensitive markers of early cortical maturation.

Methods

This longitudinal study investigates whether tactile SP and RS, assessed at 35 weeks of gestational age and at two years in children born preterm, are associated with sleep quality at two years. To quantify SP and RS, we used the same tactile oddball–omission stimulation protocol at both time points, recording brain activity using EEG. Sleep quality was monitored at two years over one week using actigraphy and daily sleep diaries. Principal component analysis was applied to extract a composite sleep index. Linear mixed models examined associations between neonatal and toddler tactile SP/RS markers and sleep outcomes, as well as developmental continuity in SP/RS responses across the two assessments.

Results

To date, follow-up data are available for 46 of the 90 infants enrolled (measures still ongoing). Preliminary analyses indicate that larger SP and RS responses are associated with better sleep quality at age two. Analyses of the longitudinal association between neonatal and toddler SP/RS responses revealed developmental continuity.

Conclusion

These results suggest that neonatal predictive processing markers may serve as early functional indicators of subsequent sleep regulation. By integrating neonatal EEG markers with objective assessments of toddler sleep, this study provides new insights into early neurobiological pathways through which preterm birth may shape sleep development, and may ultimately support personalized early-intervention strategies for populations at heightened risk for NDDs and SDs.
