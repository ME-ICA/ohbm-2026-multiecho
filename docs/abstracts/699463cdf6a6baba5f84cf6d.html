Introduction

Investigating the structure of the human brain at the columnar and laminar level is possible with 7 Tesla MRI.1 However, the required sub-millimeter resolution typically requires lengthy scan times, resulting in physiological artifacts, especially those caused by subject motion. To address this issue, image acquisition is typically accelerated by Parallel Imaging techniques like GRAPPA and SENSE. Here, scan time is decreased by undersampling k-space and reconstructing the missing data using information from multiple radio-frequency (RF) receive channels. More advanced reconstruction methods like LORAKS2 can support even higher acceleration by mapping the k-space data into higher-dimensional structured matrices, exploiting linear dependencies derived from limited spatial support and slowly varying phase. However, with the advent of deep learning methods such as variational networks (VarNet), it may be possible to achieve higher acceleration factors by sparser k-space sampling combined with a reliable deep learning reconstruction model. To explore this possibility, we compared the reconstruction performance between LORAKS and a series of supervised deep learning neural networks on ultra-high resolution MRI.

Methods

Supervised deep learning methods for reconstruction of MRI data need fully-sampled data as ground-truth. Using a typical anatomical MRI sequence where every k-space line is acquired after a separate excitation, acquiring images at a sub-millimeter resolution can take several hours. Since such long scan times are not feasible for in vivo MRI, we used post-mortem whole brain MRI of chimpanzee brains that were ethically acquired after natural or unavoidable death, as part of the Evolution of Brain Connectivity  (EBC) project: 15 post-mortem chimpanzee brains were scanned at 7 T using paired PD- and T1-weighted 3D multi-echo FLASH sequences (RF spoiled gradient echo).3 The fully sampled images were acquired with 0.3 mm isotropic resolution and no parallel imaging. Since the full 3D k-space volumes were too large for direct training on standard GPUs, the data were converted into a stack of 2D k-space planes by applying an inverse Fourier transform along the readout direction (ventro-dorsal direction). Thus, the effective image reconstruction problem was reduced to 2D. Data were retrospectively undersampled 8-fold using a 2D variable-density Poisson-disc mask.4 This algorithm enforces a minimum distance between sampling points to prevent clustering, ensuring that aliasing artifacts manifest as incoherent noise rather than coherent ghosts. A fully sampled circular autocalibration region, comprising 4% of the k-space area was retained at the center, while the sampling density decayed towards the periphery according to an inverse-distance law. This variable-density distribution facilitates effective reconstruction by compressive sensing or deep learning methods. A visualization of a representative k-space mask pattern used in this study is shown in Figure 1.

We benchmarked reconstruction performance using a diverse set of eight deep learning architectures implemented within the ATOMMIC framework,5 ensuring unified training configurations and hyperparameters consistency. These networks can be divided into three categories:

  1. Image-Domain Network: UNet, which is computationally efficient and operates purely in the image space. It acts as a post-processing filter and lacks explicit k-space data consistency (DC) constraints, limiting its ability to correct physics-based artifacts. 
  1. Cross-Domain Network: KiKiNet, which iteratively processes data across both k-space and image domains to capture complementary features.
  1. Physics-Informed Unrolled Networks: This category represents the current state-of-the-art, comprising VarNet (End-to-End Variational Network), MoDL (Model-based deep learning Reconstruction), JointICNet (Joint Deep Model-Based MR Image and Coil Sensitivity Reconstruction Network), RVN (Recurrent Variational Network), RIM (Recurrent Inference Machines), and LPDNet (Learned Primal-Dual Net). Unlike standard Convolutional Neural Networks, these architectures unroll iterative optimization algorithms and integrate MR acquisition physics directly into the network. These physics-based models typically outperform other methods by enforcing data consistency explicitly or implicitly.5 

The dataset was split 7:1:2 for training, validation, and testing. Reconstruction quality was quantitatively assessed using Normalized Mean Squared Error (NMSE), Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), and Structural Similarity Index (SSIM) metrics. All images were normalized by peak intensities prior to metric calculation and benchmarked against fully sampled ground truth and zero-filled baselines.

All the neural networks were trained under a supervised learning pipeline. A schematic workflow can be found in Figure 1. The networks were trained for 30 epochs using the AdamW optimizer with a cosine annealing scheduler. The best hyperparameters for LORAKS were found by grid search. We used a combination of L1 loss and SSIM loss (1:1 ratio) as loss function to ensure both voxel-wise accuracy and structural fidelity.

Results

Table 1 summarizes the quantitative reconstruction quality metrics. Deep learning-based methods, particularly VarNet, UNet, and RIM, achieved superior scores across all metrics. Notably, while U-Net yielded the highest PSNR and NMSE, visual inspection (Figure 2) revealed that it achieved these scores by aggressively suppressing noise, which resulted in significant over-smoothing and the loss of microstructural details. In contrast, VarNet kept a superior balance: it not only achieved the highest SSIM (0.869) but also preserved anatomical textures more faithfully than other deep learning models. 

Conversely, the LORAKS reconstruction yielded significantly lower quantitative values (SSIM: 0.542), falling below even the zero-filled baseline, which suffered from severe aliasing effects. This discrepancy is largely attributable to the sensitivity of pixel-wise metrics to the residual noise inherent in LORAKS. Under the peak-intensity normalization scheme, high-intensity noise outliers can disproportionately compress the dynamic range, weakening the scores. However, visually, LORAKS retained the most intricate anatomical definition in low contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) regions, highlighting the trade-off between perceptual quality and signal fidelity.

Conclusion

In conclusion, VarNet reconstruction demonstrated the most robust overall performance for 8-fold undersampling, achieving the best trade-off between quantitative accuracy and visual fidelity. While conventional methods like LORAKS excel at preserving fine textures in low CNR regions, their performance at high acceleration factors is constrained by the inherent limitations of linear reconstruction models and low-rank assumptions, which struggle to resolve aliasing when k-space data is extremely sparse.

In contrast, VarNet overcomes these limitations by leveraging learned data non-linear priors to resolve aliasing, making it the strategic choice for future ultra-high resolution MRI reconstruction. Another advantage is the higher speed at inference stage of VarNet than the LORAKS reconstruction. Our validation on high-resolution ex vivo chimpanzee data (ethically sourced via the EBC project) serves as critical proof of the feasibility. Our implementation establishes the foundation for translating the high acceleration and quality to human neuroimaging, potentially enabling rapid, ultra-high-resolution scans in clinical settings overcoming prohibitive acquisition times. Future work will address this translation and also finesse the quantitative assessment of the image quality to better reflect human visual assessment and emphasize the preservation of textures and anatomical details.
