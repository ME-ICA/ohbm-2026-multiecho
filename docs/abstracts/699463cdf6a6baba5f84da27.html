Introduction

Obesity is a chronic and multifactorial condition whose prevalence has increased alarmingly worldwide, representing one of the most pressing public health challenges of the 21st century, including among children and adolescents.[1,2] Beyond its well-established metabolic consequences, accumulating evidence suggests that obesity is associated with structural and functional brain alterations, particularly affecting neural networks involved in appetite regulation, decision-making, impulsivity, reward processing, and emotional control. [3,4,5] Given the global rise in pediatric and adolescent obesity, understanding its neurobiological correlates has become increasingly relevant in the international scientific and clinical context. [6,7] However, despite adolescence being a critical period of neurodevelopment characterized by profound brain maturation and reorganization, studies specifically addressing the impact of obesity on brain connectivity during this stage remain limited. This knowledge gap is particularly concerning, as alterations in functional brain circuits during adolescence may have long-lasting consequences for behavior and health across the lifespan.

A better characterization of functional connectivity patterns associated with obesity may contribute to the identification of neurofunctional targets for alternative or complementary therapeutic strategies, potentially supporting interventions aimed at improving self-regulation, eating behavior, and reward processing. [5] Such approaches may help prevent the progression to more severe metabolic, cardiovascular, and neuropsychiatric conditions later in life. Therefore, the present study aimed to examine differences in resting-state functional brain connectivity between adolescents with obesity and their normal-weight peers using resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI).

Methods

A total of 114 adolescents aged 11 to 17 years were analyzed, of whom 56 had obesity, and 58 were eutrophic. All participants underwent clinical assessments, including measurements of weight, height, body mass index (BMI), and BMI z-score (zBMI), and completed behavioral questionnaires related to binge eating (Binge Eating Scale – BES), food addiction (Yale Food Addiction Scale for Children – YFAS-C), and depressive symptoms (Children's Depression Inventory – CDI). Before the MRI examination, participants’ hunger state was assessed by self-report. 

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data were acquired on a Philips 3T scanner. High-resolution T1-weighted anatomical images were acquired for anatomical reference and co-registration purposes. Resting-state functional images were acquired using an echo-planar imaging sequence, during which participants were instructed to remain still, keep their eyes open, and fixate on a cross. Functional connectivity (FC) analyses were conducted using bivariate approaches, including seed-to-voxel and ROI-to-ROI methods, combined with permutation testing in the CONN software, to identify altered connectivity patterns in the obesity group compared with the control group. Image preprocessing was performed following the standardized guidelines of the CONN software. No participants were excluded based on the conservative thresholds adopted during the analysis. Subsequently, correlation analyses were performed between the connectivity strengths identified in the ROI-to-ROI analysis and the participants’ clinical data.

Results

Seed-to-voxel results showed that adolescents with obesity exhibited a predominantly increased pattern of functional connectivity in frontal, parietal, occipital, cerebellar, and limbic cortex regions, accompanied by a localized reduction in connectivity in the posterior portion of the cerebellum. These findings suggest broad and distributed alterations in neural networks associated with executive function, memory, attention, and visuospatial integration, as well as changes in motor and cerebellar circuits. In the ROI-to-ROI analysis, adolescents with obesity, when compared with the control group, showed reduced FC in regions associated with executive function, such as the frontoparietal network (Figure 1A). Additionally, cerebellar regions predominantly exhibited decreased connectivity, although some increases were also observed (Figure 1 BC). These findings may be related to cognition and sensorimotor integration.

In the within-group association analyses, it was observed that, among adolescents with obesity, higher scores of binge-eating symptoms (BES) and higher zBMI were associated with reduced functional connectivity between frontoparietal and cerebellar regions. Interestingly, in adolescents from the control group, an opposite pattern emerged, whereby higher zBMI and older age were associated with increased connectivity in the cerebellar areas and, to a lesser extent, with a negative association between zBMI and frontoparietal connectivity.

Regarding sex-specific association patterns, girls with obesity exhibited a predominantly negative association between zBMI and eating-related behavior scores (BES and YFAS-C) and connectivity between cerebellar regions, with only one positive association identified between zBMI and a specific cerebellar connection. Among boys with obesity, higher BES and YFAS-C scores, as well as higher zBMI, were associated with reduced connectivity between cerebellar regions. In the control group, girls exhibited both positive and negative associations, with positive associations observed between age and zBMI, as well as between zBMI and cerebellar connections. Negative associations were found involving zBMI and frontoparietal and cerebellar connectivity. Boys, in turn, exhibited an association pattern restricted to food-addiction-related scores (YFAS-C), in which higher scores were associated with decreased cerebellar connectivity and increased frontoparietal connectivity.

Conclusion

The results of this study indicate that obesity in adolescence is associated with broad and distributed alterations in functional brain connectivity in a safe state, primarily involving frontoparietal, cerebellar, limbic, occipital, and parietal networks. This pattern suggests a functional disorganization of intensive circuits critical for executive control, self-regulation, attention, memory, visuospatial integration, and sensorimotor processing, essential functions during a period marked by brain maturation.

Although seed-to-voxel analysis revealed an overall increase in functional connectivity across multiple cortical and subcortical regions, ROI-to-ROI analysis showed specific reductions in key cognitive control networks, particularly in the frontoparietal network and cerebellar circuits. This apparent contrast points to a less efficient functional organization pattern, possibly reflecting compensatory mechanisms in response to more focal dysfunctions in executive networks responsible for behavior regulation.

Intra-group association analyses reinforce this functional interpretation. In adolescents with obesity, higher zBMI and greater severity of binge eating symptoms were consistently associated with reduced connectivity between frontoparietal and cerebellar regions, indicating that the worsening of the metabolic and behavioral phenotype is related to greater impairment of neural circuits involved in self-regulation, decision-making, and control of eating behavior. In contrast, in the control group, the positive associations between zBMI, age, and cerebellar connectivity appear to reflect normative trajectories of neurofunctional maturation throughout adolescence.

Stratification by sex further revealed different patterns of association, suggesting that the impact of obesity on brain functional connectivity is modulated by biological sex. In girls with obesity, a negative association predominated between adiposity indicators, dysfunctional eating behaviors, and cerebellar connectivity, while in boys with obesity, a similar pattern was observed, but more strongly related to measures of binge eating. These findings suggest differentiated neurofunctional trajectories, possibly influenced by hormonal, maturational, and behavioral factors specific to this developmental period.

Taken together, the results indicate that obesity in adolescence is associated with specific and clinically relevant functional alterations in the frontoparietal and cerebellar circuits, with a direct impact on executive control networks and the regulation of eating behavior. These alterations tend to intensify with increasing zBMI and eating symptoms, and also show sex-dependent differences. A better understanding of these circuits may contribute to the development of alternative and personalized therapeutic strategies, including neurobehavioral and neuromodulatory interventions, with the potential to mitigate more adverse metabolic, cognitive, and psychiatric stages throughout life.
