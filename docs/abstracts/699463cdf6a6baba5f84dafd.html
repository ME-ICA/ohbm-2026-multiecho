Introduction

Subjective cognitive decline (SCD), defined as perception of memory or other cognitive decline with normal test performance, is conceptualized as a possible preclinical stage of Alzheimer’s disease (AD)1. Hippocampal volume reduction is well-established as a biomarker of AD risk2, but evidence of hippocampal atrophy is unclear in SCD. The hippocampal complex includes functionally and structurally distinct subfields (cornu ammonis (CA), dentate gyrus, and subiculum) which show selective atrophy in aging and in AD3, 4, as well as psychiatric conditions such as major depressive disorder5-7. In the current study, we compared hippocampal subfield volumes acquired using structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) at 3 Tesla between older adults with SCD, who were free of current or past depression, and cognitively unimpaired (CU) older adults. We further examined associations between hippocampal subfield volumes and both self-perception of memory ability and objective memory performance.

Methods

Participants were 51 older adults [26 SCD (13M, age 70.6 SD=5.1 years) and 25 CU (10M, age 71.4 SD=7.4 years].  SCD was established based on affirmative responses to “Do you feel your memory is becoming worse?” “If so, are you concerned?”8 and normal performance on a neuropsychological battery. CU participants performed within normal on the neuropsychological battery and did not report memory concerns. Self-perception of memory ability was quantified using the Memory Functioning Questionnaire (MFQ).9, 10  Objective memory performance was quantified using the California Verbal Learning Test (CVLT) Delayed Recall. MR scanning was performed on a 3T Siemens Trio scanner using a 12-channel head coil. A high-resolution (voxel size 0.43 x 0.43 x 3 mm) T2-weighted scan of the medial temporal lobe was acquired in an oblique-coronal plane, perpendicular to the hippocampal long axis. The Automatic Segmentation of Hippocampal Subfields (ASHS) processing pipeline was used to extract bilateral CA1, CA2, CA3, dentate gyrus, and subiculum volumes.11  Independent t-tests were used to compare hippocampal subfields between groups, with Benjamini Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons. Multivariable regression analyses of subjective memory ability and of objective memory performance, with hippocampal subfield volumes as predictors and covariates of age, sex, and total intracranial volume were conducted.
 

Results

Relative to CU, parahippocampal volume was smaller bilaterally in SCD (left: p=0.019; right: p=0.026). A trend towards smaller left subicular volume was observed in SCD (p=0.055). Multivariable regression analyses controlling for age, sex, and intracranial volume showed associations between left subiculum and MFQ-Seriousness-of-Forgetting and left CA1 and MFQ-Frequency-of-Forgetting. Hippocampal subfield volumes were not predictive of long delayed verbal recall.

Conclusion

The association between volume of subiculum, a region implicated in AD, and subjective, but not objective memory, suggests that self-awareness of change in memory in everyday life may be sensitive to subtle disease-related volumetric changes within the hippocampus in the earliest stages of the AD course. These preliminary findings implicate the subiculum and parahippocampal gyrus in the neurobiology of SCD. Importantly, these data are not confounded by the presence of past or current depression. These findings of reduced MTL volumes in SCD require replication using larger samples of SCD with biomarker evidence of AD neuropathology.
