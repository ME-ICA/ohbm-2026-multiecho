Introduction

The human brain is a metabolically expensive organ that dynamically adapts its functional organization to available energy sources. There is evidence that suggests that bioenergetic challenges, such as exercise and dietary energy restriction, show promise for neuroprotection and cognitive optimization1,2. Consequently, metabolic interventions like the ketogenic diet and fasting are increasingly recognized for their potential to enhance cognitive health and promote resilient brain aging. However, the specific neurophysiological mechanisms by which the switch from glucose reliance to alternative metabolic substrates, such as ketones, alters human brain function remain incompletely characterized. Understanding this interplay between metabolic state and brain function is paramount for developing therapeutic strategies and establishing biomarkers for metabolic interventions. 

To address this, we conducted a comparative analysis of resting-state brain dynamics under three conditions: standard diet, fasting, and ketogenic diet. We investigated how these distinct metabolic states impact macro-scale brain states and local information integration.

Methods

We utilized an openly available metabolic neuroimaging dataset3 comprising 12 individuals (mean age = 28 +/- 7 years; 4 female, 8 male).  Each participant was scanned under three conditions: 1) standard diet, 2) fasting (standard diet followed by an 12-hour overnight fast), and 3) ketogenic diet (high-fat, moderate-protein, low-carbohydrate diet for 1 week, by which point all participants were in ketosis). Ten minutes of resting-state data (eyes open, cross fixation) were acquired using an ultrafast 7-Tesla fMRI sequence (SMS factor=5, TR=802 ms, TE=20 ms, flip angle=33°, voxel size=2x2x1.5 mm, 85 slices). Standard fMRI preprocessing was performed via fMRIPrep, followed by nuisance regression that included the average WM/CSF signals, head motion parameters, and their squares and temporal derivatives. Time series were band-pass filtered (0.01-0.1Hz) and spatially smoothed with a Gaussian kernel (FWHM = 4 mm). Co-activation pattern (CAP) analysis was employed to establish transient brain states across the diet conditions via temporal clustering4. For each CAP, we quantified occurrence rate, dwell time, and transition probabilities to other CAPs. Additionally, region-wise (Schaefer1000) intrinsic timescales were quantified via the magnitude of the autocorrelation of local neural signals5. Differences across conditions were tested using repeated measures ANOVA.

Results

Analysis revealed 4 pairs of anti-correlated recurrent co-activation patterns (CAPs), representing transient macro-scale brain states (Figure 1A): CAPs 1/2 were dominated by sensorimotor and dorsal attention networks; CAPs 3/4 involved higher-order default mode and frontal networks; CAPs 5/6 reflect global activations/deactivations; and CAPs 7/8 isolated dorsal and ventral attention networks. Fasting increased occurrence rates of global-signal CAPs 5 (p=0.0135) and 6 (p=0.0060), while decreasing occurrence rates of attention-related CAPs 7 (p=0.0162) and 8 (p=0.0447). Fasting also reduced dwell times for CAPs 4 (p=0.0327), 7 (p=0.0119), and 8 (p=0.0298). Furthermore, metabolic state modulated CAP transition rates: Fasting increased transition to the global CAPs 5 and 6 (CAP1->CAP5: p=0.0244; CAP4->CAP5: p=0.0029; CAP3->CAP6: p=0.0135; CAP8->CAP6: p=0.0106), while reducing transitions into attentional states (CAP8->CAP7: p=0.0275).

Timescale analysis revealed a trend (p=0.0612) towards slower global signal fluctuations during fasting, contrasted by shorter timescales during ketogenic diet (Figure 1B). Significant network-specific differences were observed in the visual (p=0.0473) and the ventral attention (p=0.0237) networks, where fasting increased timescales (slower information integration) while ketogenic diet shortened them (faster information integration).

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that metabolic states exert distinct and opposing effects on resting-state brain dynamics. Fasting significantly alters macro-scale organization, introducing more global signal peaks, which are often associated with transient drops in arousal or vigilance, and reducing the prevalence of attention-specific network activity. Locally, fasting slows sensory information integration. In contrast, the ketogenic diet preserves macro-scale network activity relative to the standard diet but significantly enhances local dynamics by increasing the speed of information integration. Given that older brains typically exhibit slower processing speeds, this finding suggests that the ketogenic diet drives brain dynamics toward a faster, and potentially younger functional state, whereas fasting induces a transient slowing and reduced vigilance. Taken together, these findings highlight the potential of metabolic interventions, particularly ketogenic diet, as strategies to counteract age-related functional slowing and promote neuroprotective brain dynamics.
