Introduction

The human face is arguably the most frequent visual stimulus encountered from birth, with eye gaze direction providing crucial social cues, and predicting other people’s attention and intentions. Direct gaze (DG) captures attention more effectively than averted gaze (AG) [1] and, essentially, enhances face encoding in memory and subsequent face recognition [2, 3] . Although behavioral findings suggest eye contact improves face recognition, the neural mechanisms underlying this effect remain unexplored. One possible explanation is that faces with AG are processed in a feature-based fashion, in which the face is decomposed into individual components, whereas faces with DG are processed in a more holistic way and encoded as unified representations [4].  This differential DG processing fosters the formation of distinct, holistic memory representations within medial temporal lobe (MTL)—a region critical for memory processing —which consequently leads to a more accurate retrieval. 

Methods

To test this, we employed a magnetoencephalography (MEG) - adapted continuous face recognition task, presenting black and white, neutral emotion faces with DG or left- or right- AG. Healthy adults (18-35 years) were asked to judge whether the test face matched the previously encoded Probe face (i.e. during the Encoding epoch). The test face during recognition could be either identical (Match), the same identity with different gaze direction (Gaze Mismatch), or a different identity with a same gaze direction (ID Mismatch). Rapid Invisible Frequency Tagging (RIFT) was applied to the eye region during Probe presentation to entrain rhythmic neural activity at the same frequency as visual stimulation [5]. Specifically, the eye region of the probe face was flickered at 68.5 Hz, generating steady-state evoked fields that allowed us to track neural activity associated with the DG- versus AG- tagged signals.  

Results

Behavioural findings reveal a novel, context-depending role of gaze direction in facial memory. Specifically, presenting Probe face with DG improves subsequent face recognition only in ID mismatch condition, suggesting that DG enhances the salience of encoded face, facilitating the differentiation of faces during recognition. Conversely, when the identity remains the same, but the gaze is mismatched, DG, as compared to AG, disrupts subsequent face recognition. Finally, when both identities and gaze are matched, the DG has no further effect, suggesting that optimal retrieval occurs under fully congruent conditions. 

MEG analyses so far revealed a trend in gaze-related differences during the encoding and recognition phases. Increased MEG spectral power at the tagging frequency was observed during encoding, but not during recognition, indicating that RIFT successfully elicited neural responses in encoding phase. Brain-to-tagging coherence was strongest in occipital regions and became more spatially distributed across higher visual areas over time for faces presented with DG as compared to faces with AG. Preliminary analyses on neural source reconstruction during the recognition phase revealed differences in spatio-temporal dynamics and activations within gaze-processing regions and the memory network across Match, Gaze Mismatch, and ID Mismatch conditions.

Conclusion

This ongoing MEG study provides proof-of-principle that Rapid Invisible Frequency Tagging (RIFT) can be successfully applied during very brief visual presentations to track the neural propagation of gaze-direction processing. By selectively tagging the eye region, the observed neural responses can be attributed specifically to direct versus averted gaze processing, dissociating processing of other facial features. Furthermore, condition-specific neural activations during recognition suggest dynamic interactions between gaze-processing and memory-related networks. Together, these findings advance our understanding of how social attention cues shape face memory and demonstrate the utility of RIFT for disentangling fine-grained perceptual and mnemonic processes in the human brain.
