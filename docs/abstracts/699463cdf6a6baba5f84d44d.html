Introduction

The third trimester of pregnancy is a rapid period of brain development where foundational functional networks emerge.1 Early coordination between cortical and subcortical regions support cognitive and behavioural development and disruptions during this period can have downstream consequences. This period is also highly sensitive to prenatal conditions and maternal health factors such as gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) can alter the intrauterine environment in ways that influence early brain development.  

GDM is a common pregnancy complication, with a global prevalence of 14.2%.2 GDM is linked to increased maternal and neonatal risks, including adverse perinatal outcomes.2 An increase in oxidative stress and inflammation in the placenta has been associated with GDM and can lead to conditions such as fetal hypoxia, resulting in an imbalance of oxygen demand and supply.3 Such disruptions may affect the development of grey and white matter systems that support efficient neural communication.  

Even before birth, the brain exhibits an organized connectome of networks that support later cognitive and behavioural functions.4 Examining functional connectivity can help characterize how interactions among brain regions form networks that enable efficient information processing.5 Both primary and early higher-order networks are already organized in utero, emphasizing that brain connectivity is a biomarker for healthy neurodevelopment.1 Given that GDM is associated with altered placental function and fetal oxygenation, GDM may disrupt the early formation of these neural networks. 

Given the documented effects of GDM on placental function, fetal oxygenation and early neural maturation, it remains unclear whether these physiological disruptions alter the formation of functional brain networks in utero. We hypothesized that fetuses exposed to GDM would exhibit reduced cortical-subcortical connectivity, indicating early disruptions to the development of functional neural networks. 

Methods

Data were drawn from the Developing Human Connectome Project (dHCP) fetal release4, which includes 255 fetuses (137 male, 116 female, 2 unrecorded) scanned between 21–38 weeks gestational age. From this cohort, we selected 20 GDM-exposed fetuses and 40 age- and sex-matched controls (15 females, 5 males in GDM group; 30 females, 10 males in control group). All imaging was completed at St. Thomas’ Hospital, Ontario, Canada using a Philips Achieva 3T scanner with a 32-channel cardiac coil. Resting-state fMRI was acquired using a single-shot EPI sequence, and T2-weighted anatomical scans were obtained for nearly all sessions. Scans with poor quality or incomplete brain coverage were excluded. FMRI data were preprocessed using a tailored fetal pipeline that included multi-band soft SENSE reconstruction, dynamic distortion corrections, slice-to-volume reconstruction, temporal filtering, and registration to a 36-week gestational age template. 

Functional connectivity analysis was conducted using CONN (RRID:SCR_009550) and SPM (RRID:SCR_007037). First-level whole-brain analysis generated an ROI-to-ROI connectivity matrix across 49 regions, comprising cortical and subcortical regions, using Fisher-transformed correlation coefficients. Group-level analyses were performed using a general linear model to compare connectivity between GDM and control groups. Age and fetal sex were entered as covariates; however, biological sex showed no effects and was excluded from the final model. The final analysis tested the effects of group (GDM vs. control) while accounting for age. Cluster-level inferences were based on functional network connectivity and hierarchical clustering of ROI-to-ROI similarity metrics. Results were at a threshold of p < .05 (connection level) with FDR-corrected p < .05 (cluster level).  

Results

The group-level ROI-to-ROI analysis displayed significant differences in functional connectivity between the GDM and control groups (Figure 1). Fetuses in the GDM group showed a prominent pattern of thalamo-cortical hypoconnectivity, in which the thalamus showed decreased connectivity with temporal, parietal, and occipital regions. Additionally, fetuses in the GDM also displayed increased subcortical-subcortical functional connectivity compared to healthy controls. Specifically, the brainstem showed increased connectivity with the amygdala, hippocampus, and medial temporal lobe structures, while the thalamus showed greater connectivity with the basal ganglia. Overall, medial and lateral temporal lobe regions showed the greatest pattern of alterations in GDM exposed fetuses relative to healthy controls.

Conclusion

In the present study, GDM was associated with reduced functional connectivity between the thalamus and cortical regions during fetal development. The broad pattern of hypoconnectivity, particularly across temporal, parietal, and occipital pathways, suggests early disruptions in the formations of large-scale functional networks. Reduced oxygenation resulting from GDM-related alterations in placenta structure and function may contribute to lower connectivity, limiting oxygen delivery essential for healthy neural maturation and cortical growth. Hyperconnectivity between the brainstem and subcortical regions may reflect compensatory reorganization such that regions involved in homeostasis and survival are being prioritized over cortical regions. Overall, the results indicate that GDM exposure may alter the foundational organization of the fetal connectome, potentially influencing later neurodevelopmental trajectories.
