Introduction

Hippocampal mismatch responses—transient increases in activity when current experience deviates from prior expectations—play a central role in learning and in updating one’s internal models of the world. However, the computational basis of these signals has remained unclear. In previous fMRI work, we showed that hippocampal mismatch responses are driven by violations of episodic memories—what did happen—rather than by deviations from what should happen based on abstract schematic knowledge about the world.^1 While this work established robust episodic mismatch signals, the blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) response provides only an indirect index of neuronal activity and cannot resolve the underlying neurochemical processes shaping hippocampal circuit dynamics.

Building on this, the present functional magnetic resonance spectroscopy (fMRS) study tested the neurochemical mechanisms supporting episodic mismatch responses in the human hippocampus. Influential theoretical models propose that hippocampal processing modes are regulated by cholinergic and glutamatergic neuromodulation, such that elevated acetylcholine biases the system away from internally driven retrieval and toward externally driven encoding in situations requiring the encoding of novel information.^2 Within this framework, cholinergic modulation enhances the influence of entorhinal afferent input while suppressing recurrent hippocampal feedback, thereby shaping glutamatergic signalling during the processing of unexpected information. However, direct in vivo evidence for these mechanisms in humans is lacking.

Here we hypothesised that during hippocampal mismatch detection elicited by unexpected actions in video clips of everyday situations (a form of novelty processing) will be accompanied by changes in in glutamate and choline-containing metabolite concentrations detectable with fMRS.

Methods

Design

Here, we used the same custom-made video clips previously shown to elicit hippocampal episodic mismatch responses.^1 The stimulus set comprised 34 pairs of short video clips (30 seconds long on average) depicting everyday action sequences. Each pair included an Atypical version, in which an actor performed a context-incongruent action (e.g., putting flowers in a washing machine), and a Typical version, in which the atypical action was replaced by a context congruent action (e.g., putting clothes in a washing machine). Each pair of clips showed identical sequences of actions, except for these critical Target Actions (TAs). See example here: e01.eventmemory.org/ExampleVid_paper.html

During scanning, participants viewed half of the clips (17) in their Atypical version and the other half (17) in their Typical version. To ensure that Atypical Target Actions elicited episodic memory-based mismatches, all participants (N=30) viewed all 34 clips in their Typical versions prior to scanning. As a result, any Atypical TA (e.g., flowers) encountered during scanning violated participants’ memory for the previously viewed events (e.g., clothes). Clips were presented in an event-related design. See design on Figure 1A.

Scanning Parameters

All data were acquired on a 3T Siemens Prisma MRI scanner using a semi-LASER spectroscopy sequence. fMRS data were collected from a single voxel positioned to cover the right hippocampus, selected based on prior fMRI findings showing maximal episodic mismatch responses in this region. Acquisition parameters were repetition time (TR) = 3000 ms and echo time (TE) = 30 ms.

Metabolite Selection

Proton MRS allows detection of metabolites present at millimolar concentrations, with each metabolite producing a characteristic spectral pattern defined by its chemical shift (see example spectra in Figure 1B). 

The primary metabolites of interest were glutamate (Glu) and total choline (tCho; glycerophosphocholine + phosphocholine + free choline). Glu was analysed as a marker of excitatory engagement. tCho was examined as an indirect index of cholinergic modulation. ACh itself is not detectable with fMRS due to its low concentration; however, choline-containing compounds detectable with fMRS are part of the ACh synthesis and recycling cycle, and prior work has demonstrated task-related modulation of free choline during learning and attention.^3 Notably, the three choline-containing compounds exhibit overlapping resonances in the proton MRS spectrum, limiting reliable separation of individual components. For this reason, analyses focused on total choline and any cholinergic effects were interpreted cautiously.

Analysis Set Up
Differences in metabolite concentrations between Typical and Atypical conditions were assessed to test for mismatch-related neurochemical modulation. Each video clip was divided into successive TRs based on their temporal position relative to the onset of the TA (two TRs before, one at onset, and two after the end of the Target Actions). For each TR position, spectra were averaged across the 17 Typical clips and, separately, across the 17 Atypical clips, yielding a condition-specific metabolite time course in which each time point reflected the average of 17 spectra per condition. Metabolite concentration estimation was done on these TR by TR condition averages using linear-combination modelling implemented in FSL-MRS.^4

Results

For each condition, the first TR in the timecourse, corresponding to the beginning of the clips, was used as a baseline. Metabolite concentrations at subsequent TRs were expressed as percentage signal change relative to TR1.

Two separate 2 (Condition: Typical, Atypical) × 4 (TR: TRs 2 to 5 on Figure 2A) repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted on glutamate and total choline percent signal change, with Participant as a within-subjects factor. 

For total choline, there was no main effect of Condition, F(1, 29) = 0.37, p = .550, and no main effect of TR, F(3, 87) = 1.23, p = .305. The Condition × TR interaction was also not significant, F(3, 87) = 0.21, p = .889, indicating no evidence that total choline levels differed between conditions or varied across TRs.

For gluatamte, there was a significant main effect of Condition, F(1, 29) = 7.28, p = .011, indicating overall differences between Typical and Atypical conditions. There was no main effect of TR, F(3, 87) = 1.49, p = .224, and no Condition × TR interaction, F(3, 87) = 1.72, p = .169, suggesting that the condition effect was consistent across TRs.

Given a priori interest in the TR corresponding to target action onset (TR3 on Figure 2A), post-hoc paired comparisons were conducted to compare Glu concentration differences at each TR, with p-values corrected for multiple comparisons using FDR. These analyses revealed a significant difference between conditions at TR3, t(29) = −3.28, pFDR = .011, with higher glutamate levels rigth after the onset of Atypical TAs relative to Typical TAs. No significant condition differences were observed at TR2, TR4, or TR5 (all pFDRs ≥ .19).

Metabolite levels are reported as ratios relative to total creatine (tCr = Cr + PCr), which served as an internal reference; values are therefore reported as relative concentrations rather than absolute metabolite concentrations. Total creatine did not differ between conditions, supporting its use as a stable internal reference. A stable internal reference is required in MRS to normalise metabolite signals for non-biological sources of variability in signal amplitude.

For illustrative comparison, Figure 2B shows the event-related BOLD signal timecourse from a separate fMRI experiment, time-locked to TA onset. The BOLD response exhibited a delayed increase following TA onset, with a larger amplitude response in the Atypical relative to the Typical condition. Consistent with the known temporal properties of the hemodynamic response, this increase emerged several seconds after the TA, subsequent to the glutamate differences observed at TR3 in the fMRS data.

Conclusion

The present study used event-related fMRS to investigate the neurochemical basis of hippocampal episodic mismatch detection in humans. Building on prior fMRI evidence that hippocampal mismatch responses are driven by violations of episodic memories, we tested whether these responses are accompanied by task-related changes in glutamate and choline-containing metabolites. We observed transient increases in hippocampal Glu concentrations following Atypical, unexpected TAs, providing the first in vivo human evidence linking episodic mismatch detection to excitatory neurochemical dynamics. In contrast, we did not observe reliable task-related modulation of total choline.

Glu concentrations showed condition-specific changes around the onset of unexpected target actions. This finding builds on prior fMRS evidence that hippocampal glutamate dynamics track associative learning processes,^5 and extend this work by showing that such neurochemical changes can be detected transiently during mismatch detection. Moreover, unlike the BOLD response—which reflects indirect neuronal activity and temporally smoothed hemodynamic changes—glutamate modulation provides a more temporally proximal index of excitatory engagement. Consistent with this interpretation, the observed Glu changes occurred qualitatively earlier (within 3 seconds of the TA onset) than the corresponding BOLD signal increases, suggesting that glutamatergic modulation precedes and potentially contributes to the downstream hemodynamic response associated with mismatch processing.


In contrast, we did not observe mismatch-related changes in total choline concentrations. This null result should be interpreted cautiously and does not rule out a role for cholinergic modulation in hippocampal mismatch detection. ACh itself is not directly measurable with proton MRS due to its low concentration, and total choline represents a composite signal that is possibly not the best proxy for ACh dynamics.^3


Several additional factors may account for the absence of a detectable choline effect. First, cholinergic modulation may occur on a faster timescale than can be captured with the current temporal resolution (TR = 3 s). Second, cholinergic effects may be spatially restricted to hippocampal subfields, which cannot be resolved with the relatively large voxel required for sufficient SNR. Finally, hippocampal mismatch detection may rely on neuromodulators other than ACh, such as noradrenaline or dopamine, which are undetectable with fMRS.


Overall, by moving beyond hemodynamic measures, our fMRS study offers a complementary perspective on the processes underlying hippocampal comparator function. Moreover, to our knowledge, this is the first study to apply fMRS to the investigation of hippocampal mismatch detection, and one of only a small number of studies to employ event-related fMRS more generally. As such, the present work contributes also methodologically, illustrating the potential of event-related fMRS for addressing questions about the neurochemical mechanisms of cognition that are inaccessible to traditional blocked designs.


In future work, the application of general linear modelling (GLM) approaches to fMRS time series will enable formal modelling of event-related neurochemical responses, allowing metabolite dynamics to be estimated relative to specific task events while accounting for overlapping responses and nuisance variance.
