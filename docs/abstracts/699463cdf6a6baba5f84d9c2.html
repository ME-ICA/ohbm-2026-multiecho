Introduction

Infancy represents a critical period for brain development, encompassing major changes in brain growth as well as in myelination and functional specialization. Compared to prospective neuroimaging studies, a complementary approach is the repurposing of “real-world” clinical brain MRIs from pediatric health systems for retrospective research. Clinical brain MRIs offer unprecedented sample sizes to study infant brain development. However, automated segmentation of infant brain MRIs remains challenging due to technical variability in clinical settings – such as differences in scanner and acquisition protocol – and analytic complexity due to rapid anatomical changes and dynamic neurobiological changes affecting tissue contrast. Recent efforts using synthetic data and domain-randomization training have shown promise for research-quality infant scans (e.g. BIBSNet1) or clinical scans starting in early childhood (e.g. SynthSeg2). But existing methods fail on most clinical MRIs of infants and perform especially poorly in the presence of atypical or abnormal morphology1-3. These limitations prevent us from fully leveraging rich clinical archives with potentially millions of brain MRIs to model early developmental trajectories and study neurodevelopmental risk in real-world populations. Here, we address these gaps by developing ClinSeg: a robust segmentation approach explicitly tailored to early-life, clinically acquired MRI while also demonstrating comparable performance in older individuals. In addition, we use ClinSeg to augment previously published brain MRI growth charts using clinically acquired data.

Methods

We trained a 3D UNet constructed using the nnUNet framework with a combination of real and synthetic datasets. For ground-truth labeling, we utilized the Baby Open Brains (BOBs) dataset, a subset of the Baby Connectome Project (BCP) containing 71 defaced T1-weighted (T1w) scans with manual segmentations (ages 1–8 months).4 We further incorporated two clinical cohorts with suspected/identified brain pathology from the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia (CHOP): (1) SLIP-Train: 10 T1w scans aged 0–1 years passing the automated machine learning-based SynthSeg quality control (QC) module and having extreme volumetric centile scores (<3% or >97% for one or more phenotypes) based on previously published brain growth charts2,3; and (2) CHOP-Path: 73 infants with neurological findings identified through SQL-based keyword search of radiology reports followed by severity grading ascertained using the clinical large language model MedGemma.

Segmented BOBs scans (defaced) were registered to their original non-defaced BCP acquisitions using SynthMorph to ensure whole-head coverage, and label maps were transferred from the BOBs to the BCP space using ANTs with label-preserving interpolation, producing BOBs-BCP pairs. Whole-head labels were derived using a retrained SynthSeg model where training data had extracerebral labels segmented using the CHARM toolbox. Each BOBs-BCP subject was then registered to the SLIP-Train and CHOP-Path scans, generating augmented synthetic image-label pairs. These registered segmentations were fed into the SynthSeg brain generator to generate additional synthetic training scans to increase anatomical and contrast variability. We simulated different stages of myelination by augmenting the white matter segmentation using k-means clustering (k=2-5) based on the T1w voxel intensities in the BOBs dataset. This approach generated discrete white matter voxel intensities in images in the training dataset, to mimic differential myelination of white matter tracts in early brain development. 

We tested ClinSeg on two research-quality datasets with accompanying manual segmentations, Developmental Infant Brain Study (DIBS) and Child and Adolescent NeuroDevelopment Initiative (CANDI) to compare performance against BIBSNet 3.4.2 and SynthSeg+. DIBS is composed of 47 isotropic T1w scans with segmentations for bilateral cerebral white matter and cortex (0-36 months). CANDI consists of 101 T1w scans for a pediatric cohort (4-17 years of age). Segmented regions for CANDI include left and right segmentations for the cerebral cortex, cerebral white matter, cerebellar cortex, cerebellar white matter, lateral ventricles, thalamus, ventral diencephalon (VentralDC), caudate, putamen, pallidum, accumbens, hippocampus and amygdala, brainstem, 3rd and 4th ventricles. For Dice score computation, homologous left and right regions were merged, as were ventricular sub-regions (lateral, 3rd, and 4th), the putamen and pallidum, and the hippocampus and amygdala (as seen in Figure 1A,B). 

Using both ClinSeg and SynthSeg+, we generated segmentations for the CHOP SLIP cohort (47,627 scans, 7934 subjects, 8440 sessions, aged 0-44). ClinSegs segmentations passing the automated SynthSeg QC were then input into the recon-all-clinical pipeline to create cortical parcellations. We modeled global and regional imaging phenotypes using Generalized Additive Models for Location Scale and Shape (GAMLSS) with a generalized gamma distribution. Distributional parameters were mean (µ), scale (σ), and skewness (ν)5. Fixed effects included sex and log-transformed post-conception age (gestational age + postnatal age; default 280 days if unavailable). Nonlinear age effects were modeled with second- or third-order fractional polynomials. Scanner effects were harmonized using scanner-specific random effects for µ and σ. Final model selection used BIC, consistent with our prior work3,5.

Results

Evaluation of Clinseg performance showed markedly improved segmentation of infant clinical scans, with exemplar segmentations seen in Figure 1A,B. Compared with BIBSNet and SynthSeg+, ClinSeg yielded improved or comparable segmentation of cerebral cortex and white matter in the DIBS and CANDI datasets. For CANDI, we observed similarly elevated performance for ClinSeg (mean Dice = 0.86) and SynthSeg+ (mean Dice = 0.85) over BIBSNet (mean Dice = 0.8) for cerebral white matter (Fig 1C). More comparable performance was observed across pipelines for the cerebral cortex (mean Dice for ClinSeg, 0.88; mean Dice for BIBSNet,  0.87; mean Dice for SynthSeg+,  0.85). Dice performance for the subcortical and cerebellar structures were more variable, with lower performance for ClinSeg observed in the hippocampus and amygdala. In DIBS, ClinSeg outperformed both BIBSNet and SynthSeg+ across the two regions for which manual segmentations were available (mean Dice of 0.89 for both cerebral white matter and cortex in ClinSeg versus 0.83 and 0.84 respectively for SynthSeg+ and BIBSNet). 

In the SLIP cohort, based on the previously established minimum QC threshold of 0.65 for each of nine composite regions, we found that ClinSeg outperformed SynthSeg+ across the younger age groups (Figure 2A). 7268 individual subjects (7518 sessions) had passed QC for SynthSeg+ while there was an increase to 7629 (7862 sessions) passing QC using ClinSeg segmentations. The greatest improvement was observed for neonates, with scans from 107 subjects (110 sessions) passing QC using ClinSeg compared with 0 using SynthSeg+. 

Construction of growth charts using GAMLSS modeling largely recapitulated prior results for both global and regional volume measures1,5 (Fig 2B,C). For the cortical regions, we observed the canonical maturational gradient with regional volume in the sensorimotor cortex peaking earlier than in the association cortex (Fig 2C). Specifically, the right and left cuneus in the occipital lobe were the first to peak at 3.95 and 4.43 years of age, respectively. Meanwhile, late peaking regions included the right and left rostral anterior cingulate, right caudal middle frontal, and right precentral regions which peaked between 10-11 years of age. Altogether, we found a strong concordance (Pearson’s r = 0.79) between hemisphere-averaged peak ages for the updated SLIP dataset processed using ClinSeg and recon-all-clinical compared with the peak ages reported previously for the Lifespan Brain Chart Consortium (Fig 2D).

Conclusion

We introduce ClinSeg: a segmentation model trained exclusively on real and synthetic infant data that generalizes robustly across clinically acquired MRI scans and enables successful segmentation in clinical infant scans. Our model shows strong agreement with manual segmentations for cerebral white and gray matter across a wide age range and either outperforms or shows comparable performance with published segmentation models geared towards either infant or adult data. Specifically, performance is more comparable with SynthSeg+ for CANDI, an adult dataset, and performance is substantially improved over SynthSeg+ for DIBS. Furthermore, growth charts constructed using our segmentations broadly recapitulate known global and regional volume trajectories, validating the inclusion of clinically acquired infant scans in future normative modeling analyses. Future work will leverage the generalizability of our segmentation model across a wider age range to construct more robust growth charts using clinical data, with the goal of improving early identification of atypical neurodevelopment.
