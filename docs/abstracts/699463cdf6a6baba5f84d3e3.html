Introduction

The NIMH’s RDoC framework hypothesizes that disordered brain circuits give rise to clinical symptomatology1. Reflecting the highly heterogenous symptom profiles within psychiatric populations, the distributions of disordered circuits across the brain are also highly variable between individuals2. Therefore, whether a patient displays cognitive deficits concurrent with diagnostically-related clinical symptomatology should depend on whether their disordered circuits impinge upon functional brain networks that support cognitive functioning. Given that patients who experience cognitive impairment may respond differently to various forms of treatment than unimpaired patients3-5, identifying the loci of these interactions is an important research focus that could lead to novel diagnostic biomarkers and precision treatment targets. Here, we sought to identify circuits that co-fluctuated with diagnostically-related clinical symptomatology and supported cognition within a connectome-based predictive modeling (CPM) framework6,7. Understanding how strongly cognition can be predicted from connectivity in these overlapping functional connections can shed light on how disordered circuits in these regions can be used to assess the neurobiological basis of cognitive deficits in psychiatry. 

Methods

Here, we utilized a demographically and clinically diverse transdiagnostic population8. The dataset consists of fMRI data from 2 resting-state and 6 task runs, as well as questionnaires collected outside the magnet consisting of 7 real-world self-report diagnostic-related clinical assessment scales and 6 neuropsychological cognitive testing instruments. The diagnostically-related clinical assessment instruments were the Adult Temperament Questionnaire (ATQ), the Behavior Rating Inventory of Executive Function (BRIEF), the Brief Symptom Inventory (BSI), the Interpersonal Reactivity Index (IRI), the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS), the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), and the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS). The cognitive testing instruments were the Delis-Kapan Executive Function Scale (D-KEFS), the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scales (WAIS), the Wechsler Abbreviated Scale of Intelligence (WASI), the Wide Range Assessment of Memory and Learning (WRAML), the Wide Range Achievement Test (WRAT), and the Boston Naming Test (BNT). The fMRI data was preprocessed with standard preprocessing methods and the Shen 268-node atlas9 was applied to the preprocessed data, parcellating it into 268 functionally coherent nodes. Functional connectivity matrices were derived for each participant/run. The averages of all 8 functional connectivity matrices were computed for each participant and used as input in building the CPMs. 

In previous work, we identified 38 subjective diagnostically-related clinical symptomatology and 26 objective cognitive performance measures that yielded reliable brain-network correlates. In the present study, we performed a 10-fold cross-validated CPM to identify edges that overlapped between diagnostically-related clinical symptomatology and cognitive networks within training folds and predicted cognitive measures in the held-out subjects using connectivity in just the identified overlapping edges. As a control analysis, we constructed predictive models using edges within networks related to diagnostically-related clinical symptomatology that did not impinge upon cognitive networks. The number of edges selected in this control analysis was equal number of overlapping edges. Prediction strength was quantified as the Spearman correlation (ρ) between the observed measurements and the predicted values. Significance was determined by comparing the prediction strengths from 1000 CPM permutations to a null distribution generated from running CPM on randomly permuted observations.

Results

Correlations between clinical and cognitive measures were generally weak (Figure 1a; mean absolute ρ = 0.062, max absolute ρ = 0.234) and the overlap between their derived networks was sparse (mean dice similarity = 0.038). Despite this, connectivity in the overlapping edges reliably predicted cognition in 777 out of 988 instances (Figure 1b; p-FDR < 0.05; mean ρ = 0.197, max ρ = 0.402). The overlapping edges also predicted cognition more strongly than randomly selected edges within diagnostically-related clinical measures in all 777 instances that overlapping edges reliably predicted cognition. 

Conclusion

These results strongly suggest that when disordered circuits related to diagnostically-related clinical dysfunction also impinge upon networks supporting cognitive abilities, clinically-relevant cognitive dysfunction occurs. Our approach of predicting cognition from network overlap also highlights how multivariate CPM can be utilized to precisely identify where disordered circuits are distributed within an individual’s brain. These findings may inform precision psychiatry interventions that target systems linked to cognitive dysfunction.
