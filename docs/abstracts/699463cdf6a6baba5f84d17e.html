Introduction

Recent years have seen advances in the sensitivity of brain MRI to tissue microstructure owing to development in acquisition techniques and biophysical modelling1. However, these methods still lack the specificity to distinguish changes in particular cell types, ultimately limiting their clinical use for distinguishing different biological processes that underlie plasticity or early disease. Since MRI measures reflect voxel-averaged properties, advancing microstructurally sensitive MRI requires models that link MR-derived parameters to underlying cellular architecture, validated against ground-truth microstructure.

Spatial transcriptomics is a method that captures the location of gene expression on intact ex vivo tissue slices. This allows for the segmentation and classification of cells based on their gene expression profile, providing rich information about spatially resolved cellular composition. Examined here is the potential to leverage this data to enhance diffusion MRI (dMRI) models and predict density of different cell types. By varying the degree of diffusion-weighting, orientation, and diffusion time, dMRI becomes sensitive to different aspects of tissue microstructure. Using ex vivo mouse dMRI paired with spatial transcriptomic measurements from corresponding slices, we investigated whether cell density measurements could inform and constrain diffusion models. We then evaluated whether these models could be used to quantify cell-type densities across the whole brain from diffusion data alone.

Methods

We selected eight mouse strains from the BXD recombinant inbred panel known to have maximal differences in cell count. Two mice from each strain were perfusion fixed with 2mM gadolinium contrast agent and scanned ex vivo with structural T2-weighted (T2W) and diffusion MRI (dMRI). A 3D TurboRARE sequence was used for the T2W scanning (60µm3 isotropic resolution, matrix size=400x160x200, TE=36ms, echo spacing=12ms, 6 echoes, TR=350ms, BW=60kHz, acquisition time=33min). Multi-shell diffusion data was acquired using a segmented EPI (150µm isotropic; 16 directions per shell at b = [1, 2.5, 5.5, 8.5, 12.5, 17.5] ms/µm2, δ/Δ=7.5/20ms). Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI), diffusion kurtosis imaging (DKI) and soma and neurite density imaging (SANDI)2 models were used to extract a set of phenomenological measures and biophysical parameters (Figure 1A), including but not limited to fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusion (MD) and mean kurtosis (MK) from DKI, and neurite fraction (fneurite), soma fraction (fsoma), and soma radius (Rsoma) from SANDI. MRI data from all mice were registered together using our mouse-optimised iterative image registration tools (Pydpiper).

Once imaging was complete, the brains were cut and a single right hemisphere slice through the anterior hippocampus (-2mm AP, 5µm) was assayed using the 10X Xenium spatial transcriptomics platform with the mouse brain panel consisting of 247 genes and the cell segmentation kit (DAPI or nuclear staining, ATP1A1, E-Cadherin, and CD45 for membrane boundaries, 18S Ribosomal RNA labels for cytoplasm and alphaSMA/Vimentin for interior protein staining). Separate sections (50µm) through the striatum (+0.25mm AP) and prefrontal cortex (+1.2mm AP) were stained for DAPI and NeuN using immunohistochemistry (IHC).

Spatial transcriptomics slices were cell-segmented and per-cell transcripts were used to assign cell types through the Allen Institute’s MapMyCells automatic cell type mapping tool3 (Figure 1B). We then grouped the different cell types and calculated density maps for all cells, as well as neurons (glutamatergic and GABAergic), astrocytes, oligodendrocytes and immune cells. IHC slides were processed using QuPath4 and a watershed segmentation was used to calculate neuron densities. Cell density maps obtained from spatial transcriptomics and IHC were aligned to each mouse’s T2W MRI scan using the TIRL registration tool5, and then resampled into the study-specific consensus space via transform concatenation (Figure 1C, top).

Once all data was in the same space, a single random forest model (for each cell type) was trained on the spatial transcriptomics data to predict cell density from the set of dMRI maps (Figure 1C, bottom).

Results

From our ex vivo diffusion protocol we obtained high-quality diffusion data, yielding parameter maps with clear anatomical contrast across a range of microstructure-sensitive metrics (Figure 1A). Using the spatial transcriptomics data, in which every cell was segmented and classified, we trained a set of random forest regression models to predict the density of specific cell types from diffusion data. For all cell types except immune cells, cell count predictions were much better than chance on held-out voxels within the slice (Figure 2A). Furthermore, the model accurately identified which mice had higher cell counts within each region, confirming sensitivity to true inter-individual cellular variation (Figure 2B, example isocortex). These multi-parameter models outperformed individual diffusion metrics, even for measures such as FA (from DKI) and fsoma (from SANDI) which are known to correlate with oligodendrocyte and neuron density, respectively. 

We then applied the trained models to the 3D dMRI data to predict cell counts across the entire brain (Figure 2C), yielding spatially sensible maps even beyond the training slice. To validate prediction accuracy, we performed IHC and quantified neuron cell density on separate tissue slices. These were aligned to the MRI data, and the predicted neuron counts from our original model were compared to the neuron counts quantified from histology. For these new regions, predicted and actual average cell count correlated across mice, further confirming the accuracy of our model in predicting cell density across subjects and across the brain (Figure 2D).

Conclusion

This proof-of-concept study explores the potential to use spatial transcriptomics for increasing the cell-type sensitivity of MR based microstructural models. Our findings show that a transcriptomics-informed model accurately predicts region-specific neuron densities across the brain. Importantly, agreement between predicted and measured neuron densities across transcriptomic and IHC assays confirms that the model reflects genuine biological variation rather than modality-specific features. Although we have used simple modelling techniques, these results show that the same diffusion features can be reweighted to yield biologically plausible maps of distinct cell types, underscoring the potential of this approach to build increasingly sophisticated frameworks for inferring cell-type–specific properties from MRI.
