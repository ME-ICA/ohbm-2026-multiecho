Introduction

Introduction: Converging findings from structural and functional MRI suggest sex‑linked differences in the organization and dynamics of brain networks 1,2,3, implying that men and women may perceive, process and integrate information in partially distinct ways. Here, using time-resolved intersubject synchronization and individual variability analyses in a large movie-watching fMRI dataset, we demonstrate that male and female brains exhibit systematic differences in dynamic patterns of idiosynchrony during naturalistic stimulation. 

Methods

Methods: We analyzed 7T fMRI data from the Human Connectome Project acquired in two independent runs of naturalistic movie viewing. In each run, a balanced sample of healthy young adults (Run 1: 69 males, 69 females; Run 2: 67 males, 67 females) viewed three movie clips (3–4 minutes each, 20 seconds rest time between clips). Pre-processed time series were parcellated into 360 cortical regions using the HCP MMP 1.0 atlas and 14 subcortical regions from the Harvard–Oxford atlas, yielding 374 regions of interest. To quantify sex-specific individual variability, we computed dynamic within-group standard deviation (SD) of regional activity across time separately for males and females. At each time point, SD distributions across all regions were compared between the sexes using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) statistic, and temporal cluster-based permutation testing was applied to identify significant periods of sex differences. To characterize individual variability in each run, we computed ROI-by-ROI matrices of correlations in regional SD time series, and then evaluated the similarity of these matrices between the sex groups using permutation testing.  Finally, dynamic intersubject correlation (ISC) was computed using a leave-one-out sliding-window approach (window size = 40 TRs; step size = 1 TR), yielding individual- and region- specific time-resolved ISC estimates. Sex differences in ISC were assessed using generalized linear models.

Results

Results:  Both males and females exhibited substantial within-group dispersion of regional responses, quantified as the across-participant SD for each brain region at each time point, during movie viewing (Fig. 1a-b). Time-resolved KS testing of SD distributions across brain regions revealed multiple temporally contiguous clusters with significant male–female differences, indicating reliable periods of sex differences in population-level neural variability (Fig. 1a-b). However, over the entire run males and females exhibited highly similar patterns of coordinated fluctuations in individual variability across brain regions (Run 1: r = 0.823; Run 2: r = 0.828, permutation testing p < 0.001) (Fig. 1e-f). Dynamic ISC analysis revealed time-varying sex differences in neural synchrony during naturalistic movie viewing (Fig. 2a-b). There was stronger moment-to-moment synchronization among males than females in a distributed network encompassing high-level visual cortex, superior temporal sulcus, temporo-parietal junction, dorsal parietal regions, posterior cingulate cortex, and insular cortex(Fig. 2c). In contrast, there was stronger synchronization among females primarily in sensorimotor cortex, opercular and insular areas, limbic structures including the amygdala, and subcortical regions such as the thalamus (Fig. 2d). 

Conclusion

Conclusion：The findings reveal time-varying patterns of divergence in neural synchronization and individual variability between males and females during naturalistic perception. Males showed stronger synchrony with one another in visual–associative, default-mode, and cognitive control networks, whereas females showed stronger synchrony with one another in sensorimotor, salience, and affective systems. The findings suggest the existence of sex-specific modes of neural processing, that are dynamically dependent on the stimulation and context, in real-world conditions.
