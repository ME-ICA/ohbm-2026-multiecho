Introduction

Gray matter volume is a product of two genetically distinct features: cortical thickness and surface area. Despite high heritability (h² = 0.81 and 0.89, respectively), these measures show minimal genetic correlation (r = 0.08)1 and independent developmental trajectories2. While gray matter volume consistently relates to cognitive abilities cross-sectionally, longitudinal findings remain inconsistent regarding which morphometric feature drives age-related cognitive decline3,4,5. 

Methods

We analysed three independent ageing cohorts: Cam-CAN (N = 635 baseline, N = 133 longitudinal; age 18-95; ~12-year follow-up), OASIS-3 (N = 1007 baseline, N = 533 longitudinal; age 42-95; 3–15-year follow-up), and HABS-HD (N = 3656 baseline, N = 1371 longitudinal; age 50-92; 2–6-year follow-up). All cohorts provided T1-weighted structural MRI, comprehensive cognitive assessments, and Cam-CAN additionally provided polygenic scores for intelligence. Cross-sectionally, we tested dissociable pathways from age and polygenic scores to cognition via thickness and area using structural equation modelling. Longitudinally, we derived individual slopes for cognitive change and brain metrics, and tested change-change and temporal associations through linear regressions, bivariate latent change score and growth models. 

Results

Cross-sectional analyses consistently showed thickness as a significantly stronger mediator of age-cognition relationships than surface area across all cohorts (indirect effects: 14-31% vs. 9-15%). Polygenic scores related to cognition through surface area (Cam-CAN: ~15%, p < .001) but not thickness. Longitudinally, thickness declined significantly faster than surface area in all cohorts (-0.004 to -0.005 mm/year (standardised β = -0.10 to -0.21); -97.91 to -282.11 mm²/year (standardised β = -0.06 to -0.12)). Critically, latent models showed that thickness change predicted cognitive decline in all cohorts (β = 0.12-0.22, p < .001-.014, η² = 0.01-0.05), along with past thickness (β = 0.13-0.27, p <.001, η² = 0.02-0.07), while surface area showed no such relationship with cognitive change. 

Conclusion

These findings indicate a structural dissociation in relation to age and cognitive abilities: Cortical thickness appears to be a more dynamic property in relation to age and cognition, while surface area possibly reflects early genetic trait-like influences on cognitive ability. This dissociation reconciles previous inconsistent findings, and suggests that thickness and area capture distinct aspects of brain-cognition relationships across the lifespan beyond what cortical volume alone can reveal.
