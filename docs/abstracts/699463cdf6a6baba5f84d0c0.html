Introduction

Adolescence is a critical developmental period characterized by increased impulsivity, often leading to risk-taking behaviours. This behavioural phenotype is thought to arise from an imbalance between maturing cognitive control systems and affective processing. While the maturation of the salience network (SN), particularly the anterior insula and dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC), is known to be pivotal for cognitive control (Uddin, 2015), its specific interaction with the noradrenergic locus coeruleus (LC) remains under-characterised in adolescents. The LC plays a central role in regulating the SN; however, the extent to which SN-LC connectivity serves as a neural marker for individual differences in impulsivity is not fully understood. This study aimed to identify consistent neural biomarkers of adolescent impulsivity by examining functional coupling between the SN and LC. We utilised a novel experimental paradigm integrating memory tasks with varying distractor loads, alongside resting-state fMRI, to determine if SN-LC coupling tracks impulsivity across both task demands and intrinsic brain states.

Methods

Participants: Ninety-five healthy adolescents were recruited. After quality control for head motion, data from 90 participants (mean age 14.94 ± 1.7 years; 24 females) were included in the final analysis. Impulsivity was assessed using the Barratt Impulsiveness Scale-11 (BIS-11; (Patton et al., 1995)).

Experimental Design: Participants performed a mixed-block design fMRI task comprising three conditions:

  1. Immediate Memory Task (IMT): Standard memory task.
  2. Delayed Memory Task (DMT): Included a delay period with fillers.
  3. Similar Distractor Memory Task (SMT): A novel condition introducing visual distractors visually similar to cues to heighten cognitive load and necessitate precise discrimination. Trials included 'target' stimuli (response required) and 'catch' stimuli (response inhibition required).

Data Acquisition & Analysis: Imaging was conducted on a 3T Siemens Magnetom Verio scanner. We acquired T2*-weighted EPI (TR=2000ms) for both task-based (316 scans) and resting-state (200 scans) sessions. Preprocessing was performed using SPM12, including motion correction and normalisation to MNI space.

Statistical Analysis: Behavioural data were analysed using Linear Mixed-Effects Models (LMMs). For neuroimaging, we employed a General Linear Model (GLM) to identify task-activated regions. Generalised Psychophysiological Interaction (PPI) analysis was used to calculate task-modulated functional connectivity (FC) between SN nodes (anterior insula, dACC) and the LC. Furthermore, seed-based correlation analysis was performed on resting-state data to examine task-independent FC. We tested the correlation between these FC measures and individual BIS-11 scores, controlling for age and sex.

Results

Behavioural Findings: High impulsivity scores were significantly associated with lower accuracy in target trials (IMT and DMT) and a higher rate of false alarms during catch trials, particularly in the IMT. The SMT condition elicited slower reaction times for false alarms, reflecting increased processing conflict.

Neural Activation: The bilateral anterior insula (f = 39.90 for the left; f = 24.19 for the right; whole-brain FWE cluster-level corrected, p < 0.05, cluster-forming threshold: p < 0.001) and dACC (f = 27.42; whole-brain FWE cluster-level corrected, p < 0.05, cluster-forming threshold: p < 0.001) showed robust activation related to the interaction between task type and response execution. These regions were central to processing the conflict present in catch trials and high-load distractor conditions.

Functional Connectivity (Task & Rest): Crucially, connectivity analyses revealed a consistent pattern linking neural coupling to impulsivity traits:

  1. Task-Modulated FC: During the target phase of the memory tasks, stronger functional connectivity between the insula and LC, and between the dACC and LC, was positively correlated with higher impulsivity scores (bilateral insula-LC: p = 0.060; dACC-LC: p = 0.059; peak-level FWE small volume corrected within the Keren’s 2SD LC mask (Keren et al., 2009), threshold and a cluster-forming threshold of p < 0.005).
  2. Resting-State FC: This positive correlation persisted in the task-free state. Intrinsic functional coupling between the SN nodes and the LC was significantly higher in more impulsive adolescents (Insula-LC: p<0.05; ACC_Sup-LC: p=0.060; peak-level FWE small volume corrected within the Keren’s 2SD LC mask (Keren et al., 2009), threshold and a cluster-forming threshold of p < 0.005).
  3. Brain-Behaviour Correlation: Excessive dACC-LC connectivity was predictive of poorer behavioural performance, specifically higher error rates in target trials and increased false alarms in catch trials.

Conclusion

This study provides robust evidence that the functional crosstalk between the salience network and the locus coeruleus acts as a reliable neural biomarker for adolescent impulsivity. The convergence of findings across both cognitively demanding memory tasks and resting-state conditions suggests that hyper-connectivity between the SN and the noradrenergic system is a stable trait marker rather than a transient state. The novel SMT paradigm effectively highlighted the role of the SN under high cognitive load. These findings deepen our understanding of the neurobiological underpinnings of impulsivity and suggest that targeting the SN-LC circuit could be a viable strategy for therapeutic interventions aimed at improving self-regulation in adolescents.
