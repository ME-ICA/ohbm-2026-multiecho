Introduction

Prenatal brain development lays the foundation for all subsequent stages of human brain maturation. The latter half of gestation (20-40 weeks) is a critical period for the establishment of major cerebral pathways, during which the brain undergoes rapid volumetric growth and increasing cortical complexity. Cortical surface reconstruction is the most essential yet challenging task in fetal brain morphometric analysis. It converts complex cortical structures into precise geometric models, enabling the quantification of key morphological features that cannot be directly measured with conventional imaging. This capability is essential for characterizing dynamic developmental processes and for supporting the early detection of neurodevelopmental abnormalities. Accurate cortical surface reconstruction is therefore crucial for advancing fetal brain research.

However, no dedicated and reliable brain cortical surface reconstruction method currently exists for fetal MRI (magnetic resonance imaging). Most studies directly apply tools developed for adults (e.g., FreeSurfer, Brainsuite) or neonates (e.g., the dHCP structural pipeline1) to fetal data, which may lead to inaccurate reconstructions due to substantial anatomical and image-quality differences. To address these gaps, we modified the dHCP structural pipeline for reliable fetal cortical surface reconstruction. Specifically, we replaced the neonatal-focused DrawEM2 segmentation with a trustworthy AI-based fetal brain tissue segmentation and redesigned several preprocessing and surface extraction steps within the pipeline, providing justification for each modification. Using this improved pipeline, we generated large-scale developmental trajectories of cortical features from 1,158 fetuses and uncovered new insights into fetal cortical maturation. All code will be openly released upon acceptance.

Methods

Data Acquisition. With informed written consent and IRB approval, data were acquired on 1,900 pregnant women (20-40 weeks gestation) with normal fetuses using a T2-weighted (T2w) turbo spin-echo (TSE) sequence to obtain thick-slice stacks from different directions, with 4 mm slice thickness and 0.94 × 0.94 mm2 in-plane resolution. 

Volume Reconstruction. NiftyMIC3 was applied to reconstruct brain volumes at 0.8-mm isotropic resolution in template space, as it provides superior reconstruction quality compared with other fetal volume reconstruction methods. Each reconstructed volume was visually screened, and cases with failed or excessively blurred results were removed. A total of 1,304 volumes passed quality control.

 

Modified Fetal Brain Cortical Surface Reconstruction Pipeline (Fig. 1A). A publicly available trustworthy AI fetal brain tissue segmentation4 was used to replace the original neonatal-oriented DrawEM segmentation in the dHCP structural pipeline. All required derivative files were then generated from the tissue segmentation. A whole-brain mask was directly obtained, and the midsagittal plane of the corpus callosum from the segmentation was used to separate the left and right hemispheres. For each hemisphere, white-matter masks for grey–white interface surface extraction and pial masks for pial surface extraction were created in sequence. Segmentation labels were mapped to the DrawEM label system to produce consistent tissue labels for both hemispheres. To enable regional parcellation, nonlinear registration was performed to align a gestational-age-matched fetal brain atlas5 to each T2w volume and obtain region labels. Using the preprocessed files described above, the remaining steps of the original dHCP pipeline for cortical surface reconstruction were executed, including extraction of the cortical grey–white interface and pial surfaces, surface inflation, spherical projection, and cortical feature definition. In addition to the hyperparameters for label mapping, kinetic parameters in the configuration file were adjusted to enable multi‑stage optimization of cortical reconstruction and improve surface accuracy. Steps such as cropping background regions and merging hemispheric surfaces were adapted and recompiled specifically for fetal anatomy.

Cortical features measurements. Cortical features were derived using measures defined in the dHCP structural pipeline. Cortical thickness was computed as the Euclidean distance between the grey–white interface and the pial surface. Curvature was estimated on the grey–white interface surface. Sulcal depth was obtained during surface inflation and reflects the relative convexity or concavity of the grey–white interface. Surface area was calculated by summing the areas of all triangular mesh elements on the grey–white interface surface.

Developmental Trajectory Construction. Cortical surface reconstruction was completed for all cases with successful volume reconstruction. To ensure reliable developmental modeling and reduce potential bias, we excluded 146 cases that showed brain sizes slightly above or below the typical range for their gestational age based on radiology reports. The remaining 1,158 fetuses were used to generate trajectories of cortical thickness, grey–white interface curvature, sulcal depth, and surface area for the whole brain, each hemisphere, and the four major lobes (parietal, temporal, frontal, and occipital), with left and right sides analyzed separately. All measurements were regressed against gestational age using quantile generalized additive models with six-knot penalized B-splines, and the 5th, 50th, and 95th percentile trajectories were estimated.

Results

We first evaluated the improvement in cortical surface extraction achieved by our modified pipeline compared with the original dHCP structural pipeline. Figure 1B illustrates T2w volumes, the associated DrawEM segmentation, and the grey–white and pial surfaces (yellow) produced by the original dHCP pipeline for five cases in coronal or axial views. It also shows the trustworthy AI segmentation and the corresponding surfaces (green) generated by our modified pipeline. DrawEM tends to produce more aggressive segmentation and impose neonatal tissue characteristics on fetal brains, particularly in cortical gray matter and deep gray matter. These errors propagate to the surface reconstruction stage, resulting in excessive folding and implausible curvature. In contrast, our method produces more anatomically plausible surfaces by leveraging the more accurate fetal tissue segmentation from the trustworthy AI model.

Figure 1C presents results from our modified fetal cortical surface reconstruction pipeline across various gestational ages. For each week, we show the input T2w volume, the trustworthy AI segmentation, the reconstructed grey–white and pial surfaces, and cortical thickness, curvature, and sulcal depth mapped onto the inflated surfaces. Morphologically, the fetal cortex remains relatively smooth before 25 gestational weeks, with the central sulcus emerging around 25 weeks. Subsequently, sulcal–gyral differentiation occurs, secondary sulci (e.g., fine gyri in the parietal, temporal, and occipital lobes) gradually form, and cortical folding complexity progressively increases, a process that accelerates noticeably between 27 and 33 weeks. Regarding cortical features, cortical thickness increases early in gestation and peaks around 25 weeks before gradually declining. This pattern likely reflects early folding of the grey‑white interface surface while the pial surface remains relatively smooth at this stage. Curvature and sulcal depth become increasingly complex with advancing gestation, and the distribution of positive and negative values corresponds well to emerging gyral and sulcal architecture.

Figure 2 shows normative developmental trajectories for cortical features, including the 5th, 50th, and 95th percentiles, derived from 1,158 fetuses using our modified cortical surface reconstruction pipeline. Trajectories were generated for the whole brain, the left and right hemispheres, and the parietal, temporal, frontal, and occipital lobes. Whole-brain-averaged cortical features (a) revealed that cortical thickness and sulcal depth increased early in gestation and then declined with age. Cortical thickness peaked around 26–27 weeks, whereas sulcal depth began to decrease after approximately 32 weeks. In contrast, cortical curvature and surface area increased steadily across gestation, with curvature showing rapid early growth followed by slower change. Consistent with the patterns observed in Figure 1C, all cortical features exhibited accelerated developmental change between 27 and 33 weeks.

Analysis across hemispheres and major lobes showed that most regional trajectories followed patterns similar to the whole-brain averages, with the exception of sulcal depth, which remained consistently higher in the occipital lobe. No substantial hemispheric differences were observed for most cortical features, except in the occipital lobe where left-hemisphere sulcal depth was lower than the right from 20 to 32 weeks. Regionally, sulcal depth development began earliest and progressed fastest in the parietal lobe, followed by the temporal, frontal, and occipital lobes.

Conclusion

We present a modified version of the dHCP structural pipeline and show that it produces more anatomically plausible fetal cortical surface reconstructions. Using this pipeline and 1,158 typically developing fetuses, we generated developmental trajectories of key cortical features for the whole brain and major regions, revealing several important patterns and their potential developmental mechanisms.

Previous fetal morphometric studies that relied on adult or neonatal pipelines have often overestimated cortical folding complexity, leading to biased or incorrect interpretations of fetal brain development. Although a true gold standard for fetal cortical surfaces is extremely difficult to obtain—making quantitative accuracy assessment challenging—we believe that the trajectories derived from our method more closely reflect biological reality. This approach provides a reliable framework for studying fetal cortical maturation and has strong potential to advance research on early brain development.
