Introduction

Brain age gap (BAG)—the difference between predicted and chronological brain age—has emerged as a key imaging-derived marker for capturing deviations in brain aging that are commonly observed across psychiatric conditions1. Both BAG and psychiatric disorders show substantial heritability, suggesting that shared genetic factors may contribute to atypical brain aging and heightened psychiatric vulnerability. However, previous studies have been limited in scope, typically focusing on a narrow set of disorders or a single BAG modality. In this study, we conducted a comprehensive, integrative genetic investigation using large-scale genome-wide data and complementary analytic strategies to characterize genetic correlations, quantify polygenic sharing, and identify loci jointly associated with multimodal BAGs and ten major psychiatric disorders. By leveraging multiple BAG phenotypes that reflect distinct structural and functional dimensions of brain aging2, this framework provides a more complete assessment of how genetic influences on brain aging intersect with those underlying psychiatric conditions, offering a foundation for understanding the biological pathways that link aging-related neural alterations to psychiatric risk.

Methods

We investigated the shared genetic architecture between three BAG phenotypes—derived from gray matter volume (GM), white matter microstructure (WM), and functional connectivity (FC)—and ten major psychiatric disorders (ADHD, ANO, ANX, ASD, BD, MDD, OCD, PTSD, SCZ, and TS) using large-scale genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics from European-ancestry populations. Genome-wide shared genetic influences were quantified by estimating genetic correlations using linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC). We then characterized the polygenic architecture of each phenotype and quantified cross-trait polygenic overlap using MiXeR3, which models the number and distribution of shared causal variants.

To identify specific shared loci, we applied conditional and conjunctional false discovery rate (cond/conjFDR) analyses4, which leverage cross-trait enrichment to improve the detection of pleiotropic variants. Independent genomic loci were subsequently defined and annotated using functional mapping and annotation (FUMA). Candidate SNPs were functionally characterized through integrative genomic annotations and mapped to genes using positional, eQTL, and chromatin-interaction strategies. Mapped genes were then subjected to enrichment analysis based on Gene Ontology (GO) terms.

To ensure robustness, we conducted cross-method and cross-dataset validation analyses. Genetic correlations were re-estimated using high-definition likelihood (HDL); shared loci were verified using local genetic correlation analyses (LAVA); pleiotropic associations were further evaluated using PLACO and MetABF; and effect-direction consistency was assessed in independent replication datasets.

Results

Genetic correlation and polygenic modeling analyses revealed widespread but heterogeneous genetic sharing between BAG phenotypes and psychiatric disorders. LDSC identified significant genome-wide correlations for multiple BAG–disorder pairs, with the strongest involving GM-BAG with MDD, PTSD, and SCZ, WM-BAG with PTSD and SCZ, and FC-BAG with ANX; all associations were replicated in HDL. MiXeR showed substantial polygenicity across phenotypes and varying degrees of polygenic overlap, with the highest shared components observed between GM-BAG and TS and between FC-BAG and ANX, accompanied by high proportions of concordant allelic effects.

To pinpoint shared genomic architecture, we applied conjFDR and identified 98 genomic loci jointly associated with BAG phenotypes and psychiatric disorders. Functional annotation of these loci revealed 7,555 unique candidate variants, and mapped them to 754 protein-coding genes. Substantial mapped genes were shared across multiple psychiatric disorders, with some appearing in up to five disorders (Fig. 2a). Among all mapped genes, 89 were consistently supported by positional, eQTL, and chromatin-interaction evidence (Fig. 2b). Gene Ontology enrichment highlighted convergent biological pathways—most prominently neuronal development, synapse organization, and mitochondrial function (Fig. 2c)—suggesting shared mechanisms linking altered brain aging with psychiatric vulnerability. Cross-method validation further reinforced these findings: LAVA confirmed significant local genetic correlations for over half of the testable loci, PLACO and MetABF supported pleiotropy for the majority of lead variants, and independent replication datasets demonstrated strong effect-direction consistency.

Conclusion

This study systematically maps the shared genetic architecture between multimodal BAG phenotypes and ten major psychiatric disorders. By leveraging large-scale GWAS data and complementary cross-trait analytical approaches, we identify robust genetic correlations, extensive polygenic overlap, and pleiotropic loci enriched for synaptic signaling, neuronal development, and mitochondrial pathways. These shared signals span multiple BAG modalities, reflecting coordinated genetic influences on both structural and functional brain aging. Several genes are implicated across multiple disorders, highlighting transdiagnostic mechanisms that link altered brain aging to psychiatric vulnerability. Collectively, by integrating genetically informed imaging-derived phenotypes with psychiatric genomics, our work establishes a cross-level framework for elucidating the biological basis of mental illness and guiding the discovery of common therapeutic targets.
