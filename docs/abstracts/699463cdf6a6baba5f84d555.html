Introduction

Depression is a prevalent and disabling mental disorder characterized by persistent low mood, anhedonia, and cognitive dysfunction, affecting over 300 million people worldwide1. Despite expanding neuroscientific and pharmacological research, the biological mechanisms underlying depression development remain poorly understood, limiting early prediction and precision treatment2,3. Multi‑omics technologies now enable a systems-level exploration of molecular networks linking genetic variation to clinical phenotypes4,5. However, comprehensive longitudinal multi‑omics integration across genomic, proteomic, metabolomic, and clinical layers remains limited. To address this gap, we performed an integrative multi‑omics analysis in a large-scale population cohort to identify molecular biomarkers, delineate cross-omics mechanisms, and uncover potentially druggable targets for depression.

Methods

We analyzed up to 500,000 participants from the UK Biobank with available data across four omics domains: clinical laboratory tests, metabolomics, proteomics, and genomics. Incident depression was defined using ICD‑10 codes (F32 and F33), excluding pre‑baseline cases. Stage 1 analyses were performed independently within each omics dataset using Cox proportional hazards models to identify biomarkers associated with depression onset. Stage 2 analyses integrated 18,343 participants with complete data across all four omics layers. Cross‑omics associations among significant biomarkers were mapped using Bonferroni correction and clustered via the Louvain community detection algorithm to construct multi‑omics functional modules (MFMs). A transformer‑based algorithm was employed to build an integrative multi‑omics risk score. Causal inference was conducted using Mendelian randomization (MR) integrating plasma pQTL and brain eQTL data to assess causal protein effects and therapeutic potential.

Results

In total, 96 proteomic, 170 metabolomic, and 41 clinical biomarkers were significantly related to depression risk (FDR < 0.05), revealing alterations in immune, inflammatory, and lipid‑metabolic processes. Genome‑wide and meta‑analytic analyses identified 236 independent loci, including six novel genomic signals, refining the genetic architecture of depression. Multi‑omics network analysis uncovered over 20,000 significant cross-layer associations, forming three principal functional modules, including MFM1 (immune activation and lipid turnover, enriched for membrane‑related receptor signaling), MFM2 (lipid metabolism integrated with synaptic remodeling and neuroimmune pathways), and MFM3 (oxidative stress and systemic immune regulation).

An AI‑based multi‑omics risk score combining all data layers achieved the strongest association with depression incidence (HR = 1.77, P = 2.4 × 10-24), outperforming single‑layer models (Figure A and B). Higher multi‑omics risk scores correlated with higher depression and anxiety symptoms, poorer cognitive performance, and structural brain alterations, including reduced cortical thickness in the insula and superior temporal regions and disrupted white‑matter integrity in thalamic and fronto‑temporal tracts (Figure C and D).

Mendelian randomization analyses identified six proteins (ASGR1, BTN3A2, CBLN4, CHGA, DDAH1, RNASE1) with significant causal effects on depression. Integrated evidence across tissue specificity, molecular correlations, and drug data highlighted ASGR1, BTN3A2, and DDAH1 as promising peripheral targets, while CBLN4 and CHGA showed strong brain‑specific expression, implicating metabolic, immune, and neuroendocrine pathways.

Conclusion

Our integrative plasma multi‑omics analyses reveal convergent immune, metabolic, and neural mechanisms underpinning depression. The study identifies reproducible biomarkers, constructs an AI‑driven multi‑omics risk model for predictive screening, and highlights several druggable proteins as potential therapeutic targets. These findings advance a systems‑level understanding of depression etiology and provide a molecular foundation for precision prevention and intervention.
