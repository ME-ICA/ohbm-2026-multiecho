Introduction

Generalized additive models for location, scale, and shape (GAMLSS) are a powerful tool for creating normative models and centile growth curves for developmental data. Leveraging their ability to capture nonlinear and heteroskedastic distributions, GAMLSS models have been increasingly applied to human neuroimaging. However, novel applications to different data sources have produced new challenges. For many datasets, GAMLSS models are susceptible to overfitting due to the flexibility of the smoothing terms. We present three illustrative case studies of GAMLSS applications to human developmental neuroimaging data. Each required different curve-optimization approaches, suggesting that GAMLSS modeling is not a simple, out-of-the-box approach. 

Methods

GAMLSS models were fit to characterize developmental trajectories in three experiments. 1) We modeled the development of state engagement variability—a metric of neural variability measured through fMRI-derived brain states^1—in 4091 (1997 female) participants from the Philadelphia Neurodevelopmental Cohort, Healthy Brain Network, Human Connectome Project-Development, UCLA Consortium for Neuropsychiatric Phenomics, Japanese SRPBS, Transdiagnostic Connectome Project, HCP-Early Psychosis, and HCP-Aging datasets. 2) We created longitudinal normative growth curves of visual-evoked potential (VEP) morphology—a robust EEG response of visual neurodevelopment—with 1,374 VEPs across 802 infants (57 to 579 days old) from South Africa (622 obs. from 303 infants), Brazil (500 observations from 390 infants), and the United States (252 obs. from 109 infants).^2 3) We used structural connectomes from fetuses, preterm infants, and term infants in the developing Human Connectome Project (N=859; 393 female) to chart changes to whole-brain controllability.^3 We compared multiple different model fitting approaches across these examples. 

Results

In 1), we used a Bayesian Information Criterion within each model term to penalize overfitting. Using p-spline smoothing terms with the BIC provided an ideal balance of model bias and variance. With a large sample, models were robust to overfitting when using this penalty, despite the unharmonized data (Fig. 1A).

In 2), the data consisted of cohorts from three continents with otherwise harmonized data. A similar approach to 1) resulted in overfitting to non-biological oscillations in the study population, decreasing the likelihood that these curves would generalize across diverse cohorts. We developed a novel fitting procedure using a leave-one-dataset-out approach. We then optimized hyperparameters by minimizing the summed model BIC scores across iterations, leading to robust models that could be validated across datasets (Fig. 1B). 

In 3), with fewer samples per group than in previous cases, four-term distribution models were susceptible to overfitting. As a result, we limited comparisons to two- and three-term distributions. As in 1) and 2), model parameters were chosen by minimizing the BIC, providing a penalty on the degrees of freedom. The resulting curves successfully captured the trajectory of controllability throughout gestation and birth (Fig. 1C).

Conclusion

GAMLSS provides a toolset well-suited to modeling developmental trajectories in a range of distributions in human neuroimaging data. However, care is needed when fitting these models, as a one-size-fits-all approach will lead to overfitting. As new applications are explored, we encourage researchers to share their pitfalls and adaptations to inform future endeavors better. Here, we demonstrate successful modeling of multisite and multidataset longitudinal and cross-sectional data, as well as EEG, fMRI, and diffusion MRI data, with applications for neuroscience, psychiatry, and public health. This remarkable flexibility has made GAMLSS an exciting and powerful option for normative modeling.
