Introduction

The study of subjective well-being (SWB) and comfort and how to increase both has gained more attention in the last few years. Most of those studies rely on subjective scores which are hard to measure and determine. Thus, determining SWB via physiological measures has been a topic of interest. We focused on electroencephalography (EEG) and how it is related to SWB. We specifically want to investigate the behavior of short-term SWB changes. When it comes to SWB changes many papers discuss it in the light of psychological or psychiatric intervention. We focus on short-term changes, i.e., changes that occur during minutes or seconds influenced by environmental conditions. Thus, in this context short-term SWB is also closely related to comfort or thermal comfort. In the past we discussed a relationship between short-term SWB and frontal alpha asymmetry (FAA) 1 as well as the relationship between short-term SWB and asymmetries of different frequency bands and sensor locations2. While we found correlations between SWB and asymmetries on group levels, we were seeing a high diversity between individual participants, including the sensor locations and EEG frequency bands that yield the best results. That raises the question if some participants are more similar to each other than to other participants. In other words: Are there groups of people who show a similar relationship between short-term SWB and EEG? We try to answer this question by using different algorithms to correlate EEG recordings to short-term SWB. If a model developed for participant A works well for participant B, we can assume that they share a similar correlation between their EEG measures and the reported SWB.

Methods

The study was approved by the local ethics committee. We recorded EEG from participants for up to 9 minutes and asked them to rate their SWB every 30 seconds on a Likert scale ranging from 1 (worst) to 10 (best). We used an Emotiv EPOC X headset (EMOTIV, San Francisco, USA) for the recordings. After one session we changed the temperature and humidity settings of the room and recorded another session. This was done with six different temperature-humidity settings per participant. We preprocessed the EEG data using the same pipeline as in 1,2 following HAPPE 3 using EEGLAB 4 and MARA 5. We then selected the 10 s interval before the SWB was reported and labeled it with the corresponding SWB value. We compared different algorithms to see if the similarity between two participants is consistent when determined via various algorithms. We focused on the correlation between EEG and SWB via (a) calculating the FAA of the sensors AF3 and AF4, see 1, (b) calculating the relative power (RP) of a specific frequency band and at a specific sensor, or (c) using the full times series (TS)  filtered into a specific frequency band and at a specific sensor. We included the delta band (0.5–3 Hz), the theta band (4–7 Hz), the alpha band (8–13 Hz), the beta band (14–30 Hz), and the gamma band (>31 Hz).  14 channels (AF3, F7, F3, FC5, T7, P7, O1, O2, P8, T8, FC6, F4, F8, AF4) were measured. In order to establish similarity between two participants we used the algorithm (a), (b) or (c) established for participant A to predict SWB for B and calculate the distance via 

distAB(Subtrue,SWBpred)=1/n*(Sumi=1n|SWBtrue,i-SWBpred,i|)/unique(SWBtrue)

Considering FAA, we used the slope k and the intercept d of the linear regression of participant A to calculate SWB for participant B. For RP and TS, we used a k-nearest neighbor (k-NN) approach with k = 1 for each participant using as input either the RP of a specific frequency band or the filtered TS at a specific sensor and as label the SWB values. We trained those algorithms using the data of participant A and then calculated the distance to participant B via (1). After determining a distance matrix for (a) FAA, (b) RP, or (c) TS. We used k-means (k = 2, 3, 4, 5, 6) to cluster the participants. We evaluated the partitions using the Silhouette coefficient which contrasts the mean distance between a sample and all samples in the same cluster to the mean distance of the sample to all samples of the nearest cluster adjusted for the maximum of the distances. A negative Silhouette coefficient represents not well-defined clusters while a coefficient of 1 stands for well separated dense clusters. Once every participant was assigned a cluster, we compared the three partitions to each other to see if two participants tend to be clustered into the same partition or not. To determine that we calculated the adjusted Rand Index of two partitions (a-b), (a-c) and (b-c). This index compares how many pairs of elements are in the same group and how many pairs are in different groups in two given partitions divided by the total number of all possible pairs and adjusted for random clustering, i.e., assigning each participant to a cluster at random.

Results

Data from 30 participants (self-reported: 14 female, 16 male, 28 right-handed, 2 left-handed, age: 22.3 ± 4.2 years) were recorded. One participant was excluded from the analysis because this participant only reported a single SWB. The following Table 1 shows the Silhouette coefficient for the clustering resulting from the distance metric from algorithm (a) FAA, (b) RP or (c) TS. All over we can see that the algorithm using k-NN with RP values as input gives the lowest Silhouette coefficients for each number of clusters. The Silhouette coefficients for RP range from 0.35 to 0.42 which indicates some separation or moderate structure.  FAA as well as TS, give higher Silhouette coefficients than RP but vary in the number of clusters that result in the highest Silhouette coefficient, namely k = 6 for FAA and k = 4 for TS.  All of the Silhouette coefficients of either FAA or TS are >= 0.43 which indicates a strong structure or well separated clusters.

Regarding the comparison of the partitions, it was not surprising, that he adjusted Rand Index for the comparison with either FAA to RP or TS to RP was close to 0, indicating no or very little consistency between the partitions. The highest adjusted Rand Index (= 0.60) was found for the comparison between the clusters of FAA and TS for k = 2, which indicates that when we compare those two partitions, around 60% pairwise comparisons are the same even after adjusting for random clustering. Comparing the partitions of FAA and TS for k = 6 and k = 4, which gave the highest Silhouette coefficients, we find much lower adjusted Rand Indices of 0.14 and 0.07, respectively.

Conclusion

The results make us conclude that while it is possible to cluster participants into different groups according to their relationship between EEG measures and SWB these clusters are often not very well-defined and not consistent among different algorithms. Using the FAA or the TS algorithm results in well-separated clusters, especially for k = 6 or k = 4, respectively. However, those tend to not be consistent with each other. The overall best result gives a match of 60% pairwise comparisons between FAA and TS for k = 2, which does not represent consistent clustering. It was surprising to us that there was hardly any correlation between the clusters resulting from FAA and RP as those are both methods widely discussed in the literature. We conclude that while correlations between EEG and SWB hold on a group level there is a high individual variability between individuals. Our approaches to find clusters of similar participants which are more similar to each other within one group compared to other groups did not yield convincing results. The reason for that might be that the intersubject variability is so high and unique that no groups of participants can be found. On the other hand, our sample size of 30 participants is not very large and, thus, can definitely affect the results. This low number of participants also did not allow us partitions with a high number of clusters. Maybe increasing the number of clusters to k > 6 could give more consistent results. Lastly, we also want to point out that we performed the analysis with and without the two left-handed participants. The results of both analyses differed just very slightly from each other. When comparing the two left-handed participants we often found contrary results. That makes us believe that left-handed participants do not have a clear different relationship between EEG measures and SWB when compared to right-handed participants. However, only having two left-handed participants is too small of a sample size to make any final judgement about the difference between left- and right-handed participants. We conclude that while results for correlations between EEG measures and SWB hold on a group level, there is a high variability between participants, which could not be simplified by dividing them into subgroups with the metrics we considered. This should be kept in mind when working on predicting SWB from physiological measures.
