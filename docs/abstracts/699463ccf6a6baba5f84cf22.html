Introduction

Aperiodic activity has long been identified as an undesirable, though ubiquitous, feature of electrophysiological activity1, referred to under various terms (e.g., “1/f”, aperiodic, scale-free, ultra-slow). However, aperiodic activity is increasingly viewed as a carrier of meaningful information, and has been shown to be correlated with aging, state of consciousness or disease2. Furthermore, because aperiodic activity can confound  the estimation of oscillations, it could interfere with traditional functional connectivity (FC) measures  that assume oscillations. Indeed, we recently showed that putatively oscillatory EEG-based FC networks are dramatically overestimated using standard pipelines, notably within the delta, theta and gamma bands3. Here, we hypothesized that the aperiodic coupling that induces strong confounds in FC estimates has an organized structure, analogous to “traditional” oscillatory FC networks. Assuming this hypothesis holds, we aimed to investigate the architecture of resting-state aperiodic networks as well as its robustness by integrating data from two independent databases. 

Methods

Resting-state, eyes-closed EEG data of healthy volunteers were obtained from two independent databases. The first database (hereby referred to as the “Dortmund database”) was obtained from Open Neuro (https://openneuro.org/datasets/ds005385/versions/1.0.3), from which the first 30 datasets were used (64-channel EEG). Among those 30 datasets, a total of N=28 were usable (19 female, 10 male, mean age 44.2 years old, standard deviation 15). The second database (hereby referred to as the “EEGCOG database”) included a total of N=30 datasets (256-channel EEG), of which only N=26 were usable (14 female, 12 male, mean age 61.8 years old, standard deviation 7.3). Data processing was achieved using the Brainstorm module (Matlab, The Mathworks, USA).

In both databases, we applied the exact same pre-processing steps: 3 minutes (180 seconds) of resting-state, eyes-closed EEG were  extracted, and baseline correction was applied. Then, notch filtering (50 Hz) and band-pass filtering (0.5-90 Hz) were applied. Independent component analysis (ICA) was then used for the removal of artefactual components. Bad electrodes were interpolated, and finally, data were re-referenced to the global average. 

Once the data were pre-processed, source-level activity was reconstructed using the ICBM152 head template and the wMNE inverse method, and subsequently  projected on the Desikan-Killiany atlas4 (68 regions of interest, ROIs). In the source space, we applied the SPRiNT algorithm (a time-varying version of the algorithm1 implemented in Brainstorm to obtain time-dependent estimates of aperiodic parameters (exponent and offset) for each ROI. Analyses were performed using 1-second windows with 50% overlap. Recommended default parameters for SPRiNT were used. Once dynamic aperiodic parameters were estimated, we computed the correlation Ci,j between all pairs (i,j) of ROI time series for both the exponent and offset. The resulting correlation matrices defined the aperiodic functional connectivity (AFC), yielding exponent-based and offset-based AFC matrices featuring each 68x68 connections (edges). We then considered separately AFC matrices including positive values (correlation) or negative values (anti-correlation). Finally, we applied a 5% proportional threshold3 to all AFC matrices (positive, negative, exponent- and offset- based). This pipeline is summarized and illustrated in Figure 1. Network visualization was performed using the BrainNetViewer5 software in Matlab. To identify statistically significant connections at the group level, each edge was tested across all subjects using a Wilcoxon signed-rank test (significance threshold of 0.05), with FDR correction applied for multiple comparisons due to the high number of edges. 

Results

Group-level networks based on either the exponent or the offset are presented for each database (Dortmund and EEGCOG) in Figure 2. Only positive AFC matrices (correlation) are shown, since no statistically significant results were found for anti-correlation AFC matrices, a finding that was consistent across both databases. Exponent- and offset-based networks were extremely similar to each other (Figure 2A), as expected, since exponent and offset values are typically strongly correlated1. The identified networks were comparable in organization across the two databases, demonstrating a strong occipito-temporal pathway, with a projection towards the prefrontal cortex. The identified connections were very consistent between subjects, with some connections being present in almost 100% of participants (e.g., between the rostral anterior cingulate and medial orbitofrontal (in each hemisphere), or between both cuneus or both posterior cingulate, as illustrated in Figure 2B). 

Inter-individual correlation of AFC networks between subjects was strong: the in-between subject correlation for the exponent-based AFC network was 0.36 +/- 0.09 (Dortmund) and 0.28 +/- 0.08 (EEGCOG), respectively. The in-between correlation for the offset-based AFC network was 0.42 +/- 0.08 (Dortmund) and 0.29 +/- 0.08 (EEGCOG), respectively. Interestingly, the in-between subject correlation of the offset-based AFC network was significantly higher (p < 0.001, Wilcoxon signed-rank test) than for the exponent-based AFC network in the Dortmund database, which was not the case in the EEGCOG database (p = 0.06).

Conclusion

Here, we investigated the putative organization of aperiodic functional connectivity (AFC) networks in resting-state, eyes-closed EEG activity. We provided a first characterization of these networks, which were highly similar regardless of whether the exponent or the offset was used for estimation. Interestingly, at the group level and in both databases, only positively correlated AFC networks were identified (50 and 55 significant connections using the exponent or offset, respectively in the Dortmund database; and 30 and 32 respectively in the EEGCOG database), whereas no anti-correlated AFC networks were identified in either database. 

Our results reveal that, across two independent databases, AFC is organized into highly consistent and reproducible networks, featuring a strong occipito-temporal structure that projects onto the prefrontal cortex. Notably, several Default Mode Network (DMN) structures (e.g., anterior and posterior cingulate) were strongly coupled with their contralateral counterparts, but not with each other. Interestingly, this spatial organization of AFC strongly correlates with two major white matter tracts, namely the inferior longitudinal fasciculus and the inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus. While this warrants further investigation these findings provide additional support for the notion that aperiodic activity is not simply “noise”, and has identified potential aperiodic activity generators that can have a functional role (exitability regulation). Since aperiodic activity is believed to reflect, among other factors, the excitation/inhibition balance, our results suggest that AFC might provide a mechanism for large-scale excitability regulation. Therefore, some possible prospects include investigating AFC networks in epileptic patients, where hyperexcitability is known to be present, which can be of interest to investigate such large-scale excitability regulation mechanism. Another possible direction is studying the possible relevance of AFC as a biomarker for neurological disorders in general, and for the identification of epileptic zones in particular.
