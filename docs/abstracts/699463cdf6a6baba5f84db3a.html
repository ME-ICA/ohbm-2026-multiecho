Introduction

Adults with Down syndrome (DS) follow a highly predictable course of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) neuropathology, driven by lifelong overexpression of the amyloid precursor protein on chromosome 21. This genetic certainty allows AD-related pathology to be examined within a clearly defined temporal framework. Amyloid-β (Aβ) deposition emerges strikingly early in DS, with the striatum showing the first detectable signs years before cortical involvement.1 This consistent early progression in DS creates a uniquely stable framework for detecting upstream factors that influence when and where AD pathology first emerges. The nucleus basalis of Meynert (NbM), the principal cholinergic source to the cortex, shows selective vulnerability in early AD and is increasingly recognized as a potential initiator of downstream cortical pathology,2 including Aβ accumulation. The NbM is also regarded as a critical node linking AD and DS. Recent studies characterized a highly organized infra-slow (<0.1 Hz) global brain activity that is linked to both the NbM and Aβ/tau pathology at very early stages of AD. Using multimodal data from the Alzheimer’s Biomarker Consortium - Down Syndrome (ABC-DS) project,3 we investigated early NbM degeneration at the pre-amyloid stage in DS and how this subcortical change relates to altered global brain activity and emerging amyloid pathology in DS.

Methods

A total of 164 adults with Down syndrome who had structural MRI, resting-state fMRI, and Aβ Centiloid data were initially identified. Only participants with fMRI repetition times (TR) of 1.25 s or 3.0 s and with Aβ Centiloid data acquired during the same visit as the fMRI scan were included. Eight participants were excluded due to poor-quality T1-weighted images (e.g., excessive head motion), four were excluded due to failed image registration, and 19 were excluded for excessive head motion during fMRI (mean framewise displacement > 1 mm), yielding 133 individuals for analysis (41.2 ± 9.5 years; 54 women). Forty age-matched sibling control participants were also identified (44.9 ± 13.7 years, 34 women) from the ABC-DS project. Participants were divided into younger and older subgroups using an age threshold of 35 years, based on prior observations that amyloid deposition begins to accelerate around this age in DS.4 This resulted in 44 younger (18 women) and 89 older (36 women) individuals with Down syndrome, and 14 younger (9 women) and 26 older (25 women) control participants. The NbM volume was computed on structural MRI based on the Ch4 region of a stereotaxic probabilistic map and adjusted for total intracranial volume, age, and sex.2 The same procedure was applied to the control region, the primary somatosensory cortex (area 3a, PSC). Aβ Centiloid (Aβ CL) data were obtained from the ABC-DS, a threshold of 18 Centiloids was applied to classify participants as Aβ-positive. The global brain activity was quantified by global mean blood-oxygen-level–dependent (gBOLD) signal, which was derived by averaging z-scored resting-state fMRI signals across all gray-matter voxels. The strength of gBOLD activity across different brain regions was quantified by gBOLD presence, which was computed as correlations between the gBOLD and regional BOLD signal at each voxel or parcel, as described previously.5 The gBOLD presence was adjusted for both age and sex in the following analyses. Aβ-accumulation stages were defined based on a previously established nine-stage PIB-PET progression model in Down syndrome,1 in which amyloid deposition begins in the striatum and proceeds through prefrontal, cingulate, parietal, temporal, sensorimotor, visual, and finally amygdala. For analysis, stages 1–2 were grouped as early, stages 3–6 as middle, and stages 7–9 as late Aβ-accumulation stages.

Results

Overall, the NbM volume (Ch4 ROI; Fig. 1A) was significantly smaller in DS adults than in age-matched controls (Fig. 1B). Among Aβ-positive participants (Aβ CL > 18), higher cortical amyloid burden was associated with smaller NbM volume (ρ = −0.50, p = 0.0001; Fig. 1C), with age and sex controlled. The Aβ CL started to increase around age 35 yrs, with no Aβ-positive participants in the younger group (Fig. 1D). However, stratifying the NbM volume comparison by age groups revealed that the younger DS adults exhibited smaller NbM volume as compared to age-matched younger controls (p = 0.017), and this difference grew larger in the older groups (Fig. 1E). By comparison, the primary sensory cortex (area 3a) did not show evidence of group differences between DS and controls in either younger or older adults (p = 0.89 and p = 0.36, respectively; Fig. 1G), supporting the regional specificity of the NbM effect. The NbM volume remains its significant associations with Aβ CL in the older DS group (ρ = −0.37, p = 0.0004), with controlling age and sex, but this association was absent in the younger group (ρ = 0.20, p = 0.19) (Fig. 1F).4 Neverthless, smaller NbM volume in the younger group correlated with positively with gBOLD presence in various brain regions, particularly the caudate and superior frontal gyrus, which are known to accumulate Aβ early in DS. We then defined early, middle, and late Aβ accumulation regions in DS based on a previously established Aβ progression model (Fig. 2B).1 In younger DS adults, the gBOLD-NbM correlation decreased gradually from early to late stage regions, with the strongest positive correlations in the early regions of Aβ accumulation (Fig. 2C-2D). In older DS adults, Aβ CL correlated negatively with gBOLD presence, with the strongest associations found in the same set of early Aβ accumulation regions (Fig. 2E-2F).

Conclusion

Smaller NbM volume is evident in Down syndrome and associated with amyloid pathology at older ages (> 35 yrs). Notably, the NbM volume differences were present in younger ages in younger ages (£ 35 yrs), prior to detectable amyloid pathology on PET scans, and is correlated with reduced gBOLD activity in brain regions known to have the earliest Aβ accumulations in DS. Interestingly, weaker gBOLD activity in these brain regions in the older DS group correlated with more severe amyloid pathology (Aβ CL). These results identified reduced NbM volume as a possible early change in DS that corresponds with reduced gBOLD activity in regions of early Aβ accumulation. The findings suggest that the NbM may act as a key hub that amplifies the cortical Aβ pathology through infra-slow global brain activity.
