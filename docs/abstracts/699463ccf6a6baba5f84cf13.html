Introduction

Humans perceive time even in the absence of external events, implying that the brain carries an intrinsic sense of time, a latent temporal “fingerprint” maintained beneath conscious awareness. A growing body of work suggests that the heartbeat may provide the scaffolding for this internal clock [2]. Rhythmic cardiac signals offer a continuous temporal structure for perception, cognition [5], and emotion, and time itself is experienced as accelerating with age[4], a phenomenon also observed in Parkinson’s disease and dementia. This raises a central question: does the resting brain’s representation of the heartbeat encode an intrinsic sense of time, and does this mechanism change across the lifespan?

Heartbeat-evoked responses (HERs), reflecting cortical responses to afferent cardiac signals, offer a promising avenue for exploring this possibility. Cardiac inputs reach the insula, anterior cingulate, orbitofrontal cortex, and related networks via vagal and spinal pathways, and HERs have been linked to arousal, predictive processing, interoception, and aging[3]. Yet most HER studies focus on task-dependent influences of cardiac phase, leaving open how the brain processes heartbeats during rest. Resting-state HERs may therefore capture intrinsic timing architecture shaped by cardiac-cortical coupling.

Building on this foundation, the present study tests whether the resting brain carries age-dependent signatures of time perception reflected in its encoding of cardiac events. Using resting-state MEG data from the Cam-CAN cohort (N = 620, ages 18–88)[1], we extracted HERs time-locked to ECG R-peaks, computed inter-trial phase coherence to assess heartbeat-driven phase resetting, and applied Granger causality to map cortico-cortical and cortico-cardiac interactions. Together, these analyses reveal circuit-level principles through which heartbeat-driven cortical dynamics may shape the brain’s intrinsic sense of time across the lifespan.

Methods

1. Dataset and Participants
Data were obtained from the Cambridge Centre for Ageing and Neuroscience (Cam-CAN), a large-scale, population spanning from 18 to 88 years. Eyes-closed resting-state MEG and simultaneously recorded ECG were used for the study. Participants with cardiovascular disease, uncontrolled hypertension, stroke history, pacemakers, or vascular implants were excluded. Analyses were performed both continuously (age as a variable) and categorically (Young: 18–40; Middle: 41–60; Older: 61–87).

2. Data Acquisition
MEG was recorded using a 306-channel Elekta Neuromag system (102 magnetometers, 204 gradiometers) in a magnetically shielded room. Data were sampled at 1000 Hz (0.03 Hz high-pass), with continuous head-position tracking via HPI coils and EOG monitoring. Resting-state acquisition lasted ~8 min 40 s. ECG was acquired concurrently using bipolar electrodes at 1000 Hz.

3. Preprocessing
Cam-CAN preprocessing included temporal signal space separation, continuous head-motion correction, and 50-Hz noise removal. Data were detrended and band-pass filtered (1–45 Hz). Artifact components (blinks, cardiac field) were removed using ICA in MNE-Python. ECG signals were detrended and processed using the Pan-Tompkins algorithm to extract R peaks, participants with poor ECG quality (RR variability, QRS power, kurtosis/skewness criteria) were excluded, yielding 620 final subjects. Analyses were conducted in MATLAB using Fieldtrip. Figure 1 provides an overview of the processing and analysis steps.

4. Cardiovascular Metrics
IBI and HRV were derived from R-peak intervals during the first 60 s of ECG. HR was computed from mean IBI. Cam-CAN systolic/diastolic pressures were averaged across three readings. Linear regression was conducted to understand the trend across age.

5. Heartbeat-Evoked Responses (HERs)
MEG data were epoched from −200 to 800 ms around each R-peak. HERs were averaged across trials and channels. For continuous analyses, HER amplitudes (180–320 ms) were peak-normalized. For categorical analyses, data were grouped by age. Surrogate HERs were computed by time-locking to random non-R ECG points while preserving physiological IBIs.

6. Mechanism of HERs
Inter-trial phase coherence (ITPC; 4–20 Hz) was computed using wavelet transforms. Significance thresholds were derived analytically (p = 0.05). Time-frequency power differences between real and surrogate HERs were evaluated using cluster-based permutation tests (1000 iterations). 

7. Source Localization
HER generators were estimated using eLORETA with a single-shell head model of the Colin27 MRI. Leadfields were computed with ft_prepare_leadfield. Source power (180–320 ms) was baseline-normalized (−200 to −60 ms) and thresholded at the 95th percentile. Significant regions were parcellated using the AAL atlas. Continuous and categorical analyses yielded 16 combined nodes for further modeling.

8. Source Time-Series Reconstruction
Spatial filters from significant grid points were applied to each participant’s HER epochs to extract x/y/z dipoles, which were projected onto the dominant SVD component. Significant grid points were clustered (k = 3), and principal components per cluster were used as a node-wise time series (one “trial” per participant).

9. Granger Causality (GC)
Directional interactions among 16 brain nodes and ECG were estimated using the MVGC toolbox (Barnett & Seth, 2014). GC was computed as the log-ratio between full and reduced VAR models, significance was assessed against phase-shuffled null distributions with Bonferroni correction.

10. Statistical Analysis
Age and gender effects on HERs were assessed using ANOVA across early (180–320 ms), middle (460–550 ms), and late (650–750 ms) windows; only the early window showed significant age effects. To control for cardiac field artifact, ECG amplitude (180–320 ms) was regressed out from HERs. Linear regressions (using fitlm function) were performed for HERs, cardiovascular scores, ITPC, and GC metrics.

Results

Physiological metrics of cardiovascular health across the lifespan
Using the analytical pipeline shown in Figure 1, we examined ECG and MEG data from the Cam-CAN cohort (N = 620; ages 18–88). Cardiovascular health was assessed using inter-beat interval (IBI), heart rate variability (HRV), heart rate (HR), and blood pressure (BP) measures, including systolic and diastolic values. These metrics were derived from ECG time series and BP scores provided by Cam-CAN. IBI significantly decreased with age (r = –0.0994, p = 0.0133), accompanied by a significant increase in HR (r = 0.1121, p = 0.0052). HRV, however, showed no age-related decline (r = –0.0022, p = 0.9573). For BP, systolic pressure significantly increased with age (r = 0.4370, p < 0.001), while diastolic pressure showed only a marginal increase (r = 0.0734, p = 0.0910).

Heartbeat-evoked responses (HERs) across the lifespan
Simultaneous ECG–MEG recordings were segmented into 1-s cardiac epochs (–200 to 800 ms around the R-peak; Figure 3a). HERs were computed channel-wise and averaged within age groups (young: 18–40, middle: 41–60, old: 61–87) and across gender. Age had a significant effect on HERs in the 180–320 ms window (F(2,619) = 9.47, p = 0.0001), whereas gender showed no effect (F(1,619) = 0, p = 0.9631), and no interaction was observed (F(2,619) = 0.33, p = 0.7213). Surrogate analyses using shuffled ECG confirmed the absence of time-locked activity (Figure 3c). Regression analysis revealed a significant age-related decline in HER amplitude (r = –0.1963, p < 0.001; Figure 3d), robust for both males (r = –0.2161; p < 0.001) and females (r = –0.1735; p < 0.01).

Cardiac rhythms and ongoing neural oscillations
To test whether cardiac signals modulate neural oscillations through phase resetting rather than additive power changes, inter-trial phase coherence (ITPC) was computed. All age groups showed significant ITPC increases in the 180–320 ms window following the R-peak. No such effect appeared with shuffled ECG. Young and middle-aged participants showed predominant theta-band phase resetting, while older adults additionally exhibited alpha-band involvement. Time–frequency power analyses showed no significant R-peak-locked power modulation in any group (cluster permutation p = 0.05). Across the cohort, theta-band ITPC increased significantly with age (r = 0.2236, p < 0.001), whereas ITPC and power were uncorrelated (r = 0.0596, p = 0.1382). No significant ITPC was observed in beta or gamma bands.

Cortical sources underlying HERs
eLORETA source reconstruction (180–320 ms) revealed that HERs originate predominantly from fronto-temporal regions across all age groups. Continuous analysis in 5-year age bins (18–88 years) identified sources exceeding the 95th percentile threshold, which were visualized on a glass brain using MNE-Python. These sources were subsequently used for directed connectivity analyses.

Directed functional connectivity between cortico-cortical and cortico-cardiac nodes
Conditional multivariate Granger causality was applied to all HER sources and the heart. The results showed age-related reorganization of information flow. Heart-to-brain causal drive increased with age across nearly all HER sources except left IFG, whereas brain-to-heart interactions systematically declined. Additionally, strengthening of the connectivity within the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) was observed.

Conclusion

In summary, our findings show that aging reshapes both the cortical representation of cardiac signals and the directional flow of heart–brain communication. HER amplitude declines with age, whereas phase locking becomes stronger, suggesting reduced interoceptive sensitivity alongside a more rigid, heartbeat-driven temporal scaffolding. The cortical generators of HERs also shift, from predominantly frontal regions in younger adults to greater temporal involvement in older adults, accompanied by strengthened orbitofrontal connectivity, indicating large-scale network reorganization.

These age-related changes in how the brain samples cardiac cues may subtly distort the encoding of brief temporal intervals. Such moment-to-moment biases can accumulate across daily experience, offering a mechanistic account of the common observation that “time feels faster” in older adulthood. To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate how endogenous cardiac signals imprint themselves onto resting-state neural dynamics in a manner that tracks subjective time across the lifespan.

Moreover, because disorders such as Parkinson’s disease and dementia show disruptions in both interoception and time perception, heartbeat-driven neural markers identified here may hold promise as early, physiologically grounded biomarkers for neurodegenerative risk.
