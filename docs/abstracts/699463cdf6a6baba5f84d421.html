Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is a common complication during pregnancy that alters the intrauterine metabolic environment, with growing evidence that these perturbations have lasting consequences for offspring. Children born to mothers with GDM show higher risk of obesity and greater adiposity across childhood, independent of shared familial and lifestyle factors. In parallel, accumulating work links prenatal GDM exposure to neurodevelopmental and psychiatric vulnerability, including elevated risks of Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD), Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD), and learning, communication, or motor disorders.

Neuroimaging findings offer potential mechanistic explanations for these associations. Adolescents exposed to GDM demonstrate differences in cortical and subcortical morphology, functional activation patterns, and white-matter microstructure, and global cortical gray matter volume has been shown to partially mediate the relationship between prenatal GDM exposure and childhood adiposity.

Despite these well-documented endophenotype differences among children exposed to GDM, how such differences unfold across development remains unclear. Given brain maturation follows coordinated, temporally ordered trajectories, disruptions to these sequences may yield structured rather than random deviations. Furthermore, these deviations are likely to differ across individuals, reflecting underlying heterogeneity in developmental ordering. Uncovering this heterogeneity is critical, as distinct developmental patterns may lead to unique overt phenotypes, encompassing anthropometric, cognitive, behavioral, and psychiatric outcomes.

In this study, we applied a data-driven model, SuStaIn (Subtype and Stage Inference), to characterize this heterogeneity by uncovering distinct developmental trajectories and linking them to differences in clinical and behavioral phenotypes.

Methods

The ABCD® Study is an ongoing longitudinal study of adolescent development conducted across 21 U.S. sites. Data were drawn from release 5.1, including baseline through four-year follow-up (ages 9–15). The primary sample included 573 adolescents exposed to GDM and 3,557 healthy controls (HCs). GDM status was determined at baseline via parental report (“During the pregnancy with this child, did you have pregnancy-related diabetes?”), coded as a binary variable. For HCs, additional exclusions were applied for unhealthy weight status or adverse Kiddie Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia (KSADS) findings. Exclusion criteria included non-fluency in English; history of seizures; birth >12 weeks premature; birth weight <1,200 g; significant birth complications; major neurological, metabolic, or psychiatric disorders; traumatic brain injury; and autism spectrum disorder. Weight, height, and waist circumference were assessed by trained staff and used to calculate body mass index (BMI) and weight to height ratio (WHtR). Age- and sex-specific CDC percentiles and z-scores were derived, and weight status was categorized using CDC definitions. Implausible anthropometric values were excluded using thresholds for extreme BMI, weight, height, waist z-scores, or WHtR ≤0.3 or ≥1.Participants failing anthropometric or MRI quality control, or missing key covariates, were also excluded.

Behavioral and cognitive assessments included the NIH Toolbox, KSADS, and Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL).

Structural MRI (sMRI) data were collected on harmonized 3T protocols across sites. T1-weighted images were processed with FreeSurfer v5.3.0 to obtain cortical volumes, cortical thickness, and surface area using the Desikan–Killiany–Tourville atlas. Participants with abnormal radiological findings or insufficient scan quality were excluded based on ABCD DAIC procedures, including assessments of motion, intensity inhomogeneity, white matter underestimation, pial overestimation, and susceptibility artifacts.

We applied SuStaIn to identify distinct spatial patterns of neuroanatomical deviation and to order these patterns along developmental trajectories. Because SuStaIn assumes monotonic progression, cortical thickness was used as the primary input, given its well-established peak in early childhood and expected decline across the age range of our sample. sMRI measures were harmonized across sites using ComBat with age and sex as covariates, averaged across hemispheres, and aggregated into six bilateral cortical lobes (frontal, temporal, parietal, occipital, cingulate, insula). These measures were then residualized for age, sex, race/ethnicity, pubertal stage, parental education, household income, handedness, and eTIV( (Estimated Total Intracranial Volume) using healthy controls as the reference group, and subsequently standardized to z-scores.

SuStaIn was applied to an M × N matrix (M = 573; N = 6 lobes) with a z-threshold of 1 defining abnormality. Models with 1–3 subtypes were evaluated using 25 random initializations and 100,000 Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) iterations. The optimal number of subtypes was determined via 5-fold cross-validation using the Cross-Validation Information Criterion (CVIC). Subtype and stage assignments were derived from maximum-likelihood estimates. 

Subtype differences at baseline across demographic, anthropometric, pregnancy-related, behavioral, cognitive, and sMRI measures were assessed using t-tests or chi-square tests, as appropriate. Longitudinal sMRI data (baseline, Year 2, Year 4) were analyzed using linear mixed-effect models (LMMs) to estimate subtype, time, and subtype × time effects on brain morphometry, adjusting for sex and baseline age. For models with surface area or brain volume as the outcome, eTIV was included as an additional covariate. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, with FDR correction applied for multiple comparisons. The full analytic workflow is shown in Figure 1.

Results

Among  the 573 GDM-exposed participants, the mean age was 119.07 months (SD = 7.38), and the sample was nearly evenly split by sex (52.5% male, 47.5% female). The majority of participants were White (59.7%). Regarding developmental measures, nearly half of the participants (47.1%) were in the prepubertal stage, 24.1% in early puberty, and 28.8% in mid-to-post puberty. The majority of children were classified as having a healthy weight (55.7%), with 18.0% overweight, 23.6% obese, and 2.8% underweight. The average estimated total intracranial volume (eTIV) was 1,496,432 mm³ (SD = 186,111 mm³), indicating typical brain size for this age group.

The SuStaIn algorithm was applied to baseline cortical thickness measurements, resulting in the identification of two distinct subtypes among children with prenatal GDM exposure, as determined by the lowest CVIC value. Subtype 1 showed an initial thinning focus in the insula, followed by sequential involvement of the occipital and parietal regions, and later extension to the frontal, temporal, and cingulate cortices. Subtype 2, in contrast, exhibited an early onset of cortical thinning in the frontal lobe, which advanced sequentially to the temporal, parietal, occipital, cingulate, and insular regions (See Figure 2a). 

Among GDM-exposed adolescents, 333 (58.1%) were classified as Subtype 1 and 240 (41.9%) as Subtype 2. For baseline profiling, Subtype 1 was slightly older than Subtype 2 (d = 0.19, p = 0.026). Anthropometric differences indicated that Subtype 1 was taller and heavier (height: d = 0.32, p < 0.001; weight: d = 0.22, p = 0.008), although BMI-z and WHtR did not differ. Subtype 2 had marginally longer gestational age (d = −0.18, p = 0.031), while birth weight, maternal age, and prenatal exposures were comparable. Psychiatric diagnoses were generally comparable between the two subtypes, with the exception of a higher prevalence of past bipolar disorder (BP) in Subtype 2 (11.3% vs. 4.6%; p = 0.021). Current BP and ASD showed marginal significance, both trending toward higher prevalence in Subtype 2 (current BP: 1.7% vs. 0.3%; ASD: 28.2% vs. 21.9%). These effects were small, largely reflecting the low base rates of these conditions in the sample. No significant differences emerged in other KSADS domains, NIH Toolbox cognitive measures, or CBCL scores.

We examined longitudinal associations by modeling subtype, time (years since baseline), and their interaction on standardized cortical thickness, volume, and surface area across six lobes (Figure 2b). Main effects of subtype consistently showed that subtype 2 exhibited larger cortical thickness and brain volumes, but smaller surface area, compared with subtype 1, a pattern observed across all lobes. For cortical thickness, effect sizes ranged from β =  0.43 to 1.33 (all p < 0.001); for brain volume, β ranged from 0.10 to 0.32 (all p ≤ 0.002); and for surface area, subtype 1 demonstrated greater values (β = -0.30 to -0.13, all p ≤ 0.033). Time effects indicated widespread significant decreases in both cortical thickness and volume; whereas surface area showed no consistent longitudinal changes, with most regions remaining non-significant.

Subtype × time interactions revealed region-specific differences in developmental trajectories. For cortical thickness, significant interactions were observed in the frontal (β = -0.06, 95% CI [-0.10, -0.01], p = 0.014), temporal (β = -0.08, 95% CI [-0.13, -0.03], p = 0.003), cingulate (β = -0.07, 95% CI [-0.11, -0.02], p = 0.002), and insular  (β = -0.15, 95% CI [-0.21, -0.10], p < 0.001) lobes, indicating that subtype 2 showed steeper age-related thinning than subtype 1. For brain volume, significant interactions emerged in the occipital and insular regions, although these effects did not remain significant after FDR correction. No subtype × time interactions were detected for surface area.

Conclusion

Using a data-driven SuStaIn framework, we identified two neurodevelopmental subtypes among GDM-exposed children, distinguished by distinct staging sequences of cortical thickness alterations. Subtype 1, characterized by initial insular thinning, presented with lower gestational age and increased anthropometric markers (height/weight). We hypothesize this represents homeostatic dysregulation, where compromised insular function impairs the sensing of internal body states such as hunger and satiety, and resulting in somatic overgrowth. Conversely, Subtype 2 displayed early frontal lobe thinning and was associated with psychiatric history and neurodevelopmental traits. This pattern suggests a primary failure in top-down cognitive and emotional control, marking a distinct pathway toward psychiatric vulnerability rather than metabolic dysregulation.

When linked to longitudinal sMRI trajectories, Subtype 1 showed lower cortical thickness and brain volume but greater surface area at baseline relative to Subtype 2. Across the cohort, both cortical thickness and brain volume declined significantly with age, consistent with expected adolescent neurodevelopment. Importantly, Subtype 2 exhibited steeper age-related decreases in cortical thickness within the frontal, temporal, cingulate, and insular regions. This accelerated thinning directly mirrors the 'frontal-first' spatiotemporal sequence identified by our SuStaIn model, suggesting that the initial vulnerability in higher-order association cortices initiates a cascade of amplified or accelerated maturational deviation in these specific networks.

Together, these findings reveal meaningful heterogeneity in the neurodevelopment of GDM-exposed youth and demonstrate that SuStaIn can uncover distinct anatomical progression pathways linked to both perinatal characteristics and longitudinal brain maturation.
