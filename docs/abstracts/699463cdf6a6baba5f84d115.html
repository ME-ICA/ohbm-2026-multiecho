Introduction

The insula is a complex structure involved in a wide range of functions. Called ‘the hub of the brain’,1 the human insula integrates sensory, cognitive, and emotional information.2
Anatomically, it is divided by the central sulcus into anterior and posterior regions. Anterior region includes anterior, middle, and posterior short gyri while posterior region comprises anterior and posterior long gyri.1 The anterior region is associated with emotional salience and cognitive control while posterior with verbal, auditory, and motor processing. Given its functional implications, investigating its effective connectivity is crucial for advancing diagnosis and interventions across neurological and psychiatric disorders.2
Cortico-cortical evoked potentials (CCEPs) provide direct measures of effective connectivity, enabling detailed characterization of these interactions,3 but to date only a few studies with relatively limited amounts of data have reported CCEP effective connectivity of the human insula.1,2 Leveraging an extensive multicenter CCEP dataset (F-TRACT3), we significantly extend prior effective connectivity studies of the human insula, aiming to provide the highest-resolution effective connectivity map of the human insula to date.


Methods

We leverage the F-TRACT3 database of stereoelectroencephalography (SEEG) responses to intracranial low-frequency (≤1Hz) bipolar electrical stimulations from presurgical evaluation of over 1200 patients with drug-resistant epilepsy. 

We considered patient datasets with at least one contact (either recording or stimulation) located within the insula, which resulted in 897 patients (459 females, age: 26±14 years) from 21 epilepsy surgery centers.

Stimulations were performed in the awake state, at rest, following local practices. Pairs of contiguous electrode contacts were stimulated, with 30±24 pulses per run, using either monophasic 20% or biphasic 80% electrical pulses. Stimulation parameters varied in pulse intensity (3.57±1.33 mA) and duration (0.97±0.48 ms). Patients were implanted with 120±36 contacts in the whole-brain and 6±6 contacts in the insula. Thus, the resulting dataset for this study includes over 4k stimulations to the insula with recordings in the whole brain.

CCEPs were computed as the averaged evoked response over stimulation pulses, after artifact blanking, for each stimulation-recording contact pair and bad channels removals. They were band-pass filtered [1,45] Hz to limit the effect of high frequencies potentially related to epileptogenic responses, and z-normalized using a 200 ms baseline ending at 10 ms previous to the stimulation onset. CCEP features were extracted from the first significant component  (z-scored amplitude > 5; latency < 100 ms) to infer feedforward connections.

We used the  insula parcellation from Montreal4 that offers high-resolution subdivisions of the insula, comprising 19 parcels. Lausanne20185 parcellation was employed for the rest of the brain, in its scale 2 giving 141 parcels per hemisphere. Profiting from its symmetric parcels between hemispheres and because no significant difference of CCEP responses was measured between hemispheres, we merged the responses between right and left hemispheres for ipsi (arbitrarily represented on the left side) and contralateral (arbitrarily represented on the right side) connectivity, increasing the intrahemispheric statistical power. 

Probabilistic effective connectivity was computed for each pair of stimulated-recorded parcels considering the aggregated responses from all contacts within the recorded parcel as the number of significant CCEPs over total stimulations. 

To statistically compare the observed probabilities, we employed a z-test for proportions based on binomial distribution testing the following null hypothesis (with a significance level of 0.05) : there are no statistically significant differences between the high-resolution insular probabilistic connection values. For each recorded brain region, the stimulated insula subregion showing highest probability of effective connectivity was compared with the remaining 18 stimulated insula subregions. A difference was detected if at least one pair showed statistical significance. Effect size was the normalized probability difference (0-1). In this manner, for each recorded cortex region, stimulated insula subregions with highest probability and statistically similar ones were considered dominant of effective connectivity. Benjamini-Hochberg correction controlled false discovery rate.

Results

We computed a dominance map of insula outgoing effective connectivity across the whole cortex (Figure 1). For each region of the brain, it shows the insula region with statistically significant highest effective connectivity when stimulated (dominant insula region). Considering the insular anterior-posterior and superior-inferior axis, we see that they are fully representative in the whole brain by demonstrating distinct probabilistic connectivity.

Posterior superior insula is dominant in connectivity with ipsilateral parietal, central, and temporal regions of the cortex. Posterior inferior insula dominates connectivity with ipsilateral temporal, limbic and occipital regions.

Regarding anterior insula, the superior part dominates connectivity with ipsilateral frontal, parietal, central, temporal, and limbic regions. Finally, the anterior inferior insula dominates connectivity with ipsilateral frontal and limbic regions.

Contralaterally, we see dominant probabilistic connectivity between posterior superior insula with central and temporal regions; anterior superior insula dominant with frontal, central, temporal, and limbic regions; and dominance of the anterior inferior insula with parietal regions. The values of effect size vary between 0.48-0.97.

Conclusion

We leverage an extensive CCEP database to provide the  most complete high-resolution effective connectivity map of the insula showing causal, directly measured efferent projections across the whole cortex and subcortical structures, unattainable with MRI methods, to date. 

We observed that the dominance of connectivity from the insula follows the gradient marked by both anterior-posterior and superior–inferior axes.

A gradient of connectivity is observed along both posterior–anterior and superior–inferior axes: posterior insula is mainly connected with parietal, central, temporal, and limbic regions, while the anterior insula additionally connects with frontal regions; superior insula is mainly connected with frontal, parietal, central, temporal, and limbic regions, whereas the inferior insula is primarily connected with limbic regions.
