Introduction

Investigators have consistently observed decreased cerebral blood flow (CBF) and fMRI BOLD during task periods in a set of regions including the precuneus/posterior cingulate (PCC), inferior parietal lobe, and medial prefrontal cortex1,2. Together, these regions were later recognized as core components of the default mode network (DMN), a system reflecting intrinsic brain activity rather than mere absence of task engagement3. 

Although task-evoked decreases in CBF and BOLD are well established, their underlying mechanisms remain an active area of investigation. [18F]Fluorodeoxyglucose FDG PET has recently been used to investigate task-evoked deactivations in a series of studies by Hahn and colleagues4. These studies demonstrated that task-evoked changes in the cerebral metabolic rate of glucose (CMRglc)  within the PCC vary with task demand. Tasks that required engagement of the dorsal attention network decreased CMRglc in the PCC whereas tasks that activated the frontoparietal control network increased PCC metabolism. 

These studies by Hahn and coworkers employed the recently developed functional PET (fPET) technique, in which tracer is infused continuously throughout the scan rather than administered as a single bolus. Although fPET offers substantially improved temporal resolution, it also has disadvantages, including the need to model the baseline metabolic signal. With this in mind, we sought to determine whether: 1) task-evoked decreases in CMRglc are also observed with the traditional single bolus FDG PET technique, and 2) changes in CMRglc within the DMN are as task-dependent with the bolus method as they are with fPET.

Methods

Participants

Twenty-two (7 male, 15 female) cognitively unimpaired (CDR 0) older adults with a mean age of 72.6 years (SD 4.2; range 65 -78) were recruited from the Knight Alzheimer Disease Research Center and the greater St. Louis community. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All study procedures followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the Washington University School of Medicine Institutional Review Board and Radioactive Drug Research Committee.

Study Design

This study employed a within-subjects design, in which each participant was scanned at rest (eyes open), during a visuomotor task, and during a word-stem completion task (WSCT, Figure 1). The condition order was varied across participants. During the visuomotor task participants pressed a button with their right pointer finger whenever they saw a rotating circular checkerboard. The WSCT is a more demanding language processing task. Participants are shown a three-letter word stem (e.g., HOU) and asked to think of a word that completes it (e.g., HOUSE). Participants were instructed to button-press when they thought of the word and not to speak the word out loud. Each task was performed for the first 30-minutes of each task visit.

Data Acquisition

A Siemens Biograph 3T PET/MR was used to acquire MRI and PET data over a ~60-minute window beginning approximately 1-minute after FDG injection. During the task period we acquired two 2D echo-planar imaging (EPI) datasets to measure task-evoked BOLD activity, one at the beginning of the task period and one at the end. This 5-minute sequence consisted of 150 volumes acquired with a TR of 2 s, a flip angle of 70 degrees, a TE of 28 ms, an echo spacing of 0.6 ms, a 64 x 64 acquisition matrix with 32 slices, and 4 mm isotropic voxels. PET data were sampled dynamically and then reconstructed into variable duration frames (24 x 5 s, 9 x 20 s, 10 x 60 s, and 10 x 300 s) using on-scanner tools.

Data Analysis

MRI preprocessing used standard tools, including sMRIPrep 0.16 (RRID:SCR_016216) and FEAT (FMRI Expert Analysis Tool) in FSL 6.0.6.1 (RRID:SCR_002823). First-level BOLD processing included motion and distortion correction, spatial smoothing (5 mm FWHM), nonlinear volumetric spatial normalization (MNI152NLin6Asym), and linear regression using a double-gamma hemodynamic response function. Group-level analyses used 248 gray matter ROIs from the the Schaefer 200 parcel cortical atlas, the Tian 16-region subcortical atlas, and 3) Nettekoven 32-region cerebellar atlas. 

PET data were also motion corrected, registered to MNI152NLin6Asym space, and sampled into the same 248 ROIs as the BOLD data. We then fit a reversible two-compartment model to each regional time-activity curve using image-derived input functions in order to estimate CMRglc.

Statistics

All statistical analyses were conducted using hierarchal linear models with brms 2.22.0 (RRID:SCR_023862) an R 4.4.1 (RRID:SCR_001905) package for Bayesian inference. We followed the approach pioneered by Chen et al., in which all regions are fit together within a single model5. Our primary outcome measure was whether the 95% highest density interval (HDI) for the difference between rest and task excluded zero.

Results

There was a trend for a global increase in CMRglc during both tasks (visuomotor: 2.6% HDI [-7.8, 13.6] , WSCT: 8.1% HDI [-2.8, 19.4]). Consistent with this trend, CMRglc increased during the WSCT in every region, with the 95% HDI excluding zero in 44 of 248 regions (Figure 2), including areas, such as the visual cortex and left prefrontal cortex, where BOLD signals increased (Figure 2). However, CMRglc also increased in the right lateral prefrontal cortex and bilateral PCC, regions where BOLD signals were unchanged or even decreased. 

The visuomotor task also increased CMRglc across the brain, although the visual cortex was the only region where the 95% HDI excluded zero (Figure 2). BOLD signals in the visual cortex also increased during the visuomotor task, though the spatial extent of BOLD activation exceeded that of CMRglc. Surprisingly, task-evoked BOLD decreases included both lateral and medial dorsal prefrontal regions in addition to canonical DMN areas.

Conclusion

Using a traditional single bolus FDG PET approach, we found that BOLD deactivations were not accompanied by decreases in CMRglc. Instead, there was a global trend toward elevated CMRglc, with several task-deactivated regions showing significant increases. The lack of task-evoked decreases in CMRglc was consistent across tasks, suggesting that task-negative responses are not necessarily driven by reduced metabolic demand.

Previous fPET studies have reported mixed results, with tasks activating the frontoparietal control network increasing CMRglc in the PCC and tasks activating the dorsal attention network decreasing CMRglc4. Differences in approach undoubtedly play a role in the discrepancy between our work and studies employing fPET. fPET is typically analyzed using a generalized linear model, which requires estimation of a “baseline” regressor to isolate task-evoked changes in CMRglc. In contrast, the bolus approach assumes CMRglc is constant across the scan, an assumption not met here since each task occupied only half of the 60-minute acquisition. 

Perhaps a more relevant difference between the bolus approach and fPET is how rest and task conditions are defined. fPET acquires both conditions within the same session, eliminating between-session variance, whereas the bolus approach requires separate scans on different days. The widespread CMRglc increases we observed could indicate an increase in arousal, from a rest scan with no external demands to a task scan requiring sustained alertness and engagement. Such changes would be difficult to detect with standard fPET or BOLD designs, where participants must remain prepared for the next block even during “rest” periods. 

In conclusion, task-evoked fMRI BOLD deactivations were coupled with a widespread increase in CMRglc. The underlying mechanism is unclear, but may reflect an increase in arousal between task and rest states.
