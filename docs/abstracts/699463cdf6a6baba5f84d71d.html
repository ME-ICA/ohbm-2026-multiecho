Introduction

Exposure to propofol can produce heterogeneous responses, from the expected suppression to transient paradoxical excitation ^1. Electroencephalography (EEG) complexity and entropy are promising markers of consciousness across sleep, anesthesia and disorders of consciousness ^2, but different types of complexity measures are often conflated. Type I complexity, such as Lempel–Ziv complexity (LZC), scales linearly with entropy, whereas Type II complexity, such as statistical complexity (SC), follows an inverse parabolic relation and is maximized when signals are balanced between order and randomness ^3. The Complexity–Entropy Causal Plane (CECP) characterizes entropy and Type II complexity, distinguishing stochastic, chaotic, and structured dynamics3. Increasing evidence suggests that baseline, resting state, fluctuations shape brain’s response to perturbation (anesthesia) ^4. In this study, we investigate how EEG dynamics diverge between paradoxical excitation and suppression and identify dynamics which predispose individuals to specific trajectories in response to propofol. We first hypothesize that paradoxical effects are observable in both Type I and Type II complexity measures of EEG. We further hypothesize that paradoxical excitation is reflected in divergent trajectories of brain dynamics from wakefulness into anesthetic-induced unconsciousness on the CECP. Finally, we hypothesize that brain activity before drug exposure shapes susceptibility to anesthesia and individual drug responses, with more random brain activity associated with higher anesthetic susceptibility and lower paradoxical excitation.

Methods

We retrospectively analyzed EEG from two independent cohorts of healthy adults receiving propofol. The first dataset was previously published by Chennu et al., (2016) ^5 (n = 20) used target-controlled infusion and included resting state baseline, mild, and moderate sedation, and recovery, with an auditory discrimination task classifying participants as “responsive” or “drowsy” at moderate sedation. The RecCognition dataset (n = 8) used escalating infusions (100–300 μg/kg/min) from baseline to deep sedation. EEG was filtered (0.5–45 Hz), resampled to 250 Hz, segmented into 10 second epochs, artifact rejected, and average-referenced using MNE-Python. For each participant and sedation level, we extracted LZC (Type I) using NeuroKit, Permutation Entropy and SC (Type II) using Ordpy and summarized median whole brain values. Group differences were assessed with Mann-Whitney U tests. Pearson correlations related baseline SC to changes in SC during moderate sedation, behavioral responsiveness, effect-site propofol concentration, and time-to-loss-of-consciousness. Simulated oscillatory signals, colored noise, and fractional Brownian motion were used to anchor dynamical regimes in the CECP.

Results

We replicated the paradoxical increase in LZC during moderate sedation reported by Boncompte et al., (2021) ^1 and extended this by showing that Type II complexity (SC) exhibits a complementary pattern—decreasing at moderate sedation and increasing at deep sedation. In the Chennu dataset ^5, we replicated the paradoxical increase in LZC at moderate sedation in the responsive group (U = 41.0, p = 0.027), with a slight decrease in the drowsy group (U = 28.0, p = 0.710). At moderate sedation, LZC was higher in responsive than drowsy participants (U = 7.0, p = 0.001). SC showed the opposite pattern: responsive participants exhibited a significant decrease (U = 131.0, p = 0.018) and slightly increased in drowsy participants (U = 18.0, p = 0.456). In the RecCognition dataset, LZC increased at light and moderate sedation, then sharply decreased at deep sedation (U = 58.0, p = 0.005), while SC increased from baseline to deep sedation (U = 13.0, p = 0.050), indicating that paradoxical excitation is transient and vanishes at deeper sedation. These results demonstrate that both Type I and II complexity measures capture paradoxical excitation that is only expressed at moderate sedation and vanishes during deep sedation.

Having established that both Type I and Type II complexity measures track group-level changes with sedation, we next focus on whether baseline Type II brain complexity is associated with individual neural and behavioral responses to propofol. Baseline SC was strongly associated with the change in SC from baseline to moderate sedation (r = –0.70, p < 0.001; responsive subgroup r = –0.89, p < 0.001) and correlated with behavioral scores in drowsy participants (r = –0.88, p = 0.009), independent of drug concentration. In the RecCognition dataset, higher baseline SC was associated with shorter time-to-loss-of-consciousness (r = –0.46, p = 0.30). Across both datasets, CECP trajectories revealed a shared high-entropy/low-complexity region (SC ≈ 0.137) at moderate sedation. Individuals with higher baseline SC remained longer in the transient paradoxical excitation regime before suppression, whereas those with lower baseline SC quickly transitioned into suppression (see Figure 1).

Superimposing empirical EEG data from the RecCognition dataset on the CECP reveals a structured degradation pathway towards propofol-induced unconsciousness. This progression to unconsciousness results in a distinct inflection point at moderate sedation, where the trajectory shifts direction as complexity first decreases, then increases sharply during deep sedation (see Figure 1). The trajectory of every participant passes through this inflection point. While previous results showed that baseline complexity was associated with the response to sedation, the CECP reveals the direction of this change and how propofol sedation alters the balance between order and disorder in neural activity.

Notably, moderate sedation occupies a region of decreased complexity and increased entropy, representing a transient, paradoxical state of heightened disorder. Taken together, these results show that the CECP not only maps neural state transitions but also captures the divergent dynamics underlying sedation response. This identification of the inflection point with the CECP provides a principled framework for interpreting divergent outcomes (paradoxical excitation vs. suppression) based on relative position to the inflection point before exposure to anesthesia.

Conclusion

By disentangling Type I and Type II complexity, we show that “paradoxical” excitation is an expected transient regime along the trajectory to pharmacologically induced unconsciousness. Baseline complexity reflects intrinsic neural susceptibility to propofol and is associated with how long neural trajectories dwell in a high-entropy/low-complexity regime before collapsing into suppression. These findings suggest that resting state EEG complexity and CECP positioning could inform personalized dosing and provide a dynamical-systems framework for interpreting anesthetic state transitions.
