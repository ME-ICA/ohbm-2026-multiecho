Introduction

The vestibular system is essential for balance, gaze stabilisation, and spatial orientation. Its dysfunction can substantially impair daily activities. Vestibular disorders commonly produce vertigo, dizziness, nausea, and nystagmus, affecting nearly 35% of adults.1 Yet, despite their impact, both clinical and preclinical studies indicate that many patients experience substantial or even near-complete recovery over time.2 The neural mechanisms supporting this recovery, often referred to as vestibular compensation, remain incompletely understood. Vestibular signals originate in the inner ear and project to the vestibular nuclei before reaching widespread cortical and subcortical regions involved in multisensory integration, motor control, and spatial cognition. Because of this extensive connectivity, vestibular lesions trigger distributed changes across the brain rather than localised alterations alone. Positron emission tomography (PET) allows us to study network-level adaptations. [18F]FDG-PET (18-fluorine fluorodeoxyglucose) measures glucose metabolism, while [18F]UCB-H PET quantifies synaptic density via SV2A binding. Both modalities have revealed metabolic and structural reorganisation after vestibular lesions in rodent models.2,3 Here, we investigate longitudinal changes in metabolic and structural connectivity following a vestibular lesion to better understand the neural basis of vestibular compensation and to inform potential strategies for improving recovery in patients with persistent symptoms.

Methods

This study included 48 rats, equally recruited from 2 recent experiments.2,3 All animals underwent baseline [18F]FDG-PET and [18F]UCB-H PET scans before receiving a vestibular lesion. Postoperative PET imaging was performed at weeks 1 (n = 47 FDG; 45 UCB-H), 3 (n = 47 FDG; 46 UCB-H), 5 (n = 46 FDG; 38 UCB-H), and 9 (n = 45 UCB-H). Because all data were acquired at a single centre and recovery patterns were comparable across cohorts, the datasets were pooled to improve statistical power. Only a single static PET image per subject per tracer was available, and each image was pre-processed using the same pipeline: rigid registration to CT (when available), alignment to a selected reference PET image, averaging and Gaussian smoothing (FWHM = 0.4) to create tracer-specific templates, brain extraction using template-derived masks, registration to the Schiffer rat atlas,4 smoothing (FWHM = 0.4), whole-brain normalisation, and parcellation into 58 atlas-based ROIs. For resting-state network (RSN) identification, each subject’s PET scans at baseline were concatenated using fslmerge and treated as a single time series for MELODIC ICA, yielding 15 independent components (ICs) per tracer. Dual regression was performed at the subject level per session, and only stage-one outputs were retained to derive subject-specific component expression values. Longitudinal network changes were assessed using linear mixed-effects models with FDR correction across ICs. Additionally, covariance differences across sessions were evaluated using the covariance test5 on 51 ROIs (excluding the pons, pituitary, cerebellar blood, cerebellar white matter, and olfactory regions) to characterise temporal changes in metabolic and structural covariance following vestibular lesion.

Results

ICA identified similar RSNs across tracers, including the cerebellar network (CN), visual network (VN), sensory-motor network (SMN), limbic network (LN), vestibular network (VesN), frontal network (FN), and insular network (IN). [18F]FDG-PET additionally showed olfactory network, while [18F]UCB-H PET uniquely revealed a default mode network. Linear mixed-effects models demonstrated significant longitudinal changes in RSNs. The CN decreased across sessions for both [18F]FDG-PET (F(3, 137.85) = 32.78, p < .001) and [18F]UCB-H PET (F(4, 172.47) = 16.63, p < .001). The VesN in [18F]FDG-PET showed early reductions at weeks 1 and 3 with partial recovery by week 5 (F(3, 136.84) = 9.48, p < .001), whereas [18F]UCB-H PET declined progressively across all time points (F(4, 170.38) = 9.72, p < .001). The FN decreased consistently in [18F]FDG-PET (F(3, 137.7) = 22.46, p < .001), while [18F]UCB-H PET showed a marked increase over time (F(4, 170.83) = 98.94, p < .001). Covariance analyses showed significant metabolic reorganisation in [18F]FDG-PET. From baseline to week 1 (test statistic = 18.6199, p < .001), 10 contributing region pairs were identified, with increased covariance between the inferior colliculus and the auditory cortices (baseline: 0.30–0.31; week 1: 0.70–0.83), the left insula (baseline: 0.22; week 1: 0.77), the medial prefrontal cortices (baseline: 0.35–0.41; week 1: 0.75–0.77), the orbitofrontal cortices (baseline: 0.42–0.45; week 1: 0.79–0.83), and the left somatosensory cortex (baseline: 0.40; week 1: 0.82). The only decreasing connection involved the right ventral tegmental area (baseline: 0.38; week 1: -0.29). For baseline vs. week 3 (test statistic = 38.94, p < .001), 133 contributing pairs indicated widespread increases in cortical–cortical covariance (baseline: 0.46–0.76; week 3: 0.83–0.93), cortical–subcortical covariance (baseline: -0.07–0.52; week 3: -0.60–0.90), and cortical–sensory interactions (baseline: 0.45–0.93; week 3: 0.82–0.96). Cortical–brainstem/cerebellar relationships became more negative (baseline: -0.28 to -0.26; week 3: -0.71 to -0.66). Subcortical–subcortical covariance increased (baseline: -0.60–0.57; week 3: -0.61–0.83), as did subcortical–sensory covariance (baseline: -0.18–0.54; week 3: -0.65–0.89) and sensory–sensory covariance (baseline: 0.57–0.91; week 3: 0.80–0.96). The auditory–medulla pair also became more negative (baseline: –0.52; week 3: -0.77). For baseline vs. week 5 (test statistic = 29.80, p < .001), 57 contributing pairs showed continued strengthening of cortical–cortical covariance (baseline: 0.47–0.54; week 5: 0.89–0.90), cortical–subcortical covariance (baseline: -0.05–0.52; week 5: -0.60–0.82), and cortical–sensory covariance (baseline: 0.44–0.90; week 5: 0.85–0.93). Subcortical–sensory covariance increased (baseline: -0.11–0.54; week 5: -0.59–0.87), and sensory–sensory covariance reached its highest values (baseline: 0.80–0.91; week 5: 0.94–0.96). Cortical–medulla interactions became more negative (baseline: -0.28 to -0.26; week 5: -0.72 to -0.71), and the inferior colliculus–medulla pair showed a marked shift (baseline: 0.02; week 5: -0.59). On the other hand, the covariance difference analysis did not reveal any significant differences between baseline and week 1 (test statistic = -5.8034, p = 0.97), week 3 (test statistic = -1.0777, p = 0.78), week 5 (test statistic = -1.938, p = 0.41), or week 9 (test statistic = -4.9525, p = 0.91) for [18F]UCB-H PET.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that vestibular lesions lead to widespread changes in brain networks, and metabolic and synaptic PET tracers capture distinct aspects of this process. Resting-state analyses revealed consistent alterations across cerebellar, vestibular, and frontal networks. [18F]FDG-PET showed early disruptions followed by partial recovery, indicating dynamic metabolic reorganisation, whereas [18F]UCB-H PET showed more gradual, steady changes over time. These differences suggest that metabolic activity and synaptic density adapt on different timescales during vestibular compensation. Covariance analyses revealed pronounced metabolic reconfiguration in [18F]FDG-PET. Across the first five weeks, we observed strengthened cortical–cortical, cortical–subcortical, and sensory-related covariance, along with increasingly negative cortical–brainstem interactions. These widespread changes indicate active recruitment and rebalancing of large-scale functional networks during vestibular compensation. In contrast, [18F]UCB-H PET showed no significant differences in covariance over time. Together, these findings suggest that vestibular recovery is supported by substantial metabolic network plasticity, while structural changes may primarily manifest as synaptic loss rather than coordinated shifts in connectivity. Multimodal PET, therefore, provides a complementary window into the multi-timescale processes underlying vestibular compensation and may aid in identifying biomarkers relevant for diagnosis, prognosis, and targeted rehabilitation strategies.
