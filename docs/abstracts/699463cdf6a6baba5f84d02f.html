Introduction

Successful execution of goal-directed behaviour relies upon selective attention to focus on task-relevant stimuli, while ignoring or suppressing distracting stimuli. At what stage in sensory processing this selection occurs is debated, with experimental findings interpreted in favour of both early and late selection. Perceptual load theory (load theory) offers a resolution, arguing that the locus of attention is dependent upon the perceptual load of the current task [1].

It is unclear if load theory applies multimodally, specifically, in describing interoceptive-exteroceptive interactions. Interoception is defined as the sensing of the internal physiological state of the body. Most interoceptive processing occurs non-consciously, however, the conscious perception of certain bodily signals can be facilitated by focused attention. An interesting theoretical question, therefore, is whether changes in exteroceptive perceptual load influence interoceptive attentional allocation. 

In this study, we exploited the heartbeat-evoked potential (HEP; neural signals epoched around ECG-derived event-markers) as an established measure of volitional interoceptive attention. Intentional heartbeat focus alters HEP amplitude, yet it is unknown whether the HEP is sensitive to alterations in a putative supramodal allocation of attentional resources. To address this question, participants performed a visual perceptual load task during multi-modal EEG and ECG measurement. A heartbeat focus condition was also included. 

Our primary pre-registered hypothesis was that HEP amplitude would differ between high and low visual load (HL and LL). We also gathered a measure of conscious awareness of bodily signals, predicting individual variability in interoceptive sensibility may moderate the differences between HL vs LL. We further investigated whether visual load affects how the heartbeat influences behavioural outcomes and evaluated whether the HEP predicts subsequent reaction time (RT). 

Methods

Participants 

53 non-clinical participants (24F, 29M) were recruited. 

Perceptual load task 

Participants took part in a visual perceptual load task performed during simultaneous EEG and ECG acquisition. Each participant attended to a sequence of asymmetrical crosses that appeared centrally on a monitor. A rapid keypress response was required whenever a target cross was presented. 

Visual perceptual load was modulated by task demand. In LL blocks, the target cross was a certain colour only. In HL blocks, the target cross was a certain colour and orientation. In addition to the HL and LL blocks (collectively described as ‘CROSS-focus’ blocks), a ‘HEART-focus’ block was included. In this block, participants were instructed to look at the same sequence of asymmetrical crosses but focus on their heartbeat. 

The experiment consisted of 20 HL, 20 LL, and 10 HEART-focus blocks presented in a counterbalanced, pseudorandomised order. Each block consisted of 32 trials of stimulus (cross) presentations. Crosses were displayed for 250ms, with an interstimulus interval of 450, 500 or 550ms. 

Body perception questionnaire

Participants completed subsections of the Body Perception Questionnaire (BPQ) [2]. This questionnaire is used as a measure of ‘interoceptive sensibility’, reflecting the subjective awareness of bodily sensations in day-to-day life. 

Data acquisition

The experiment took place in a dimly lit closed room containing a Faraday cage. Continuous EEG recordings from 32 frontal-central electrodes were obtained using a 64-channel ANT Neuro waveguard original cap, sampled at 1000 Hz with an ANT Neuro amplifier. Electrode impedance was typically kept <10 kΩ. 

ECG was measured by attaching one AgCl electrode below the right lateral clavicle, and another above the left iliac crest.

EEG and ECG preprocessing

The data was subject to a band-pass filter of 0.4-40Hz, re-referenced to the average of the mastoids, then down sampled to 250Hz. Epoching occurred relative to the ECG R-peak (-200ms to 600ms post-R-peak), with pre-stimulus baseline correction using the period of -172 to -24ms pre-R peak.

Analysis

Behavioural data

The d-prime (d’) for each CROSS-focus block was entered into a two-way repeated measures ANOVA. The two factors were load (‘high’ and ‘low’) and block number (1-20). Block number was included in the ANOVA to check for a training effect. The mean RT data from each participant was also subject to a paired t-test to compare HL and LL. 

HL vs LL 

A mass-univariate approach was used to compare HEP amplitude generated during HL vs LL Paired t-tests were performed across all 32 frontal-central electrodes in the time region +0.2-+0.6s post R-peak (101 time points). The Benjamin-Hochberg procedure (BH) with a false-discovery rate (FDR) of 5% was used to correct for multiple comparisons. 

Interoceptive vs exteroceptive focus

As an exploratory analysis outside our primary pre-registered hypothesis, we used the same mass univariate approach as the High versus low perceptual load analysis to investigate HEP modulations due to HEART- versus CROSS-focus. 

BPQ correlation

For both HL vs LL and Interoceptive versus exteroceptive focus, the peak difference between conditions was correlated with each participant's scores on BPQ subsections. If significant channel/time-points were identified, the average region difference was also calculated and correlated with the BPQ subscales. 

Cardiac cycle analysis

The influence of visual perceptual load and the heartbeat on reaction time (RT) and miss probability was investigated using a two-way ANOVA, where the factors were load ('high' and 'low') and cardiac phase ('systole' and 'diastole'). 

HEP and CROSS-focus performance analysis

We investigated whether HEP amplitude preceding target onset (R-peaks -850 to -650ms pre-target onset) correlated with RT across all 32 frontal-central channels using spatiotemporal cluster-based permutation testing. 

Results

Behavioural data 

RTs in HL blocks were significantly greater than in LL blocks (t(50) = 20.2, p<0.0001). The two-way repeated measures ANOVA on the d-prime data also revealed a significant main effect of load (F(1, 49) = 202, p<0.0001) and a load by block interaction (F(19, 931) = 2.54, p<0.001). After Greenhouse-Geisser correction, the main effect of block was no longer significant (p = 0.06). 

Overall, the behavioural data confirm a successful manipulation of load, but also a training effect differentially affecting the HL condition. For this reason, we conducted an analysis using HEPs from the first 6 experimental blocks, when the training effect was minimal.

High versus low perceptual load 

Mass univariate analysis with an FDR (BH) of 5% revealed no significant differences in HEP amplitude between high and low visual perceptual load (n=50, 32 frontal-central electrodes, +0.2-+0.6s post R-peak).

The exploratory analysis using HEPs generated in the first 6 blocks, when the training effect should be minimal, also did not reveal any significant channel-time differences.

BPQ correlation

No correlations between BPQ subscales and the average cluster or peak difference data survived significance testing. 

We also correlated the two subscales of the BPQ with each participant’s resting state HEP. The cluster-based permutation analysis performed across all 32 channels from +0.4 to +0.6s post R-peak (51 timepoints) found no significant clusters (cluster p-value of body awareness subscale = 0.11; p-value of subdiaphragmatic reactivity = 0.30, number of permutations = 1000).

Cardiac cycle analysis 

The two-way ANOVA conducted to investigate the influence of load and cardiac cycle on RT failed to reveal a main effect of cardiac cycle (F(1, 49) = 1.03, p = 0.32) or an interaction effect (F(1, 49) = 0.79, p = 0.38). No main effect of cardiac cycle (F(1, 49) = 0.54, p = 0.47) nor a load by cardiac cycle interaction (F(1,49) = 0.0016, p = 0.97) were observed when analysing the miss data. 

HEP and cross-focus performance 

The spatiotemporal cluster-based permutation test identified a HEP cluster which significantly correlated with subsequent RT during LL blocks (p = 0.049). The cluster was located relatively late in the HEP analysis window and was distributed across left, right and midline channels. This result shows that a more positive HEP amplitude pre-target onset was associated with a faster RT.

We repeated the same steps on the ECG data associated with each epoch included in the LL analysis and found no significant clusters, meaning that cardiac field artifact was unlikely to be responsible for these results. 

One possibility might be that the HEP reflects changes in interbeat-interval (IBI), which could be the underlying cause of the RT correlation. For each participant, we correlated the IBI corresponding to each epoch entered into the original analysis (i.e., the time between the R-peak that HEPs were generated from, and the following R-peak) with subsequent RT and performed a one-sample t-test on the 31 correlation coefficients. IBI did not correlate with RT (t(31) = 0.56, p=0.58), indicating that the original result was not due to changes in IBI. 

To demonstrate that the HEP-RT correlation arises from a differential neural response to the heartbeat, we ran a further permutation analysis. For target events included in the original analysis, the times at which R-peaks occurred within the -850 to -650ms pre-target range were randomly shuffled. The EEG data was then epoched around these shuffled R-peaks, and an identical analysis to the original was performed. This process was repeated 1000 times, resulting in a distribution of summed T-scores from which a p-value for the ‘true’ R-peak event analysis could be generated. The resulting p-value was 0.004, indicating that the HEP-RT correlation was due to fluctuations in heart-brain communication.

Conclusion

HEP and perceptual load

Visual perceptual load had no influence on the HEP, indicating independent allocation of attentional resources. One interpretation for this result is the potential primacy of interoceptive representation and control for survival. For instance, neural tracking and control of blood pressure changes, signalled to the brain by afferent baroreceptor firing (presumed to be a source of the HEP) is vital, regardless of current goals. An architecture in which greater exteroceptive perceptual load reduces representational resources for interoceptive processing and dependent physiological control may therefore be suboptimal. 

Previous interpretations of HEP findings propose a trade-off between interoceptive and exteroceptive attention [3]. The results from this study question this explanation; if a bidirectional trade-off is occurring, then increasing exteroceptive attentional allocation should modulate HEP amplitude.

Interoceptive sensibility correlations

Adaptive physiological regulation, in response to current goals, arguably depends on the capacity to adjust the salience of interoceptive inputs flexibly. Questionnaire measures of self-reported sensitivity to inner bodily signals may then reflect a more embedded level of predictive conscious interoceptive perception. 

However, across both interoceptive versus exteroceptive focus, and HL versus LL, the two BPQ subscales did not correlate with HEP differences. This analysis was motivated by a previous work reporting a correlation (which did not survive multiple comparison correction) between the supra-diaphragmatic subscale of the BPQ and the peak HEP difference between interoceptive and exteroceptive focus [4]. The current results did not replicate this finding and overall do not support the above theoretical account; questionnaire measures were independent of HEP modulations across different attentional conditions. 

Cardiac cycle and perceptual load 

Following the logic that the HEP likely does not index all heart-brain interactions, we also analysed reaction times, hit rate and miss rate during systole and diastole during high and low load. No main effect of cardiac cycle, or a load-by-cardiac-cycle interaction was observed. Overall, our results do not point to the common allocation of attentional resources across modalities, in that a cardiac cycle effect was not reduced under high visual load. 

HEP and behavioural data correlation 

While reaction time and detection sensitivity reflect different neural processes, the basic structure of our findings is similar to those reported by Park and colleagues; with pre-stimulus HEP predicting subsequent measured outcomes and an absence of cardiac cycle effect [5].

We interpret these results as supporting a model in which the HEP reflects greater synchrony between decision-relevant regions. Motivated by the role of mid-frontal theta in promoting neural synchrony, we suggest that the HEP - which is generated by phase-resetting of theta oscillations - captures cortical activity which biases the generation of certain behaviours around the cardiac cycle.
