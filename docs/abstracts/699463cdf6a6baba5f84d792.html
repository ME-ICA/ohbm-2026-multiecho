Introduction

Neuromelanin (NM) is a dark pigment that accumulates within catecholaminergic neurons of the substantia nigra (SN) and locus coeruleus (LC). In Parkinson’s disease (PD), these NM-containing neurons are selectively lost, and NM-MRI signal reductions closely mirror this degeneration1. Although NM formation has long been attributed to the spontaneous oxidation of dopamine, accumulating evidence suggests that enzymatic mechanisms may also contribute2. Tyrosinase, the rate-limiting enzyme for peripheral melanin synthesis, has been detected at very low transcript levels in the human SN, but its functional contribution to NM formation remains unproven.
 
 To test whether endogenous brain tyrosinase contributes to NM synthesis, we examined individuals with Oculocutaneous Albinism Type 1 (OCA1), caused by biallelic TYR mutations and absent or severely reduced tyrosinase activity. By comparing their NM-MRI signal to healthy controls, we aimed to determine whether tyrosinase deficiency results in reduced NM accumulation in vivo.

Methods

Population study

Eight adults with genetically confirmed Oculocutaneous Albinism Type 1 (OCA1/AB; 6 females, 2 males; age 51.9±18.0, MDS-UPDRS III 1.1±2.1) were included. All carried biallelic TYR mutations leading to absent or severely reduced tyrosinase activity3. Age- and sex-matched healthy volunteers (HV, n = 55, age 61.0±10.0, 26 females and 29 males, MDS-UPDRS III 9.3±16.2) were selected from the ICEBERG database at the Paris Brain Institute (NCT02305147). For contextual comparison, data from patients with idiopathic Parkinson’s disease (PD, n = 154, age 62.1±9.1, 57 females and 97 males, MDS-UPDRS III 26.5±7.4). Participants underwent standardized neurological assessments, including the MDS-UPDRS, MoCA, PDSS-2, SCOPA-AU, PDQ-39, and BDI-II, to exclude subclinical parkinsonism or cognitive impairment.

Imaging acquisition

Imaging included neuromelanin-sensitive MRI (NM-MRI) (TA = 6 min 55 s, TE = 13 ms, TR = 890 ms, flip angle = 180°, FOV = 220 mm, slice thickness = 3 mm), quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM)4 (TA = 9 min 18 s, TE = 4.00–37.00 ms, TR = 40 ms, flip angle = 20°, FOV = 192 mm, slice thickness = 1 mm), R2* mapping, and structural MRI, acquired on 3T Siemens Prisma scanners. NM-MRI used a multi-echo T1-weighted turbo spin-echo sequence optimized for SN and LC contrast, whereas QSM and R2* maps were derived from multi-echo gradient-echo acquisitions6. Structural images were acquired using MPRAGE sequences (TA = 4 min 26 s, TE = 2.28 ms, TR = 1900 ms, flip angle = 8°, FOV = 256 mm, slice thickness = 1 mm).

Imaging processing

NM-MRI, QSM, and R2* images were co-registered and preprocessed using FSL and ANTs (Figure 1). Automated segmentation was performed for the SN and the LC. From each region, the contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) and corrected volumes (Cvol, ROI volume/total intracranial volume) were calculated according to the following mathematical equations:

CNR = mean_over_slices [ (Sig_SN – Sig_BND) / STD_BND ]

Cvol = ( number_of_voxels × voxel_size × slice_thickness ) / total_intracranial_volume

QSM and R2* metrics were derived from the same regions to assess iron-related properties4. SN subregional analysis used a probabilistic anatomical template delineating the anteroventral (AV), anterodorsal (AD), posteroventral (PV), and posterodorsal (PD) subdivisions (Figure 1).

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using R and SPSS. Group differences were tested via ANCOVA (age, sex). Multiple comparisons used Bonferroni correction (p<0.05). Percent changes were additionally estimated by t-tests and Wilcoxon tests.

Results

Compared with HV, OCA1 participants showed no significant differences in CNR in the LC (mean±sd HV/AB: 127.7±5.1/128.1±3.7, 0.3% difference, p = 0.8)(Figure 2a). For the SNc, a non-significant decrease was observed in OCA1 compared with HV for the CNR (mean±sd HV/AB: 1.61±0.2/1.49±0.2, −7.2% difference, p = 0.16, Figure 2b–c) and the Cvol (mean±sd HV/AB: 0.32±0/0.33±0, 1.3% difference, p = 0.7) (Figure 2b).
 
 In contrast, the PD group showed significant reductions in all metrics in SNc and LC1 compared with HV and OCA1 subjects (all p < 0.05) (Figure 2a-c) . The NM-MRI signal in OCA1 reflects stable NM levels rather than reduced pigment formation. QSM and R2* values indicated an increased iron-related contrast in OCA1 compared with HV4 (p < 0.001; Figure 2c-d), This increase was 52.5% (mean±sd HV/AB: 0.07±0/0.1±0.0, p = 1.8e-5) and 31.4% (mean±sd HV/AB: 26.6±3.6/35±2.2, p = 2.3e-5), respectively. Both QSM and R2* values were significantly higher in OCA1 (p < 0.001; Figure 2d–e). In the posteroventral region of the SN, PD participants showed the expected increase in iron content relative to HV (p < 0.05), whereas no significant difference was observed between HV and OCA1(Figure 2f-g)5.

Conclusion

No significant reduction in NM-MRI signal or NM volume was detected in OCA1 participants compared with healthy volunteers. The absence of major CNR differences suggests that tyrosinase deficiency alone does not preclude NM formation in the human SN or LC. These findings indicate that alternative, non-tyrosinase enzymatic or spontaneous oxidation pathways of dopamine may sustain NM synthesis in vivo2.
 
 The marked increase in iron-sensitive signals (R2* and QSM) in OCA1 suggests altered iron homeostasis. The enhanced values may reflect increased iron concentration or altered iron–melanin binding dynamics, indicating that the chelation capacity of NM might differ in tyrosinase-deficient neurons.


The variability observed among OCA1 individuals could reflect heterogeneous residual tyrosinase activity depending on underlying mutations. Limitations include the small OCA1 sample size and cross-sectional design. Interpretation relies on NM-MRI as a proxy for NM content2.

In this first in-vivo investigation of tyrosinase-deficient humans, NM-MRI revealed no consistent NM depletion in OCA1, whereas iron-sensitive imaging demonstrated increased iron-related contrast. These findings suggest that human NM formation involves tyrosinase-independent oxidative pathways. Future longitudinal and post-mortem studies combining NM-MRI, QSM, histology, and biochemical analysis are needed to confirm these results and clarify iron alterations in OCA1.
