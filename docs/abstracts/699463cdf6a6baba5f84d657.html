Introduction

Physical activity (PA) has been shown to improve ADHD related behaviours.^1 However, PA programs are not tailored for ADHD and program benefits vary. To optimise PA interventions for ADHD, researchers need a better understanding of the neurobiological mechanisms through which PA benefits children with ADHD.

The brain’s subcortical grey matter system is a promising target for understanding the neurostructural mechanisms that underlie the benefits of PA for children with ADHD. In support of this, research shows that PA can induce neuroplastic changes to subcortical grey matter networks that have been associated with ADHD related behaviours.^2-3 These include the cerebellar cortices, the hippocampus, and the amygdala.

However, studies are yet to measure grey matter structural properties and symptom changes following PA in the same group of children with ADHD. Thus, if PA induced grey matter changes underlie the benefits of PA for children with ADHD remains unknown. The aim of this study was to examine if neuroplastic grey matter changes following PA were related to changes in ADHD related behaviours in children with and without ADHD.

Methods

This study reports on Quiet Minds for Cardio Kids, a waitlist-controlled clinical trial (ACTRN12624000517572). A group of 28 children with and without ADHD aged 8 to 14 years (mean age = 10.07 years, 9 females, 18 children with a clinical diagnosis of ADHD) were randomised to a treatment (n = 17) or a waitlist (n = 11) group. Participants completed a nine-week PA program and up to three assessments (Figure 1).

Each assessment included self-report surveys, parent questionnaires, behavioural tasks, a cognitive assessment, and a neuroimaging scan. Participants were assessed against the DSM-5 criteria for ADHD using a clinical interview (Young DIVA-5) and the Conners 4 ADHD Rating Scale (Parent form). Since ADHD symptoms are often considered to exist on a continuum, ADHD was conceptualised dimensionally in this study.

The treatment group took part in a PA program after their first assessment, returned for a second assessment at 10 weeks (Assessment 2), and a third assessment at 20 weeks (Assessment 3). The waitlist group waited 10 weeks before their second assessment and then followed the same intervention and assessment schedule as the treatment group.

ADHD related behaviours were measured at each assessment using T-scores from the Conners 4 content scales.^4 These included inattention/executive dysfunction, hyperactivity, impulsivity, and emotional dysregulation. PA was measured during and outside the intervention using actigraphy accelerometer and self-report measures.

MR scanning was conducted at each assessment using a Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma 3T scanner. High resolution T1-weighted multi-echo MPRAGE images were acquired in the sagittal plane, using the following parameters: TR = 1900 ms, TI = 900 ms, TE = 2.49 ms, flip angle = 9°, voxel size = 0.9 mm3, FoV = 240 mm, 192 contiguous slices with an acquisition time of 4:26 min.

Images were processed in FreeSurfer (version 8.0). Processing steps included skull stripping, bias field correction, grey-white matter segmentation and cortical surface reconstruction. The cerebellar cortices, the hippocampus and the amygdala were segmented from the Aseg atlas and volumetric measures were derived using the standard FreeSurfer processing pipeline. Quality control measures included both visual inspection and automated methods (MRIQC).

The PA intervention was an adapted version of an existing tennis-based PA program that combines aerobic exercises, music, and socially inclusive PAs. The program was adapted for children with ADHD to include fun and engaging cardio-based (e.g., running, skipping) and cognitively engaging (e.g., tennis) activities. Outside of the intervention, participants continued their usual PAs.

Children took part in the group-based intervention through a one-week intensive block via a school holiday program (4 x 45min) and then twice per week (2 x 45min) for eight weeks during the school term. Each session consisted of a dynamic warm-up, tennis-based activities (skill-based exercises), and non-tennis activities (cardio-based exercises). The sessions were accompanied by music from a standardised track-list.

Data were analysed in R (version 4.4.2) using the lme4 package. To examine changes in ADHD related behaviours and grey matter structural properties, we adopted linear mixed models. Data were collapsed across the intervention and waitlist groups, resulting in four timepoints for the assessments (baseline, pre-intervention, post-intervention, follow-up).

Timepoint was modelled as a fixed effect. Models were conducted with a random intercept to account for repeated observations per participant. Differences between timepoints were modelled using planned contrasts. Contrasts were defined as pre-intervention - baseline (Baseline), post-intervention - pre-intervention (Intervention), and follow-up - post-intervention (Retention).

Results

Analysis of ADHD related behaviours (n = 28, 71 observations) suggested that scores on hyperactivity (95% CI [-7.62, -0.14], p = .044, d = 0.39), impulsivity (95% CI [-8.72, -1.53], p = .007, d = 0.54), and emotional dysregulation (95% CI [-8.50, -1.81], p = .004, d = 0.58), were significantly lower after the intervention than before the intervention (Figure 2). No significant change (except for a small increase in inattentive behaviours during the baseline period) was observed for the baseline and retention periods.

Structural neuroimaging data (n = 28, 65 scans) suggested that subcortical volumes in the right hippocampus (95% CI [21,62, 94.70], p = .003, d = 0.60), and in the right amygdala (95% CI [4.65, 79.76], p = .030, d = 0.43) were significantly greater after the intervention compared to before the intervention (Figure 2). No significant change was observed for the baseline and retention periods. No significant change in cortical volume was observed in the cerebellar cortices following the intervention.

Conclusion

Results suggest that the PA program in this study may be effective at reducing ADHD related behaviours. The observed changes in hyperactivity, impulsivity and executive dysfunction appear to parallel changes in cortical volume of the right hippocampus and the right amygdala. One possible interpretation is that volumetric properties of the right hippocampus and the right amygdala represent neurostructural mechanisms through which PA benefits children with and without ADHD.

This finding is of potential clinical relevance as it provides complementary clinical and neuroimaging evidence that can assist in identifying neurobiological markers for objectively evaluating PA interventions. In doings so, research from this ongoing clinical trial may contribute to the development and validation of targeted and effective PA programs for children with ADHD. This is of direct benefit to families who seek effective, accessible, and affordable treatment for ADHD.
