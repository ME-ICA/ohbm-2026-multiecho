Introduction

Diffusion MRI (dMRI)-based tractometry is an essential method for evaluating the age dependency of white matter tissue properties in living human brains1. While diffusion tensor imaging (DTI)-based metrics, such as fractional anisotropy (FA), have been widely used in previous studies, their sensitivity may be compromised because DTI utilizes a simpler model, only assuming Gaussian diffusion, and does not fully represent different populations of water molecules inside biological tissues. In contrast, diffusion kurtosis imaging (DKI) has been proposed to model the non-Gaussian component of the diffusion signal2. DKI provides alternative diffusion metrics for measuring white matter tissue properties, such as kurtosis FA (KFA). Here, we aim to perform an empirical study to evaluate how DTI and DKI differ in their sensitivity for identifying age-related changes in white matter tissue properties by comparing data acquired from different age groups. 

Methods

We acquired dMRI data from 162 healthy participants, divided into an adolescent group (n=98, age=12±2) and a middle-aged/elderly group (n=64, age=57±10). Data were acquired with a multi-band EPI sequence using a Siemens 3T PrismaFit scanner using a multi-shell protocol (6 b = 0 s/mm2 images; b = 1000 and 2000 s/mm2, 50 directions per b-value; 2 mm isotropic voxels) to enable robust fitting for both DTI and DKI. dMRI data were preprocessed using FSL to correct for susceptibility-induced distortion, head motion, and eddy-current distortion. We then performed constrained spherical deconvolution-based probabilistic tractography to generate streamlines in the white matter of the whole brain. We employed pyAFQ3 to automatically segment 28 major white matter bundles and extract their tract profiles, which are summaries of either FA or KFA at 100 nodes along each tract. Firstly, we performed an exploratory analysis to compare the tract profiles of FA and KFA across age groups, to assess how each metric differs in sensitivity to age-related white matter tissue differences in each tract. We then performed statistical analysis by calculating the effect size of differences between the adolescents and middle-aged/elderly groups for each metric and tract. Finally, we computed the dissociation index (DI), a normalized difference between the effect sizes of FA and KFA, to identify group differences. We calculated DI in all nodes along white matter tracts to evaluate differences between DTI and DKI. 

Results

Figure 1 depicts the profile of FA (left panel) and KFA (right panel) along the superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF). We found that FA significantly fluctuated across nodes from anterior to posterior, while KFA exhibited relatively similar values along the tract in both age groups. As a result, KFA exhibited significant differences between age groups in a larger number of nodes continuously compared with FA, suggesting that KFA is more sensitive to age-related white matter tissue differences in specific portions of the SLF, most likely regions where the SLF crosses with other tracts and DTI loses its sensitivity to capture biological properties. 


In contrast, based on DI analysis, we found that FA exhibits higher sensitivity for identifying age-related tissue differences along the uncinate fasciculus, unlike the pattern observed in the SLF. Compared with SLF and uncinate, the vertical occipital fasciculus (VOF) exhibited generally lower age-group differences in both metrics, suggesting that age dependency varies across tracts. 

Conclusion

We performed an exploratory analysis to compare the utility of DTI and DKI for tractometry analysis for detecting age dependency. We found that DKI has superior sensitivity along the SLF, most likely because its sensitivity to non-Gaussian diffusion may help identify biological information that DTI may fail to identify. However, at present, we also found that the advantage of DKI is not uniformly observed across all white matter tracts, suggesting that the optimal model for identifying age dependency may be different based on the underlying anatomical characteristics of white matter tracts.
