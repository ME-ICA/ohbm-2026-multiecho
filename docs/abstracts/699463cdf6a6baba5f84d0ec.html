Introduction

Psychiatric disorders exhibit substantial clinical and neurobiological heterogeneity that is poorly captured by traditional symptom-based diagnostic frameworks. Although neuroimaging and electrophysiological approaches have sought to identify objective biomarkers, findings have often lacked robustness across cohorts and sites. A key challenge lies in the field’s predominant reliance on case-control designs and group-level averages, which obscure meaningful inter-individual variability and hinder the development of clinically actionable markers.

Normative modeling has recently emerged as a powerful alternative framework to address these challenges. Rather than contrasting group means, normative models estimate population-level trajectories of brain measures as a function of relevant covariates (e.g., age, sex), and quantify individual-level deviations from these norms1. This approach enables subject-specific inference, offering a principled way to characterize neurobiological heterogeneity across psychiatric populations. Importantly, deviation profiles derived from normative models can be leveraged to move beyond diagnosis-centric analyses, enabling the identification of transdiagnostic patterns and biologically informed subtypes.

Here, we present a comprehensive application of normative modeling to large-scale developmental EEG data, illustrating its value across multiple analytic levels. We first use normative deviation scores to quantify and map heterogeneity across transdiagnostic psychiatric cohorts, revealing highly individualized and spatially distributed patterns of abnormality. We then leverage multivariate deviation signatures to identify transdiagnostic neurophysiological subtypes, linking distinct deviation patterns to differential behavioral and clinical profiles. Together, this work positions normative modeling as a framework for characterizing heterogeneity and deriving biologically grounded subtypes in precision psychiatry.

Methods

Datasets

We analyzed resting-state EEG recordings from children and adolescents aged 5–18 years, aggregated across multiple independent datasets. These included publicly available datasets as well as datasets obtained through established research collaborations. All contributing studies received approval from their respective institutional review boards or ethics committees, and the associated ethical documentation is reported in the original publications for each dataset. Additional methodological information is available in2.

The full cohort consisted of 1,701 participants, including a reference healthy control (HC) group and a transdiagnostic clinical cohort diagnosed with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), autism spectrum disorder (ASD), anxiety disorders (ANX), and learning disorders (LD). The clinical sample included 1,143 patients, many of whom presented comorbidities involving two or more of these conditions.

EEG Preprocessing and Feature Extraction

Resting-state EEG data underwent standard preprocessing procedures for artifact rejection as described in2.

For large-scale feature extraction and clustering analyses, EEG data were downsampled to the standard 10–20 montage comprising 19 channels. A comprehensive electrophysiological feature set was extracted, spanning spectral power (including canonical frequency bands and aperiodic 1/f components), leakage-corrected functional connectivity, time-domain signal descriptors, complexity metrics, and Catch22 time-series features. In total, 1,957 features were computed for each participant (103 features per channel). A complete feature inventory is provided in3.

For connectivity analyses, high-density 128-channel EEG recordings were preserved and source reconstructed using eLORETA. Signals were then parcellated into 68 cortical regions following the Desikan–Killiany atlas, and functional connectivity was estimated using amplitude envelope correlation (AEC) with zero-lag correction to mitigate spurious leakage effects.

Normative Modeling

Normative developmental trajectories were modeled for each EEG feature (feature–channel or feature–connection combination) using Generalized Additive Models for Location, Scale, and Shape (GAMLSS). Models were trained on an 80% stratified subset of the HC cohort, enabling age-dependent estimation of the mean, variance, and higher-order distributional parameters. Following model fitting, all clinical participants as well as the remaining HC test subset were projected onto these models to obtain feature-wise deviation scores (z-scores) reflecting departures from typical neurodevelopment. Extreme deviations were defined as values exceeding |z| > 2.

Quantifying Neurobiological Heterogeneity

Neurobiological heterogeneity within each diagnostic group was characterized by assessing the proportion of individuals who exhibited at least one extreme deviation across spectral or connectivity features. To examine the consistency of deviation patterns across participants, spatial overlap maps were generated by aggregating extreme deviations at the channel or source level within each diagnostic group.

Deviation-Based Subtyping

To identify transdiagnostic electrophysiological subtypes, multivariate deviation-score matrices derived from the full feature set were integrated using Similarity Network Fusion (SNF), and the resulting fused network was partitioned using spectral clustering. Subtypes were characterized in terms of their diagnostic composition and dominant electrophysiological deviation patterns. Behavioral and clinical relevance was evaluated by comparing cognitive and symptom measures across the identified subtypes. Finally, within each subtype, multiple linear regression analyses were performed to examine relationships between electrophysiological deviations and key behavioral domains.

Results

Normative modeling quantifies electrophysiological heterogeneity

Across spectral features, the proportion of individuals exhibiting at least one negative extreme deviation was modest. The highest rates were observed in the theta band for ASD (44%), the alpha band for ADHD (29%), LD (25%), and ANX (23%), and the beta band for HC(test) (35%). Among individuals with at least one deviation, spatial overlap of deviating channels was consistently low, never exceeding 40% across groups and frequency bands (e.g., delta: ADHD = 31%, ANX = 32%; beta: ASD = 28%, HC(test) = 31%; gamma: LD = 40%). Similar patterns were observed for positive deviations, indicating substantial inter-individual variability in the spatial distribution of spectral abnormalities.

In contrast, functional connectivity features showed a markedly higher proportion of affected individuals. For negative deviations, prevalence reached up to 87% (delta: HC(test) = 87%, ASD = 71%; theta: ADHD = 82%, ANX = 83%, LD = 82%). However, within-group spatial overlap of deviant connections remained extremely low, not exceeding 14% across bands (e.g., alpha: HC(test) = 14%, ASD = 9%, ADHD = 10%, LD = 10%; gamma: ANX = 12%). Positive connectivity deviations were slightly less prevalent (maximum 73% for ASD in the beta band), with modestly higher overlap (e.g., alpha: HC(test) = 23%; beta: LD = 24%; gamma: ASD = 21%), though spatial consistency remained limited.

Normative deviation-based clustering reveals transdiagnostic electrophysiological subtypes

Clustering of individual EEG deviation profiles identified three transdiagnostic neurophysiological subtypes: C1 (n = 581, 46.3%), C2 (n = 400, 31.9%), and C3 (n = 273, 21.8%). Clusters differed significantly in diagnostic composition (χ² = 180.67, p < 0.0001) but showed no associations with demographic variables. Cluster C1 was enriched for ANX (14.3%) and LD (13.9%), as well as ADHD comorbidities (ADHD+ANX: 13.4%; ADHD+LD: 12.9%). Cluster C2 was dominated by HC (22.8%) and included a substantial proportion of ASD (15.7%). Cluster C3 was largely composed of ADHD and related comorbidities (ADHD+ANX: 14.7%; ASD+ADHD: 13.6%; ADHD+LD: 12.8%; ADHD: 12.5%), alongside ASD (13.9%), and notably contained no HC individuals.

Identified subtypes show distinct electrophysiological profiles

The three subtypes exhibited distinct multivariate deviation patterns. Clusters C1 and C3 showed larger-magnitude and often oppositely directed deviations, whereas C2 was characterized by distributions closer to normative values.,Interestingly, the directionality of deviations in C2 generally mirrors that observed in C3. Identified subtypes show distinct behavioral profiles

Behavioral profiles differed significantly across clusters in 10 of 13 assessed domains (Kruskal–Wallis, p < 0.05). Cluster C1, comprising a higher proportion of individuals with anxiety and learning disorders, was marked by pronounced deficits in executive function and elevated emotional reactivity. Cluster C3, which included a substantial number of individuals with ADHD and common comorbidities, exhibited notably higher scores in hyperactivity-related domains. In contrast, Cluster C2, predominantly composed of healthy controls, showed the lowest composite z-scores across multiple domains, including attention, depression, internalization, anxiety, and social functioning, reflecting a broadly normative behavioral profile. (Fig. 2c 2d) 

Deviation profiles predict cluster-specific behavioral variation

Within each subtype, EEG deviation scores significantly predicted behavioral variability (multiple linear regression, all p < 0.05). Strong associations were observed for emotional dysregulation (r = 0.53) and internalization (r = 0.51) in C1; attention (r = 0.75) and internalization (r = 0.62) in C2; and hyperactivity (r = 0.75) and social functioning (r = 0.75) in C3, supporting a direct link between subtype-specific neurophysiological deviations and clinical phenotypes. Conclusion

Overall, our findings demonstrate that normative modeling offers a powerful and scalable framework for characterizing the profound electrophysiological heterogeneity that underlies neurodevelopmental and psychiatric conditions. By shifting the focus from group-level averages to individualized deviation profiles, this approach uncovers highly personalized patterns of spectral and connectivity abnormalities that remain hidden within traditional diagnostic categories. Furthermore, the multivariate deviation signatures reveal robust transdiagnostic neurophysiological subtypes that map onto distinct behavioral and clinical dimensions, illustrating how normative models can organize heterogeneity into biologically meaningful structure.

Importantly, this work serves as a proof of concept, showcasing the potential of EEG-based normative modeling when applied at scale. While further methodological refinement and longitudinal validation are needed, these results highlight how deviation-based analyses can form the foundation for future precision psychiatry.
