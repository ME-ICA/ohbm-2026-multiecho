Introduction

The relationship between the individual sulcal morphology and functional activity has been underexplored in fMRI studies. The oculomotor system is an excellent candidate to study this relationship systematically because of the multiple eye fields localized along the branches of the precentral sulcus (PCS; ^1). Previous fMRI studies found consistent activations for saccades along the superior precentral sulcus (sPCS) near its junction with the superior frontal sulcus (SFS), corresponding to the frontal eye field (Amiez and Petrides, 2018; ^2). An earlier fMRI study investigated the localization of active voxels during saccades and smooth pursuit relative to the fundus of the sPCS and medial PCS (mPCS), finding that saccade-related activity lies in their anterior banks of these sulci across subjects (^3). More recently, population receptive field (pRF) modeling uncovered several maps corresponding to putative precentral eye fields (PEF; Mackey et al. 2017). Despite the interesting qualitative observation that the foveal representation lies at the junction of the sPCS and SFS (Mackey et al. 2017), a more systematic quantitative link based on individual sulcal morphology is lacking. Here, we analyzed a comprehensive MRI dataset focused on retinotopic and visuomotor functional mapping (Lascombes et al. 2025, VSS) to investigate the depth-eccentricity relationship within the PEF. 

Methods

We collected MRI data using a 3T Siemens Prisma in 20 healthy participants (age 19-54 years, 10 females, 2 authors). We collected fMRI data for retinotopy, saccade, and smooth pursuit tasks (see Figure 1, panel A, for details). We carried out MRI data preprocessing using fMRIPrep 22.1.1 (Esteban et al., 2019) based on Nipype 1.8.5 (Gorgolewski et al., 2011; Figure 1, panel B). We reconstructed the brain surfaces (Dale et al., 1999) and we imported them into BrainVISA (Geffroy et al. 2011) to run the Morphologist pipeline, which automatically segmented the sulci of interest using the CNN method (^4) and computed their surface area. Aided by these segmentations, we classified PCS types based on Germann et al. (2005). We also computed the depth potential function (^5) on the native white surface using α = 0.03 to obtain depth maps (normalized by the maximum value) for each participant. For fMRI, the BOLD reference was co-registered to the T1w reference using boundary-based registration (Greve & Fischl, 2009) with 12 degrees of freedom. The BOLD timeseries were resampled onto fsnative surfaces. We performed a leave-one-out averaging of the 5 run signal timeseries, and we fitted a pRF model for each combination at every vertex within regions of interest based on the multimodal parcellation (MMP; Glasser et al. 2016). Finally, we created a conjunction map including significantly active vertices in all of our retinotopy, and the saccade and smooth pursuit GLM results. We then extracted the depth values for each corresponding active vertex in the conjunction map and split them by MMP1 cluster grouping regions in the medial PCS, superior PCS, and inferior PCS. We applied two models: a linear regression model and a model where eccentricity was weighted by the pRF model fit (leave-one-out R²) to test its relationship to depth.

Results

We segmented the 5 branches of the PCS in 20 participants and extracted their surface area and mean depth (Figure 2). Across hemispheres (40), 80% of the participants exhibited a continuous sPCS, and the remaining (20%) a discontinuous one. Considering the iPCS, 82.5% participants exhibited a long dorsal branch (corresponding to intermediate PCS) extending into the middle frontal gyrus for half of its length, while 17.5% had a relatively shorter branch. Finally, in the mPCS, 20% of participants lacked a clearly developed medial or marginal PCS. Next, we examined the relationship between cortical depth as estimated by the depth potential function and pRF eccentricity. Neither linear nor reliability-weighted linear regressions revealed a meaningful association at the subject level, as effect sizes across the MMP1 clusters of interest were negligible (|r| < 0.1).

Conclusion

These results suggest that gross sulcal morphology, and more specifically cortical depth, shows only a weak association with eccentricity preference in the PCS, even when accounting for pRF reliability, standing somewhat in contrast with previous qualitative observations. Our study highlights the importance of considering neuroanatomical variability as a necessary prerequisite for characterizing structure-function relationships within the precentral eye fields.
