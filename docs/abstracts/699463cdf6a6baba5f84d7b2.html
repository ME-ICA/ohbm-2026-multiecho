Introduction

T1-weighted (T1w) and FLuid-Attenuated Inverse Recovery (FLAIR) MRI sequences are essential for assessing brain anatomy, diagnosing neurodegenerative diseases, and monitoring volume or lesion changes over time. Their diagnostic value, however, depends on image quality, which can be compromised by low signal-to-noise ratio, poor contrast, or motion artefacts.

Quality control (QC) in research datasets, such as the UK Biobank1, the European Prevention of Alzheimer’s Dementia2 and the Italian Neuroimaging Network Initiative3, has been well-studied using image quality metrics often obtained with tools like MRIQC4. These studies benefit from standardised protocols, high-resolution imaging, preselected patients, and QC at acquisition time, resulting in high-quality, homogeneous data. Clinical research datasets5, while more heterogeneous than pure research cohorts, still operate within a framework that limits variability in scanner models, acquisition sites, and patient characteristics.

However, the translation of neuroimaging methods into routine clinical practice requires validation on highly heterogeneous data, characterised by great variability in scanner models and acquisition site specificities, variable acquisition protocols, and a broad spectrum of patient-specific factors. Robust and generalisable quality assessment of clinical routine cohorts is paramount to the development of such methods, as the great diversity of the data comes with specific challenges.

This work presents preliminary findings from an exploration of the quality of routine T1w and FLAIR brain MRI scans, sourced from a clinical data warehouse encompassing 39 hospitals in the Parisian area. Our study aims to describe the quality of these scans and propose an analysis of variability sources, highlighting the unique challenges and features of clinical routine data.

Methods

Previous work from our team provided manual quality control (QC) labels, derived from the annotation of 5,500 3D T1w6 and over 1,000 3D FLAIR7 brain MRI scans by two independent annotators. Scans were assessed for the presence and severity of three common artefacts: motion, noise, and poor grey-white matter contrast. Artefacts were classified as moderate if brain structures (particularly white vs. grey matter) remained distinguishable, and severe if structural differentiation was significantly impaired. Scans were labelled as ‘straight rejects’ if slices were missing, the anatomy was incorrect, the sequence was mislabelled, or if non-scan images (e.g., segmentation maps) were provided. For T1w scans, the injection of gadolinium as a contrast agent was also recorded. Consensus between annotators was established for all labels. The label distributions are displayed in Figure 1.

The present work studies the applicability and limitations of metrics-based QC approaches for the analysis of the quality of clinical routine scans. We explored sources of heterogeneity, spanning from the acquisition context (scanner models and manufacturers, acquisition parameters) to the patient specificities (age, sex, specific pathologies). A central objective was to study the correlation between visual QC scores, assessed by experts, and quantitative metrics computed from the clinical scans. 

Established quantitative metrics were selected for the analysis. The Signal-to-Noise ratio (SNR) is a classic method for the quantification of noise in a scan. Its computation relied on the image background and the grey matter, localised thanks to segmentation maps, outputs of the Synthseg algorithm8. The Normalised Difference between White and Grey Matter (ND-WGM) was used to characterise the contrast between white and grey matter, especially important with anatomical sequences used for volumetry or lesion detection. We also relied on the Average Edge Strength (AES) and Tenengrad measure (TG), reference-free metrics that provide promising results in terms of correlation with radiological assessment of image quality9.

The values and ranges of these metrics are analysed with regards to the manual annotation labels as well as the different patient-related and acquisition variables, such as the scanner model, the age or the sex of the patients.

Results

Significant differences between the T1w and FLAIR cohorts are highlighted by the quantitative analysis. Overall, the T1w cohort is of poorer quality, with a higher prevalence of severe artefacts, compared to the FLAIR cohort. This may be due to the more recent introduction of the 3D FLAIR in the Parisian hospitals (in the years 2010) whereas the T1w cohort includes images dating further back, hence acquired on older generations of scanners. 

The analysis with regards to patient demographics shows no significant difference in the distribution of the FLAIR images between men and women. Age however is a more significant factor, as there is a notable drop in visual quality with increasing age, especially for patients over 60.  80% of scans for patients under 60 are of good quality, but only about 40% for those over 60.  This drop is especially visible in terms of the number of scans with poor contrast. There is also a decrease in the absolute number of scans for patients over 65 years, impacting the number of scans of good quality. This phenomenon is unclear to us, further investigations would be needed to understand if this is due to the clinical practice or to other factors, such as the choice of focusing on 3D sequences which require longer acquisition times and hence may be less used in older patients for various reasons. 

Some limitations of the metrics-based approach can also be observed. Whereas for the T1w cohort, distributions of SNR for different classes of overall image quality (good, moderate, poor) yielded notable differences, these results cannot be replicated on the FLAIR cohort, which is mostly made up of images of good and moderate quality. SNR distributions are very similar (Figure 2, top), with similar statistical descriptors (mean, std). Furthermore, a more in-depth analysis of the SNR distributions stratified by scanner model shows discrepancy in values and observed tendencies. Surprising results are also observed and may require additional attention, such as the SNR increasing with the noise level, for the Philips INGENIA scanner model.

For the study of contrast classes (Figure 2, bottom), ND-WGM values allow for a better distinction of the classes, however values vary between scanner models. 

Our FLAIR cohort presented a limited number of images with motion, hence limiting the statistical power of the analysis of AES and Tenengrad measures. Preliminary results indicate variations in values between scanner models as for the aforementioned metrics, with some promising results for the scanner model from which originate most of the scans with motion. A more thorough analysis using the T1w cohort may provide more insight into the potential of these metrics.

All these results highlight the impact of the data heterogeneity on the use of metrics in the context of clinical routine data. Beyond the scan itself, it is essential to consider the impact of the acquisition setting and the patient specificities. Harmonisation methods may contribute to mitigating these effects by producing more homogeneous contrast profiles between scanner models.

Future work will carry a more in-depth analysis of the T1w cohort, which is more heterogeneous and contains a larger volume of images with moderate and severe artefacts. This would also allow for an improved comparison to our FLAIR cohort. Analysis of unlabelled FLAIR and T1w data would also prove invaluable, relying on classification results from previously developed methods, such as described in Loizillon et al.7 A focus on different pathology groups may also highlight variability inherent to clinical routine brain MRIs. 

Conclusion

Quality control tools for clinical data are critical to understand the specificities of the clinical routine practice, but also for the development and assessment of robust computed-based methods, from segmentation to computer-aided diagnosis. This study provides a preliminary exploration of the quality of routine T1w and FLAIR brain MRI scans from a large clinical data warehouse, relying on manual annotation of over 1,000 FLAIR and 5,500 brain T1w MRI scans. Our work showcases significant differences in image quality between T1w and FLAIR sequences, with T1w scans presenting more frequent and severe artefacts, which may be due to the sequence having been in use for longer in the Parisian hospitals. Age appeared to be a significant factor of image quality with a decrease in quality, especially in terms of poor contrast in patients over 60. No significant sex-based differences were observed.

The analysis also underscores the challenges faced by traditional metrics-based quality control, due to the clinical setting. Though there is some potential with metrics such as signal-to-noise ratio, normalised difference of white and grey matter intensities, and reference-free motion metrics such as the average edge strength and Tenengrad measures, significant discrepancies have been observed, with the scanner models being great sources of variability in the ranges and values of said metrics. These findings highlight the need for tools, from quality control to site harmonisation methods, that can mitigate and account for the inherent variability of clinical routine data. 

Additional work is needed to further explore the T1w cohort of 5,500 manually annotated scans, which presents more heterogeneity than our FLAIR cohort. Exploration of the unlabelled data may bring additional insights, relying on quality predictions from methods such as presented in Loizillon et al7. Pathology-specific analyses may also provide valuable knowledge for the development of robust and generalisable QC tools. Addressing these challenges would help gain a better understanding of the specificities of large clinical cohorts and identify pain points that need to be tackled in order to bridge the gap between research and clinical practice through the development of robust methods suited to more complex clinical settings.
