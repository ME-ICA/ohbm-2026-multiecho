Introduction

The claustrum is a bilateral sheet of grey matter concealed laterally by the insula and medially by the putamen. Despite its thin anatomy, it stands out by its highly networked nature with reciprocal connections to virtually all cortical and multiple subcortical regions, suggesting its involvement in higher-level cognition. However, since its enclosed location and structural fineness pose challenges for detailed investigation, its function remains largely unknown.
A well-established aspect of the claustrum’s physiology speaks for its involvement in perceptual processing. Several electrophysiological studies in animals demonstrated unimodal visual, auditory, and somatosensory zones within the nucleus. With respect to the visual domain, previous research in felines and macaques also suggests topographically organized connections between the visual claustrum zone and the visual cortices.1,2 In a previous 7T fMRI study, we could identify a homologue of such a visual claustrum zone in a distinct ventral part of the human claustrum.3 Revealing the precise functional properties of this specialized sensory zone in humans will advance our understanding of the claustrum’s contribution to visual cognition.
In the present study we explored the topographic characteristics of the human visual claustrum zone using population receptive field (pRF) mapping for fMRI on the 7T retinotopy dataset of the Young Adult Human Connectome Project.4 We provide detailed comparisons with established pRF properties of the lateral geniculate nucleus, the primary visual cortex, as well as two sets of higher-level cortical topographic maps along the dorsal (intraparietal sulcus) and the ventral (ventral occipital) streams, and find both similarities with other visual areas as well as unique claustrum characteristics.

Methods

The HCP dataset includes the retinotopy data for 181 subjects (age range = 22-35, 109 female).4 Standard apertures for the visual pRF mapping stimuli were used, including wedges, rings, and bars. Apertures uncovered colourful changing object textures updated at a rate of 15 Hz within a circular area of 16° visual angle in diameter (Figure 1). A fixation grid and a central attention task requiring responses to a colour-changing dot were used to support gaze stability. Acquisition and preprocessing of MRI and fMRI data were performed by the HCP research team.4 Structural data were obtained at 3T with 0.7mm and functional at 7T with 1.6mm isotropic resolution. Structural preprocessing included surface reconstruction, and nonlinear surface- and volume-based registration to standard MNI spaces. Functional preprocessing involved corrections for head motion and distortion, nonlinear registration of cortical data, denoising, and nonlinear registration of volumetric data.
The visual claustrum ROI was derived from a previous high-resolution 7T fMRI study.3 Other ROIs were defined by means of commonly used MRI atlases. For pRF mapping we used the recently published high-performance GPU empowered (GEM) approach.5 We estimated the aggregated receptive field of the population of neurons contained within each voxel using a 2-D Gaussian model defined by visual-field position and pRF size. Best-fit pRF parameters were determined by comparing the generated model time courses with the obtained fMRI signal changes and minimizing the residual sum-of-squares. A two-stage coarse-to-fine fitting procedure was used. Since the goodness-of-fit for pRF models is expected to decrease in higher-level visual areas and in subcortical structures, the reported results are based on a variance explained threshold of 1.5% for all ROIs. Resulting pRF parameters include x- and y-position, used to derive eccentricity and polar angle metrics, and pRF size.
For the characterization of the topography of the human visual claustrum, we quantified several major visual field representation properties based on the pRF parameters and performed parametric statistical inference where appropriate. Lateralisation of the visual field representation was examined based on the pRF x-position and the laterality index quantified as the difference of ipsi- and contralaterally responsive voxels relative to their combined total. To explore the representation of meridians, we quantified the extent of horizontal and vertical meridian coverage in the interval [-1.5°, 1.5°] and calculated the meridian bias, defined as the difference in voxel count between the horizontal and the vertical meridian coverage. The increase of pRF size with eccentricity, typical for visual areas, was analysed by performing a linear fit to the eccentricity and pRF size data for each subject. Given the preference for peripheral stimulation reported for the feline visual claustrum,1 we also quantified the proportions of voxels with central and peripheral field representations. We examined the fractional volume as a function of eccentricity for each hemisphere and calculated the eccentricity value at the 50% fractional volume threshold, and also computed the average eccentricity value of both hemispheres per subject. Finally, to determine the relation between topography and anatomy, we computed linear fits for eccentricity and polar angle as a function of the three MNI space coordinates (x = left-right; y = posterior-anterior; z = inferior-superior) for each subject.
We used paired t-tests to test for differences in examined parameters within each inspected region. For comparisons between the claustrum and each of the other ROIs we used paired t-tests with Bonferroni correction to account for multiple comparisons. To determine the significance of relations between parameters we tested for nonzero slopes of the assessed linear fits using one-sample t-tests.

Results

Paired t-tests comparing the average pRF x-position across voxels between hemispheres for each ROI confirmed that the visual claustrum zone exhibits a representational bias towards the contralateral visual field, as did the other visual areas examined (claustrum: t(179) = 4.67; p <  .001; d = 0.35; all other areas: t(180) ≥ 37.05, p < .001, d ≥ 2.75; Figure 2A). Correspondingly, laterality indices were negative for all ROIs, indicating a predominant representation of the contralateral hemifields. However, the laterality index was significantly lower for the visual claustrum than for each of the other ROIs, suggesting a weaker lateralisation of visual responses (for all Bonferroni corrected paired t-tests: t(180) ≥ 17.65, pcorr < .001, d ≥ 1.82; Figure 2B).
The claustrum showed a homogenous distribution of polar angles across visual space. Paired t-tests indicated no significant difference between the extent of vertical and horizontal meridian coverage for the claustrum, while the other areas exhibited an overrepresentation of the horizontal meridian and an underrepresentation of the vertical meridian (claustrum: t(180) = 0.37, p = .709, d = 0.03; all other areas: t(180) ≥ 5.65, p < .001, d ≥ 0.42; Figure 2C). A comparison of the meridian bias values between the claustrum and the other visual areas confirmed that the extent of horizontal and vertical meridian representations in the claustrum differs significantly less than in the other ROIs (for all Bonferroni corrected paired t-tests: t(180) ≤ -5.66, pcorr < .001, d ≤ -0.59; Figure 2D).
Similar to other examined visual regions, the visual claustrum showed a significant positive relationship between pRF size and eccentricity (t-test on the slope against zero for the claustrum: t(161) = 2.54; p = .012, d = 0.20; all other areas: t(180) ≥ 14.08, p < .001, d ≥ 1.05; Figure 2E shows the fit for an example single subject). The average slope parameter for the association between pRF size and eccentricity was significantly weaker in the claustrum compared to other ROIs (for all Bonferroni corrected paired t-tests: t(161) ≤ -4.41, pcorr < .001, d ≤ -0.46; Figure 2F).
Relative to other areas, the visual claustrum demonstrated a bias towards more peripheral visual field locations, with 50% of its volumes representing the central 3.67° of eccentricity on average. The other ROIs showed larger representations devoted to the central visual field, with 50% of their volumes representing the central 2.20°–2.81°. Statistical comparison confirmed a significantly higher average eccentricity at 50% fractional volume threshold for the visual claustrum relative to each of the other ROIs (for all Bonferroni corrected paired t-tests: t(180) ≥ 9.87, pcorr < .001, d ≥ 0.91; Figure 2G). The overrepresentation of more peripheral visual areas in the claustrum relative to the other areas was further confirmed by comparing the average eccentricity of both hemispheres for the visual claustrum with the average eccentricity for the other ROIs (for all Bonferroni corrected paired t-tests: t(180) ≥ 10.83, pcorr < .001, d ≥ 0.98; Figure 2H).
Eccentricity values increased significantly from posterior-to-anterior positions, with more anterior regions representing more peripheral visual field locations (M = 0.04, SD = 0.22, t(180) = 2.31, p = .022, d = 0.17; Figure 2I). A trend for a decrease of eccentricity from inferior-to-superior positions was observed, which however did not reach significance (M = -0.03, SD = 0.21, t(180) = -1.87, p = .063, d = -0.14; Figure 2I). We did not find a systematic eccentricity change along the left-right dimension in either the left (p = .337) or the right visual claustrum (p = .973). We did not detect any systematic change in the polar angle values along the posterior-anterior dimension (p = .190), the inferior-superior dimension (p = .117), or the left-right axis for either the left (p = .302) or the right visual claustrum (p = .246).

Conclusion

In this study we used pRF mapping on the HCP 7T retinotopy dataset to characterise the visual field representation of the human visual claustrum zone. The claustrum exhibited properties typical for a visually responsive area such as the contralateral visual field bias and an increase in pRF size with increasing eccentricity. Compared to other low- and higher-level visual areas, the visual claustrum exhibited weaker lateralisation of responses towards the contralateral hemifield and a weaker increase of receptive field sizes with increasing eccentricity. In contrast to other examined visual areas, it showed no horizontal meridian bias and a larger representation of the visual periphery.
The horizontal meridian bias is thought to stem from the distribution of photoreceptors in the retina. The fact that this bias is present in all examined visual areas but not the visual claustrum zone may emphasize its special position in the visual processing stream. Both feline and macaque studies demonstrated that the visual claustrum zone receives input from multiple cortical areas, but comparably less from subcortical structures.1,2 Crucially, neither of the subcortical visual nuclei has claustral projections. Such a projection architecture together with the absence of a horizontal meridian bias may define the visual claustrum as a non-cortical higher-level stage of information processing, which is more abstracted from the sensory input-related biases.
A larger representation of more peripheral visual field regions distinguishes the human visual claustrum zone from most visually responsive regions and was previously only shown in felines.1 The fact that this feature remained preserved in evolution up to the human species implies its ecological importance. A more homogeneous representation of all eccentricities, without emphasizing the fovea could be important for multiple functions such as optic flow processing, similar to motion area V6, or reorienting attention to salient peripheral stimuli. The latter function is consistent with the claustrum’s proposed role in saliency processing and distractor suppression.
Although the demonstrated coarsely ordered retinotopy is consistent with animal work, the layout of the topographic map inferred in our study diverges from a previous report in macaques.2 The study used tracer injections at different locations within visual area V4 to map the topographic organization of the claustrum, finding two coarse topographic maps, a smaller and less consistently found dorsal map and a larger ventral map. Within the larger ventral map, which is likely to dominate our fMRI results, the eccentricity values increase from inferior-to-superior positions, while in our data an opposite trend is observed. One potential reason for this discrepancy is that the study focused on the topography of V4 projections only. It is not clear whether different retinotopic areas consistently project to the same parts of the visual claustrum in humans and which of the projections is the primary drive of the visual responses within the claustrum. Hence, the topography of projections from V4 may be inconsistent with our findings because projections from other areas follow a different organization principle. In fact, the significant gradient of eccentricities from posterior-to-anterior, which we observed in the claustrum is consistent with the eccentricity gradient in the early visual cortex V1-V3, where values also increase from the occipital pole towards anterior positions.
Our findings demonstrate claustrum properties common to typical visual cortical and subcortical areas, and features that appear to be unique to the claustrum as a higher-level visual processing area. This study is the first to characterise the topography of the visual claustrum zone in humans, yielding important information for further investigation of the claustrum’s role in visual processing.
