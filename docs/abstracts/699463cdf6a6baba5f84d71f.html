Introduction

Experiencing socio-economic adversity in the perinatal period is one of the most robust predictors of later unfavourable outcomes, including poor academic performance and diagnosis of mental illness 1. Although reliably reproduced, the biological mechanisms of this association are unknown. Studies of adolescents have demonstrated that early life socio-economic adversity is linked with alterations in white matter microstructure quantifiable by diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) 2. Parental occupation is one of the most widely cited single socioeconomic factors conferring hazard to neurodevelopment. Understanding this mechanism, and when in the developmental pathway risk is conferred, are important first steps towards both understanding mechanisms and eventually improving public health. On this background we present an analysis of the impact of parental occupation on neonatal white matter development.  

Methods

This study is based on a sample of infants scanned soon after birth from the developing Human Connectome Project (dHCP). Individuals were included based on the following criteria: gestational age at birth >37 weeks, completed diffusion MRI (dMRI) scan capture sequence, no visually detectable abnormalities on scan, complete set of socioeconomic variables (mother’s job, father’s job, Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD), Area Social Housing %, Area Population Density, Cognitively Stimulating Parenting Scale (collected at 18 months post birth), Maternal Education, Paternal Education, Maternal Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS). MRI data was registered to a white matter skeleton using DTITK and FSL as previously described 3. The relationship between parental occupation and white matter development was assessed via tract based spatial statistics (TBSS), initially with gestational age at birth (GA at birth), post menstrual age at scan (PMA at scan) and sex as covariates. To understand how other socio-economic factors influence the relationship between parental occupation neonatal and white matter microstructure, we subsequently ran models including inter household and intra household socioeconomic factors also included as covariates.

Results

237 infants were included in the final sample. Mean gestational age at birth was gestational age at birth was 40.0 weeks, and mean post menstrual age at scan was 41.3 weeks.

There was no voxelwise relationship between mother’s job and any measure of neonatal white matter microstructure. Widespread voxelwise associations were observed with father’s job however, with better employment correlating to lower mean diffusivity (MD) and axial diffusivity (AD), thought to represent more mature brain development). This relationship was unaffected (and even strengthened in the first stepped model) by the subsequent addition of area deprivation measures, and household deprivation measures including parental education as covariates. The corticospinal tracts bilaterally were the most involved anatomical region (Figure 1).

Conclusion

We find multiple univariable associations between paternal, but not maternal occupation and neonatal white matter microstructure.  This finding is distinct from others previously reported in later life as at the time of scan acquisition participants had an average of 9 days of postnatal experience. This relationship remained present even when other socioeconomic variables were introduced to the model. The exact mechanism underlying this remains elusive, but given the short duration of postnatal life at the time of data acquisition it likely does not relate to the commonly proposed hypothesis of better access to resources in childhood. These findings are of interest in both research and public health domains, and further work should focus on understanding the mechanistic pathways underlying them.
