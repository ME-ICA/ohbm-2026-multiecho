Introduction

Psychiatric diagnosis has long relied on symptom-based classification systems such as the Diagnostic and Statistical manual of Mental Disorders (DSM). While clinically useful, these frameworks often fail to capture the underlying neurobiological mechanisms of mental disorders1. High comorbidity and pronounced phenotypic heterogeneity challenge efforts to identify biologically grounded markers, limiting progress towards precision psychiatry. These limitations have driven a shift toward neurophysiological measures, such as electroencephalography (EEG) that provide objective, scalable, and biologically proximal indices of brain function, with greater potential to capture individual variability and support precision psychiatry. However, most prior EEG biomarker studies in psychiatric conditions, including Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) have relied on traditional case-control designs. By focusing on group-averaged differences, these approaches implicitly assume diagnostic homogeneity and overlook substantial inter-individual variability, contributing to inconsistent findings and limited clinical translation. Normative modeling offers an alternative framework by estimating population-level reference trajectories and quantifying individual deviations from these norms as a function of relevant covariates, such as age and sex2. This approach enables subject-level inference and has successfully revealed biologically meaningful heterogeneity in neuroimaging studies, yet remains underexplored in EEG research. Here, we apply normative modeling to large-scale, multisite resting-state EEG data to characterize individualized neurodevelopmental deviations in ADHD. By clustering deviation profiles, we aim to identify electrophysiologically distinct subtypes and evaluate their clinical and cognitive relevance, moving beyond case–control comparisons toward a more individualized and biologically informed understanding of ADHD heterogeneity.

Methods

We analyzed a large multi-site resting-state EEG dataset comprising 1,779 participants aged 5-18 years (64.1% female), including 1,221 individuals diagnosed with ADHD and 558 healthy controls (HC). The EEG data were aggregated from multiple sources, including publicly available datasets and datasets shared through established research collaborations. All contributing studies obtained approval from their respective local ethics committees or institutional review boards which are reported in the original publications associated with each dataset. More information can be found in3. Artifact-cleaned recordings of 19 channels were used for feature extraction. From each channel, we extracted 103 quantitative features spanning time domain, spectral domain, and signal complexity, yielding 1,957 features per participant. To address the high dimensionality and redundancy of the feature space, we applied Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to standardized features, and retained 24 principal components (PCs) that explained 67% of total variance. The HC group was split into training (80%) and test (20%) sets, stratified by age, sex and site, resulting in 446 HC in the training set and 112 HC in the test set. The ADHD group was included entirely in the test set. Using the HC training set, we estimated normative developmental trajectories for each PC as a function of age, sex, and acquisition site using Generalized Additive Models for Location, Scale, and Shape (GAMLSS). For each test participant (HC and ADHD), we computed subject-specific deviation scores (z-scores) reflecting the degree to which their PC values deviated from normative expectation. Deviation profiles were then subjected to unsupervised spectral clustering to identify electrophysiological subtypes. The resulting clusters were inspected and characterized based on their diagnostic composition, ADHD subtype profiles (clinical ADHD diagnostic subtypes), clinical and behavioral symptom scores, and patterns of electrophysiological deviations.

Results

Normative modeling produced age-related trajectories that fit the healthy control (HC) training data well, enabling computation of deviation scores for all test participants. Spectral clustering applied to these deviation profiles yielded three electrophysiological subgroups comprising 593 participants in Cluster C0 (45.0%), 203 in C1 (15.4%), and 521 in C2 (39.6%). All three clusters included predominantly ADHD participants: C0 = 557 ADHD (93.9%), C1 = 190 ADHD (93.6%), and C2 = 459 ADHD (88.1%). Clinical ADHD subtype composition differed across clusters. In C1 and C2, ADHD-Combined Type was the most common subtype (52.1% in C1; 45.1% in C2), followed by ADHD-Inattentive Type (37.2% in C1; 41.1% in C2). Conversely, Cluster C0 was dominated by ADHD-Inattentive Type (47%), with ADHD-Combined Type representing 40%. Other subtypes (Hyperactive/Impulsive, Other Specified, Unspecified) were less frequent and similarly distributed across clusters. Clusters showed statistically significant but small differences in age (Kruskal-Wallis p < 0.05; post-hoc Dunn’s tests significant with small effect sizes, Cohen’s d in the low-to-moderate range) and sex (χ² p < 0.05, Cramer’s V = 0.09), indicating weak demographic influences on cluster membership. Normalized Mutual Information (NMI) analyses identified the principal components that most strongly contributed to clustering. PC1 showed the highest contribution (NMI = 0.34), followed by PC2 (0.19) and PC19 (0.12). Visualization of the top PCs revealed partially overlapping but distinct separation patterns, with the greatest inter-cluster distinctions expressed along PC1 (fig. a). Deviation score heatmaps demonstrated clear electrophysiological differences between clusters. Cluster C1 exhibited the most pronounced negative deviations on PC1 and PC2, indicating substantial divergence from normative trajectories. Cluster C2 showed moderate negative deviations, whereas Cluster C0 remained closest to normative values, especially along PC1. These gradient patterns suggest varying degrees of neurodevelopmental atypicality across subgroups. Behavioral analyses of the three-cluster solution revealed distinct clinical profiles. The Kruskal-Wallis H-test showed significant inter-cluster differences across multiple domains (p < 0.05), confirmed by Dunn’s post-hoc tests. Cluster C0 showed elevated executive dysfunction, Cluster C1 had significantly higher hyperactivity scores, and Cluster C2 exhibited reduced internalizing symptoms. No significant differences emerged among the remaining 11 behavioral domains (fig. b). These results indicate that deviation-based electrophysiological clusters correspond to meaningful cognitive and symptom profiles.

Conclusion

Our findings show that applying a normative modeling framework to EEG data can capture individualized deviations from typical neurodevelopment and reveal electrophysiologically distinct subtypes within ADHD. These subgroups display meaningful differences in clinical symptoms and cognitive performance, indicating that EEG-based deviation profiles may serve as biologically grounded markers of ADHD heterogeneity. By moving beyond traditional case-control comparisons and quantifying subject-level departures from normative trajectories, this work supports a dimensional, neurobiologically informed perspective on psychiatric classification. These results highlight the limitations of categorical diagnostic systems and demonstrate the value of individualized modeling approaches for advancing precision psychiatry. Future work should examine the reproducibility of these subtypes in independent cohorts and assess their longitudinal stability and clinical utility.
