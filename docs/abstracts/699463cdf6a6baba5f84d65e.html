Introduction

The habenula has been implicated in substance use disorders and related psychiatric illness (e.g., mood disorders). Alcohol exposure alters habenula function, and manipulation of the structure’s function affects ethanol drinking and behavior of animals withdrawn from chronic alcohol exposure. The habenula is part of the “anti-reward” system in the brain, bearing neurons that represent negative emotional value through inhibitory actions on dopaminergic nuclei (e.g., ventral tegmental area - VTA). These effects can contribute to withdrawal and reinstatement of drug self-administration, implicating the habenula in the progression of addiction. The habenula in humans is functionally linked to the salience network which is important in withdrawal and dependence.

Neuroimaging studies of human habenula function have been limited because of challenges in precise localization of this small epithalamic structure and its segmentation. Recent advances in machine learning-based approaches to segmentation combined with availability of large neuroimaging data sets (e.g., UK Biobank) that use MRI protocols with sufficient spatial resolution provide the opportunity to refine assessments of habenula function. This combination offers an unprecedented opportunity to examine habenula circuitry related to alcohol use disorder (AUD).

We examined the relationship between functional connectivity (FC) of the habenula and severity of alcohol use using fMRI and alcohol-use data from the UK Biobank. Specifically, we examined habenula FC with subcortical mesocorticolimbic tract nuclei (e.g., VTA, ventral striatum) and the salience network. Both resting state and task-based FC were assessed. We hypothesized that habenula FC with these areas would be related to severity of alcohol use.

Methods

We examined data from 2,981 individuals from the UK Biobank study (44-82 years of age, Mean=61.96, SD= 7.37, 1,668 female – 55%). Alcohol-use was assessed using the Alcohol Use Disorder Identification Test (AUDIT). Individuals were grouped into four levels of alcohol use: 54.14% (n = 1,614) were classified as minimal drinkers (AUDIT score 0–4), 22.74% (n = 678) as low-risk drinkers (AUDIT score 5–7), 14.42% (n = 430) as hazardous drinkers (AUDIT score 8–11), and 8.69% (n = 259) met criteria for alcohol use disorder (AUDIT score ≥12).

Habenula segmentations were conducted on T1 images using an automated deep-learning algorithm that has shown >95% accuracy1. Quality control was conducted using both automated procedures and visual inspection. 

FMRI data included rest and the Affective Face Matching Task, analyzed separately. Habenula (Hb) FC maps were obtained from whole brain analyses. Hb ROI time-series were separately extracted from unsmoothed but otherwise fully preprocessed resting-state MRI data in MNI space. After regressing out timeseries from two thalamic ROIs adjacent to the habenula approximately corresponding to the dorsomedial and centromedian nuclei, extracted Hb timeseries values were correlated with the timeseries of each cortical surface vertex and subcortical voxel in 32K CIFTI gray-ordinate space using Connectome Workbench. Results were z-transformed to yield final participant-level maps from which mean values were extracted for VTA, ventral striatum, and salience network ROIs in publicly available atlases. The probabilistic VTA atlas2 thresholded at 75% tissue probability, was binarized as recommended, and salience network (bilateral anterior insula and dACC) was extracted from network parcellations defined by Yeo et al3. Group analysis was conducted on participant mean values, using linear modeling in R, including covariates containing demographic information (age, sex, ethnicity, education, income), motion, and scanner model. 

Results

For resting state habenula FC, only FC with the ventral striatum showed a significant group effect (p=0.02) with posthoc analyses showing a significant difference in the hazardous drinkers (t=2.76, p=0.006) and those with AUD (t=2.13, p=0.04) relative to minimal drinkers. Resting state FC to VTA and Salience Network did not show a significant group effect. Task-based habenula FC showed a significant group effect for the ventral striatum (p=0.001) and salience network (P=0.006), but not the VTA. Posthoc analyses showed a significant difference between the AUD and minimal drinking groups in habenula-ventral striatum FC (t=3.4, p=0.0006) and habenula-salience network FC (t=2.18, p=0.03) (Figure 1). 

Conclusion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first large-scale analysis of habenula functional connectivity with respect to substance use. Both resting state and task-based habenula FC with ventral striatum (a direct target of the VTA) distinguish individuals with AUD from those who drink minimally, with task-based habenula-salience network FC also showing this relationship. The lack of relationship between habenula-VTA FC with alcohol use may indicate potential methodological issues, such as poor precision in localizing the structure with a template mask and low SNR in midbrain areas using multiband sequences.

Habenula-salience network FC shows a relationship with alcohol use during the affective face matching task but not during rest. This suggests that task-based FC of the habenula better indexes aspects of substance use that are not directly related to the mesocorticolimbic system, such as negative affect during withdrawal or incentive salience – two major components of substance use maintenance. The affective face matching task (e.g., processing facial expressions that depict anger, fear, or sadness) elicits negative affect. Given the sensitivity of the habenula to negative outcomes, the task may activate the habenula’s influence on neural targets (e.g., salience network) underlying these components.

Further analyses will examine the potential for habenula FC in classifying alcohol use groups using machine learning methods. Overall, this study provides the initial groundwork for examining the potential for habenula neuromodulation as a possible treatment modality for alcohol use disorder.
