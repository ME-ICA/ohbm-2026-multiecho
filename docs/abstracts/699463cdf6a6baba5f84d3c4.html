Introduction

Both Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and small vessel disease (SVD) are age-related diseases, which regularly occur together. While the hallmarks of AD, namely amyloid plaques and tau tangles, are mostly confined to gray matter areas, most SVD-related lesions, i.e., white matter hyperintensities (WMH), enlarged perivascular spaces (ePVS), cerebral microbleeds (CMB), and lacunes, occur in the white matter. Myelin loss has been well-documented in both diseases, however their joint impact on myelin integrity has not been characterized so far. In this study, we describe myelin levels within normal appearing white matter (NAWM), WMH, and AD-relevant fiber tracts. We further describe their association with AD and SVD-type pathologies.

Methods

We assessed 763 individuals of which 150 showed elevated cortical amyloid-PET binding but no elevated tau-PET binding in the meta-temporal region (A+T-), 141 showed both elevated amyloid-PET and tau-PET binding (A+T+), and 472 individuals were both amyloid- and tau-PET normal (A-T-). This included 339 cognitively normal (58 A+), 162 with subjective cognitive decline (71 A+), and 253 with mild cognitive impairment (153 A+) from the Swedish BioFINDER2 cohort. To quantify myelin, the χ-separation method was applied to multi-echo gradient echo (ME-GRE) MRI scans1, which enables the separation of the total susceptibility χ into diamagnetic (χ-negative, e.g., myelin) and paramagnetic (χ-positive , e.g., iron) sources. This separation is possible based on their differential effect on the phase components but common effect on the magnitude component of the ME-GRE scans. Myelin is the largest diamagnetic source in the brain, and therefore contributer to the χ-negative signal. We calculated voxel-level covariate-adjusted difference scores of χ-negative (Δ-ppm) for all individuals based on parameter estimates of the control group (CN A-). This was done to account for the normative heterogeneous myelination and to quantify region-specific myelin abnormalities. To test associations with χ-negative levels either within WMH or NAWM regions we used linear models with cortical amyloid, meta-temporal tau levels, WMH volume, ePVS count, number of both deep and lobar CMB, and below mentioned covariates. For the fixed JHU-DTI Atlas ROIs, we used the regular |χ-negative| measures instead of the difference scores. Global amyloid PET uptake levels were averaged across cortical brain regions. For the SVD-related lesions, we employed nnU-Net methods to segment and quantify WMH based on FLAIR scans2 and ePVS based on T1w scans3. Lacunes and CMBs were manually counted. For statistical analysis, we used paired t-tests and linear models with age, sex, years of education, APOE ε4 status, and WMH volume as covariates.

Results

Group comparison showed that |χ-negative| values were reduced in the NAWM of the A+T- and A+T+ group compared to the A-T- group in the hippocampal section of the cingulum bundle, the left inferior thalamic radiation, and the right posterior thalamic radiation (Figure 1A), suggesting reduced levels of myelin in A+ individuals. These tracts were preselected based on their AD-relevance, however, the results were not corrected for multiple comparisons. Higher global amyloid PET was associated with lower |χ-negative| values within the NAWM, independently of SVD lesions (t(751)=-2.80, p = 0.005, Figure 1C), supporting the association between amyloid deposition and demyelination. |χ-negative| values were also reduced in areas of WMH (Figure 1B), which was exacerbated in proportion to an increase in WMH volume (t(751)=-3.83, p<0.001, Figure 1D), and number of ePVS (t(751)=-4.58, p<0.001, Figure 1E).

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that both higher amyloid deposition and vascular lesion burden are associated with myelin loss in AD. The amyloid-associated myelin loss occurs in a region-dependent manner, where group differences in myelin are particularly present in temporal WM regions, and lower whole-brain NAWM myelin levels are associated with higher amyloid levels. The effects of vascular lesion burden appear to be more focal and associated with areas of WMH. That said, it needs to be highlighted that individuals were not diagnosed according to the Boston criteria, nor did we include individuals with late-stage AD. Therefore, our results most likely reflect processes within the early stages of each disease.  Given that myelin is essential for signal transfer in the brain and is a druggable brain substrate, our findings encourage future studies to assess the clinical impact of myelin loss in AD.
