Introduction

Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) measures changes associated with neural activity, offering high spatial but limited temporal resolution1. Since its discovery, the Blood Oxygenation Level-Dependent effect (BOLD) has been used as a proxy of neural activity, with Echo Planar Imaging (EPI) being the primary sequence for detecting fMRI activation. EPI combines high sampling speed and sensitivity to BOLD signal changes with whole-brain coverage and typical 2-3s temporal and 3mm-isotropic spatial resolution2 at 3T. Efforts to accelerate fMRI acquisition include echo volumar imaging, multi-shot 3D EPI, and simultaneous multi-slice EPI3. 3D-radial acquisition sampling patterns, combined with CS reconstruction, have also emerged as a viable approach, enabling flexible readout binning and reconstruction from under-sampled k-space data by leveraging signal sparsity. In previous work, CS-based reconstructions successfully detected percent signal changes at 0.25s temporal resolution4 while maintaining 1mm-isotropic spatial resolution.

Despite these advances, a comprehensive quantitative assessment of the specific effects of under-sampling is still lacking. Here, we address this gap by systematically evaluating CS reconstructions. Specifically, we characterize (1) spatiotemporal trade-offs at different under-sampling levels using point spread function (PSF) analysis and (2) task-related image quality through t-score quantification at different temporal resolutions.

Methods

20 healthy volunteers were acquired using a GRE sequence with a 3D radial phyllotaxis trajectory5 and a 64-channel head coil, with ethics approval (Protocol VD 2018-00240). Visual stimulation with a flickering checkerboard, alternating 15 s of active stimulation and 25 s of rest, was repeated 33 times and synchronized via a Sync-box (NordicNeuroLab). The fMRI sequence parameters were the following: TR=28.5ms, TE=25ms, FOV=192x192x192mm, 1mm³-isotropic voxels, FA=12°, for a total acquisition time of 22min. Anatomical T1w images (MPRAGE, TE=2.43ms, TR=1890ms, TI=955ms, FA=9°, FOV=256x256mm, 192 slices, voxelsize =1mm isotropic) were acquired for ROIs segmentation. Motion-corrected 3D volumes were reconstructed at 2.5s, 1s, 0.5s, and 0.25s temporal resolutions, using a CS k-t sparse SENSE algorithm (λ=0.01, ρ=0.06) with temporal Fourier regularization. Data from different trials were grouped based on their timing relative to the stimulus (Fig1), producing a single averaged 3D volume per bin. Resulting 4D datasets capture the trial-averaged evolution of the BOLD response over a trial of 40s at different temporal resolutions. 

Point Spread Function (PSF).

The PSF quantifies the spatial blurring introduced by an imaging technique, limiting its spatial resolution. Here, we focus on additional blurring caused by temporal under-sampling; hence we estimate the extra blur between our reference images (2.5s) and the under-sampled images (1s, 0.5s, 0.25s). Parametric fitting of a 3D Gaussian filter was used as a measurement of the PSF, optimizing the filter width to minimize the difference between the under-sampled image and reference image convolved with the Gaussian filter. 

Task t-scores. 

A general linear model 

(GLM, model: 𝑦=𝛽0+𝛽1𝑋+residuals)
 was fitted to each voxel time course, where y represents the single-voxel time course, X is the expected BOLD response, and β₁ is the estimated activation amplitude. Task t-scores, defined as β₁/std(residuals), with β₁ capturing task-related signal and std(residuals) reflecting a measure of unexplained variance. Analyses focused on the bilateral calcarine ROI to assess the effects of under-sampling on signal quality, also examining β₁ and residuals separately.


Results

Blurring increased with temporal under-sampling, with estimated PSF widths of 1.24mm, 2.42mm, 6.37mm for 1s, 0.5s, 0.25s, respectively, reflecting the smoothing introduced by CS reconstruction. At 0.5s, the PSF width of 2.42mm indicates that image quality remained high, with slight smoothing. In contrast, at 0.25s, the estimated PSF width of 6.37mm reflects more significant blurring. Differences between the blurred reference and under-sampled images were primarily localized at edges, with minimal impact on the ROI at 0.5s. Observed distortions for the 0.25s reconstruction suggest that CS-induced blurring at this temporal resolution cannot be fully described by simple Gaussian smoothing, thereby limiting the reliability of our FWHM estimate at 0.25s resolution. As presented in Fig2, t-scores decreased with increasing temporal resolution. Consistent with the PSF analysis results, this degradation was important only for the 0.25s reconstructions, where under-sampling introduced notable spatial blurring. Across subjects, average t-score on ROI decreased by 12.42% at 500ms and by 40.5% at 250ms compared to the 2.5s reference, while a modest improvement of 4.78% was observed for the 1s reconstructions. This small improvement likely reflects the benefit of having more timepoints, improving the GLM fitting. Fig2 also shows that the t-scores changes reflect a concurrent increase in residual noise and reduction in activation strength (β₁) at higher temporal resolutions. 

Conclusion

Our results confirm that high-temporal-resolution fMRI using 3D radial trajectories and CS reconstruction is limited by under-sampling-induced blurring. Both PSF and t-score analysis revealed that image quality decreases at 0.25s. Despite these limits, reconstructed images up to 0.5s preserved meaningful task-related signals, with modest blurring. These findings start delineating both the potential and limitations of CS fMRI.
