Introduction

Human higher-order cognition is widely thought to emerge from coordinated interactions among multiple large-scale neural circuits distributed across the brain. While prior work has linked specific cognitive processes to particular networks or circuits [1,2], cognitive functions are inherently multidimensional and substantially intercorrelated across domains [3], suggesting that individual differences are organized along shared axes rather than isolated faculties. In parallel, inter-individual variability in large-scale neural circuits is structured and follows population-level covariation patterns. These converging observations raise a fundamental question: does a stable, low-dimensional latent structure govern the joint variation of cognition and large-scale circuits across individuals?

Resting-state functional connectivity (RSFC) summarizes intrinsic large-scale network organization and is widely used to relate brain networks to individual differences in behavior and cognition [4]. Multivariate covariance-decomposition frameworks, including singular value decomposition (SVD), can reveal low-dimensional patterns of covariation between whole-brain RSFC and behavioral profiles. Yet it remains unclear whether RSFC exhibits a compact latent organization that can jointly explain variability across diverse cognitive domains in healthy young adults.

Here, we leverage resting-state fMRI data from the Human Connectome Project S1200 sample to address this gap. Using SVD, we jointly analyze whole-brain RSFC and 21 cognitive measures spanning memory, executive control, fluid intelligence, language, processing speed, self-regulation, spatial orientation, attention, and working memory. We aim to identify the dominant latent dimension linking RSFC to cognition and to characterize its associated cognitive profile and large-scale network architecture.

Methods

Resting-state functional connectivity construction
We analyzed resting-state fMRI and cognitive behavioral data from the Human Connectome Project Young Adult (HCP-YA). Four runs of rs-fMRI (R1-LR , n=1009;  R1-RL , n=1051; R2-LR, n=1001 ; R2-RL, n=1001) were preprocessed using the HCP minimal pipeline. We use R1_LR rsfMRI as the discovery set, and the remaining three as the validation set. To construct RSFC, we extracted time series from 271 whole-brain regions of interest (ROIs), which includes 200 cortical regions(from the Schaefer200 cortical parcellation) , 54 subcortical regions(from the Melbourne Subcortex Atlas) and 17 cerebellar regions(from the Buckner Atlas). We then constructed a 271 × 271 functional connectivity matrix for each participant by using Pearson correlation. RSFC upper triangular matrix was vectorized for each subject and we regressed out potential confounding effects, including mean FD, age, handedness, and sex.

Cognitive function measures

We selected 21 cognitive function measures spanning episodic memory, fluid intelligence, cognitive flexibility, Inhibition, processing speed, working memory, verbal episodic memory, sustained attention, spatial orientation, vocabulary comprehension, reading decoding, and impulsivity. Behavioral variables were z-scored across participants. 

Singular value decomposition analysis

We used singular value decomposition (SVD) to examine the relationship between resting-state functional connectivity (RSFC) and cognitive function (CF) measures in the discovery dataset. SVD is a multivariate data-driven approach that identifies low-dimensional latent dimensions by decomposing the cross-covariance structure between two sets of variables[5].

In the present analysis, one variable set corresponded to whole-brain RSFC features, and the other corresponded to behavioral measures spanning multiple cognitive domains. RSFC and CF data were first correlated across participants to construct an RSFC–CF covariance matrix. This covariance matrix was then subjected to singular value decomposition, yielding a set of orthogonal latent variables (LVs) that capture maximally covarying patterns between brain connectivity and cognitive function.

Each LV consisted of an RSFC pattern at the connection level (“RSFC loadings”) and a cognitive function profile (“CF loadings”). Individual-specific RSFC and CF composite scores for each LV were obtained by projecting each participant’s RSFC and CF data onto the corresponding loading vectors.Associations between individual variables and latent dimensions were quantified as Pearson correlations between each variable and the corresponding LV composite score.

Results

SVD identified 21 latent variables (LVs) representing coupled patterns of RSFC and cognitive function measures. The first three latent variables (LV1, LV2, and LV3) accounted for 37.75%, 9.41%, and 8.71% of the total RSFC–cognition covariance, respectively (55.87% cumulatively). Permutation testing (1,000 permutations) revealed that LV1 (p = 0.001) and LV3 (p = 0.004) were statistically significant, whereas LV2 was not (p = 0.319). Based on effect size and reliability, subsequent analyses focused on LV1.

To assess stability, RSFC data from each of the remaining three runs were projected onto the LV1 RSFC loading pattern derived exclusively from the discovery run. Although all four runs were acquired from the same participants, this out-of-run projection yielded robust brain–behavior associations across repeated acquisitions, with correlation coefficients of r = 0.35 (R1-LR), r = 0.52 (R1-RL), r = 0.48 (R2-LR), and r = 0.40 (R2-RL), each significant at permutation p < 0.0001. These results demonstrate strong run-to-run reproducibility of the identified RSFC–cognition latent dimension and its robustness to acquisition-specific variability.

To characterize the behavioral features underlying the latent dimensions, we examined Pearson correlations between individual cognitive variables and the LV1 behavioral composite scores. These associations indicate the extent to which each cognitive measure is expressed along the latent dimension. LV1 behavioral scores showed strong correlations with measures of fluid intelligence (PMAT24_A_CR: r = −0.73; PMAT24_A_SI: r = 0.70), vocabulary comprehension and reading decoding (PicVocab and ReadEng: r = −0.70), and spatial orientation (VSPLOT_TC: r = −0.67; VSPLOT_OFF: r = 0.69). Measures of processing speed, sustained attention, and working memory exhibited moderate correlations with LV1 behavioral scores. Together, these results indicate that LV1 captures a distributed behavioral profile spanning multiple higher-order cognitive domains, rather than reflecting a single domain-specific ability.

To characterize the neural architecture associated with LV1, we examined correlations between individual RSFC features and the LV1 RSFC composite scores. These RSFC–LV1 correlations were subsequently averaged within and between canonical functional networks to summarize large-scale network organization. The resulting network-level correlation patterns revealed clear network specificity across nine canonical systems (VN, SMN, DAN, VAN, LN, FPCN, DMN, SubC, and Cerebellum).

Strong negative correlations were observed among sensory and attention-related networks, particularly between the visual and somatomotor networks (r = −0.32), visual and ventral attention networks (r = −0.27), and somatomotor and dorsal attention networks (r = −0.25), indicating relative segregation of lower-order systems along LV1. In contrast, higher-order networks showed positive correlations with attention systems: the frontoparietal control network exhibited positive correlations with the somatomotor (r = 0.15), dorsal attention (r = 0.08), and visual networks (r = 0.09), while the default mode network showed positive correlations with the dorsal attention (r = 0.10), ventral attention (r = 0.19), and somatomotor networks (r = 0.10). Ventral attention network connections displayed a mixed profile, with negative correlations with sensory networks and positive correlations with the default mode and subcortical networks (r = 0.10). Limbic network contributions were weak (|r| < 0.08), whereas subcortical and cerebellar networks showed modest positive correlations primarily with attention and somatomotor systems. Overall, LV1 was characterized by selective integration of attention and control networks alongside segregation of sensory and motor systems.

Conclusion

This study identifies a robust latent dimension that links resting-state functional connectivity with multidimensional cognitive performance. The dominant dimension, LV1, accounts for 37.75% of the shared RSFC–behavior covariance and reflects an integrated cognitive axis spanning fluid intelligence, executive control, language abilities, spatial orientation, processing speed, and episodic memory. Its behavioral architecture highlights the interdependence of diverse cognitive domains rather than isolated contributions.

Neurally, LV1 is characterized by a distributed pattern of connectivity involving sensory–motor and visual systems, dorsal attention networks, and higher-order control networks including the FPCN and DMN. The observed negative coupling among sensory-attention systems and positive coupling with control networks suggests that LV1 indexes the balance between segregation and integration across large-scale networks—a fundamental organizational principle of brain function. The network-level symmetry and hierarchical patterning align with theoretical models of cognitive control, efficiency, and adaptive information processing.

Cross-run projection analyses demonstrated strong reproducibility of LV1, confirming its stability across acquisition conditions and highlighting its potential as a reliable latent marker of cognitive variation. The stability and interpretability of this latent axis provide a promising foundation for future applications, including individual-level prediction, cross-population generalization, and exploration of developmental or clinical alterations.

Overall, these results illustrate how multivariate covariance decomposition integrates complex behavioral profiles with high-dimensional brain connectivity, revealing a coherent neurocognitive architecture. The latent dimension identified here provides new insights into how distributed neural systems support cognitive diversity, offering a unifying framework for understanding the neural basis of human cognitive differences.
