Introduction

In psychiatric populations, overlapping symptoms between different diagnoses and high heterogeneity within any given diagnosis can be observed1. This variety hampers clinical practice in diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment, leading to a shift in psychiatric research towards transdiagnostic approaches. Those approaches have a more differentiated perspective on psychiatric populations allowing to identify relations between different symptoms and diagnoses2. Additionally, they enable the detection of subgroups within the population according to specific phenotypes, establishing order within the heterogeneity of the psychiatric population. However diagnostic labels are still used by clinicians and need to be considered in transdiagnostic approaches. 

Analogous to the G-factor for intelligence, a psychopathology factor (P-factor) was introduced to describe covariances of psychopathology. Three dimensions are currently investigated reflecting correlation effects on psychopathology1: phenotypes, genes, and neural. These dimensions and their intermediate factors are investigated to obtain transdiagnostic connections from the psychiatric population on different levels. In the phenotype dimension, mainly symptoms and behavioral characteristics are investigated. Some connections between personality traits and psychiatric disorders have been reported3 which motivates the core idea of this study. 

This work aims to detect connections between major psychiatric disorders, psychiatric symptoms, and personality traits by conducting a meta-analysis based on correlations reported in the literature. Biological factors like genomics, neuroanatomy and -physiology as well as comorbidities are considered as mediating factors to construct a network that provides insight into the different levels of the P-factors. In this abstract, only the results based on genome-wide association studies (GWAS) and neuroimaging are presented. 

Methods

Study selection

The literature selection for the meta-analysis was conducted according the PRISMA guidelines4. Studies were included when they investigated at least one of the five most frequent psychiatric disorders (major depressive disorder (MDD), attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), autism spectrum disorder (ASD), schizophrenia (SCZ), bipolar disorder (BPD)) and at least one personality trait. There were no restrictions on the method used or other additional information. The search was performed in the PubMed database on September 23rd, 2025 using this search string: ("personality trait*" OR "big five" OR 'NEO-FFI' OR "Five-Factor Inventory") AND ("anxiety disorder" OR "depression" OR "major depressive disorder" OR "MDD" OR "bipolar disorder" OR "schizophrenia" OR "attention deficit hyperactivity disorder" OR "ADHD" OR "autism spectrum disorder" OR "ASD") AND ("psychiatric") NOT ('treatment'). The results were filtered for the last 10 years to compile a comprehensive overview of the recent research. Studies were excluded when they met at least one exclusion criteria: 1) no connection between personality traits and disorders or symptoms was quantified; 2) the paper is a review; 3) the paper is not published in English or not accessible. 

Network construction

Information of five major categories was extracted: demographic, clinical, personality, biological, and assessment medium. Demographic data includes information about the population size, age, gender, education, and ethnicity. Information about the disorders, the symptoms and comorbidities were collected under the clinical category. Personality combines all personality traits that were examined. Biological data contains information about genetic loci, brain anatomy and physiology, hormones, neurotransmitter, and proteins. The assessment medium combines all types of structured and semi-structured interviews, questionnaires, and laboratory procedures for assessing any of the other categories. Correlations and group comparisons between the properties of these categories are examined as connections. For different levels of abstraction, nodes are combined, when they embody the same object but were assessed with different mediums or when they share a common domain. Multiple edges between the same nodes are combined by calculating the weighted mean between edges with the same direction. Edges with different correlation directions are not combined to highlight contractionary results. Only significant correlations as reported in the original studies are considered. 

Network analysis

The approach of the network analysis was based on social network analyses. This allows the identification of the nodes with the highest focus in research as operationalized by the degree centrality, finding transitive connection between correlations, and clustering or community algorithms to identify subgroups of nodes. Additionally contradictory results are compiled by allowing positive as well as negative correlation between nodes. Different abstraction levels and different domains of the P-factor are used to get further insight into the connections and identify similarities and differences across the domains.

Results

Study selection

The initial database search resulted in 403 papers. 97 papers were excluded based on abstract screening. The identified papers can be grouped into four partially overlapping categories: connection between psychiatric disorders and personality traits based on GWAS studies (n=10), neuroimaging (n=7), genomics (specific genes; n=83), and phenotypes only. The information of the smallest two categories were extracted and modeled first, resulting in 17 papers analyzed for the abstract. Three neuroimaging papers are excluded because they do not report a direct connection between a personality trait and a disorder or a symptom, resulting in 14 papers total.

Network construction

Due to the uneven distribution of studies and study participants across the four categories, we conducted the network analyses separately for the categories. Note that the number of participants in studies investigating genetic correlation are around 50 times higher than in studies with neuroimaging or phenotypic data only. This would lead to an over-representation in favor of the genetic correlation when the weighted mean is calculated. In this initial approach, the connections of the neuroimaging are only used to complement the results of the graph obtained from the GWAS studies. 

The initial GWAS network consists of 45 nodes and 101 edges constructed from 590 correlations, 22 personality traits, 38 diagnosis, 21 comorbidities, seven symptoms, 17 genetic loci and one hormone. Combining nodes and removing nodes that have only one connection to another node, results in a more abstract but readable graph with 21 nodes and 77 edges (Fig. 1).

Network analysis

The degree centrality analysis identifies neuroticism (0.605) as the node with the most connections, followed by depressive disorders (0.419). No contradictory correlations were found. Anxiety disorder forms a central node connecting strongly to nodes like neuroticism, depressive disorder, negative affectivity, and sleep with strong positive correlations and subjective well-being with a strong negative correlation (Fig. 1). Additionally, depressive disorders have strong correlations to post traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and schizophrenia, whereas neuroticism has strong correlations to negative affectivity and sleep. A strong correlation can also be observed between mania disorder and schizophrenia. Antisocial has one strong correlation to ADHD, whereas openness has several moderate correlations to different psychiatric disorders. The other personality traits have only weak correlations to other nodes. No connection between depressive disorder and depression symptoms is investigated. 

We did not find any overlap between the connections mediated by neuroimaging and GWAS correlations. Two neuroimaging studies connect reward processing deficits to ADHD, schizophrenia and people with schizotypal personality traits. Two other studies connect aggression and impulsivity with BPD and borderline personality traits.

Conclusion

In the subset of 14 papers analyzed, we observed a wide variety of results investigating different psychiatric disorders and personality traits. A total of 91 clinical factors and 39 personality traits were investigated. Our network analysis combined the findings of the past 10 years and gives further insight into the connection in the psychiatric population.  

Degree centrality

We identified neuroticism as a central node in the network indicating that this personality trait is investigated most frequently in combination with psychiatric disorders. Depressive disorder is the second central node, indicating that the disorders with the biggest impact on the health system worldwide is indeed also focused in research5. 

Correlation analysis

Overall, the structure of the network reveals anxiety disorder as a heavily connected node between depressive disorder and personality traits like neuroticism and negative affectivity. Since neuroticism is constructed based on subscales named anxiety, depressed, impulsiveness and vulnerability traits, these connections are plausible, highlighting the continuum between personality traits and their clinical manifestation in psychiatric symptoms. The rather small correlations of the other personality traits of the big five to anxiety and depressive disorder support the independence of those personality traits. Sleep shows a strong correlation to neuroticism and anxiety disorder. However, the only sleep related symptom investigated is nightmares. Therefore, further investigation of the impact of sleep is needed. We observe a strong correlation between mania disorder and schizophrenia which is driven by the correlation of BPD and schizophrenia. In contrast, depressive disorder and schizophrenia are only moderately connected. Finally, the missing connection between depressive disorder and depressive symptoms demonstrates the methodological discrepancy that studies are either working with psychiatric diagnoses or with symptoms. 

Overlap of genetic correlation and neuroimaging

No overlap was identified between the studies investigating genetic correlation and neuroimaging. Neuroimaging studies focus more on behavioral traits like aggression, impulsivity and reward dependence measuring behavioral changes during tasks. Such investigations cannot be done in GWAS studies. However, the connections investigated in the GWAS studies highlight the potential for neuroimaging studies to shed light on the intermediate neural phenotypes connecting personality and psychopathology.  

Conclusion

The small number of studies investigated in this analysis limits the current results[RA1] . The lack of connections between symptoms and disorders shows that studies mostly focus only on one of the clinical factors. Despite this, no overlap between the different domains of the P-factor could be observed. However with the increasing number of studies, it becomes more likely to identify an overlap. In sum, different abstraction levels will bring greater insight into specific subnetworks allowing clustering and community algorithms to group nodes and to identify specified subdomains to symptoms, disorders and personality traits.
