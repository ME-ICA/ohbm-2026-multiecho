Introduction

Children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) experience difficulties with prosodic processing, a key component of speech comprehension, speech production, and social communication. In childhood, speech envelope tracking (marker of prosodic processing) is initially supported by a bilateral network, becoming increasingly right lateralized through adolescence.1 Atypical lateralization of gross language functions has been previously reported in children with ASD, but the network supporting prosodic processing of naturalistic speech stimuli has not been studied in these children. We hypothesize that children with ASD will have decreased right lateralization (i.e., an immature pattern) for prosodic processing compared to typically developing controls (CTL).

Methods

Using 151-channel whole-head MEG (CTF, Coquitlam, Canada) at 2400 Hz sampling, we characterized the networks underlying speech envelope tracking in 21 children with ASD and 27 controls, ages 6 to 12 years. Participants completed a naturalistic stories-listening task, split into three ~60-second trials, each preceded and succeeded by 10 seconds of speech-shaped noise with identical spectral content and amplitude envelope. The speech envelope was extracted by passing the audio through a 32‑band gammatone filterbank (50 – 8000 Hz); band outputs were rectified, raised to the 0.6 power to approximate loudness perception, averaged, low-pass filtered at 12 Hz, and aligned to digitized-audio embedded within the MEG recording through maximization of cross-correlation. MEG trials were filtered from 0.5 to 12 Hz to isolate phonemic and prosodic rhythms. Surrogate audio tracks were generated using an Iterative Amplitude Adjusted Fourier Transform (IAAFT) procedure, and neuromagnetic signals coherent with the speech envelope and with 50 surrogate envelopes were localized using dynamic imaging of coherent sources (DICS).2 Coherence to the speech envelope was normalized by coherence to surrogate envelopes, permitting statistical (z) interpretation of observed neural tracking. Normalized coherence was compared across groups using non‑parametric cluster‑based permutation analyses, controlling for age, with cluster mass defined as the sum of t‑values across contiguous suprathreshold voxels. We applied two cluster-forming thresholds: individual voxel-based nonparametric threshold, and a conventional t-threshold, at alpha = 0.05. The former permits sensitive exploration of group effects; the latter positions the current study within the context of prior neurophysiological and neuroimaging studies employing cluster-based statistical analyses. We also computed conventional laterality indices, LI = (L-R)/(L+R), for suprathreshold (p < 0.05) voxels, to investigate relationships between lateralization of prosodic processing and language performance (Peabody Picture Vocabulary Test, or PPVT-4, for receptive vocabulary,3 and the Expressive Vocabulary Test, or EVT-3, for expressive vocabulary).4

Results

The cluster-based analyses with individual voxel-based cluster-forming thresholds revealed a single, predominantly left-lateralized cluster, for ASD > CTL (p < 0.05), while controlling for age (Figure 1). Using conventional cluster-based analyses, no significant group differences were observed (p > 0.05). We generated a single prosodic processing map, comprised of nodes with normalized coherence significantly greater than zero in a single-sample t-test (FDR‑corrected, q < 0.05) for data from both groups, after controlling for age. In children with ASD, right-hemisphere engagement of the prosodic network was positively correlated with receptive vocabulary scores (r = -0.44, p < 0.05). We observed no relationship between LIs and language performance in controls (p > 0.05).

Conclusion

Children with ASD show relative left lateralization for prosodic processing, compared to controls. Within the ASD cohort, right lateralization was correlated with stronger receptive vocabulary. In future studies, we hope to assess whether early lateralization for prosody is predictive of later outcomes, in children with ASD. Neural coherence to the speech envelope may serve as a neuromagnetic biomarker for language prognostication in children with ASD, including those who are minimally verbal or pre-verbal.
