Introduction

Observing an action performed by another individual activates brain areas involved in the execution of similar actions in the observer’s brain. This mechanism is provided by the mirror neuron system (MNS), a cortical temporo-parieto-frontal network which allows a visuo-motor matching of the observed action with the observer's own motor representation. Recent studies suggested that, in addition to cortical areas, also cerebellar lobules V, VI and VIII, dentate nucleus, thalamus and basal ganglia, are activated during both the observation and execution of hand actions. ^1, ^2 Importantly, direct and indirect connections between these structures and cortical areas endowed with mirror properties have been reported. ^3 Several studies showed that also motor imagery recruits the same brain regions belonging to the MNS. However, to the best of our knowledge, few fMRI studies included all three conditions within the same well-controlled paradigm, and none of them investigated goal-directed grasping actions. Moreover, most 3T fMRI studies employed acquisition with a voxel size of 3 mm or larger, which limits the fine-grained investigation of functional activations of subcortical and cerebellar structures during these tasks. ^4 Thus, in the present 3T fMRI study we investigated cortical, subcortical, and cerebellar involvement during observation, motor imagery, and execution of goal-directed grasping actions using a higher whole-brain spatial resolution (i.e., 1.75 mm voxel size). 

Methods

Twenty-six healthy right-handed participants were involved in the study (13 females; mean age = 25 years; SD = 2.8). The fMRI study consisted of three runs: (a) action observation, in which participants passively observed videos of goal-directed reaching-grasping actions performed by an actor with the right hand, from a first-person perspective (OBS PASS). As control condition, a still image of the first frame of each video (i.e., right hand next to the object) was presented (OBS CTRL); (b) motor imagery, during which pictures of objects were presented, and participants were required to imagine to perform a reaching-grasping action in order to use the objects using their right hand (IMA MOT). As control condition, they had to passively observe the objects (IMA CTRL); (c) action execution, in absence of visual feedback, which included a goal-directed condition (reaching-grasping of a sphere placed next to the right hand – EXE GRASP). As control condition, participants had to execute an opening-closing movement of the right hand (EXE MOVE). Functional T2*-weighted images were acquired on a 3T MRI scanner (General Electric Signa Premier). A gradient-EPI sequence was used with the following acquisition parameters: 80 axial slices covering the whole brain, TR = 2000 ms, voxel size of 1.75x1.75x1.75 mm. Data pre-processing was performed with SPM12 using a standard pipeline. Functional volumes were normalized and re-sampled in 1.7 mm3 voxels and then spatially smoothed with a 4-mm Gaussian kernel (FWHM). All functional images were inspected using the artifact detection tools (ART) with a 1.7 mm (i.e., voxel size) movement threshold. To ensure data quality at subcortical and cerebellar levels, the temporal signal-to-noise ratio (tSNR) was calculated for each fMRI run separately within the thalamus, basal ganglia, and dentate nucleus. Individual tSNR maps were then registered to standard MNI space and averaged to create a group-level map. Whole-brain first-level analyses were performed using a subject-specific normalized binary explicit mask derived from the sum of segmented grey matter, white matter, and CSF tissue. Moreover, in order to assess specific subcortical and cerebellar activations, three additional models were computed using separately explicit masks of thalamus, basal ganglia (including putamen, globus pallidus, caudate nucleus, red nucleus, subthalamic nucleus, and substantia nigra), and dentate nucleus. The six motion parameters estimated (i.e., translation and rotation) were used as predictors of no interest in each first-level model. At second level, a group-based statistical analysis was performed for each considered map (whole brain, thalamus, basal ganglia, dentate nucleus), thus resulting in four second-level models. In order to identify the significant cortical voxels showing a shared activation during observation, imagery and execution of goal-related actions, conjunction analysis (OBS PASS & IMA MOT & EXE GRASP > rest) was carried out. Finally, given the crucial role of the ventral intermediate nucleus (Vim) of the thalamus in the upper-limb motor control and its anatomical size and location, small volume correction (SVC) was performed within left Vim in each experimental condition to investigate its functional involvement in action observation, motor imagery, and action execution (minimum number of voxels, k > 10). Overall, statistical inference was drawn, except when stated, at the voxel level with a threshold of p < 0.001 uncorrected, and at the cluster level with a threshold of p < 0.05 FWE. 

Results

ART did not identify any participant as motion outlier: in all subjects the proportion of outlier volumes in each run was <4%. The resulting tSNR was suitable to support reliable analyses in subcortical and cerebellar regions (mean tSNR ± SD of the thalamus = 64.4 ± 9.7, basal ganglia = 67.3 ± 9.7, dentate nucleus = 80.2 ± 9). Thus, our dataset was qualitatively appropriate and optimal for studying functional activations in the subcortical structures. At cortical level, functional activations in each run were examined using direct experimental-control contrasts (i.e., experimental condition > control condition). For subcortical structures (i.e., thalamus and basal ganglia) and dentate nucleus, considering the tSNR characteristics associated with a whole-brain 3T acquisition at 1.75 mm voxel size, activation patterns were reported separately for experimental and control conditions to provide a more accurate characterization of the specific parts of these structures involved during the tasks. During action observation task, the contrast OBS PASS > OBS CTRL revealed a bilateral activation of a cortical temporo-parietal network encompassing the superior temporal sulcus and inferior parietal lobule (IPL), together with the left dorsal premotor cortex (PMd). At subcortical and cerebellar levels, OBS PASS engaged the pulvinar bilaterally, the left VLP and MD thalamic nuclei, the left external globus pallidus (GPe) (p-FWE cluster level = 0.06), and the dentate nucleus bilaterally, whereas OBS CTRL elicited more restricted activation of the left pulvinar, the right caudate nucleus and the left dentate nucleus. During motor imagery task, the contrast IMA MOT > IMA CTRL engaged a left-lateralized cortical fronto-parietal network including IPL, PMd, premotor ventral cortex (PMv), dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, and supplementary motor area (SMA). At subcortical and cerebellar levels, IMA MOT elicited an activation of the left pulvinar (larger than in IMA CTRL), together with robust bilateral involvement of the putamen, GPe, and caudate nuclei, while no activations emerged in the dentate nucleus (only in IMA MOT). During action execution task, the contrast EXE GRASP > EXE OPENCLOSE elicited a stronger activation of primary sensorimotor cortices, together with right cerebellar lobules V, VI and VIII. At thalamic level, the activation was present in the left VPL, MD and CM nuclei in both EXE GRASP and EXE OPENCLOSE conditions. In addition, the left pulvinar and bilateral VLP were engaged during EXE GRASP only. In the basal ganglia, both conditions activated the putamen bilaterally, with EXE GRASP showing more extensive left pallidal and right caudate nucleus involvement. Furthermore, both motor conditions engaged the right dentate nucleus with highly spatial overlapping clusters. SVC analyses within the left Vim revealed that OBS PASS activated its dorsal portion, whereas EXE GRASP recruited both dorsal and ventral sectors of this thalamic nucleus. No Vim activations were reported in the remaining conditions. Finally, the cortical activation map emerging from the conjunction analysis between OBS PASS, IMA MOT and EXE GRASP showed a shared activation of bilateral IPL, PMd, PMv, SMA, cerebellar lobules V and VI, and the right cerebellar lobule VIII. 

Conclusion

Overall, the data quality of our fMRI dataset was adequate to allow robust analyses in subcortical and cerebellar regions in a non-invasive manner at 3T. In particular, we identified a consistent parietal, premotor, and cerebellar network involved during observation, imagery and execution of goal-directed grasping actions, thus reinforcing the idea that all these conditions recruit similar brain regions. ^4 Moreover, at subcortical level, this study confirms that motor imagery engages mainly basal ganglia (motor inhibition mechanisms), whereas action observation activates specific sectors of thalamus, basal ganglia, and dentate nucleus, supporting the hypothesis of an integrated cortico-subcortical-cerebellar network. ^1, ^2, ^3 This suggests that each of these structures has a specific role during action observation: performing internal simulation (dentate nucleus), processing visual information/involving motor resonance mechanisms (thalamus), and inhibiting unwanted motor outcomes (basal ganglia). Taken together, this evidence indicates a clear role of subcortical and cerebellar structures in motor simulation, inhibition, and execution of actions. These findings could open new avenues for the development of rehabilitation strategies that harness these cortico-subcortical-cerebellar pathways to promote motor recovery through action observation and imitation. ^5
