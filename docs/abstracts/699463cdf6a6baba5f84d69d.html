Introduction

Structural connectivity (SC) delineates the brain’s white-matter architecture that supports inter-regional communication1. In contrast, functional connectivity (FC) reflects statistical coupling between regional neural activities over time. While formally distinct, FC patterns are constrained by the underlying structural scaffold2, which may explain why brain regions with direct anatomical connections tend to exhibit stronger functional correlations, although indirect pathways and network dynamics also contribute to FC2. Motivated by the observed coupling between structure and function and the potential to simplify the interpretation of feature relevance, we explore whether information from the structural connectome could serve as a biologically informed and target-independent feature selector (FS) to improve the prediction of individual traits from FC data. Here, we focus on predicting three example phenotypes: age, fluid intelligence, and alcohol use (AUDIT-C score), and on comparing an SC-based feature selection approach with FC-magnitude-based FS and random FS. We hypothesised that SC-based FS would outperform FC-based FS, based on previous results for an fMRI-FC fingerprinting task.

Methods

We used pre-processed resting-state fMRI and relevant phenotypic data from 17,152 UK Biobank participants3. Imaging data were pre-processed by UK-Biobank pipeline and used as provided5. Data were split into 80% exploration and 20% validation sets. We will not report on the latter here because we will preregister our findings from the exploration set first. Participants with valid resting-state scans and complete phenotypic measures (age, sex, fluid intelligence, and non-zero AUDIT-C score) were included. The functional connectome was constructed using the CONN atlas (132 ROIs), computing FC as Fisher Z-transformed Pearson’s correlations between each pair of ROI time series. A group-averaged structural connectome (SC) was derived from the HCP S1200 young adult cohort based on MRI diffusion-based tractography4 and resampled to the same atlas. Streamline counts between regions were normalized by region volume before conversion to ranks. An average functional connectome was computed for the exploration set and converted to ranks based on the magnitude of the absolute value of FC.

We implemented three FS strategies: (i) SC-based; (ii) FC-based; and (iii) 10 random selectors. Feature selection was applied across a range of FS percentiles (FSPs), and we also performed a complementary inverse-selection analysis by removing the strongest features first, which should result in a stronger drop in performance for the better selector.

We used nested cross-validation (10 outer, 3 inner folds) on the exploration set for model training and hyperparameter tuning and Kernel Ridge Regression(KRR) as the prediction model. Prediction accuracy was measured as the Pearson’s correlation between predicted and true phenotypes/targets on the concatenated 10 outer test folds.

We regressed out age (for non-age targets), sex, and mean relative head motion from target variables in the training folds and applied the fitted model to the corresponding test fold. We evaluated five preregistered hypotheses introduced in the Introduction: H1.1 that feature selection (FS) yields higher accuracy than using all features; H1.2 that performance degrades only when Feature Selection percentage (FSP) falls below a critical threshold; H2.1 that SC-based FS outperforms FC-based FS; H2.2 that both outperform random FS; and H2.3 that removing the strongest connections first leads to a sharper performance drop than removing weak connections.

To test these, we computed Pearson’s r across different FSPs and compared the area under the curve (AUC)using 1,000 bootstrap samples drawn from subject-level predictions to obtain empirical confidence intervals and p-values. We defined the degradation point as the first FSP at which the mean r lies below the 90% CI of r-value for predictions using the full-matrix . Peak r-values were considered significantly better than the full matrix-based prediction, if they exceeded the corresponding 90% CI.

Results

Across all targets, peak prediction occurred at FSP ≥ 30%, with no peak r values significantly exceeding the full-matrix baseline (Fig. 1a,c). Degradation, defined as the first FSP where r dropped below the full-matrix 90% CI, emerged at ≤ 50% for age, ≤ 6% for AUDIT-C, and ≤ 15% for fluid intelligence .

In the AUDIT-C example (Fig. 1a), both “strongest kept” and “strongest removed” conditions showed stable performance at moderate to high FSPs, with similar profiles for SC- and FC-based selection and consistently lower performance for random selection. 

AUC comparisons (Fig. 1b) showed that SC- and FC-based selection outperformed random for all targets. SC and FC did not differ significantly for age or AUDIT-C. In contrast, FC-based selection outperformed SC-based selection for fluid intelligence, a pattern also supported by inverse feature selection.

Peak and degradation values for all methods are reported in Fig. 1c, confirming that feature selection does not significantly outperform utilising all connections, but it does reveal target-specific degradation thresholds and consistent advantages over random selection.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that incorporating structural connectivity information to guide feature selection in functional-connectivity models is a viable strategy that does not substantially compromise predictive accuracy for FSP ≥ 50%. However, it did not prove to be superior to within-cohort FC-based FS. While FC-based FS was even statistically better than SC-based FS in some analyses, SC-based selection is still advantageous for two reasons: a) it refers back to physically existing connections, and b) we presume that it is not a function of within-subject dynamics and is less dependent on the sample than FC. Moreover, both SC- and FC-based FS outperformed random feature selection, reinforcing the benefit of biologically grounded, yet target-independent, dimensionality reduction.
