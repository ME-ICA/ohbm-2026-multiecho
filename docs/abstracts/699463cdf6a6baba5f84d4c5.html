Introduction

Effective connectivity models, such as Dynamic Causal Modelling1 (DCM), are widely used to infer directed interactions between brain regions from fMRI data within a principled Bayesian framework. In practice, however, effective connectivity estimates often vary substantially across sessions or experimental manipulations raising questions about how such differences should be interpreted. We show here large apparent changes in estimated connectivity do not necessarily reflect large changes in the underlying network dynamics.

Effective connectivity is shaped by the experiment’s observation model, including neurovascular coupling and input structure, which restricts which changes in connectivity parameters can be detected from fMRI data. Classical identifiability analyses ask whether parameters can, in principle, be recovered; here, we address a complementary question: which continuous transformations of effective connectivity leave measured neural responses unchanged under a given experiment. We show that blind spots in effective connectivity estimation arise from structured, connection-preserving transformations that we call singular transformations, such that connectivity estimates that appear strongly different may in fact be equivalent up to a singular transformation.

To investigate how observability shapes effective connectivity estimates in practice, we experimentally perturb fMRI measurement in rodents, using a bioengineered probe2 (NOSTIC) that selectively enhances neurovascular coupling in specific neuronal populations. This experimentally reshapes the observability structure of the experiment. We show that network differences are much more specific after factoring out singular transformations. Together, our results demonstrate that effective connectivity estimates are inseparable from the observability geometry imposed by the experiment and highlight the need to account for intrinsic blind spots when interpreting connectivity differences.

Methods

Theoretical framework. 

Dynamic causal modelling. Dynamic causal modelling describes the flow of neural activity between selected regions and its mapping to measured BOLD responses. A linear DCM without modulatory effects is defined by  

dx/dt = Ax + Bu

y = h ∗ Cx

where denotes latent neural states, the effective connectivity matrix, experimental inputs, the neurovascular coupling matrix, and a hemodynamic kernel. 

Neurovascular coupling and observability. The matrix C encodes the sensitivity of measured BOLD responses to underlying neural populations and thus defines the observation model. Observed regions with strong entries in C are assumed to be embedded in a larger, partially unobserved network comprising regions that are weakly stimulated or weakly coupled to the vasculature. Observability theory guarantees that any region that has a directed path to an observed region will eventually influence observed activity. Thus, most naïve perturbations to the effective connectivity between latent nodes will change the data.

Singular transformations of effective connectivity. We characterize singular perturbations as continuous, invertible transformations of a base effective connectivity matrix A of the form

A(s) = eMs A e-Ms

where M is a diagonal matrix and s a scalar parameter. Restricting M to be diagonal ensures that transformations rescale existing connections without introducing new edges, thereby preserving network topology. Because M commutes with itself, these transformations preserve the flow generated by A, implying that transformations confined to unobserved subspaces remain unobservable in the measured data. Any diagonal M with identical values along observed dimensions generates a singular manifold, which consists in all observationally equivalent effective connectivity matrices (Fig. 1).

Singular projection. Given a reference connectivity matrix A and an alternative estimate, A’, we compute the singular projection of A’ onto A by estimating the best-fitting singular transformation from A to A’. We estimate M and s by minimizing the constrained least-squares problem

∑i,j ( log |Aij| - log |A'ij| - s (Mii-Mjj) )2; subject to CM = 0

over connections present in both matrices with matching sign. The residual captures intrinsic differences that cannot be reduced by singular transformations, i.e., differences modulo singular transformations.

Data acquisition and modelling. 

Animal subjects. All animal procedures were performed in strict compliance with US Federal guidelines, with oversight by the MIT Committee on Animal Care under approved protocol number 0721-059-24. Male and female Sprague-Dawley rats (250-350g) were acquired from Charles River Laboratories (Wilmington, MA).

Viral injection and imaging. To selectively enhance neurovascular coupling, rats received stereotaxic injections of HSV vectors expressing NOSTIC, a nitric-oxide–based activity-dependent fMRI probe, into the posteromedial thalamic nucleus (POm; 2 µL). Animals were scanned on a 7 T Bruker Biospec MRI using a custom surface coil. T2-weighted anatomical (RARE) and functional (EPI; TR = 2500ms, slice thickness = 1 mm) images were acquired before and after systemic NOS inhibition (1400W, 25 mg kg⁻¹, i.v.). Forepaw stimulation (2 mA) was delivered at 3, 9, and 27 Hz in pseudorandomized 10 s blocks.

Data analysis and modelling. fMRI data were analyzed using a GLM in SPM3. ROIs were defined using the WHS rat brain atlas, and regional activity was summarized using the first principal component. Responses were lateralized to the right hemisphere. Effective connectivity was estimated using regression DCM with a connectivity structure derived from the Neurome rat connectome, driven by inputs to the VPL thalamus. Group-level effects were assessed using Parametric Empirical Bayes4, and Bayesian model reduction5 was used to identify the connectivity structure supported by the data.

Results

NOSTIC expressed in thalamic and cortical areas increase BOLD responses. Immunohistology staining showed NOSTIC expression in thalamic nuclei (POm, anterior pretectal nucleus, APT), zona incerta (ZI), primary somatosensory cortex (S1), among others (Fig. 2, b). In these regions, NOSTIC generated an increase in BOLD signal compared to the post-1400W baseline (Fig. 2, c). This is especially prominent in thalamic regions which show positive BOLD responses before 1400W injection and no visible response after 1400W injection.

Effective connectivity under NOSTIC is more interconnected. The effective connectivity computed in both pre- and post-1400W injection reveals that a complex thalamocortical network best explains the response to forepaw stimulation (Fig. 2, d-f). In both conditions, BMR supported that all self-connections are necessary to explain the data. In the pre-1400W condition, BMR identified a minimal network supported by the data consisting of 13 interregional connections between 11 of the 14 selected regions (6 on the left hemisphere, 5 on the right hemisphere), including 3 thalamic regions. In the post-1400W, BMR a minimal network supported by the data consisting of 4 interregional connections between 4 of the 14 selected regions (1 on the left hemisphere, 3 on the right hemisphere), and none of the selected thalamic regions.

Estimates before and after 1400W differ strongly in corticothalamic connections. Inspecting the difference between the networks before and after 1400W injection (Fig. 2, i) revealed large discrepancies spanning a wide range of connections, with 20 interregional and 5 self-connections falling beyond the 95% posterior credible interval. These difference spanned corticothalamic connections, particularly between primary (M1) and secondary (M2) motor cortices and thalamic nuclei (from M2 to POm and ZI in the left hemisphere, from M1 to ZI, VP, POm and from M2 to POm in the left hemisphere).

Singular transformations are numerically invisible.  We inspect the effect of various transformation on flow of the projected pre-1400W matrix (Fig. 2, h.). All thalamic regions were considered as unobserved in the 1400W condition, due to responses of order 0.1% signal change. Naïve projection would consist in replacing post-1400W connectivity estimates involving thalamic regions with estimates from the pre 1400W sessions. Masking projection would simply assume that connectivity estimates involving thalamic regions are 0 in the post-1400W conditions. Singular projection uses the closest matrix lying in the singular manifold of the post-1400W connectivity.  The relative difference of observed flow is measured as the Frobenius norm of the difference in observed flow over the Frobenius norm of the post-1400W flow. For naïve projection, it increases with time to plateau around 25%. For masking projections, the relative error diverges exponentially quickly, suggesting that one should not ignore background connections. In comparison, singular transformations are numerically invisible, reaching a relative difference of about 10^-12 after 1,000 seconds, due to well-known instabilities of Pade approximations of matrix exponential.

Differences beyond singular transformations are sparse. Comparing the pre-1400W connectivity and its projection onto the singular manifold (Fig. 2, j-k) suggests that differences are supported by a small subset of interregional connections. Differences beyond the 95% posterior credible interval were found in three connections, all in the right hemisphere: the connection from M2 to M1 and the two reciprocal connections between M1 and S1. Comparison with the difference between pre- and post-1400W estimates (Fig. 2, i) shows that most differences were absorbed by the singular transformation.

Conclusion

Changes in effective connectivity estimates are often interpreted as reflecting changes in underlying neural interactions. Our results show that this interpretation must be made with care. Even within a fully Bayesian framework, effective connectivity estimates are constrained by the observation model of the experiment, giving rise to continuous families of connectivity matrices that are observationally equivalent. These singular transformations preserve network topology and predicted responses while producing large apparent differences in estimated connectivity.

By experimentally reshaping neurovascular coupling using the NOSTIC probe, we demonstrate that changes in the observation model can dramatically alter effective connectivity estimates. In our data, enhancing observability led to a denser apparent thalamocortical network, while suppressing this enhancement resulted in sparser connectivity estimates. However, once singular transformations were factored out, the connectivity differences supported by the data were confined to a small subset of corticocortical connections, indicating that much of the apparent differences were attributable movement along unobservable directions in parameter space.

These findings highlight a fundamental distinction between state observability and parameter observability in effective connectivity models. While latent neural activity may be theoretically recoverable through its influence on observed regions, the parameters governing interactions between latent populations can remain weakly constrained or unobservable. Bayesian inference faithfully reflects this structure, but without explicit consideration of singular transformations, differences between connectivity estimates may be misinterpreted.

More broadly, our work emphasizes that effective connectivity is inseparable from the observation model imposed by the experiment, including neurovascular coupling, input structure, and measurement sensitivity. Accounting for singular geometry provides a principled way to compare connectivity estimates across conditions, assess which differences are supported by the data, and distinguish intrinsic network changes from experimental blind spots. This framework complements existing identifiability analyses and offers practical guidance for interpreting effective connectivity results and designing experiments that better constrain the networks of interest.
