Introduction

Olfaction plays a crucial role in daily life, influencing eating behavior and social function and facilitating detection of environmental hazards1,2. Approximately 20% of the population experiences olfactory dysfunction3. Functional MRI has been used for nearly 30 years to examine odor-induced brain activation in individuals with olfactory dysfunction. However, small and non-diverse samples have limited the ability to explore how neural alterations relate to clinical features such as etiology, duration and severity of olfactory dysfunction. This limits generalizability and hinders translation of findings into clinical practice. Furthermore, regional differences in task‑evoked activation are associated with regional gray matter volume differences4. Yet, most MRI studies have examined structural or functional aspects of the central olfactory system in isolation, without considering their interrelation. To address these gaps, we aim to investigate the association between structural and functional brain characteristics of olfactory dysfunction in a large, clinically diverse cohort. 

Methods

We collected data from patients who visited the Smell and Taste Center (Ede, the Netherlands) between August 2015 and December 2024 and were clinically diagnosed with olfactory dysfunction. The dataset comprises 516 patients (288 females, 228 males; mean age 56.0 years, SD = 15.8). Etiologies were: post‑infectious (n = 160; 31%), idiopathic (n = 129; 25%), chronic rhinosinusitis (n = 98; 19%), brain trauma (n = 62; 12%), congenital anosmia (n = 31; 6%), and other causes (n = 36; 7%). All patients completed an extensive chemosensory assessment encompassing orthonasal, retronasal, and gustatory testing. Olfactory performance was assessed using either the University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test (UPSIT) (n = 61; mean ± SD = 18.3 ± 7.1; 0–40 point scale) or the Sniffin’ Sticks TDI composite score (n = 455; mean ± SD =16.3 ± 6.8; 1–48 point scale). To enable combined analyses across test types, the scores were standardized (z-scored) within each test group. 

Structural MRI scans were acquired on a 3 T Siemens Magnetom Verio  or a 3 T Philips Elition scanner with the use of a 32-channel head coil. For patients scanned before December 2023, a 2D T1‑weighted sequence was used (TR/TE = 1900/2.26 ms; flip angle = 9°; 192 sagittal slices, resolution  1 x 1 x 1 mm). For patients scanned from January 2024 onward, a 3D T1‑weighted fast‑field‑echo sequence was used (TR/TE = 980/4.5 ms; flip angle = 8°; 450 sagittal slices, resolution 0.8 x 0.8 x 0.8  mm). N = 516 patients underwent functional MRI during odor stimulation. For n = 486 patients, a 2D gradient‑echo EPI scan was acquired (TR/TE = 2240/25 ms; flip angle = 90°; GRAPPA factor = 2; 45 axial slices; resolution 3 × 3 × 3 mm; 847 dynamics). For n = 63, a single‑shot multi-band gradient‑echo EPI scan was acquired (TR/TE = 950/25 ms; flip angle = 57°; multiband factor = 3, SENSE factor = 2.2 (AP); 60 axial slices; resolution 2.2 × 2.2 × 2.2 mm; 940 dynamics). The fMRI paradigm consisted of two blocks in which odors were presented via a computer-controlled olfactometer, separated by one minute of rest. Each participant was presented with 20 chocolate odor trials, 20 beef odor trials, and 20 odorless trials, as well as 10 chocolate-picture and 10 beef-picture trials. Trials were preceded by a fixation cross turning red (3200 ms), cueing participants to sniff. A subset of trials included VAS ratings of odor intensity or odor–picture congruency. Stimuli were presented with a variable ISI (11–20 s).

Functional MRI data were preprocessed and analyzed in SPM12 using a classical general linear model approach. At the subject level, we modeled the following conditions: chocolate odor, beef odor, odorless blanks, chocolate odor+picture, beef odor+picture, and rating events (intensity/matching). Six head‑motion parameters were included as nuisance regressors. Odor-evoked activation was assessed with an odor > blank contrast, where 'odor' encompasses both odor and odor+picture trials. Contrast images of all patients were entered into a group‑level one‑sample t‑test with sex and scanner as covariates. Because our primary estimate was the total association between olfactory function and brain activation, irrespective of age, the age covariate was not included in our primary model. First, we performed a regions of interest analysis in a priori olfactory regions (bilateral piriform cortex, anterior cingulate cortex,  insula, amygdala, entorhinal cortex, hippocampus, parahippocampal gyrus, caudate, putamen, gyrus rectus, orbitofrontal gyri and superior temporal pole), applying small‑volume correction across the mask (voxel‑level PFWE < 0.05). Second, to test whether olfactory function relates to the brain responses, we added normalized TDI or UPSIT scores as an additional covariate to the group-level tests for the odor and odor+picture > blank contrasts, including sex and scanner as covariates. Inference for this association was restricted to the same ROI mask (puncorrected ≤ .001). 

Results

Odor-evoked brain activation was observed in the bilateral inferior frontal gyrus, amygdala, hippocampus, and parahippocampal gyrus (Figure 1a). Olfactory function showed a weak positive correlation with odor‑evoked activation in the right amygdala (Figure 1b; r = 0.15, peak T = 3.66, k = 10).

Conclusion

These preliminary results indicate that patients with olfactory dysfunction exhibit odor-evoked activation in key olfactory regions, with reduced right amygdala responses associated with greater degree of dysfunction. Ongoing analyses will further clarify how clinical characteristics, brain structure, and structure–function relationships relate to olfactory dysfunction. Further analyses will examine 1) correlations between odor‑evoked brain activation and clinical characteristics (etiology, duration), 2) Voxel-based morphometry on the anatomical images (CAT12) to test effects of etiology/duration and their relation to olfactory function, and 3) structure–function relationships (VBM versus fMRI).
