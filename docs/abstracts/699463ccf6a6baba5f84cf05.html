Introduction

The detection of hypometabolic areas in 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography (PET) is essential for diagnosing and treating neurological disorders such as Alzheimer's disease or epilepsy. In clinical practice, PET images are mostly analysed visually, with sensitivity and specificity greatly varying depending on the observer's experience. Quantitative analysis would help define a more objective limit between normal and pathological findings.

Traditional voxelwise approaches register the subject's PET to a standard space and compare it to a population of controls using z-scores. However, their sensitivity is limited by intersubject variability in non-pathological tracer uptake, and registration to standard space may conceal subtle anomalies. To address these limitations, subject-specific approaches for anomaly detection have been proposed that register multiple healthy controls to the target subject and combine them through image fusion to create a healthy appearance model1.

While recent anomaly detection approaches mainly use deep generative models capable of reconstructing pseudo-healthy images2, these earlier methods relied on deformable image registration and fusion techniques. Image synthesis using registration has become less common in recent years due to concerns regarding speed and accuracy. However, recent advances in deep learning-based registration have improved both aspects3, making it worthwhile to revisit registration-based methods.

Building on recent work demonstrating the potential of deep learning-based registration for image synthesis4, we propose to apply this strategy to pseudo-healthy image synthesis for anomaly detection and assess its relevance against current generative approaches2,4. This work comprises two steps: selecting a high-performing registration model from several public models, then evaluating a complete anomaly detection method based on registration and fusion.

Methods

The proposed anomaly detection framework creates a subject-specific model of healthy PET uptake using the target subject's T1-weighted (T1w) magnetic resonance image (MRI) and a database of healthy controls (paired T1w MRI and PET images)1. Each control T1w image is deformed to the target T1w, and the corresponding PET images are mapped using the same transformations. A local image similarity measure is used to weight and fuse the registered PET images, generating a subject-specific model of healthy PET appearance (mean and standard deviation). The resulting voxelwise z-score map evaluates the deviation of the subject's uptake with respect to the subject-specific pseudo-healthy model. The approach is summarised in Figure 1.

All T1w MRI and FDG PET images were preprocessed using Clinica pipelines5. T1w images underwent bias field correction using N4ITK and affine registration to MNI space (ICBM 2009c template) using ANTs, resulting in 169×208×179 images with 1 mm isotropic voxels. PET images were rigidly registered to the subject's T1w image, then transported to MNI space using the T1w to MNI transformation, intensity normalised using a reference region (pons and cerebellum) to obtain SUVR maps, and cropped to the same dimensions.

The anomaly detection framework relies on a database of pairs of coregistered T1w MR and PET images from healthy controls. For each control, the T1w image was non-rigidly registered to the target subject's T1w using a deep learning approach, then the resulting deformation field was applied to the corresponding PET image. A structural similarity (SSIM) map was calculated voxel by voxel between the target T1w MRI and each registered healthy control T1w using a Gaussian kernel. These SSIM maps were then transformed into continuous weights via an exponential decay function applied to the SSIM ranks across healthy controls. Using ranks ensures stable weight distributions across voxels.

The registered PET volumes were combined according to these weights to estimate, at each voxel, a weighted pseudo-healthy mean and standard deviation. Finally, the anomaly map was obtained by computing a z-score between the subject's PET image and the pseudo-healthy appearance model.

We compared five deep learning-based registration methods representing different architectural approaches: SITReg6, a multi-resolution architecture enforcing symmetry, inverse consistency and topology preservation by construction; UniGradICON7, a foundation model trained across multiple datasets with strong generalisation capabilities; VoxelMorph8, an unsupervised learning framework optimising standard image matching objective functions based on the image intensities; TransMorph9, a hybrid Transformer-ConvNet model with larger receptive fields for capturing long-range spatial relationships; and cLapIRN10, a conditional registration method allowing control of deformation field smoothness.

The methods were evaluated according to three standard criteria. First, anatomical similarity was assessed using the Dice score between brain masks. Second, regularity of the deformation field was measured by the percentage of negative Jacobian determinant (folding rate), indicating smoothness. Third, cycle consistency was evaluated by registering image A to image B and then image B to image A, where composing both transformations should yield the identity. The best-performing registration method was then integrated into the anomaly detection pipeline to generate subject-specific abnormality maps for control and Alzheimer's disease subjects.

Results

To select the registration model most suited to our pipeline, we evaluated the five public deep learning-based methods using ADNI data11,12. The original goal of ADNI was to test whether serial MRI, PET, other biological markers, and clinical and neuropsychological assessment can be combined to measure the progression of mild cognitive impairment and early Alzheimer's disease. The current goals include validating biomarkers for clinical trials, improving the generalizability of ADNI data by increasing diversity in the participant cohort, and to provide data concerning the diagnosis and progression of Alzheimer’s disease to the scientific community. 

The comparison of registration methods (Figure 2, top) evaluated anatomical similarity (Dice score), deformation field regularity (negative Jacobian rate), and cycle consistency. SITReg6 and UniGradICON7 offered the best overall performance, achieving high Dice scores and low negative Jacobian rates, indicating anatomically accurate registrations with regular deformation fields suitable for local fusion. Both methods also demonstrated good cycle consistency. UniGradICON was selected for the anomaly detection pipeline due to its excellent trade-off between accuracy and ease of use.

The obtained anomaly maps show, for control subjects, only weak localised deviations, while Alzheimer's subjects present extensive hypometabolism in the relevant regions (Figure 2, bottom), consistent with the clinical literature12. The weights obtained by computing the structural similarity revealed that the contribution of a small subset of atlases dominates in each region, confirming the relevance of local weighting.

Conclusion

We showed that a modernised registration and fusion approach can produce anatomically coherent anomaly maps while relying on a pre-trained diffeomorphic registration model. The comparison of five state-of-the-art deep learning-based registration methods showed that SITReg6 and UniGradICON7 achieved the best performance across multiple criteria, with UniGradICON7 offering an excellent balance between accuracy and practical usability.

The preliminary anomaly maps obtained with UniGradICON7 show encouraging results, with patterns that align with expected clinical presentations. However, further evaluation is needed to fully assess the clinical utility of this approach. Building on previous validation strategies1, future work should include both direct evaluation and task-based assessment. First, the pseudo-healthy PET images and anomaly maps of cognitively normal subjects should be quantitatively compared with their actual PET images to ensure minimal spurious deviations. Second, the anomaly maps could be used as features for individual classification tasks to assess their ability to extract clinically relevant information. Additionally, quantitative validation using region-of-interest analysis in areas typically affected by Alzheimer's disease, and comparison with expert visual readings would help establish the sensitivity and specificity of the approach.

A systematic comparison with deep generative approaches, such as variational autoencoders and diffusion models, is essential to determine the relative strengths and limitations of registration-based versus generative model-based methods for pseudo-healthy image synthesis. This comparison should consider not only reconstruction accuracy but also computational requirements, data efficiency, interpretability, and robustness to out-of-distribution data.
