Introduction

Long-term aerobic exercise is one of the most robust lifestyle factors supporting brain health and inducing neuroplasticity^¹. Previous work has demonstrated exercise-related changes in brain structure, functional activation, and undirected functional connectivity. However, the effects of long-term exercise on directed (effective) brain connectivity, which reflects the causal flow of neural information across large-scale networks, remain poorly understood. Characterizing how sustained exercise reshapes directed network topology may provide mechanistic insight into the frequently reported cognitive and emotional benefits of physical training. The present randomized controlled trial investigated the effects of a 12-week aerobic exercise intervention on frequency-specific directed resting-state EEG connectivity in healthy young adults. We hypothesized that long-term exercise would induce systematic reorganization of global and local network properties indexed by graph-theoretical measures.

Methods

Physically inactive young adults (mean age = 23.37 years) were randomized to an aerobic exercise group (A; n = 44) or a passive control group (P; n = 32). The intervention consisted of 12 weeks of cycling ergometer training (3 sessions/week), including 6 weeks of moderate-intensity continuous exercise followed by 6 weeks of moderate- to high-intensity interval exercise.
Resting-state, eyes-open EEG (6 min) was recorded at baseline, mid-intervention (6 weeks), and post-intervention (12 weeks) using a 64-channel BioSemi system. After automated and manual artifact rejection, directed functional connectivity was estimated using the Directed Transfer Function (DTF^²), as implemented in the eConnectome toolbox^3, across six frequency bands (delta, theta, alpha, beta, gamma, and broadband). Connectivity matrices were thresholded to retain the strongest 15% of connections.
Seven weighted graph-theoretical metrics indexing global integration, local efficiency, segregation, and hub organization were computed using the Brain Connectivity Toolbox^4. Within-group pre–post changes were tested separately for the exercise and control groups using linear mixed-effects models. Multiple comparisons across metrics and frequency bands were controlled using FDR correction (p < 0.05).

Results

No significant pre–post changes were observed in the control group for any network metric or frequency band (all FDR-corrected p > 0.05). Likewise, no significant effects were found from pre- to mid-intervention in either group (all p > 0.05). In contrast, robust pre–post changes emerged selectively in the exercise group across multiple frequency bands (linear mixed-effects models; all FDR-corrected p < 0.05).

In the delta band, the exercise group showed significant reductions in global efficiency (GE) and assortativity (r) from pre- to post-intervention (GE: t = −3.80, d = 0.98; r: t = −3.01, d = 0.79). In the theta band, GE decreased (t = −3.28, d = 0.85). In the alpha band, GE decreased (t = −5.74, d = 1.49), whereas transitivity increased (t = 2.00, d = 0.40). In the beta band, GE was reduced (t = −4.54, d = 1.17). In the gamma band, GE decreased (t = −2.12, d = 1.17), while local efficiency, clustering coefficient, and rich-club coefficient increased (LE: t = 2.03, d = 0.52; CC: t = 2.91, d = 0.75; rcc: t = 2.60, d = 0.68). Finally, in the broadband signal, global efficiency decreased (t = −3.81, d = 0.98) and was accompanied by a reduction in assortativity (t = −3.01, d = 0.77).

Collectively, these within-group results indicate a training-related shift from globally distributed toward more locally specialized and hub-organized directed network communication in the exercise group, with no corresponding changes in the control group.

Conclusion

Long-term aerobic exercise induced systematic, frequency-dependent reorganization of directed resting-state EEG networks, evident as significant pre–post changes only in the exercise group. Increases in local efficiency, clustering, and rich-club organization in the gamma band point to enhanced local specialization and hub-based integration within higher-frequency networks implicated in sensorimotor and cognitive processing. Concurrent decreases in global efficiency—particularly in low-frequency bands—suggest pruning of redundant long-range coupling, consistent with a more efficient and selective global network architecture.
 
This pattern indicates that exercise-related plasticity may optimize the balance between global integration and local specialization rather than uniformly increasing connectivity strength. These network-level adaptations may contribute to the cognitive and emotional benefits frequently reported following long-term aerobic exercise.
