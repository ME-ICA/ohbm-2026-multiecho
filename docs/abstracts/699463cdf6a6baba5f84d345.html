Introduction

Pain is a complex phenomenon which arises from the interaction of bottom-up and top-down processes. It is well known that it is much more than the bare transmission of the nociceptive signal from the periphery to cortical areas. This bottom-up (feedforward) signal is integrated and modulated by cognitive and affective factors. However, the exact nature and neural correlates of the interaction of bottom-up and top-down processes is yet to be determined. Previous evidence suggest that distraction related modulation can already occur at lower levels in the ascending pain neuraxis such as the spinal cord 1 and the thalamus2. Additionally, pain-related activity in cortical brain areas is modulated3 which may reflect a consequence of the altered signalling from the spinal cord and thalamus, but also modulation at the cortico-cortical level. However, additional pathways in terms of cortico-cortical, feedback connections can also be the source of such a modulated cortical activity4. The neocortex is a complex structure with functionally distinct layers as suggested by the canonical microcircuit model5, namely feedforward and feedback connections are more related to middle and deep/superficial layer activity, respectively. With the advent of high-resolution laminar fMRI, the contribution of different layers (and consequently the direction of information) in pain processing can be disentangled. Here, we used high resolution fMRI to test the distraction effect on pain signal, whether it is a result of top-down cortico-cortical modulation, the modulation of bottom-up, or the combination of both.

Methods

To investigate bottom-up and top-down processes in pain experience, we invited thirty-four participants to perform a cognitive task while they received concomitantly a painful heat temperature stimulation in this preregistered study (https://osf.io/68je5). Participant’s invitation was based on our preregistration criteria of showing considerable behaviour distraction analgesia. The cognitive task (n-back) consisted of two levels of attentional load, modulating the level of distraction. Additionally, the individually calibrated heat stimulation elicited two levels of pain experience (Figure 1). Participants provided subjective pain ratings (VAS100, anchors: 0- not painful at all, 100-extremely painful). High-resolution fMRI (3D GRE-EPI, isotropic 0.85 mm resolution, TE/TR = 24/3000 ms) was collected during the task with a 7T MRI system (Terra 7 T, Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany). Additional whole brain anatomical images were collected (MP2RAGE, 0.75 mm isotropic resolution, TE/TR/TI1/TI2 = 1.81/6000/800/2750 ms). Functional image preprocessing contained motion correction, distortion correction, and registration to the anatomical image with SPM, FSL, ANTS. Individual functional ROI of the secondary somatosensory cortex, contralateral to the stimulation, was used. The pial and white matter surfaces were determined with Freesurfer. Between the pial and white matter vertex pairs of the secondary somatosensory cortex, the preprocessed fMRI timeseries data was equidistantly sampled (SPM) at 20 data points (third order polynomial interpolation). Using temporal general linear model, beta-parameters of each condition were estimated. The laminar dependency of the main effect of stimulus temperature (bottom-up process) and the main effect of distraction (top-down process) were estimated. Linear mixed model analysis was performed to test laminar pattern difference between the two processes (main interest: interaction effect between cortical depth and process). Additionally, the relation of behaviour distraction analgesia to the layer dependent brain activity was investigated. The layer dependent brain activity difference between the two processes was correlated with behaviour analgesia. 

Results

Cognitive modulation was significantly affected pain perception on the behaviour level. Participants reported on average 6.6 point decrease on the VAS100 (confidence interval (CI): [4.34, 8.86], p<0.001) in the high distraction condition (Figure 1). Additionally, higher temperature elicited higher pain perception by 33.23 point (CI [30.98, 35.49], p<0.001). The significant interaction between the temperature and level of distraction indicated higher distraction effect in the high temperature condition (ES: 3.52, CI [0.32, 6.71], p=0.031). The laminar profile of the bottom-up process indicated a more pronounced middle layer activity (reversed U-shape profile, Figure 2). Additionally, the top-down process has a similar pattern but importantly with a significant superficially shifted activity pattern (p<0.05). Furthermore, this relative superficial shift was significantly correlated with behaviour distraction analgesia (Figure 2) (p<0.05).

Conclusion

Our 7-T fMRI study reveals distinct laminar patterns underlying the bottom-up and top-down modulation of pain in participants with a robust distraction effect at the behavioural level. First, the relative shift towards superficial layers in the distraction contrast (main effect of distraction) suggests the engagement of feedback mechanisms during the top-down modulation of pain, which might originate from cortico-cortical connections. Importantly, this was associated with behavioural attentional analgesia. Second, the similar laminar pattern of both the distraction contrast and the nociceptive contrast have an important consequence. As the middle layers not only responded to the change in nociceptive input (main effect of temperature), but also to the pain modulation by cognitive load, our study lends further support to the top-down modulation of afferent nociceptive input as previously suggested by eg. spinal cord imaging1. In sum, our study is first to reveal the distinct patterns of bottom-up and top-down modulation of pain at the level of the cortical layers which opens up new trajectories to elucidate interindividual differences in the perception and modulation of pain in health and disease.
